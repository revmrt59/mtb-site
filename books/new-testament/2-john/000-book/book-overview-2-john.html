2 John

2 John - Book Overview

Background

Author

The letter comes from "the Elder" (v. 1), and the church has long recognized him as John the apostle. Irenaeus, who as a young man heard Polycarp, a disciple of John, quotes 2 John and names John the Lord's disciple as its author. The letter also speaks John's language. Truth, love, the commandment "from the beginning," abiding, antichrist, and joy made full all appear in John's Gospel and in 1 John.

John does not give his name, and he did not need to. His readers knew who "the Elder" was. The title likely reflects both his age and his recognized role among the churches, though the letter itself does not state his age or where he lived.

Audience

John writes "to the elect lady and her children" (v. 1) and closes with greetings from "the children of your elect sister" (v. 13). The lady is most likely a local church, her children its members, and her sister the church where John was. Some read her as an individual Christian woman and her family, and a few take Kyria ("lady") as her name. But John moves to plural "you" in verses 6, 8, 10, and 12, writes about loving "one another," and warns about whom to receive into the house. That reads best as a congregation meeting in a home.

John does not develop the image further. Still, a church addressed as a chosen lady, loved "in truth," fits the wider biblical picture of God's people as a bride belonging to Christ (2 Corinthians 11:2; Ephesians 5:25–27).

Date

2 John was likely written around AD 85–95, near the time of 1 John. The letter gives no date. This estimate rests on early church testimony that John ministered late in life in the province of Asia, on the close ties in language and concern between 2 John and 1 John, and on the kind of false teaching the letter confronts. Ephesus is the traditional place of writing.

Historical Setting

First-century churches met in homes. The household was the church's meeting place, guest room, and base for ministry. Traveling teachers and missionaries moved from church to church and depended on believers to house them, feed them, and send them on their way (3 John 5–8). This system carried the gospel across the Roman world. It also meant a church's hospitality could carry a false message as easily as a true one.

Teachers had arisen who did not confess that Jesus Christ had come in the flesh (v. 7). 1 John warns against a closely related error and speaks of some who "went out from us" (1 John 2:19; 4:1–3). The two letters address the same kind of threat, though the letters do not tell us whether the same people are in view.

2 John has only 13 verses. It pairs naturally with 3 John. 2 John warns a church not to welcome false teachers; 3 John commends a believer for welcoming true ones.

Occasion

John has met some of the lady's children and rejoices greatly that they are "walking in truth" (v. 4). At the same time, "many deceivers have gone out into the world" (v. 7). A church that gladly opened its home to traveling teachers could give these deceivers a base for their ministry without realizing it. John hopes to visit soon (v. 12), but the matter cannot wait, so he sends a short letter ahead.

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

2 John is a short pastoral letter about remaining in the truth of Jesus Christ. It joins three concerns: encouragement to keep loving one another by walking in God's commandments, warning about deceivers who deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh, and instruction about whom the church should receive and support. A warm greeting opens the letter, and John's longing to see the believers face to face closes it. In a single page, personal affection, apostolic instruction, and doctrinal warning stand side by side.

Central Message

God's people are to remain in the truth of Jesus Christ, walking in love and obedience and refusing to support teaching that would lead them away from Him, so that they keep fellowship with the Father and the Son. For John, truth and love are not two values to be balanced. Truth is the life in which love is lived.

Why This Book Matters

2 John shows how a loving church protects its people. Some assume that loving people means affirming every belief about Jesus. John, remembered as the apostle of love, writes the same letter that pleads "that we love one another" (v. 5) and tells the church not to give a platform to those who deny Christ. Guarding the truth about Jesus is one of the ways a church loves its people.

The letter is also one of the few places in the New Testament that speaks directly to a church's hospitality toward traveling teachers. Read with 3 John, it gives practical wisdom for deciding whom a church welcomes, supports, and sends into ministry.

Key Themes to Watch For

  • Truth that abides. Truth is something God's people live in, not only something they know; it "abides in us and will be with us forever" (vv. 1–4).

  • Love lived through obedience. "This is love, that we walk according to His commandments" (vv. 5–6).

  • Remaining in the teaching of Christ. Fellowship with the Father and the Son is kept by abiding in the truth about Jesus (vv. 7, 9).

  • Faithful perseverance. Believers must watch themselves so "we do not lose those things we worked for" (v. 8).

  • Discerning hospitality. A church must not lend its home and blessing to those who deny Christ (vv. 10–11).

  • Joy in fellowship. John longs to speak face to face, "that our joy may be full" (v. 12).

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

Truth. In John's writings, truth is God's reality revealed in Jesus, who is "the truth" (John 14:6), and made known by "the Spirit of truth" (John 14:17). To "walk in truth" (v. 4) is to live in line with that revelation, not only to agree with it.

The commandment from the beginning. The command to love one another is not a new rule John is adding (v. 5). It is Jesus' own command (John 13:34), which these believers have heard since their life in Christ began.

Coming in the flesh. This is the confession that Jesus Christ truly became human, with a real body. Some early teachers claimed that the divine Christ only seemed human or was separate from the man Jesus. John names anyone who denies the incarnation "a deceiver and an antichrist" (v. 7).

Antichrist. John uses the word for anyone who opposes Christ or puts a false Christ in His place (1 John 2:22). He tells believers they have heard "that the Antichrist is coming," yet "even now many antichrists have come" (1 John 2:18). The many deceivers already at work carry "the spirit of the Antichrist" (1 John 4:3), the same opposition to Christ that will come to a head in the one still to come.

The doctrine of Christ. "Doctrine" means teaching (v. 9). The phrase can mean the teaching about Christ or the teaching Christ Himself gave. In 2 John the immediate emphasis is the apostolic truth about Christ's Person, especially that He came in the flesh (v. 7). That truth stands at the center of all that Christ taught and the apostles handed down.

Receiving and greeting. In this setting, receiving a traveling teacher "into your house" and greeting him with a blessing meant welcoming him into the church's life and supporting his ministry (vv. 10–11). John forbids that support and endorsement for those who deny Christ, because it makes a believer a partner in spreading a false message. He is not forbidding ordinary kindness, personal hospitality, or conversation with people who hold different beliefs.

Book Flow

2 John 1:1–3: A Greeting from the Elder

John greets the elect lady and her children, "whom I love in truth." Truth is the bond he shares with all who know it, and it "abides in us and will be with us forever" (v. 2). His greeting is also a promise: grace, mercy, and peace "will be with you" from the Father and the Son, "in truth and love."

2 John 1:4–6: Walking in Truth and Love

John rejoices greatly at finding some of her children walking in truth. He then pleads for the commandment they have had from the beginning, "that we love one another." He defines love as walking according to God's commandments, joining love and obedience into one way of life.

2 John 1:7–11: Beware of Deceivers

Verse 7 turns the letter. "Many deceivers" deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh, so the believers must watch themselves and not lose what has been worked for among them. Whoever does not abide in the doctrine of Christ "does not have God," but whoever abides in it "has both the Father and the Son" (v. 9). The church must not receive or bless those who bring a different teaching, or it "shares in his evil deeds."

2 John 1:12–13: Final Greetings

John has much more to say but would rather come and "speak face to face, that our joy may be full." The letter ends as it began, with family language, as the children of the elect sister send their greetings.

Christ and Life

John presents "the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father" (v. 3), a phrase found only here in the New Testament. The title is relational. Jesus is the Son who shares the Father's life, and John reveals that relationship with the Father cannot be separated from relationship with His Son. Grace, mercy, and peace come from the Father and the Son together, and the whole letter treats the truth about Jesus as inseparable from knowing God.

John insists that Jesus Christ came "in the flesh" (v. 7). The eternal Son truly became human, with a real body, not the appearance of one. The wider New Testament shows that He rose bodily and remains fully human as He reigns and intercedes for His people (Luke 24:39; 1 Timothy 2:5; Hebrews 7:24–25). A Christ who only seemed human could not truly share our life or bear our sin. The Christ John proclaims did both.

This is why verse 9 carries such weight. Whoever abides in the doctrine of Christ "has both the Father and the Son." Right teaching about Jesus is not an end in itself. Fellowship with God comes through Christ Himself, and sound teaching reveals and guards the truth about the One through whom we know the Father. No one can reject the true Son and still have the Father (John 14:6; 1 John 2:23). The doctrine of Christ is precious because Christ Himself is precious.

The truth John speaks of is also alive. It "abides in us and will be with us forever" (v. 2). These words echo Jesus' promise of the Spirit of truth, who would "abide with you forever" and "be in you" (John 14:16–17). Believers do not merely hold correct ideas about Jesus. By the Spirit, the truth makes its home in them, and they walk in it (v. 4). Sound doctrine matters deeply, and it is meant to be lived from the inside.

That life shows itself in love expressed through obedience (vv. 5–6). Jesus said, "If anyone loves Me, he will keep My word; and My Father will love him, and We will come to him and make Our home with him" (John 14:23). Obedience is not a cold substitute for love. It is how love for God is lived out, and it is the setting in which the Father and the Son make their home with those who love Him.

The letter closes with John's longing that "our joy may be full" (v. 12). Fellowship with the Father and the Son overflows into fellowship with one another, and John wrote his first letter for the same reason, "that your joy may be full" (1 John 1:3–4). Remaining in the truth of Christ leads to full joy.

Reading Guide

One-Sentence Summary

In a brief letter to a beloved church, John rejoices over believers walking in truth, calls them to love one another through obedience, and warns them not to receive or support teachers who deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh.

Read This Book Remembering...

Read 2 John as a letter of love. The warning about deceivers comes from a pastor who loves this church "in truth," rejoices over their faithfulness, and longs to see them face to face. It is not a call to suspicion but a call to remain in the truth of Christ and in the fellowship and joy that truth brings.

Remember that verses 10–11 concern support and endorsement of those who spread a false Christ, not ordinary kindness toward people who believe differently. And remember that John planned to say much more in person (v. 12), so read the letter alongside 1 John, which develops the same concerns more fully.

Helpful Notes

Verse 8. The NKJV reads "that we do not lose those things we worked for, but that we may receive a full reward." Some manuscripts read "you" in place of "we." The NKJV wording includes the labor of John and his co-workers among these believers, which could be lost if the church turned from the truth.

Verse 9. "Transgresses" follows manuscripts that read a word meaning to step over or go beyond a line. Many early manuscripts instead read a word meaning to "go ahead" or "run ahead," which most modern translations follow. Some interpreters suggest the deceivers presented themselves as advanced beyond the apostles' teaching, but the letter does not say so. Either reading describes leaving the teaching of Christ behind.

Cerinthus. Irenaeus records a tradition, passed down from Polycarp, that John once left a bathhouse in Ephesus on learning that Cerinthus was inside. Cerinthus taught that the divine Christ was separate from the man Jesus. The story illustrates the kind of error John opposed, but 2 John does not name Cerinthus, and nothing connects him directly to the situation behind this letter.

Twin letters. 2 John and 3 John share a writer called "the Elder," a similar length, and nearly identical closings about not wanting to write with ink and hoping to speak face to face (v. 12; 3 John 13–14). Each is short enough to fit on a single sheet of papyrus, and many scholars think that is how they were written, though the letters themselves do not say so.
