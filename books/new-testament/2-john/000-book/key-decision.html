2 John Key Questions

Important questions in the text, and how MTB understands them.

Revision 4 · November 26, 2026

Jump to: Book Questions · Passage Questions

Book Questions

Who is “the Elder”? (2 John 1:1)

The writer calls himself only “the Elder” and never gives his name. MTB, with long church tradition, understands him to be John the apostle. The letter’s language closely matches John’s Gospel and 1 John, especially on truth, love, abiding, and the commandment heard “from the beginning.”

Some interpreters point instead to another leader named John, often called John the Elder. The letter itself does not settle the question, so MTB holds the apostolic identification without overstating it. His age, home, and wider role in the church are inferences, not statements of the letter.

Read: 2 John 1:1, John 13:34, 1 John 2:7

Who is “the elect lady”? (2 John 1:1, 13)

MTB reads the elect lady as a local church and “her children” as its members. The closing greeting from “the children of your elect sister” (v. 13) fits naturally as greetings from a sister church.

Other faithful readers see a real Christian woman and her family. Some even take “lady” (Greek kyria) as her personal name. This remains a legitimate alternative, and the letter’s message does not depend on settling it.

Some connect the elect lady with the Bride of Christ. That is a wider biblical picture (2 Corinthians 11:2; Ephesians 5:25–27), but 2 John itself does not develop it.

Read: 2 John 1:1, 2 John 1:13, 2 Corinthians 11:2, Ephesians 5:25-27

Is “truth” in 2 John just correct doctrine? (2 John 1:1–6, 9)

In 2 John, truth is the apostolic message about Jesus Christ, and it cannot be separated from Jesus Himself, who said, “I am the way, the truth, and the life” (John 14:6). Abiding in the truth is more than holding correct beliefs. It is remaining in Christ. At the same time, no one can claim to know Christ while setting aside the truth about Him.

The truth that “abides in us and will be with us forever” (v. 2) echoes Jesus’ promise of the Spirit of truth, who abides forever (John 14:16–17). That is a real biblical parallel, but 2 John does not say the truth in verse 2 is the Holy Spirit.

This is also why truth and love are not two values to balance. John loves “in truth” (v. 1) and treats walking in truth and walking in love as one life (vv. 4–6). Truth is the life in which love is lived.

Read: 2 John 1:1–6, 2 John 1:9, John 14:6, John 14:16-17 · Related: Does verse 9 make right doctrine the way to have God?

Passage Questions

2 John 1:4–6 · Walking in Truth and in Love

If love means walking in His commandments, is love just rule-keeping? (vv. 5–6)

John says love is walking according to God’s commandments, and the commandment is that we love one another. This is Jesus’ own command (John 13:34), and it flows from relationship with God. The Father and the Son make their home with those who love Jesus and keep His word (John 14:23). Obedience is how love lives, not a replacement for it.

Read: 2 John 1:5–6, John 13:34, John 14:23 · Related: Is truth just correct doctrine?

2 John 1:7–9 · Abide in the Doctrine of Christ

Does “coming in the flesh” mean His incarnation or His return? (v. 7)

MTB reads it as affirming the genuine incarnation: the eternal Son truly became human (John 1:14; 1 John 4:2–3). The present-tense wording does not by itself point to the Second Coming.

Some draw from the same wording that Christ remains fully human now. That truth is taught elsewhere in the New Testament (Luke 24:39; 1 Timothy 2:5; Hebrews 7:24–25), but MTB does not build it on the grammar of this verse.

Read: 2 John 1:7, John 1:14, 1 John 4:2-3, Hebrews 7:24-25

Is the Antichrist one future person or many deceivers now? (v. 7)

John says the one who denies Christ’s coming in the flesh “is a deceiver and an antichrist.” His focus is the many deceivers already at work, who carry “the spirit of the Antichrist” (1 John 4:3). A coming Antichrist is a wider conclusion drawn from 1 John 2:18. It is not something 2 John establishes by itself.

Read: 2 John 1:7, 1 John 2:18, 1 John 4:3

What does John mean by not losing “those things we worked for”? (v. 8)

John writes, “Look to yourselves, that we do not lose those things we worked for, but that we may receive a full reward.” MTB follows the NKJV “we.” John ties his own apostolic labor to these believers’ faithfulness: if deceivers draw them away, the work done among them is lost. Paul speaks the same way about the churches he served (Philippians 2:16; 1 Thessalonians 2:19–20).

Many manuscripts and modern translations read “you” throughout, so that the believers’ own work is in view. Either way, the warning is real. MTB does not speculate about the exact nature of the “full reward” or build teaching on degrees of reward from this verse.

Read: 2 John 1:8, Philippians 2:16, 1 Thessalonians 2:19-20

Is “the doctrine of Christ” teaching about Christ or teaching from Christ? (v. 9)

Both are possible. The context favors the apostolic teaching about Christ, especially who He is and that He came in the flesh. That emphasis does not mean that all of Christ’s teaching is only about His identity.

Read: 2 John 1:9–10

Does verse 9 make right doctrine the way to have God? (v. 9)

Whoever abides in the doctrine of Christ “has both the Father and the Son.” Fellowship with God comes through Christ Himself. Remaining in the truth about Him is inseparable from belonging to Him, but correct wording is not a substitute for knowing Him. Both halves of the verse keep their full force.

Read: 2 John 1:9, John 17:3, 1 John 2:23 · Related: Is truth just correct doctrine?

2 John 1:10–11 · Do Not Receive the Teacher Who Denies Christ

Does John forbid kindness or hospitality toward unbelievers, including family? (vv. 10–11)

No. John warns against knowingly supporting or advancing teachers who deny the true Jesus Christ. In the early church, traveling teachers depended on believers to house them and send them on their way (compare 3 John 5–8). Welcoming such a teacher helped his message spread. Whether John has a private home or a house church in view is inferred, not stated. Refusing to “greet him” (v. 10) means withholding the welcome that would signal fellowship with his mission, for “he who greets him shares in his evil deeds” (v. 11). It is not a ban on ordinary courtesy.

These verses are not a general ban on conversation, ordinary hospitality, or relationships with unbelievers, including family members. Believers can keep loving and welcoming people who believe differently without endorsing their teaching. The real question is whether our actions materially support the spread of teaching that denies Christ.

Read: 2 John 1:10–11, 3 John 5-8 · Related: Separating from other Christians?

Can these verses justify separating from other Christians over secondary disagreements? (vv. 7–11)

No. John’s warning targets teachers who deny foundational truth about Jesus Christ and actively spread that error. It does not call genuine believers to divide over secondary doctrines or denominational differences. Other passages of Scripture address serious doctrinal or moral error in the church. 2 John is not a general teaching on church discipline or separation.

Read: 2 John 1:7–11 · Related: Kindness toward unbelievers
