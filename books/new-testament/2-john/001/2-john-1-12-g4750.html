FACE - ( stoma , Strong's G4750 )

Summary:

The expression rendered "face to face" in 2 John 12 literally translates as "mouth to mouth" (*stoma pros stoma*). While Greek possesses a specific word for face (*prosopon*), John deliberately employs *stoma* ("mouth") to emphasize direct, unmediated, conversational fellowship. Borrowing an idiom deeply rooted in Old Testament thought—where God spoke to Moses "mouth to mouth" (Numbers 12:8)—John highlights the intimacy, warmth, and full spiritual joy that flows from personal presence and vocal interaction, surpassing what written correspondence on papyrus and ink can convey.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • In ancient Greco-Roman communication, letters written on papyrus (*chartes*) with ink (*melan*) were indispensable for maintaining long-distance relationships, yet they were recognized as secondary substitutes for personal presence.

  • The Greek noun *stoma* refers physically to the mouth as the organ of speech and voice. When paired in the doubled prepositional idiom *stoma pros stoma* ("mouth to mouth"), it mirrors the Semitic expression *peh el-peh* found in Hebrew Scripture.

  • This idiom evokes the imagery of two individuals standing close enough to hear the breath, tone, and spoken words of one another directly, leaving no room for misunderstanding or transactional distance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The noun *stoma* denotes the mouth as the organ of speech, the opening through which voice and breath pass, or metaphorically the edge of an instrument such as a sword.

  • In relational contexts, *stoma* serves as a synecdoche for personal speech and presence.

  • When combined with the preposition *pros* ("toward" or "facing") in the construction *stoma pros stoma*, the phrase functions idiomatically to mean direct, personal, and interactive conversation without an intermediate medium.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • *Stoma* appears frequently across the New Testament, referring most commonly to human speech, divine revelation through the prophets, or spiritual utterance.

  • The word picture in 2 John 12 is distinct from *prosopon pros prosopon* ("face to face"), which focuses primarily on visual perception and appearance (as in 1 Corinthians 13:12).

  • By using *stoma pros stoma*, John paints a pastoral picture of verbal communion—hearing the voice, sharing heart-to-heart testimony, and experiencing mutual dialogue where warmth, inflection, and immediate response cultivate shared joy.

4. Theological Implications

  • Christian fellowship is fundamentally relational and incarnational. Just as God revealed Himself supremely not merely through written tablets but through the living Word who became flesh, Christian love thrives on personal community.

  • Written communication is vital for doctrine and instruction, yet physical absence creates a longing that only personal presence can satisfy.

  • The fulfillment of joy (*chara*) in the body of Christ is connected to shared presence and direct communion, anticipating the ultimate face-to-face fellowship believers will enjoy with Christ in eternity.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In 2 John 12, John balances the necessity of his brief written letter with his eager desire for personal visitation.

  • The context of 2 John involves warnings against false teachers who deceive believers. In such critical times, warm pastoral presence and direct speech reinforce true doctrine and protect the flock far more effectively than ink on paper.

  • The purpose clause "that our joy may be full" shows that true Christian joy is completed through direct, unhindered fellowship among believers walking in truth and love.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 3 John 14 – "but I hope to see you shortly, and we shall speak face to face."

  • Numbers 12:8 – "I speak with him face to face, even plainly, and not in dark sayings; and he sees the form of the LORD."

  • Matthew 12:34 – "Brood of vipers! How can you, being evil, speak good things? For out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks."

  • Romans 10:10 – "For with the heart one believes unto righteousness, and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Papyrus (*chartes*) was made from sliced reed stalks pressed together, and ink (*melan*) was a mixture of soot, gum, and water. While practical, ink could fade or obscure vocal tone, whereas direct speech carried genuine emotion and pastoral clarity.

  • Pastors and teachers should note that digital or written communication, while useful, cannot fully replace personal shepherd-care, physical presence, and direct verbal encouragement in local church life.
