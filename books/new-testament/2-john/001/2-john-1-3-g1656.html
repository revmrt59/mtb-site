MERCY - ( eleos , Strong's G1656 )

Summary:

Mercy (*eleos*) refers to God's active, compassionate relief extended to those who are in a state of misery, distress, or spiritual helplessness. While grace addresses human guilt and unworthiness by bestowing undeserved favor, mercy addresses human misery and helplessness by withholding deserved judgment and providing loving relief. In 2 John 1:3, John integrates mercy into a triadic apostolic greeting—grace, mercy, and peace—affirming that God's covenant loyalty and compassionate relief flow continuously from both the Father and Jesus Christ to sustain believers walking in truth and love.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • In classical usage, *eleos* described a feeling of pity or sympathy aroused by the sight of someone suffering undeserved hardship.

  • In the Septuagint, *eleos* was predominantly chosen to translate the rich Hebrew concept of *chesed* (covenant faithfulness, steadfast love) as well as *rachamim* (deep emotional compassion, tender mercy).

  • This background infused *eleos* with profound biblical depth: it is not merely a passive feeling of pity, but active, covenantal sympathy expressed through tangible action to relieve the suffering and helpless.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • *Eleos* denotes kindness, clemency, or active compassion shown toward those who are suffering, needy, or in distress.

  • It carries the specific nuance of withholding deserved punishment or judgment from a wrongdoer while providing divine relief.

  • The semantic range spans from tender pity and active relief to covenant loyalty and divine deliverance.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Active Samaritan Neighborliness: Illustrated in Luke 10:37, where the one who showed mercy (*eleos*) to the wounded traveler demonstrated true neighborliness through direct personal action and cost.

  • Covenant Deliverance: Highlighted in the Magnificat (Luke 1:50, 54, 78), where God's mercy reflects His enduring faithfulness to Abraham and his descendants in sending the Messiah.

  • Sovereign Salvation: Expressed in Titus 3:5, where God saves believers not on the basis of human works of righteousness, but according to His own mercy through regeneration and renewal.

  • Apostolic Blessing: Combined with grace and peace in pastoral greetings (1 Timothy 1:2; 2 Timothy 1:2; 2 John 1:3) as an ongoing divine supply for Christian life and ministry.

4. Theological Implications

  • Essential Divine Attribute: Mercy is a fundamental aspect of God's character. He is described as being "rich in mercy" (Ephesians 2:4) and taking delight in extending compassion to fallen humanity.

  • Distinction Between Grace and Mercy: Grace (*charis*) addresses human guilt by granting undeserved favor and spiritual blessings. Mercy (*eleos*) addresses human misery and weakness by withholding deserved punishment and granting compassionate relief.

  • Christological Source: Mercy originates equally from "God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father," demonstrating the full deity of Jesus Christ and the unified divine origin of redemptive grace and comfort.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • Context of Deception and Vulnerability: In 2 John 1:3, John writes to a local assembly facing deceptive itinerant teachers who denied the incarnation.

  • Assured Divine Supply: John states "Grace, mercy, and peace *will be* with you" as a confident indicative statement rather than a wishful prayer, assuring vulnerable believers of God's continuous presence and active protection.

  • Guardrail of Truth and Love: The flow of divine mercy operates within the dual framework of "truth and love," demonstrating that God's tender compassion never compromises doctrinal truth nor abandons loving fellowship.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 9:13 – "But go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy and not sacrifice.' For I did not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance."

  • Ephesians 2:4-5 – "But God, who is rich in mercy, because of His great love with which He loved us, even when we were dead in trespasses, made us alive together with Christ (by grace you have been saved),"

  • Hebrews 4:16 – "Let us therefore come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need."

  • Titus 3:5 – "not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy He saved us, through the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit,"

  • 1 Peter 1:3 – "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who according to His abundant mercy has begotten us again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead,"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The verbal form *eleeo* ("to have mercy") is the characteristic cry of the desperate throughout the Gospels, such as the blind men (Matthew 9:27), the Canaanite woman (Matthew 15:22), and the ten lepers (Luke 17:13). In every instance, it represents an appeal to divine power born of conscious human helplessness.

  • In pastoral application, divine mercy serves as an anchor for suffering believers, reassuring them that their weaknesses and trials are met with God's active, personal care rather than harsh condemnation.
