WALKING - ( peripateo , Strong's G4043 )

Summary:

The Greek verb *peripateo* literally means "to walk around," but in the New Testament, it is overwhelmingly used metaphorically to describe a person's lifestyle, conduct, and daily manner of living. In 2 John 1:4, "walking in truth" depicts believers whose daily habits, decisions, and ethical choices are consistently guided by and aligned with God's revealed truth, bringing deep joy to the apostolic community.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word *peripateo* is a compound verb derived from *peri* (around, about) and *pateo* (to tread, step, or walk). Historically, it referred to literal physical movement—walking from place to place, pacing back and forth, or traveling on foot. In classical Greece, this word became famously associated with the "Peripatetic" school of philosophy founded by Aristotle, whose followers walked around the courtyard of the Lyceum while engaged in teaching and academic debate.

In Jewish thought and the Old Testament, physical walking was the premier metaphor for moral and spiritual conduct. The Hebrew concept of *halakhah* (from *halakh*, "to walk") represented the body of Jewish law guiding daily life. To walk with God, or to walk in His ways, meant living in obedience to His covenant. The New Testament writers adopted *peripateo* to translate this rich Semitic concept, transforming a simple physical action into a vivid picture of the believer's ongoing spiritual journey and daily lifestyle.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Literally, *peripateo* means to walk, to go about, or to conduct oneself physically on foot.

Metaphorically, the word denotes:

  • To conduct one's life, to behave, or to pass one's existence.

  • To be occupied with or walk in a specific path of moral behavior.

  • To order one's behavior according to a particular standard or rule of life.

In the Epistles, this figurative use completely eclipses the literal meaning. It focuses on the habitual pattern of a person's life rather than an isolated action. A person's "walk" is the visible, outward expression of their inward condition and commitments.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The New Testament writers use *peripateo* to present several striking contrasts of life paths:

  • The Sphere of Walk: Believers are exhorted to walk "in the light" rather than "in darkness" (John 8:12; 1 John 1:6–7). The sphere in which one walks determines what is visible, safe, and true.

  • The Guide of the Walk: Paul contrasts walking "according to the flesh" with walking "according to the Spirit" (Romans 8:4). The former is directed by sinful human nature, while the latter is guided and empowered by the Holy Spirit.

  • The Standard of the Walk: Believers are commanded to walk "worthy of the calling" (Ephesians 4:1) and to walk "in love" (Ephesians 5:2), showing that Christian conduct must match the high reality of redemption.

By utilizing *peripateo*, the biblical writers remind readers that the Christian life is not a static state of contemplation but a continuous, dynamic movement. Walking requires putting one foot in front of the other; it is progressive, deliberate, and practical.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, *peripateo* bridges the gap between doctrine and devotion, or orthodoxy and orthopraxy.

  • Faith in Motion: True saving faith is never merely an intellectual assent to dogma. It must express itself in a physical, observable manner of life. If one claims to abide in Christ, he must "walk just as He walked" (1 John 2:6).

  • The Power of Habit: A walk consists of thousands of small steps. In the same way, the Christian life is built upon daily, seemingly minor decisions of obedience. Growth is cumulative, accomplished through the steady, habitual practice of righteousness.

  • A Communal Journey: Walking often implies companionship. To walk in truth is to walk in fellowship with God and with other believers who are traveling the same narrow path.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 2 John 1:4, the apostle John writes, "I rejoiced greatly that I have found some of your children walking in truth..." This letter was addressed to "the elect lady and her children" (likely a local church and its members) at a time when dangerous, deceptive teachers were active, denying the true humanity of Jesus.

In this context, "walking in truth" carries a specific weight:

  • Active Orthodoxy: It was not enough that these believers *held* to the truth in theory; they were *walking* in it. Their daily lives, relationships, and endurance in the face of false teachers proved that the truth of the gospel was their ruling principle.

  • Obedience to Love: John immediately connects this walk with the commandment "that we love one another" (2 John 1:5). Walking in truth is inseparable from walking in love.

  • Pastoral Joy: For the aging Apostle, there was no greater satisfaction than seeing the next generation of believers maintain a steadfast, active, and visible walk in the truth of God, resisting the cultural and theological drifts of their age.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 8:12: "Then Jesus spoke to them again, saying, 'I am the light of the world. He who follows Me shall not walk in darkness, but have the light of life.'"

  • Ephesians 4:1: "I, therefore, the prisoner of the Lord, beseech you to walk worthy of the calling with which you were called,"

  • Ephesians 5:2: "And walk in love, as Christ also has loved us and given Himself for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma."

  • 3 John 1:4: "I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A helpful distinction can be made between "walking" and "running." While the Christian race is sometimes described as a run requiring high endurance (Hebrews 12:1), "walking" represents the sustainable, day-by-day pace of ordinary life. It reminds the believer that God is glorified not only in moments of intense crisis or spiritual ecstasy but in the ordinary, step-by-step faithfulness of daily living. Walking is a pace accessible to all, requiring consistency rather than sudden bursts of natural strength.
