WROTE - ( graphō , Strong's G1125 )

Summary:

The Greek verb *graphō* primarily means to carve, engrave, or write down characters on a surface to record a lasting message. In the New Testament, it frequently refers to recorded Scripture, apostolic epistles, or divine commandments. In 2 John 1:5, John uses *graphō* in the aorist tense to emphasize that his directive to love one another is not a new or novel invention he is penning on the spot. Instead, it is the ongoing, unalterable truth delivered to believers from the very beginning of the Gospel message.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • Historically, *graphō* described the physical act of scratching, engraving, or carving marks into hard surfaces such as wood, stone, metal, or wax tablets.

  • Over time, the term broadened from physical incising to standard writing with ink upon papyrus, parchment, or vellum.

  • In classical and biblical usage, written communication carried a sense of permanence, legal weight, and official authority, distinguishing fixed written decrees from transient spoken words.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • To form letters or written characters upon a material surface.

  • To record a message, letter, document, or book.

  • To formulate legal statutes, written covenants, or commandments.

  • In biblical theology, the word conveys authoritative, enduring revelation preserved for posterity.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Literal writing: Recording names, letters, public inscriptions, or personal correspondence (Luke 1:63; John 19:19; 1 Corinthians 5:9).

  • Figurative writing: Describing God's law inscribed directly upon human hearts and minds rather than external tablets (2 Corinthians 3:3; Hebrews 8:10).

  • Canonically authoritative writing: Expressing divine revelation recorded under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit to serve as an unalterable standard of truth.

4. Theological Implications

  • Scriptural Authority: God chose to preserve His truth in written form so that doctrine would remain fixed, objective, and verifiable across generations.

  • Permanence of Divine Law: Written revelation guards against the corruption of human tradition and subjectivism. The call to love is anchored in previously recorded revelation.

  • Apostolic Mandate: Apostolic epistles carry binding, holy authority for local assemblies, functioning as God's written word to the church.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In 2 John 1:5, the elder addresses the "elect lady," clarifying that he is "not as though I wrote a new commandment to you."

  • The aorist verb *egrapsa* ("I wrote" / "I am writing") addresses the misconception that John is introducing a novel theological requirement or human invention.

  • John explicitly connects his written message to "that which we have had from the beginning: that we love one another."

  • By framing his writing this way, John protects the church against deceptive teachers who offer "advanced" or "new" doctrines, grounding Christian conduct in the timeless, original message of Jesus Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 19:22 — "Pilate answered, 'What I have written, I have written.'"

  • Romans 15:15 — "Nevertheless, brethren, I have written more boldly to you on some points, as reminding you, because of the grace given to me by God,"

  • Galatians 6:11 — "See with what large letters I have written to you with my own hand!"

  • 1 John 2:14 — "I have written to you, fathers, because you have known Him who is from the beginning. I have written to you, young men, because you are strong, and the word of God abides in you, and you have overcome the wicked one."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The noun form *graphē* (Strong's G1124) is the exclusive New Testament term for "Scripture."

  • The perfect passive form *gegraptai* ("it stands written") was utilized by Jesus during His temptation in the wilderness (Matthew 4:4, 7, 10) as the supreme defensive weapon against demonic falsehood, demonstrating the absolute authority of the written Word.
