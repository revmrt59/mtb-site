COMMANDMENT - ( entolē , Strong's G1785 )

Summary:

The Greek noun *entolē* refers to an authoritative order, charge, or precept. In the New Testament, particularly in John's writings, it transcends legalistic duty to represent the very heart of the Christian walk: the divine summons to love one another. In 2 John 1:5, this "commandment" is not a novel innovation but the foundational instruction believers have possessed from the very inception of their faith, binding truth and love together in Christian community.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun *entolē* is derived from the verb *entellomai*, which means to command, charge, or commission. This verb is a compound of the preposition *en* ("in" or "upon") and *telos* ("an end," "consummation," or "goal"). At its historical root, the word carries the picture of laying a charge upon someone to direct them toward a specific, completed goal. It is not an arbitrary rule designed to restrict freedom, but a purposeful, goal-oriented instruction given by an authority to guide subordinates toward a desired destination.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

*Entolē* designates an official injunction, mandate, or moral precept. In classical Greek, it was used for the official instructions given to ambassadors or commissioned officers. In the Septuagint and the New Testament, it represents the specific commandments of God. While the Greek word *nomos* refers to the law as an entire system, *entolē* focuses on the individual, concrete demands within that law. It signifies an obligation that is binding, authoritative, and non-negotiable.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the New Testament, *entolē* occurs 67 times, frequently in the Gospels and the Epistles of John. John uses the word to paint a picture of family expectations. Just as a father gives instructions to his children for their safety and flourishing, God gives *entolai* to His children. This word picture removes legalistic coldness from the term. Keeping the commandments is presented as the natural, affectionate response of a child to a loving Father.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, *entolē* bridges the gap between doctrine and ethics. It shows that Christian faith is never merely intellectual; it demands active obedience. Furthermore, John deeply connects *entolē* with love (*agapē*). To love God is to keep His commandments, and His primary commandment is that we love one another. This prevents Christian love from degenerating into mere sentimentality, anchoring it instead in objective, obedient actions of the will.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 2 John 1:5, the Apostle John pleads with the "elect lady" (likely a local church or an esteemed sister in Christ) to walk in love. He carefully qualifies this "commandment" as "not... a new commandment... but that which we have had from the beginning." In the ancient world, "newness" was often viewed with suspicion, associated with dangerous innovations or heretical departures from truth. By anchoring the commandment of love in "the beginning" (the start of their Christian experience and Christ's earthly ministry), John establishes its absolute authority, stability, and timelessness. It is both old in its history and perpetually fresh in its daily application.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 22:38 - "This is the first and great commandment."

  • John 13:34 - "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another; as I have loved you, that you also love one another."

  • 1 John 2:7 - "Brethren, I write no new commandment to you, but an old commandment which you have had from the beginning. The old commandment is the word which you heard from the beginning."

  • 1 John 5:3 - "For this is the love of God, that we keep His commandments. And His commandments are not burdensome."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

It is vital to distinguish *entolē* from *dogma*. While *dogma* refers to decrees, rules, or ordinances (often ceremonial or human-made), *entolē* refers to ethical and moral instructions flowing directly from God's character. Believers are freed from the "dogmas" of the old law (Colossians 2:14), but they are called to fulfill the *entolē* of love, which is the fulfillment of the law itself.
