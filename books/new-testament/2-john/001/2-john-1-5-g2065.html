PLEAD - ( erōtaō , Strong's G2065 )

Summary:

The Greek verb *erōtaō* carries the core meaning of making an earnest request, asking, entreating, or pleading on terms of relational closeness. In classical Greek, the word primarily signified asking a question to gain information. In New Testament usage, however, its meaning broadened significantly to express a warm, personal entreaty or request between equals or close acquaintances. In 2 John 1:5, John uses *erōtaō* to frame his call to love not as a cold legalistic directive, but as a tender, deeply personal pastoral appeal. Rather than commanding from a posture of harsh authority, the elderly apostle pleads with the elect lady and her house church to walk faithfully in the foundational truth they received from the beginning.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • In ancient Greek literature, *erōtaō* originally described the act of questioning or asking for information.

  • As Koine Greek developed, the term absorbed the nuances of Hebrew requests, evolving from mere inquiry into earnest petitioning and personal pleading.

  • Unlike words that imply a distant petitioner groveling before a monarch, *erōtaō* conveys a sense of direct access and personal connection. It depicts one standing in a position of spiritual intimacy or mutual respect, making a heart felt request.

  • In apostolic ministry, this word paints a vivid picture of pastoral care: an elder brother in Christ coming alongside a beloved congregation to entreat them toward righteousness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The primary meanings of *erōtaō* include:

  • To ask or inquire (to seek information).

  • To request, beseech, entreat, or plead (to ask for an action to be taken).

  • To pray or make petition (used frequently of Jesus asking the Father).

  • The semantic range spans from simple conversation to deep intercession and warm exhortation.

  • In context of spiritual leadership, it denotes an earnest, respectful petition that seeks to persuade the heart rather than compel outward compliance by force.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Questioning: Used when individuals ask Jesus or others direct questions about doctrine, identity, or events.

  • Personal Requests: Used when people approach Jesus to request healing, assistance, or hospitality.

  • Divine Intercession: Uniquely employed to describe Jesus praying to God the Father, highlighting the intimate, equal communion within the Trinity.

  • Pastoral Entreaty: Employed by apostles to urge believers toward holy living, love, and church order.

4. Theological Implications

  • Reflects the posture of gospel leadership: true spiritual authority seeks to persuade through love rather than coerce through legalism.

  • Reveals the nature of Christian fellowship: believers are bound together by love and mutual respect, where appeals to truth resonate through shared grace.

  • Demonstrates the nature of Christ's prayer life: Jesus does not approach the Father as an outsider, but as the Son who intercedes with familial intimacy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In 2 John 1:5, John writes to "the elect lady and her children" regarding the central duty of Christian life: mutual love.

  • The translation "plead" captures the tender urgency of the venerable apostle. John possesses apostolic authority to command, yet he chooses to entreat.

  • By emphasizing that this is "not as though I wrote a new commandment to you, but that which we have had from the beginning," John uses *erōtaō* to gently stir their memory and commitment.

  • The choice of word demonstrates that genuine Christian love cannot be forced; it must be voluntarily embraced as a response to pastoral entreaty and God's established truth.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 14:16 – "And I will pray the Father, and He will give you another Helper, that He may abide with you forever—"

  • John 17:9 – "I pray for them. I do not pray for the world but for those whom You have given Me, for they are Yours."

  • 1 Thessalonians 4:1 – "Finally then, brethren, we urge and exhort in the Lord Jesus that you should abound more and more, just as you received from us how you ought to walk and to please God;"

  • 1 Thessalonians 5:12 – "And we urge you, brethren, to recognize those who labor among you, and are over you in the Lord and admonish you,"

  • 2 John 1:5 – "And now I plead with you, lady, not as though I wrote a new commandment to you, but that which we have had from the beginning: that we love one another."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Distinguishing *erōtaō* from *aiteō*:

  • *Aiteō* generally signifies a request made by an inferior to a superior (such as a subject petitioning a king).

  • *Erōtaō* signifies a request made on more equal, familiar, or relational grounds.

  • Remarkably, Jesus never uses *aiteō* when praying to the Father; He consistently uses *erōtaō*, underscoring His divine equality and intimacy with God.

  • For sermon preparation, this distinction illustrates how pastors ought to address the flock: not as overlords, but as loving shepherds who plead on the basis of shared brotherhood in Christ.
