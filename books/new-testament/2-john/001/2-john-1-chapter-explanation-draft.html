Chapter Study

2 John 1

Verse-by-Verse
2 John 1:1
NKJV

2 John 1:1 The Elder, To the elect lady and her children, whom I love in truth, and not only I, but also all those who have known the truth,(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:1 This letter is from John, the elder. I am writing to the chosen lady and to her children, whom I love in the truth — as does everyone else who knows the truth — (NLT)

Main Idea

John, calling himself "the Elder," writes to "the elect lady and her children," most likely a local church and its members, though some understand this as an individual Christian woman and her family. He assures them of his love, shared by all who know the truth, a love rooted in the truth found in Jesus Christ.

Verse Explanation

John, calling himself "the Elder," writes to "the elect lady and her children," most likely a local church and its members, though some understand this as an individual Christian woman and her family. He assures them of his love, shared by all who know the truth, a love rooted in the truth found in Jesus Christ.

"The Elder,"

The Greek word for "elder" means an older man, and in the early church it also became the title for a church leader. The writer does not give his name, but he did not need to. His readers knew exactly who "the Elder" was, and the title alone carried authority. Early church tradition identifies him as the apostle John, and the letter's language closely matches John's Gospel and his first letter. Third John opens the same way. John was probably an old man by this time, the last of the apostles who had walked with Jesus. Peter also called himself "a fellow elder and a witness of the sufferings of Christ" (1 Peter 5:1). John writes as a senior leader caring for believers he knows.

"To the elect lady and her children,"

"Elect" means chosen by God. The New Testament uses it for believers, whom God has called to belong to Him. Peter writes to those who are "elect according to the foreknowledge of God the Father" (1 Peter 1:2). The Greek word for "lady" is the feminine form of the word for "lord," and it was a respectful way to address a woman.

The elect lady is most likely a local church, pictured as a woman, and "her children" are its members. Scripture often describes God's people as a woman, and the church is called the bride of Christ (Ephesians 5:25–27; Revelation 21:2). At the close of the letter, John sends greetings from "the children of your elect sister" (verse 13), which fits a sister congregation sending its greetings. Some understand the lady to be an individual Christian woman and her family, and that reading is possible. Either way, John is writing to people God has chosen and who belong to His family.

"whom I love in truth,"

"In truth" means that John's love is genuine, not polite words at the start of a letter. It also means his love is grounded in the truth revealed in and about Jesus Christ. John does not love them simply because he likes them or because they treat him well. He loves them because they share the truth of Christ with him. That truth is the bond between them.

"and not only I, but also all those who have known the truth,"

John's love is not his alone. Everyone who has come to know the truth shares it. "Known" points to people who came to know the truth and continue to know it. This is more than learning facts about Jesus. It is knowing the One who said, "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). This congregation was not standing alone. Believers far beyond their city loved them, because the same truth had brought them all into one family.

Study Notes

The opening names neither the sender nor the recipient, and both have raised questions. "The Elder" has traditionally been identified as the apostle John. The church historian Eusebius, writing in the fourth century, quoted the early bishop Papias, who listed the apostles, John among them, and then separately mentioned "the elder John." Eusebius concluded that there were two Johns and suggested the second might have written some of the Johnian writings. Some scholars follow him and assign 2 and 3 John to this elder. Others read Papias as speaking of the same John twice, once among the apostles and once as still living. The traditional identification remains well supported, but the question is debated.

The Greek phrase for "elect lady" is eklektē kyria. Some have taken one of these words as a personal name, either "the lady Electa" or "the elect Kyria." Kyria is attested as a woman's name. "Electa" faces a difficulty, because verse 13 calls the sister "elect" as well, so both sisters would bear the same name. The letter's grammar also supports the congregational reading. John addresses the lady with a singular "you" in verses 4–5 and 13, but in verses 6, 8, 10, and 12 he shifts to a plural "you," as if speaking to a group of people under one name.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] The Elder Who No Longer Needed His Name. “The elder to the chosen lady and her children.” The word for elder (presbyteros) means “an older man,” and by the time this letter was written John was likely the last living apostle, an old man at Ephesus. This is the John whom Jesus nicknamed a “son of thunder” (Mark 3:17), who wanted to call fire down on a Samaritan village (Luke 9:54) and asked for the seat at Jesus’ right hand (Mark 10:37). Now he does not even sign his name, just as in his Gospel he never names himself but is “the disciple whom Jesus loved.” He writes with apostolic authority, but he carries it as pastoral, fatherly care. True spiritual authority in the later years of ministry rests less on titles and more on character, faithfulness over time, and the spiritual weight of having walked with God. Decades with Jesus had not made John larger in his own eyes; they had made him gentler and smaller. Authority that has been with Jesus long enough no longer needs a title to be heard. Have our years with Jesus made us gentler and smaller in our own eyes, or more eager for recognition?

[Christ & Life] The Lord’s Lady. “To the chosen lady.” “Lady” (kyria) is the feminine form of kyrios, “Lord,” and occurs in the New Testament only here and in verse 5. Many interpreters have read her as a local church personified as a mother whose “children” are its members, with her “chosen sister” (v. 13) a sister congregation. If so, John has given the congregation the feminine of her Master’s title: she is the Lord’s lady, chosen before she ever chose Him. Whether she was a particular woman hosting a house church or a congregation, the spiritual reality is the same. The church is a chosen bride, dignified, royal, and entrusted with raising spiritual children. She is both singular, one “lady,” and corporate, a household of “children,” just as each believer is individually chosen and yet all together form one bride. Scripture’s story moves toward a chosen woman made ready for her Lord (Ephesians 5:25–27; Revelation 19:7; 21:9). When we encounter Jesus deeply, He awakens in us a deep affection for His church; we cannot love the Bridegroom without loving His bride. A church is not an organization Christ manages; she is a bride He chose and loves. Do we speak of the church as something we attend, or as a bride Christ chose?

[Foundational Truth] The Perimeter of Christian Love. “Whom I love in the truth.” John immediately defines the boundaries of his affection. Christian love is not a vague, borderless sentimentality; biblical love is never blind. It is rooted, bounded, and energized by eternal truth. “In truth” can also mean “truly, really”: John’s love is genuine because it stands on what is real. We cannot genuinely love fellow believers outside the context of the truth we share.

[Discovery] Loving From the Indwelling Life. In John’s writings “the truth” is not finally a set of doctrines but a Person, Jesus Christ (John 14:6). So to love “in the truth” is to love from the shared, indwelling life of Christ Himself. That moves our fellowship out of mere human sympathy and into eternal, divine reality. Believers do not merely like one another; the life of Christ in one recognizes and loves the life of Christ in the other.

[Seed] The Invisible Network of the Truth. “And not I only, but also all who know the truth.” The phrase means those who came to know the truth and still know it. Truth here is not a sterile set of facts but a living ecosystem that produces love among all who live in it. It creates an immediate, unbreakable, invisible network of fellowship among strangers. If you know the truth, you are bound in affection to everyone else who knows it, though you have never met.

[Insight] A Lady Without a Name. Interpreters have read “the chosen lady” as a woman named Kyria, an unnamed Christian mother and her household, or a local church. The plural “you” in verses 6, 8, 10, and 12 favors a church. John may have withheld names because of persecution. Whatever her identity, the letter treats her the way God treats His people: by her calling, not her fame.

2 John 1:2
NKJV

2 John 1:2 because of the truth which abides in us and will be with us forever:(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:2 because the truth lives in us and will be with us forever. (NLT)

Main Idea

This love among believers exists because the truth lives in them and will stay with them forever. Since truth is bound up with Jesus Himself, those who belong to Him share a lasting bond that cannot be broken.

Verse Explanation

This love among believers exists because the truth lives in them and will stay with them forever. Since truth is bound up with Jesus Himself, those who belong to Him share a lasting bond that cannot be broken.

"because of the truth"

In the previous verse John said that he loves this church and its members "in truth," and that "all those who have known the truth" love them as well (1:1). Here he gives the reason for that shared love: the truth itself. In John's writings, "truth" means more than accurate information. It is the reality God has made known in Jesus Christ. John wrote that the Word who became flesh was "full of grace and truth" (John 1:14), and Jesus said of Himself, "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6). So the truth John speaks of is the message about Jesus that the apostles proclaimed, and it cannot be separated from Jesus Himself. To know the truth is to know Him.

That is why believers who had never met could love one another. Their bond did not rest on shared background, personality, or local friendship. It rested on the same Lord and the same message about Him.

"which abides in us"

The Greek word translated "abides" means to remain, stay, or make one's home. It is one of John's favorite words. Jesus used it when He said, "Abide in Me, and I in you" (John 15:4). The truth is not something believers merely agree with from a distance. It has come to live in them and goes on living in them. Jesus promised the disciples "another Helper, that He may abide with you forever—the Spirit of truth" (John 14:16–17). Many interpreters see John's words here as an echo of that promise, since it is through the Spirit that the truth about Christ remains in the hearts of His people.

John says "us," including himself with the believers he writes to. The same truth lives in the apostle and in the members of a church he may never have visited, and that is what makes them one family.

"and will be with us forever:"

The truth that lives in believers is not temporary. Opinions change and teachers come and go, but God's truth lasts: "The grass withers, the flower fades, but the word of our God stands forever" (Isaiah 40:8). John made a similar contrast in his first letter: "the world is passing away, and the lust of it; but he who does the will of God abides forever" (1 John 2:17).

Because the truth will be with believers forever, the love built on it lasts forever too. Friendships based on common interests or convenience can fade, but love that grows out of shared life in Christ rests on something that will never pass away.

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Christ & Life] The Truth Moved In to Stay. “For the sake of the truth which abides in us and will be with us forever.” The verb menō means to remain, to stay, to make one’s home; the truth is not a visitor in the believer but a resident. As one old preacher said, we entertain it not as a guest but as the master of the house. John’s wording echoes Jesus’ promise in the upper room about the Spirit of truth: “that He may abide with you forever … He dwells with you and will be in you” (John 14:16–17). So the truth in this verse speaks with the voice of a Person. It has agency: it lives, it abides, it lasts forever. It is Christ, who is the truth (John 14:6), the living, risen Christ making His home in our hearts by the Spirit of truth, the uncreated life of God at work in a human vessel. We do not merely hold the truth; the Truth holds us. Two prepositions carry the promise: the truth abides in us and will be with us, life within and company alongside. What we hold as doctrine is the presence of Someone who has promised never to leave. Is the truth a guest in our lives, welcome when convenient, or the master of the house?

[Foundational Truth] The Permanence of the Indwelling. “And will be with us forever,” literally, “into the age,” with “with us” in the emphatic position. In a world of shifting philosophies and fading empires, the truth God has planted in His people is no passing philosophy but a permanent fixture. What God establishes within the human spirit outlasts time itself, and that permanence is the security of the saint.

2 John 1:3
NKJV

2 John 1:3 Grace, mercy, and peace will be with you from God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father, in truth and love.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:3 Grace, mercy, and peace, which come from God the Father and from Jesus Christ — the Son of the Father — will continue to be with us who live in truth and love. (NLT)

Main Idea

John assures his readers that grace, mercy, and peace will be with them from God the Father and from Jesus Christ, whom he carefully identifies as the Son of the Father. These blessings come "in truth and love," the two themes that run through the whole letter.

Verse Explanation

John assures his readers that grace, mercy, and peace will be with them from God the Father and from Jesus Christ, whom he carefully identifies as the Son of the Father. These blessings come "in truth and love," the two themes that run through the whole letter.

"Grace, mercy, and peace"

Grace is God's kindness given freely to people who have not earned it. Mercy is His compassion toward those who are guilty and helpless, holding back the judgment they deserve and meeting them in their need. Peace is the Hebrew idea of shalom: not only the end of hostility with God but the wholeness and well-being that come from being right with Him. Grace is the source, mercy is that grace reaching people in their need, and peace is what it produces in their lives. Paul uses the same three words in greeting Timothy (1 Timothy 1:2; 2 Timothy 1:2), and here they come from John, the Elder, to the church he writes to and its members.

"will be with you"

Most letters in the New Testament open with a wish or prayer that grace and peace would be given. John speaks with confidence instead: these blessings will be with them. He is not hoping that God might be kind to these believers. He is assuring them that God's grace, mercy, and peace will stay with those who belong to the truth. That assurance matters in a letter that goes on to warn about deceivers. The church's security rests on what God will surely give.

"from God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ,"

Only God can give grace and mercy, and John names two sources: the Father and Jesus Christ. Repeating the word "from" places Jesus alongside the Father as the giver of these blessings, not merely as the channel through which they pass. John could say this only because Jesus is Himself God, the Word who "was with God, and... was God" (John 1:1).

"the Son of the Father,"

This exact phrase appears only here in the New Testament. John could have stopped with the name "Jesus Christ," but he adds this description to stress the relationship between Jesus and the Father. Jesus is not a lesser messenger sent from God. He is the Father's own Son, and to know Him is to know the Father. John's careful wording fits the danger he will soon address: deceivers who deny the truth about Jesus Christ, when confessing the Son can never be separated from having the Father.

"in truth and love."

These blessings are received and enjoyed within the truth revealed in and about Jesus Christ, and they show themselves in love. Grace, mercy, and peace are not found apart from the truth about Christ, and people who receive them do not keep them to themselves but love one another. John holds the two together throughout this short letter. Love that abandons the truth is not the love God gives, and truth held without love does not reflect the God who gave His grace and mercy so freely.

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] A Promise, Not a Wish. “Grace, mercy and peace will be with us.” Paul’s greetings usually have no verb (“Grace to you and peace”), and Peter and Jude pray that grace be “multiplied”; John uses a plain future: “will be.” The greeting is a statement of assurance, not a prayer. It repeats the words of verse 2: the truth “will be with us,” and grace, mercy and peace “will be with us.” John is not hoping God will be kind to this house; he is announcing that He will. The God who guarantees that His truth will stay also guarantees that His grace stays with it.

[Insight] Grace Bends Down to Become Mercy. Grace, mercy, and peace stand together only here and in Paul’s letters to Timothy, and this is the only appearance of “mercy” anywhere in John’s writings. The order is deliberate. Grace is God’s free, unmerited love in His own heart, the fountain; it has no cause outside God, who loves because He is God. Mercy is that love stooping to human misery, stepping into our weakness, failure, and the consequences of sin, as grace answers our guilt. Peace, shalom, is what results in us: wholeness and restoration once grace and mercy have done their work. The order runs from the first motion of God to the final satisfaction of man. We cannot know His peace without first receiving His mercy, and His mercy is born entirely of His grace. The old apostle, writing to a church under threat, does not begin with the danger but with the downward flow of God’s goodness. Everything God asks of this church in the verses that follow rests on what He has already given.

[Christ & Life] The Son of the Father. “From God the Father and from Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father.” The title “the Son of the Father” occurs nowhere else in Scripture. John repeats “from” before both names, so that grace flows from the Son as truly as from the Father, in full equality. In a letter written against teachers who split the man Jesus from the divine Christ, the greeting itself confesses that Jesus Christ is the Father’s own Son, striking at the error before John names it. The title also speaks of intimacy: He is the Son who belongs to the Father, and the warm relationship within the Godhead is what believers are invited into. Salvation is not a transaction with a distant deity but an invitation into the love between a Father and His Son, and grace comes to us from both.

[Seed] The Twin Banks of the River of Blessing. Grace, mercy, and peace will be with us “in truth and love,” a pair that appears together nowhere else and sums up the letter. Truth and love act as the two banks of a river. Without both banks firmly in place, the waters of grace, mercy, and peace spread into a muddy swamp of compromise or dry into a rigid desert of legalism. To abandon either is to dam up the flow of God’s blessing. Lasting peace is impossible outside the boundaries of truth and love. Breathe the twin atmosphere of truth and love daily, for only there can we carry the grace and mercy of the Son to a broken generation.

[Christ & Life] Grace Is a Person, Not a Package. Grace and peace are not abstract commodities we “get” from God. They are realized only as we possess the Person of Christ as our very life, and as we experience the Father’s deep affection for us. The blessing comes “from” Father and Son because it comes in Them.

2 John 1:4
NKJV

2 John 1:4 I rejoiced greatly that I have found some of your children walking in truth, as we received commandment from the Father.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:4 How happy I was to meet some of your children and find them living according to the truth, just as the Father commanded. (NLT)

Main Idea

John expresses great joy at finding some of the lady's children living faithfully according to the truth, just as the Father commanded. A believer's steady, truthful way of life brings deep gladness to those who care for their spiritual well-being.

Verse Explanation

John expresses great joy at finding some of the lady's children living faithfully according to the truth, just as the Father commanded. A believer's steady, truthful way of life brings deep gladness to those who care for their spiritual well-being.

"I rejoiced greatly"

John, the Elder, begins the body of his letter with joy. He uses the same words in 3 John: "For I rejoiced greatly when brethren came and testified of the truth that is in you, just as you walk in the truth" (3 John 3). He adds there, "I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth" (3 John 4). For John, nothing brought more gladness than seeing believers stay faithful. His joy also shows how much he loved this church. He was not writing as a distant critic but as an aged leader who cared deeply about how its members were doing.

"that I have found some of your children"

The "children" are the members of the church John writes to. John does not say how he "found" them. He may have met some of them as they traveled, or heard reports about them, but the letter does not explain. "Some" may mean only the members he had come to know, not that the rest had gone astray. Even so, the warnings that follow show that the church was not out of danger. Deceivers were active (verse 7), so news that some members were standing firm was especially welcome.

"walking in truth,"

In Scripture, "walking" describes a person's whole way of life, how they conduct themselves day by day. Genesis says that "Enoch walked with God" (Genesis 5:24), and the psalmist prayed, "Teach me Your way, O LORD; I will walk in Your truth" (Psalm 86:11). John often uses the word this way: "If we say that we have fellowship with Him, and walk in darkness, we lie and do not practice the truth" (1 John 1:6). He also wrote that whoever claims to abide in Christ "ought himself also to walk just as He walked" (1 John 2:6). The Greek form here describes something ongoing. These believers were steadily living this way, not just doing it now and then.

To walk "in truth" means to live in line with the truth revealed in and about Jesus Christ. It is more than holding correct beliefs. It is that truth shaping how a person lives. John's joy came from seeing what these believers confessed show up in how they lived.

"as we received commandment from the Father."

Walking in truth is not a way of life believers invent for themselves. It is obedience to what the Father has commanded. "We" places John alongside his readers. He and they stand under the same command from the same Father. John states that command plainly elsewhere: "And this is His commandment: that we should believe on the name of His Son Jesus Christ and love one another, as He gave us commandment" (1 John 3:23). Believing the truth about the Son and loving one another belong together in one command, which John goes on to explain in the next two verses (verses 5–6).

None.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] What Makes an Old Apostle Very Glad. “I was very glad to find some of your children walking in truth.” The word “very” appears in John’s writings only here and in 3 John 3, both times about believers walking in the truth. “I have found” is the same verb Andrew used when he ran to Simon with “We have found the Messiah” (John 1:41). An apostle who had seen the risen Christ, outlived his friends, and watched churches divide finds his greatest gladness in meeting the children of this house living what they believe (3 John 4). For the pastor, the discipler, or the parent, there is no greater joy than seeing the next generation not only affirming doctrine but ordering their daily lives by it, the seed of the gospel turning into holy living. The joy of finding Christ is completed in the joy of finding others walking with Him. Pastors and parents still know no greater gladness. Whose walk with God brings us joy, and have we told them?

[Application] Rejoice Over the Some. “Some of your children” means “some from among.” Perhaps John was too courteous to mention those who were not walking in truth; good parents do not always have good children, as Cain, Ishmael, and Esau remind us; or perhaps he simply reports the ones he met. Either way, he does not pretend everyone is perfectly aligned with God, and he does not let the “some” he has not seen silence his joy over the “some” he has. Many godly parents and pastors carry an ache for those who have wandered and lose the gladness God offers in those who are walking. Draw deep joy from the faithful ones and the real growth you can see. Thank God for the walkers by name, and keep praying for the rest from a heart that is still glad. Has grief over those who are not walking stolen our gladness over those who are?

[Foundational Truth] Truth Is a Road, Not a Chair. “Walking in truth.” “Walk” describes a settled way of life, the Jewish idiom for daily conduct and habitual behavior (1 John 1:6–7; 2:6). Truth is not a chair to sit in; it is a path to walk on. It requires momentum, balance, and direction. A believer who has right doctrine but no righteous motion is not walking in truth; walking means taking the doctrine out of the notebook and putting it into our shoes. It is much easier to study the truth, or even argue about it, than to practice it. And walking is not taking a step or two; it is a whole way of going. A faith that does not command our feet has not truly conquered our hearts. Where has God’s truth actually begun to govern how we live, and where do we only agree with it from a safe distance?

[Christ & Life] The Normal Christian Walk. Spiritual reality is proven in the ordinary steps of obedience. This “normal Christian walk” is simply the outward, natural expression of the inward, living Christ having His way in us. Walking in truth is not our performance for Him but His life moving through us.

[Insight] Walking by a Father’s Word. “Just as we have received commandment from the Father.” Walking in truth is not merely a philosophy; it is obedience to a Father’s word, a command received “from alongside the Father.” The road has an Author, and He is our Father.

2 John 1:5
NKJV

2 John 1:5 And now I plead with you, lady, not as though I wrote a new commandment to you, but that which we have had from the beginning: that we love one another.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:5 I am writing to remind you, dear friends, that we should love one another. This is not a new commandment, but one we have had from the beginning. (NLT)

Main Idea

The command to love one another is not a new idea but the teaching believers have had from the beginning of their faith. John urges his readers to keep living out this familiar command rather than treat it as something they have already mastered.

Verse Explanation

The command to love one another is not a new idea but the teaching believers have had from the beginning of their faith. John urges his readers to keep living out this familiar command rather than treat it as something they have already mastered.

"And now I plead with you, lady,"

John, the Elder, turns from his joy over the faithful children to a personal request. The Greek word translated "plead" usually means to ask or request. John is one of the apostles and could simply give orders. Instead he speaks as someone who loves this church and wants its members to respond willingly. The "lady" is the church he is writing to, and his appeal is meant for all its members together.

"not as though I wrote a new commandment to you,"

John is not adding anything to what they already believe. He made the same point in his first letter: "Brethren, I write no new commandment to you, but an old commandment which you have had from the beginning" (1 John 2:7). The command was new when Jesus first gave it on the night before His death: "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another; as I have loved you, that you also love one another" (John 13:34). It was new because Jesus' own love set the pattern. By the time John wrote, that command had shaped Christian life for decades. He calls it old because it has always been part of the faith, not because it no longer matters.

"but that which we have had from the beginning:"

"From the beginning" refers to the time when these believers first received the gospel. Love for one another was not something added later to the message. It was part of the message from the start: "For this is the message that you heard from the beginning, that we should love one another" (1 John 3:11). John says "we," not "you," so he places himself under the same command. The aging apostle needed to obey it as much as the newest believer did.

This matters because others were coming to the churches with a message that departed from what believers had first received (verse 7). John does not ask his readers to move on to something fresh. He asks them to hold on to what they already have.

"that we love one another."

This is love within the family of believers, love that Christians show to each other. Jesus made it the mark of His followers: "By this all will know that you are My disciples, if you have love for one another" (John 13:35). John explains what this love looks like: "By this we know love, because He laid down His life for us. And we also ought to lay down our lives for the brethren" (1 John 3:16). It is not just warm feeling or polite words. "My little children, let us not love in word or in tongue, but in deed and in truth" (1 John 3:18). It shows itself in practical care, especially when a brother or sister is in need (1 John 3:17).

John has just rejoiced that some of the church's members are walking in truth (verse 4), and he now places love beside that faithfulness. The two belong together. A church that holds firmly to the truth about Jesus must also love one another, because loving one another was part of that truth from the start. The command was familiar, but a familiar command can still be neglected. John asks the church to keep practicing it, not to treat it as something it has already outgrown.

Spirit & Life

[Discovery] The Same Sermon an Old Man Kept Preaching. “Not as though I were writing to you a new commandment, but the one which we have had from the beginning, that we love one another.” An ancient tradition records that when John at Ephesus was so old that his disciples could scarcely carry him into the assembly, he would say at each meeting only, “Little children, love one another.” When they grew tired of hearing it and asked why he always said this, he answered, “It is the commandment of the Lord, and if this only be done, it is enough.” In this verse we hear the same old man saying the same thing in ink. The church rarely needs a new commandment as much as it needs to keep the one it was given, and the voice that keeps repeating it may be the most loving voice in the room.

[Application] An Apostle Who Asks. “Now I ask you, lady.” John had the right to command, yet he uses the verb “ask, request,” the very verb Jesus used for His own requests to the Father in the upper room: “I ask on their behalf” (John 17:9, 15, 20). He pleads rather than demands; true love cannot be legislated with a heavy hand; it must be requested and entered into willingly. Love will succeed where authority will not, and often the more authority is pressed, the more it is ignored. John also places himself under the command he gives: not “that you love one another,” but “that we love one another.” Leaders who ask, and who stand under the same word they preach, reach hearts that orders never touch. Is there someone we are trying to change by authority whom God is asking us to approach with a humble request?

[Insight] The Ancient Newness of the Command. John says this is not a new command, yet in 1 John 2:8 he calls it a new one. The command to love is as old as the nature of God Himself, rooted already in the Law (Leviticus 19:18), and it was “from the beginning” of their Christian lives. Yet it is new in kind, because it was perfectly lived out in the incarnation of Jesus (John 13:34) and must be freshly kindled by the Spirit in every generation. Spiritual maturity does not require novel doctrines; the profoundest realities of the faith are ancient. The enemy uses the craving for newness to distract believers from the hard, daily practice of the oldest command: love the brethren.

[Application] Love Is an Act of the Will. Love here is a commandment. That means agape love is fundamentally an act of the will, an obedience of the hands and feet, whatever the shifting temperature of our emotions. It has been called “undefeatable goodwill.” Love is the badge of the true Christian and the test of genuine faith. And it is hard to fake: belief may be feigned and confession may be only of the lips, but love is much harder to counterfeit.

[Christ & Life] Love Flows Like Sap in the Vine. We cannot manufacture this love. It must flow out of the divine life within, like sap in the vine (John 15:5). We can only give what we have received, so we position ourselves to encounter the Father’s love, and then pour it out on one another. “Love, and do what you will,” said Augustine, knowing that true divine love will fulfill the righteous requirements of God. This is not a burdensome code but the liberating law of love that governs the kingdom.

2 John 1:6 Love means doing what God has commanded us, and he has commanded us to love one another, just as you heard from the beginning. (NLT)

Main Idea

Love is not just a feeling; it is shown by living according to God's commandments. The command believers heard from the start is to keep walking in that love, so obedience and love go hand in hand.

Verse Explanation

Love is not just a feeling; it is shown by living according to God's commandments. The command believers heard from the start is to keep walking in that love, so obedience and love go hand in hand.

"This is love, that we walk according to His commandments."

In the previous verse John asked the church to "love one another." Here he explains what that love looks like. To "walk" means to live or conduct one's life, and the verb points to a steady, ongoing way of living rather than an occasional act. Love, then, is not first a feeling. It is a life lived the way God has told us to live. "His commandments" are the Father's, since John has already spoken of the commandment "from the Father" (1:4). John includes himself with "we." The apostle stands under the same commands as the church.

Jesus taught His disciples the same thing: "If you love Me, keep My commandments" (John 14:15), and "He who has My commandments and keeps them, it is he who loves Me" (John 14:21). John repeats it in his first letter: "For this is the love of God, that we keep His commandments. And His commandments are not burdensome" (1 John 5:3). Love for God and love for His people cannot be separated from obeying Him. In verse 4 John rejoiced over believers "walking in truth." Here that same walk is described as walking "according to His commandments." The life that holds to the truth is also the life that loves, and both show up in obedience.

"This is the commandment, that as you have heard from the beginning, you should walk in it."

John moves from many "commandments" to one "commandment." All of God's instructions come together in a single call. In his first letter John sums it up this way: "And this is His commandment: that we should believe on the name of His Son Jesus Christ and love one another, as He gave us commandment" (1 John 3:23). Believing in Jesus and loving one another belong to the same command.

"As you have heard from the beginning" means since they first received the faith. John is not giving them anything new. He is calling them to keep living by what they were taught at the start. "Walk in it" most naturally means walk in love, though the wording could also point back to the commandment. Either way the meaning is the same, because John has tied the two together so tightly. Love is shown by obeying God's commandments, and the commandment is to live a life of love. Obedience without love becomes cold rule-keeping, and love without obedience becomes empty sentiment. John calls for love that is shown "in deed and in truth" (1 John 3:18).

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] Love Walks, and the Walk Is Love. “And this is love, that we walk according to His commandments. This is the commandment, just as you have heard from the beginning, that you should walk in it.” John defines love as obedience and then defines the commandment as love, a deliberate circle (John 14:15, 21; 1 John 5:3). Love produces obedience, and obedience proves love; they are the same substance seen from two angles. Love that does not obey is sentiment, emotional humanism; obedience that does not love is sterile legalism, law without the Lawgiver’s heart. The circle keeps each one honest. Our culture defines love as validation; John defines it as obedience to God. Love is an action, a matter of the will, not merely a feeling, and God’s commands are the safe, life-giving boundaries within which it can flourish. The circle also contradicted the false teachers’ claim to a purely intellectual enlightenment that asked nothing of how they lived. Love is best shown by obedience. We do not measure our love for God by how strongly we feel toward Him but by walking where His word leads, and no one walks that road long without learning to love. Where does our love for God stay in our feelings and never reach our feet?

[Application] Love That Says No Is Still Love. John defines love by obedience rather than by feeling, and obedience does not always look like comfort. Love gives, forgives, and bears with another person, but it also corrects, sets a boundary, or speaks a hard truth when that is what genuinely helps someone. The test is never simply whether something feels loving, but whether it seeks what is truly good for the other person, as God has defined good, not as the moment defines it. A culture that measures love mainly by affirmation and avoided conflict will misread some of love’s most faithful acts as its opposite.

[Christ & Life] The Indwelling Christ Fulfills the Command. Here is the mystery of the gospel: the indwelling Christ fulfills the law in and through us when we yield to Him. True spiritual love is a fierce, unrelenting obedience to the highest authority, the Lord Jesus Himself, and yet it is not religious drudgery. It is a passionate, joyful pursuit of the Bridegroom’s will in everyday life.

2 John 1:7
NKJV

2 John 1:7 For many deceivers have gone out into the world who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh. This is a deceiver and an antichrist.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:7 I say this because many deceivers have gone out into the world. They deny that Jesus Christ came in a real body. Such a person is a deceiver and an antichrist. (NLT)

Main Idea

Many deceivers have gone out into the world who refuse to confess that Jesus Christ truly came as a real human being. Such a person is called a deceiver and an antichrist, because denying Jesus' genuine humanity strikes at the heart of the Christian message.

Verse Explanation

Many deceivers have gone out into the world who refuse to confess that Jesus Christ truly came as a real human being. Such a person is called a deceiver and an antichrist, because denying Jesus' genuine humanity strikes at the heart of the Christian message.

"For many deceivers have gone out into the world"

"For" gives the reason behind John's urging in the verses just before. He has called the church to keep walking in the commandment they had heard from the beginning, because there were people actively trying to lead them away from it. The Greek word for "deceivers" describes someone who leads others astray and causes them to wander off the right path. These were not simply mistaken people. They were spreading their error and drawing others after them.

Jesus had warned that "many false prophets will rise up and deceive many" (Matthew 24:11), and John uses nearly the same language in his first letter: "many false prophets have gone out into the world" (1 John 4:1). "Have gone out" may also recall what John said of those who had left the fellowship of believers: "They went out from us, but they were not of us; for if they had been of us, they would have continued with us" (1 John 2:19). These teachers were on the move. John does not name them or describe their whole system of teaching, and their exact identity is not known. What marks them is the denial he names next.

"who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh."

To "confess" means to acknowledge openly and agree with what is true. These deceivers would not acknowledge that Jesus Christ truly came as a real human being. "Flesh" here means a genuine human body and a real human life. John had written in his Gospel, "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:14), and he opened his first letter by describing the One "which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon, and our hands have handled" (1 John 1:1). The apostles had walked with Jesus, eaten with Him, and touched Him. He was not a spirit who only seemed human.

This was no minor disagreement. The eternal Son of God took on real flesh and blood so that He could die for sinners. Hebrews explains that because God's children share in flesh and blood, Jesus "Himself likewise shared in the same, that through death He might destroy him who had the power of death, that is, the devil" (Hebrews 2:14). If Jesus did not truly become human, then He did not truly die, and His blood did not truly pay for sin. John gives the same test in his first letter: "Every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is of God, and every spirit that does not confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is not of God" (1 John 4:2–3). Whether a teacher confesses this truth shows whether he comes from God.

"This is a deceiver and an antichrist."

John moves from "many deceivers" to "this," meaning that anyone who teaches this denial fits the description. He gives two titles. A "deceiver" leads people away from the truth. An "antichrist" stands against Christ and puts something false in His place. In the New Testament, the word "antichrist" appears only in John's letters. The believers had been taught that "the Antichrist is coming," and John told them that "even now many antichrists have come" (1 John 2:18). He also wrote that the spirit of the Antichrist "is now already in the world" (1 John 4:3). John is not saying each of these teachers is the final Antichrist. He means that their teaching already carries the same opposition to Christ. To deny who Jesus truly is, however religious the teacher may sound, is to work against Christ Himself.

Study Notes

John's wording here differs from the parallel test in 1 John 4:2. There the Greek has a perfect tense, "has come in the flesh," pointing to a completed event with lasting results. Here it is a present participle, literally "Jesus Christ coming in flesh." A few interpreters have taken this as a reference to Christ's future return in bodily form. However, Greek present participles of this verb can describe who someone is, not when an action happens. John the Baptist's disciples asked Jesus, "Are You the Coming One, or do we look for another?" (Matthew 11:3). The close link with 1 John 4:2 and the emphasis throughout John's letters on what the apostles saw and touched make the incarnation the most natural meaning. The present tense alone does not settle the question.

Who these teachers were is uncertain. Later writers describe teachings that would fit John's warning. One held that Christ only seemed to have a body, a view later called Docetism, from the Greek word for "to seem." Another was taught by Cerinthus, who, according to Irenaeus, said that a divine Christ came upon the man Jesus at His baptism and left Him before the crucifixion. Either kind of teaching would deny what John insists on. But John gives too little detail to tie his opponents to any known group.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] Wanderers Make Wanderers. “For many deceivers have gone out into the world.” The word for deceiver belongs to the Greek family for wandering and leading astray; the “wandering stars” of Jude 13 are planētai, the source of our word “planet.” A deceiver is first a wanderer and then one who gathers others to wander with him, one who causes others to wander; such people are “deceiving and being deceived” (2 Timothy 3:13). These teachers had “gone out,” the verb John used of those who “went out from us” (1 John 2:19); they had left the fellowship and were traveling the circuit of house churches. Error rarely stays home. The cure for a wandering heart is not more motion but abiding.

[Application] Deception Is on the Move. Deception is not passive; it is a proactive, invasive force moving out into the world and looking for a way into the church. Believers must live with spiritual alertness, aware that the world system is constantly flooded with teaching that denies the incarnate Christ. (The KJV reads “entered into the world”; the better text reads “gone out.” Either way, they were moving, and they were coming.)

[Christ & Life] A Bridge Broken at the Nearer End. “Those who do not acknowledge Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh.” Some taught that Jesus only seemed to have a body (Docetism, from the Greek for “to seem”); Cerinthus taught that the divine Christ came on the man Jesus at His baptism and left Him before the cross. Behind both lay the idea that spirit is good and flesh is evil. Either way, God never truly took on human flesh, never truly died, and never truly came near. “A Savior not quite God is a bridge broken at the farther end,” said one old bishop; the error John confronts breaks the bridge at the nearer end, a Savior not truly man. The whole story of Scripture is God coming to dwell with His people, and the incarnation is where He moved all the way in: “The Word became flesh, and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). To deny His flesh is to deny that God came to where we are.

[Foundational Truth] The Incarnational Test of Orthodoxy. The spirit of antichrist is identified by one refusal: it will not confess that Jesus came in the flesh. Why is the body of Jesus the ultimate battleground? Because if God did not take on real flesh, then the material world is simply evil, the physical cross was an illusion, the blood atonement never happened, bodily resurrection is a myth, and our own bodies cannot be temples of the Holy Spirit. The incarnation is the linchpin of redemption and the bedrock of our union with God: because Christ came in the flesh, He joined humanity to God. The identity of Jesus is the hill to die on. Get Jesus wrong, and you lose everything.

[Christ & Life] He Has Not Taken Off Our Flesh. In 1 John 4:2 the confession is that Jesus “has come” in the flesh; here John uses the present participle, “coming in the flesh.” Some take the present to describe the incarnation as an abiding fact rather than a finished event: Jesus came, and He continues in the flesh. Others refer it to His return; others warn against pressing the tense. Whatever the nuance, Scripture is clear that the risen Jesus did not lay aside His humanity. He ascended in a body of flesh and bones (Luke 24:39, 51); “there is one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5); and He will return “in like manner” (Acts 1:11). A Man with scars sits at the Father’s right hand. The flesh He took for us He keeps for us.

[Insight] Against Christ, and Instead of Christ. “This is the deceiver and the antichrist.” Only John uses the word antichrist, and the prefix anti can mean “against” or “instead of”: the antichrist spirit opposes Christ by offering a substitute Christ. John is not calling each teacher the final Antichrist; he names the type. The spirit of antichrist is not only a future political figure but any present teaching that diminishes the glory and reality of the incarnate Son of God.

[Christ & Life] The Son Who Came Near. John’s warning is anchored in a single fact: the Son of God truly took on flesh and entered the world He made. God did not send information about Himself from a safe distance; He came near, entered homes, touched people others avoided, wept with the grieving, and finally gave Himself on a cross. Guarding the truth about who Jesus is matters because it guards this very pattern. A church that defends the doctrine of the incarnation but never moves toward people the way the incarnate Christ did has kept the teaching and lost its shape.

[Seed] Discernment Is Knowing the Real. Discernment is not suspicion of people; it is such intimate familiarity with the true Christ that the counterfeit is recognized at once.

2 John 1:8
NKJV

2 John 1:8 Look to yourselves, that we do not lose those things we worked for, but that we may receive a full reward.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:8 Watch out that you do not lose what we have worked so hard to achieve. Be diligent so that you receive your full reward. (NLT)

Main Idea

Believers are warned to watch themselves carefully so that the spiritual progress they and their teachers have labored for is not lost through drifting from the truth. The goal is to receive a full reward, which means staying faithful to Christ all the way to the end.

Verse Explanation

Believers are warned to watch themselves carefully so that the spiritual progress they and their teachers have labored for is not lost through drifting from the truth. The goal is to receive a full reward, which means staying faithful to Christ all the way to the end.

"Look to yourselves,"

This is a call to be on guard and watch carefully. The command is plural, so John is speaking to the members of the church he writes to. He has just warned about the deceivers who deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh (verse 7), and now he tells believers to watch over their own lives. Jesus used the same kind of warning when He spoke of false teachers who would come: "Take heed that no one deceives you" (Matthew 24:4). Deceivers do their damage when believers are not paying attention. John does not tell them to watch the false teachers more closely. He tells them to keep a careful eye on themselves.

"that we do not lose those things we worked for,"

John includes himself and others who taught these believers. The word "worked" refers to hard, tiring labor. What had been worked for was the spiritual growth that came through hearing and living by the truth: their faith in Christ, their love for one another, and the walk in truth that gave John such joy (verse 4). That growth was the fruit of teaching, prayer, and faithful living, both theirs and their teachers'. Paul felt the same concern for the Galatians when they were being drawn away from the gospel: "I am afraid for you, lest I have labored for you in vain" (Galatians 4:11). If these believers welcomed teaching that denied who Jesus is, all that labor could be wasted.

"but that we may receive a full reward."

The word "reward" means wages, what is paid for work done, and "full" means complete, with nothing missing. Scripture teaches that God rewards faithful service: "each one will receive his own reward according to his own labor" (1 Corinthians 3:8). The risked loss is real. Jesus urged a church facing pressure to "hold fast what you have, that no one may take your crown" (Revelation 3:11). John does not explain here exactly how this reward relates to salvation or to persevering in faith, and Christians understand that relationship in different ways. What is clear is his purpose. He wants these believers to stay faithful to Christ so that they lose none of what God intends to give them. John expresses the same concern in his first letter: "abide in Him, that when He appears, we may have confidence and not be ashamed before Him at His coming" (1 John 2:28).

Study Notes

The NKJV follows the majority of Greek manuscripts, which have "we" in all three clauses. Most modern translations follow a group of older manuscripts that read "you" in the first and last clauses and "we" in the middle: "that you may not lose what we have worked for, but may receive a full reward." A few manuscripts read "what you worked for" instead.

On the reading behind most modern translations, the picture is slightly sharper. The church members are the ones who could lose or receive, and what is at stake is the labor that John and other faithful teachers had invested in them. The NKJV reading places John alongside the church as sharing the same risk and the same hope. Neither reading changes the main point. The warning is real, and faithfulness to the truth about Christ matters for what believers will receive. Neither reading settles how this reward relates to salvation.

Spirit & Life

[Application] Guard the House From the Inside First. “Watch yourselves.” Before John tells the church what to do about the deceivers at the door (vv. 10–11), he tells them to look at themselves. The command is a present imperative: keep looking, keep watching. It is like securing your own fortress before going out to fight. Deceivers succeed less by their strength than by our unguarded places: pride, spiritual boredom, an itch for something new, the pull of deceptive cultural currents. The first defense against error is not suspicion of others but honest attention to our own hearts. Are we watching our own hearts, or only watching for error in other people?

[Application] Discernment That Starts With a Mirror. “Watch yourselves” is especially pointed in an age when exposing other believers’ errors can become an occupation of its own. Online ministries and churches alike can spend enormous energy cataloging other Christians’ theology, sermons, songs, and associations while giving little attention to their own hearts. John’s order matters: before the church is told what to do about the deceiver at the door, it is told to examine itself. Biblical watchfulness begins at home, with the honest question of where I am coasting or compromised, not with a list of everyone else’s faults.

[Foundational Truth] God Wants to Pay in Full. “That you do not lose what we have accomplished, but that you may receive a full reward.” “Accomplished” means labored, and “reward” is a worker’s wage, the word for the laborers’ pay in the vineyard (Matthew 20:8) and the reaper’s wages in the harvest (John 4:36). John pictures a field that he and the church have worked together, now at risk of being lost just before payday; he wants them to hold on with him to the finish. The danger is not losing salvation but losing the fullness of what faithfulness would bring, the reward tested at the judgment seat of Christ (1 Corinthians 3:12–15). A “full” reward implies there can be a partial one: salvation is a free gift, but rewards are tied to our ongoing abiding, watchfulness, and faithful stewardship of the truth placed in us. Yet the God of this verse is not looking for reasons to withhold. He is a generous Master who wants to pay in full (Revelation 22:12), and ultimately He Himself is our “exceedingly great reward” (Genesis 15:1).

[Seed] The Joint Venture of Spiritual Labor. “What we have accomplished.” John sees these believers’ growth as a shared investment; the apostle’s labor and their cooperation are woven together. If they fall to deception, part of his labor and joy is lost too. Spiritual progress can be lost: what apostles, pastors, and mentors have poured into us can be squandered by later carelessness. We are stewards not only of our own faith but of the labor others have invested in us. (The better text reads “that you do not lose what we have worked for, but that you may receive.”)

[Application] Don’t Stumble Near the Finish Line. There is a tragedy in running the race well only to stumble near the end. Live for the smile of the Master rather than the comforts of the age, with the full reward of Christ’s presence as your highest motive. Our daily choices carry eternal weight; steward your spiritual momentum fiercely.

2 John 1:9
NKJV

2 John 1:9 Whoever transgresses and does not abide in the doctrine of Christ does not have God. He who abides in the doctrine of Christ has both the Father and the Son.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:9 Anyone who wanders away from this teaching has no relationship with God. But anyone who remains in the teaching of Christ has a relationship with both the Father and the Son. (NLT)

Main Idea

Anyone who moves away from the true teaching about Jesus Christ and does not remain in it does not have a genuine relationship with God. Those who remain faithful to the truth about Christ enjoy fellowship with both the Father and the Son.

Verse Explanation

Anyone who moves away from the true teaching about Jesus Christ and does not remain in it does not have a genuine relationship with God. Those who remain faithful to the truth about Christ enjoy fellowship with both the Father and the Son.

"Whoever transgresses"

To "transgress" means to step over a line or go beyond a boundary. John has just warned about the deceivers who deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh (verse 7), and here he describes what they do: they cross the line of the truth about Christ and leave it behind. Many modern translations read "goes ahead" or "runs ahead," following a different Greek reading. Either way, the person has moved past the teaching they first received instead of staying with it. The word "whoever" makes the warning personal. It applies to anyone who takes this step, whether a visiting teacher or a member of the church who is drawn away.

"and does not abide in the doctrine of Christ"

"Abide" is one of John's favorite words. It means to remain, to stay, to make one's home somewhere. Jesus used it when He told His disciples, "Abide in Me, and I in you" (John 15:4). John has already said that the truth "abides in us and will be with us forever" (verse 2). Those in whom the truth lives must also go on living in it.

"Doctrine" simply means teaching. "The doctrine of Christ" is the teaching about Jesus Christ that the apostles proclaimed: who He is, and that the Son of God truly came as a real human being. This is the same message John urged in his first letter: "Therefore let that abide in you which you heard from the beginning. If what you heard from the beginning abides in you, you also will abide in the Son and in the Father" (1 John 2:24). Remaining in this teaching is more than holding correct ideas. Jesus said, "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6), so staying with the truth about Him cannot be separated from staying with Him. Someone who abandons the truth about who Jesus is has walked away from Jesus Himself.

John's concern here is not a disagreement over secondary matters. It is the foundation itself: the identity of Jesus Christ and His coming in the flesh.

"does not have God."

To "have" God means to truly know Him and belong to Him. Whatever a person may claim about knowing God, if he leaves the truth about Christ behind, he does not have God at all. John said the same thing plainly in his first letter: "Whoever denies the Son does not have the Father either" (1 John 2:23). No one can reach God by going around His Son.

"He who abides in the doctrine of Christ has both the Father and the Son."

The person who stays with the teaching about Christ does not lose anything. He has God fully, both the Father and the Son. John began the letter by calling Jesus "the Son of the Father" (verse 3), and here he shows why that matters. The Father and the Son cannot be separated. Jesus Himself taught that "He who does not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent Him" (John 5:23). To receive the Son is to receive the Father, and to hold to the truth about the Son is to enjoy fellowship with both. The believers John writes to did not need some new teaching to know God more deeply. Everything they needed was found in remaining with the Christ they had believed from the beginning.

Study Notes

Two different Greek words lie behind the translations of this verse. The NKJV's "transgresses" translates parabainōn, the reading of most later manuscripts. Many modern translations read "goes ahead" or "runs ahead," translating proagōn, found in several of the oldest manuscripts, including the important fourth-century codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus. Elsewhere proagōn is an ordinary word for going before or leading the way. Here it describes moving forward past the teaching about Christ instead of remaining in it. Some interpreters suggest the word may echo the false teachers' own claim to offer something more advanced. That is a reasonable guess, but the letter does not describe their claims.

"The doctrine of Christ" could grammatically mean the teaching Christ Himself gave or the teaching about Christ. The context favors teaching about Christ, since verse 7 has just named the denial of His coming in the flesh. The two are not far apart, because Jesus' own teaching centered on who He is and His relationship to the Father.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] Running Ahead of the Teaching. “Anyone who goes too far and does not abide in the teaching of Christ does not have God.” The verb means “to go before, lead forward,” here in its bad sense: “goes on ahead,” “runs ahead,” “goes further than is right.” The false teachers claimed to be the progressives, the advanced thinkers, the men of the open and adventurous mind, possessors of a higher knowledge that had moved beyond the simple gospel of the apostles. John answers that progress beyond the foundational teaching of Christ is not enlightenment; it is abandonment. To leave the boundaries of Christ’s teaching is to leave God Himself. Progress that denies fundamental truth is going backward. Like a child who runs ahead of his father on a mountain trail, the one who outruns Christ’s teaching does not arrive sooner; he gets lost. Jesus Christ must be the touchstone of all thinking. The question to ask of any “new” teaching is not whether it is fresh but whether it is still inside Christ. Are we drawn to teaching because it sounds new or advanced?

[Christ & Life] The Prize of Sound Doctrine Is God Himself. “The one who abides in the teaching, he has both the Father and the Son.” To “have” God is John’s language for a real, living relationship: “He who has the Son has life” (1 John 5:12; 2:23). Abiding in the teaching means abiding in living union with the Person of Christ. The point is not to win arguments or guard a tradition but to possess the Father and the Son; when we remain in Christ, we receive not merely a teaching but the Father and the Son as our inward, abundant supply of life. Doctrine is the doorway, and the Father and the Son are the home it opens into. That is why John cares so much about what is taught concerning Jesus: a different Christ cannot bring anyone to the Father (John 14:6), and we cannot experience the true God through a false Jesus. We study the teaching so that through it we may have Him, and our study is not finished until we have entered. Is our knowledge of doctrine bringing us to have the Father and the Son, or only to have opinions about Them?

[Foundational Truth] You Cannot Bypass the Son. There is no middle ground here between having God and being without Him. You cannot go around the Son to get to the Father. Any spirituality or religion that claims to possess the Father while altering or diminishing the Son is utterly bankrupt; a doctrine that diminishes Christ strips a person of a true relationship with the Father. Christ is the only place the Father is found. Don’t go on without Christ, or you will lose God.

If someone comes as a teacher but does not bring the true teaching about Christ, believers should not welcome him into their homes or offer him the kind of greeting that signals support. If someone comes as a teacher but does not bring the true teaching about Christ, believers should not welcome him into their homes or offer him the kind of greeting that signals support. This concerns hospitality that would help spread false teaching, not a ban on ordinary kindness toward people who disagree.

"If anyone comes to you"

John has already warned that "many deceivers have gone out into the world" (v. 7). Here that warning reaches the church's own doorstep: one of these traveling deceivers may arrive in person. In the early church, teachers and missionaries moved from town to town and depended on believers for lodging, meals, and help for the next stage of their journey. John valued this kind of hospitality. He praised Gaius for receiving traveling brothers and sending them on "in a manner worthy of God" (3 John 6). In Greek, "you" here is plural, so John is speaking to the whole congregation, not only to one household. Churches in that day met in homes (Romans 16:5). Welcoming a visiting teacher into the house could mean giving him a place to teach the gathered believers.

"and does not bring this doctrine,"

"This doctrine" is the doctrine of Christ John has just mentioned, the apostolic teaching about who Jesus Christ is, including the truth that He came as a real human being. The word "bring" pictures a teacher arriving with a message he intends to spread. John does not tell the church to judge such a visitor by his gifts, his warmth, or his claims to spiritual insight. The deciding question is what he teaches about Jesus. John has in mind someone who comes to promote a denial of the basic truth about Christ, not a fellow believer who differs on lesser matters.

"do not receive him into your house"

Receiving a traveling teacher meant giving him a room, food, and supplies, and often a platform among the believers. That support made his work possible. John wrote that believers should receive faithful workers "that we may become fellow workers for the truth" (3 John 8). The same principle works in reverse. Housing and supplying a false teacher would make the church a partner in spreading his error, which is why John adds that the one who welcomes him "shares in his evil deeds" (v. 11). John is not forbidding help to a stranger in need or conversation with someone who holds wrong beliefs. He is forbidding the church to become a base of operations for someone working against the truth about Christ.

"nor greet him;"

The Greek word behind "greet" was the ordinary word of greeting, meaning "rejoice" or "greetings." It is the same word that opens letters in the New Testament, translated "Greetings" (Acts 15:23; James 1:1). For a visiting teacher, this greeting was more than a polite hello. It welcomed him as a fellow servant and wished his work well, signaling to him and to the church that he came with their approval. That is the greeting John says to withhold. Believers are not to treat such a person rudely or refuse ordinary courtesy. They are simply not to give a blessing that would tell everyone his message has their support.

Study Notes

An early Christian manual called the Didache, probably written around the late first or early second century, shows how churches handled traveling teachers. It told believers to welcome a teacher whose teaching agreed with what they had received. If a teacher taught a different doctrine that undermined it, they were not to listen to him. It also gave practical tests. A traveling apostle should stay a day, or two if needed, and one who stayed three days, or who asked for money, was to be treated as a false prophet. These instructions show that John's concern was a real and recurring problem. Churches depended on visiting teachers, and they needed clear ways to tell which ones deserved their support.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] Your Door Is a Pulpit. “If anyone comes to you and does not bring this teaching, do not receive him into your house.” Church buildings did not appear until the third century; the church met in homes (Romans 16:5; Colossians 4:15; Philemon 2), and traveling teachers depended on believers to house and feed them while they taught. An early Christian manual, the Didache, instructs churches to receive a teacher who teaches these things, but not to listen to one who turns and teaches another doctrine, and it warns that a traveling teacher who stays three days or asks for money is a false prophet. To take such a man into the house was to give him a bed, a table, a gathered church, the host’s credibility, and a recommendation to the next town; refusing him lodging effectively shut down his ministry. Hospitality was the road the gospel traveled on (3 John 5–8), and the same road could carry poison. What we give a platform to, we help spread.

[Application] Check What He Brings. “And does not bring this teaching.” The test John gives is not the visitor’s warmth, gifting, eloquence, or credentials, but what he carries in. A teacher is a messenger, and messengers are judged by the message. Like a border guard who waves through a friendly traveler without checking the cargo, churches are often more impressed by the carrier than by what he brings. The Bereans checked the cargo against the Scriptures even when the carrier was Paul (Acts 17:11).

[Application] Love That Is Fierce With Wolves. In the ancient world hospitality was a sacred duty, yet John commands a radical boundary. True love protects the flock, and refusing hospitality to wolves is an act of supreme love for the sheep. Love for the flock requires a shepherd to be fierce with the wolves; tolerance of a doctrine that denies the incarnation is not love but an abdication of protection. There is a holy intolerance for error that refuses to sacrifice truth on the altar of a false, superficial unity. Guard the sacred fire of your inner life, your home, and your church from false teaching, because toxic theology brings toxic spiritual realities. We are called to love all people, but we are forbidden to empower, endorse, or fund ministries that deny the biblical Jesus.

[Insight] Holy Severity, Rightly Bounded. The early church felt this severity. An old story tells that John, finding the false teacher Cerinthus in a bathhouse at Ephesus, rushed out, crying, “Let us flee, lest the bath fall, for Cerinthus, the enemy of the truth, is within.” Yet the command has edges. It has been called an emergency regulation, and emergency regulations can make bad law if stretched. It does not forbid ordinary courtesy, emergency help, or friendship, and it does not forbid speaking with someone at the door in order to reach him with the truth. It forbids receiving and endorsing a teacher who is actively spreading a false Christ.

2 John 1:11
NKJV

2 John 1:11 for he who greets him shares in his evil deeds.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:11 Anyone who encourages such people becomes a partner in their evil work. (NLT)

Main Idea

Welcoming and encouraging a teacher who denies the truth about Jesus makes a believer a partner in the harm that teacher causes. Support for a false message, even unintentionally, helps that message do its damage.

Verse Explanation

Welcoming and encouraging a teacher who denies the truth about Jesus makes a believer a partner in the harm that teacher causes. Support for a false message, even unintentionally, helps that message do its damage.

"for he who greets him"

"For" gives the reason behind the command just before it: do not receive into your house or greet a teacher who does not bring the doctrine of Christ (1:10). Here "greets" means a greeting that signals support. It is the kind of welcome that would tell everyone this teacher is a trusted brother whose message deserves a hearing. John is not forbidding ordinary politeness toward someone who is wrong. He is saying that a believer should not lend his name, his home, or his encouragement to a man who goes from church to church denying that Jesus Christ came in the flesh (1:7).

"shares in his evil deeds."

The Greek verb translated "shares" belongs to the same word family John uses for "fellowship" in his first letter: "truly our fellowship is with the Father and with His Son Jesus Christ" (1 John 1:3). To share in something is to become a partner in it. A believer who welcomes and supports a false teacher may not believe his error at all. But by opening the door, he helps that teacher do his work, and so he takes part in it.

The "evil deeds" are not only private sins in the teacher's life. They are the harm his teaching does. He leads people away from the truth about Jesus, which can cause believers to lose what they have worked for (1:8). Anyone who departs from the doctrine of Christ "does not have God" (1:9). Helping such a teacher therefore means helping to draw people away from the Father and the Son. That is why John treats the matter so seriously.

The same principle works in the other direction. In his next letter John commends believers who welcome faithful traveling teachers, saying, "We therefore ought to receive such, that we may become fellow workers for the truth" (3 John 8). Hospitality makes a person a partner in the message of whoever he supports. When believers support a faithful teacher, they share in the work of the truth. When they support a deceiver, they share in the damage he does.

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] A Greeting Can Be a Partnership. “For the one who gives him a greeting participates in his evil deeds.” “Participates” is the verb form of koinōnia, the word John used for “our fellowship … with the Father and with His Son Jesus Christ” (1 John 1:3). Fellowship is never neutral: the same kind of bond that joins us to God can, when misplaced, join us to another man’s evil work. False teaching is not just an unfortunate error; it is a wicked work, one that actively does harm. The greeting in view is not a polite hello on the street but a formal, public welcome that treats the teacher as a brother and his work as the Lord’s work; in that culture, hospitality and greeting conferred trust, patronage, and spiritual solidarity. We can become guilty of sins we did not physically commit. Financial, verbal, or social endorsement of a false ministry makes a believer a partner in the damage it does (1 Timothy 5:22). There is an unseen economy here: we are credited, for good or ill, with what we facilitate, fund, or formally endorse, and where we direct our support reveals the true alignment of our hearts. Loyalty to Christ must outrank cultural politeness. What are we giving a platform to, in our homes, our feeds, or our recommendations, that does not bring the truth about Jesus?

2 John 1:12
NKJV

2 John 1:12 Having many things to write to you, I did not wish to do so with paper and ink; but I hope to come to you and speak face to face, that our joy may be full.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:12 I have much more to say to you, but I don’t want to do it with paper and ink. For I hope to visit you soon and talk with you face to face. Then our joy will be complete. (NLT)

Main Idea

John has much more to say but chooses not to put it all on paper, hoping instead to visit and talk face to face. Personal fellowship, he explains, will make their joy complete in a way a letter cannot.

Verse Explanation

John has much more to say but chooses not to put it all on paper, hoping instead to visit and talk face to face. Personal fellowship, he explains, will make their joy complete in a way a letter cannot.

"Having many things to write to you,"

John's letter is short, and he admits there is much more he wants to say. He has written only what was most urgent: a reminder to love one another as God commanded, a warning about the deceivers who deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh, and instructions not to welcome and support them. Everything else he is saving for later. John closes his third letter in almost the same way (3 John 13–14), which shows how he preferred to handle the rest of what was on his heart.

"I did not wish to do so with paper and ink;"

The "paper" was papyrus, a writing sheet made from pressed strips of a reed plant. The ink was usually made from soot mixed with gum and water. A letter as brief as 2 John would fit on a single sheet. John is not saying writing is wrong; he has just written this letter, and his Gospel and first letter were written so that others could believe and know they have eternal life. But a letter has limits. It cannot answer questions, read a listener's face, or offer the warmth of being present. Some of what John had to say, perhaps including more about the situation with the false teachers, may have been better handled in person, though he does not say why he chose to wait.

"but I hope to come to you and speak face to face,"

The Greek expression behind "face to face" literally reads "mouth to mouth," meaning direct, personal conversation with nothing in between. John, the Elder, wanted to be with the church John writes to and its members, not simply to send them instructions from a distance. His words "I hope" show that his plans were sincere but not certain. He longed to come, but whether and when he would arrive was not entirely in his hands.

"that our joy may be full."

Earlier John told them, "I rejoiced greatly that I have found some of your children walking in truth" (verse 4). That joy was already great, but it was not yet complete. Hearing good news from a distance brought gladness, while being together in person would bring that gladness to its fullness. The word "our" matters here. This joy belongs to John and the believers together, because it comes from their shared life in the truth and in Christ.

John used nearly the same words at the start of his first letter: "And these things we write to you that your joy may be full" (1 John 1:4). He learned that language from Jesus, who told His disciples, "These things I have spoken to you, that My joy may remain in you, and that your joy may be full" (John 15:11). The full joy Jesus promised is found in fellowship with Him, and it overflows into fellowship among those who belong to Him. That is why John wanted more than a letter could give. He wanted to sit with these believers and share in person the joy they had in common.

None.

Spirit & Life

[Discovery] Mouth to Mouth. “Though I have many things to write to you, I do not want to do so with paper and ink; but I hope to come to you and speak face to face.” The Greek says “mouth to mouth,” the phrase God used of His unique friendship with Moses: “I speak with him mouth to mouth” (Numbers 12:8). “Paper” is papyrus, used only here in the New Testament, a fragile sheet of reed strips; “ink” is literally “black,” a mixture of soot, gum, and water. The whole letter could fit on one sheet of papyrus, yet John wants for this church the kind of speaking God gave His friend Moses. God’s goal with His people has always been intimacy, not just information.

[Application] Never Write When You Could Speak. Some things are better spoken than written. Church leaders and Christian people would do well to resolve never to write when they could speak. A page cannot carry a face, a tone, tears, or the chance to answer a question, and letters often harden what a conversation could have healed. John still had “many things” to say, and he saved them for the room. Hard conversations belong in person whenever possible (Matthew 18:15), and so do the tender ones. Is there a conversation we have been trying to handle by text or email that God wants us to have face to face?

[Application] What We Send, We No Longer Control. John’s reason for setting down his pen applies with new force to texting and social media. A sent message can be forwarded, screenshotted, or resurfaced years after the moment that produced it, and the writer rarely controls where it travels next. Writing also cannot carry tone; sarcasm, humor, and correction read very differently on a screen than they sound in a voice. Email and messaging serve ordinary information well; they rarely serve correction, conflict, or anything carrying strong emotion. The wiser course is often to wait until the emotion has cooled, and sometimes the wisest of all is to stop typing and go speak with the person.

[Insight] The Limits of Paper and Ink. Even under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, John recognizes the limits of mediated communication. Information can travel on paper; spiritual impartation and human communion need presence. Even inspired apostolic writing is a poor substitute for being there, and true fellowship requires physical presence that no letter, and no modern medium, can replace.

[Christ & Life] Joy Made Full Face to Face. “That your joy may be made full.” The phrase means joy brought to completion and kept full, the same words that close the opening of 1 John (1:4; compare John 15:11; 17:13). Joy is partial while we are apart; complete joy is relational and embodied. This mirrors the believer’s ultimate hope. We now have the “paper and ink” of Scripture, which brings us immense joy, and the written Word points us to living encounter with the Word made flesh. But our joy will only be made completely full when we see Christ face to face (1 Corinthians 13:12). Until then, the fullness of Christ is not known in isolation but in the gathered, face-to-face body, and nothing substitutes for face-to-face intimacy, whether with other believers or with God in the secret place.

[Application] Joy Was Never Meant to Be Virtual. John wants to see this church face to face so their joy may be full, a desire that reaches back to how people were made. Humanity was created for relationship with God and with each other, and some of life’s deepest joy is relational. That joy has been under attack since Eden: Adam and Eve hid after they sinned, and people have been finding reasons to hide ever since, fear, shame, old wounds, or simple busyness. Digital connection can imitate relationship without delivering it, leaving people who message often but rarely sit across a table with anyone still lonely. The fullness John longs for is found the way it has always been found: in the room, with the people God has placed around us.

2 John 1:13
NKJV

2 John 1:13 The children of your elect sister greet you. Amen.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:13 Greetings from the children of your sister, chosen by God. (NLT)

Main Idea

John closes with greetings from "the children of your elect sister," most likely the members of the church where John is writing from, sending love to their fellow believers. The ending reminds readers that local congregations are part of one larger family chosen by God.

Verse Explanation

John closes with greetings from "the children of your elect sister," most likely the members of the church where John is writing from, sending love to their fellow believers. The ending reminds readers that local congregations are part of one larger family chosen by God.

"The children of your elect sister"

John opened the letter by writing "to the elect lady and her children" (1:1), the church he was writing to and its members. He closes with her "elect sister" and her children. The two figures frame the letter, and together they point to sister congregations. The elect sister is most likely the church where John was living when he wrote, and her children are its believers. Some understand the lady in verse 1 as an individual Christian woman. On that view, these would be her nephews and nieces, perhaps living near John.

Like the lady, the sister is "elect," meaning chosen by God. Both congregations owe their existence to the same God, which is why John can call them sisters. Peter closes a letter in a similar way: "She who is in Babylon, elect together with you, greets you" (1 Peter 5:13). Many interpreters see that phrase as a reference to a church as well. Christians in different cities were not strangers to one another. They were members of one family, children of the same Father.

"greet you."

The "you" here is singular in Greek. The whole congregation is addressed as the one lady, the sister receiving word from her sister.

This greeting stands in contrast to John's warning a few verses earlier. A teacher who denies that Jesus Christ came in the flesh was not to be received, and the readers were not to "greet him" (1:10). A greeting of that kind signals support, and "he who greets him shares in his evil deeds" (1:11). Here, a greeting passes freely between two churches that share the same truth about Christ. John was not asking the believers to be cold toward people. He was showing that a Christian greeting carries real weight. It expresses fellowship, so it is withheld from those working against the truth and gladly given to those who hold to it. John hoped to visit soon and speak "face to face" (1:12). Until he could come, this greeting carried the love of one church to another.

None.

Spirit & Life

[Seed] Greetings From a Chosen Sister. “The children of your chosen sister greet you.” The letter that opened with a “chosen lady” closes with a “chosen sister,” so “chosen” frames the whole letter. If these are churches, the sister is likely John’s own congregation, probably at Ephesus; congregations are sisters and their members cousins in one family, sending greetings across the miles, sisters in the election of grace, keeping watch over one another. There are many local churches but one body of Christ sharing one divine life, a family that stretches across every border. The greetings of the New Testament are not polite filler; they are the arteries through which the lifeblood of Christian solidarity flows. In a letter that has just forbidden one greeting (v. 10), John ends by passing along another; the closed door of verse 10 did not make the church closed. No local church is an orphan, and none should live as if it were; we are never fighting the battle alone. Greet other churches, pray for them by name, and remember that the same Father chose them too.
