Chapter Study

2 John 1

Verse-by-Verse
2 John 1:1

NKJV

2 John 1:1 The Elder, To the elect lady and her children, whom I love in truth, and not only I, but also all those who have known the truth,(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:1 This letter is from John, the elder. I am writing to the chosen lady and to her children, whom I love in the truth — as does everyone else who knows the truth — (NLT)

Main Idea

John greets the elect lady and her children, most likely a local church and its members, with a love that is genuine and grounded in the truth. He is not alone in loving them; all who have known the truth love them as well.

Verse Explanation

“The Elder,”

The writer identifies himself only by his title. “Elder” can describe an older man or a recognized leader among the churches, and here it likely carries both senses. The letter does not give his name, but the church has long recognized him as John the apostle. Irenaeus, who as a young man heard John’s disciple Polycarp, names John as the author. The letter’s language of truth, love, abiding, and the commandment “from the beginning” also matches John’s Gospel and 1 John. His use of the title without further identification suggests that his readers already knew him and recognized his authority.

“To the elect lady and her children,”

“Elect” means chosen by God. The “lady” is most likely a local church, addressed as a mother, with “her children” as its members. Several details support this reading. John moves to the plural “you” in verses 6, 8, 10, and 12. He urges them to love “one another.” He also gives instructions about whom to receive into the house, which fits a congregation meeting in a home. The letter closes with greetings from “the children of your elect sister” (v. 13), most naturally another church. Some read the lady as an individual Christian woman and her family; that alternative is noted below.

“whom I love in truth,”

John’s love for them is genuine, and it rests on the truth they share. “In truth” can mean “truly,” but in John’s writings it also points to the truth of God revealed in Jesus Christ. Both senses fit here. John’s love is real because it is rooted in that truth.

“and not only I, but also all those who have known the truth,”

John is not the only one who loves them. Everyone who has come to know the truth shares this love, including believers who have never met them. “Have known” describes knowledge that began in the past and continues, a settled knowledge of the truth rather than a passing acquaintance with Christian ideas. The opening verse already joins truth and love, and the letter keeps them together throughout. Verse 2 gives the reason this love reaches so widely.

Study Notes

The Elder. The Greek word presbyteros is related to presbys, “an old man.” In the New Testament it describes both older men and leaders of local churches (Acts 14:23). An apostle could use it of himself. Peter calls himself “a fellow elder” (1 Peter 5:1).

The elect lady: the alternative reading. The Greek word for “lady,” kyria, is the feminine form of kyrios, “lord.” It appears in the New Testament only here and in verse 5. In ordinary Greek letters, kyria could be a courteous form of address, much like “dear lady.” Some interpreters take her to be an individual Christian woman and her household. A few take Kyria as her personal name, and others have proposed Electa. The Electa reading is unlikely, because her sister is also called “elect” in verse 13. Describing a church as a woman has a close parallel in 1 Peter 5:13: “She who is in Babylon, elect together with you, greets you.” Scripture elsewhere pictures God’s people as a bride belonging to Christ (2 Corinthians 11:2; Ephesians 5:25–27), though 2 John does not develop that image.

Spirit & Life

John writes to a church that many believers have never visited, yet “all those who have known the truth” love them. Love among believers does not wait for acquaintance. Before Christians ever meet, they already belong to one another because the same truth has taken hold of them both. That is why a believer can pray with real concern for persecuted Christians whose names he will never know, or meet a stranger from another church and recognize family within minutes.

The letter opens with a man who does not give his name. This is the John whom Jesus named one of the “Sons of Thunder” (Mark 3:17), who once wanted to call down fire on a Samaritan village (Luke 9:54). The letter does not tell us why he chose the title “the Elder,” but the contrast is striking. The same man now writes with the affection of a spiritual father, loving these believers in truth. Walking with Jesus changes how a person relates to others.

2 John 1:2

NKJV

2 John 1:2 because of the truth which abides in us and will be with us forever:(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:2 because the truth lives in us and will be with us forever. (NLT)

Main Idea

John gives the reason for this shared love: the truth that abides in believers and will be with them forever. This truth is not merely information they hold but a lasting reality that lives in them and joins them together.

Verse Explanation

“because of the truth”

John now explains the love described in verse 1. Believers love this church “because of the truth.” The truth is the ground of their love. People who share it belong to one another, even across distance and without having met.

“which abides in us”

“Abides” means to remain, stay, or dwell. It is one of John’s favorite words. The truth is not merely information believers have heard or acknowledged. It is God’s revealed truth that they have received and that now abides in them. In John’s writings, truth is God’s reality revealed in Jesus, who said, “I am the way, the truth, and the life” (John 14:6).

“and will be with us forever:”

John adds a promise. The truth that abides in believers will remain; it will not fail or pass away. Deceivers are already at work (v. 7), and John will soon urge his readers to watch themselves and to abide in the doctrine of Christ (vv. 8–9). The permanence of the truth does not make those warnings unnecessary.

Study Notes

The truth and the Spirit of truth. John’s wording closely resembles Jesus’ promise of the Spirit of truth, “that He may abide with you forever,” who “dwells with you and will be in you” (John 14:16–17). 2 John does not identify the abiding truth with the Holy Spirit. Even so, the parallel helps explain why John can speak of truth as something that lives in believers. In John’s writings, truth is personal in Jesus (John 14:6), and the Spirit is the One who guides believers into it (John 16:13) and bears witness to it (1 John 5:6).

Abide. The Greek word menō appears here and twice in verse 9. The two verses form a pair: the truth abides in believers (v. 2), and believers are to abide in the doctrine of Christ (v. 9). The same pattern of mutual abiding appears in Jesus’ words, “Abide in Me, and I in you” (John 15:4). “Forever” translates a Greek phrase meaning “into the age,” a common New Testament expression for what is permanent.

Spirit & Life

John speaks of truth the way someone speaks of a person who lives in the house. Much of what people hear about God passes through them like a visitor, welcomed for a while and then gone. The truth John describes has come to stay. It lives within believers and has the right to direct how they think, decide, and love.

Jesus used nearly the same words for the Spirit of truth, who would “abide with you forever” and “be in you” (John 14:16–17). The God who reveals the truth also dwells with His people and keeps it alive in them. The permanence belongs to the truth and to the God who gave it. Truth that has come to stay calls for a people who stay with it (vv. 8–9).

2 John 1:3

NKJV

2 John 1:3 Grace, mercy, and peace will be with you from God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father, in truth and love.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:3 Grace, mercy, and peace, which come from God the Father and from Jesus Christ — the Son of the Father — will continue to be with us who live in truth and love. (NLT)

Main Idea

John states with confidence that grace, mercy, and peace will be with them, coming from God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father. These gifts come “in truth and love,” joining the two words that govern the whole letter.

Verse Explanation

“Grace, mercy, and peace”

Grace is God’s undeserved favor and kindness. Mercy is His compassion toward people in their need. Peace is the wholeness and well-being that come from being reconciled to God. Together they describe God’s goodness toward His people and its result in their lives.

“will be with you”

Many New Testament greetings take the form of a wish or prayer: “Grace to you and peace be multiplied” (1 Peter 1:2). John instead makes a plain statement about the future. These blessings “will be with you.” He used the same expression for the truth in verse 2.

“from God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father,”

These blessings come from both the Father and the Son. By repeating “from,” John places Jesus Christ beside the Father as a source of grace, mercy, and peace. That is language suited only to One who shares the Father’s divine nature. The title “the Son of the Father” appears nowhere else in the New Testament. It emphasizes relationship: Jesus is the Son who shares the Father’s life and makes the Father known (John 1:14, 18). In a letter that warns against those who deny the truth about Jesus Christ (v. 7), the greeting itself confesses who He is.

“in truth and love.”

The greeting closes by naming the two realities that run through the whole letter. God’s grace, mercy, and peace reach His people in truth and love, and His people are to live in both. John does not treat truth and love as two values to be balanced against each other. Love is lived within the truth, and the truth is lived out in love.

Study Notes

Grace, mercy, and peace. In the NKJV, these three words appear together as a greeting elsewhere only in 1 Timothy 1:2, 2 Timothy 1:2, and Titus 1:4. Many early manuscripts of Titus 1:4 lack “mercy.” This is the only place “mercy” (eleos) appears in any of John’s writings.

“With you” or “with us.” The NKJV reads “with you.” Many early manuscripts read “with us” (followed by the ESV), which repeats the wording of verse 2 exactly. Under either reading, the blessing is stated as a certainty, not a wish.

“The Lord.” The earliest manuscripts do not include “Lord” before “Jesus Christ.” The NKJV includes it, following other manuscripts. The difference does not affect the meaning, since the verse already places Jesus beside the Father as the source of grace.

Spirit & Life

John writes to a church that deceivers may soon visit, yet his first words are not a warning. They are a certainty: grace, mercy, and peace “will be with you.” Before he asks anything of them, he settles them in what God has already determined to give. Everything he asks in this letter rests on that foundation. Believers under pressure do not hold on to earn God’s kindness. They hold on because His kindness has been promised to stay.

These gifts come “from God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father.” The title is relational, and so is the gift. Grace can be pictured as God’s kindness flowing from His heart, mercy as that kindness reaching down to our need, and peace as what it produces in us. Believers are not receiving favors from a distant power. They are being drawn into the love the Father and the Son share.

2 John 1:4–6: Walking in Truth and Love

2 John 1:4

NKJV

2 John 1:4 I rejoiced greatly that I have found some of your children walking in truth, as we received commandment from the Father.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:4 How happy I was to meet some of your children and find them living according to the truth, just as the Father commanded. (NLT)

Main Idea

John rejoices greatly that some of the lady’s children are walking in truth. They are living out what they believe, in obedience to the commandment received from the Father.

Verse Explanation

“I rejoiced greatly”

Before John gives any instruction or warning, he tells the church what brought him joy. He writes as a pastor who is glad over the faithfulness of God’s people. That joy sets the tone for the letter. The warnings that come later arise from the same love.

“that I have found some of your children walking in truth,”

John has met some of the church’s members. The letter does not say where or how. “Some” refers to those he found. It need not mean the others were unfaithful, and the verse should not be read that way.

“Walking” describes an ongoing way of life, the settled direction of a person’s conduct rather than a single step. To walk “in truth” is to live in line with God’s revealed truth, not merely to agree with it. What these believers held to be true was being lived out in how they conducted their lives.

“as we received commandment from the Father.”

Walking in truth is obedience to God. The Father has given a command, and these believers are doing what He said. By saying “we received,” John places himself under the same command as the church. Verse 5 identifies the commandment he has in mind.

Study Notes

“Greatly.” The Greek word lian appears in John’s letters only here and in 3 John 3, both times in connection with believers walking in the truth. John states the same joy in 3 John 4: “I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth.”

Walk. The Greek verb peripateō, “to walk about,” is a common biblical picture of a person’s conduct (1 John 1:7; 2:6). Here it is a present participle, describing continuing action. The verb appears three times in verses 4–6.

The commandment from the Father. 1 John 3:23 brings belief and love together in a single commandment: “And this is His commandment: that we should believe on the name of His Son Jesus Christ and love one another.” Walking in truth includes both what believers confess about Jesus and how they love one another, which are the two concerns of this letter.

Spirit & Life

What brings a person great joy reveals what he values most. John had seen the risen Christ and given his life to the churches. Yet what made him rejoice “greatly” was finding believers whose daily lives matched what they believed. He wrote the same to Gaius: “I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth” (3 John 4).

John noticed, and he made sure they knew it. Leaders and parents can easily see what is going wrong and miss where truth has moved from belief into daily steps. Few things strengthen a believer more than hearing that someone has seen Christ at work in his ordinary obedience. When we see it, we should say so.

2 John 1:5

NKJV

2 John 1:5 And now I plead with you, lady, not as though I wrote a new commandment to you, but that which we have had from the beginning: that we love one another.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:5 I am writing to remind you, dear friends, that we should love one another. This is not a new commandment, but one we have had from the beginning. (NLT)

Main Idea

John appeals to the church to keep the commandment to love one another. He is not giving them something new; this is the commandment they have had from the beginning.

Verse Explanation

“And now I plead with you, lady,”

“And now” moves the letter from John’s joy to his request. “Plead” translates a word meaning to ask or request. John has apostolic authority, yet he chooses to express his instruction as a personal appeal rather than a formal command. He addresses the church directly as “lady,” continuing the image from verse 1.

“not as though I wrote a new commandment to you,”

John is not adding a rule or introducing new teaching. This is worth noticing in a letter that will soon warn about those who bring a different doctrine (vv. 9–10). John’s instruction rests on what the church has already received.

“but that which we have had from the beginning:”

The command has been with them since their life in Christ began, when they first heard the gospel. It goes back to Jesus Himself, who gave it to His disciples on the night before His death: “A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another; as I have loved you, that you also love one another” (John 13:34). It was new when Jesus gave it. It is old to these believers because they have had it from the beginning. “From the beginning” is a fixed point John returns to in verse 6.

“that we love one another.”

This is the content of the command. John again includes himself: “we.” The love in view is the love of believers for one another within the family of God, modeled on Christ’s love for them (John 13:34; 1 John 3:16).

Study Notes

Old and new. John uses the same contrast in 1 John 2:7–8: “Brethren, I write no new commandment to you, but an old commandment which you have had from the beginning.” He then adds, “Again, a new commandment I write to you.” The Greek word for “new” (kainos) means new in kind or quality. The command was new when Jesus gave it because of its standard: “as I have loved you” (John 13:34).

“Plead.” The verb erōtaō means to ask or request. John uses the same verb in John 17 for Jesus’ requests to the Father on behalf of His disciples (John 17:9, 15, 20).

Spirit & Life

John is not writing anything new. He is repeating the oldest command the church has. Jerome records a tradition that in extreme old age John would say at each gathering only, “Little children, love one another.” When asked why, John answered that it was the Lord’s command, and if this alone were done, it would be enough. The church seldom needs a new commandment as much as it needs to keep the one it was given at the beginning.

John also pleads rather than commands, and he says “we,” not “you.” He stands under the same word he asks them to keep. A leader who appeals as someone who must also obey invites the church into shared obedience rather than demanding compliance from above.

2 John 1:6

NKJV

2 John 1:6 This is love, that we walk according to His commandments. This is the commandment, that as you have heard from the beginning, you should walk in it.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:6 Love means doing what God has commanded us, and he has commanded us to love one another, just as you heard from the beginning. (NLT)

Main Idea

John defines love as walking according to God’s commandments, and he identifies the commandment they have heard from the beginning as a call to walk in love. Genuine love is expressed through obedience, and obedience flowing from love is more than outward compliance.

Verse Explanation

“This is love,”

John now defines the love he has just asked for. He does not leave love to be defined by feeling or good intention.

“that we walk according to His commandments.”

Love shows itself in a way of life directed by God’s commands. Jesus said, “If you love Me, keep My commandments” (John 14:15). Love for God and for one another is lived out through obedience. That obedience is not mere rule-keeping. It flows from relationship with God, as Jesus promised: “If anyone loves Me, he will keep My word; and My Father will love him, and We will come to him and make Our home with him” (John 14:23).

“This is the commandment, that as you have heard from the beginning,”

John now turns the statement around. Having defined love by the commandments, he defines the commandment by love. The singular “commandment” brings John’s attention back to the command to love one another, while the plural “commandments” describes the broader life of obedience to God. John shows that love and obedience belong together. “As you have heard from the beginning” repeats the point of verse 5. John is calling them back to what they first received, not forward to something new.

“you should walk in it.”

“It” most naturally refers to love, the subject of this verse and the one before it. The commandment is to walk in love. John brings the two truths together. Genuine love is expressed through obedience to God, and the commandment he particularly emphasizes is that believers walk in love.

Study Notes

“Walk in it.” The Greek phrase en autē, “in it,” is feminine. Both “love” (agapē) and “commandment” (entolē) are feminine nouns in Greek, so the grammar allows either. Some interpreters read “walk in the commandment.” The context favors love, and the meaning is close under either reading, since the commandment is to love.

Love and commandments in John’s writings. John joins love and obedience repeatedly (John 14:15, 21; 15:10; 1 John 2:5; 5:3). 1 John 5:3 adds a note that guards against reading this as burdensome duty: “For this is the love of God, that we keep His commandments. And His commandments are not burdensome.”

Spirit & Life

John will not let love and obedience separate, and each keeps the other honest. Love that ignores God’s commandments becomes a feeling that bends to whatever seems kind in the moment. Obedience without love becomes outward compliance, doing the right things while the heart stays distant from God and from people.

This gives believers an honest measure of their love for God. The first question is not how strongly we feel toward Him but where our lives are going. A person may worship with deep feeling on Sunday and refuse to forgive a brother on Monday. Another may keep every outward rule while holding others at arm’s length. Neither is the walk John describes. And no one walks it alone. The Father and the Son make Their home with those who love Jesus and keep His word (John 14:23).

2 John 1:7–11: Beware of Deceivers

2 John 1:7

NKJV

2 John 1:7 For many deceivers have gone out into the world who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh. This is a deceiver and an antichrist.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:7 I say this because many deceivers have gone out into the world. They deny that Jesus Christ came in a real body. Such a person is a deceiver and an antichrist. (NLT)

Main Idea

John explains why this call matters now: many deceivers have gone out into the world who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh. Anyone who denies that the Son of God truly became human is a deceiver and an antichrist.

Verse Explanation

“For many deceivers have gone out into the world”

“For” ties the warning to what comes before it. The church must keep walking in truth and love because deceivers are at work. A deceiver is someone who leads others astray. John says there are “many,” so this is not an isolated case. They “have gone out into the world,” meaning they are traveling and spreading their teaching. That detail prepares for John’s instructions about receiving travelers into the house (v. 10). 1 John speaks of some who “went out from us” (1 John 2:19), a closely related situation. The letters do not tell us whether the same people are in view.

“who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh.”

To “confess” is to openly acknowledge and agree with what is true. The issue is the incarnation: the eternal Son of God truly became a human being with a real body. “The Word became flesh and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). Some early teachers claimed that the divine Christ only seemed to be human, or that He was separate from the man Jesus. If the Son of God did not truly become human, then He did not truly live, suffer, and die as one of us, and the gospel loses its foundation. The wider New Testament also shows that Jesus rose bodily and remains fully human as He reigns and intercedes for His people (Luke 24:39; 1 Timothy 2:5; Hebrews 7:24–25).

“This is a deceiver and an antichrist.”

John names anyone who denies this truth. “Antichrist” means one who opposes Christ, often by putting a false Christ in His place. In 1 John, John says that “even now many antichrists have come” (1 John 2:18) and that the spirit of the Antichrist is already at work (1 John 4:3). His statement here concerns the deceivers already active. John’s label applies to a denial of who Jesus Christ is. It does not authorize believers to call other Christians deceivers or antichrists because they disagree over spiritual gifts, worship practices, or other secondary matters.

Study Notes

Deceivers. The Greek word planos belongs to a word family associated with wandering and leading astray. The “wandering stars” of Jude 13 are planētai, the source of the English word “planet.” A deceiver leads others to wander from the truth.

“Gone out” or “entered into.” The NKJV reads “have gone out into the world,” while the KJV reads “are entered into the world,” reflecting different Greek readings. Both describe the activity of deceivers spreading their teaching.

“Coming” in the flesh. In 1 John 4:2 the confession is that Jesus Christ “has come” in the flesh. Here John uses a present participle, “coming.” Some interpreters take the present tense to describe the incarnation as an abiding fact, and a few refer it to Christ’s return. The tense alone should not be pressed. The continuing humanity of Jesus rests on the wider New Testament witness, not on this participle.

Antichrist. The word antichristos appears in the New Testament only in John’s letters (1 John 2:18, 22; 4:3; 2 John 7). The prefix anti can mean “against” or “in place of.” The Greek of verse 7 reads “the deceiver and the antichrist,” using the article to describe the type of person. The NKJV’s “a deceiver and an antichrist” conveys that sense. The expectation of a coming Antichrist is a broader conclusion from 1 John 2:18; 2 John does not establish it by itself.

Early church background. Later Christian writers describe teachings that denied the true humanity of Jesus. Docetism, from the Greek dokeō, “to seem,” held that Jesus only seemed to have a body. According to Irenaeus, Cerinthus taught that the divine Christ came upon the man Jesus at His baptism and left Him before the crucifixion. These accounts come from early church tradition. 2 John does not name either teaching or say which view the deceivers held.

Spirit & Life

A Savior who is not truly God would be a bridge broken at the far end. The error John confronts breaks the bridge at the near end. A Christ who only seemed human never truly reached us. He never truly shared our weakness, suffered, or died in our place.

The confession John defends is that God came all the way to where we are. From the garden to the tabernacle, God sought to dwell with His people, and in Jesus “the Word became flesh and dwelt among us” (John 1:14). He did not send a message about Himself from a safe distance. He took a body, touched the sick, wept at a grave, and bled on a cross. He did not lay that humanity aside when He rose (Luke 24:39; Hebrews 7:24–25). Any message that makes Jesus less than truly human takes away the nearness of God the gospel announces.

2 John 1:8

NKJV

2 John 1:8 Look to yourselves, that we do not lose those things we worked for, but that we may receive a full reward.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:8 Watch out that you do not lose what we have worked so hard to achieve. Be diligent so that you receive your full reward. (NLT)

Main Idea

John warns believers to watch themselves so that the spiritual work already accomplished among them is not lost and they may receive a full reward. Remaining watchful and faithful matters because deception can undo what has been built.

Verse Explanation

“Look to yourselves,”

John tells his readers to watch themselves. The first defense against deception is careful attention to their own faithfulness.

“that we do not lose those things we worked for,”

“We” includes John and those who labored with him in bringing the gospel to these believers and teaching them. The fruit of that labor was the faith, love, and obedience now present in the church, and it could be lost if the church were drawn away by deceivers. John’s concern here is the loss of what has been built through faithful labor. The verse is not a direct teaching on whether a believer can lose salvation, and it should not be used to settle that broader question. Verse 9 adds its own serious warning about anyone who does not abide in the doctrine of Christ.

“but that we may receive a full reward.”

“Reward” is the word for a worker’s wages. John wants the work to be carried through to its completion, with nothing lost and the full reward received. The verse does not describe what that reward is, and John’s point does not depend on knowing. Faithfulness should continue to the end.

Study Notes

“Look.” The Greek verb blepō is a present imperative, “keep watching,” describing ongoing watchfulness rather than a single glance.

“We” or “you.” The NKJV reads “we” throughout the verse. Many manuscripts read, “that you do not lose what we worked for, but that you may receive a full reward” (similar to the ESV). Under that reading, the believers are warned not to lose the fruit of the apostles’ labor among them. Under either reading, the same work is at stake.

Labor and wages. “Worked for” translates ergazomai, “to labor.” “Reward” (misthos) is a laborer’s pay (Matthew 20:8). Jesus used the same word for the reaper who “receives wages, and gathers fruit for eternal life” (John 4:36). “Receive” (apolambanō) can mean to receive in full. Paul uses related language for a worker’s labor being tested and rewarded (1 Corinthians 3:12–15).

Spirit & Life

Before John tells the church what to do about deceivers at the door, he says, “Look to yourselves.” Deception seldom wins by its own strength. It enters through unguarded places: spiritual boredom, a craving for something new, pride in being among the insightful few, or a grievance that makes a person ready to listen to anyone who will validate it. This order also exposes a counterfeit discernment that catalogs other Christians’ errors while ignoring its own drift. John’s warning begins with a mirror.

The goal is positive. John wants nothing lost and the reward received in full. God is not looking for reasons to withhold from His people. Watchfulness is care for something precious He has built, so that it is carried through to completion.

2 John 1:9

NKJV

2 John 1:9 Whoever transgresses and does not abide in the doctrine of Christ does not have God. He who abides in the doctrine of Christ has both the Father and the Son.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:9 Anyone who wanders away from this teaching has no relationship with God. But anyone who remains in the teaching of Christ has a relationship with both the Father and the Son. (NLT)

Main Idea

Whoever departs from the doctrine of Christ and does not abide in it does not have God, but whoever abides in it has both the Father and the Son. Remaining in the true teaching about Christ is inseparable from a real relationship with God.

Verse Explanation

“Whoever transgresses”

“Transgresses” means to step beyond a boundary. Many early manuscripts read a word meaning “goes ahead” (see Study Notes). Either reading describes a person who moves beyond the teaching of Christ, leaving it behind rather than remaining in it.

“and does not abide in the doctrine of Christ”

“Abide” returns from verse 2. The truth abides in believers, and believers are to abide in the doctrine of Christ. “Doctrine” means teaching. “The doctrine of Christ” can mean the teaching about Christ or the teaching Christ Himself gave. Here the immediate emphasis is the apostolic truth about Christ’s Person, especially that He came in the flesh (v. 7). That truth stands at the center of all Christ taught, though it does not exhaust His teaching. To abide in it is to remain in it and continue living in it.

“does not have God.”

In John’s writings, to “have” God means to have a real, living relationship with Him. Whoever leaves the truth about Christ does not have God, whatever spiritual claims he makes. No one can reject the true Son and still have the Father (John 14:6; 1 John 2:23).

“He who abides in the doctrine of Christ has both the Father and the Son.”

John now states the positive side. Fellowship with God comes through Christ Himself, and sound teaching reveals and guards the truth about the One through whom we know the Father. Those who remain in that truth have both the Father and the Son. “Both” shows that the Father cannot be had apart from the Son. The verse echoes the greeting, where grace, mercy, and peace come from the Father and from the Son together (v. 3).

Study Notes

“Transgresses” or “goes ahead.” The NKJV follows manuscripts reading parabainō, “to step over” or “go beyond.” Many early manuscripts read proagō, “to go before” or “lead forward,” which most modern translations follow (“goes too far,” NASB; “goes on ahead,” ESV). Some interpreters suggest the deceivers presented themselves as advanced beyond the apostles’ teaching. The letter does not say so.

Having the Father and the Son. John uses the same language in 1 John 2:23: “Whoever denies the Son does not have the Father either; he who acknowledges the Son has the Father also.” Compare 1 John 5:12: “He who has the Son has life.”

Spirit & Life

Not every new idea is growth. Moving beyond the teaching of Christ can feel like progress, but it is like a child who runs ahead of his father on a mountain trail. He does not arrive sooner; he gets lost. Believers keep growing in knowledge, love, and obedience, but they grow deeper into Christ, never beyond Him, because there is nothing beyond Him to reach.

The one who abides in the doctrine of Christ “has both the Father and the Son.” Holding to the truth about Jesus is not about winning arguments or protecting a tradition. It is about having God Himself. An accurate description matters when you are looking for someone. It does not replace the person, but a false one will lead you to someone else. A different Christ cannot bring anyone to the Father. The real Christ brings us to both the Father and the Son.

2 John 1:10

NKJV

2 John 1:10 If anyone comes to you and does not bring this doctrine, do not receive him into your house nor greet him;(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:10 If anyone comes to your meeting and does not teach the truth about Christ, don’t invite that person into your home or give any kind of encouragement. (NLT)

Main Idea

John instructs the church not to receive into their house or greet anyone who comes without this doctrine. In context, this means refusing hospitality and endorsement that would advance the ministry of traveling teachers who deny the truth about Christ, not withholding ordinary courtesy.

Verse Explanation

“If anyone comes to you”

First-century churches met in homes, and traveling teachers moved from church to church. They depended on believers to house them, feed them, and send them on their way (3 John 5–8). John has in view a teacher arriving to minister among them, not a passing visitor.

“and does not bring this doctrine,”

“This doctrine” is the doctrine of Christ just described (v. 9). The test John gives concerns the message the teacher carries.

“do not receive him into your house”

Receiving such a teacher meant giving him lodging, meals, access to the gathered believers, and help for his journey to the next church. For a teacher who denied Christ, that welcome would supply the resources, credibility, and opportunity needed to spread his message. John forbids giving that kind of support. His concern is not simply whom believers welcome into their homes, but what they help advance through that welcome. The underlying principle is that believers must not participate in advancing teaching that denies Jesus Christ.

John is not forbidding believers to associate with, speak to, or show ordinary kindness and hospitality toward people who do not share their faith. This passage must not be used to justify cutting off relationships with unbelieving family members or withdrawing love and kindness from people who believe differently.

“nor greet him;”

“Greet” translates the ordinary Greek greeting, which literally meant “rejoice.” In this context, greeting such a teacher would communicate welcome or approval that could assist his ministry. John’s concern is not ordinary courtesy but participation in advancing false teaching.

Study Notes

House churches. Paul greets several churches meeting in homes (Romans 16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19; Colossians 4:15; Philemon 2). If the elect lady represents a congregation, “your house” naturally refers to the household in which that church gathered.

Two comings. The verb “comes” (erchetai) is the same verb used for Jesus Christ “coming” in the flesh (v. 7). John measures the one who comes to the house by what he confesses about the One who came. The Greek construction (“if anyone comes”) assumes that such visits were a real possibility.

The Didache. This early Christian manual describes how churches evaluated traveling teachers and prophets. It distinguishes those who taught the truth from those who brought different teaching and warns against supporting false teachers (Didache 11). This illustrates the importance of discernment and hospitality in the early church.

Spirit & Life

John does not tell the church to evaluate the visitor’s warmth, gifting, eloquence, or reputation. He tells them to check what he brings. A teacher may be likable and gifted and still carry a message that leads people away from the true Christ. The Bereans received the word gladly, yet they still searched the Scriptures to see whether what Paul taught was so (Acts 17:11).

That test belongs before a church opens its pulpit, before a family welcomes a teacher’s influence into its home, and before believers recommend a ministry. Genuine discernment is not suspicion of every Christian who differs from us. John’s test concerns who Jesus is. It does not make deceivers of believers who disagree over spiritual gifts, worship practices, or secondary matters, and it does not forbid ordinary kindness toward people who believe differently.

2 John 1:11

NKJV

2 John 1:11 for he who greets him shares in his evil deeds.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:11 Anyone who encourages such people becomes a partner in their evil work. (NLT)

Main Idea

John gives the reason: the one who greets such a teacher shares in his evil deeds. Support that helps false teaching spread makes a person a participant in the harm it does.

Verse Explanation

“for he who greets him”

John now explains why he forbids the greeting. His warning concerns meaningful participation in advancing false teaching, not ordinary human relationships or every interaction with someone who believes differently.

“shares in his evil deeds.”

“Shares” translates a word related to “fellowship.” Welcoming and supporting such a teacher creates a partnership with him. His deeds are “evil” because his teaching leads people away from the true Christ and therefore away from God (v. 9). Support that helps that teaching spread makes the supporter a participant in the harm it does.

Study Notes

Sharing and fellowship. “Shares” (koinōneō) is the verb form of koinōnia, the word John uses in 1 John 1:3: “truly our fellowship is with the Father and with His Son Jesus Christ.” Paul gives a similar warning in 1 Timothy 5:22: “nor share in other people’s sins.”

Fellow workers or partners in evil. 3 John gives the other side of the same principle. Believers who receive and support true teachers “become fellow workers for the truth” (3 John 8). Support joins the giver to the work it advances, whether true or false.

Labor and deeds. “Deeds” (erga) is related to the verb “worked for” in verse 8. The two verses set side by side the labor that built up the church and the evil deeds of the deceiver that would tear it down.

Spirit & Life

“Shares” belongs to the same word family as “fellowship.” The bond that joins believers to the Father, the Son, and one another can be misplaced and join a person to someone else’s harmful work. What we help advance, we take part in.

Today this speaks less to a guest room than to the many other ways support is given. Money given to a ministry, a platform offered to a speaker, an endorsement, or a shared post can each help a message travel further. When that support knowingly advances teaching that denies Christ, we become participants in what we help spread. The question is not whether we are kind to people, which we always should be. It is whether we are helping a teaching that denies Christ reach more people.

2 John 1:12–13: Final Greetings

2 John 1:12

NKJV

2 John 1:12 Having many things to write to you, I did not wish to do so with paper and ink; but I hope to come to you and speak face to face, that our joy may be full.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:12 I have much more to say to you, but I don’t want to do it with paper and ink. For I hope to visit you soon and talk with you face to face. Then our joy will be complete. (NLT)

Main Idea

John has many more things to say but chooses not to write them with paper and ink. He hopes to come and speak face to face so that the joy he shares with them may be full.

Verse Explanation

“Having many things to write to you,”

The letter is short, but not because John had little to say.

“I did not wish to do so with paper and ink;”

“Paper” refers to papyrus, the common writing material of the day, made from the papyrus reed. John chooses not to put the rest of what he has to say in writing. He would rather say it in person.

“but I hope to come to you and speak face to face,”

John hopes to visit them. Being present allows for conversation, questions, encouragement, and personal care that a letter cannot fully give. He closes 3 John with nearly the same words (3 John 13–14).

“that our joy may be full.”

John states the purpose of his visit. Their joy will be full when they are together in the fellowship of the truth. “Our” joy is shared, belonging to John and the church alike. The letter that began with John’s great joy over believers walking in truth (v. 4) ends with his longing for full joy in their company. John uses the same language in 1 John 1:4, where fellowship “with the Father and with His Son Jesus Christ” (1 John 1:3) leads to joy made full. Jesus spoke of His own joy remaining in His disciples “that your joy may be full” (John 15:11).

Study Notes

Paper and ink. “Paper” (chartēs) appears only here in the New Testament and refers to a sheet of papyrus. Papyrus was made by laying strips of the reed’s pith crosswise, pressing them together, and drying them. “Ink” (melan) literally means “black.” Ink was commonly made from soot mixed with gum and water. John mentions “pen and ink” in 3 John 13.

“Face to face.” The Greek reads literally “mouth to mouth.” The Hebrew of Numbers 12:8, where God says He speaks with Moses “face to face” (NKJV), uses the same picture, literally “mouth to mouth.”

“Our joy” or “your joy.” The NKJV reads “our joy.” Some manuscripts read “your joy,” which a few translations follow (NASB). The difference is slight, since John’s hope is for joy shared in their fellowship.

“Full.” The Greek uses a perfect passive participle of plēroō, expressing joy brought to completion. Verse 8 uses a related adjective (plērēs) for the “full reward.” The two expressions are worth observing together, though John does not explicitly develop them as a separate theme.

Spirit & Life

John uses an expression that literally means “mouth to mouth,” emphasizing personal conversation. Similar imagery appears in Numbers 12:8, where God describes His direct communication with Moses, although that passage concerns a unique prophetic relationship. John had much more to say, and he wanted to say it in person. In a broader way, that desire reflects God’s own heart. God spoke through the prophets, through Scripture, and finally in His Son (Hebrews 1:1–2), and the story ends with His servants seeing His face (Revelation 22:4). He gives His Word in writing to lead His people to Himself.

Some things are better said in person. A page cannot carry a tone of voice, tears, or the chance to answer a question as it arises. Hard conversations and tender ones especially belong face to face whenever possible (Matthew 18:15). Believers still find some of their deepest joy in the presence of one another.

2 John 1:13

NKJV

2 John 1:13 The children of your elect sister greet you. Amen.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:13 Greetings from the children of your sister, chosen by God. (NLT)

Main Idea

John closes with greetings from the children of the lady’s elect sister, most likely the members of a sister church. The letter ends in fellowship among those who share the truth.

Verse Explanation

“The children of your elect sister”

The “elect sister” is most likely another church, probably the one where John was when he wrote. Her “children” are its members. The letter opened with the elect lady (v. 1) and closes with her elect sister, both described as chosen by God. The church John writes to belongs to a wider family of churches.

“greet you.”

After instructing the church not to greet a teacher who denies Christ (vv. 10–11), John passes on a greeting between believers who share the truth. The letter ends in warm fellowship, not suspicion.

“Amen.”

The letter closes with an affirmation of all that has been said (see Study Notes).

Study Notes

Greet. “Greet” here translates aspazomai, “to greet, welcome, or embrace,” a different Greek word from the greeting in verses 10–11.

The elect sister. If the lady is an individual, her sister sends no greeting herself, only her children, which some interpreters take to mean she was absent or no longer living. If the lady is a church, the sister is most naturally John’s own congregation, traditionally Ephesus.

“Amen.” The NKJV includes a closing “Amen.” Many manuscripts do not include it.

Spirit & Life

Only three verses after forbidding a greeting, John sends one. He is not closing the church’s doors to people. The door he closes in verse 10 protects the fellowship he celebrates here.

The sister church is “elect,” chosen by the same God who chose the lady. No local church is an orphan. Congregations in different places belong to one family because the same Father chose them. Greeting other churches, praying for them by name, and rejoicing in what God is doing among them are small but real expressions of that shared life.
