Chapter Study

2 John 1

Verse-by-Verse
2 John 1:1

NKJV

2 John 1:1 The Elder, To the elect lady and her children, whom I love in truth, and not only I, but also all those who have known the truth,(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:1 This letter is from John, the elder. I am writing to the chosen lady and to her children, whom I love in the truth — as does everyone else who knows the truth — (NLT)

Main Idea



John writes as a recognized spiritual leader to believers he deeply loves, emphasizing from the opening sentence that their relationship is grounded in the truth God has revealed.

Verse Explanation



“The Elder” is how John identifies himself. The title can refer to an older man, but here it carries the sense of a recognized spiritual leader with authority among the churches. John does not need to introduce himself by name. His readers evidently know who “the Elder” is and recognize his authority.

“To the elect lady and her children” identifies the recipients. There are two main possibilities. John may be writing to a particular Christian woman and her children, or he may be using “lady” figuratively for a local church and “her children” for the believers who belong to it. The church interpretation is likely, especially because John moves naturally between singular and plural forms of address and closes by referring to “the children of your elect sister” (v. 13), which likely describes another congregation.

The word “elect” means chosen. It points to these believers as people who belong to God because He has graciously called and chosen them to be His people. John is not merely addressing an organization or gathering; he is writing to people whose identity is rooted in God.

“Whom I love in truth” describes the character of John’s love for them. “Truth” here is not simply sincerity, as though John were saying, “I genuinely love you.” His love exists within their shared relationship to the truth God has revealed. In Scripture, truth is not something each person creates for himself or defines by personal experience. God is the source of truth, His Word is truth (John 17:17), and truth is ultimately revealed and centered in Jesus Christ. John loves these believers as people who have received and embraced that truth.

“And not only I, but also all those who have known the truth” expands this relationship beyond John personally. Other believers who know the truth share this love for them. To “know the truth” means more than possessing correct information. It describes people who have come to know and embrace what God has revealed and whose lives are characterized by it. Shared truth therefore creates genuine fellowship among believers, even among those who may not know one another personally.

Remark(s)



John immediately joins two ideas that will control the entire letter: truth and love. Biblical love is not separated from truth, and biblical truth is not meant to produce coldness toward people. Christian fellowship is built upon both. Believers love one another because they belong to the same Christ, have received the same truth, and are learning to live according to it.

2 John 1:2

NKJV

2 John 1:2 because of the truth which abides in us and will be with us forever:(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:2 because the truth lives in us and will be with us forever. (NLT)

Main Idea



John’s love for these believers is grounded in the truth they share—a truth that lives within believers and will remain with them forever.

Verse Explanation



“Because of the truth” explains why John and other believers love the “elect lady and her children” mentioned in verse 1. Their relationship is not based merely on friendship, personality, background, or common interests. They are joined together because they share the truth God has revealed. This does not mean John loves an abstract set of ideas more than people. It means the truth has brought them into a shared relationship with God and therefore into genuine fellowship with one another.

John’s use of “truth” should not be understood in the modern sense of something personal or self-defined—“my truth” as opposed to “your truth.” Biblical truth originates with God. God determines what is true, His Word is truth (John 17:17), and Jesus identifies Himself as “the truth” (John 14:6). Believers do not create truth; they receive what God has revealed, come to know it, and live according to it.

“Which abides in us” shows that truth is not merely something Christians agree with intellectually. “Abides” carries the idea of remaining, continuing, or dwelling. The truth has taken up a lasting place within God’s people. John frequently uses this language of abiding to describe an enduring spiritual reality rather than something temporary or superficial.

“And will be with us forever” emphasizes the permanence of that truth. Human opinions change, cultures redefine what they consider acceptable, and false teachers introduce new claims, but God’s truth does not expire or need to be replaced. This is especially important in 2 John because John will soon warn about deceivers who deny the truth about Jesus Christ. The church is not free to change what God has revealed in order to accommodate changing ideas.

There may also be a deeper personal dimension to John’s language. The truth that abides with believers cannot ultimately be separated from Jesus Christ, who is Himself “the truth,” or from the Holy Spirit, whom John elsewhere calls “the Spirit of truth” (1 John 4:6). John’s primary emphasis here, however, is that the truth believers have received is enduring and will remain with God’s people forever.

Remark(s)



Christian fellowship rests on something permanent. Believers may come from different backgrounds, cultures, and circumstances, but they are united by the truth God has revealed. Because that truth “abides in us” and “will be with us forever,” the foundation of genuine Christian fellowship does not change with the culture around it.

2 John 1:3

NKJV

2 John 1:3 Grace, mercy, and peace will be with you from God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father, in truth and love.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:3 Grace, mercy, and peace, which come from God the Father and from Jesus Christ — the Son of the Father — will continue to be with us who live in truth and love. (NLT)

Main Idea



John assures believers that God’s grace, mercy, and peace are theirs through the Father and Jesus Christ, and that these blessings are experienced within a life characterized by truth and love.

Verse Explanation



“Grace, mercy, and peace will be with you” is more than a conventional greeting. John expresses confidence in what God provides for His people. Grace is God’s undeserved favor and His generous provision toward those who could never earn it. Mercy emphasizes God’s compassion toward people in their weakness and need. Peace is the wholeness and settled well-being that comes from being brought into a right relationship with God. Together, these words describe God’s gracious care for His people.

The wording “will be with you” is unusually confident. John does not merely say that he hopes or wishes these blessings will come to them. He speaks with assurance because their source is God Himself. The believers are about to hear warnings concerning deception and false teaching, but they face those dangers with the continuing grace, mercy, and peace of God.

“From God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father” identifies the source of these blessings and makes a significant statement about Jesus. John places the Father and the Son together as the source of grace, mercy, and peace. Calling Jesus “the Son of the Father” also prepares for the central doctrinal issue of the letter. John will soon confront teachers who deny the truth about Jesus Christ. Christianity cannot retain the Father while rejecting or redefining the Son. The Father and the Son cannot be separated in this way.

“In truth and love” describes the sphere in which Christian life and fellowship are to exist. Truth is what God has revealed and made known, centered ultimately in Jesus Christ. It is not personal opinion or something individuals create for themselves. Love is the self-giving commitment to seek the good of others in accordance with God’s character and commands. John refuses to separate the two. Love does not require believers to compromise what God has revealed, and commitment to truth does not give believers permission to become harsh or unloving.

This pairing prepares the reader for everything that follows. John will call believers to love one another, but he will also warn them not to support teachers who deny the truth about Christ. Those instructions are not contradictory. They show what Christian life “in truth and love” actually looks like.

Remark(s)



John's opening greeting joins truth and love in a way that prepares us for the entire letter. Genuine Christian love remains faithful to what God has revealed, and God's truth teaches His people how to love. The grace, mercy, and peace that come from the Father and the Son characterize a people who hold firmly to the truth while genuinely loving one another.

2 John 1:4

NKJV

2 John 1:4 I rejoiced greatly that I have found some of your children walking in truth, as we received commandment from the Father.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:4 How happy I was to meet some of your children and find them living according to the truth, just as the Father commanded. (NLT)

Main Idea



John rejoices because he has found believers living according to the truth God has commanded, showing that truth is meant to direct the way God’s people actually live.

Verse Explanation



“I rejoiced greatly” expresses John’s deep joy over what he had discovered among these believers. His joy is not centered on their circumstances, success, or reputation, but on their faithfulness to God. For John, one of the greatest joys of spiritual leadership is seeing people not merely hear the truth but live according to it.

“That I have found some of your children” refers to people John had personally encountered or had reliable knowledge about. If the “elect lady” represents a local church, her “children” are members of that congregation. The word “some” does not necessarily imply that the others were living wrongly. John is describing those whom he had encountered and could personally testify were walking faithfully.

“Walking in truth” is a biblical way of describing a life governed by what God has revealed. To walk describes the ongoing direction and pattern of a person’s life, not a single act or moment. To walk “in truth” therefore means more than believing correct doctrine. It means allowing God’s revealed truth to direct what a person believes, chooses, values, and does.

This is important because “truth” can easily be reduced either to correct information or to personal sincerity. John means neither. Believers do not walk in “their own truth.” They walk according to the truth that comes from God. Truth is received from God, believed, and then lived. A person can claim to believe biblical truth, but John expects that truth to become visible in the direction of that person’s life.

“As we received commandment from the Father” establishes the authority behind this way of living. Walking in truth is not an optional expression of unusually devoted Christians. God the Father has commanded His people to live according to what He has revealed. John therefore connects truth with obedience. God does not reveal truth merely so that His people can know what is correct; He reveals truth so that they will live accordingly.

The phrase also prepares for John’s discussion of love in the following verses. Truth, obedience, and love belong together. The Father commands His people both to walk in truth and to love one another, so genuine Christian living cannot separate them.

Remark(s)



John's joy reveals an important mark of spiritual maturity: the truth we believe should become visible and real in the way we live. Knowing what Scripture teaches matters, but biblical truth is intended to transform how believers walk.

2 John 1:5

NKJV

2 John 1:5 And now I plead with you, lady, not as though I wrote a new commandment to you, but that which we have had from the beginning: that we love one another.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:5 I am writing to remind you, dear friends, that we should love one another. This is not a new commandment, but one we have had from the beginning. (NLT)

Main Idea



John urges believers to love one another, not as a new teaching, but as a command that has belonged to Christian life from the beginning.

Verse Explanation



“And now I plead with you, lady” shows the pastoral tone of John’s appeal. He writes with apostolic authority, yet he does not present the command harshly. The word translated “plead” carries the idea of urging, appealing, or encouraging. If “lady” represents the church, John is addressing the congregation personally as he calls them back to something foundational to their life together.

“Not as though I wrote a new commandment to you” makes clear that John is not introducing a new requirement. The command to love was already familiar to them. Jesus had commanded His disciples to love one another (John 13:34), and John repeatedly emphasized this command in his earlier writing. The issue is not discovering some new spiritual teaching but remaining faithful to what they had already received.

“But that which we have had from the beginning” points back to the message about Jesus that believers received when they first came to faith. In John’s writings, “from the beginning” often directs readers back to the original apostolic message about Christ rather than later teachings claiming to offer something new or more advanced. This becomes especially important because John will soon warn about deceivers who are departing from the truth about Jesus.

John’s point is that genuine Christianity does not need to move beyond the foundational teaching received from Christ and His apostles. The believers already possess the command that should govern their relationships.

“That we love one another” states the command directly. Biblical love is more than affection, friendliness, or accepting whatever another person chooses. It is a committed desire for another person’s good that reflects God’s character and acts according to His truth. Jesus made this kind of love a distinguishing mark of His disciples.

The words “one another” emphasize the mutual nature of this command. Christian love is not meant to flow in only one direction or to be practiced by a few unusually caring believers. It is to characterize the life of the entire believing community.

The placement of this command is significant. John has just spoken about walking in truth, and he now speaks about loving one another. The two belong together because the truth believers have received teaches them what genuine love looks like. Christian love therefore lives according to what God has revealed.

Remark(s)



John does not present spiritual maturity as constantly searching for something new. Sometimes maturity means returning to what Christ commanded “from the beginning” and living it faithfully. Loving one another is not an optional addition to sound doctrine; it is one of the clearest ways the truth believers confess becomes visible in their life together.

2 John 1:6

NKJV

2 John 1:6 This is love, that we walk according to His commandments. This is the commandment, that as you have heard from the beginning, you should walk in it.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:6 Love means doing what God has commanded us, and he has commanded us to love one another, just as you heard from the beginning. (NLT)

Main Idea



In modern culture, love is often understood primarily as affection, acceptance, affirmation, or strong feelings. John tells us what genuine love looks like: it walks according to God's commandments. Love is not something each person is free to define for themselves. God shows us what love is through His character, His Word, His Son, and His commands.

Verse Explanation



“This is love, that we walk according to His commandments.” John now defines the love he commanded in verse 5. In modern culture, love is often understood primarily as affection, acceptance, affirmation, or a strong emotional attachment. John gives love an objective standard: God’s commandments. Biblical love cannot be defined independently of what God has revealed.

To “walk according to His commandments” means to order the continuing direction of one’s life according to what God commands. “Walk” again describes an ongoing pattern of life rather than isolated acts of obedience. John is not teaching that believers earn God’s love by keeping commandments. He is explaining what love looks like when it is lived out. Love for God and love for others express themselves through obedience to God.

This also protects the meaning of love from becoming subjective. A person may sincerely believe that a particular action is loving, but sincerity alone does not determine whether it is love in the biblical sense. God defines love according to His own character and will. Therefore, something God has forbidden cannot become genuinely loving simply because a person feels compassionate in doing it.

“This is the commandment, that as you have heard from the beginning, you should walk in it.” John again emphasizes that he is not introducing something new. “From the beginning” points back to the apostolic teaching about Christ that these believers had received from the beginning of their Christian life. They do not need a newer or supposedly more advanced message. They need to continue living what they have already received.

The singular “commandment” gathers the matter into the central command to love one another, while “His commandments” shows that this love is expressed through obedience to everything God commands. Love and obedience therefore cannot be separated. God commands His people to love, and He defines how that love is to be lived.

The final phrase, “you should walk in it,” again makes love a way of life. Love is not merely something believers feel, discuss, or affirm. It is something they continually practice through lives ordered according to God’s will.

Remark(s)



John removes the tension people sometimes create between love and obedience. Biblical love does not ask believers to choose between loving people and obeying God. Obedience to God defines the path in which love walks. This becomes especially important in the verses that follow, where John will require believers to respond firmly to false teachers. Refusing to support teaching that denies Christ may appear unloving by some definitions of love, but John has already established that genuine love must remain within what God has commanded.

2 John 1:7

NKJV

2 John 1:7 For many deceivers have gone out into the world who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh. This is a deceiver and an antichrist.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:7 I say this because many deceivers have gone out into the world. They deny that Jesus Christ came in a real body. Such a person is a deceiver and an antichrist. (NLT)

Main Idea



John warns that many false teachers have entered the world denying the truth about Jesus Christ, making it essential for believers to hold firmly to the truth about who He is.

Verse Explanation



“For many deceivers have gone out into the world” explains why John has placed such strong emphasis on walking in truth and remaining in what believers heard from the beginning. The danger is not hypothetical. “Many deceivers” are actively spreading teaching that leads people away from the truth about Jesus.

A “deceiver” is someone who causes others to wander from what is true. John’s concern is therefore greater than ordinary disagreement or differences over secondary matters. These teachers are distorting truth at the center of the Christian faith: the identity of Jesus Christ.

“Who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh” identifies the specific error. To “confess” Jesus means more than simply speaking His name or acknowledging that He existed. It means openly affirming the truth about who He is.

“Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh” affirms the real incarnation of the Son of God. The eternal Son truly became human. Jesus did not merely appear to have a physical body, nor was “Christ” a spiritual power temporarily associated with the human Jesus. The One who is Jesus Christ truly came as a human being.

John had confronted the same issue in 1 John: “Every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is of God” (1 John 4:2). The wording here, “coming in the flesh,” emphasizes the same essential truth about Christ. Christianity rests upon the real incarnation of the Son of God.

This matters because denying Christ’s true humanity damages the gospel itself. If the Son of God did not truly become human, then He did not truly live among us, suffer in our humanity, shed His blood, die, and rise bodily as the incarnate Son. What someone believes about Jesus is therefore not a minor doctrinal detail. The identity of Christ stands at the heart of Christian faith.

“This is a deceiver and an antichrist.” John gives a strong description of anyone who teaches this denial. “Antichrist” here does not necessarily refer to the final end-times figure commonly called the Antichrist. John also uses the term more broadly for those who deny the truth about Christ and work against Him (1 John 2:18, 22).

The word therefore identifies the spiritual character of the teaching. A message may use Christian language and speak about Jesus, yet if it rejects the biblical truth about who Jesus is, John does not regard it as another legitimate form of Christianity. It is deception opposed to Christ.

Remark(s)



John makes the identity of Jesus a decisive test of Christian teaching. Not every message that speaks positively about Jesus teaches the biblical Jesus. The question is not merely, “Do they talk about Jesus?” but “Do they confess Jesus Christ as God has revealed Him?” Love does not require believers to treat every claim about Christ as equally true. The love John has just described walks according to God’s commands and therefore remains firmly joined to the truth about Jesus Christ.

2 John 1:8

NKJV

2 John 1:8 Look to yourselves, that we do not lose those things we worked for, but that we may receive a full reward.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:8 Watch out that you do not lose what we have worked so hard to achieve. Be diligent so that you receive your full reward. (NLT)

Main Idea



John warns believers to remain spiritually alert so that deception does not undo the progress already made and rob them of the full reward that comes from faithful perseverance.

Verse Explanation



“Look to yourselves” is a direct call to careful spiritual attention. The phrase means to watch yourselves, pay close attention, or be on guard. After warning about the “many deceivers” in verse 7, John tells believers that they cannot treat false teaching casually. They have a responsibility to guard what they believe and remain faithful to the truth they have received.

John’s warning also shows that spiritual deception is a genuine danger for believers. Past faithfulness does not remove the need for present vigilance. They must continue examining what they hear against the truth about Christ that was given “from the beginning.”

“That we do not lose those things we worked for” points to what had already been accomplished through the ministry of John and his fellow workers among these believers. There is a textual variation here, with some manuscripts reading essentially “what you have worked for.” Either reading makes the basic point clear: genuine spiritual labor and progress have already taken place, and John does not want false teaching to undermine it.

To “lose” what had been worked for does not necessarily mean losing salvation. The immediate issue is remaining faithful to apostolic truth in the face of deceivers. John is warning that believers can suffer real spiritual loss when they abandon or compromise what has been faithfully taught and built into their lives.

“But that we may receive a full reward” looks forward to God’s recognition of faithful service and perseverance. The New Testament regularly teaches that God rewards faithfulness (1 Corinthians 3:8, 14; 2 Corinthians 5:10). John wants the work begun among these believers to reach its intended completion rather than being damaged by deception.

A “full reward” therefore emphasizes finishing faithfully. John is not presenting eternal life as wages earned through human effort. Salvation is God’s gift. Reward, however, is connected with faithfulness in how believers live and serve after receiving that gift.

The verse places responsibility on believers without suggesting that they preserve themselves by their own strength. God’s truth abides in His people (v. 2), yet they are still commanded to “look to yourselves.” God’s keeping grace does not make spiritual vigilance unnecessary.

Remark(s)



John reminds believers that beginning well does not remove the need to finish well. Spiritual progress should never produce carelessness. What has taken years to build through truth, teaching, obedience, and faithful ministry can be seriously damaged when deception is welcomed or truth is abandoned. His concern is not merely that believers possess truth today, but that they remain faithful to it all the way to the end.

2 John 1:9

NKJV

2 John 1:9 Whoever transgresses and does not abide in the doctrine of Christ does not have God. He who abides in the doctrine of Christ has both the Father and the Son.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:9 Anyone who wanders away from this teaching has no relationship with God. But anyone who remains in the teaching of Christ has a relationship with both the Father and the Son. (NLT)

Main Idea



John warns that anyone who moves beyond and abandons the truth about Jesus Christ does not have God, while those who remain faithful to the truth about Jesus have fellowship with both the Father and the Son.

Verse Explanation



“Whoever transgresses and does not abide in the doctrine of Christ does not have God.” John now explains why the deception described in verse 7 is so serious. The word translated “transgresses” can carry the idea of going ahead, advancing, or moving beyond a boundary. In this context, John is describing someone who does not remain within the truth about Christ that the apostles taught.

This is especially important because false teachers often present their teaching as spiritual progress. They may claim to possess deeper knowledge, greater insight, or a more advanced understanding. John turns that claim upside down. Moving beyond what God has revealed about Christ is not spiritual advancement when it means leaving the truth behind.

To “abide” means to remain, continue, or stay. John has already said that the truth “abides in us” (v. 2). Now he describes the responsibility of believers to remain in “the doctrine of Christ.”

The word “doctrine” simply means teaching. It should not be understood as dry theological information disconnected from life. “The doctrine of Christ” most naturally refers here to the true apostolic teaching concerning Jesus Christ—who He is and what God has revealed about Him. The immediate context is verse 7, where the deceivers refuse to confess “Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh.” John is therefore concerned particularly with teaching that preserves the true identity of Jesus.

“Does not have God” exposes the seriousness of rejecting that teaching. Someone cannot abandon the truth about Jesus while claiming to maintain a proper relationship with God. John does not allow a person to say, in effect, “I believe in God; I simply understand Jesus differently.” The Father has revealed Himself through the Son, so rejecting the Son as He has truly been revealed is rejecting the God who sent Him.

“He who abides in the doctrine of Christ has both the Father and the Son.” John now states the positive side. Those who remain in the true teaching about Christ live in fellowship with both the Father and the Son.

The word “both” matters. John refuses to separate relationship with the Father from relationship with Jesus Christ. Christianity is not generic belief in God with Jesus added as an optional feature. The Father and the Son are inseparably joined in the salvation and fellowship believers have received.

This verse also explains why John will give such strong instructions concerning false teachers in verses 10–11. The issue is not a minor disagreement among Christians. The teachers John has in view have crossed a fundamental boundary by abandoning the apostolic truth about Jesus Christ.

Remark(s)



John exposes a dangerous idea: that moving beyond established Christian truth necessarily represents spiritual progress. Newer is not always deeper, and different is not always growth. When a teaching moves beyond what God has revealed and changes the identity of Jesus Christ, John calls believers not to advance with it but to remain in “the doctrine of Christ.” True spiritual maturity does not outgrow the truth about Jesus; it becomes increasingly rooted in it.

2 John 1:10

NKJV

2 John 1:10 If anyone comes to you and does not bring this doctrine, do not receive him into your house nor greet him;(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:10 If anyone comes to your meeting and does not teach the truth about Christ, don’t invite that person into your home or give any kind of encouragement. (NLT)

Main Idea



John tells believers not to provide the hospitality and support that would help false teachers spread teaching that rejects the truth about Jesus Christ.

Verse Explanation



“If anyone comes to you and does not bring this doctrine” continues directly from verse 9. “This doctrine” refers to the true teaching about Jesus Christ that John has just described—the apostolic truth about who Jesus is, including the confession that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh (v. 7). John is not talking about ordinary disagreements among Christians or differences over secondary matters. He is addressing teachers whose message departs from essential truth about Christ.

“Do not receive him into your house” must be understood against the setting of the early church. Traveling Christian teachers and missionaries depended heavily on hospitality. Inns could be expensive, unsafe, or morally questionable, so believers often opened their homes to traveling ministers. Churches also commonly met in homes. Receiving a teacher could therefore involve much more than giving someone a meal or a place to sleep. It could provide lodging, financial assistance, a meeting place, credibility, and access to other believers.

John is not establishing a general prohibition against showing ordinary kindness to unbelievers or people with false beliefs. His concern is participation in the spread of false teaching. To receive these particular teachers in the normal ministry-hospitality setting could materially assist their mission and communicate that the church recognized them as legitimate Christian teachers.

“Nor greet him” can sound unusually severe to modern readers if “greet” is understood as simply saying hello. That is probably not John’s point. In this setting, a greeting could communicate welcome, approval, fellowship, or endorsement. John is warning believers not to give false teachers a reception that identifies them as accepted Christian workers.

This instruction must also be read alongside John’s repeated command to love. John has not suddenly abandoned love in order to defend truth. He has already defined love as walking according to God’s commandments (v. 6). Christian love therefore does not require believers to help someone spread teaching that leads others away from Christ.

Remark(s)



John draws an important distinction between loving a person and supporting that person’s ministry or message. Believers can treat people with dignity and kindness without providing the resources, platform, credibility, or endorsement that enables false teaching to spread. In John’s setting, opening one’s home could directly advance a traveling teacher’s ministry. His command is therefore not about refusing basic courtesy; it is about refusing to become a partner in spreading teaching that denies the truth about Jesus Christ.

2 John 1:11

NKJV

2 John 1:11 for he who greets him shares in his evil deeds.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:11 Anyone who encourages such people becomes a partner in their evil work. (NLT)

Main Idea



John explains that knowingly supporting or endorsing teachers who deny the truth about Christ can make believers participants in the spread of their harmful work.

Verse Explanation



“For he who greets him” explains the reason for John’s command in verse 10. As the previous verse makes clear, “greet” should not be reduced to the modern idea of simply saying hello or treating someone politely. In the setting of traveling teachers and house churches, receiving and welcoming a teacher could communicate acceptance, fellowship, and support for his ministry.

John is not forbidding ordinary kindness toward people who believe differently. His concern is a welcome that helps a false teacher function as a recognized Christian worker. Such support could include providing lodging, resources, access to the congregation, or credibility within the Christian community.

“Shares in his evil deeds” states the danger. The word translated “shares” carries the idea of participating in, having fellowship with, or becoming a partner in something. A believer does not have to personally teach false doctrine to contribute to its spread. Providing meaningful support or endorsement to the person spreading it can make that believer a participant in the work being accomplished.

The phrase “evil deeds” includes the destructive ministry of the deceivers John has been describing. They deny the truth about Jesus Christ (v. 7), refuse to remain in the teaching of Christ (v. 9), and seek opportunities to spread their message. Their teaching is not merely an intellectual mistake. Because it leads people away from the true Christ, John regards the work itself as evil.

This principle extends beyond first-century hospitality. The particular form of support may change, but the underlying issue remains: believers should consider whether their actions are helping advance teaching that contradicts the truth about Christ. Support, endorsement, access, resources, or a platform can communicate partnership even when a person does not personally agree with everything being taught.

John’s instruction must still be kept within its context. He is addressing serious false teaching about the identity of Jesus, not every theological disagreement among Christians. Applying this verse to secondary doctrinal differences would extend John’s warning beyond the situation he is addressing.

Remark(s)



John shows that partnership carries responsibility. Believers must consider not only what they personally teach but also whether their support is helping serious false teaching about Christ spread. His concern is not association with people who are wrong or separation over ordinary Christian disagreements, and disagreements in the non-essentials of the faith . It is becoming a participant in work that leads people away from the true Jesus Christ.

2 John 1:12

NKJV

2 John 1:12 Having many things to write to you, I did not wish to do so with paper and ink; but I hope to come to you and speak face to face, that our joy may be full.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:12 I have much more to say to you, but I don’t want to do it with paper and ink. For I hope to visit you soon and talk with you face to face. Then our joy will be complete. (NLT)

Main Idea



John chooses not to communicate everything by letter because personal, face-to-face fellowship can accomplish something written words cannot and can bring fuller joy to believers.

Verse Explanation



“Having many things to write to you” shows that John has more he wants to communicate than what appears in this short letter. Second John is brief not because John has exhausted everything these believers need to hear, but because he deliberately limits what he puts in writing.

“I did not wish to do so with paper and ink” refers to the normal writing materials of John’s day. “Paper” most likely refers to a sheet made from papyrus, while ink was commonly made from a dark pigment mixed with a binding agent. Letters allowed teaching and communication across significant distances, but John recognizes their limitations.

His statement is especially interesting because John clearly values written communication—he is writing this letter precisely because its warnings about truth, love, and false teaching matter. Yet he does not treat written words as a complete substitute for personal presence.

“But I hope to come to you and speak face to face” expresses John’s desire for direct personal fellowship. The expression translated “face to face” is literally “mouth to mouth,” an idiom describing direct, personal conversation. John wants to be physically present so they can speak with one another rather than continuing only through written correspondence.

This would allow something a letter cannot fully provide. Questions could be asked, misunderstandings clarified, concerns discussed, encouragement given, and fellowship enjoyed personally. John’s ministry is not merely the transfer of information. Truth is taught within relationships among God’s people.

“That our joy may be full” gives the purpose of John’s hoped-for visit. “Full” carries the idea of being brought to completeness or fullness. Their shared joy already exists, but personal fellowship would bring that joy to a fuller expression.

The phrase “our joy” is significant. John does not envision only his own joy or theirs. Christian fellowship produces shared joy because believers are joined together in Christ and in the truth they have received.

Remark(s)



John values written teaching, but he also recognizes its limits. Some things are best communicated “face to face.” Christian life is not merely receiving biblical information; it includes relationships in which truth can be discussed, clarified, encouraged, and lived together. For John, truth and fellowship belong together, and personal fellowship can bring a fullness of joy that communication from a distance cannot completely reproduce.

2 John 1:13

NKJV

2 John 1:13 The children of your elect sister greet you. Amen.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:13 Greetings from the children of your sister, chosen by God. (NLT)

Main Idea



John closes the letter with greetings from another community of believers, reinforcing the shared identity and fellowship that unite God’s people in the truth.

Verse Explanation



“The children of your elect sister greet you.” John ends the letter using the same family language with which he began it. In verse 1 he addressed “the elect lady and her children,” and now he sends greetings from “the children of your elect sister.”

If, as is likely, the “elect lady” represents a local church, then the “elect sister” represents another local church, probably the congregation where John was when he wrote. Her “children” would therefore be the believers who belonged to that congregation. The parallel wording between verses 1 and 13 strengthens this interpretation, although it remains possible that John is speaking about two actual Christian women and their families.

The word “elect” means chosen. As in verse 1, it identifies these believers as people who belong to God because He has chosen them to be His people. Different congregations may meet in different places, but they share the same fundamental identity as God’s people.

“Greet you” here is a normal expression of Christian affection and fellowship. This is worth noticing because John used similar language very differently in verses 10–11. There he warned believers not to “greet” teachers who deny the truth about Christ in a way that would welcome, endorse, or support their ministry. Here the greeting is entirely appropriate because it expresses genuine fellowship between believers who share the truth.

The contrast helps clarify John’s teaching. He is not opposed to hospitality, welcome, or fellowship. He is protecting genuine Christian fellowship from being extended in a way that supports teaching opposed to Christ. Fellowship has meaning precisely because it is grounded in a shared relationship with the true Jesus.

Remark(s)



The letter begins and ends with believers connected across distance by the truth they share. John has warned strongly about false fellowship, but his purpose is not isolation. He wants to protect true fellowship—believers loving, welcoming, and encouraging one another because they belong to God and remain in the truth about Jesus Christ.
