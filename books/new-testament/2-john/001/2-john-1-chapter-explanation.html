2 John 1

Verse-by-Verse
2 John 1:1
2 John 1:1
NKJV

2 John 1:1 The Elder, To the elect lady and her children, whom I love in truth, and not only I, but also all those who have known the truth,(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:1 This letter is from John, the elder. I am writing to the chosen lady and to her children, whom I love in the truth — as does everyone else who knows the truth — (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder, who does not give his name, writes to “the elect lady and her children,” most likely a local church chosen by God and its members, though the words could also describe a Christian woman and her family. He loves them “in truth,” and so do all who have known the truth, because the truth that joins believers to Christ also joins them to one another.

Verse Explanation

The Elder, who does not give his name, writes to “the elect lady and her children,” most likely a local church chosen by God and its members, though the words could also describe a Christian woman and her family. He loves them “in truth,” and so do all who have known the truth, because the truth that joins believers to Christ also joins them to one another.

“The Elder,”

The writer gives no name, only a title. The Greek word for “elder” means an older man, and in the early church it also became the title of a church leader. Peter called himself “a fellow elder” (1 Peter 5:1). The first readers knew exactly who “the Elder” was, and the title alone carried the weight of a trusted leader writing to people he knew. The church has long identified him as John the apostle, and the letter’s language closely matches John’s Gospel and 1 John. Third John opens with the same title (3 John 1).

“To the elect lady and her children,”

“Elect” means chosen by God. The New Testament uses the word for believers whom God has called to belong to Himself, as when Peter writes to those who are “elect according to the foreknowledge of God the Father” (1 Peter 1:2). The Greek word for “lady” is the feminine form of the word for “lord,” a respectful way to address a woman.

The elect lady is most likely a local church, addressed as a woman, and “her children” are its members. The letter’s closing greeting from “the children of your elect sister” (verse 13) fits one congregation greeting another, and the way the Elder moves between addressing the lady and addressing a whole group of people supports the same reading. The words could also describe an individual Christian woman and her family. Either way, the Elder writes to people God has chosen and drawn into His family.

“whom I love in truth,”

The Elder’s love is genuine, not a polite formula at the head of a letter. It is also love grounded in the truth revealed in and about Jesus Christ. He does not love these believers because they are pleasant or because they treat him well. He loves them because the same truth of Christ has laid hold of him and of them, and that truth is the bond between them.

“and not only I, but also all those who have known the truth,”

This love is not the Elder’s alone. Everyone who has come to know the truth shares it. To have “known the truth” is more than having learned facts about Jesus. It is to have come to know the One who said, “I am the way, the truth, and the life” (John 14:6), and to go on knowing Him. This congregation did not stand alone. Believers far beyond their city loved them, because the same truth had brought them all into one family.

Study Notes

The opening names neither the sender nor the recipient. The Elder has traditionally been identified as John the apostle.

The church historian Eusebius, writing in the fourth century, quoted the early bishop Papias, who listed John among the Lord’s disciples and then separately mentioned “the elder John.” Eusebius concluded that there were two Johns and suggested that the second was probably the one who saw the Revelation.

Some scholars follow Eusebius and assign 2 and 3 John to this elder. Others read Papias as speaking of the same John twice, once among the apostles and once as still living. The traditional identification remains well supported, but the question is debated.

The Greek phrase for “elect lady” is eklektē kyria. Some have taken one of these words as a personal name, either “the lady Electa” or “the elect Kyria.” Kyria is found as a woman’s name, though rarely and mostly in later sources. “Electa” faces a difficulty, because verse 13 calls the sister “elect” as well, so both sisters would bear the same name.

The grammar also bears on the question. The lady is addressed with a singular “you” in verses 4–5 and 13, but verses 6, 8, 10, and 12 use a plural “you,” as if a group of people were being addressed under one name.

2 John 1:2
2 John 1:2
NKJV

2 John 1:2 because of the truth which abides in us and will be with us forever:(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:2 because the truth lives in us and will be with us forever. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder gives the reason for this shared love: “the truth which abides in us and will be with us forever.” The truth revealed in Jesus Christ has made its home in believers and will never leave them, and that living, lasting truth is the ground of their love for one another.

Verse Explanation

The Elder gives the reason for this shared love: “the truth which abides in us and will be with us forever.” The truth revealed in Jesus Christ has made its home in believers and will never leave them, and that living, lasting truth is the ground of their love for one another.

“because of the truth”

The Elder now gives the reason for the shared love of verse 1. It rests on “the truth.” In John’s writings truth is more than accurate information. It is the reality God has made known in Jesus Christ. The Word who became flesh was “full of grace and truth” (John 1:14), and Jesus said of Himself, “I am the way, the truth, and the life” (John 14:6). The truth the Elder speaks of is the apostolic message about Jesus, and it cannot be separated from Jesus Himself.

That is why believers who had never met could love one another. Their bond did not depend on shared background, personality, or local friendship. It rested on the same Lord and the same truth about Him.

“which abides in us”

The Greek word translated “abides” means to remain, to stay, or to make one’s home. It is one of John’s favorite words. Jesus used it when He said, “Abide in Me, and I in you” (John 15:4). The truth has come to live in believers, and it goes on living in them. The words closely parallel Jesus’ promise of another Helper, “that He may abide with you forever,” whom He called “the Spirit of truth” (John 14:16–17). The Elder does not name the Spirit here, but the parallel helps the reader see that the truth of Christ is not a lifeless idea stored in the mind. It dwells in God’s people.

The Elder says “us,” including himself with the believers he writes to. The same truth lives in him and in members of a church far from where he is, and that is what makes them one family.

“and will be with us forever:”

The truth that lives in believers is not temporary. Opinions change and teachers come and go, but “the word of our God stands forever” (Isaiah 40:8). John drew the same contrast in his first letter: “the world is passing away, and the lust of it; but he who does the will of God abides forever” (1 John 2:17). Because the truth will be with believers forever, the love that grows from it rests on something that will never pass away.

2 John 1:3
2 John 1:3
NKJV

2 John 1:3 Grace, mercy, and peace will be with you from God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father, in truth and love.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:3 Grace, mercy, and peace, which come from God the Father and from Jesus Christ — the Son of the Father — will continue to be with us who live in truth and love. (NLT)

Main Idea

The greeting becomes a confident promise: grace, mercy, and peace “will be with you” from God the Father and from “the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father.” These gifts come from the Father and the Son together, and they come “in truth and love,” the two words the rest of the letter will develop.

Verse Explanation

The greeting becomes a confident promise: grace, mercy, and peace “will be with you” from God the Father and from “the Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father.” These gifts come from the Father and the Son together, and they come “in truth and love,” the two words the rest of the letter will develop.

“Grace, mercy, and peace”

Grace is God’s kindness given freely to people who have not earned it. Mercy is His compassion toward the guilty and helpless, meeting them in their need instead of giving them what they deserve. Peace carries the fullness of the Hebrew idea of shalom: not only the end of hostility with God but the wholeness and well-being that come from being right with Him. Grace is the source, mercy is that grace reaching people in their need, and peace is what it produces in their lives. Paul uses the same three words when he greets Timothy (1 Timothy 1:2; 2 Timothy 1:2).

“will be with you”

Most New Testament letters open with a prayer that grace and peace would be given. The Elder speaks with confidence instead. These blessings “will be with you.” He is not hoping God might be kind to these believers; he is assuring them that God’s grace, mercy, and peace will remain with those who belong to the truth. That assurance matters in a letter that will soon warn about deceivers. The church’s security rests on what God will surely give.

“from God the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ,”

Only God can give grace and mercy, and the Elder names two sources, the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. Repeating the word “from” places Jesus beside the Father as the giver of these blessings, not merely the channel through which they pass. That is fitting only because Jesus is Himself God. As John’s Gospel opens, “the Word was God” (John 1:1).

“the Son of the Father,”

In the NKJV this exact phrase appears only here. The Elder could have stopped with the name “the Lord Jesus Christ,” but he adds this description to stress the relationship between Jesus and the Father. Jesus is not a lesser messenger sent from God. He is the Father’s own Son, and through Him the Father is made known (John 1:18; 14:9). This careful wording prepares for the danger the Elder will soon address, teachers who deny the truth about Jesus Christ, when in fact the Son and the Father cannot be separated (verse 9).

“in truth and love.”

Grace, mercy, and peace are received within the truth revealed in and about Jesus Christ, and they show themselves in love. The Elder joins the two words here, and the rest of the letter develops them: truth lived in love (verses 4–6) and truth guarded (verses 7–11). Truth and love are not rival values to be balanced against each other. Believers live out the truth about Christ by loving one another.

2 John 1:4–6 — Walking in Truth and in Love

2 John 1:4
2 John 1:4
NKJV

2 John 1:4 I rejoiced greatly that I have found some of your children walking in truth, as we received commandment from the Father.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:4 How happy I was to meet some of your children and find them living according to the truth, just as the Father commanded. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder rejoices greatly that he has found some of the lady’s children “walking in truth,” living day by day in line with what God has revealed, just as the Father commanded. He speaks of those he has met; his words do not imply that the others had turned away.

Verse Explanation

The Elder rejoices greatly that he has found some of the lady’s children “walking in truth,” living day by day in line with what God has revealed, just as the Father commanded. He speaks of those he has met; his words do not imply that the others had turned away.

“I rejoiced greatly”

The body of the letter begins with joy. The Elder uses the same words in 3 John: “For I rejoiced greatly when brethren came and testified of the truth that is in you, just as you walk in the truth” (3 John 3), and he adds, “I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth” (3 John 4). Nothing gladdened him more than believers who stayed faithful. His joy also shows his heart toward this church. He writes as one who loves them, not as a distant critic, and the warnings that follow come from that same love.

“that I have found some of your children”

The “children” are the members of the church addressed as the lady. The Elder does not say how he “found” them. He may have met some of them as they traveled or received reports about them, but the letter does not explain. “Some” refers to those he had come to know. It does not imply that the rest had turned away. Still, the warnings that follow show that the church faced real danger, so news that some members were standing firm was especially welcome.

“walking in truth,”

In Scripture, “walking” describes a person’s whole way of life, how he conducts himself day by day. “Enoch walked with God” (Genesis 5:24), and the psalmist prayed, “Teach me Your way, O LORD; I will walk in Your truth” (Psalm 86:11). John uses the word the same way: whoever says he abides in Christ “ought himself also to walk just as He walked” (1 John 2:6). The Greek form describes something ongoing. These believers were steadily living this way, not doing so only now and then.

To walk in truth is to live in line with the truth revealed in and about Jesus Christ. It includes believing that truth, but it reaches into daily conduct, so that what believers confess about Jesus is visible in how they live. That is what gave the Elder such joy.

“as we received commandment from the Father.”

Walking in truth is not a way of life believers invent for themselves. It is obedience to what the Father has commanded. “We” places the Elder alongside his readers under the same command from the same Father. John states that command plainly in his first letter: “And this is His commandment: that we should believe on the name of His Son Jesus Christ and love one another, as He gave us commandment” (1 John 3:23). Believing the truth about the Son and loving one another belong to one command, which the next two verses take up.

2 John 1:5 I am writing to remind you, dear friends, that we should love one another. This is not a new commandment, but one we have had from the beginning. (NLT)

Main Idea

With personal warmth the Elder pleads with the lady, not to introduce a new commandment but to renew the one they have had “from the beginning”: “that we love one another.” This is Jesus’ own command, heard when their life in Christ began, and by saying “we” the Elder places himself under it as well.

Verse Explanation

With personal warmth the Elder pleads with the lady, not to introduce a new commandment but to renew the one they have had “from the beginning”: “that we love one another.” This is Jesus’ own command, heard when their life in Christ began, and by saying “we” the Elder places himself under it as well.

“And now I plead with you, lady,”

The Elder turns from his joy over the faithful children to a personal appeal. The Greek word translated “plead” usually means to ask or request. He could simply have given orders. Instead he speaks as one who loves this church and wants its members to respond willingly. The appeal is addressed to the “lady,” and through her it reaches all her children together.

“not as though I wrote a new commandment to you,”

The Elder is not adding anything to what they already believe. John made the same point in his first letter: “Brethren, I write no new commandment to you, but an old commandment which you have had from the beginning” (1 John 2:7). The command had been new when Jesus gave it on the night before His death: “A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another; as I have loved you, that you also love one another” (John 13:34). It was new because Jesus’ own love set its measure. By the time of this letter it had been part of Christian life for decades. It is old because it has always belonged to the faith, not because it has stopped mattering.

“but that which we have had from the beginning:”

“From the beginning” points back to the time these believers first received the gospel. Love for one another was not added to the message later. It was there from the start: “For this is the message that you heard from the beginning, that we should love one another” (1 John 3:11). The Elder says “we,” not “you,” placing himself under the same command as the newest believer.

This matters because others were coming with a message that departed from what believers first received (verse 7). The Elder does not ask his readers to move on to something fresh. He asks them to hold on to what they already have.

“that we love one another.”

This is love within the family of believers. Jesus made it the mark of His followers: “By this all will know that you are My disciples, if you have love for one another” (John 13:35). John shows what it looks like: “By this we know love, because He laid down His life for us. And we also ought to lay down our lives for the brethren” (1 John 3:16). It is more than warm feeling or kind words, for John also wrote, “let us not love in word or in tongue, but in deed and in truth” (1 John 3:18).

The Elder has just rejoiced over children walking in truth (verse 4), and now he places love beside that faithfulness. A church that holds firmly to the truth about Jesus must also love one another, because loving one another was part of that truth from the start. A familiar command can still be neglected, so he pleads with them to keep practicing it.

NKJV

2 John 1:6 This is love, that we walk according to His commandments. This is the commandment, that as you have heard from the beginning, you should walk in it.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:6 Love means doing what God has commanded us, and he has commanded us to love one another, just as you heard from the beginning. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder now defines the love he has pleaded for: “This is love, that we walk according to His commandments.” Love for God and for one another shows itself in obedience to what God has said, and the commandment they have heard from the beginning is that they keep walking in that love.

Verse Explanation

The Elder now defines the love he has pleaded for: “This is love, that we walk according to His commandments.” Love for God and for one another shows itself in obedience to what God has said, and the commandment they have heard from the beginning is that they keep walking in that love.

“This is love, that we walk according to His commandments.”

In verse 5 the Elder asked the church to love one another. Here he says what that love is. To “walk” is to conduct one’s life, and the word points to a steady way of living rather than an occasional act. Love is seen in a life lived the way God has told His people to live. “His commandments” are the Father’s, since verse 4 spoke of the commandment received “from the Father,” and again the Elder includes himself in “we.”

Jesus taught His disciples the same thing: “If you love Me, keep My commandments” (John 14:15). John repeats it: “For this is the love of God, that we keep His commandments. And His commandments are not burdensome” (1 John 5:3). Love does not shrink to rule-keeping, because it flows from relationship with God, yet it cannot be separated from obeying Him. In verse 4 the faithful were “walking in truth”; here the same walk is described as walking “according to His commandments.” The life that holds to the truth is the life that loves, and both are seen in obedience.

“This is the commandment, that as you have heard from the beginning, you should walk in it.”

The Elder moves from many “commandments” to one “commandment.” All of God’s instructions come together in a single call. “As you have heard from the beginning” means since they first received the faith, so once more he is calling them to keep living by what they were first taught. “Walk in it” most naturally means walk in love, though the wording could point back to the commandment itself. The meaning is the same either way, because the Elder has bound the two together so tightly. Love is shown by keeping God’s commandments, and the commandment is to live a life of love.

2 John 1:7–9 — Abide in the Doctrine of Christ

2 John 1:7
2 John 1:7
NKJV

2 John 1:7 For many deceivers have gone out into the world who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh. This is a deceiver and an antichrist.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:7 I say this because many deceivers have gone out into the world. They deny that Jesus Christ came in a real body. Such a person is a deceiver and an antichrist. (NLT)

Main Idea

The opening “For” ties the warning to the call to love, because many deceivers have gone out into the world who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh. To deny that the Son of God truly became human strikes at the heart of the faith, and the Elder calls the one who teaches it “a deceiver and an antichrist.”

Verse Explanation

The opening “For” ties the warning to the call to love, because many deceivers have gone out into the world who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh. To deny that the Son of God truly became human strikes at the heart of the faith, and the Elder calls the one who teaches it “a deceiver and an antichrist.”

“For many deceivers have gone out into the world”

The opening “For” links this warning to verses 4–6. The Elder has called the church to keep walking in the commandment they heard from the beginning, because there were people actively trying to lead them away from it. The Greek word for “deceivers” describes those who lead others astray, causing them to wander from the right path. They were not merely mistaken. They were spreading their error and drawing others after them.

Jesus had warned that “many false prophets will rise up and deceive many” (Matthew 24:11), and John uses almost the same words in his first letter: “many false prophets have gone out into the world” (1 John 4:1). “Have gone out” may also recall those of whom John wrote, “They went out from us, but they were not of us” (1 John 2:19). The Elder does not name these teachers or describe their whole system, and their identity is not known. What marks them is the denial he names next.

“who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh.”

To “confess” is to acknowledge openly what is true. These deceivers would not acknowledge that Jesus Christ came as a real human being. “Flesh” here means a genuine human body and a real human life. John’s Gospel declares, “And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us” (John 1:14), and 1 John begins with what the apostles had heard, seen, and handled with their own hands (1 John 1:1). Jesus was not a spirit who only seemed human.

This was no minor disagreement. The eternal Son of God took on real flesh and blood so that He could die for sinners. Because God’s children share in flesh and blood, Jesus “Himself likewise shared in the same, that through death He might destroy him who had the power of death, that is, the devil” (Hebrews 2:14). If Jesus did not truly become human, He did not truly die, and His blood did not truly pay for sin. John gives the same test in his first letter: “every spirit that does not confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is not of God” (1 John 4:3).

“This is a deceiver and an antichrist.”

The Elder moves from “many deceivers” to “this,” meaning that anyone who teaches this denial fits the description. He gives two names. A “deceiver” leads people away from the truth. An “antichrist” stands against Christ and puts something false in His place. In the New Testament the word appears only in John’s letters. The believers had heard “that the Antichrist is coming,” and John told them, “even now many antichrists have come” (1 John 2:18). The spirit of the Antichrist “is now already in the world” (1 John 4:3). The Elder does not say that each of these teachers is the coming Antichrist. He means that their teaching already carries the same opposition to Christ. To deny who Jesus truly is, however religious the teacher may sound, is to work against Christ Himself.

Study Notes

The wording here differs from the parallel test in 1 John 4:2. There the Greek wording, “has come in the flesh,” describes a completed event with lasting results. Here the wording literally means “Jesus Christ coming in flesh.”

A few interpreters have taken the present tense as a reference to Christ’s future return in bodily form. This Greek verb form can, however, describe who someone is rather than when an action happens, as in the question put to Jesus, “Are You the Coming One, or do we look for another?” (Matthew 11:3). The close link with 1 John 4:2 makes the incarnation the most natural meaning, though the present tense alone does not settle the question.

Who these teachers were is uncertain. Later writers describe teachings that would fit the Elder’s warning. One held that Christ only seemed to have a body, a view later called Docetism, from the Greek word for “to seem.” Another was attributed to Cerinthus, who, according to Irenaeus, taught that a divine Christ came upon the man Jesus after His baptism and left Him before the crucifixion. Either view would deny what the Elder insists on, but the letter gives too little detail to tie the deceivers to any known group.

2 John 1:8
2 John 1:8
NKJV

2 John 1:8 Look to yourselves, that we do not lose those things we worked for, but that we may receive a full reward.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:8 Watch out that you do not lose what we have worked so hard to achieve. Be diligent so that you receive your full reward. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because the danger is real, the Elder tells them, “Look to yourselves.” If they are drawn away, the fruit of the labor shared by the Elder and these believers could be lost, but his desire is that “we may receive a full reward,” though the verse does not define exactly what that reward or that loss will be.

Verse Explanation

Because the danger is real, the Elder tells them, “Look to yourselves.” If they are drawn away, the fruit of the labor shared by the Elder and these believers could be lost, but his desire is that “we may receive a full reward,” though the verse does not define exactly what that reward or that loss will be.

“Look to yourselves,”

This is a call to be on guard. The command is plural, so the Elder is speaking to all the members of the church. Having just warned about deceivers (verse 7), he tells believers to keep watch over their own lives. Jesus gave a similar warning about false teachers: “Take heed that no one deceives you” (Matthew 24:4). Deceivers do their damage when believers stop paying attention, so the Elder does not tell them to study the false teachers more closely. He tells them to keep a careful eye on themselves.

“that we do not lose those things we worked for,”

The NKJV’s “we” includes the Elder with the believers he writes to. The word translated “worked” suggests real labor. What had been worked for was the fruit of the truth among these believers: their faith in Christ, their love for one another, and the walk in truth that gave the Elder such joy (verse 4). That fruit came through faithful teaching, prayer, and obedience, both theirs and their teachers’. Paul felt the same concern when the Galatians were being drawn away from the gospel: “I am afraid for you, lest I have labored for you in vain” (Galatians 4:11). If these believers welcomed teaching that denied who Jesus is, that labor could be lost.

“but that we may receive a full reward.”

A “reward” is what is paid for work done, and “full” means complete, with nothing missing. The danger of loss is real, and so is the prospect of receiving all that God intends to give. The Elder does not explain here how this reward relates to salvation or to persevering in faith, and Christians understand that relationship in different ways. His purpose is clear. He wants these believers to stay faithful to Christ so that nothing of what has been given and worked for is lost. John expressed the same concern in his first letter: “abide in Him, that when He appears, we may have confidence and not be ashamed before Him at His coming” (1 John 2:28).

Study Notes

The NKJV follows the majority of Greek manuscripts, which have “we” in all three clauses. Most modern translations follow a different Greek reading, with “you” in the first and last clauses and “we” in the middle, so that the readers are the ones who could lose or receive, and the labor at stake is what the Elder and other teachers had invested in them.

Other important manuscripts, including Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Alexandrinus, read “what you worked for” in the middle clause. On that reading the labor belongs to the readers themselves.

2 John 1:9
2 John 1:9
NKJV

2 John 1:9 Whoever transgresses and does not abide in the doctrine of Christ does not have God. He who abides in the doctrine of Christ has both the Father and the Son.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:9 Anyone who wanders away from this teaching has no relationship with God. But anyone who remains in the teaching of Christ has a relationship with both the Father and the Son. (NLT)

Main Idea

Whoever “transgresses,” stepping beyond the teaching about Christ instead of remaining in it, “does not have God.” But the one who abides in the doctrine of Christ “has both the Father and the Son,” for remaining in the truth about Jesus cannot be separated from living fellowship with God through Christ Himself.

Verse Explanation

Whoever “transgresses,” stepping beyond the teaching about Christ instead of remaining in it, “does not have God.” But the one who abides in the doctrine of Christ “has both the Father and the Son,” for remaining in the truth about Jesus cannot be separated from living fellowship with God through Christ Himself.

“Whoever transgresses”

To “transgress” is to step over a line or go beyond a boundary. The Elder has just described the deceivers (verse 7), and here he names what such a person does: he crosses the boundary of the truth about Christ and leaves it behind. Many modern translations read “goes ahead” or “runs ahead,” following a different Greek reading, but either way the person has moved past the teaching he first received instead of staying with it. “Whoever” makes the warning personal. It applies to anyone who takes this step, whether a visiting teacher or a church member drawn away.

“and does not abide in the doctrine of Christ”

“Abide” is the word used in verse 2, where the truth “abides in us.” Those in whom the truth lives must also go on living in it. “Doctrine” simply means teaching. “The doctrine of Christ” is the apostolic teaching about Jesus Christ, especially who He is and that He truly came in the flesh. John urged the same in his first letter: “Therefore let that abide in you which you heard from the beginning. If what you heard from the beginning abides in you, you also will abide in the Son and in the Father” (1 John 2:24).

Remaining in this teaching cannot be separated from remaining in Christ Himself. Jesus said, “I am the way, the truth, and the life” (John 14:6), so the one who abandons the truth about who Jesus is has walked away from Jesus. Right words about Him are no substitute for knowing Him, yet no one can know Him while rejecting the truth about Him. The Elder’s concern is not a disagreement over secondary matters. It is the foundation itself, the identity of Jesus Christ and His coming in the flesh.

“does not have God.”

To “have” God is to know Him and belong to Him. Whatever a person may claim, if he leaves the truth about Christ behind, he does not have God. John said it just as plainly in his first letter: “Whoever denies the Son does not have the Father either” (1 John 2:23). No one reaches the Father by going around His Son.

“He who abides in the doctrine of Christ has both the Father and the Son.”

The one who stays with the teaching about Christ loses nothing. He has God, both “the Father and the Son.” The greeting called Jesus “the Son of the Father” (verse 3), and here that relationship bears fruit. The Father and the Son cannot be separated, for Jesus said, “He who does not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent Him” (John 5:23). To hold to the truth about the Son is to enjoy fellowship with both. These believers did not need some new teaching to know God more deeply. Everything they needed was found in remaining with the Christ they had believed from the beginning.

Study Notes

Two different Greek words lie behind the translations of this verse. The NKJV’s “transgresses” translates parabainōn, the reading of most later manuscripts. Many modern translations read “goes ahead” or “runs ahead,” translating proagōn, found in several of the oldest manuscripts, including the fourth-century manuscripts known as Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus.

Elsewhere the Greek verb proagō is an ordinary word for going before or leading the way. Here it would describe moving forward past the teaching about Christ instead of remaining in it. Some interpreters suggest the word echoes the false teachers’ own claim to offer something more advanced. That is a reasonable guess, but the letter does not describe their claims.

“The doctrine of Christ” could grammatically mean the teaching Christ Himself gave or the teaching about Christ. The context favors teaching about Christ, since verse 7 has just named the denial of His coming in the flesh.

2 John 1:10–11 — Do Not Receive the Teacher Who Denies Christ

2 John 1:10
2 John 1:10
NKJV

2 John 1:10 If anyone comes to you and does not bring this doctrine, do not receive him into your house nor greet him;(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:10 If anyone comes to your meeting and does not teach the truth about Christ, don’t invite that person into your home or give any kind of encouragement. (NLT)

Main Idea

If anyone comes to them without this doctrine of Christ, they must not receive him into their house or greet him. The Elder has in view teachers who traveled from church to church and depended on believers’ welcome and support, so he forbids giving a Christ-denying ministry a home and a blessing, not ordinary kindness or conversation.

Verse Explanation

If anyone comes to them without this doctrine of Christ, they must not receive him into their house or greet him. The Elder has in view teachers who traveled from church to church and depended on believers’ welcome and support, so he forbids giving a Christ-denying ministry a home and a blessing, not ordinary kindness or conversation.

“If anyone comes to you”

The warning about deceivers who “have gone out into the world” (verse 7) now reaches the church’s own door. In the early church, teachers and missionaries traveled from town to town and depended on believers for lodging, meals, and help for the next stage of their journey. The Elder valued this hospitality. In 3 John he commends Gaius for receiving traveling brothers and urges sending them on “in a manner worthy of God” (3 John 6). The “you” here is plural, so the instruction concerns the whole congregation, not only one household.

“and does not bring this doctrine,”

“This doctrine” is the doctrine of Christ named in verse 9, the apostolic teaching about who Jesus Christ is, including His coming in the flesh. The word “bring” pictures a teacher arriving with a message he intends to spread. The church is not told to judge such a visitor by his gifts, his warmth, or his claims to insight. The deciding question is what he teaches about Jesus. The Elder has in mind someone who comes promoting a denial of the foundational truth about Christ, not a fellow believer who differs on lesser matters.

“do not receive him into your house”

Receiving a traveling teacher meant giving him a room, food, and supplies, and often a hearing among the believers. Churches commonly met in homes (Romans 16:5), so welcoming a teacher into the house may also have meant giving him a place to teach the gathered church, though the letter does not describe the setting in detail. That support made his work possible. In 3 John the Elder writes that believers should receive faithful workers “that we may become fellow workers for the truth” (3 John 8). The same principle works in reverse. Housing and supplying a Christ-denying teacher would make the church a partner in spreading his error.

The Elder is not forbidding help to a stranger in need, conversation with someone who holds wrong beliefs, ordinary hospitality, or loving relationships with family members and others who believe differently. He is forbidding the church to become a base of operations for someone working against the truth about Christ.

“nor greet him;”

The Greek word behind “greet” was the common word of greeting, related to the verb “rejoice.” The same word opens letters in the New Testament, where it is translated “Greetings” (Acts 15:23; James 1:1). For a visiting teacher, this greeting was more than a polite hello. It welcomed him as a fellow servant and wished his work well, showing him and the church that he came with their approval. That is the greeting the Elder tells them to withhold. Believers are not to treat such a person rudely. They are not to give a blessing that tells everyone his message has their support.

Study Notes

An early Christian manual called the Didache, probably written in the late first or early second century, shows how churches handled traveling teachers. It told believers to receive a teacher whose teaching agreed with what they had received, but not to listen to one who taught a different doctrine that undermined it.

It also gave practical tests. A traveling apostle was to stay one day, or two if necessary, and one who stayed three days, or who asked for money, was to be treated as a false prophet.

2 John 1:11

NKJV

NLT

2 John 1:11 Anyone who encourages such people becomes a partner in their evil work. (NLT)

Main Idea

The reason follows: “he who greets him shares in his evil deeds.” To welcome and send such a teacher on with a blessing is to become a partner in spreading teaching that denies Christ, so the matter touches the church’s own faithfulness to Him.

Verse Explanation

The reason follows: “he who greets him shares in his evil deeds.” To welcome and send such a teacher on with a blessing is to become a partner in spreading teaching that denies Christ, so the matter touches the church’s own faithfulness to Him.

“for he who greets him”

“For” gives the reason behind the command of verse 10. The greeting in view is the one that signals support, the kind of welcome that tells everyone this teacher is a trusted brother whose message deserves a hearing. The Elder is not forbidding ordinary courtesy toward someone who is wrong. He is saying that a believer should not lend his name, his home, or his encouragement to one who goes from church to church denying that Jesus Christ came in the flesh (verse 7).

“shares in his evil deeds.”

The Greek verb translated “shares” belongs to the same word family John uses for “fellowship” in his first letter: “truly our fellowship is with the Father and with His Son Jesus Christ” (1 John 1:3). To share in something is to become a partner in it. A believer who welcomes and supports a false teacher may not believe his error at all, but by opening the door he helps that teacher do his work and so takes part in it.

The “evil deeds” are not only private sins in the teacher’s life. They are the harm his teaching does as it leads people away from the truth about Jesus, causing them to lose what has been worked for (verse 8). Whoever leaves the doctrine of Christ “does not have God” (verse 9). Helping such a teacher therefore means helping to draw people away from the Father and the Son, which is why the Elder treats the matter so seriously.

The same principle runs the other way. In 3 John the Elder commends welcoming faithful traveling teachers, “that we may become fellow workers for the truth” (3 John 8). Support makes a person a partner in the message of the one he supports. Those who support a faithful teacher share in the work of the truth, and those who support a deceiver share in the damage he does.

2 John 1:12
2 John 1:12
NKJV

2 John 1:12 Having many things to write to you, I did not wish to do so with paper and ink; but I hope to come to you and speak face to face, that our joy may be full.(NKJV)

NLT

2 John 1:12 I have much more to say to you, but I don’t want to do it with paper and ink. For I hope to visit you soon and talk with you face to face. Then our joy will be complete. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder has many more things to say but does not want to write them “with paper and ink.” He hopes instead to come and “speak face to face, that our joy may be full,” because the joy they share in Christ is made complete when they are together.

Verse Explanation

The Elder has many more things to say but does not want to write them “with paper and ink.” He hopes instead to come and “speak face to face, that our joy may be full,” because the joy they share in Christ is made complete when they are together.

“Having many things to write to you,”

The letter is short, and the Elder admits there is much more he wants to say. He has written what was most urgent: the call to love one another, the warning about deceivers who deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh, and the instruction not to receive and support them. The rest can wait. Third John closes in almost the same way (3 John 13–14).

“I did not wish to do so with paper and ink;”

The “paper” was papyrus, a writing sheet made from strips of a reed plant pressed together, and ink was commonly made from soot mixed with gum and water. The Elder is not saying that writing is wrong. He has just written this letter, and John’s Gospel was written “that you may believe” (John 20:31). But a letter has limits. It cannot answer questions, read a listener’s face, or give the warmth of being present. The Elder does not say why he chose to wait, only that he would rather speak in person.

“but I hope to come to you and speak face to face,”

The Greek expression behind “face to face” literally reads “mouth to mouth,” meaning direct, personal conversation with nothing in between. The Elder wanted to be with this church and its members, not merely to send them instructions from a distance. “I hope” shows that his plans were sincere but not certain. He longed to come, but whether and when he would arrive was not entirely in his hands.

“that our joy may be full.”

In verse 4 he “rejoiced greatly” over news of children walking in truth. That joy was real but not yet complete. Being together in person would bring it to its fullness. “Our” matters here. The joy belongs to the Elder and the believers together, because it comes from their shared life in the truth and in Christ.

John used nearly the same words at the start of his first letter: “And these things we write to you that your joy may be full” (1 John 1:4). He learned that language from Jesus, who said, “These things I have spoken to you, that My joy may remain in you, and that your joy may be full” (John 15:11). The full joy Jesus promised is found in fellowship with Him, and it overflows into fellowship among those who belong to Him. That is why the Elder wanted more than a letter could give.

Study Notes

The closing of 3 John is strikingly similar. There the Elder writes that he does not wish to write “with pen and ink” but hopes to see his reader shortly and “speak face to face” (3 John 13–14). The parallel is one reason the two letters are usually assigned to the same writer.

NKJV

Main Idea

The letter closes with greetings from “the children of your elect sister,” most naturally the members of the church where the Elder is, sending their love to a sister congregation. The family language that opened the letter returns at its end, and the “Amen” seals it.

Verse Explanation

The letter closes with greetings from “the children of your elect sister,” most naturally the members of the church where the Elder is, sending their love to a sister congregation. The family language that opened the letter returns at its end, and the “Amen” seals it.

“The children of your elect sister”

The letter opened with “the elect lady and her children” (verse 1), and it closes with her “elect sister” and her children. The two figures frame the letter. If the lady is a congregation, the elect sister is most naturally the church where the Elder was when he wrote, and her children are its members. If the lady is an individual Christian woman, these would be her nephews and nieces, apparently living near the Elder.

Like the lady, the sister is “elect,” chosen by God. Both owe their place in God’s family to the same God, which is why the Elder can call them sisters. Peter closes a letter in a similar way: “She who is in Babylon, elect together with you, greets you” (1 Peter 5:13). Christians in different cities were not strangers to one another. They were members of one family, children of the same Father.

“greet you. Amen.”

This greeting stands in deliberate contrast to the warning a few verses earlier. The readers were not to “greet” a teacher who denied that Jesus Christ came in the flesh (verse 10), because “he who greets him shares in his evil deeds” (verse 11). Here a greeting passes freely between two families of believers who share the same truth about Christ. A Christian greeting expresses fellowship, so it is withheld from those working against the truth and gladly given to those who hold it. Until the Elder could come and speak face to face (verse 12), this greeting carried the love of one church to another, and the “Amen” closes the letter.

Study Notes

The “you” in “greet you” is singular in Greek, matching the singular address to the lady in verses 4–5.
