Book Questions

What is sound teaching for: knowing the truth, or being changed by it? (2 Timothy 1:13; 2:22–26; 3:10)

Second Timothy is full of concern for truth: “Hold fast the pattern of sound words” (1:13), handle the word rightly (2:15), “preach the word” (4:2). But Paul never treats correct teaching as an end in itself. He tells Timothy to hold it “in faith and love which are in Christ Jesus” (1:13), to pursue love “out of a pure heart” (2:22), and to correct opponents “in humility” (2:25).

MTB reads the letter through this conviction: God gives His Word to reveal Himself through His holy Son and to make us wholly His. Paul said the same in his first letter: “the purpose of the commandment is love from a pure heart, from a good conscience, and from sincere faith” (1 Timothy 1:5). The Word forms people who trust God, love Him and others, and even love those who wound them, as Paul does in 4:16.

As a spiritual application, knowledge pursued for its own sake, for status, or as ammunition for arguments misses God’s purpose, even when it is technically correct.

Read: 2 Timothy 1:13–14, 2 Timothy 2:22–26, 2 Timothy 3:10, 2 Timothy 4:16, 1 Timothy 1:3–7 · Related: What does “All Scripture is given by inspiration of God” claim?

2 Timothy 1 · 3 questions

What was “the gift of God” Timothy was to stir up? (vv. 6–7)

Paul reminds Timothy “to stir up the gift of God which is in you through the laying on of my hands” (v. 6). MTB understands the gift as the Spirit’s empowering for the ministry Paul charged Timothy to carry out. It equips him to guard the gospel and pass it on to others (1:14; 2:2).

The next verse shows what the gift produces: “For God has not given us a spirit of fear, but of power and of love and of a sound mind” (v. 7). Timothy is to use it with courage, sharing in suffering for the gospel “according to the power of God” (v. 8).

Read: 2 Timothy 1:6–8, 2 Timothy 1:13–14, 2 Timothy 2:2, 1 Timothy 4:14

What did Paul commit to Christ, and what did Timothy guard? (vv. 12–14)

Paul says, “I know whom I have believed and am persuaded that He is able to keep what I have committed to Him until that Day” (v. 12). MTB’s preferred reading is that Paul entrusted himself and his future to Christ. Some read it instead as the ministry or message God entrusted to Paul, which God will guard.

Verse 14 is clearer: “That good thing which was committed to you, keep by the Holy Spirit who dwells in us.” Timothy guards the gospel and sound teaching that Paul handed on, and he does it by the Spirit’s power. The two acts shed light on each other: Paul trusts Christ with himself, and Timothy is trusted with the gospel, which he must then pass on to “faithful men” (2:2).

Read: 2 Timothy 1:12–14, 2 Timothy 2:2

Was Paul praying for a man who had died? (vv. 16–18)

Paul prays, “The Lord grant mercy to the household of Onesiphorus” (v. 16), and, “The Lord grant to him that he may find mercy from the Lord in that Day” (v. 18). Because Paul speaks of his household and of his past help, some conclude Onesiphorus had died.

The text does not say whether he was living or dead, and it gives no basis for a practice of praying for the dead. What Paul does make clear is the man’s costly loyalty: he searched Rome for Paul and “was not ashamed of my chain” when others turned away (vv. 15–17).

Read: 2 Timothy 1:15–18, 2 Timothy 4:19

2 Timothy 2 · 2 questions

“If we deny Him, He also will deny us,” yet “He remains faithful.” Which is it? (vv. 11–13)

The faithful saying has four lines: “If we died with Him, we shall also live with Him. If we endure, we shall also reign with Him. If we deny Him, He also will deny us. If we are faithless, He remains faithful; He cannot deny Himself” (vv. 11–13).

MTB reads all four together. Christ is true to His promises and to His warnings, because “He cannot deny Himself.” Those who endure will reign with Him. A settled, final denial of Christ will meet His denial. True saving faith perseveres.

That leaves room for repentance after a fall. Peter denied Jesus and was restored (Luke 22:54–62; John 21:15–19). Judas betrayed Him and did not return (Matthew 27:3–5). These are illustrations, not a way to judge individual cases, which belong to Christ. Christians differ on how these lines bear on whether a believer can be lost.

Read: 2 Timothy 2:11–13, Matthew 10:32–33, Luke 22:31–34, 54–62, John 21:15–19, Matthew 27:3–5

Does Paul forbid arguing about doctrine? (vv. 14–26)

Paul tells Timothy not “to strive about words to no profit, to the ruin of the hearers” (v. 14), and to avoid “foolish and ignorant disputes” (v. 23). In the same passage he names Hymenaeus and Philetus, who say “the resurrection is already past,” because their teaching overthrows “the faith of some” (vv. 17–18).

MTB distinguishes necessary correction from quarrels. Error that overthrows faith must be named. But “a servant of the Lord must not quarrel but be gentle to all,” correcting opponents “in humility,” in the hope that “God perhaps will grant them repentance” (vv. 24–25). The goal of correction is the person’s rescue, not winning the argument.

Read: 2 Timothy 2:14–26, 1 Timothy 1:3–7 · Related: What is sound teaching for: knowing the truth, or being changed by it?

2 Timothy 3 · 2 questions

Are “the last days” still in the future? (vv. 1–5)

“In the last days perilous times will come” (v. 1). MTB understands the last days as the whole period from Pentecost to Christ’s return (Acts 2:16–17; Hebrews 1:2). Paul’s command, “And from such people turn away!” (v. 5), shows Timothy was already facing these people.

So the passage calls for present discernment. It is not a sign list that dates Christ’s return.

Read: 2 Timothy 3:1–9, Acts 2:16–21, Hebrews 1:1–2

What does “All Scripture is given by inspiration of God” claim? (vv. 15–17)

“All Scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete, thoroughly equipped for every good work” (vv. 16–17). Scripture comes from God, and its usefulness rests on that divine origin.

The “Holy Scriptures” Timothy knew from childhood were the Scriptures of Israel, which “are able to make you wise for salvation through faith which is in Christ Jesus” (v. 15). MTB holds that Scripture is sufficient for what this passage says it does: to bring people to salvation in Christ and to equip God’s servant fully for every good work. Timothy already had what he needed in the Word he had received.

MTB unequivocally receives the whole Bible, Old and New Testaments, as verbally and plenarily inspired and inerrant. God breathed out every word (verbal inspiration) in every part and on every subject it addresses (plenary inspiration), working through human authors as the Spirit guided them. Scripture is therefore true and without error in all that it affirms in the original manuscripts (inerrancy), and it is wholly sufficient, reliable, and authoritative for faith, doctrine, and life.

Read: 2 Timothy 3:14–17, 2 Timothy 4:1–5, 2 Peter 1:20–21 · Related: What is sound teaching for: knowing the truth, or being changed by it?

2 Timothy 4 · 3 questions

Is “the crown of righteousness” only for Paul and other faithful leaders? (vv. 6–8)

Paul says the Lord, “the righteous Judge, will give to me on that Day, and not to me only but also to all who have loved His appearing” (v. 8). The crown is the victor’s wreath. MTB understands it as Christ’s final vindication and inheritance given to those who finish in faith.

It is not reserved for apostles or a spiritual elite. It belongs to all who love His appearing. The endurance Paul describes, “I have kept the faith” (v. 7), is the fruit of saving faith, not a way of earning the reward.

Read: 2 Timothy 4:6–8, James 1:12, 1 Peter 5:4

Mark once left Paul. Why does Paul now call him “useful to me for ministry”? (v. 11)

“Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for ministry” (v. 11). Years earlier, Mark had left Paul and Barnabas partway through a mission, and Paul refused to take him on the next journey (Acts 13:13; 15:36–39). MTB reads these passages together as an encouragement: a worker who once failed can become useful again.

Read: 2 Timothy 4:9–11, Acts 13:13, Acts 15:36–39, Colossians 4:10

Paul expected to die. How could he say the Lord “will deliver me from every evil work”? (vv. 16–18)

At his first defense, everyone abandoned Paul, “But the Lord stood with me and strengthened me, so that the message might be preached fully through me... Also I was delivered out of the mouth of the lion” (v. 17). That earlier rescue made it possible for the gospel to be proclaimed.

Paul’s final confidence is different. He knows his death is near (vv. 6–7), yet he says, “the Lord will deliver me from every evil work and preserve me for His heavenly kingdom” (v. 18). Through death, the Lord will bring him safely home. These are two different deliverances. “The mouth of the lion” is most likely a picture of mortal danger; its exact meaning is left open.

Paul also models love for those who wound us. Of those who deserted him he prays, “May it not be charged against them” (v. 16).

Read: 2 Timothy 4:6–8, 2 Timothy 4:14–18, Psalm 22:21
