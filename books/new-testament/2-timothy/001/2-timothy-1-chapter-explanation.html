Chapter Study

2 Timothy 1

Verse-by-Verse
2 Timothy 1:1
2 Timothy 1:1
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:1 Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God, according to the promise of life which is in Christ Jesus,(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:1 This letter is from Paul, chosen by the will of God to be an apostle of Christ Jesus. I have been sent out to tell others about the life he has promised through faith in Christ Jesus. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul introduces himself as an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God, so his office comes from God’s choosing rather than his own. Writing as a prisoner who expects to die, he ties his apostleship to “the promise of life which is in Christ Jesus.”

Verse Explanation

Paul introduces himself as an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God, so his office comes from God’s choosing rather than his own. Writing as a prisoner who expects to die, he ties his apostleship to “the promise of life which is in Christ Jesus.”

“Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ”

Paul opens the way letters of his day opened, by naming the writer first. He calls himself “an apostle of Jesus Christ,” one sent out with Christ’s authority to carry His message. Timothy did not need to be told who Paul was. They had worked side by side for years. Paul still uses his full title because this letter is a charge, not only a personal note. What he tells Timothy to guard, preach, and hand on comes with the authority of the One who sent him.

“by the will of God,”

Paul did not appoint himself, and no church voted him into the office. He is an apostle “by the will of God.” He had once tried to destroy the church, and his calling came only because God chose him. That matters in this letter. Paul is chained in Rome and many have turned away from him (1:15). His standing does not depend on how things look or on who still stands with him. It rests on God’s choice.

“according to the promise of life which is in Christ Jesus,”

Paul ties his apostleship to “the promise of life.” He was sent to announce the life God has promised, and he serves in line with that promise. The words are striking from a man who expects to die soon (4:6). The first thing Paul mentions about his calling is life, not death. That life is “in Christ Jesus.” It is found in union with Him and nowhere else. Paul will soon say that Christ has abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel (1:10). Facing execution, he holds on to the promise he was sent to proclaim.

Study Notes

The phrase “according to” translates a Greek preposition that can express either the purpose of Paul’s apostleship (he was sent to make the promise of life known) or the standard it agrees with (his calling fits that promise). Both ideas fit Paul’s other letters, and the difference does not change the main point.

The order “Jesus Christ” and then “Christ Jesus” in the same verse reflects the Greek. Paul uses both orders throughout his letters without any clear difference in meaning.

2 Timothy 1:2
2 Timothy 1:2
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:2 To Timothy, a beloved son: Grace, mercy, and peace from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Lord.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:2 I am writing to Timothy, my dear son. May God the Father and Christ Jesus our Lord give you grace, mercy, and peace. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul addresses Timothy as “a beloved son,” the warm relationship that stands behind every command in the letter. He greets him with grace, mercy, and peace that come from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Lord together.

Verse Explanation

Paul addresses Timothy as “a beloved son,” the warm relationship that stands behind every command in the letter. He greets him with grace, mercy, and peace that come from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Lord together.

“To Timothy, a beloved son:”

Paul now names the one he writes to. Timothy came from Lystra. His mother was a Jewish believer and his father was Greek, and he had a good reputation among the believers there when Paul took him along on his travels (Acts 16:1–3). He became Paul’s closest coworker.

Paul calls him “a beloved son.” Timothy was not Paul’s son by birth. He was his son in the faith, and their bond was as close as a father’s with his son. Paul told the Philippians that Timothy had served with him “as a son with his father” (Philippians 2:22). Every command in this letter comes from that relationship. Paul writes as a father who loves his son and wants him to stand firm after his father is gone.

“Grace, mercy, and peace from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Lord.”

Paul usually greets churches with “grace” and “peace.” In his letters to Timothy he adds “mercy.” Grace is God’s undeserved kindness, mercy is His compassion toward people in need, and peace is the wholeness and well-being that come from being reconciled to Him. Timothy faced hardship, opposition, and the loss of his spiritual father. He needed all three.

The blessing comes “from God the Father and Christ Jesus our Lord” together. Paul names the Father and the Son as one source of grace, mercy, and peace. He would not do that with any created being. Calling Jesus “our Lord” also matters in a letter about loyalty under pressure. Timothy belongs to Christ and serves under Him, whatever the cost.

Study Notes

Paul’s first letter to Timothy opens with the same three words, “Grace, mercy, and peace” (1 Timothy 1:2). There Paul calls him “a true son in the faith.” Here he calls him “a beloved son,” which puts the emphasis on Paul’s affection.

Timothy is called “my beloved and faithful son in the Lord” in 1 Corinthians 4:17, where Paul sends him to remind the Corinthians of Paul’s ways in Christ.

2 Timothy 1:3–5 — Thanksgiving for Timothy’s Genuine Faith

2 Timothy 1:3
2 Timothy 1:3
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:3 I thank God, whom I serve with a pure conscience, as my forefathers did, as without ceasing I remember you in my prayers night and day,(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:3 Timothy, I thank God for you — the God I serve with a clear conscience, just as my ancestors did. Night and day I constantly remember you in my prayers. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul thanks God, whom he serves with a pure conscience in continuity with his forefathers, and tells Timothy that he remembers him in prayer without ceasing, night and day. His imprisonment has not interrupted either his service to God or his prayers for Timothy.

Verse Explanation

Paul thanks God, whom he serves with a pure conscience in continuity with his forefathers, and tells Timothy that he remembers him in prayer without ceasing, night and day. His imprisonment has not interrupted either his service to God or his prayers for Timothy.

“I thank God, whom I serve with a pure conscience, as my forefathers did,”

Paul begins with thanksgiving. Even in chains he has something to thank God for, and he will soon say what it is: Timothy’s genuine faith (1:5).

He describes God as the One “whom I serve.” The Greek word was often used for serving God in worship, and Paul’s whole life had become that kind of service. He serves “with a pure conscience.” He is not claiming to be sinless. He means that he serves God honestly, with nothing hidden and no double motive. Paul made the same claim before the Jewish council: “I have lived in all good conscience before God until this day” (Acts 23:1).

Paul serves God “as my forefathers did.” He does not see his faith in Christ as a break from the faith of Israel. He worships the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, the same God his ancestors served. In Christ, that God has kept His promises. This sets up the next verses, where Paul speaks of faith handed down through Timothy’s family as well.

“as without ceasing I remember you in my prayers night and day,”

Paul’s thanks rise as he prays for Timothy, and he prays for him “without ceasing,” “night and day.” The words describe constant, regular prayer. A prisoner could not visit or travel, but he could pray, and Paul kept carrying Timothy before God. Timothy would read this knowing that he had not been forgotten.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “serve” is the word used in the Greek Old Testament for the service and worship Israel offered to God. Paul uses the same verb when he tells Felix, “so I worship the God of my fathers” (Acts 24:14).

The Greek word order of this verse is not easy to follow. The NKJV joins Paul’s thanks to his prayer (“as without ceasing I remember you”), and the main sentence runs on through 1:5. Most translations understand the thanksgiving to be completed by Paul’s remembrance of Timothy’s faith in 1:5.

2 Timothy 1:4
2 Timothy 1:4
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:4 greatly desiring to see you, being mindful of your tears, that I may be filled with joy,(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:4 I long to see you again, for I remember your tears as we parted. And I will be filled with joy when we are together again. (NLT)

Main Idea

Remembering Timothy’s tears, Paul greatly desires to see him so that he may be filled with joy. The verse shows the depth of affection between the two men and prepares for Paul’s later appeals for Timothy to come to him.

Verse Explanation

Remembering Timothy’s tears, Paul greatly desires to see him so that he may be filled with joy. The verse shows the depth of affection between the two men and prepares for Paul’s later appeals for Timothy to come to him.

“greatly desiring to see you,”

Paul longs to see Timothy. The words “greatly desiring” express strong feeling, not polite interest. This is the first hint of the request Paul will make plainly at the end of the letter: “Be diligent to come to me quickly” (4:9) and “Do your utmost to come before winter” (4:21). The apostle who gives Timothy a solemn charge is also a lonely prisoner who wants his friend beside him.

“being mindful of your tears,”

Paul remembers Timothy’s “tears.” The letter does not say when or why Timothy wept. It may have been at their last parting, but Paul does not say. The memory shows how close the two men were. Timothy was not a hired assistant. He loved Paul, and his grief at their separation stayed with Paul in prison.

“that I may be filled with joy,”

Seeing Timothy again would fill Paul “with joy.” Facing death, Paul still values friendship. He does not pretend that faith makes human company unnecessary. He wants to see the son he loves.

Study Notes

In Greek, “being mindful of your tears” could be read with what comes before it, so that remembering Timothy’s tears is what makes Paul long to see him, as the NKJV punctuation suggests. It could also be read with what follows, so that the memory of those tears is tied to the joy Paul hopes for when they meet. Either reading keeps the tears, the longing, and the hoped-for joy together.

Tears at parting appear elsewhere in Paul’s ministry. When he said goodbye to the Ephesian elders at Miletus, “they all wept freely” (Acts 20:37). Timothy had traveled with Paul on that journey (Acts 20:4), though the occasion of the tears in this verse is not stated.

2 Timothy 1:5
2 Timothy 1:5
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:5 when I call to remembrance the genuine faith that is in you, which dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice, and I am persuaded is in you also.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:5 I remember your genuine faith, for you share the faith that first filled your grandmother Lois and your mother, Eunice. And I know that same faith continues strong in you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul’s thanksgiving centers on Timothy’s genuine faith, which lived first in his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice and which Paul is persuaded now lives in Timothy also. Faith that is real was handed down in Timothy’s family, and Paul is confident it has taken root in him.

Verse Explanation

Paul’s thanksgiving centers on Timothy’s genuine faith, which lived first in his grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice and which Paul is persuaded now lives in Timothy also. Faith that is real was handed down in Timothy’s family, and Paul is confident it has taken root in him.

“when I call to remembrance the genuine faith that is in you,”

Here is the main reason for Paul’s thanks. He remembers Timothy’s “genuine faith.” The Greek word means faith without pretense, faith that is not an act. Paul has seen Timothy under pressure for years, and he knows his faith is real. This is the foundation for everything Paul is about to ask. He does not call a pretender to suffer for the gospel. He calls a man whose faith is genuine to live out what is already true of him.

“which dwelt first in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice,”

This faith “dwelt first” in Timothy’s grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice. Acts says that Timothy’s mother was a Jewish woman who believed (Acts 16:1). These two women taught Timothy the Holy Scriptures from childhood (3:15), and their faith made a home in their lives. The word “dwelt” suggests faith that settled in and lived there, not a passing interest.

Timothy’s father was Greek, and nothing suggests he shared this faith. The faith that reached Timothy came through his grandmother and mother. Paul honors them by name. Their quiet faithfulness at home prepared a man who would carry the gospel across the Roman world.

“and I am persuaded is in you also.”

Paul ends with confidence: he is “persuaded” that the same faith now lives in Timothy. Faith cannot be inherited like a family name. Each person must believe for himself. But faith can be taught, modeled, and handed on, and Timothy’s faith had become his own. Paul uses the same word in 1:12 for his own settled confidence in Christ.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “genuine” means “without hypocrisy.” The same word describes the “sincere faith” that is the aim of Paul’s charge in 1 Timothy 1:5.

Lois and Eunice are named only here in the New Testament. Acts 16:1 describes Timothy’s mother as a Jewish believer but does not give her name.

The verb translated “dwelt” is the same verb Paul uses in 1:14 for the Holy Spirit “who dwells in us.” The same verb appears for faith living in Timothy’s family and for the Spirit living in believers.

2 Timothy 1:6–7 — Stir Up the Gift of God

2 Timothy 1:6
2 Timothy 1:6
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:6 Therefore I remind you to stir up the gift of God which is in you through the laying on of my hands.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:6 This is why I remind you to fan into flames the spiritual gift God gave you when I laid my hands on you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because of that genuine faith, Paul reminds Timothy to stir up the gift of God he received through the laying on of Paul’s hands. Timothy is to rekindle what God gave him for the ministry to which he was set apart, rather than let it grow dim under pressure.

Verse Explanation

Because of that genuine faith, Paul reminds Timothy to stir up the gift of God he received through the laying on of Paul’s hands. Timothy is to rekindle what God gave him for the ministry to which he was set apart, rather than let it grow dim under pressure.

“Therefore I remind you”

“Therefore” builds on what Paul has just said. Because Timothy’s faith is genuine, Paul can appeal to him. He does not issue a bare command. He says, “I remind you.” Timothy already knows what Paul is about to say. He needs to be stirred to act on it.

“to stir up the gift of God which is in you”

The Greek verb behind “stir up” pictures fanning a fire back into full flame. A fire that has burned low does not need new wood so much as fresh air. Timothy is to rekindle what God has already given him. The words do not prove that Timothy had failed. Paul writes to a man under heavy pressure, facing opposition and soon to lose his spiritual father, and pressure can dampen a fire. Paul wants the flame burning brightly.

What Timothy has is “the gift of God.” It came from God, not from Timothy’s talent or training, and it is “in you,” a present possession. The passage does not list what the gift included. In context it is God’s provision for the ministry Timothy was given: preaching, teaching, guarding the gospel, and caring for God’s people. The next verse connects it with the Spirit God gives.

“through the laying on of my hands.”

The gift came “through the laying on of my hands.” God is the giver, and Paul’s hands did not create the gift. Still, Paul names the laying on of his hands as the way the gift came to Timothy. He does not explain how the gift was imparted, and the passage does not say exactly what the gift was. Paul’s first letter probably refers to the same occasion and says that the elders also laid their hands on Timothy (1 Timothy 4:14). Here Paul mentions only his own hands, which suits the personal, father-to-son tone of this letter.

Study Notes

The verb translated “stir up” appears only here in the New Testament. It joins a word meaning “again” with a word meaning to kindle a fire. Outside the New Testament it was used for rekindling a fire or reviving something that had grown weak.

The Greek word translated “gift” is the word Paul uses elsewhere for gifts God gives through the Spirit. In 1 Timothy 4:14 Paul writes, “Do not neglect the gift that is in you, which was given to you by prophecy with the laying on of the hands of the eldership.” The two passages probably describe the same event from different angles.

2 Timothy 1:7
2 Timothy 1:7
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:7 For God has not given us a spirit of fear, but of power and of love and of a sound mind.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:7 For God has not given us a spirit of fear and timidity, but of power, love, and self-discipline. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul gives the reason Timothy can do this: God has not given a spirit of fear, but of power and of love and of a sound mind. What God supplies for ministry is not timidity but strength, love for people, and a clear and disciplined mind.

Verse Explanation

Paul gives the reason Timothy can do this: God has not given a spirit of fear, but of power and of love and of a sound mind. What God supplies for ministry is not timidity but strength, love for people, and a clear and disciplined mind.

“For God has not given us a spirit of fear,”

“For” gives the reason Timothy can rekindle the gift. God has given something, and fear is not it. The word translated “fear” means timidity or cowardice, the kind of fear that shrinks back from duty. Timothy faced real dangers: Paul was in chains, others had abandoned him, and false teachers were active. The pressure to keep quiet and stay safe was strong. Paul says that timidity does not come from God.

Paul writes “us,” not “you.” He includes himself. He is not lecturing Timothy from a place of safety. He has faced the same pressures and found God’s provision sufficient.

“but of power and of love and of a sound mind.”

In place of timidity, God gives “power,” strength to do what He has called a person to do, even under pressure. This connects with 1:8, where Timothy is to suffer for the gospel “according to the power of God.”

God also gives “love.” Power without love becomes harsh. Paul’s charge aims at love (1 Timothy 1:5), and in this letter he will tell Timothy to correct even opponents with gentleness (2:24–25). Courage in ministry is meant to serve people, not to defeat them.

God gives “a sound mind” as well. The phrase describes clear, disciplined thinking and self-control. A person ruled by fear reacts in panic. A person with a sound mind thinks clearly, keeps his balance, and does what is right.

Many interpreters understand the “spirit” here as the Holy Spirit, whom God gives and who produces power, love, and self-control. Others take it as the attitude or disposition God gives. The two readings are closely related, since such a disposition comes from the Spirit at work in the believer. Paul speaks plainly of “the Holy Spirit who dwells in us” in 1:14.

Study Notes

The Greek noun translated “fear” is not the usual New Testament word for fear.

The Greek noun translated “sound mind” is related to the verb and adjective used in the Pastoral Letters for being sober-minded and self-controlled (for example, “sober-minded” in 1 Timothy 3:2).

2 Timothy 1:8–12 — Sharing in Gospel Suffering Without Shame

2 Timothy 1:8
2 Timothy 1:8
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:8 Therefore do not be ashamed of the testimony of our Lord, nor of me His prisoner, but share with me in the sufferings for the gospel according to the power of God,(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:8 So never be ashamed to tell others about our Lord. And don’t be ashamed of me, either, even though I’m in prison for him. With the strength God gives you, be ready to suffer with me for the sake of the Good News. (NLT)

Main Idea

Therefore Timothy must not be ashamed of the testimony of our Lord or of Paul, whom he calls the Lord’s prisoner, but must share with him in the sufferings for the gospel. He is to endure them “according to the power of God,” not by his own strength.

Verse Explanation

Therefore Timothy must not be ashamed of the testimony of our Lord or of Paul, whom he calls the Lord’s prisoner, but must share with him in the sufferings for the gospel. He is to endure them “according to the power of God,” not by his own strength.

“Therefore do not be ashamed of the testimony of our Lord,”

A second “Therefore” draws a conclusion from God’s gift. Since God gives power, love, and a sound mind, Timothy must “not be ashamed.” Shame was a powerful force in the Roman world. To be linked with a crucified Jew or a chained prisoner was humiliating, and many people would quietly distance themselves from both.

“The testimony of our Lord” is the witness about Christ, the gospel message. Paul wrote to the Romans, “For I am not ashamed of the gospel of Christ, for it is the power of God to salvation” (Romans 1:16). Timothy must hold the same conviction now that standing with the gospel had become dangerous.

“nor of me His prisoner,”

Timothy must not be ashamed of Paul either. Paul calls himself “His prisoner,” the Lord’s prisoner, not Rome’s. The Roman authorities held the chains, but Paul was in prison because he belonged to Christ and preached Him. That changes how Timothy should see Paul’s chains. They are not a disgrace. They are part of his service to Christ. Paul will soon describe people who turned away from him (1:15) and one man who “was not ashamed of my chain” (1:16).

“but share with me in the sufferings for the gospel”

Instead of shrinking back, Timothy is to “share with me in the sufferings for the gospel.” Paul is not asking Timothy to seek out suffering. He is asking him not to avoid it when it comes with faithfulness to the gospel. The invitation is personal: “with me.” Timothy will not suffer alone. He will stand beside his spiritual father.

“according to the power of God,”

Timothy will not endure in his own strength. He suffers “according to the power of God,” the same power God gives in 1:7. God never calls His servants to bear what He will not give them strength to bear. The next two verses show what kind of God this is.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “share with me in the sufferings” combines “with” and “suffer hardship.” Paul uses a related verb in 2:3 and in 2:9 (“I suffer trouble”), and the verb in 4:5 (“endure afflictions”) comes from the same family.

The Greek word behind “ashamed” appears three times in this chapter: Timothy is not to be ashamed (1:8), Paul is not ashamed (1:12), and Onesiphorus was not ashamed of Paul’s chain (1:16). The repetition ties Paul’s appeal, his own example, and the example of Onesiphorus together.

2 Timothy 1:9
2 Timothy 1:9
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:9 who has saved us and called us with a holy calling, not according to our works, but according to His own purpose and grace which was given to us in Christ Jesus before time began,(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:9 For God saved us and called us to live a holy life. He did this, not because we deserved it, but because that was his plan from before the beginning of time — to show us his grace through Christ Jesus. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul describes the God whose power sustains them: He has saved us and called us with a holy calling, not according to our works but according to His own purpose and grace. That grace was given to us in Christ Jesus before time began, so salvation rests on God’s eternal purpose rather than on human effort.

Verse Explanation

Paul describes the God whose power sustains them: He has saved us and called us with a holy calling, not according to our works but according to His own purpose and grace. That grace was given to us in Christ Jesus before time began, so salvation rests on God’s eternal purpose rather than on human effort.

“who has saved us and called us with a holy calling,”

Paul now describes the God whose power sustains Timothy. First, God “has saved us.” Salvation is already accomplished. God has rescued us from sin and its consequences and brought us into a right relationship with Himself. Second, He has “called us with a holy calling.” God’s call brings people to Himself and sets them apart for Him. It is “holy” because it comes from the holy God and calls people to belong to Him and to live for Him.

“not according to our works,”

Paul makes clear that this salvation and calling are “not according to our works.” God did not save us because of anything we did. Our deeds did not earn His favor or move Him to call us. This matters for Timothy. If his standing with God depended on his performance, the pressures he faced could make him wonder whether he still belonged. Because it depends on God, he can stand firm.

“but according to His own purpose and grace”

The real basis of salvation is God’s “own purpose and grace.” “Purpose” points to God’s plan and intention. He decided to save. “Grace” is His undeserved favor. He saves people who could never deserve it. The word “own” stresses that this purpose and grace come from God alone and from nothing outside Him.

“which was given to us in Christ Jesus before time began,”

This grace “was given to us in Christ Jesus before time began.” Before the world was made, God determined to give grace to His people in Christ. Paul speaks as if it had already been given, because God’s eternal purpose is that certain. The grace is “in Christ Jesus.” It was always connected to Christ and comes to people only through union with Him. Timothy’s salvation rests on a decision God made before anything existed, and no prison or desertion can undo it.

Study Notes

The phrase “before time began” reads literally before eternal times, or before the ages of time. The same expression appears in Titus 1:2, where God “promised before time began.”

The Greek word for “saved” describes a completed action. Paul presents salvation here as something God has already done, while elsewhere in this letter he also looks ahead to salvation “with eternal glory” (2:10) and to being preserved for Christ’s heavenly kingdom (4:18).

2 Timothy 1:10
2 Timothy 1:10
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:10 but has now been revealed by the appearing of our Savior Jesus Christ, who has abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel,(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:10 And now he has made all of this plain to us by the appearing of Christ Jesus, our Savior. He broke the power of death and illuminated the way to life and immortality through the Good News. (NLT)

Main Idea

God’s eternal grace has now been revealed by the appearing of our Savior Jesus Christ, here meaning His first coming. Christ has abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel, which is why this message is worth suffering for.

Verse Explanation

God’s eternal grace has now been revealed by the appearing of our Savior Jesus Christ, here meaning His first coming. Christ has abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel, which is why this message is worth suffering for.

“but has now been revealed by the appearing of our Savior Jesus Christ,”

God’s grace, given before time began, “has now been revealed.” What was hidden in God’s eternal purpose has come into the open. It was revealed “by the appearing of our Savior Jesus Christ.” Here “appearing” refers to Christ’s first coming: His birth, life, death, and resurrection. Later in the letter the same word refers to His return (4:1, 8). Christ appeared once to save, and He will appear again to judge and to reward.

Paul calls Jesus “our Savior.” In 1:9 Paul said that God saved us. Now he calls Jesus our Savior. The Father’s saving purpose was carried out through the Son.

“who has abolished death”

Christ “has abolished death.” The Greek word means to make something powerless, to render it ineffective. Christians still die, and Paul himself is about to die (4:6). But death has lost its power to hold them. Through His death and resurrection Christ broke death’s grip. Death is now a departure into Christ’s presence, not a defeat.

“and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel,”

Christ has also “brought life and immortality to light.” Life here is the life of God, the life Paul mentioned in 1:1 (“the promise of life”). “Immortality” means incorruptibility, life that cannot decay or end. Christ did not invent this hope, but He brought it fully into view by rising from the dead.

This happens “through the gospel.” The good news announces what Christ has done and invites people to receive it by faith. That is why the gospel is worth suffering for (1:8). Paul is not risking his life for a set of ideas. He is suffering for the message that defeats death.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “appearing” in this verse is the same word used in 4:1 and 4:8 for Christ’s return. In this verse it refers to His first coming.

The Greek verb translated “abolished” is the same verb used in 1 Corinthians 15:26: “The last enemy that will be destroyed is death.” It describes making something powerless or bringing it to nothing.

The Greek word translated “immortality” means incorruption, freedom from decay. The same word appears in 1 Corinthians 15:54, where “this corruptible has put on incorruption.”

2 Timothy 1:11
2 Timothy 1:11
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:11 to which I was appointed a preacher, an apostle, and a teacher of the Gentiles.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:11 And God chose me to be a preacher, an apostle, and a teacher of this Good News. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul states that he was appointed to this gospel as a preacher, an apostle, and a teacher of the Gentiles. His present sufferings are bound up with the commission God gave him.

Verse Explanation

Paul states that he was appointed to this gospel as a preacher, an apostle, and a teacher of the Gentiles. His present sufferings are bound up with the commission God gave him.

“to which I was appointed a preacher, an apostle, and a teacher of the Gentiles.”

Paul was “appointed” to this gospel. God placed him in this ministry. He names three roles.

He is “a preacher,” a herald who announces the message on behalf of the King. A herald does not make up his message. He proclaims what he has been given.

He is “an apostle,” one sent with Christ’s authority (1:1).

He is “a teacher of the Gentiles.” Paul explained and applied the gospel to non-Jewish peoples. When Christ called him, the Lord said Paul was “a chosen vessel of Mine to bear My name before Gentiles” (Acts 9:15).

These three roles explain Paul’s suffering, which he names in the next verse. He is in chains because he faithfully carried out the work God gave him.

Study Notes

Some ancient manuscripts do not include “of the Gentiles” in this verse, and translations based on them, such as the NLT, leave out the phrase. Paul uses nearly the same wording in 1 Timothy 2:7, where he was appointed “a preacher and an apostle” and “a teacher of the Gentiles in faith and truth,” so the description of his ministry is the same either way.

The Greek word translated “preacher” refers to a herald, the official who publicly announced a ruler’s messages. The related verb appears in 4:2: “Preach the word!”

2 Timothy 1:12
2 Timothy 1:12
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:12 For this reason I also suffer these things; nevertheless I am not ashamed, for I know whom I have believed and am persuaded that He is able to keep what I have committed to Him until that Day.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:12 That is why I am suffering here in prison. But I am not ashamed of it, for I know the one in whom I trust, and I am sure that he is able to guard what I have entrusted to him until the day of his return. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because of that commission Paul suffers, yet he is not ashamed, for he knows whom he has believed. He is persuaded that Christ is able to keep what Paul has committed to Him, his own life and future, until that Day when Christ returns.

Verse Explanation

Because of that commission Paul suffers, yet he is not ashamed, for he knows whom he has believed. He is persuaded that Christ is able to keep what Paul has committed to Him, his own life and future, until that Day when Christ returns.

“For this reason I also suffer these things;”

“For this reason” points back to Paul’s appointment as preacher, apostle, and teacher. Because he was faithful to that calling, he now suffers “these things.” His imprisonment is not an accident or a sign that something went wrong. It is what faithfulness to the gospel has cost him.

“nevertheless I am not ashamed,”

“Nevertheless” turns the sentence. In spite of his chains, Paul is “not ashamed.” He has just told Timothy not to be ashamed (1:8), and now he shows him the example. Paul is not asking Timothy to do anything he has not done himself.

“for I know whom I have believed”

Paul explains why he is not ashamed. He can say, “I know whom I have believed.” He does not say “what” but “whom.” His confidence rests on a Person he trusts and knows. He has walked with Christ for decades, and he knows Him well enough to trust Him with his life.

“and am persuaded that He is able to keep what I have committed to Him until that Day.”

Paul is “persuaded,” fully convinced, that Christ “is able to keep” what Paul has entrusted to Him. The picture is of a valuable deposit placed in safekeeping. Paul has committed himself and his future to Christ: his life, his ministry, and his eternal destiny. As death approaches, Paul is sure that Christ will keep all of it safe.

Christ keeps it “until that Day,” the day of His return. Paul mentions “that Day” again in 1:18 and 4:8. On that day Christ will reward His faithful servants, and Paul is confident that everything he has entrusted to Christ will be there, kept safe.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “what I have committed to Him” is literally “my deposit.” This reading takes it as what Paul has entrusted to Christ: himself and his future. Some interpreters take it instead as what God has entrusted to Paul, the gospel and his ministry, which Christ will guard. The same word appears in 1:14 for what has been entrusted to Timothy, and in 1 Timothy 6:20 (“Guard what was committed to your trust”).

The Greek verb translated “I have believed” describes a past act with a continuing result: Paul put his trust in Christ, and that trust continues.

2 Timothy 1:13–14 — Holding Fast the Sound Words and Guarding the Deposit

2 Timothy 1:13
2 Timothy 1:13
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:13 Hold fast the pattern of sound words which you have heard from me, in faith and love which are in Christ Jesus.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:13 Hold on to the pattern of wholesome teaching you learned from me — a pattern shaped by the faith and love that you have in Christ Jesus. (NLT)

Main Idea

Timothy must hold fast the pattern of sound words he heard from Paul, keeping the shape of the apostolic teaching intact. He is to hold it “in faith and love which are in Christ Jesus,” so that sound teaching is never separated from trust in Christ and love for people.

Verse Explanation

Timothy must hold fast the pattern of sound words he heard from Paul, keeping the shape of the apostolic teaching intact. He is to hold it “in faith and love which are in Christ Jesus,” so that sound teaching is never separated from trust in Christ and love for people.

“Hold fast the pattern of sound words which you have heard from me,”

Paul turns back to direct command. Timothy must “hold fast” what he has received. The word “pattern” describes an outline or model, a standard to follow. Paul has given Timothy a clear shape of the gospel and its teaching. Timothy must keep that shape and not let it be bent or blurred.

The words are “sound words.” “Sound” means healthy, whole, and life-giving. Sound teaching produces spiritual health in those who receive it. False teaching, by contrast, spreads like a disease (2:17).

These are words Timothy “heard from me.” Timothy did not invent them or collect them from many teachers. He received them from Paul, the apostle Christ appointed. Timothy’s task is to keep what he has been given.

“in faith and love which are in Christ Jesus.”

Timothy must hold the sound words “in faith and love.” The way he holds the truth matters. He holds it “in faith,” trusting the God the truth reveals. He holds it “in love,” caring for the people the truth is meant to serve. Paul does not want a man who knows correct doctrine but is cold or quarrelsome. Correct teaching without faith becomes empty, and without love it becomes harsh.

This faith and love are “in Christ Jesus.” They come from union with Him. Timothy does not produce them by his own effort. They flow from his life in Christ.

Study Notes

Paul uses the Greek word translated “pattern” in 1 Timothy 1:16, where his own conversion serves “as a pattern to those who are going to believe on Him.”

The same Greek word for “sound” describes “sound doctrine” in 1 Timothy 1:10 and Titus 1:9.

2 Timothy 1:14
2 Timothy 1:14
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:14 That good thing which was committed to you, keep by the Holy Spirit who dwells in us.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:14 Through the power of the Holy Spirit who lives within us, carefully guard the precious truth that has been entrusted to you. (NLT)

Main Idea

With the help of the Holy Spirit who dwells in believers, Timothy must guard the good thing committed to him, the gospel and sound teaching Paul handed on. Paul has entrusted himself to Christ’s keeping, and now Timothy is responsible to keep what Paul has entrusted to him.

Verse Explanation

With the help of the Holy Spirit who dwells in believers, Timothy must guard the good thing committed to him, the gospel and sound teaching Paul handed on. Paul has entrusted himself to Christ’s keeping, and now Timothy is responsible to keep what Paul has entrusted to him.

“That good thing which was committed to you,”

“That good thing which was committed to you” refers to the gospel and the sound teaching Paul handed on to Timothy. It is a “good thing,” valuable and beautiful. It was “committed” to Timothy, given into his care as a deposit. Paul has entrusted himself to Christ (1:12). Now he reminds Timothy that the gospel has been entrusted to him.

“keep by the Holy Spirit who dwells in us.”

Timothy must “keep” it, guard it like a treasure. False teachers were twisting the message, and some who had heard Paul were turning away. Timothy must make sure the gospel is not changed, lost, or watered down.

He does not guard it alone. He keeps it “by the Holy Spirit who dwells in us.” God has not left the protection of the gospel to human strength. The Spirit lives in believers, and He gives power, love, and a sound mind (1:7). The Spirit enables Timothy to recognize error, hold to the truth, and stand firm under pressure. Paul says “us,” including himself. The same Spirit who strengthened Paul will strengthen Timothy.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “that good thing which was committed” is the same word Paul used in 1:12 for what he had committed to Christ. In 1:12 Paul entrusts himself to Christ’s keeping; here Timothy keeps what Paul has entrusted to him. Timothy must in turn entrust the teaching to faithful men (2:2).

The Greek verb translated “keep” means to guard or protect. It is the same verb Paul uses in 1:12 for Christ’s keeping and in 1 Timothy 6:20 (“Guard what was committed to your trust”).

2 Timothy 1:15–18 — Those Who Turned Away and Onesiphorus Who Stood Firm

2 Timothy 1:15
2 Timothy 1:15
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:15 This you know, that all those in Asia have turned away from me, among whom are Phygellus and Hermogenes.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:15 As you know, everyone from the province of Asia has deserted me — even Phygelus and Hermogenes. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul reminds Timothy of something he already knows: all those in Asia have turned away from him, among them Phygellus and Hermogenes. The verse records their desertion without explaining their reasons, and it shows the pressure under which Timothy is called to stand firm.

Verse Explanation

Paul reminds Timothy of something he already knows: all those in Asia have turned away from him, among them Phygellus and Hermogenes. The verse records their desertion without explaining their reasons, and it shows the pressure under which Timothy is called to stand firm.

“This you know,”

Paul turns from command to report. “This you know.” Timothy already knew what Paul is about to say. The news was painful, and Paul mentions it to remind Timothy of the setting in which he must stand firm.

“that all those in Asia have turned away from me,”

“Asia” here is the Roman province of Asia, in what is now western Turkey. Ephesus was its leading city, and Timothy was most likely serving there or nearby. Paul had ministered in Ephesus for years, and the gospel had spread from there through the province (Acts 19:10).

Now “all those in Asia have turned away from me.” Paul does not mean every believer in the province. He means a group of believers from Asia whom Timothy would recognize. The letter does not say who they were, where they turned away, or why. What is clear is that people Paul had counted on turned away from him while he was a prisoner.

“among whom are Phygellus and Hermogenes.”

Paul names two of them, Phygellus and Hermogenes. These men are not mentioned anywhere else in Scripture, and Timothy evidently knew them. Paul does not explain their reasons. In the verses that follow, Onesiphorus stands in contrast to them: he sought Paul out and was not ashamed of his chain (1:16–17). His example shows Timothy what costly loyalty looks like.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “have turned away” describes turning away or deserting. The text does not say that these men abandoned the faith. It says they turned away from Paul.

The phrase “those in Asia” could also refer to believers from Asia who were in Rome. The letter does not settle the question, and the main point remains the same: people Paul had reason to trust did not stand with him.

2 Timothy 1:16
2 Timothy 1:16
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:16 The Lord grant mercy to the household of Onesiphorus, for he often refreshed me, and was not ashamed of my chain;(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:16 May the Lord show special kindness to Onesiphorus and all his family because he often visited and encouraged me. He was never ashamed of me because I was in chains. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul asks the Lord to grant mercy to the household of Onesiphorus, because Onesiphorus often refreshed him and was not ashamed of his chain. In contrast to those in Asia, Onesiphorus did exactly what Paul urged Timothy to do in 1:8.

Verse Explanation

Paul asks the Lord to grant mercy to the household of Onesiphorus, because Onesiphorus often refreshed him and was not ashamed of his chain. In contrast to those in Asia, Onesiphorus did exactly what Paul urged Timothy to do in 1:8.

“The Lord grant mercy to the household of Onesiphorus,”

Against the dark background of 1:15, Paul names one man who stood firm. He prays that “the Lord grant mercy to the household of Onesiphorus.” The name means “bringing profit” or “useful,” and Onesiphorus lived up to it. Paul asks the Lord to show mercy to his whole “household,” his family and those who lived in his home.

Paul’s prayer for the household is the first of two prayers for mercy in this passage. In 1:18 he prays separately for Onesiphorus himself on “that Day.” Paul again greets the household at the end of the letter (4:19). The letter does not say why Paul greets the household and not Onesiphorus. Some think he had died, and others think he was simply away from home. Scripture leaves that open.

“for he often refreshed me,”

Onesiphorus “often refreshed” Paul. The word pictures bringing a breath of fresh air or cooling relief. He did not visit once out of duty. He came back again and again, bringing encouragement and practical help to a prisoner in chains.

“and was not ashamed of my chain;”

Most important, Onesiphorus “was not ashamed of my chain.” This is exactly what Paul has urged Timothy to do (1:8). Others turned away from Paul when he was in chains, but Onesiphorus was not embarrassed to be seen with a prisoner. His loyalty gives Timothy a living example of the courage Paul is asking for.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “refreshed” occurs only here in the New Testament.

“Chain” is singular. A prisoner in Roman custody was often chained to a soldier (compare Acts 28:16, 20). The same word appears in 2:9 (“even to the point of chains”).

2 Timothy 1:17
2 Timothy 1:17
NKJV

2 Timothy 1:17 but when he arrived in Rome, he sought me out very zealously and found me.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 1:17 When he came to Rome, he searched everywhere until he found me. (NLT)

Main Idea

When Onesiphorus arrived in Rome, he sought Paul out very zealously until he found him. Finding a chained prisoner took persistence and carried real risk, which shows how costly his loyalty was.

Verse Explanation

When Onesiphorus arrived in Rome, he sought Paul out very zealously until he found him. Finding a chained prisoner took persistence and carried real risk, which shows how costly his loyalty was.

“but when he arrived in Rome,”

“But” contrasts Onesiphorus with those who turned away. When he “arrived in Rome,” he did not hide or keep his distance. The verse also confirms where Paul is writing from: Rome, the capital of the empire, where he is held as a prisoner.

“he sought me out very zealously and found me.”

Onesiphorus “sought me out very zealously.” Finding Paul was not easy. Rome was a huge city, and a man accused of a crime might be held in any of several places. Asking around for a prisoner, especially one whose friends were deserting him, could bring suspicion. Onesiphorus kept looking until he “found me.” His persistence showed love and real courage. He was willing to take risks and endure difficulty to reach a friend in chains.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “very zealously” means diligently, eagerly, or with urgency. It describes effort, not merely interest.

The letter does not say why Onesiphorus came to Rome. Some suggest he came on business, and others that he came specifically to help Paul. Either way, he made the effort to find Paul once he arrived.

NLT

2 Timothy 1:18 May the Lord show him special kindness on the day of Christ’s return. And you know very well how helpful he was in Ephesus. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul prays that the Lord will grant Onesiphorus mercy from the Lord “in that Day,” giving his loyal kindness an eternal horizon. He adds that Timothy knows very well how many ways Onesiphorus had already served Paul at Ephesus.

Verse Explanation

Paul prays that the Lord will grant Onesiphorus mercy from the Lord “in that Day,” giving his loyal kindness an eternal horizon. He adds that Timothy knows very well how many ways Onesiphorus had already served Paul at Ephesus.

“The Lord grant to him that he may find mercy from the Lord in that Day—”

Paul now prays for Onesiphorus himself: “The Lord grant to him that he may find mercy from the Lord in that Day.” The repetition of “the Lord” is unusual. Paul asks the Lord to grant that Onesiphorus will find mercy from the Lord. The wording keeps the focus on the Lord as the one who gives the mercy.

Onesiphorus “found” Paul after searching hard (1:17). Now Paul prays that Onesiphorus will “find mercy” on “that Day,” the day of Christ’s return, which Paul mentioned in 1:12. Onesiphorus showed mercy to a prisoner, and Paul prays that the Lord will show him mercy when Christ comes. His costly loyalty is given an eternal horizon.

“and you know very well how many ways he ministered to me at Ephesus.”

Paul adds a reminder: Timothy knows “very well” how Onesiphorus “ministered” to him at “Ephesus.” His kindness in Rome was not a one-time act. He had served Paul in many ways before. Timothy had seen it firsthand, which suggests that Onesiphorus and his household were part of the church Timothy now served. Paul uses his example to show what faithful, unashamed love looks like.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “ministered” is related to the word for a servant or deacon. It describes practical service.

Some have argued from this verse that Onesiphorus had died and that Paul is praying for the dead. The verse does not say that he had died, and Paul’s prayer concerns the mercy Onesiphorus would receive on the day of Christ’s return. The letter leaves his circumstances at the time of writing open.
