Chapter Study

2 Timothy 2

Verse-by-Verse
2 Timothy 2:1
2 Timothy 2:1
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:1 You therefore, my son, be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:1 Timothy, my dear son, be strong through the grace that God gives you in Christ Jesus. (NLT)

Main Idea

Drawing a conclusion from the examples at the end of chapter 1, Paul tells Timothy, his son, to be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus. The strength Timothy needs for what follows comes from Christ’s grace, not from his own resources.

Verse Explanation

Drawing a conclusion from the examples at the end of chapter 1, Paul tells Timothy, his son, to be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus. The strength Timothy needs for what follows comes from Christ’s grace, not from his own resources.

“You therefore, my son,”

“Therefore” draws a conclusion from chapter 1. Paul has described people who turned away from him (1:15) and one man, Onesiphorus, who stood firm (1:16–18). Now he turns to Timothy: “You therefore.” The word “you” is emphatic. Whatever others have done, Timothy must take his own stand. Paul calls him “my son,” returning to the affection of 1:2. The command that follows comes from a father who loves him.

“be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus.”

Timothy must “be strong.” Paul does not tell him to make himself strong by effort or willpower. He is to be strong “in the grace that is in Christ Jesus.” Grace is God’s undeserved favor, and here it is also God’s power at work in His people. The strength Timothy needs comes from Christ’s grace, not from his own resources.

The Greek verb is passive and can be understood as “be strengthened.” Timothy is to keep drawing strength from a source outside himself. Everything Paul asks in this chapter, from teaching others to enduring hardship to correcting opponents gently, depends on this grace. Without it, Timothy’s duties would crush him. With it, he can do what God has called him to do.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “be strong” is in the passive voice and a present tense that describes ongoing action: Timothy is to keep on being strengthened. Paul uses the same verb in 4:17, where “the Lord stood with me and strengthened me.”

2 Timothy 2:2
2 Timothy 2:2
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:2 And the things that you have heard from me among many witnesses, commit these to faithful men who will be able to teach others also.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:2 You have heard me teach things that have been confirmed by many reliable witnesses. Now teach these truths to other trustworthy people who will be able to pass them on to others. (NLT)

Main Idea

What Timothy heard from Paul, confirmed among many witnesses, he must commit to faithful men who will be able to teach others also. The teaching Timothy guards must also be handed on, so that it reaches another generation of teachers.

Verse Explanation

What Timothy heard from Paul, confirmed among many witnesses, he must commit to faithful men who will be able to teach others also. The teaching Timothy guards must also be handed on, so that it reaches another generation of teachers.

“And the things that you have heard from me among many witnesses,”

Timothy has received a body of teaching. These are “the things that you have heard from me,” the gospel and the sound words Paul described in 1:13. Timothy did not receive them in secret. He heard them “among many witnesses.” Others heard the same teaching and could confirm it. Paul’s gospel was not a private message passed to an inner circle. It was public, open, and confirmed by many.

“commit these to faithful men”

Timothy must “commit these to faithful men.” The word “commit” belongs to the same family as the word Paul used in 1:12 and 1:14 for something entrusted. Timothy must guard the deposit (1:14), and he must also hand it on. Guarding the gospel does not mean locking it away. It means passing it on intact to people who will keep it.

Timothy must choose “faithful men,” people who are trustworthy and reliable. They must be faithful to the truth and faithful in character. In a time when some were turning away and false teachers were spreading error, choosing the right people mattered greatly.

“who will be able to teach others also.”

These faithful men must “be able to teach others also.” The chain of teaching reaches beyond Timothy. Paul taught Timothy, Timothy will teach faithful men, and they will teach others. That makes four generations in one verse: Paul, Timothy, faithful men, and others. Paul is about to die (4:6). He wants to make sure that the gospel will be handed on long after he and Timothy are gone.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “commit” is related to the noun used in 1:12 and 1:14 for the deposit that is entrusted and guarded. The same family of words appears in 1 Timothy 1:18, “This charge I commit to you.”

The phrase “among many witnesses” could also be read as through many witnesses, meaning that many others confirmed Paul’s teaching to Timothy. Either way, the teaching was public and confirmed by others.

“Men” translates a general Greek word for human beings. Paul’s concern is that the people entrusted with the teaching be reliable and able to teach others.

2 Timothy 2:3–7 — The Soldier, the Athlete, and the Farmer

2 Timothy 2:3
2 Timothy 2:3
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:3 You therefore must endure hardship as a good soldier of Jesus Christ.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:3 Endure suffering along with me, as a good soldier of Christ Jesus. (NLT)

Main Idea

Timothy must endure hardship as a good soldier of Jesus Christ. Paul calls him to accept suffering as part of serving under Christ’s command.

Verse Explanation

Timothy must endure hardship as a good soldier of Jesus Christ. Paul calls him to accept suffering as part of serving under Christ’s command.

“You therefore must endure hardship”

“Therefore” marks a new appeal. Timothy must be strong in grace and pass on the teaching. He must also “endure hardship.” Faithful ministry will bring suffering, and Timothy must accept it rather than run from it. Paul made the same appeal in 1:8: “share with me in the sufferings for the gospel.”

“as a good soldier of Jesus Christ.”

Paul gives the first of three pictures: a “good soldier.” Soldiers expect hardship. They endure long marches, poor food, harsh conditions, and danger. They accept these as part of their service. Timothy serves in Christ’s army, and he must expect the same.

He is a soldier “of Jesus Christ.” He does not serve himself, the church, or Paul. He serves under Christ’s command. Christ is his commander, and Christ is the one he must please (2:4).

Study Notes

The Greek text behind the NKJV reads “You therefore must endure hardship.” Many other manuscripts read a verb meaning to share in suffering, the same verb used in 1:8, and translations based on them, such as the NLT, read “Endure suffering along with me.” Either way, Timothy is called to accept hardship in Christ’s service.

Paul uses military language elsewhere for Christian service. He calls coworkers “fellow soldier” (Philippians 2:25; Philemon 1:2) and charges Timothy to “wage the good warfare” (1 Timothy 1:18).

2 Timothy 2:4
2 Timothy 2:4
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:4 No one engaged in warfare entangles himself with the affairs of this life, that he may please him who enlisted him as a soldier.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:4 Soldiers don’t get tied up in the affairs of civilian life, for then they cannot please the officer who enlisted them. (NLT)

Main Idea

A soldier on active service does not entangle himself with the affairs of this life, because his aim is to please the one who enlisted him. Timothy, too, must keep his attention on pleasing Christ, who called him into service.

Verse Explanation

A soldier on active service does not entangle himself with the affairs of this life, because his aim is to please the one who enlisted him. Timothy, too, must keep his attention on pleasing Christ, who called him into service.

“No one engaged in warfare entangles himself with the affairs of this life,”

Paul develops the soldier picture. A soldier on active duty does not get tangled up in ordinary civilian business. The word “entangles” pictures a net or snare that traps and holds back. “The affairs of this life” are the ordinary concerns of making a living, running a household, and managing business. These things are not sinful. They are simply not what a soldier on duty is there to do. A soldier who gets caught up in them cannot give his full attention to his mission.

The verse does not forbid ordinary work. Paul himself worked as a tentmaker to support himself (Acts 18:3). The point is focus. Timothy must not let ordinary concerns distract him from the mission Christ has given him.

“that he may please him who enlisted him as a soldier.”

The soldier keeps free of distractions for a reason: “that he may please him who enlisted him as a soldier.” His goal is to please his commander. Timothy’s goal is the same. Christ called him into His service, and Timothy’s highest aim is to please Christ, not to please people, gain comfort, or protect himself.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “entangles” appears in 2 Peter 2:20, where people are “again entangled” in the world’s pollutions. It describes being caught or wound up in something.

The Greek verb translated “enlisted him as a soldier” means to gather or recruit an army. It appears only here in the New Testament.

2 Timothy 2:5
2 Timothy 2:5
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:5 And also if anyone competes in athletics, he is not crowned unless he competes according to the rules.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:5 And athletes cannot win the prize unless they follow the rules. (NLT)

Main Idea

An athlete is not crowned unless he competes according to the rules. Faithful service must be carried out in the way the Lord requires if it is to receive His reward.

Verse Explanation

An athlete is not crowned unless he competes according to the rules. Faithful service must be carried out in the way the Lord requires if it is to receive His reward.

“And also if anyone competes in athletics,”

Paul turns to a second picture: an athlete. Athletic games were well known across the Greek and Roman world, and the Isthmian Games were held near Corinth. Timothy would have understood the picture immediately.

“he is not crowned unless he competes according to the rules.”

An athlete “is not crowned unless he competes according to the rules.” The winner received a crown, a wreath of leaves, but only if he followed the rules of the contest. An athlete who broke the rules was disqualified, no matter how fast he ran or how hard he fought.

The same is true in Christ’s service. Timothy must serve in the way the Lord requires. Success by worldly standards means nothing if it comes by compromising the truth or cutting corners. Faithful service, carried out God’s way, will receive His reward. Paul will speak of his own reward at the end of his race: “the crown of righteousness” (4:8).

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “competes in athletics” is related to the English word “athlete.” “Crowned” translates a verb related to the noun for the victor’s wreath given at the games, the noun translated “crown” in 4:8. To be crowned was to receive that wreath as the winner.

The Greek word translated “according to the rules” means lawfully, in keeping with the rules of the contest. Ancient athletes had to meet training requirements and follow the regulations of the games.

Paul uses the same athletic picture in 1 Corinthians 9:25: “they do it to obtain a perishable crown, but we for an imperishable crown.”

2 Timothy 2:6
2 Timothy 2:6
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:6 The hardworking farmer must be first to partake of the crops.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:6 And hardworking farmers should be the first to enjoy the fruit of their labor. (NLT)

Main Idea

The hardworking farmer must be the first to share in the crops. The one who labors faithfully can look forward to enjoying the fruit of that labor.

Verse Explanation

The hardworking farmer must be the first to share in the crops. The one who labors faithfully can look forward to enjoying the fruit of that labor.

“The hardworking farmer must be first to partake of the crops.”

The third picture is a farmer. The emphasis falls on “hardworking.” Farming demands steady, often exhausting labor: plowing, planting, weeding, watering, and harvesting. A lazy farmer gets little harvest. The hardworking farmer “must be first to partake of the crops.” The one who has labored has the first right to share in the fruit of the harvest.

Paul’s point is that faithful labor leads to reward. Timothy’s work in teaching and ministry may be long and tiring, but it is not in vain. The one who works hard for the Lord will share in the harvest. The three pictures fit together: the soldier stresses single-minded devotion, the athlete stresses obedience to the rules, and the farmer stresses steady labor. All three look forward to a reward.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “hardworking” means to labor to the point of weariness. Paul uses the same verb for his own labor in ministry (for example, 1 Timothy 4:10, “we both labor and suffer reproach”).

Some interpreters read this verse as teaching that ministers deserve material support from those they serve, as Paul argues in 1 Corinthians 9:7–11. In this passage the emphasis falls on faithful labor and its reward, and the verse does not mention payment.

2 Timothy 2:7
2 Timothy 2:7
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:7 Consider what I say, and may the Lord give you understanding in all things.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:7 Think about what I am saying. The Lord will help you understand all these things. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul tells Timothy to consider what he has said about the soldier, the athlete, and the farmer, and he prays that the Lord will give him understanding in all things. Timothy must think the images through, while the understanding itself comes as the Lord’s gift.

Verse Explanation

Paul tells Timothy to consider what he has said about the soldier, the athlete, and the farmer, and he prays that the Lord will give him understanding in all things. Timothy must think the images through, while the understanding itself comes as the Lord’s gift.

“Consider what I say,”

Paul does not explain every detail of his three pictures. He tells Timothy to “consider” them, to think them through and work out what they mean for his ministry. Paul trusts Timothy to reflect on what he has said and apply it.

“and may the Lord give you understanding in all things.”

Thinking is Timothy’s part, but understanding is the Lord’s gift. Paul prays that “the Lord” will “give you understanding in all things.” Careful thought and dependence on God belong together. Timothy must use his mind, and he must look to the Lord to grant insight. Neither replaces the other.

Study Notes

The Greek text behind the NKJV reads “may the Lord give you understanding.” Many other manuscripts read a promise that the Lord will give understanding, rather than a prayer. Both readings agree that understanding comes from the Lord.

The Greek verb translated “consider” means to think about something carefully, to reflect on it and grasp its meaning.

2 Timothy 2:8–10 — Remember the Risen Christ and the Unchained Word

2 Timothy 2:8
2 Timothy 2:8
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:8 Remember that Jesus Christ, of the seed of David, was raised from the dead according to my gospel,(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:8 Always remember that Jesus Christ, a descendant of King David, was raised from the dead. This is the Good News I preach. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul calls Timothy to remember that Jesus Christ, of the seed of David, was raised from the dead according to Paul’s gospel. Christ’s descent from David ties Him to God’s promises to David’s line, and His resurrection stands at the center of the message Timothy must hold.

Verse Explanation

Paul calls Timothy to remember that Jesus Christ, of the seed of David, was raised from the dead according to Paul’s gospel. Christ’s descent from David ties Him to God’s promises to David’s line, and His resurrection stands at the center of the message Timothy must hold.

“Remember that Jesus Christ,”

Paul now gives Timothy the center of the gospel to hold onto. “Remember.” In the middle of hardship, Timothy must keep Jesus Christ before his mind. Remembering is more than recalling facts. It means keeping Christ at the center of his thoughts and drawing strength from who He is and what He has done.

“of the seed of David,”

Jesus is “of the seed of David.” He descended from King David. God had promised David that one of his descendants would reign on his throne forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16). Jesus is that descendant. By naming David, Paul reminds Timothy that the gospel is the fulfillment of God’s ancient promises. Jesus is the promised King.

His descent from David also points to His full humanity. Jesus was a real man, born into a real family line. He suffered as a man and died as a man, which makes Him able to understand and help those who suffer.

“was raised from the dead”

Jesus “was raised from the dead.” This is the heart of the gospel. Jesus died and rose again. His resurrection proves that He is who He claimed to be, that His sacrifice was accepted, and that death has been defeated (1:10). For Timothy, who faced suffering and possibly death, the resurrection was the ground of hope. If Jesus rose, then those who belong to Him will rise too. This matters all the more in this chapter, because some false teachers were saying the resurrection had already happened (2:18).

“according to my gospel,”

This is “my gospel,” the gospel Paul preached. Paul does not mean that he invented it. He means it was the gospel entrusted to him, the message he proclaimed and for which he suffered. It was the same gospel he had handed to Timothy.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “was raised” describes a completed act with continuing results: Jesus was raised, and He remains the risen One.

Paul uses the phrase “according to my gospel” in Romans 2:16 and Romans 16:25 as well.

Paul names David’s line and the resurrection together at the opening of Romans, where Jesus Christ is “born of the seed of David according to the flesh” and declared to be the Son of God with power “by the resurrection from the dead” (Romans 1:3–4).

2 Timothy 2:9
2 Timothy 2:9
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:9 for which I suffer trouble as an evildoer, even to the point of chains; but the word of God is not chained.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:9 And because I preach this Good News, I am suffering and have been chained like a criminal. But the word of God cannot be chained. (NLT)

Main Idea

For that gospel Paul suffers trouble as if he were an evildoer, even to the point of chains. Yet “the word of God is not chained,” and the message keeps advancing while its messenger is bound.

Verse Explanation

For that gospel Paul suffers trouble as if he were an evildoer, even to the point of chains. Yet “the word of God is not chained,” and the message keeps advancing while its messenger is bound.

“for which I suffer trouble as an evildoer, even to the point of chains;”

Paul suffers “for” the gospel, because of it. He says, “I suffer trouble as an evildoer.” The word “evildoer” means a criminal, someone who commits serious crimes. Paul is being treated like a common criminal, not because he has done wrong, but because he preaches Christ. It is the shame Paul urged Timothy not to fear (1:8).

His suffering goes “even to the point of chains.” Paul is literally bound. This is the reality of his situation in Rome.

“but the word of God is not chained.”

“But” brings a triumphant contrast. Paul is chained, but “the word of God is not chained.” The messenger can be bound, but the message cannot. While Paul sits in prison, the gospel continues to spread through others, through his letters, and through the testimony of his suffering. No prison can stop God’s word from doing its work.

This was encouraging for Timothy. Paul’s chains might make it seem as if the gospel was losing ground. Paul assures him otherwise. God’s word is still free and still advancing.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “evildoer” is the same word Luke uses for the criminals crucified with Jesus (Luke 23:32, “two others, criminals”). Paul’s treatment as a criminal resembles his Lord’s.

The Greek verb translated “is not chained” is the passive form of the verb for binding, and the word for “chains” earlier in the verse comes from the same root. Paul’s wording sets the two side by side: Paul wears chains, but the word of God is not bound.

2 Timothy 2:10
2 Timothy 2:10
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:10 Therefore I endure all things for the sake of the elect, that they also may obtain the salvation which is in Christ Jesus with eternal glory.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:10 So I am willing to endure anything if it will bring salvation and eternal glory in Christ Jesus to those God has chosen. (NLT)

Main Idea

Therefore Paul endures all things for the sake of the elect, the people God has chosen, so that they also may obtain the salvation which is in Christ Jesus with eternal glory. His suffering serves the salvation of others.

Verse Explanation

Therefore Paul endures all things for the sake of the elect, the people God has chosen, so that they also may obtain the salvation which is in Christ Jesus with eternal glory. His suffering serves the salvation of others.

“Therefore I endure all things for the sake of the elect,”

“Therefore” points back to the unchained word. Because God’s word is still at work, Paul’s suffering is not wasted. He can say, “I endure all things,” bearing up under hardship without giving up. He does it “for the sake of the elect.” The elect are the people God has chosen. Paul suffers so that they may be reached and strengthened.

God’s choosing does not make Paul’s work unnecessary. Paul knows that God has chosen people, and that is exactly why he labors and endures. God uses the preaching of the gospel and the faithfulness of His servants to bring His chosen ones to salvation.

“that they also may obtain the salvation which is in Christ Jesus”

Paul’s goal is “that they also may obtain the salvation which is in Christ Jesus.” He wants others to receive the salvation he has received. Salvation is “in Christ Jesus.” It is found only in union with Him.

“with eternal glory.”

This salvation comes “with eternal glory.” It includes not only rescue from sin now but the glory of God’s presence forever. Present suffering is temporary. The glory that follows is eternal. Paul endures now because he knows what is coming for those who belong to Christ.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “endure” is related to the word used in 2:12 (“If we endure”). It means to remain under a burden, bearing it patiently without giving up.

The Greek word translated “elect” means chosen ones. Paul uses the same word in Titus 1:1, “according to the faith of God’s elect.”

2 Timothy 2:11–13 — The Faithful Saying

2 Timothy 2:11
2 Timothy 2:11
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:11 This is a faithful saying: For if we died with Him, We shall also live with Him.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:11 This is a trustworthy saying: If we die with him,we will also live with him. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul introduces a faithful, trustworthy saying that begins with a promise: if we died with Christ, we shall also live with Him. Those who share in His death will share in His risen life.

Verse Explanation

Paul introduces a faithful, trustworthy saying that begins with a promise: if we died with Christ, we shall also live with Him. Those who share in His death will share in His risen life.

“This is a faithful saying:”

Paul introduces a “faithful saying,” a trustworthy statement. This phrase appears several times in Paul’s letters to Timothy and Titus, always to mark a statement that can be fully relied on. The saying that follows has four lines, each beginning with “if.” The four lines belong together and must be read as one whole. Two lines promise, one warns, and the last rests everything on Christ’s faithfulness.

“For if we died with Him,”

The first line looks back to the believer’s union with Christ in His death. “If we died with Him” describes what happened when a person came to faith in Christ. Believers share in Christ’s death. Their old life under sin and self has come to an end. Paul taught the same truth in Romans 6:8: “Now if we died with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with Him.”

In the setting of this letter, the words may also include dying for Christ’s sake. Paul expects to be executed soon (4:6). For him, dying with Christ was about to become very literal. Whether the death is spiritual or physical, the promise is the same.

“We shall also live with Him.”

The promise follows: “We shall also live with Him.” Those who share in Christ’s death will share in His risen life. Death is not the end for those who belong to Christ. They will live with Him now and forever. This is the hope that makes suffering for the gospel bearable.

Study Notes

“This is a faithful saying” occurs five times in the Pastoral Letters (1 Timothy 1:15; 3:1; 4:9; 2 Timothy 2:11; Titus 3:8). In each case it marks a statement of special importance.

The Greek verb translated “died with” describes a completed act. It points to a decisive death with Christ rather than a continuing process.

The lines of 2:11–13 have a balanced, rhythmic form, and many scholars think they may come from an early Christian hymn or confession. The NKJV prints them as poetry.

2 Timothy 2:12
2 Timothy 2:12
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:12 If we endure, We shall also reign with Him. If we deny Him, He also will deny us.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:12 If we endure hardship,we will reign with him.If we deny him,he will deny us. (NLT)

Main Idea

The saying continues with a second promise, that those who endure will also reign with Christ, and then a warning: “If we deny Him, He also will deny us.” Read with the whole saying, the warning concerns a settled, final denial of Christ, which meets His denial.

Verse Explanation

The saying continues with a second promise, that those who endure will also reign with Christ, and then a warning: “If we deny Him, He also will deny us.” Read with the whole saying, the warning concerns a settled, final denial of Christ, which meets His denial.

“If we endure, We shall also reign with Him.”

The second line promises reward for faithfulness. “If we endure” means continuing to stand firm under hardship and pressure, without giving up. Paul has just said that he endures all things (2:10). Timothy is called to endure as well (2:3).

The promise is “We shall also reign with Him.” Those who endure will share in Christ’s rule when His kingdom comes in fullness. Suffering now leads to reigning later. Believers who endure are not crushed by their trials. They are on their way to the throne.

“If we deny Him, He also will deny us.”

The third line is a serious warning. “If we deny Him” means to disown Christ, to refuse to acknowledge Him as Lord, to turn away from Him. Jesus Himself said, “But whoever denies Me before men, him I will also deny before My Father who is in heaven” (Matthew 10:33).

The consequence is solemn: “He also will deny us.” Christ will not acknowledge as His own those who have finally disowned Him. Read with the whole saying, this warning concerns a settled, final denial of Christ, a turning away that is never repented of. Christ means what He says, and the warning must be heard at full weight.

The Gospels show the difference between a fall and a final denial. Peter denied Jesus three times, yet he repented, wept bitterly, and was restored by the risen Lord (Luke 22:31–32; John 21:15–17). Judas betrayed Jesus and did not return (Matthew 26:47–50; 27:3–5). Peter’s story shows that a believer who falls still has room to repent. The warning in this line concerns those who finally and lastingly deny Christ. It is not meant to drive struggling believers to despair, and it does not give anyone permission to treat denial lightly.

Study Notes

The two conditions use different verb forms in Greek. “If we endure” describes continuing endurance. “If we deny” is in a future tense in many manuscripts, looking ahead to a possible act of denial. The tense alone does not show that the denial is final; that reading comes from the saying as a whole and from Jesus’ words in Matthew 10:33.

The Greek verb translated “deny” is the same word used in Matthew 10:33 and in the accounts of Peter’s denial (for example, Matthew 26:72, “But again he denied with an oath”).

2 Timothy 2:13
2 Timothy 2:13
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:13 If we are faithless, He remains faithful; He cannot deny Himself.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:13 If we are unfaithful,he remains faithful,for he cannot deny who he is. (NLT)

Main Idea

The last line says that if we are faithless, He remains faithful, because He cannot deny Himself. The saying rests on Christ’s unchanging character, so that He stays true both to His promises and to His warning.

Verse Explanation

The last line says that if we are faithless, He remains faithful, because He cannot deny Himself. The saying rests on Christ’s unchanging character, so that He stays true both to His promises and to His warning.

“If we are faithless,”

The fourth line is the most surprising. “If we are faithless” means to be unfaithful, to fail in trust or loyalty. The Greek word can mean either disbelieving or being unfaithful. The line names failure toward Christ without saying how far it goes, and the rest of the line shows why the saying carries both comfort and warning.

“He remains faithful;”

The answer is “He remains faithful.” Christ’s faithfulness does not depend on ours. He keeps His word whether we are strong or weak. This line rests the whole saying on Christ’s unchanging character. He will keep His promises to those who died with Him and endure (2:11–12). He will also keep His warning to those who finally deny Him (2:12).

“He cannot deny Himself.”

The reason is that “He cannot deny Himself.” Christ cannot act against His own nature or break His own word. If He promised life to those who trust Him, He will give it. If He warned of judgment for those who deny Him, that warning stands. His faithfulness guarantees both the promise and the warning.

This line gives hope to a believer who has stumbled. Christ’s faithfulness is stronger than our failures. He does not abandon His people when they waver. But it also guards against careless confidence. Christ’s faithfulness does not mean He will ignore final denial. He remains true to everything He has said.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “are faithless” is built from the word for faith with a negative prefix. The setting, a saying about loyalty to Christ, favors the sense of unfaithfulness here.

Interpreters have read this line in two main ways: as a promise that Christ keeps His own when they fail, or as a reinforcement of the warning in 2:12. The four-line arrangement helps explain why both readings arise. In the first three lines, what we do is answered by a matching result. This line breaks the pattern: our faithlessness is not answered by His faithlessness. The reason given, “He cannot deny Himself,” points to Christ’s own character rather than to a matching reward or penalty.

The phrase “He cannot deny Himself” echoes the Old Testament conviction that God does not lie or go back on His word (for example, Numbers 23:19; Titus 1:2).

2 Timothy 2:14–18 — The Approved Worker and the Spreading Error

2 Timothy 2:14
2 Timothy 2:14
NKJV

2 Timothy 2:14 Remind them of these things, charging them before the Lord not to strive about words to no profit, to the ruin of the hearers.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 2:14 Remind everyone about these things, and command them in God’s presence to stop fighting over words. Such arguments are useless, and they can ruin those who hear them. (NLT)

Main Idea

Timothy must remind those he teaches of these things and charge them before the Lord not to strive about words. Such quarrels profit no one and bring ruin to those who listen.

Verse Explanation

Timothy must remind those he teaches of these things and charge them before the Lord not to strive about words. Such quarrels profit no one and bring ruin to those who listen.

“Remind them of these things,”

Paul turns to Timothy’s ministry among others. Timothy must “remind them of these things.” The word “them” most likely refers to the faithful men of 2:2 and to the church Timothy serves. “These things” are the truths Paul has just given, especially the faithful saying in 2:11–13. Believers need constant reminding of what they already know.

“charging them before the Lord”

Timothy must charge them “before the Lord.” This is a solemn command given in the Lord’s presence. It reminds everyone that they answer to Christ for how they handle His truth.

“not to strive about words to no profit,”

The charge is “not to strive about words.” This describes fighting over words, arguing about terms, phrases, or fine distinctions in ways that lead nowhere. The false teachers in Ephesus loved this kind of debate. Paul says it is “to no profit.” It does not build anyone up, deepen faith, or advance the gospel.

Worse, word fights lead “to the ruin of the hearers.” The people who listen to such quarrels are harmed. They become confused, unsettled, or drawn away from simple faith in Christ. Paul wants Timothy to protect believers from teaching that tears them down.

The Greek word translated “ruin” means overthrow or destruction. It is related to the English word “catastrophe.”

“and they overthrow the faith of some.”

The result was that “they overthrow the faith of some.” Their teaching was not merely mistaken. It was destructive. People who accepted it were losing their faith. When the hope of resurrection is removed, faith loses its foundation. Paul names this error because it was doing real damage to real people.

NKJV

NLT

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Nevertheless, God’s solid foundation stands, bearing a seal with two inscriptions: “The Lord knows those who are His,” and “Let everyone who names the name of Christ depart from iniquity.” God’s knowledge of His own gives security, and turning from iniquity marks those who belong to Him.

“Nevertheless the solid foundation of God stands,”

“Nevertheless” brings hope after the bad news of 2:17–18. Some people’s faith is being overthrown, but “the solid foundation of God stands.” God’s foundation is firm and unshaken. False teachers can unsettle individuals, but they cannot destroy what God has built.

The verse does not say exactly what the “foundation” is. It is most often understood as the church, God’s people whom He has established and who rest on Christ. Others take it as God’s truth or God’s work of salvation. Each reading points to the same reality: what God has built stands secure.

“having this seal:”

The foundation bears a “seal.” In the ancient world, a seal marked ownership and guaranteed authenticity. Inscriptions were sometimes carved on a building’s foundation stones. God’s foundation has two inscriptions.

“The Lord knows those who are His,”

The first inscription is “The Lord knows those who are His.” These words recall Numbers 16:5, where Moses said, “Tomorrow morning the LORD will show who is His and who is holy.” In that story, Korah and his followers rebelled against Moses and Aaron, and God made clear who truly belonged to Him.

For Timothy, this was a great comfort. False teachers might confuse many, but the Lord knows His own. He is not confused about who belongs to Him. No error can snatch His people from Him. Their security rests on His knowledge of them, not on their own grasp of the truth.

“and, “Let everyone who names the name of Christ depart from iniquity.””

The second inscription balances the first: “Let everyone who names the name of Christ depart from iniquity.” Those who claim to belong to Christ must turn away from sin. In Numbers 16 Moses told the people to “Depart now from the tents of these wicked men!” (Numbers 16:26). Those who belong to the Lord separate themselves from evil.

The two inscriptions belong together. God knows His people, which gives them security. His people turn from iniquity, which gives evidence that they belong to Him. Both sides are true. God’s knowledge comes first, and holy living follows. In the setting of this chapter, departing from iniquity includes turning away from the false teaching of 2:16–18.

Study Notes

The first inscription closely matches the Greek translation of Numbers 16:5, which reads, in English, God knew those who are His. The second inscription is not a direct quotation. It draws on Old Testament language such as Numbers 16:26 and Isaiah 52:11 (“Depart! Depart! Go out from there, Touch no unclean thing”).

The Greek text behind the NKJV reads “the name of Christ.” Many other manuscripts read the Lord in place of Christ, and the NLT reads “All who belong to the LORD.” The meaning is the same.

The Greek word translated “seal” refers to the mark of ownership pressed into wax or clay, or the inscription carved on a stone. It marked something as belonging to its owner and authenticated it.
