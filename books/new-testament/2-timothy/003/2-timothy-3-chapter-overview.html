Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 3
Main Idea:

Against the self-centered loves and empty religion of the last days, Paul twice turns to Timothy with “But you,” pointing him to the life he has seen in Paul and to the God-given Scriptures that make him wise for salvation and equip him for every good work.

Setting:

Paul’s command “from such people turn away!” (3:5) shows that Timothy already faces the dangers Paul describes. The last days are best read as the period from Pentecost to Christ’s return (compare Acts 2:16–21), so Timothy is living in them.

Chapter Outline:

  • 2 Timothy 3:1–5 — Perilous Times and a Form of Godliness

  • 2 Timothy 3:6–9 — Deceivers Who Resist the Truth

  • 2 Timothy 3:10–13 — Paul’s Example Amid Persecution

Key Verse:

“All Scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness, that the man of God may be complete, thoroughly equipped for every good work.” (2 Timothy 3:16–17)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Paul’s description of the last days begins and ends with love that has gone wrong. People will be “lovers of themselves, lovers of money” (3:2), and the list closes with “lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God” (3:4). Between those lines comes what follows when God is not loved first: ingratitude, slander, cruelty, betrayal, and contempt for what is good. Most sobering of all, these people keep “a form of godliness” while denying its power (3:5). The danger Paul names looks religious.

Some of these people slip into households and take captive those weighed down by sin, who are “always learning and never able to come to the knowledge of the truth” (3:7). Like Jannes and Jambres, who opposed Moses, they resist the truth, though their folly will become plain to all (3:8–9). Learning that never arrives at the truth leaves people open to deception.

Twice Paul turns to Timothy with “But you” (3:10, 14). First he points to his own life. Timothy has watched Paul’s teaching, faith, longsuffering, love, and perseverance, and he knows how the Lord delivered Paul out of the persecutions at Antioch, Iconium, and Lystra (3:10–11). Then Paul points to the Scriptures Timothy has known since childhood, which make him “wise for salvation through faith which is in Christ Jesus” (3:15). Because “All Scripture is given by inspiration of God,” it teaches, rebukes, corrects, and trains, so that the man of God is “thoroughly equipped for every good work” (3:16–17). Timothy needs no new message to face the deceivers. The Word he already has is enough, and it is the Word Paul will charge him to preach in 4:1–5.

Chapter Sections

2 Timothy 3:1–5 — Perilous Times and a Form of Godliness

Paul warns that the last days will bring perilous times, marked by loves turned toward self, money, and pleasure rather than toward God. Such people keep the outward appearance of godliness while denying its power, and Timothy must turn away from them.

2 Timothy 3:6–9 — Deceivers Who Resist the Truth

Some of these people work their way into households and take captive those burdened by sin, who keep learning without ever reaching the truth. Like Jannes and Jambres, who opposed Moses, they resist the truth, but their advance has limits and their folly will become plain.

2 Timothy 3:10–13 — Paul’s Example Amid Persecution

Timothy has closely followed Paul’s teaching and way of life and has seen his faith, love, and endurance through persecutions the Lord delivered him from. All who desire to live godly lives in Christ Jesus will be persecuted, while evil men and impostors grow worse, deceiving others and being deceived themselves.

2 Timothy 3:14–17 — Continuing in the Scriptures Given by God

Timothy must continue in what he learned from teachers he can trust and in the Holy Scriptures he has known since childhood, which lead to salvation through faith in Christ Jesus. All Scripture comes from God and is useful to teach, reprove, correct, and train in righteousness, so that God’s servant is fully equipped for every good work.

Why This Chapter Matters

Second Timothy 3 contains one of the Bible’s clearest statements about Scripture. The Holy Scriptures Timothy knew from childhood were Israel’s sacred writings (3:15), but Paul’s statement reaches to “All Scripture” (3:16). God inspired every word of Scripture, in both the Old and New Testaments, working through the human writers, and the original writings are true in everything they affirm. Paul ties its authority and sufficiency to its purpose: Scripture makes people wise for salvation through faith in Christ and fully equips God’s servant for the work God gives (3:15–17). Paul says this in the middle of a crisis of deception, and his answer to that crisis is to continue in the Word.

The chapter also measures religion by what it produces. The people of the last days have the form of godliness without its power, while Timothy has seen Paul’s doctrine joined to faith, love, and perseverance (3:10). God gives His Word to bring people to Christ and make them like Him, and knowledge that leaves the heart turned toward self has missed that purpose.

Key Themes to Watch For

Disordered loves. The last days are marked by love turned toward self, money, and pleasure rather than toward God (3:2–4).

Form without power. Outward religion can continue while its power is denied (3:5).

Learning that leads to truth. Endless learning that never reaches the truth stands against the Scriptures that lead to salvation in Christ (3:7, 14–15).

Persecution and deliverance. A godly life in Christ meets persecution, and the Lord delivered Paul out of it (3:11–12).

Scripture from God. All Scripture comes from God and equips His servant for every good work (3:16–17).

Key Words & Concepts

The last days (3:1). Best read as the whole period from the coming of the Spirit at Pentecost to Christ’s return. That span comes from reading this passage together with Acts 2:16–21, where Peter applies Joel’s prophecy about the last days to Pentecost and speaks of the coming day of the Lord. This passage itself does not mention Pentecost and gives no timetable for Christ’s return.

A form of godliness (3:5). The outward shape of religion. These people keep its appearance while denying the power that makes a life godly.

Jannes and Jambres (3:8). These names do not appear in the Old Testament. They are most likely the names Jewish tradition gave to Pharaoh’s magicians, who imitated Moses’ signs (Exodus 7:11, 22).

The Holy Scriptures (3:15). The sacred writings of Israel, our Old Testament, which Timothy learned from childhood. Paul says they lead to salvation through faith in Christ Jesus.

Given by inspiration of God (3:16). The Greek word means “God-breathed.” God inspired every word of Scripture in both Testaments through the human writers He guided, so the original writings carry His authority, are true in everything they affirm, and can do the saving and equipping work Paul describes.

The man of God (3:17). An Old Testament title for God’s servants, used of Moses (Deuteronomy 33:1) and of prophets. Paul uses it of Timothy (1 Timothy 6:11), and here it describes the servant God equips through His Word.
