Chapter Study

2 Timothy 4

Verse-by-Verse
2 Timothy 4:1
2 Timothy 4:1
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:1 I charge you therefore before God and the Lord Jesus Christ, who will judge the living and the dead at His appearing and His kingdom:(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:1 I solemnly urge you in the presence of God and Christ Jesus, who will someday judge the living and the dead when he comes to set up his Kingdom: (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul solemnly charges Timothy before God and the Lord Jesus Christ, who will judge the living and the dead at His appearing and His kingdom. Christ’s future appearing, kingdom, and judgment give weight to everything Paul is about to command.

Verse Explanation

Paul solemnly charges Timothy before God and the Lord Jesus Christ, who will judge the living and the dead at His appearing and His kingdom. Christ’s future appearing, kingdom, and judgment give weight to everything Paul is about to command.

“I charge you therefore before God and the Lord Jesus Christ,”

Paul gives Timothy his most solemn charge. “Therefore” ties it to what he has just said about Scripture (3:14–17). Because Scripture is God-breathed and fully equips the man of God, Timothy must preach it.

The charge is given “before God and the Lord Jesus Christ.” Paul calls God and Christ as witnesses. Timothy is not answering only to Paul. He stands in the presence of God and of Christ, and he will give account to them for how he carries out his ministry.

“who will judge the living and the dead”

Christ “will judge the living and the dead.” Everyone, those alive at His return and those who have died, will stand before Him. Peter preached the same truth: Christ “was ordained by God to be Judge of the living and the dead” (Acts 10:42). Timothy’s ministry will be evaluated by the One who judges all people. That gives weight to everything Paul is about to command.

“at His appearing and His kingdom:”

The judgment will take place “at His appearing and His kingdom.” “His appearing” refers to Christ’s return. In 1:10 the same word referred to His first coming. Now it points to His second coming, when He will be revealed in glory. “His kingdom” refers to His reign, which will be fully established when He returns. Christ’s future appearing, kingdom, and judgment are the backdrop for Timothy’s charge. Timothy serves now in light of what is coming.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “charge” means to testify solemnly or to warn earnestly. Paul uses the same verb in 2:14 (“charging them before the Lord”) and in 1 Timothy 5:21.

The text behind the NKJV includes “therefore” and reads “at His appearing.” Many other manuscripts omit “therefore” and read and by His appearing, making Christ’s appearing and kingdom part of what Paul swears by. The NLT reads “when he comes to set up his Kingdom.” Either way, Christ’s appearing and kingdom give the charge its weight.

2 Timothy 4:2
2 Timothy 4:2
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:2 Preach the word! Be ready in season and out of season. Convince, rebuke, exhort, with all longsuffering and teaching.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:2 Preach the word of God. Be prepared, whether the time is favorable or not. Patiently correct, rebuke, and encourage your people with good teaching. (NLT)

Main Idea

Timothy must preach the word and be ready in season and out of season, convincing, rebuking, and exhorting with all longsuffering and teaching. He is to speak with courage whether the moment seems right or not, yet always with patience and careful instruction.

Verse Explanation

Timothy must preach the word and be ready in season and out of season, convincing, rebuking, and exhorting with all longsuffering and teaching. He is to speak with courage whether the moment seems right or not, yet always with patience and careful instruction.

“Preach the word!”

The charge is short and direct: “Preach the word!” “Preach” means to proclaim like a herald, to announce publicly with authority. The same word family describes Paul as “a preacher” in 1:11. “The word” is God’s word, the Scripture Paul has just described (3:16–17) and the gospel message at its center. Timothy is not to preach his own ideas or popular opinions. He is to proclaim what God has said.

“Be ready in season and out of season.”

Timothy must “be ready in season and out of season.” “Be ready” means to stand by, to be prepared and on duty. “In season” means when the time is favorable, when people want to hear. “Out of season” means when the time is unfavorable, when people do not want to hear. Timothy must preach whether the moment seems convenient or not, whether it is welcomed or resisted. He cannot wait for ideal conditions.

“Convince, rebuke, exhort,”

Paul names three tasks. “Convince” means to show people their error, to prove what is wrong, to bring conviction. “Rebuke” means to warn sternly and confront wrongdoing. “Exhort” means to encourage, urge, and comfort. Timothy’s preaching must sometimes expose, sometimes confront, and sometimes encourage. Faithful preaching includes all three.

“with all longsuffering and teaching.”

These tasks must be done “with all longsuffering and teaching.” “Longsuffering” is patience with people. Timothy must not lose his temper or give up when people are slow to respond. “Teaching” is careful instruction. Correction without instruction leaves people knowing they are wrong but not knowing what is right. Timothy must explain the truth patiently and clearly.

The balance matters. Timothy must speak with courage whether the moment seems right or not, yet always with patience and careful instruction. Paul has already said that the Lord’s servant must be “gentle to all, able to teach, patient” (2:24). Bold preaching and patient care belong together.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “preach” is related to the word for a herald, the official who publicly announced a ruler’s messages.

The phrase “in season and out of season” translates two Greek adverbs that differ by a single prefix. The wordplay presents the two as opposites that together cover every occasion.

The Greek verb translated “convince” is related to the noun translated “reproof” in 3:16. What Scripture does, Timothy must do as he preaches it.

2 Timothy 4:3
2 Timothy 4:3
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:3 For the time will come when they will not endure sound doctrine, but according to their own desires, because they have itching ears, they will heap up for themselves teachers;(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:3 For a time is coming when people will no longer listen to sound and wholesome teaching. They will follow their own desires and will look for teachers who will tell them whatever their itching ears want to hear. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul warns that a time will come when people will not endure sound doctrine. Because they have itching ears, they will heap up for themselves teachers who suit their own desires.

Verse Explanation

Paul warns that a time will come when people will not endure sound doctrine. Because they have itching ears, they will heap up for themselves teachers who suit their own desires.

“For the time will come when they will not endure sound doctrine,”

“For” gives the reason for urgency. “The time will come when they will not endure sound doctrine.” People will refuse to put up with healthy, life-giving teaching. “Sound doctrine” is the healthy teaching Paul has spoken of throughout his letters to Timothy (compare 1:13). Some will find it unbearable, because it confronts their sins and calls them to change.

“but according to their own desires,”

Instead of sound doctrine, they will follow “their own desires.” Their wishes, not God’s truth, will set the standard for what they want to hear.

“because they have itching ears,”

They have “itching ears.” The picture is of ears that want to be tickled, to hear something pleasing, new, or exciting. People with itching ears want to be entertained and soothed, not challenged or corrected.

“they will heap up for themselves teachers;”

To satisfy their itching ears, “they will heap up for themselves teachers.” “Heap up” pictures piling up teachers, one after another. They gather teachers who say what they want to hear. They are not looking for truth. They are looking for teachers who suit their own desires. When one teacher fails to please them, they find another.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “endure” means to bear with or put up with. Paul uses it in 2 Corinthians 11:1, “do bear with me.”

The Greek phrase translated “itching ears” uses a verb meaning to scratch or tickle. It describes hearers who want their ears pleased.

The Greek verb translated “heap up” means to pile up or accumulate. It appears only here in the New Testament.

2 Timothy 4:4
2 Timothy 4:4
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:4 and they will turn their ears away from the truth, and be turned aside to fables.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:4 They will reject the truth and chase after myths. (NLT)

Main Idea

Such hearers will turn their ears away from the truth and be turned aside to fables. Rejecting the truth leaves them open to stories that have no substance.

Verse Explanation

Such hearers will turn their ears away from the truth and be turned aside to fables. Rejecting the truth leaves them open to stories that have no substance.

“and they will turn their ears away from the truth,”

The result is predictable. “They will turn their ears away from the truth.” They refuse to listen to what is true. They deliberately turn away from it because the truth does not please them.

“and be turned aside to fables.”

Having turned from the truth, they will “be turned aside to fables.” “Fables” are myths, stories without substance or truth. People who reject the truth do not stop believing things. They believe falsehoods instead. Rejecting the truth leaves them open to stories that have no substance.

The wording has a meaningful contrast. First they actively “turn their ears away.” Then they “be turned aside,” a passive form. Those who choose to turn from the truth end up being carried away by error they no longer control.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “fables” is the word from which English gets “myth.” Paul warns against fables in 1 Timothy 1:4; 4:7 and Titus 1:14.

The Greek verb translated “be turned aside” is used in 1 Timothy 1:6 and 1 Timothy 5:15 for people who have turned away from the truth or the faith.

2 Timothy 4:5
2 Timothy 4:5
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:5 But you be watchful in all things, endure afflictions, do the work of an evangelist, fulfill your ministry.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:5 But you should keep a clear mind in every situation. Don’t be afraid of suffering for the Lord. Work at telling others the Good News, and fully carry out the ministry God has given you. (NLT)

Main Idea

But Timothy must be watchful in all things, endure afflictions, do the work of an evangelist, and fulfill his ministry. Whatever his hearers do, he must stay alert and complete the work God has given him.

Verse Explanation

But Timothy must be watchful in all things, endure afflictions, do the work of an evangelist, and fulfill his ministry. Whatever his hearers do, he must stay alert and complete the work God has given him.

“But you be watchful in all things,”

“But you” turns again to Timothy, as in 3:10 and 3:14. Whatever others do, Timothy must stand firm. He must “be watchful in all things.” The word means to be sober, clear-headed, alert. While others are intoxicated by false teaching and drawn to fables, Timothy must keep his head clear. He must stay alert to danger and keep his thinking sound.

“endure afflictions,”

He must “endure afflictions.” Faithful ministry will bring suffering. Timothy must accept it and keep going. Paul has made this appeal throughout the letter (1:8; 2:3).

“do the work of an evangelist,”

He must “do the work of an evangelist.” An evangelist proclaims the good news of Jesus Christ. Timothy must keep the gospel at the center of his ministry. He is not only to correct error and teach believers. He must also proclaim the good news to those who do not know Christ.

“fulfill your ministry.”

Finally, Timothy must “fulfill your ministry.” He must carry out his calling fully and completely, leaving nothing undone. Paul is about to describe his own finished course (4:7). Now Timothy must complete his. Whatever his hearers do, he must stay alert and complete the work God has given him.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “be watchful” means to be sober. Peter uses it in 1 Peter 5:8: “Be sober, be vigilant.”

The Greek word translated “evangelist” appears only three times in the New Testament: here, in Acts 21:8 (“Philip the evangelist”), and in Ephesians 4:11.

The Greek verb translated “fulfill” means to carry out fully. Paul uses it again in 4:17, where the message is “preached fully” through him.

2 Timothy 4:6–8 — Paul’s Finished Course and the Crown of Righteousness

2 Timothy 4:6
2 Timothy 4:6
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:6 For I am already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time of my departure is at hand.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:6 As for me, my life has already been poured out as an offering to God. The time of my death is near. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul says he is already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time of his departure is at hand. He describes his approaching death as an offering to God, and its nearness explains the urgency of his charge to Timothy.

Verse Explanation

Paul says he is already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time of his departure is at hand. He describes his approaching death as an offering to God, and its nearness explains the urgency of his charge to Timothy.

“For I am already being poured out as a drink offering,”

“For” explains the urgency of Paul’s charge. Paul is about to die. He says, “I am already being poured out as a drink offering.” In Israel’s worship, wine was poured out alongside certain sacrifices as an offering to God (Numbers 28:7). Paul sees his life as an offering poured out to God. His death will be the final pouring.

The word “already” shows that the process has begun. Paul’s life is being poured out even now, and death is near.

“and the time of my departure is at hand.”

“The time of my departure is at hand.” “Departure” pictures a ship loosing its moorings to set sail, or soldiers striking their tents to break camp. Death for Paul is not an end. It is a departure to a new place. He told the Philippians that he had “a desire to depart and be with Christ, which is far better” (Philippians 1:23).

Paul’s death “is at hand.” It is near. He describes his approaching death as an offering to God, and its nearness explains the urgency of his charge to Timothy. Paul will not be around much longer, and Timothy must carry on.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “being poured out as a drink offering” appears in only one other place in the New Testament, Philippians 2:17: “if I am being poured out as a drink offering on the sacrifice and service of your faith.”

The Greek word translated “departure” is used outside the New Testament for unmooring a ship or breaking camp. Paul uses the related verb in Philippians 1:23.

The letter does not say how or when Paul died. Early church tradition holds that he was executed in Rome under the emperor Nero, probably in the mid-60s AD.

2 Timothy 4:7
2 Timothy 4:7
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:7 I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:7 I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, and I have remained faithful. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul has fought the good fight, finished the race, and kept the faith. Looking back, he sees his life as a contest faithfully completed and a trust faithfully kept.

Verse Explanation

Paul has fought the good fight, finished the race, and kept the faith. Looking back, he sees his life as a contest faithfully completed and a trust faithfully kept.

“I have fought the good fight,”

Paul looks back on his life with three statements. First, “I have fought the good fight.” The picture is of an athletic contest, a struggle requiring effort and discipline. Paul’s life was a contest, and it was a “good” one because it was for Christ and the gospel. Paul has fought faithfully to the end.

“I have finished the race,”

Second, “I have finished the race.” Paul had run the course God marked out for him. He did not quit or turn aside. He told the Ephesian elders years earlier that he wanted to “finish my race with joy” (Acts 20:24). Now he has done it.

“I have kept the faith.”

Third, “I have kept the faith.” This can mean that Paul has guarded the gospel, the body of truth entrusted to him, or that he has remained faithful to Christ. Both are true. Paul has guarded what was entrusted to him, just as he urged Timothy to do (1:14), and he has remained loyal to his Lord.

Looking back, Paul sees his life as a contest faithfully completed and a trust faithfully kept. He is not boasting. He is testifying to God’s faithfulness in sustaining him, and he is showing Timothy what a finished course looks like.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “fight” describes an athletic contest or struggle. Paul uses the same phrase in 1 Timothy 6:12: “Fight the good fight of faith.”

The three Greek verbs in this verse describe completed actions with lasting results. Paul speaks of his life as already complete, though he had not yet died.

2 Timothy 4:8
2 Timothy 4:8
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:8 Finally, there is laid up for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous Judge, will give to me on that Day, and not to me only but also to all who have loved His appearing.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:8 And now the prize awaits me — the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous Judge, will give me on the day of his return. And the prize is not just for me but for all who eagerly look forward to his appearing. (NLT)

Main Idea

There is laid up for Paul the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous Judge, will give him on that Day as His final vindication and reward. The same crown awaits “all who have loved His appearing,” so Paul’s hope belongs to every believer who finishes in faith.

Verse Explanation

There is laid up for Paul the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous Judge, will give him on that Day as His final vindication and reward. The same crown awaits “all who have loved His appearing,” so Paul’s hope belongs to every believer who finishes in faith.

“Finally, there is laid up for me the crown of righteousness,”

“Finally” points to what remains. Paul’s contest is finished, and the reward awaits. “There is laid up for me the crown of righteousness.” “Laid up” means stored, reserved, set aside. The crown is waiting for Paul.

The “crown” is a victor’s wreath, the prize given to the winner of an athletic contest (compare 2:5). It is “the crown of righteousness.” The phrase is best read as Christ’s final vindication and the inheritance He gives to those who finish in faith. The righteous Judge declares His servant faithful and gives him the reward.

“which the Lord, the righteous Judge, will give to me on that Day,”

The crown is given by “the Lord, the righteous Judge.” Paul was about to face a Roman court that would condemn him unjustly. But Paul knew that a higher court and a righteous Judge would have the final word. Christ judges with perfect justice. He will reward faithfulness and set right every wrong.

He will give the crown “on that Day,” the day of His return (1:12, 18). Paul does not expect his reward from human judges. He waits for Christ’s verdict.

“and not to me only but also to all who have loved His appearing.”

The crown is “not to me only but also to all who have loved His appearing.” Paul’s hope is not unique to him. It belongs to every believer who longs for Christ’s return. “His appearing” is His second coming (4:1). Those who have “loved” it are those who eagerly long for Christ, who live in expectation of His return and want to see Him. Such love for His appearing grows from saving faith and shows itself in endurance. The same crown awaits all who have loved His appearing, so Paul’s hope belongs to every believer who finishes in faith.

Study Notes

The phrase “crown of righteousness” can be read in more than one way: a crown that rewards righteous living, a crown that consists of righteousness, or a crown given by the righteous Judge.

The Greek verb translated “have loved” is the same verb used in 4:10 for Demas, who “loved this present world.” The two verses set two loves side by side.

2 Timothy 4:9–13 — Come Quickly: Coworkers and Requests

2 Timothy 4:9
2 Timothy 4:9
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:9 Be diligent to come to me quickly;(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:9 Timothy, please come as soon as you can. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul urges Timothy to be diligent to come to him quickly. The request shows how much Paul wants Timothy with him as his life nears its end.

Verse Explanation

Paul urges Timothy to be diligent to come to him quickly. The request shows how much Paul wants Timothy with him as his life nears its end.

“Be diligent to come to me quickly;”

Paul turns from his solemn charge to personal requests. “Be diligent to come to me quickly.” “Be diligent” means to make every effort, to hurry. Paul wants Timothy to come “quickly.” Time is short (4:6). Paul longs to see his son in the faith before he dies (1:4). The request shows how much Paul wants Timothy with him as his life nears its end.

The apostle who has just spoken of his crown also needs a friend. Paul is facing death, and he wants Timothy beside him.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “be diligent” is the same verb used in 2:15 (“Be diligent to present yourself approved to God”) and 4:21 (“Do your utmost to come before winter”).

2 Timothy 4:10
2 Timothy 4:10
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:10 for Demas has forsaken me, having loved this present world, and has departed for Thessalonica"”Crescens for Galatia, Titus for Dalmatia.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:10 Demas has deserted me because he loves the things of this life and has gone to Thessalonica. Crescens has gone to Galatia, and Titus has gone to Dalmatia. (NLT)

Main Idea

Demas has forsaken Paul, “having loved this present world,” and has gone to Thessalonica, while Crescens has gone to Galatia and Titus to Dalmatia. Only Demas’s departure is described as desertion, and its cause was love for this present world.

Verse Explanation

Demas has forsaken Paul, “having loved this present world,” and has gone to Thessalonica, while Crescens has gone to Galatia and Titus to Dalmatia. Only Demas’s departure is described as desertion, and its cause was love for this present world.

“for Demas has forsaken me,”

“For” gives the reason for Paul’s request. “Demas has forsaken me.” Demas had been one of Paul’s coworkers. He is mentioned with Luke in Colossians 4:14, and Paul called him one of his “fellow laborers” in Philemon 1:24. Now Demas has left. “Forsaken” means abandoned, deserted. Demas left Paul when Paul needed him most.

“having loved this present world,”

Demas left because he “loved this present world.” He was drawn by the attractions of life in this age, whether comfort, safety, or something else. The letter does not say exactly what drew him. His love for this world outweighed his commitment to Paul and the gospel. Paul has just spoken of those who “loved His appearing” (4:8). Demas loved something else.

“and has departed for Thessalonica—”

Demas “departed for Thessalonica,” a major city in Macedonia, in what is now northern Greece. The letter does not say why he went there.

“Crescens for Galatia, Titus for Dalmatia.”

Paul mentions two other coworkers who had left. Crescens went to Galatia, a region in central Asia Minor. Titus went to Dalmatia, a region along the eastern coast of the Adriatic Sea. Paul does not describe their departures as desertion. He does not say why they went. Only Demas’s departure is described as desertion, and its cause was love for this present world.

Study Notes

Demas is mentioned in only three places: Colossians 4:14, Philemon 1:24, and here.

A few ancient manuscripts read “Gaul” in place of “Galatia.” The Greek name for Galatia could refer to the region in Asia Minor or to Gaul in western Europe, whose people were related to the Galatians.

Dalmatia was part of the region Paul calls Illyricum in Romans 15:19, along the eastern shore of the Adriatic Sea.

2 Timothy 4:11
2 Timothy 4:11
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:11 Only Luke is with me. Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for ministry.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:11 Only Luke is with me. Bring Mark with you when you come, for he will be helpful to me in my ministry. (NLT)

Main Idea

Only Luke is with Paul, and he asks Timothy to get Mark and bring him along, for Mark is useful to him for ministry. Paul values Mark’s help in the work.

Verse Explanation

Only Luke is with Paul, and he asks Timothy to get Mark and bring him along, for Mark is useful to him for ministry. Paul values Mark’s help in the work.

“Only Luke is with me.”

“Only Luke is with me.” Of Paul’s coworkers, only Luke remains. Luke was a physician (Colossians 4:14) and the writer of the Gospel of Luke and Acts. He had traveled with Paul for many years, and he stayed with Paul to the end. Paul’s loneliness is clear. Others have left, but Luke is still there.

“Get Mark and bring him with you,”

Paul asks Timothy to “get Mark and bring him with you.” Mark is most likely the John Mark of Acts, the cousin of Barnabas (Colossians 4:10). Early in Paul’s travels, Mark left the mission and went home (Acts 13:13). Later, Paul refused to take him on another journey, and the disagreement was so sharp that Paul and Barnabas parted ways (Acts 15:37–39). The letter does not say how the relationship was mended. By the time of Colossians and Philemon, Mark was with Paul again (Colossians 4:10; Philemon 1:24).

“for he is useful to me for ministry.”

Paul wants Mark “for he is useful to me for ministry.” The man Paul once refused to take along is now someone Paul specifically requests. Paul values Mark’s help in the work.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “useful” is the same word used in 2:21 for a vessel “useful for the Master.” Paul uses it also in Philemon 1:11 for Onesimus.

Luke uses the word “we” in several passages of Acts, including the voyage to Rome (for example, Acts 27:1–2), which indicates that he traveled with Paul.

2 Timothy 4:12
2 Timothy 4:12
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:12 And Tychicus I have sent to Ephesus.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:12 I sent Tychicus to Ephesus. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul has sent Tychicus to Ephesus. Even in prison, Paul continues to send coworkers where they are needed.

Verse Explanation

Paul has sent Tychicus to Ephesus. Even in prison, Paul continues to send coworkers where they are needed.

“And Tychicus I have sent to Ephesus.”

Tychicus was another of Paul’s trusted coworkers. He came from the province of Asia (Acts 20:4) and served as Paul’s messenger, carrying letters and news to the churches (Ephesians 6:21; Colossians 4:7). Now Paul has “sent” him “to Ephesus.”

Even in prison, Paul continues to send coworkers where they are needed. His concern for the churches did not stop because he was chained. The letter does not say why he sent Tychicus to Ephesus. Some suggest he carried this letter and was to take Timothy’s place while Timothy traveled to Rome. That is possible, though the letter does not say so.

Study Notes

Tychicus is named in Acts 20:4; Ephesians 6:21; Colossians 4:7; Titus 3:12; and here.

If Timothy was in Ephesus, as 1:18 and 1 Timothy 1:3 suggest, the wording “I have sent to Ephesus” is a little unexpected. Some take the verb as a letter writer’s way of describing a sending that happens as the letter goes out, with Tychicus carrying it. The letter leaves Timothy’s exact location open.

2 Timothy 4:13
2 Timothy 4:13
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:13 Bring the cloak that I left with Carpus at Troas when you come"”and the books, especially the parchments.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:13 When you come, be sure to bring the coat I left with Carpus at Troas. Also bring my books, and especially my papers. (NLT)

Main Idea

Timothy is to bring the cloak Paul left with Carpus at Troas when he comes, along with the books, especially the parchments. Even as he faces death, Paul still needs ordinary things: a cloak for warmth and his books and parchments.

Verse Explanation

Timothy is to bring the cloak Paul left with Carpus at Troas when he comes, along with the books, especially the parchments. Even as he faces death, Paul still needs ordinary things: a cloak for warmth and his books and parchments.

“Bring the cloak that I left with Carpus at Troas when you come—”

Paul makes a practical request. “Bring the cloak.” This was a heavy outer garment, a cloak worn for warmth and protection in cold weather. Paul had left it “with Carpus at Troas.” Troas was a seaport on the northwest coast of Asia Minor, on the route between Ephesus and Macedonia. Paul had visited it several times (Acts 16:8; 20:6). Carpus is not mentioned anywhere else. He was evidently a believer at Troas who kept Paul’s belongings.

Winter was coming (4:21), and Paul needed his cloak. The apostle, facing death, still felt the cold.

“and the books, especially the parchments.”

Paul also asks for “the books, especially the parchments.” “Books” were scrolls, usually made of papyrus. “Parchments” were made of animal skins and were more durable and more expensive. The letter does not say what they contained.

Even as he faces death, Paul still needs ordinary things: a cloak for warmth and his books and parchments. He wanted these things with him to the end.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “cloak” appears only here in the New Testament. It refers to a heavy, sleeveless outer garment.

The Greek word translated “parchments” is borrowed from Latin. It refers to writing material made from prepared animal skins, sometimes bound in notebook form.

2 Timothy 4:14–18 — Alexander’s Harm and the Lord Who Stood with Paul

2 Timothy 4:14
2 Timothy 4:14
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:14 Alexander the coppersmith did me much harm. May the Lord repay him according to his works.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:14 Alexander the coppersmith did me much harm, but the Lord will judge him for what he has done. (NLT)

Main Idea

Alexander the coppersmith did Paul much harm, and Paul says, “May the Lord repay him according to his works.” Paul leaves justice in the Lord’s hands.

Verse Explanation

Alexander the coppersmith did Paul much harm, and Paul says, “May the Lord repay him according to his works.” Paul leaves justice in the Lord’s hands.

“Alexander the coppersmith did me much harm.”

Paul warns about “Alexander the coppersmith.” “Coppersmith” means a worker in copper, bronze, or other metals. Alexander “did me much harm.” The letter does not say what form the harm took. What is clear is that Alexander had seriously hurt Paul.

The name Alexander was common. A man named Alexander is mentioned in 1 Timothy 1:20 and another in Acts 19:33. It is not certain that either is the same person as the coppersmith.

“May the Lord repay him according to his works.”

Paul’s response is “May the Lord repay him according to his works.” Paul does not seek revenge. He leaves justice in the Lord’s hands. God will give Alexander what his works deserve. This echoes Psalm 62:12: “For You render to each one according to his work.” Paul trusts the righteous Judge (4:8) to deal with Alexander justly.

Study Notes

The text behind the NKJV reads a wish, “May the Lord repay him.” Many other manuscripts read a statement that the Lord will repay him. Either way, Paul leaves justice to the Lord.

The Greek word translated “coppersmith” describes any worker in metal.

Timothy must also beware of Alexander, because he has greatly resisted the words of Paul and his companions. The warning protects Timothy from an active opponent of the message.

Timothy must also beware of Alexander, because he has greatly resisted the words of Paul and his companions. The warning protects Timothy from an active opponent of the message.

“You also must beware of him,”

Paul warns Timothy: “You also must beware of him.” Alexander was still a danger. Timothy must be careful and watch out for him. Paul leaves judgment to the Lord, but he does not leave Timothy unprotected. He gives a clear warning.

“for he has greatly resisted our words.”

The reason is that Alexander “has greatly resisted our words.” “Our words” may mean the message Paul and his companions preached, or the words Paul spoke in his defense. Either way, Alexander opposed them strongly, and the warning protects Timothy from an active opponent.

Paul shows how to respond to such a person. He does not take revenge (4:14), but he also does not ignore the danger. He leaves justice to God and warns others to be careful.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “resisted” is the same verb used in 3:8 for Jannes and Jambres, who “resisted Moses.”

“Our words” could refer to the message Paul and his companions preached, or to Paul’s defense in court. The letter does not specify.

NKJV

2 Timothy 4:16 At my first defense no one stood with me, but all forsook me. May it not be charged against them.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:16 The first time I was brought before the judge, no one came with me. Everyone abandoned me. May it not be counted against them. (NLT)

Main Idea

At Paul’s first defense no one stood with him, and all forsook him. Yet Paul prays, “May it not be charged against them,” showing mercy to those who failed him.

Verse Explanation

At Paul’s first defense no one stood with him, and all forsook him. Yet Paul prays, “May it not be charged against them,” showing mercy to those who failed him.

“At my first defense no one stood with me,”

Paul describes his “first defense,” a time when he answered charges against him. The letter does not say when it took place or whether it was an earlier stage of his present trial. At that defense “no one stood with me.” Paul had no one beside him. Those who could have stood with him stayed away.

“but all forsook me.”

“All forsook me.” Paul was abandoned. The word “forsook” is the same word used for Demas (4:10). Those who should have supported Paul deserted him when he needed them most.

“May it not be charged against them.”

Paul’s response is remarkable: “May it not be charged against them.” He prays that God will not hold their failure against them. He does not ask for judgment on those who abandoned him. He asks for mercy. Stephen prayed similarly as he was being stoned: “Lord, do not charge them with this sin” (Acts 7:60).

Paul’s response to Alexander (4:14) and his response to those who deserted him are different. Alexander actively opposed the message. Paul does not say why the others failed to stand with him. He leaves Alexander to God’s justice and prays mercy for those who failed him.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “defense” refers to a legal defense in court. Many interpreters think the “first defense” was a preliminary hearing in Paul’s current trial. Others think it refers to his earlier imprisonment and release. The letter does not settle the question.

The Greek verb translated “charged” means to count, credit, or reckon. Paul uses the same verb in Romans 4 for righteousness being “accounted” to those who believe.

2 Timothy 4:17
2 Timothy 4:17
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:17 But the Lord stood with me and strengthened me, so that the message might be preached fully through me, and that all the Gentiles might hear. Also I was delivered out of the mouth of the lion.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:17 But the Lord stood with me and gave me strength so that I might preach the Good News in its entirety for all the Gentiles to hear. And he rescued me from certain death. (NLT)

Main Idea

But the Lord stood with Paul and strengthened him so that the message might be preached fully through him and all the Gentiles might hear. Paul was also delivered “out of the mouth of the lion,” a picture of rescue from grave danger.

Verse Explanation

But the Lord stood with Paul and strengthened him so that the message might be preached fully through him and all the Gentiles might hear. Paul was also delivered “out of the mouth of the lion,” a picture of rescue from grave danger.

“But the Lord stood with me and strengthened me,”

“But” brings a dramatic contrast. No one stood with Paul, “but the Lord stood with me.” Paul was not alone after all. The Lord was present with him at his defense. The Lord also “strengthened me,” giving Paul courage and power to speak boldly.

“so that the message might be preached fully through me,”

The Lord stood with Paul for a purpose: “so that the message might be preached fully through me.” Paul says that through him the message was fully proclaimed, even as he answered the charges against him.

“and that all the Gentiles might hear.”

The purpose included “that all the Gentiles might hear.” Paul does not say who was present. He sees what happened at his defense as part of God’s purpose that the message reach the Gentiles, the nations to whom Christ had sent him (Acts 9:15).

“Also I was delivered out of the mouth of the lion.”

Paul was “delivered out of the mouth of the lion.” This is a picture of rescue from grave danger. The letter does not say exactly what the lion represents. Some think it means a literal death sentence or execution, others the emperor Nero, and others the devil (1 Peter 5:8). The language echoes Psalm 22:21, “Save Me from the lion’s mouth.” Whatever the precise danger, the Lord rescued Paul from it at his first defense.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “stood with” means to stand beside or support. It is a different verb from the one translated “stood with” earlier in 4:16, though both describe standing beside someone.

The Greek verb translated “preached fully” is the same verb translated “fulfill” in 4:5. Paul tells Timothy to fulfill his ministry, and through Paul the message was fully proclaimed.

Paul was a Roman citizen (Acts 22:25–28), and Roman citizens were not normally thrown to wild beasts. Most interpreters therefore take the lion as a picture rather than a literal animal.

Paul is confident that the Lord will deliver him from every evil work and preserve him for His heavenly kingdom, and he gives the Lord glory forever. As death approaches, Paul trusts the Lord to bring him safely into that kingdom.

“And the Lord will deliver me from every evil work”

Paul is confident about the future. “The Lord will deliver me from every evil work.” This does not mean Paul expected to escape death. He has just said that his departure is at hand (4:6). Paul means that the Lord will keep him from every evil that could truly harm him, from anything that could separate him from Christ or ruin his faith.

“and preserve me for His heavenly kingdom.”

The Lord will “preserve me for His heavenly kingdom.” Paul’s earlier rescue (4:17) was temporary. His final rescue will be permanent. The Lord will bring him safely into His kingdom. Death will not stop the Lord’s keeping. It will be the doorway into the kingdom. As death approaches, Paul trusts the Lord to bring him safely into that kingdom.

“To Him be glory forever and ever. Amen!”

Paul breaks into praise: “To Him be glory forever and ever. Amen!” Facing death, Paul does not complain. He worships. The Lord deserves glory forever, and Paul gives it to Him.

The Greek verb translated “deliver” is the same verb used in 4:17 (“I was delivered”) and 3:11 (“the Lord delivered me”). Paul moves from past deliverance to future deliverance.

The phrase “heavenly kingdom” appears only here in the New Testament.

Paul sends greetings to Prisca and Aquila and to the household of Onesiphorus. He remembers friends who had served the gospel alongside him and the family of the man who sought him out in Rome.

Verse Explanation

Paul sends greetings to Prisca and Aquila and to the household of Onesiphorus. He remembers friends who had served the gospel alongside him and the family of the man who sought him out in Rome.

“Greet Prisca and Aquila,”

Paul sends greetings. Prisca and Aquila were a married couple who had served with Paul for many years. Prisca is a shorter form of Priscilla, the name Acts uses. They were tentmakers, like Paul, and Paul stayed with them in Corinth (Acts 18:2–3). They later traveled with him to Ephesus (Acts 18:18–19), where they taught Apollos “the way of God more accurately” (Acts 18:26). Paul says they “risked their own necks for my life” (Romans 16:3–4).

“and the household of Onesiphorus.”

Paul also greets “the household of Onesiphorus.” This is the man who sought Paul out in Rome and was not ashamed of his chain (1:16–17). Paul again greets the household rather than Onesiphorus himself, as in 1:16, and says nothing about his present circumstances.

Study Notes

Prisca and Aquila are also named in Acts 18:2, 18, 26; Romans 16:3; and 1 Corinthians 16:19. In several of these passages, including this verse, Prisca or Priscilla is named before her husband.

Main Idea

Paul tells Timothy that Erastus stayed in Corinth and that he left Trophimus sick at Miletus. These brief notes keep Timothy informed about the coworkers who are no longer with Paul.

Verse Explanation

Paul tells Timothy that Erastus stayed in Corinth and that he left Trophimus sick at Miletus. These brief notes keep Timothy informed about the coworkers who are no longer with Paul.

“Erastus stayed in Corinth,”

Paul gives news about two coworkers. Erastus “stayed in Corinth.” He had served with Paul before. Paul sent Timothy and Erastus into Macedonia during his ministry in Ephesus (Acts 19:22). An Erastus is also named in Romans 16:23 as the treasurer of the city of Corinth. It is not certain they are the same person, though both are connected with Corinth.

“but Trophimus I have left in Miletus sick.”

Trophimus was left “in Miletus sick.” Trophimus came from Ephesus (Acts 21:29) and had traveled with Paul (Acts 20:4). Miletus was a seaport south of Ephesus. Paul had to leave Trophimus behind because he was ill. Paul does not say why Trophimus was not healed. God does not always remove sickness, even from His faithful servants.

These brief notes keep Timothy informed about the coworkers who are no longer with Paul.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “sick” means weak or ill. It describes physical illness.

Miletus lay on the coast about thirty miles south of Ephesus. Paul met the Ephesian elders there on his way to Jerusalem (Acts 20:15–17).

2 Timothy 4:21
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:21 Do your utmost to come before winter. Eubulus greets you, as well as Pudens, Linus, Claudia, and all the brethren.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:21 Do your best to get here before winter. Eubulus sends you greetings, and so do Pudens, Linus, Claudia, and all the brothers and sisters. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul urges Timothy to do his utmost to come before winter, repeating the request of 4:9. He passes on greetings from Eubulus, Pudens, Linus, Claudia, and all the brethren.

Verse Explanation

Paul urges Timothy to do his utmost to come before winter, repeating the request of 4:9. He passes on greetings from Eubulus, Pudens, Linus, Claudia, and all the brethren.

“Do your utmost to come before winter.”

Paul repeats his request: “Do your utmost to come before winter.” He made the same appeal in 4:9. Now he adds urgency: “before winter.” In the ancient world, sea travel stopped during winter because storms made it dangerous. If Timothy did not come before winter, he might not be able to come until spring, and by then Paul might be dead. Paul also needed his cloak (4:13) before the cold set in.

“Eubulus greets you, as well as Pudens, Linus, Claudia, and all the brethren.”

Paul passes on greetings from believers in Rome. Eubulus, Pudens, Linus, and Claudia are not mentioned anywhere else in the New Testament. They were evidently believers in Rome who knew Timothy or wanted to greet him. Paul also sends greetings from “all the brethren,” the wider community of believers in Rome.

These names show that Paul was not completely alone. Although only Luke was with him constantly (4:11), other believers in Rome cared about him.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “do your utmost” is the same verb translated “be diligent” in 4:9.

Early church tradition names a Linus as the first leader of the church in Rome after the apostles. Whether he is the Linus of this verse cannot be confirmed.

Ancient sailing was considered dangerous from late autumn to early spring. Acts records the danger of a voyage late in the season, when “sailing was now dangerous” (Acts 27:9).

2 Timothy 4:22
2 Timothy 4:22
NKJV

2 Timothy 4:22 The Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Grace be with you. Amen.(NKJV)

NLT

2 Timothy 4:22 May the Lord be with your spirit. And may his grace be with all of you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul closes with a blessing: “The Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Grace be with you.” The letter that opened with grace ends with grace.

Verse Explanation

Paul closes with a blessing: “The Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Grace be with you.” The letter that opened with grace ends with grace.

“The Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit.”

Paul closes with a blessing for Timothy: “The Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit.” “Your” is singular. This blessing is personal, for Timothy himself. Paul prays that the Lord will be with Timothy’s spirit, his inner life, strengthening and keeping him.

“Grace be with you. Amen.”

Paul adds a final blessing: “Grace be with you.” “You” is plural in Greek. This blessing is for Timothy and for the believers with him. The letter that opened with grace (1:2) ends with grace. Grace is the source of everything Timothy needs (2:1). “Amen” affirms the blessing.

These are among the last recorded words of the apostle Paul. He ends not with a complaint or a final command, but with a prayer for grace.

Study Notes

The Greek text behind the NKJV reads “The Lord Jesus Christ.” Many other manuscripts read simply “The Lord,” and some omit the final “Amen.” The NLT reads “May the Lord be with your spirit.”

The shift from a singular “your spirit” to a plural “you” indicates that Paul expected the letter to be read beyond Timothy himself.
