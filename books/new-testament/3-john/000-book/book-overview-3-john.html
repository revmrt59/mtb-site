Book Overview

At a Glance

Revision 4 · November 26, 2026
Author

“The Elder” (1:1), best identified as John the apostle. The letter shares its title and much of its language with 2 John.

Audience

Gaius, a beloved believer whom the Elder counts among his spiritual children (1:1, 4), together with the friends around him who are greeted by name (1:14).

Date

The letter gives no date. This overview uses about AD 85–95, near the time of 1 and 2 John, as a working date. Ephesus is the traditional suggestion for the place of writing.

Historical Setting

Traveling believers carried the gospel from church to church and relied on other believers to house them and send them on their way. In the church the Elder had written to, a leader named Diotrephes was shutting them out.

Purpose

The Elder writes to commend Gaius for his faithful welcome, to urge him to keep helping the traveling workers, to expose the conduct of Diotrephes, to commend Demetrius as an example of the good Gaius is to imitate, and to prepare for a personal visit.

Central Message

Gaius’s walk in the truth shows itself in faithful love for the brethren who “went forth for His name’s sake” (1:7), and by receiving and sending such workers, believers “become fellow workers for the truth” (1:8). Against the example of Diotrephes, “who loves to have the preeminence” (1:9), the Elder calls Gaius, “do not imitate what is evil, but what is good” (1:11).

Key Verse

“We therefore ought to receive such, that we may become fellow workers for the truth.” (1:8)

Key Themes

Walking in the truth; faithful love for traveling believers; sending workers forward in a manner worthy of God; becoming fellow workers for the truth; the love of preeminence; imitating what is good; trustworthy testimony; fellowship face to face.

Book Flow

  • I. Greeting and Joy in Gaius’s Faithfulness (1:1–1:4): prayer for Gaius and joy that he walks in the truth

  • II. Support Faithful Workers and Reject Self-Seeking Opposition (1:5–1:12): Gaius’s welcome, the opposition of Diotrephes, and the good example of Demetrius

  • III. The Hope of Personal Fellowship and Final Greetings (1:13–1:14): a visit hoped for, peace, and greetings by name

Go Deeper

Background

Author

The letter opens, “The Elder, To the beloved Gaius” (1:1). The writer gives no name, and he did not need to. Gaius knew exactly who “the Elder” was. The same title opens 2 John, and the two letters speak with one voice. In both, the writer rejoices “greatly” over believers who walk in truth (1:3; 2 John 1:4). Both close almost the same way: the writer has many things to write but would rather not put them in writing, because he hopes to “speak face to face” (1:13–14; 2 John 1:12). Second John mentions setting aside paper and ink; 3 John mentions pen and ink.

The Elder is best identified as John the apostle, whose Gospel and first letter share the same concern for truth, love, and a life that walks in what God has revealed. Some have suggested instead a church leader known as John the Elder. He writes with the warmth of a spiritual father and the standing of a recognized leader among the churches. That is why the refusal of Diotrephes to receive him (1:9) is so serious.

Audience

The letter is addressed to one man, “the beloved Gaius, whom I love in truth” (1:1). The phrase joins sincere affection with love grounded in the gospel truth the two men share. “Beloved” is the Elder’s word for him four times (1:1, 2, 5, 11), and each new turn in the letter begins with it. Even the hard paragraph about Diotrephes is followed at once by “Beloved” (1:11). The Elder also counts Gaius among “my children” (1:4). Gaius may have come to faith through the Elder’s ministry, or he may simply have been under his spiritual care.

Gaius was a common name, and the New Testament mentions several men who bore it (Acts 19:29; Acts 20:4; Romans 16:23; 1 Corinthians 1:14). Nothing connects any of them with this Gaius. What the letter does show is a believer whose home was open to travelers. The Elder tells Gaius what Diotrephes is doing in “the church” (1:9–10), but the letter does not say whether Gaius belonged to that congregation or lived nearby. The closing greetings reach beyond Gaius to “the friends” around him, each to be greeted “by name” (1:14).

Date

Third John gives no date or place of writing. This overview uses about AD 85–95 as a working date, near the time of 1 and 2 John, with which it shares its language and concerns. Ephesus is the traditional suggestion for the place of writing. The message of the letter does not depend on settling either question.

Historical Setting

First-century churches met in homes, and the gospel traveled with people. Preachers and teachers moved from town to town, and believers along the way gave them lodging and helped “send them forward on their journey” (1:6) for the next stage of the trip. Paul himself hoped the believers in Rome would help him on his way to Spain (Romans 15:24). Hosts like Gaius played a real part in that movement of the gospel.

The workers in this letter went out for Jesus’ name, “taking nothing from the Gentiles” (1:7). The Elder draws his conclusion from that fact: “We therefore ought to receive such, that we may become fellow workers for the truth” (1:8). They most likely refused support from the unbelievers they were trying to reach so that money would not hinder the gospel (compare 1 Corinthians 9:12). The letter gives this practice as the reason for its appeal.

Travelers could not simply be welcomed on their own word. Second John warns a church not to receive teachers who deny Christ (2 John 1:10–11), so a good report from trusted believers mattered. That is why testimony runs through 3 John. The brethren testify about Gaius (1:3, 6), and Demetrius is commended with “a good testimony from all” (1:12).

Occasion

Brethren reported to the Elder that Gaius walks in the truth, and they bore witness of his love before the church (1:3, 6). The Elder had also written to a church where Diotrephes was prominent, but Diotrephes “does not receive us” (1:9). Diotrephes was speaking against the Elder, refusing the traveling brethren, and putting out of the church those who wanted to welcome them (1:10).

So the Elder writes to Gaius directly. He rejoices over him, asks him to keep sending the workers on their way, says that he will deal with Diotrephes’s deeds if he comes (1:10), calls Gaius to imitate what is good (1:11), and commends Demetrius as one who does it (1:12). Demetrius may have carried the letter, though the letter does not say so. The Elder hopes to follow soon in person (1:14).

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Third John is a personal letter about welcoming the people who carry the gospel. Its appeal grows out of the Elder’s joy that Gaius walks in the truth (1:3–4). That walk takes visible form in faithful love for traveling believers, even strangers, and the Elder asks Gaius to keep receiving them and sending them on their way, because those who do so “become fellow workers for the truth” (1:8). Walking in the truth makes Gaius a partner in the work of those who go.

The letter sets two responses to these workers side by side. Gaius welcomes them and sends them forward. Diotrephes, who “loves to have the preeminence” (1:9), refuses them and puts out of the church those who would receive them (1:10).

Between these two responses the Elder places a plain command: “do not imitate what is evil, but what is good” (1:11). He then introduces Demetrius, who has “a good testimony from all” (1:12), as an example of the good he urges Gaius to imitate. Prayer, joy, peace, and greetings by name frame the whole letter, so even its warning is spoken in love.

Why This Book Matters

Third John shows what walking in the truth looks like on an ordinary day. For Gaius, truth was not only something he believed. It was what travelers found when they came to his door: a meal, a bed, and help for the road. The Elder says Gaius does this “faithfully” (1:5) and sets its standard at “a manner worthy of God” (1:6). God notices hidden, practical kindness done for His people, and He counts it as part of His work.

The letter also shows that the church’s mission belongs to more than those who travel. Gaius never leaves home in this letter, yet the Elder says that those who receive such workers “become fellow workers for the truth” (1:8). By receiving and sending them, Gaius shares in the work of those who go.

Third John also exposes a danger that sound teaching alone cannot prevent. The Elder brings no charge of false doctrine against Diotrephes. His failure is the love of first place, and it led him to slander, to shut out God’s servants, and to cast believers out of the church (1:9–10). A church can guard its teaching and still be wounded by a leader who wants control.

Book Flow

I. Greeting and Joy in Gaius’s Faithfulness (1:1–1:4)

The Elder greets “the beloved Gaius, whom I love in truth” (1:1) and prays that Gaius’s circumstances and health will match the health of his soul (1:2). Brethren have come and “testified of the truth that is in you, just as you walk in the truth” (1:3). That report gives the Elder his highest joy: “I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth” (1:4). Everything the Elder goes on to ask grows out of this joy.

II. Support Faithful Workers and Reject Self-Seeking Opposition (1:5–1:12)

Gaius’s walk in the truth takes visible form in his love for “the brethren and for strangers” (1:5). The Elder urges him to keep sending them forward “in a manner worthy of God” (1:6), because they “went forth for His name’s sake, taking nothing from the Gentiles” (1:7). Receiving such workers makes believers “fellow workers for the truth” (1:8).

The movement then turns to the opposite example. Diotrephes “does not receive us” (1:9). He speaks against the Elder “with malicious words,” refuses the brethren, and forbids those who wish to receive them, “putting them out of the church” (1:10). Verse 11 is the hinge of the movement: “Beloved, do not imitate what is evil, but what is good.” With Diotrephes’s conduct just described and Demetrius about to be commended, the command tells Gaius which example to follow. Demetrius then appears as the example of that good. He “has a good testimony from all, and from the truth itself,” and the Elder and those with him add their own witness (1:12).

III. The Hope of Personal Fellowship and Final Greetings (1:13–1:14)

The Elder has “many things to write” but does not wish to continue “with pen and ink” (1:13), because some matters need to be dealt with face to face. He hopes to see Gaius “shortly,” and “we shall speak face to face” (1:14). A letter that had to address conflict closes with “Peace to you,” greetings from friends, and a request to “greet the friends by name” (1:14).

Key Themes to Watch For

Walking in the truth. Gaius’s life matches the truth that is in him, and others can see it (1:3–4).

Faithful love for strangers. Gaius serves believers he has never met because they belong to Christ (1:5–6).

Sending in a manner worthy of God. Welcome includes equipping workers for the road ahead (1:6).

Fellow workers for the truth. Those who receive and send share in the work of those who go (1:7–8).

The love of preeminence. The desire of Diotrephes to be first turns into words and actions that shut people out (1:9–10).

Imitating what is good. Gaius must choose which example to follow, and doing good shows that a person “is of God” (1:11). Diotrephes shows the evil to avoid; Demetrius shows the good to follow (1:12).

Trustworthy testimony. Reports of faithful conduct give the Elder grounds for joy and commendation (1:3, 6, 12).

Fellowship face to face. The Elder longs for presence, peace, and greetings by name (1:13–14).

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

The Elder. A title for a respected spiritual leader. It marks the writer as a recognized father in the faith to these believers. The letter does not explain why he chose this title rather than his name.

Truth and walking in truth. In John’s writings, truth is God’s reality revealed in Jesus, who said, “I am the way, the truth, and the life” (John 14:6). “Walk” describes the steady direction of a life. To walk in the truth is to live consistently with what God has revealed (1:3–4).

“For His name’s sake.” The workers went out for the sake of Jesus (1:7). Early believers often spoke of Him simply as the Name (Acts 5:41), and some Greek editions of this verse read that way.

“Send them forward on their journey.” These words translate a single Greek verb used for helping travelers on to the next stage of their trip, often with food, money, or companions (1:6; compare Romans 15:24; Titus 3:13).

“The Gentiles.” In 1:7 the word most likely refers to unbelieving outsiders, not to Gentile Christians. The workers took nothing from them, most likely so that money would not hinder the gospel (compare 1 Corinthians 9:12), and the Elder gives that practice as the reason believers ought to receive such workers (1:7–8).

“Loves to have the preeminence.” In Greek this phrase is a single word describing a person who loves to be first (1:9). It appears nowhere else in the New Testament.

Receiving. Diotrephes “does not receive us” (1:9). He rejects the Elder and those with him, which may also mean refusing the Elder’s authority or setting aside his earlier letter. Verse 10 describes a separate refusal: Diotrephes will not welcome the traveling brethren, though the Elder says believers “ought to receive such” workers (1:8, 10).

“Putting them out of the church.” Diotrephes was expelling believers from the fellowship of the congregation because they wanted to welcome the brethren (1:10).

“From the truth itself.” Demetrius has a good testimony from people and also “from the truth itself” (1:12). This is best understood to mean that his conduct agreed with the truth, so that the truth stood as a witness on his behalf. The phrase may also carry a secondary sense, picturing the truth of the gospel as a witness in its own right alongside the church and the Elder.

Christ and Life

Third John never uses the name Jesus or the title Christ, yet He stands at the center of everything the letter commends. The workers “went forth for His name’s sake” (1:7). Their travel, Gaius’s open home, and the Elder’s joy all turn on Him. The letter shows Christ present in the ordinary business of church life: lodging guests, supplying travelers, writing letters, and facing conflict.

Jesus told His disciples, “He who receives you receives Me, and he who receives Me receives Him who sent Me” (Matthew 10:40). He promised that even “a cup of cold water” given to one of His disciples would not lose its reward (Matthew 10:42). When Gaius welcomed the brethren, he was welcoming Christ’s own messengers, and through them, Christ Himself.

The Elder says that those who receive such workers “become fellow workers for the truth” (1:8). An episode from David’s life illustrates a similar principle. After a battle, David ruled that “as his part is who goes down to the battle, so shall his part be who stays by the supplies; they shall share alike” (1 Samuel 30:24). Third John does not say how rewards are shared, but it does call those who receive and send the workers fellow workers with those who go.

Diotrephes “loves to have the preeminence” (1:9). Paul uses a closely related word of Christ: “that in all things He may have the preeminence” (Colossians 1:18). First place in the church belongs to Christ alone, and Christ used His place to serve. He “made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant” (Philippians 2:7), and Paul calls believers to the same mind: “Let nothing be done through selfish ambition or conceit” (Philippians 2:3). Diotrephes reached for the place that belongs to Christ and used it to shut people out.

The letter’s command to imitate good also points to the source of a good life: “He who does good is of God, but he who does evil has not seen God” (1:11). Doing good is the visible fruit of a life that comes from God and has come to know Him (compare 1 John 2:3–6). Gaius’s walk in the truth, his open door, and the soul that prospers (1:2) are marks of that life, and God’s grace is their source.

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Verse 2 is a prayer, not a promise. The Elder prays that things may go well for Gaius in his health and circumstances, in keeping with the way his soul is already thriving (1:2). The word translated “prosper” literally means to go well or to have a good journey. This is a prayer for a beloved friend. It is not a promise of health or wealth to every believer.

Diotrephes is a warning, not a pattern for every dispute. The letter gives Diotrephes no title, but his actions point to a leader with real authority in that church (1:9–10). His sin is pride and the love of being first, not false doctrine. The letter does not say whether Gaius belonged to the same congregation, what the Elder’s earlier letter said, or how the planned visit turned out. His example gives no warrant for treating every disagreement with a church leader as the same offense. Scripture calls believers to honor and submit to those who lead them (Hebrews 13:7, 17; 1 Thessalonians 5:12–13). That call is not unconditional when a leader uses his position against Christ’s faithful servants (Acts 5:29), but it never becomes a license for rebellion or retaliation. Preeminence in the church belongs to Christ alone (Colossians 1:18).

Generous welcome has limits. Gaius welcomes workers who went out “for His name’s sake” (1:7). Second John gives the other side: a church must not receive teachers who do not confess Jesus Christ as coming in the flesh (2 John 1:7, 10–11). Read together, the two letters show that discernment and generous hospitality belong together. Believers welcome and send those who serve Christ’s name, and they refuse to support those who deny Him.

Verse 11 describes the direction of a life. “He who does evil has not seen God” is not a demand for sinless perfection. John also wrote, “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves” (1 John 1:8). The verse speaks of the course a life takes.

This is a warm letter. Even with a conflict in view, the Elder writes with “beloved,” joy, peace, and greetings by name. His firmness about Diotrephes comes from the same love that rejoices over Gaius.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

Verse numbering. The NKJV numbers 3 John as 14 verses. Many modern translations divide verse 14 into two verses, so the closing greetings carry a separate verse number in those editions.

The earlier letter. “I wrote to the church” (1:9) refers to a letter the Elder had already sent. Some suggest it was 2 John, but 3 John does not identify it, and it may not have survived.

Demetrius. Demetrius was a common name. Some have connected this Demetrius with the silversmith of Ephesus who opposed Paul (Acts 19:24), but nothing in Scripture supports that identification.

Acceptance in the early church. The church historian Eusebius, writing in the early fourth century, listed 2 and 3 John among the disputed books that were nevertheless recognized by many, noting that they might be by the apostle or by another man of the same name (Church History 3.25.3). The church went on to receive both letters as Scripture.

References. Third John has one chapter, so verses may be cited as 3 John 1:7 or simply as verse 7.
