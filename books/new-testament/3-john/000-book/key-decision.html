Book Questions

Who is “the Elder”? (3 John 1:1)

MTB'S READING

The writer calls himself only “The Elder” (3 John 1:1) and never gives his name. MTB, with long church tradition, understands him to be John the apostle, the same writer as 2 John. The letter’s language of truth, love, walking, and witness fits that identification.

Some interpreters point instead to another leader named John, often called John the Elder. The letter itself does not settle the question, and it does not say why he uses the title. A date around AD 85–95 and Ephesus as his home are traditional suggestions, not statements of the letter.

Read: 3 John 1:1, 2 John 1:1

2 John says not to receive certain teachers, but 3 John says to receive the workers. Do they conflict? (3 John 1:5–8)

MTB'S READING

No. They are two sides of the same faithfulness. In 2 John the Elder warns against welcoming teachers who deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh, because “he who greets him shares in his evil deeds” (2 John 1:7–11). In 3 John he urges Gaius to receive workers who “went forth for His name’s sake,” so that believers “may become fellow workers for the truth” (3 John 1:7–8).

The principle is the same in both letters: giving help makes a person a participant in what the one helped is spreading. Faithful discernment protects the church from teaching that denies Christ and makes room for those who go out for His name. This does not soften 2 John’s warning against welcoming or supporting teachers who deny Christ. Ordinary kindness toward people who believe differently is a different matter from endorsing or advancing such a teacher’s work.

Read: 3 John 1:5–8, 2 John 1:7–11 · Related: What does it mean to become “fellow workers for the truth”?

3 John 1:1–4 · The Elder Prays for Gaius and Rejoices in His Walk

Does verse 2 promise health and wealth to believers? (v. 2)

MTB'S READING · CAUTION

No. The Elder writes, “I pray that you may prosper in all things and be in health, just as your soul prospers” (v. 2). This is a personal prayer for a beloved friend. It is not a promise to every believer, and it does not tell us that Gaius was sick or poor.

The prayer is real, and it is right to pray for one another’s health and everyday needs. But the Elder’s greatest joy is Gaius’s inner life: brethren have testified that he walks in the truth (vv. 3–4). The thriving soul comes first.

Read: 3 John 1:2–4

3 John 1:5–8 · Welcoming and Sending Those Who Went Out for His Name

Who were the “brethren” and “strangers” Gaius helped? (v. 5)

LIKELY

The NKJV reads, “you do faithfully whatever you do for the brethren and for strangers” (v. 5). MTB understands these most likely as one group: fellow believers who were strangers to Gaius personally. They are the same people who “have borne witness of your love before the church” and who “went forth for His name’s sake” (vv. 6–7).

Gaius’s welcome did not depend on knowing them first. What mattered was whose name they served.

Read: 3 John 1:5–7

Does verse 7 mean ministry should never accept money from unbelievers? (v. 7)

CAUTION

These workers “went forth for His name’s sake, taking nothing from the Gentiles” (v. 7). “Gentiles” here appears to mean the unbelievers among whom they worked. Because they took nothing from them, the Elder says, “We therefore ought to receive such” (v. 8). Their practice explains why believers should support them.

They most likely refused support from the very people they were trying to reach so that money would not become a stumbling block to the gospel (compare 1 Corinthians 9:12). The verse describes faithful workers in that setting. It does not set a universal rule for how every ministry must be funded.

Read: 3 John 1:5–8, 1 Corinthians 9:12 · Related: Do 2 John and 3 John conflict?

What does it mean to become “fellow workers for the truth”? Do those who send share the reward? (v. 8)

MTB'S READING

By receiving these workers and sending them forward “in a manner worthy of God” (v. 6), Gaius takes part in their work. Those who send and support share in the mission with those who go, so that together they “become fellow workers for the truth” (v. 8). That partnership is the letter’s central appeal.

Jesus said, “He who receives you receives Me,” and promised that even a cup of cold water given in a disciple’s name will not lose its reward (Matthew 10:40–42). David ruled that those who stayed by the supplies would “share alike” with those who went down to the battle (1 Samuel 30:24). These passages honor those who send. 3 John itself establishes partnership in the work but does not say how Jesus will reward those who serve Him faithfully.

Read: 3 John 1:5–8, Matthew 10:40–42, 1 Samuel 30:21–25 · Related: Do 2 John and 3 John conflict?

3 John 1:9–10 · Diotrephes Refuses the Elder and Shuts Out the Brethren

Who was Diotrephes? Was he a church officer or a false teacher? (vv. 9–10)

LIKELY

John does not give Diotrephes a formal title, so his exact office is not stated, yet his actions strongly point to a pastor, elder, or dominant church leader. He “loves to have the preeminence,” rejects the Elder’s authority, spreads “malicious words” against him, refuses to welcome traveling brothers, forbids others from receiving them, and puts members “out of the church” (vv. 9–10). That level of control, especially putting members out of the church, indicates real governing authority in the congregation, whether through a formal office or as a controlling house-church host.

Notice what John does not accuse him of: false doctrine. Unlike the deceivers in 1 and 2 John who denied Christ (2 John 1:7), Diotrephes’s sin is pride, territorial control, and the love of being first. What John’s earlier letter to the church contained (v. 9) and how his planned visit resolved the conflict (v. 10) are not recorded.

Read: 3 John 1:9–10, 2 John 1:7, 1 Peter 5:1–3 · Related: Can these verses justify opposing church leaders?

Can these verses justify opposing church leaders I disagree with? (vv. 9–10)

CAUTION

No. Diotrephes is exposed for specific deeds: using his influence to speak maliciously, shut out faithful workers, and put out of the church those who would welcome them. The letter does not treat every disagreement with a church leader as the same offense.

Scripture calls believers to honor, pray for, submit to, and obey those who rule over them (Hebrews 13:7, 17–18; 1 Thessalonians 5:12–13). That call is real, but it is not unconditional. When a leader, like Diotrephes, uses his position to slander, shut out faithful servants of Christ, or oppose the work of the truth, faithfulness to God comes first (Acts 5:29). That is a boundary against taking part in sin, not a license for rebellion or personal retaliation. Even then, the Elder’s way is open and personal: he plans to come and address the matter directly (vv. 10, 14).

As a spiritual application, his example searches anyone who has influence. Christ is the One who is to “have the preeminence” in all things (Colossians 1:18), and He “made Himself of no reputation” (Philippians 2:7). The question for every leader and member is whose honor our service seeks.

Read: 3 John 1:9–10, Colossians 1:18, Philippians 2:3–8, Hebrews 13:7, Hebrews 13:17–18, 1 Thessalonians 5:12–13, Acts 5:29 · Related: Who was Diotrephes?

3 John 1:11–12 · Imitate What Is Good: Demetrius Commended

Does “he who does evil has not seen God” mean one sin proves a person does not know God? (v. 11)

MTB'S READING · CAUTION

The Elder writes, “He who does good is of God, but he who does evil has not seen God” (v. 11). Doing good gives visible evidence of a real relationship with God (1 John 2:3–6), and that good is the fruit of His grace. “Has not seen God” concerns knowing Him, not physical sight.

The verse describes the course of a life, not a single failure. John also writes that if we say we have no sin we deceive ourselves, and that God forgives us when we confess (1 John 1:8–9). In context the command is concrete: Gaius must choose which example to follow, Diotrephes or Demetrius.

Read: 3 John 1:11–12, 1 John 2:3–6, 1 John 1:8–9 · Related: What is the testimony “from the truth itself”?

What does it mean that Demetrius has a good testimony “from the truth itself”? (v. 12)

LIKELY

Demetrius is commended by three witnesses: “all,” “the truth itself,” and the Elder and those with him (v. 12). MTB understands the witness of the truth most likely as Demetrius’s life agreeing with the truth, so that his conduct itself speaks for him.

As a spiritual application, it is good to be well spoken of, but a life that agrees with the truth is the deeper testimony. Some suggest Demetrius carried this letter to Gaius. That is possible, but it is only an inference.

Read: 3 John 1:11–12 · Related: Does one sin prove a person does not know God?
