Chapter Study

3 John 1

Verse-by-Verse
3 John 1:1

NKJV

3 John 1:1 The Elder, To the beloved Gaius, whom I love in truth:(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:1 This letter is from John, the elder. I am writing to Gaius, my dear friend, whom I love in the truth. (NLT)

Main Idea

John writes to Gaius as a beloved friend and fellow believer, expressing a deep love for him that is rooted in their shared commitment to the truth.

Verse Explanation

“The Elder”

John identifies himself simply as “the Elder.” This likely reflects both his recognized leadership among the churches and his advanced age. He does not need a fuller introduction because Gaius knows who is writing.

“To the beloved Gaius”

Gaius was a common Roman name, and the New Testament mentions several men by that name. We cannot confidently identify this Gaius with any of them. What matters here is John's affection for him. He calls Gaius “beloved” four times in this short letter (vv. 1, 2, 5, 11).

“whom I love in truth”

John's love for Gaius is not merely personal affection. “In truth” carries the idea of love shaped by and grounded in the truth of Christ. This continues a major emphasis from 2 John: Christian truth and Christian love belong together.

Study Notes

“The Elder” is the same title used by the writer of 2 John (2 John 1:1). Early Christian tradition strongly associates these letters with the apostle John, though the letter itself does not explicitly say, “John the apostle.”

The Greek word translated “love” is agapaō (ἀγαπάω), the familiar New Testament word for committed, self-giving love. John's wording is especially fitting because 3 John will show what love grounded in truth looks like in practice, particularly through Gaius's hospitality and support of faithful Christian workers.

Spirit & Life

John's opening quietly gives us a healthy picture of Christian relationships: truth does not make love colder, and love does not require us to loosen our grip on truth. In Christ, the two strengthen one another. Gaius is loved deeply, but he is loved “in truth.” This is affection with spiritual substance behind it.

3 John 1:2

NKJV

3 John 1:2 Beloved, I pray that you may prosper in all things and be in health, just as your soul prospers.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:2 Dear friend, I hope all is well with you and that you are as healthy in body as you are strong in spirit. (NLT)

Main Idea

John prays that Gaius would flourish in every area of life and enjoy physical well-being just as his inner spiritual life is already flourishing.

Verse Explanation

“Beloved, I pray that you may prosper in all things and be in health”

John expresses genuine concern for Gaius's whole life. “Prosper” carries the sense of things going well or succeeding in one's course. Along with “be in health,” it shows that John's care for Gaius extends beyond his spiritual life to his everyday circumstances and physical well-being.

“just as your soul prospers.”

This is a remarkable commendation of Gaius. John can point to the flourishing condition of his inner life as the standard by which he prays the rest of his life would flourish. The following verses explain why John can say this: Gaius is walking faithfully in the truth.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “prosper” (euodoō) originally carried the idea of having a good journey or succeeding along one's way. Here it naturally means to fare well or prosper.

This verse should not be turned into a guarantee that spiritual faithfulness necessarily produces financial prosperity or physical health. John is expressing a personal prayer for Gaius, not stating a universal promise. In fact, his prayer that Gaius “be in health” may suggest some concern about Gaius's physical condition, although the text does not tell us enough to know.

Spirit & Life

There is something worth noticing in John's words: Gaius possesses an inward prosperity that John can already see. Before John prays for better circumstances, he recognizes the health of Gaius's soul. Scripture consistently places the deepest measure of flourishing there, in a life increasingly shaped by truth, faithfulness, and fellowship with God.

3 John 1:3

NKJV

3 John 1:3 For I rejoiced greatly when brethren came and testified of the truth that is in you, just as you walk in the truth.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:3 Some of the traveling teachers recently returned and made me very happy by telling me about your faithfulness and that you are living according to the truth. (NLT)

Main Idea

John was deeply encouraged by the testimony of fellow believers who had seen Gaius’s faith firsthand. The truth he believed was clearly shaping the way he lived.

Verse Explanation

“For I rejoiced greatly when brethren came and testified of the truth that is in you”

Fellow believers had visited John and spoken about Gaius. What they reported brought John great joy because the truth was not merely something Gaius knew or professed. It was “in” him, taking root in his life and shaping who he was.

“just as you walk in the truth.”

Their testimony was confirmed by Gaius’s conduct. To “walk” is a common biblical picture for the ongoing pattern of a person’s life. Gaius was consistently living according to the truth he believed. His doctrine and his daily life agreed.

Study Notes

The word “testified” (martyreō) means to bear witness or give testimony. John was not simply expressing his own impression of Gaius. Other believers who had encountered him were independently reporting his faithfulness.

There is also a meaningful progression in the verse: “the truth that is in you” becomes “you walk in the truth.” Truth received inwardly was becoming visible outwardly. This connection between possessing the truth and living it is central to John’s understanding of genuine Christian faith.

Spirit & Life

There is a quiet beauty in having a life that others can testify about without needing our explanation. Gaius did not merely say that truth mattered to him. People encountered his life and saw it. When the truth of Jesus increasingly becomes part of us, it eventually becomes visible through us.

3 John 1:4

NKJV

3 John 1:4 I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:4 I could have no greater joy than to hear that my children are following the truth. (NLT)

Main Idea

Few things brought John greater joy than seeing those he had spiritually cared for continuing to live faithfully according to the truth.

Verse Explanation

“I have no greater joy than to hear”

John reveals something of his pastoral heart. His deepest satisfaction was not simply that people had received his teaching, but that he continued to hear reports of their faithfulness.

“that my children walk in truth.”

“My children” is an affectionate spiritual description of believers John had helped nurture in the faith. Whether John personally led each of them to Christ is not stated. What matters is the relationship: he regarded them with the care of a spiritual father.

To “walk in truth” means that truth had become the continuing pattern of their lives. John rejoiced not merely that they knew the truth, but that they were living it.

Study Notes

The phrase “no greater joy” is emphatic. John is describing the extraordinary joy produced by seeing lasting spiritual fruit in people he loves.

There is a close connection with verse 3. The brethren had testified that Gaius was “walking in the truth,” and John now explains why that report affected him so deeply.

Spirit & Life

John gives us a glimpse of the heart of spiritual fathering. The goal is not simply to impart truth but to see people become established in it and live it for themselves. There is a unique joy in watching someone you have invested in continue with Jesus, not because you are carrying them, but because truth has taken hold of their own life.

3 John 1:5

NKJV

3 John 1:5 Beloved, you do faithfully whatever you do for the brethren and for strangers,(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:5 Dear friend, you are being faithful to God when you care for the traveling teachers who pass through, even though they are strangers to you. (NLT)

Main Idea

John commends Gaius for faithfully serving fellow believers, including traveling Christian workers whom he did not personally know.

Verse Explanation

“Beloved, you do faithfully whatever you do for the brethren”

Gaius’s actions toward other believers were an expression of his faithfulness to God. John does not focus on one isolated act. “Whatever you do” points to a consistent pattern of generously serving and caring for fellow Christians.

“and for strangers,”

These “strangers” were not unbelieving outsiders but fellow believers who were personally unknown to Gaius. The following verses show that they were traveling Christian workers who had gone out “for His name’s sake” (v. 7). Gaius welcomed and supported them even though he had no previous relationship with them.

Study Notes

Hospitality carried greater importance in the first-century world than it often does today. Travel could be difficult, and inns could be expensive, unsafe, or morally questionable. Traveling Christian workers therefore depended heavily upon believers who would receive them into their homes and provide practical support.

This becomes especially important in the contrast between Gaius and Diotrephes later in the letter. Gaius receives the brethren faithfully; Diotrephes refuses to receive them and attempts to stop others from doing so (vv. 9-10). Hospitality in 3 John is therefore more than courtesy. It becomes a practical expression of partnership with God's work.

Spirit & Life

Gaius shows what it looks like when “walk in truth” (v. 4) moves from principle into practice. He serves people from whom he may receive nothing personally in return. His faithfulness to Jesus enlarges the circle of people he is willing to love and serve.

3 John 1:6

NKJV

3 John 1:6 who have borne witness of your love before the church. If you send them forward on their journey in a manner worthy of God, you will do well,(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:6 They have told the church here of your loving friendship. Please continue providing for such teachers in a manner that pleases God. (NLT)

Main Idea

The believers Gaius had served testified publicly about his love, and John encourages him to continue supporting them generously as they travel in God’s service.

Verse Explanation

“who have borne witness of your love before the church.”

The traveling believers mentioned in verse 5 had told the church about the love Gaius had shown them. His hospitality was not merely practical assistance. They recognized it as genuine Christian love expressed through tangible care.

“If you send them forward on their journey in a manner worthy of God, you will do well,”

John now encourages Gaius to continue what he has been doing. To “send them forward on their journey” meant more than saying goodbye. It involved providing what they needed for the next stage of their ministry, which could include food, money, supplies, lodging, or other practical assistance.

“In a manner worthy of God” sets a striking standard for that support. Gaius is to care for those serving Christ in a way appropriate to the God they represent and the mission they are carrying.

Study Notes

The verb behind “send them forward on their journey” (propempō) is used elsewhere for helping Christian workers continue their ministry (Acts 15:3; Romans 15:24; 1 Corinthians 16:6; Titus 3:13). It often carries the idea of materially equipping someone for the journey, not merely accompanying them partway.

“You will do well” is a courteous expression of approval and encouragement. John is affirming Gaius while urging him to continue this pattern of generosity.

Spirit & Life

John lifts ordinary generosity into something much larger. Gaius may be providing meals, lodging, or traveling expenses, but through those simple acts he is participating in the work of God. We do not have to be the ones traveling or preaching to share meaningfully in what God is doing. Sometimes our part is to strengthen and supply those He has sent.

3 John 1:7

NKJV

3 John 1:7 because they went forth for His name's sake, taking nothing from the Gentiles.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:7 For they are traveling for the Lord, and they accept nothing from people who are not believers. (NLT)

Main Idea

These Christian workers deserved the church’s support because they had gone out in service to Jesus and were deliberately depending on God’s people rather than unbelievers for their provision.

Verse Explanation

“because they went forth for His name’s sake,”

John explains why Gaius should support these traveling believers. They had “went forth” because of “His name,” referring to Jesus. His name represents who He is and His authority. Their journey and ministry were undertaken for Him and the advancement of His work.

“taking nothing from the Gentiles.”

These workers were not financing their ministry by seeking support from the unbelieving people among whom they ministered. “Gentiles” here is best understood in the sense of unbelievers or pagans rather than simply non-Jewish people. They chose instead to depend upon the support of fellow believers such as Gaius.

Study Notes

“For His name’s sake” is literally “for the Name.” Early Christians could use “the Name” as a powerful shorthand for Jesus and their allegiance to Him. A similar expression appears in Acts 5:41, where the apostles rejoice that they were counted worthy to suffer for “His name.”

Their practice of “taking nothing from the Gentiles” also protected the character of their ministry. The gospel was not being offered in exchange for money, nor were these missionaries making themselves financially dependent upon those they were seeking to reach.

Spirit & Life

There is a beautiful simplicity in “they went forth for His name’s sake.” Jesus Himself was sufficient reason for them to go. Their ministry was ultimately not about building their own reputation or advancing their own interests. They went because His name was worthy to be known.

That gives the support Gaius provides in verses 5-6 even greater significance. He is not merely helping travelers. He is helping people who have ordered their lives around the name of Jesus.

3 John 1:8

NKJV

3 John 1:8 We therefore ought to receive such, that we may become fellow workers for the truth.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:8 So we ourselves should support them so that we can be their partners as they teach the truth. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because these workers have gone out for the sake of Jesus, believers should support them and, by doing so, become active partners in spreading the truth.

Verse Explanation

“We therefore ought to receive such,”

“Therefore” connects directly with verse 7. Because these workers have gone out “for His name’s sake” and are not seeking support from unbelievers, fellow Christians have a responsibility to receive and support them. “Ought” makes this more than optional generosity. John sees it as a fitting responsibility of God’s people.

“that we may become fellow workers for the truth.”

Those who support faithful Christian workers participate in their work. Gaius may not be traveling with them, but his hospitality and provision make their ministry possible. In this way, he becomes a “fellow worker” with them in advancing the truth.

Study Notes

“Receive” (hypolambanō) can carry the idea of taking someone under one’s care or supporting them. In context, it includes the practical hospitality and assistance described in verses 5-6.

The phrase “fellow workers for the truth” is especially significant. John does not say merely that believers support the workers; their support makes them participants in the work itself. This same principle appears negatively in 2 John 10-11, where supporting false teachers makes someone share in their evil deeds. Together, 2 and 3 John show that whom we support matters because support creates partnership.

Spirit & Life

Not everyone is called to “go forth” in the same way, but everyone can have a part in what Jesus is doing. Some go, others receive, provide, pray, encourage, and give. John sees both as genuine participation in the work. When we strengthen those who faithfully carry the truth, we become “fellow workers for the truth.”

3 John 1:9

NKJV

3 John 1:9 I wrote to the church, but Diotrephes, who loves to have the preeminence among them, does not receive us.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:9 I wrote to the church about this, but Diotrephes, who loves to be the leader, refuses to have anything to do with us. (NLT)

Main Idea

John had written to the church, but Diotrephes rejected his apostolic influence because he wanted to occupy the leading position among the believers.

Verse Explanation

“I wrote to the church,”

John had previously sent some form of communication to this congregation. That letter has apparently not survived, unless John is referring to another known letter, which cannot be established from the text.

“but Diotrephes, who loves to have the preeminence among them,”

The problem was deeper than a simple disagreement. Diotrephes “loves to have the preeminence,” meaning he wanted to be first or occupy the leading place among them. John identifies his desire for prominence as lying behind his behavior.

“does not receive us.”

Diotrephes refused to acknowledge John and those associated with him. The next verse shows that this rejection was active: he spoke maliciously against them, refused to receive traveling believers, and punished church members who wanted to receive them.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “loves to have the preeminence” (philoprōteuō) occurs only here in the New Testament. It combines the ideas of loving and being first. The issue is not simply that Diotrephes held leadership. His problem was loving the position of being first.

“Does not receive us” probably includes rejecting John’s authority and refusing his representatives. This makes the contrast with Gaius especially sharp. Gaius faithfully “receives” Christ’s servants (vv. 5-8); Diotrephes refuses them because their presence threatens his control.

Spirit & Life

Diotrephes exposes a subtle danger in spiritual leadership: it is possible to love being first while serving the One who taught us to become servants. The problem is not leadership, influence, or responsibility. It is the heart’s attachment to prominence.

His desire to be first eventually affects how he treats truth, spiritual authority, fellow believers, and those serving Jesus. What begins as an inward desire for position does not remain inward.

3 John 1:10

NKJV

3 John 1:10 Therefore, if I come, I will call to mind his deeds which he does, prating against us with malicious words. And not content with that, he himself does not receive the brethren, and forbids those who wish to, putting them out of the church.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:10 When I come, I will report some of the things he is doing and the evil accusations he is making against us. Not only does he refuse to welcome the traveling teachers, he also tells others not to help them. And when they do help, he puts them out of the church. (NLT)

Main Idea

John intends to confront Diotrephes because his desire for control has produced malicious accusations, rejection of faithful believers, and the misuse of church authority against those who disagree with him.

Verse Explanation

“Therefore, if I come, I will call to mind his deeds which he does,”

John does not intend to ignore what Diotrephes is doing. If he visits the church, he will bring these actions into the open and address them. The following phrases identify exactly what has gone wrong.

“prating against us with malicious words.”

Diotrephes was spreading harmful accusations against John and his associates. “Prating” carries the idea of making empty or foolish charges. His words were not merely careless; John describes them as “malicious.”

“And not content with that, he himself does not receive the brethren,”

His opposition moved beyond words into actions. Diotrephes refused hospitality to the traveling Christian workers whom Gaius had faithfully received and supported.

“and forbids those who wish to, putting them out of the church.”

Diotrephes also tried to prevent other believers from receiving these workers. Those who resisted him faced exclusion from the church. His personal desire for preeminence had become controlling behavior that harmed both faithful workers and members of the congregation.

Study Notes

John describes an escalating pattern: malicious speech against John, refusal to receive faithful workers, interference with believers who wanted to receive them, and finally expulsion from the church. Verse 9 reveals the heart behind the pattern, while verse 10 shows what that heart produced.

“Putting them out of the church” indicates that Diotrephes possessed enough influence or authority to impose serious consequences. John does not criticize church discipline itself. The problem is that Diotrephes is apparently using it to enforce his own will rather than to protect the church from genuine sin or error.

Spirit & Life

Diotrephes is a sobering picture of what happens when the desire “to have the preeminence” (v. 9) is allowed to grow. Leadership that should protect and serve people can gradually become a means of controlling them.

John also models something important. Love does not require silence when spiritual authority is being misused and people are being harmed. There are times when faithfulness to Jesus and care for His people require unhealthy behavior to be named and confronted.

3 John 1:11

NKJV

3 John 1:11 Beloved, do not imitate what is evil, but what is good. He who does good is of God, but he who does evil has not seen God.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:11 Dear friend, don’t let this bad example influence you. Follow only what is good. Remember that those who do good prove that they are God’s children, and those who do evil prove that they do not know God. (NLT)

Main Idea

John urges Gaius not to follow the harmful example of Diotrephes but to imitate what is good, because a person’s conduct reveals something real about his relationship with God.

Verse Explanation

“Beloved, do not imitate what is evil, but what is good.”

After describing Diotrephes’s behavior, John turns directly to Gaius. Diotrephes may have influence in the church, but his example is not to be followed. Gaius is to pattern his life after what is good rather than allowing another person’s position or influence to determine whom he imitates.

“He who does good is of God,”

Doing good does not earn a relationship with God. Rather, a life characterized by what is good gives evidence of belonging to Him. John regularly connects genuine relationship with God to a transformed way of living.

“but he who does evil has not seen God.”

John states the contrast strongly. Persistent evil is incompatible with genuinely knowing God. “Seen God” speaks of experiencing and knowing God rather than physically seeing Him. A person cannot claim genuine fellowship with God while consistently choosing a way of life opposed to His character.

Study Notes

“Imitate” (mimeomai) means to follow or pattern oneself after someone. John immediately follows this command by presenting Demetrius in verse 12 as a positive example, creating a deliberate contrast: Diotrephes is the example not to follow; Demetrius is a man whose life has received a good testimony.

John’s language resembles 1 John, where conduct repeatedly serves as evidence of spiritual reality (1 John 2:3-6; 3:6-10). The point is not sinless perfection but the direction and settled character of a person’s life.

Spirit & Life

Who we allow to become our example matters. Position, confidence, influence, and even spiritual authority do not necessarily make someone worth imitating. John gives Gaius a simpler test: look at the fruit.

The safest pattern is not the person who appears strongest or most important, but the life in which the character of God is becoming visible.

3 John 1:12

NKJV

3 John 1:12 Demetrius has a good testimony from all, and from the truth itself. And we also bear witness, and you know that our testimony is true.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:12 Everyone speaks highly of Demetrius, as does the truth itself. We ourselves can say the same for him, and you know we speak the truth. (NLT)

Main Idea

Demetrius stands in sharp contrast to Diotrephes. His character and faithfulness to the truth are affirmed by everyone who knows him, by the truth itself, and personally by John.

Verse Explanation

“Demetrius has a good testimony from all,”

John now gives Gaius a positive example to follow after warning him not to imitate evil (v. 11). Demetrius had earned a consistently good reputation among those who knew him. His character was not merely his own assessment of himself; others could testify to it.

“and from the truth itself.”

Demetrius’s life agreed with the truth he professed. The truth itself could be pictured as a witness on his behalf because his conduct measured up to it. This is stronger than simply having a good reputation. What people said about Demetrius was consistent with the standard of the gospel.

“And we also bear witness, and you know that our testimony is true.”

John adds his own testimony to that of the others. Gaius knows John well enough to trust his judgment and recognize the reliability of his commendation of Demetrius.

Study Notes

Demetrius may have been the person carrying 3 John to Gaius, which would explain why John gives him such a strong recommendation, but the letter does not explicitly say so.

There is a deliberate contrast between Diotrephes and Demetrius. Diotrephes “loves to have the preeminence” (v. 9), while Demetrius simply “has a good testimony.” One seeks standing; the other has earned respect through a life consistent with the truth.

Spirit & Life

Demetrius gives us a different picture of spiritual influence. Nothing here is said about his position, title, ability, or prominence. What matters is his testimony.

There is something powerful about a life that does not need to advertise its faithfulness. Others see it, the truth confirms it, and mature believers can confidently affirm it. Character eventually speaks for itself.

3 John 1:13

NKJV

3 John 1:13 I had many things to write, but I do not wish to write to you with pen and ink;(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:13 I have much more to say to you, but I don’t want to write it with pen and ink. (NLT)

Main Idea

John has much more he wants to communicate to Gaius, but he prefers to save it for a personal visit rather than put everything into writing.

Verse Explanation

“I had many things to write,”

John’s brief letter does not exhaust what he wants to say. There are additional matters he would like to discuss with Gaius, perhaps including the situation involving Diotrephes, although John does not specify them.

“but I do not wish to write to you with pen and ink;”

John deliberately limits what he puts into the letter. “Pen and ink” simply refers to the ordinary materials used for writing, but the phrase prepares for his explanation in verse 14: he hopes to speak with Gaius personally.

Study Notes

This closely parallels the ending of 2 John, where John likewise says that he has much to communicate but prefers “to speak face to face” (2 John 1:12). Together, the endings remind us that these letters arose within real relationships, not merely as theological documents.

The “pen” was likely a reed pen, while the “ink” was typically made from a carbon-based mixture. The detail is ordinary, but it gives us a small glimpse into the physical process by which New Testament letters were written.

Spirit & Life

John values written communication, but he recognizes its limits. Some things are better handled in personal presence, where conversation can carry understanding, relationship, tone, and response in ways a letter cannot.

That is especially meaningful in a letter containing relational tension. John is willing to write what needs to be written, but his goal is not merely to send words. He wants to be with the people involved.

3 John 1:14

NKJV

3 John 1:14 but I hope to see you shortly, and we shall speak face to face. Peace to you. Our friends greet you. Greet the friends by name.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:14 For I hope to see you soon, and then we will talk face to face. (NLT)

Main Idea

John closes with the hope of seeing Gaius personally, speaking face to face, and extending peace and personal greetings within their shared community of believers.

Verse Explanation

“but I hope to see you shortly, and we shall speak face to face.”

John expects his written communication to give way to personal fellowship. “Face to face” is literally “mouth to mouth,” a vivid expression for direct, personal conversation. John has more to discuss, but he would rather do so in Gaius’s presence.

“Peace to you.”

This is more than a conventional farewell. In a letter containing the conflict caused by Diotrephes, “peace” is particularly fitting. John desires the well-being and wholeness that come from God for Gaius.

“Our friends greet you.”

John sends greetings from believers who are with him. Calling them “friends” emphasizes the genuine personal relationships within this Christian community.

“Greet the friends by name.”

John asks Gaius to personally greet the believers where he is. “By name” gives the closing an intimate quality. These are not anonymous members of a congregation but individuals personally known and valued.

Study Notes

“Face to face” differs slightly from the expression in 2 John 1:12, but both communicate the same desire for personal conversation rather than continued correspondence.

The use of “friends” is unusual in New Testament letter closings, where “brothers” or “saints” is more common. It fits the strongly relational character of 3 John and may echo Jesus’s own language for His disciples as friends (John 15:13-15), though a direct connection cannot be established.

Spirit & Life

“Greet the friends by name” is a fitting final picture for a letter concerned with healthy and unhealthy spiritual leadership. Diotrephes loves preeminence and pushes people away; John knows people personally and wants them greeted individually.

God’s people are never merely a crowd. Spiritual life is deeply relational, and mature leadership does not simply value the work being done. It values the people themselves.
