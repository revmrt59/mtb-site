Chapter Study

3 John 1

Verse-by-Verse
3 John 1:1
3 John 1:1
NKJV

3 John 1:1 The Elder, To the beloved Gaius, whom I love in truth:(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:1 This letter is from John, the elder. I am writing to Gaius, my dear friend, whom I love in the truth. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder, writing without giving his name, addresses Gaius as “the beloved” and says he loves him “in truth.” His love is sincere and grows out of the truth they share in Christ, and that bond frames everything the letter will ask of Gaius.

Verse Explanation

The Elder, writing without giving his name, addresses Gaius as “the beloved” and says he loves him “in truth.” His love is sincere and grows out of the truth they share in Christ, and that bond frames everything the letter will ask of Gaius.

“The Elder,”

The writer gives a title instead of his name. The Greek word for “elder” describes an older man, and in the early church it also became the title of a recognized leader. Here the definite article makes it a personal title: Gaius knows exactly who “the Elder” is, and the writer expects that title to carry weight with him. The same title opens 2 John. The church has long identified the writer as John the apostle, who by this time would have been an old man.

“To the beloved Gaius,”

The letter is addressed to one man. Gaius was among the most common names in the Roman world, and nothing in the letter connects him with any other Gaius in the New Testament. What the letter does show is a believer who welcomed traveling Christians (verses 5–8) and to whom the Elder could write plainly about what a man named Diotrephes was doing in the church (verses 9–10).

Before the Elder says anything about what Gaius does, he calls him “beloved.” The word means one who is dearly loved. The Elder uses it four times in this short letter (verses 1, 2, 5, 11), and each later use opens a new turn in what he says. Gaius is first of all someone the Elder loves, and the requests and warnings that follow come from that love.

“whom I love in truth:”

The words “whom I love in truth” also appear in the opening of 2 John (2 John 1:1). To love “in truth” means to love sincerely, without pretense. It also means to love within the truth of God revealed in Jesus Christ, the truth that both the Elder and Gaius have received. The two senses belong together. The Elder’s love is real because it rests on a shared relationship with God, and that shared truth is what binds two believers who may live far apart.

The word “truth” will appear again and again in this letter (verses 1, 3, 4, 8, 12). The Elder loves Gaius in the truth, Gaius walks in the truth, faithful workers serve the truth, and the truth itself bears witness to Demetrius. The first verse already sets the theme that holds the letter together.

Study Notes

The Greek word for “elder” is presbyteros. It can mean simply an older man, but in the New Testament it is also used for the leaders of local churches (Acts 14:23; Titus 1:5). With the article, “the Elder” functions as a title of respect that the readers would recognize without further explanation.

The letter does not name its writer. Christian tradition has generally attributed 2 and 3 John to John the apostle. The church historian Eusebius, writing in the early fourth century, noted that some in his day doubted whether these two letters came from the apostle or from another man named John (Church History 3.25.3). The close match in language with 2 John and 1 John supports common authorship of the three letters.

In Greek, “in truth” has no article here, as in 2 John 1:1. Without the article the phrase can describe the manner of the Elder’s love (truly, sincerely), while the letter’s repeated use of “the truth” with the article (verses 3, 4, 8, 12) points to the content of the faith. The opening phrase can carry both ideas.

3 John 1:2
3 John 1:2
NKJV

3 John 1:2 Beloved, I pray that you may prosper in all things and be in health, just as your soul prospers.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:2 Dear friend, I hope all is well with you and that you are as healthy in body as you are strong in spirit. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder prays that Gaius may “prosper in all things and be in health, just as your soul prospers.” He cares about Gaius’s body and everyday circumstances, but he measures them by the health of Gaius’s inner life with God, which is already thriving; the verse is a personal prayer, not a promise of health or wealth to every believer.

Verse Explanation

The Elder prays that Gaius may “prosper in all things and be in health, just as your soul prospers.” He cares about Gaius’s body and everyday circumstances, but he measures them by the health of Gaius’s inner life with God, which is already thriving; the verse is a personal prayer, not a promise of health or wealth to every believer.

“Beloved, I pray”

The Elder turns to Gaius with the same affectionate word, “Beloved,” and begins with prayer. Ancient letters often opened with a wish for the reader’s good health. The Elder takes that familiar courtesy and makes it a genuine prayer to God on Gaius’s behalf. His care for Gaius is not limited to doctrine or church matters. He brings the whole of Gaius’s life before God.

“that you may prosper in all things and be in health,”

The Elder prays that things may go well for Gaius “in all things.” The word translated “prosper” speaks of things going well, of a good and successful course. Gaius welcomed traveling believers (verses 5–6), and that kind of care could take time, energy, and resources. The Elder prays that God will make Gaius’s circumstances go well.

He also prays that Gaius may “be in health.” The Elder does not treat the body as unimportant or prayer for it as unspiritual. He asks God for Gaius’s physical well-being. The verse does not say Gaius was sick, and it should not be read as a diagnosis. It is the ordinary prayer of a friend who cares about the person he loves.

“just as your soul prospers.”

The last phrase sets the measure for the whole prayer. Gaius’s soul, his inner life with God, is already doing well. The Elder prays that his circumstances and health will go as well as his soul is going. This is a striking order. Most people would measure a person’s well-being by outward success or health. The Elder measures it by the condition of the soul and prays that everything else will match it.

The prayer is personal. It expresses the Elder’s love for one believer whose soul he knows to be thriving. It is not a promise that every believer will have health and wealth, and it does not make outward prosperity the evidence of spiritual health. The verse gives the inner life priority, and it also shows that God’s people may freely pray for one another’s bodies and daily needs.

Study Notes

Health wishes were a standard part of the opening of personal letters in the Greek-speaking world, and many surviving papyrus letters begin with a prayer or wish that the recipient is well. The Elder’s prayer follows this custom but adds a comparison with the health of Gaius’s soul, which goes beyond the usual formula.

The Greek verb translated “prosper” (euodoō) was formed from words meaning “good” and “way” or “road,” and it originally described a good journey. By New Testament times it was used more generally for things going well. Paul uses it when he prays that he may find a way to come to Rome (Romans 1:10) and in his instruction to set aside giving “as he may prosper” (1 Corinthians 16:2).

The verb translated “be in health” (hygiainō) is the same verb Paul uses for “sound doctrine” (1 Timothy 1:10; Titus 1:9). Here it has its ordinary sense of physical health.

In this verse “soul” translates the Greek word psychē and refers to Gaius’s inner life, his life as a person before God, in distinction from his body and outward circumstances.

3 John 1:3
3 John 1:3
NKJV

3 John 1:3 For I rejoiced greatly when brethren came and testified of the truth that is in you, just as you walk in the truth.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:3 Some of the traveling teachers recently returned and made me very happy by telling me about your faithfulness and that you are living according to the truth. (NLT)

Main Idea

The opening “For” explains the Elder’s confidence about Gaius’s soul: brethren came and “testified of the truth that is in you, just as you walk in the truth.” The truth Gaius holds can be seen in the way he lives, and the report made the Elder rejoice greatly.

Verse Explanation

The opening “For” explains the Elder’s confidence about Gaius’s soul: brethren came and “testified of the truth that is in you, just as you walk in the truth.” The truth Gaius holds can be seen in the way he lives, and the report made the Elder rejoice greatly.

“For I rejoiced greatly”

The opening “For” explains why the Elder could pray so confidently about Gaius’s soul. He had good evidence. He “rejoiced greatly” at what he had heard, the same words he uses in 2 John 1:4, where he rejoiced to find some of the elect lady’s children “walking in truth.” In both letters the Elder’s great joy comes from the same source.

“when brethren came and testified of the truth that is in you,”

The “brethren” are fellow believers. They may well be the travelers Gaius had welcomed, who in verse 6 bear witness of his love before the church. They came to the Elder and “testified,” bearing witness to what they knew of Gaius. Their testimony showed that the truth was more than something Gaius professed; it could be seen in how he lived.

They testified “of the truth that is in you.” The truth was not a borrowed creed that Gaius repeated. It had become his own and was at work within him. The brethren could not see inside Gaius’s heart, but they could see the truth that lived there by the way it showed itself.

“just as you walk in the truth.”

This phrase explains how the brethren knew the truth was in Gaius: he walked in it. In Scripture, to “walk” describes the steady direction and conduct of a life. Gaius’s daily life matched the truth he believed. There was no gap between what he professed and how he lived, and that consistency was visible to those who knew him.

Study Notes

In Greek, “came” and “testified” are present participles, which can describe repeated action. The wording may indicate that brethren kept coming and kept bringing the same report about Gaius, rather than a single visit, though the grammar alone does not settle the point.

In Greek, the phrase “the truth that is in you” reads literally “your truth” (sou tē alētheia). The expression presents the truth as something Gaius possesses and lives by, not merely as a body of teaching he has heard.

The verb “testified” (martyreō) means to bear witness to what one has seen or knows. The same word family appears again in verse 6 (“borne witness”) and three times in verse 12, where it describes the witness to Demetrius.

3 John 1:4
3 John 1:4
NKJV

3 John 1:4 I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:4 I could have no greater joy than to hear that my children are following the truth. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder names his highest joy: “I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth.” He speaks as a spiritual father, and nothing gladdens him more than hearing that those in his care live consistently with the truth.

Verse Explanation

The Elder names his highest joy: “I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth.” He speaks as a spiritual father, and nothing gladdens him more than hearing that those in his care live consistently with the truth.

“I have no greater joy”

The Elder now generalizes from Gaius to all those in his care. He has no joy greater than the one he is about to name. Coming from an old man who had served Christ for decades, that is a weighty statement. His highest joy is not success, influence, or the size of a ministry.

“than to hear”

The Elder’s joy comes by report. He is not present with Gaius. He hears from others how those he loves are living, as he did in verse 3. Faithful believers who carry such news become messengers of joy to those who care for them.

“that my children walk in truth.”

The Elder calls those in his care “my children.” He speaks as a spiritual father. Gaius may have come to faith through the Elder’s ministry, or he may have been under the Elder’s pastoral care for some time. Either way, the Elder feels toward him what a father feels toward his children.

His joy is that they “walk in truth.” The phrase picks up the end of verse 3 and states the Elder’s deepest desire for all his spiritual children. Their outward prosperity and health matter to him enough to pray for them (verse 2), but nothing gives him greater joy than knowing that their lives follow the truth.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “greater” (meizoteran) adds an extra comparison ending to a word that already means “greater.” The doubled form is emphatic: no joy ranks above this one.

The Greek wording of “my children” places emphasis on “my” (ta ema tekna), so that the phrase means my own children. The Elder uses similar family language for believers throughout 1 John, where he addresses his readers as “little children” (1 John 2:1).

3 John 1:5–8 — Welcoming and Sending Those Who Went Out for His Name

3 John 1:5
3 John 1:5
NKJV

3 John 1:5 Beloved, you do faithfully whatever you do for the brethren and for strangers,(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:5 Dear friend, you are being faithful to God when you care for the traveling teachers who pass through, even though they are strangers to you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Turning to Gaius’s hospitality, the Elder tells him, “you do faithfully whatever you do for the brethren and for strangers.” Gaius’s care for fellow believers who were strangers to him is an act of faithfulness, the visible form his walk in the truth has taken.

Verse Explanation

Turning to Gaius’s hospitality, the Elder tells him, “you do faithfully whatever you do for the brethren and for strangers.” Gaius’s care for fellow believers who were strangers to him is an act of faithfulness, the visible form his walk in the truth has taken.

“Beloved, you do faithfully whatever you do”

With another “Beloved,” the Elder turns from his joy to Gaius’s hospitality. He says that Gaius does “faithfully” whatever he does for the travelers. The Greek expression means that he does a faithful thing, an act that shows his faith and his trustworthiness. Practical care, such as lodging, meals, or supplies for the road, may seem ordinary work, but the Elder counts it as faithfulness. Gaius’s walk in the truth (verses 3–4) takes this practical, visible form.

“Whatever you do” covers whatever care Gaius gave the travelers, which could include lodging, meals, and help on their way. The Elder sees no part of that work as too small to matter.

“for the brethren and for strangers,”

Gaius serves “the brethren,” fellow believers. He also serves “strangers.” The context suggests that these strangers were themselves believers who were unknown to Gaius personally. Gaius welcomed them because they belonged to Christ and were serving Him, and that was enough. His love did not depend on long acquaintance or personal friendship.

In the first century, Christian travelers relied on such welcome. Inns were often expensive and had a poor reputation, and a believer’s home offered safety, food, and fellowship. Hospitality of this kind allowed the gospel to travel from town to town.

Study Notes

The NKJV reads “for the brethren and for strangers,” following manuscripts that can suggest two groups. Many other manuscripts, followed by most modern translations, have wording that means Gaius served the brethren, especially when they were strangers, identifying the brethren and the strangers as the same people. Either reading describes Gaius’s welcome of believers he did not previously know.

The word translated “strangers” (xenos) means a foreigner or someone unknown. Related words in the New Testament describe hospitality as love shown to strangers (Romans 12:13; Hebrews 13:2).

3 John 1:6
3 John 1:6
NKJV

3 John 1:6 who have borne witness of your love before the church. If you send them forward on their journey in a manner worthy of God, you will do well,(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:6 They have told the church here of your loving friendship. Please continue providing for such teachers in a manner that pleases God. (NLT)

Main Idea

These travelers have “borne witness of your love before the church,” so Gaius’s kindness has been reported openly. The Elder asks him to keep sending them on their way “in a manner worthy of God,” supplying what they need for the next stage of their journey as befits servants of God, and says that in doing so he “will do well.”

Verse Explanation

These travelers have “borne witness of your love before the church,” so Gaius’s kindness has been reported openly. The Elder asks him to keep sending them on their way “in a manner worthy of God,” supplying what they need for the next stage of their journey as befits servants of God, and says that in doing so he “will do well.”

“who have borne witness of your love before the church.”

The travelers had “borne witness of your love before the church.” They had spoken publicly about him, most likely in the gathering of the church where the Elder was. Gaius’s care had been given privately, but it was reported openly among believers. The Elder calls what they saw “your love.” Gaius’s practical care was love in action.

“If you send them forward on their journey”

The Elder now makes his request. To “send them forward on their journey” (verse 6) meant more than saying goodbye at the door. It meant helping travelers on to the next stage of their trip, which could include food, money, supplies, or companions for the road. Paul asked churches for the same kind of help (Romans 15:24; 1 Corinthians 16:6), and he told Titus to send two workers “on their journey with haste, that they may lack nothing” (Titus 3:13). The Elder asks Gaius to keep giving this help, either to the same workers on a later visit or to others like them.

“in a manner worthy of God,”

The Elder sets the standard for this help. Gaius is to send them “in a manner worthy of God.” The measure is not merely what is customary, convenient, or polite. These travelers serve God, so the way they are treated should fit the God they serve. Paul uses the same standard for the whole Christian life when he urges believers to “walk worthy of God” (1 Thessalonians 2:12).

“you will do well,”

The phrase “you will do well” was a common courteous way of making a request, much like saying please. The Elder does not command Gaius. He asks him warmly, confident that Gaius will want to do what is right. The sentence continues into verse 7, which explains why this help is so important.

Study Notes

The verb translated “send them forward on their journey” (propempō) was the regular word for helping travelers on their way. In the New Testament it often describes the support churches gave to those engaged in ministry (Acts 15:3; Romans 15:24; 1 Corinthians 16:6, 11; Titus 3:13).

The phrase “before the church” uses the Greek word ekklēsia, the regular word for a local congregation. The church in view here is most likely the Elder’s own congregation, where the returning travelers made their report. The letter does not name its location.

3 John 1:7
3 John 1:7
NKJV

3 John 1:7 because they went forth for His name's sake, taking nothing from the Gentiles.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:7 For they are traveling for the Lord, and they accept nothing from people who are not believers. (NLT)

Main Idea

The reason for this care lies in the workers’ purpose and practice: “they went forth for His name’s sake, taking nothing from the Gentiles.” They traveled for the sake of Jesus and accepted no support from the unbelieving outsiders they went to reach, so their needs fell to believers.

Verse Explanation

The reason for this care lies in the workers’ purpose and practice: “they went forth for His name’s sake, taking nothing from the Gentiles.” They traveled for the sake of Jesus and accepted no support from the unbelieving outsiders they went to reach, so their needs fell to believers.

“because they went forth for His name’s sake,”

Here is the reason the travelers deserve such care. They “went forth,” setting out to carry the message of Christ. They did it “for His name’s sake.” The Greek has no word for “His” here. It speaks simply of the Name, and the NKJV supplies “His” to make clear whose name is meant. Early believers spoke of Jesus this way. When the apostles were beaten in Jerusalem, they rejoiced that they were “counted worthy to suffer shame for His name” (Acts 5:41).

3 John never uses the name Jesus or the title Christ, yet He is the reason behind everything the travelers do. They left home, depended on strangers, and labored on the road for His sake.

“taking nothing from the Gentiles.”

The travelers also had a settled practice: they took “nothing from the Gentiles.” In this letter, “Gentiles” most likely refers to unbelieving outsiders, the people the travelers went to reach with the gospel. They accepted no support from them. Their purpose was to give the gospel, not to profit from those who heard it. Their refusal may also have kept their message distinct from traveling teachers of the day who sought money from their audiences.

Because they would not take support from unbelievers, their needs fell to believers. This is the link to the Elder’s conclusion in verse 8. If the workers refuse help from those they are trying to reach, then the church must provide for them.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “went forth” (exerchomai) means to go out. In 2 John 1:7 the NKJV says that many deceivers “have gone out into the world,” and many Greek editions use the same verb there, though some manuscripts of 2 John read a verb meaning “entered.” The two letters describe travelers with opposite purposes: the deceivers spread a denial of Christ, while these workers go out for the sake of His name.

The Greek word translated “Gentiles” here is ethnikos, an adjective formed from the word for “nations.” Jewish speakers used related words for non-Jews in general. In 3 John the word points to unbelieving outsiders rather than to Gentile Christians, who would have been among the believers supporting the travelers.

The participle “taking” (lambanontes) in verse 7 and the verb “receive” (hypolambanein) in verse 8 come from the same Greek root. The Elder sets the two side by side: the workers take nothing from unbelievers, so believers ought to take the workers in.

3 John 1:8
3 John 1:8
NKJV

3 John 1:8 We therefore ought to receive such, that we may become fellow workers for the truth.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:8 So we ourselves should support them so that we can be their partners as they teach the truth. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder draws the conclusion: “We therefore ought to receive such, that we may become fellow workers for the truth.” Believers who welcome and support these workers become partners in their work, so those who send share with those who go in spreading the truth.

Verse Explanation

The Elder draws the conclusion: “We therefore ought to receive such, that we may become fellow workers for the truth.” Believers who welcome and support these workers become partners in their work, so those who send share with those who go in spreading the truth.

“We therefore ought to receive such,”

The Elder draws his conclusion. “Therefore” ties this verse to the travelers’ purpose and practice in verse 7. Because they went out for Christ and accepted nothing from unbelievers, believers “ought” to support them. The word expresses obligation, something owed. Supporting such workers is not an optional kindness for the especially generous. It is part of the love believers owe one another.

The Elder says “We,” including himself with Gaius. And he speaks of receiving “such,” people of this kind. The obligation applies to workers like these, who go out for the Name and depend on believers. It does not establish a rule for every request for support. The Elder’s companion warning in 2 John 1:10–11 makes clear that believers must not support those who deny Christ.

To “receive” here means to welcome, take in, and provide for. It is the opposite of what Diotrephes does when he “does not receive the brethren” (verse 10).

“that we may become fellow workers for the truth.”

The purpose of receiving such workers is that believers “may become fellow workers for the truth.” Those who host, feed, and send the travelers share in the work the travelers are doing. Gaius may never preach in another town, but by supporting those who do, he becomes their partner. The work belongs to everyone who has a part in it, those who go and those who send.

The partnership is “for the truth,” the truth of God revealed in Jesus Christ. That truth is what the travelers carry, and supporting them advances its spread. Jesus taught His disciples, “He who receives you receives Me” (Matthew 10:40). Welcoming His messengers is a way of welcoming the One who sent them, and 3 John shows that such welcome makes a believer a true partner in the work.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “ought” (opheilō) expresses obligation or debt. John uses the same verb in 1 John 3:16 (“we also ought to lay down our lives for the brethren”) and 1 John 4:11.

The Greek verb translated “receive” here (hypolambanō) means to take up or take under one’s care, and in this setting refers to hospitality and support. A different verb (epidechomai) is used for Diotrephes’s refusal to “receive” in verses 9 and 10.

The phrase “fellow workers for the truth” could also be translated with “with” in place of “for.” If that rendering is taken, the truth itself is pictured as at work in the world, and believers work alongside it. Either way, those who support the travelers share in the work of spreading the truth.

3 John 1:9–10 — Diotrephes Refuses the Elder and Shuts Out the Brethren

3 John 1:9
3 John 1:9
NKJV

3 John 1:9 I wrote to the church, but Diotrephes, who loves to have the preeminence among them, does not receive us.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:9 I wrote to the church about this, but Diotrephes, who loves to be the leader, refuses to have anything to do with us. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder had written to the church, but Diotrephes, “who loves to have the preeminence among them,” refuses to receive the Elder and those with him. His desire to be first sets him against the Elder’s authority, though the letter does not say what position he held or what the earlier letter contained.

Verse Explanation

The Elder had written to the church, but Diotrephes, “who loves to have the preeminence among them,” refuses to receive the Elder and those with him. His desire to be first sets him against the Elder’s authority, though the letter does not say what position he held or what the earlier letter contained.

“I wrote to the church,”

The Elder now turns to a problem. He had already written “to the church,” the congregation where Diotrephes held influence. The letter does not say whether Gaius himself belonged to that church. The letter does not say what that earlier letter contained or whether it has survived. It may have asked the church to welcome the same traveling workers.

“but Diotrephes, who loves to have the preeminence among them,”

The word “but” introduces the obstacle. Diotrephes is named directly, and he is described by what he loves: he “loves to have the preeminence,” to be first. His desire is for position and control “among them,” among the believers of that church. Gaius is defined at the start of the letter as one who is loved (verse 1). Diotrephes is defined by his love of being first.

The letter does not say what office Diotrephes held. He clearly had enough influence to refuse the Elder and to put people out of the church (verse 10), but whether he was an elder, a host of the church, or simply a dominant member is not stated. The letter also raises no charge of false teaching against him. His failure, as the Elder describes it, lies in his ambition and in what that ambition led him to do.

“does not receive us.”

Diotrephes “does not receive us.” He refused to accept the Elder and those with him, rejecting their authority and, apparently, the earlier letter. In Greek the verb is in the present tense, describing an ongoing refusal. A man who loves to be first will resist anyone whose authority might limit his own.

Study Notes

An early, well-attested reading adds the Greek word ti, “something,” after “I wrote,” and some modern translations follow it. The NKJV reads “I wrote to the church.” Either way, the Elder refers to an earlier letter. Some have suggested that it was 2 John, but neither letter identifies it, and it may not have survived.

The phrase “who loves to have the preeminence” translates a single Greek participle (philoprōteuōn) describing one who loves to be first. It occurs nowhere else in the New Testament. A related verb (prōteuō) appears once, in Colossians 1:18, where Christ is the one who has “the preeminence” in all things.

The Greek verb translated “receive” in verses 9 and 10 (epidechomai) appears in the New Testament only in these two verses. In verse 9 it describes accepting the Elder and his authority; in verse 10 it describes welcoming the traveling brethren.

The name Diotrephes means nourished by Zeus, a name drawn from the chief god of the Greek world. Such names were common and say nothing about the bearer’s own beliefs.

3 John 1:10
3 John 1:10
NKJV

3 John 1:10 Therefore, if I come, I will call to mind his deeds which he does, prating against us with malicious words. And not content with that, he himself does not receive the brethren, and forbids those who wish to, putting them out of the church.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:10 When I come, I will report some of the things he is doing and the evil accusations he is making against us. Not only does he refuse to welcome the traveling teachers, he also tells others not to help them. And when they do help, he puts them out of the church. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because of this, if the Elder comes he will “call to mind his deeds”: Diotrephes speaks against the Elder and his companions “with malicious words,” refuses to receive the brethren himself, and forbids those who want to welcome them, “putting them out of the church.” The verse traces how his love of first place has turned his influence into slander and a means of shutting others out.

Verse Explanation

Because of this, if the Elder comes he will “call to mind his deeds”: Diotrephes speaks against the Elder and his companions “with malicious words,” refuses to receive the brethren himself, and forbids those who want to welcome them, “putting them out of the church.” The verse traces how his love of first place has turned his influence into slander and a means of shutting others out.

“Therefore, if I come, I will call to mind his deeds which he does,”

Because of Diotrephes’s refusal, the Elder plans to act. “If I come” shows that the visit is a real intention but not yet certain (compare verse 14). If he does come, he “will call to mind” Diotrephes’s deeds, bringing them into the open before the church. The Elder does not threaten revenge or punishment. He intends to name what Diotrephes has been doing so that the church can see it plainly.

The Elder speaks of “his deeds which he does,” present and ongoing actions. The rest of the verse lists them.

“prating against us with malicious words.”

First, Diotrephes has been “prating against us with malicious words.” To prate is to talk on and on foolishly. His charges against the Elder and his companions were empty talk, without substance, but they were spoken with ill will. “Malicious words” are evil words, meant to damage. Diotrephes used speech to undermine the Elder’s standing in the church.

“And not content with that, he himself does not receive the brethren,”

“Not content with that,” Diotrephes went further. Words were not enough for him, and his opposition turned into action. “He himself does not receive the brethren”: he personally refused to welcome traveling believers like those Gaius had welcomed. The kind of workers the Elder asks Gaius to send forward “in a manner worthy of God” (verse 6) found Diotrephes’s door closed.

“and forbids those who wish to, putting them out of the church.”

Diotrephes did not stop with his own refusal. He “forbids those who wish to” receive the brethren, using his influence to stop others from showing hospitality. He was also “putting them out of the church”: those who wished to receive the brethren were driven out of the fellowship of the congregation.

The verse traces a progression: refusing the Elder, slandering him, refusing the brethren, forbidding others to welcome them, and finally expelling those who wished to. Each step widens the harm. The love of being first (verse 9) has turned Diotrephes’s influence into a means of shutting people out of the very fellowship Christ intends to be open to His servants. The letter gives this as the Elder’s account. It does not report how Diotrephes would explain himself or how the visit turned out.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “call to mind” (hypomimnēskō) means to remind or bring to remembrance. Jesus uses the same verb in John 14:26 for the Holy Spirit’s work of bringing to remembrance what He had said. Here it describes the Elder bringing Diotrephes’s actions to the church’s attention.

The Greek verb translated “prating” (phlyareō) occurs only here in the New Testament. It describes talking nonsense, idle and empty chatter. A related adjective describes “gossips” in 1 Timothy 5:13.

The phrase “putting them out” uses the Greek verb ekballō, which means to throw out or drive out. The same verb describes the man born blind being cast out of the synagogue in John 9:34.

3 John 1:11–12 — Imitate What Is Good: Demetrius Commended

3 John 1:11
3 John 1:11
NKJV

3 John 1:11 Beloved, do not imitate what is evil, but what is good. He who does good is of God, but he who does evil has not seen God.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:11 Dear friend, don’t let this bad example influence you. Follow only what is good. Remember that those who do good prove that they are God’s children, and those who do evil prove that they do not know God. (NLT)

Main Idea

Addressing Gaius again as “Beloved,” the Elder tells him, “do not imitate what is evil, but what is good.” The one who does good shows that he “is of God,” while the one who does evil “has not seen God,” that is, does not truly know Him; the verse describes the direction of a life, not a claim that believers never sin.

Verse Explanation

Addressing Gaius again as “Beloved,” the Elder tells him, “do not imitate what is evil, but what is good.” The one who does good shows that he “is of God,” while the one who does evil “has not seen God,” that is, does not truly know Him; the verse describes the direction of a life, not a claim that believers never sin.

“Beloved, do not imitate what is evil, but what is good.”

The Elder turns back to Gaius with “Beloved,” marking a new turn in the letter. Gaius has two examples in front of him: Diotrephes, whose conduct the Elder has just described, and Demetrius, whom the Elder is about to commend. The command tells Gaius how to respond to both. He is not to “imitate what is evil” but “what is good.”

To imitate is to follow an example. People tend to become like those they watch and admire. Diotrephes was pressing those who wanted to receive the workers, forbidding them and putting them out of the church. The Elder urges Gaius not to follow that example but to follow what is good.

“He who does good is of God,”

The Elder gives the reason. “He who does good is of God.” A life that does good shows its origin. Such a person belongs to God and draws his life from Him. Good conduct is the visible evidence of a relationship with God and the fruit of His grace, not a way of earning that relationship.

“but he who does evil has not seen God.”

The contrast follows. “He who does evil has not seen God.” To have “seen God” here means to know Him, to have a real and living relationship with Him. John writes in his first letter, “Whoever sins has neither seen Him nor known Him” (1 John 3:6). A life whose settled course is doing evil shows that the person does not truly know God, whatever position or claim he may have.

The verse describes the direction of a life, not a single act. John also teaches that believers sin and need forgiveness: “If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins” (1 John 1:9). The Elder is not saying that anyone who ever does wrong has never known God. He is saying that a person’s settled way of life reveals whether he knows the God who is good.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “imitate” (mimeomai) is related to the English word “mimic.” In the New Testament it is used of following a good example (2 Thessalonians 3:7, 9; Hebrews 13:7).

The phrases “he who does good” and “he who does evil” each translate a single Greek word (agathopoiōn and kakopoiōn). Each word joins “good” or “evil” with a word meaning to do, and describes a person whose characteristic conduct is doing good or doing evil.

3 John 1:12
3 John 1:12
NKJV

3 John 1:12 Demetrius has a good testimony from all, and from the truth itself. And we also bear witness, and you know that our testimony is true.(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:12 Everyone speaks highly of Demetrius, as does the truth itself. We ourselves can say the same for him, and you know we speak the truth. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder offers Demetrius as an example of the good to imitate: he “has a good testimony from all, and from the truth itself.” Others speak well of him, his conduct so agrees with the truth that the truth itself bears witness for him, and the Elder and those with him add their own witness, which Gaius knows to be true.

Verse Explanation

The Elder offers Demetrius as an example of the good to imitate: he “has a good testimony from all, and from the truth itself.” Others speak well of him, his conduct so agrees with the truth that the truth itself bears witness for him, and the Elder and those with him add their own witness, which Gaius knows to be true.

“Demetrius has a good testimony from all,”

The Elder now names the good example. “Demetrius” is not otherwise known, and the letter does not say who he was or why he was coming. He may have been the one who carried this letter to Gaius, which would explain why the Elder commends him, but the letter does not say so. What the letter does say is that he “has a good testimony from all.” Those who knew him spoke well of him.

“and from the truth itself.”

Demetrius also has testimony “from the truth itself.” This is best understood to mean that his conduct agreed so fully with the truth that the truth itself stood as a witness for him. Others spoke well of him, and his life matched the truth they all believed. Human reputation can be mistaken, but a life that measures up to the truth of God has the strongest possible witness.

“And we also bear witness,”

The Elder adds a third witness: “we also bear witness.” The Elder and those with him personally vouch for Demetrius. Gaius now has three testimonies to rely on: the report of others, the agreement of Demetrius’s life with the truth, and the word of the Elder and his companions.

“and you know that our testimony is true.”

Finally, the Elder appeals to Gaius’s own experience: “you know that our testimony is true.” Gaius knows the Elder and trusts him. In the face of Diotrephes’s “malicious words” (verse 10), Gaius can rely on the Elder’s word about Demetrius. With such a commendation, Gaius can welcome Demetrius with confidence.

Study Notes

Demetrius was a common Greek name, derived from Demeter, the goddess of grain and harvest. Some have suggested that this Demetrius was the silversmith of Ephesus who opposed Paul (Acts 19:24), but nothing in Scripture supports that identification.

Letters of commendation were common among early Christians, who used them to introduce travelers to believers who did not know them (compare Romans 16:1–2; 2 Corinthians 3:1). Verse 12 functions as a brief commendation of Demetrius, which supports the view that he carried the letter, though the letter does not state it.

The closing words, “you know that our testimony is true,” closely resemble John 21:24, “we know that his testimony is true,” at the end of John’s Gospel. The likeness is one of several shared expressions between the Gospel and the letters.

3 John 1:13–14 — Hope of Speaking Face to Face and Final Greetings

3 John 1:13
3 John 1:13
NKJV

3 John 1:13 I had many things to write, but I do not wish to write to you with pen and ink;(NKJV)

NLT

3 John 1:13 I have much more to say to you, but I don’t want to write it with pen and ink. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Elder has “many things to write,” but he does not wish to set them down “with pen and ink.” He deliberately leaves the rest unwritten, setting it aside for the visit he hopes to make.

Verse Explanation

The Elder has “many things to write,” but he does not wish to set them down “with pen and ink.” He deliberately leaves the rest unwritten, setting it aside for the visit he hopes to make.

“I had many things to write,”

The Elder begins to close the letter. He “had many things to write.” He has said what was most needed, but there is more he could say.

“but I do not wish to write to you with pen and ink;”

He chooses not to set the rest down “with pen and ink.” The “but” and the next verse show his reason: he hopes to see Gaius soon. He deliberately leaves the rest unwritten, saving it for the conversation he hopes to have in person. The verse ends with a semicolon in the NKJV, and the thought continues directly into verse 14.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “pen” (kalamos) means a reed. Writers cut reeds to a point to use as pens. The “ink” (melan, literally “black”) was commonly made from soot or lampblack mixed with gum and water.

The closing of 3 John closely matches 2 John 1:12. There the Elder writes that he did not wish to write “with paper and ink,” but hoped to come and “speak face to face, that our joy may be full.” The similar wording supports the view that the same writer wrote both letters.

3 John 1:14 For I hope to see you soon, and then we will talk face to face. (NLT)

Main Idea

Instead, he hopes to see Gaius “shortly,” and “we shall speak face to face.” He closes with “Peace to you,” greetings from the friends with him, and a request to “greet the friends by name,” so that each believer is greeted personally.

Verse Explanation

Instead, he hopes to see Gaius “shortly,” and “we shall speak face to face.” He closes with “Peace to you,” greetings from the friends with him, and a request to “greet the friends by name,” so that each believer is greeted personally.

“but I hope to see you shortly, and we shall speak face to face.”

The Elder states his hope: he hopes “to see you shortly.” The visit he mentioned in verse 10 (“if I come”) now becomes a confident expectation. When he comes, “we shall speak face to face.” The Elder looks forward to being with Gaius in person, and his closing suggests that some things are better said in conversation than in writing.

“Peace to you.”

The Elder closes with “Peace to you.” It was a common greeting among Jews and Christians, a wish for well-being and wholeness from God. In a letter that has dealt with conflict, the Elder ends by wishing Gaius peace.

“Our friends greet you.”

Those with the Elder send their greetings. The Elder calls them “friends,” a word of warm affection that describes believers bound together by shared love.

“Greet the friends by name.”

The Elder asks Gaius to “greet the friends by name,” one by one. The letter ends with care for each person, not a general greeting to a group. In a letter that has had to describe people being shut out, the Elder ends by greeting the friends around Gaius one by one.

Study Notes

The phrase “face to face” reads literally mouth to mouth in Greek (stoma pros stoma). A similar expression appears in the Greek translation of Numbers 12:8, where the LORD says He speaks with Moses “face to face” in the NKJV.

The NKJV numbers 3 John as 14 verses. Many editions place the final greetings in a fifteenth verse. The text is the same; only the numbering differs.
