Book Overview

At a Glance

Colossians
Author

Paul, “an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God,” names Timothy as “our brother” in the greeting (1:1). He writes as a prisoner and signs the closing greeting “by my own hand” (4:18).

Audience

The letter is addressed to “the saints and faithful brethren in Christ who are in Colosse” (1:2). This church learned the gospel from Epaphras (1:7), and Paul wants the letter read in the church at Laodicea as well (4:16).

Date

The early AD 60s is most likely, during Paul’s imprisonment in Rome (compare Acts 28:16, 30–31). Colossians itself does not say where or when it was written.

Historical Setting

Paul is in chains (4:3, 18) and writes to a church he may never have visited (2:1). Fellow workers and messengers link him with the neighboring churches of Colosse, Laodicea, and Hierapolis (4:7–16).

Purpose

Paul writes so that the Colossians, who “received Christ Jesus the Lord,” will keep walking in Him (2:6) and not be deceived “with persuasive words” (2:4). He also wants them to grow to maturity, “filled with the knowledge of His will” (1:9), so that he may “present every man perfect in Christ Jesus” (1:28).

Central Message

Jesus Christ is the Son through whom all things were created and in whom all of God’s fullness dwells. God made Him head of the church “that in all things He may have the preeminence” (1:18). Through His cross God has forgiven believers and made peace, so those who belong to Christ are “complete in Him” (2:10). Because they died with Christ, they do not have to submit to human regulations that promise what He has already given. Because they were raised with Him, they seek the things above and live a new life in which His lordship reaches the church, the household, prayer, and their speech toward outsiders.

Key Verse

“For in Him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily; and you are complete in Him, who is the head of all principality and power” (2:9–10).

Key Themes

Christ’s preeminence and fullness (1:15–19; 2:9–10); union with Christ in His death and resurrection (2:12, 20; 3:1–4); peace and victory through the cross (1:20–22; 2:13–15); the mystery now revealed, “Christ in you” (1:26–27); Christ the Head and the church His body (1:18; 2:19); true wisdom in Christ set against human tradition (2:3, 8, 23); and thanksgiving (1:3, 12; 2:7; 3:15–17; 4:2).

Book Flow

  • I. Thanksgiving and Prayer for a Fruitful Gospel Life (1:1–1:14): thanks for the gospel’s fruit, prayer for a worthy walk, and rescue into the Son’s kingdom

  • II. The Preeminent Christ and Paul’s Ministry of His Mystery (1:15–2:5): Christ supreme in creation, the church, and reconciliation; Paul labors for their maturity

  • III. Remain Rooted in Christ and Reject Human Regulations (2:6–2:23): complete in Christ and free from human tradition, religious judgments, and regulations

  • IV. Live the New Life Under Christ’s Lordship (3:1–4:6): raised with Christ to a new life in the church, the household, prayer, and gracious speech

  • V. Messengers, Greetings, and Final Instructions (4:7–4:18): Tychicus and Onesimus, greetings from fellow workers, a charge to Archippus, and “Remember my chains”

Go Deeper

Background

Author

Colossians names Paul as its author at the beginning, in the middle, and at the end. He opens as "Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God" (1:1). He speaks personally in the middle of the letter, where he writes that "I, Paul, became a minister" of the gospel (1:23). He closes with "This salutation by my own hand—Paul" (4:18). That last line suggests that someone else wrote down most of the letter and Paul took the pen for the final greeting. It also makes his last request, "Remember my chains," come from his own hand.

Paul names "Timothy our brother" beside him in the greeting (1:1). This is why the opening thanksgiving and prayer speak as "we" (1:3, 9). When the letter turns to Paul's own calling and struggles, the voice changes to "I" (1:23–2:5; 4:3–4). Timothy shares in the greeting, but Paul speaks as the apostle. He describes his calling as "the stewardship from God which was given to me for you" (1:25): a responsibility to make known the mystery of Christ among the Gentiles (1:27). That calling explains why he can write with such authority and personal care to a church that first heard the gospel from someone else.

Audience

Paul writes "to the saints and faithful brethren in Christ who are in Colosse" (1:2). Before he says anything about their problems, he calls them God's holy people and faithful members of His family. Most of them were probably Gentiles. Paul reminds them that they "once were alienated and enemies" (1:21). He speaks of the mystery made known "among the Gentiles" (1:27) and of "the uncircumcision of your flesh" (2:13). This background matters for reading the letter. When Paul says that believers were circumcised "with the circumcision made without hands" (2:11), he is assuring Gentile believers that they already belong fully to God in Christ.

The Colossians learned the gospel from Epaphras, "our dear fellow servant" (1:7). He was "one of you" (4:12), a man from their own city, and he went on "laboring fervently for you in prayers" (4:12). Because their faith came through a trusted local teacher who worked alongside Paul, Paul can urge them to stay "established in the faith, as you have been taught" (2:7). He is confirming what they already received, not correcting it.

Many believers in the region had never met Paul. He writes of his concern for "as many as have not seen my face in the flesh" (2:1). The letter does not settle whether that group includes the Colossians themselves. What Paul does make clear is that the church was healthy. He has heard of their faith in Christ, their love for all the saints, and their hope laid up in heaven (1:4–5). He rejoices "to see your good order and the steadfastness of your faith in Christ" (2:5).

The first hearers reached beyond one congregation. The letter mentions believers in Laodicea and Hierapolis (4:13), "Nymphas and the church that is in his house" (4:15), and Archippus, who receives a personal charge about his ministry (4:17). Within the church were wives and husbands, children and fathers, and enslaved people and their masters (3:18–4:1). The household instructions speak to real people who sat together when the letter was read.

Date

Colossians does not state where or when it was written, but it does say that Paul was in chains (4:3, 18). The setting that best fits is Paul's imprisonment in Rome in the early AD 60s. Acts describes Paul in Rome living under guard for two years in a rented house, welcoming visitors and openly preaching the kingdom of God (Acts 28:16, 30–31). That picture matches the many companions and messengers named at the end of Colossians. It also gives weight to Paul's request that God "would open to us a door for the word" (4:3). Paul asks for an open door for the gospel while his own movements are restricted.

Historical Setting

Paul writes as a prisoner, and he is not alone. Aristarchus is "my fellow prisoner" (4:10), yet Paul still has fellow workers around him and can send and receive messengers (4:7–14). The setting adds force to what Paul writes about Christ. A man in chains declares that all things, "whether thrones or dominions or principalities or powers," were created through Christ and for Him (1:16).

Paul's companions include Tychicus, Onesimus, Aristarchus, "Mark the cousin of Barnabas," "Jesus who is called Justus," Epaphras, "Luke the beloved physician," and Demas (4:7–14). Paul notes that Aristarchus, Mark, and Justus are his "only fellow workers for the kingdom of God who are of the circumcision" (4:11), meaning the only Jewish believers among his coworkers. Jewish and Gentile believers were serving side by side, which is a living example of the new people Paul describes, where "there is neither Greek nor Jew … but Christ is all and in all" (3:11).

Colosse belonged to a group of neighboring churches. Epaphras has "a great zeal for you, and those who are in Laodicea, and those in Hierapolis" (4:13). The churches greet one another, and Paul tells the Colossians that when this letter "is read among you," it should also be read in the Laodicean church, while they read the letter from Laodicea (4:16). Letters like this one were read aloud to the gathered church and passed from congregation to congregation. Colossians was written to be heard together, and the believers met in homes such as the one where Nymphas hosted a church (4:15).

One of the messengers gives the letter a personal connection. Onesimus is "a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you" (4:9). Colossians says no more about him, but read alongside Philemon 10–16, he is the same Onesimus whom Paul asks Philemon to receive no longer simply as an enslaved man but as a beloved brother. Exactly how Onesimus came to be separated from Philemon is not spelled out. This connection gives the instructions to bondservants and masters (3:22–4:1) a real-life counterpart. Among the letter's first readers, the gospel was changing the relationship between an enslaved man and his master.

Occasion

The news that set the letter in motion was good. Epaphras "declared to us your love in the Spirit" (1:8), and from "the day we heard it," Paul and Timothy did not stop praying for the Colossians (1:9). Paul's thanksgiving, prayer, and teaching grow out of that report. His concern for them runs deep. He wants them to know "what a great conflict I have for you" (2:1), meaning the effort and struggle he spends on behalf of believers he may never have met.

Paul also writes to protect them. He warns "lest anyone should deceive you with persuasive words" (2:4) and "Beware lest anyone cheat you" (2:8). Most of the warning reads as a guard for a steady church. Yet his question, "why … do you subject yourselves to regulations" (2:20), suggests that some pressure was already being felt.

Paul describes the teaching by its features rather than by name. It came as "philosophy and empty deceit, according to the tradition of men" (2:8). It judged believers over "food or in drink, or regarding a festival or a new moon or sabbaths" (2:16). It took delight in "false humility and worship of angels," and its teacher was "intruding into those things which he has not seen" (2:18). It imposed rules such as "Do not touch, do not taste, do not handle" (2:21) and promoted "neglect of the body" (2:23). Paul also speaks of a circumcision "made without hands" (2:11), though the letter never says the teachers demanded circumcision. The exact system behind these features, and the role it gave to angels or other spiritual beings, remains unknown. This pressure is also different from the old sinful practices Paul tells believers to put to death in 3:5–11. Against all of it, Paul's answer is that "you are complete in Him" (2:10).

Paul sends Tychicus to the church together with Onesimus, "that he may know your circumstances and comfort your hearts" (4:8). Tychicus most likely carried the letter. He would bring Paul's news, and Paul hoped he would return with word of how the church was doing.

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Colossians is a letter about Jesus Christ, and Paul reaches Him almost at once. After his opening thanksgiving and prayer, he describes the Son as “the image of the invisible God,” the One through whom everything was made, “whether thrones or dominions or principalities or powers” (1:15–16). The Son is “the head of the body, the church,” and “the firstborn from the dead” (1:18). Every other subject in the letter rests on this portrait. If Christ is who Paul says He is, then no power in heaven or on earth can rival Him, and those who belong to Him are not missing anything that matters.

From that center Paul keeps returning to what believers already have in Christ. They have “redemption through His blood, the forgiveness of sins” (1:14). Christ has reconciled them “in the body of His flesh through death” (1:22). They have received fullness in Him (2:10). They were circumcised “with the circumcision made without hands” and were buried and raised with Him (2:11–12). God made them alive, forgave all their trespasses, cancelled the record against them by nailing it to the cross, and disarmed the hostile powers (2:13–15). Paul strengthens the church by pointing to what God has already given. He does not suggest that something is still missing.

Chapter 2 confronts the teaching described above under Occasion. Paul grants that its rules have “an appearance of wisdom,” yet he says they “are of no value against the indulgence of the flesh” (2:23). He traces the trouble to its root: “not holding fast to the Head” (2:19). Paul does not offer a rival system of his own. He brings the church back to Christ. He also keeps this pressure separate from the old sins he later tells the Colossians to put to death (3:5–11). The false teaching was a set of religious regulations competing with Christ. The sins of chapter 3 belong to the old life that Christ’s people leave behind.

The second half of the letter describes the life that grows out of belonging to Christ. Believers seek “those things which are above” (3:1), put to death and put off their old ways, and put on the new man, where “Christ is all and in all” (3:11). They clothe themselves with tender mercies, kindness, and humility. They forgive one another as Christ forgave them, and above all they put on love, “the bond of perfection” (3:14). The peace of God rules their hearts, and the word of Christ lives richly among them (3:15–16). This same life reaches into the household, where wives and husbands, children and fathers, enslaved people and masters all answer to the Lord (3:18–4:1). It then reaches outward into prayer and gracious speech toward outsiders (4:2–6).

Paul’s own ministry runs through the letter. He serves as a steward of “the mystery which has been hidden from ages and from generations, but now has been revealed to His saints” (1:26). He rejoices in sufferings he bears “for the sake of His body, which is the church” (1:24). He labors to “present every man perfect in Christ Jesus” (1:28), and he struggles in prayer for believers who “have not seen my face in the flesh” (2:1). The apostle who writes these exalted words about Christ is a prisoner who cares deeply for churches that may never have met him.

The letter ends among real people: Tychicus and Onesimus carrying news, Epaphras laboring in prayer, Aristarchus sharing Paul’s imprisonment, and Mark, Justus, Luke, Demas, Nymphas, and Archippus. The Christ who holds all things together is Lord over these named believers and their ordinary work. All the way through, Paul’s concern is that the church will keep going as it began, “if indeed you continue in the faith” (1:23).

Why This Book Matters

Colossians gives one of the New Testament’s fullest statements of the Son’s relationship to creation. All things were created “by Him,” and “All things were created through Him and for Him” (1:16). Paul adds that “in Him all things consist” (1:17). In other words, the Son holds the universe together, and creation exists for His sake. Every visible and invisible power, however exalted, owes its existence to Him. Colossians ties the beginning of creation, its continuing order, and its final goal to the Son.

The letter also answers a longing that has never gone away: the feeling that something more is needed beyond Christ, whether special spiritual experiences, stricter rules, or required observances. Paul’s answer is that believers are already “complete in Him” (2:10). Colossians shows that God’s provision in Christ is sufficient. Anyone who has Christ is not waiting for another teacher or practice to make up what is missing.

Colossians is also one of the clearest places in Scripture to see how grace produces holiness. The same letter that rejects “Do not touch, do not taste, do not handle” (2:21) also commands, “put to death your members which are on the earth” (3:5). The difference lies in where each command comes from. Paul grounds freedom from human regulations in having died with Christ (2:20). He grounds the call to put off sin and put on new life in having been raised with Him (3:1). Holiness grows out of union with Christ. It does not come from rule-keeping. This guards readers against two opposite errors: thinking that rules can make a person holy, and treating sin carelessly.

Throughout the letter, God acts first. The Father “has qualified us,” and He “has delivered us from the power of darkness” (1:12–13). God “willed to make known” His mystery (1:27), and He “made alive” those who were dead in their trespasses (2:13). Colossians shows the goodness of God taking the initiative. Salvation begins with what God has done, and the believer’s response follows.

Finally, Colossians brings Christ’s lordship into ordinary relationships. Enslaved people are told to work “as to the Lord and not to men” (3:23), and masters are reminded, “you also have a Master in heaven” (4:1). The Lord who rules every power in heaven also governs daily work in the household and the way those with authority treat those under it.

Book Flow

I. Thanksgiving and Prayer for a Fruitful Gospel Life (1:1–1:14)

Paul and Timothy give thanks for the Colossians’ faith, love, and hope. The gospel they learned from Epaphras is “bringing forth fruit” among them, as it is in all the world. Paul prays that they will be “filled with the knowledge of His will” and will “walk worthy of the Lord.” The prayer ends with the Father rescuing His people into the kingdom of His Son, in whom they have redemption (1:13–14). That mention of the Son leads straight into the next movement, so the division at 1:15 marks a change of emphasis rather than a break.

II. The Preeminent Christ and Paul’s Ministry of His Mystery (1:15–2:5)

Paul describes the Son’s supremacy in creation (1:15–17), in the church as its head (1:18), and in reconciliation, “having made peace through the blood of His cross” (1:20). He then turns this truth toward the Colossians. They were once enemies of God, and now they are reconciled, provided they continue in the faith (1:21–23). Next Paul explains his own calling to make known the mystery now revealed among the Gentiles, a ministry in which he rejoices to suffer for the sake of Christ’s body, the church (1:24–29). The movement closes with his struggle for believers who have never seen him. He reminds them that in Christ “are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge,” and he says this “lest anyone should deceive you with persuasive words” (2:3–4).

III. Remain Rooted in Christ and Reject Human Regulations (2:6–2:23)

The letter turns on 2:6–7: “As you therefore have received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk in Him.” This appeal is the hinge of Colossians. It joins everything Paul has said about Christ to the warnings and commands that follow. Paul warns against teaching that is “not according to Christ” (2:8). In 2:9–10 he sets out the heart of his answer: all the fullness of deity dwells in Christ, and believers are complete in Him. He recounts what God has done for them in Christ (2:11–15). He tells them to let no one judge them over food or festivals (2:16–19). He then asks why, if they “died with Christ,” they would submit to human regulations again (2:20–23).

IV. Live the New Life Under Christ’s Lordship (3:1–4:6)

“If then you were raised with Christ” (3:1) answers the “if you died with Christ” of 2:20. Because they died and rose with Christ, believers put to death and put off their old ways and put on the new humanity, where ethnic and social divisions give way to Christ. The peace of God and the word of Christ govern their life together (3:15–16). The household instructions follow as one unit, addressed in pairs to wives and husbands, children and fathers, and enslaved people and masters (3:18–4:1). The movement ends with prayer for “a door for the word,” a mention of Paul’s chains, and a call to walk “in wisdom toward those who are outside,” with speech “seasoned with salt” (4:2–6).

V. Messengers, Greetings, and Final Instructions (4:7–4:18)

Paul sends Tychicus with Onesimus, “who is one of you,” to bring news and comfort (4:7–9). Greetings come from Aristarchus, “my fellow prisoner,” and from Mark and Justus. Epaphras sends greetings too. He is “always laboring fervently for you in prayers,” asking that they “stand perfect and complete in all the will of God.” Luke and Demas also send greetings (4:10–14). Paul greets the believers in Laodicea and the church that meets in the house of Nymphas. He asks that the letters be exchanged and charges Archippus to fulfill his ministry (4:15–17). He closes in his own hand: “Remember my chains. Grace be with you.” (4:18).

Key Themes to Watch For

Christ’s preeminence and fullness (1:15–19; 2:9–10; 3:11). The Son holds first place in creation, in the church, and in the new humanity, and all the fullness of deity dwells in Him.

In Christ and with Christ (1:27; 2:6–13; 2:20; 3:1–4). Believers share in Christ’s death and resurrection, and His presence among them is their hope of glory.

Peace and victory through the cross (1:20–22; 2:13–15; 3:15). Christ’s death reconciles believers to God, defeats the hostile powers, and brings peace to the one body.

The mystery now revealed (1:26–27; 2:2–3; 4:3–4). God’s purpose, once hidden, is now made known in Christ, and Paul asks for prayer so that he can make it clear.

Head and body (1:18; 1:24; 2:19; 3:15). Christ rules the church and supplies its growth, and its members are called to peace as one body.

True wisdom and the mere appearance of wisdom (1:9–10; 1:28; 2:3; 2:8; 2:23; 3:10; 3:16; 4:5). Real wisdom and knowledge are found in Christ, while human tradition only looks wise.

Thanksgiving (1:3; 1:12; 2:7; 3:15; 3:17; 4:2). Thanks to God appear in every chapter as the natural response of people who have received so much in Christ.

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

Firstborn (1:15, 18). When Paul calls the Son “the firstborn over all creation,” he means first in rank and authority. He does not mean that the Son was the first thing God created. The very next verse says that all things were created “by Him,” which places the Son on the Creator’s side of the line. “The firstborn from the dead” (1:18) points to His supremacy as the risen One, “that in all things He may have the preeminence.”

Fullness and “the Godhead bodily” (1:19; 2:9–10). “Fullness” here means the complete divine nature, and “Godhead” is an older English word for deity. When Paul says that this fullness dwells in Christ “bodily,” he means that God in His completeness is present in Christ, who has a real human body. Believers in turn receive fullness in Him (2:10), so they do not need to look anywhere else for what only God can supply.

Principalities and powers (1:13, 16; 2:10, 15). These words name ruling powers. The list in 1:16 includes them among things “visible and invisible.” Colossians says they were created through Christ, that He is “the head of all principality and power,” and that at the cross He “disarmed principalities and powers” and “made a public spectacle of them.” Their defeat and subjection to Christ is different from the forgiveness and reconciliation that believers receive. When 1:20 speaks of God reconciling “all things” through Christ, it describes peace made on a cosmic scale. The verse should not be pressed to settle the fate of every creature.

“The basic principles of the world” (2:8, 20). Paul sets this phrase against Christ and links it with human tradition and regulations. Its exact meaning is debated. It may refer to elementary religious teachings and rules, or to forces associated with the present world order. Paul’s point holds either way: believers who “died with Christ from the basic principles of the world” are no longer bound to them.

Mystery (1:26–27; 2:2; 4:3). In Colossians a mystery is God’s purpose, hidden in past ages and now openly revealed. It includes Christ’s presence among Gentile believers. It is not secret knowledge reserved for a select few. Paul preaches it to “every man” (1:28) and asks for prayer so that he “may make it manifest” (4:4).

Shadow and substance (2:16–17). Paul calls food and drink rules and the observance of festivals, new moons, and sabbaths “a shadow of things to come, but the substance is of Christ.” A shadow points to something real, and Christ is the reality these observances pointed to. On that basis Paul forbids anyone to judge believers over them. Wider questions about the Sabbath need their own treatment from the rest of Scripture.

“False humility and worship of angels” (2:18). Readers debate whether this phrase means worship offered to angels or a claim to join in the worship that angels offer. Colossians does not explain what role the teaching gave to angels or other beings thought to stand between God and people, so that question is best left open. Helpful Notes explains a difference among manuscripts later in this verse.

Circumcision without hands and baptism (2:11–12). Paul says believers were circumcised “with the circumcision made without hands,” were “buried with Him in baptism,” and were “raised with Him through faith in the working of God.” Salvation is by grace through faith alone. Paul does not spell out how baptism relates to that inward work. His words are best understood this way: water baptism is obedient faith that confesses Jesus as Lord and outwardly acts out burial and resurrection with Him, and in that obedience God works inwardly by grace, confirming to believers the reality of their new life in Christ. The saving power belongs to Christ and to the working of God, received through faith. Paul does not say exactly when or how God does this inward work. His comparison also does not require matching every detail of circumcision to baptism.

The old man and the new man (3:9–10). The “old man” is the former humanity, the person and the people believers were apart from Christ. The “new man” is the new humanity they now belong to, which “is renewed in knowledge.” The idea is shared as well as personal, because in this new humanity “there is neither Greek nor Jew, circumcised nor uncircumcised” (3:11).

Greek, Jew, barbarian, Scythian, slave, free (3:11). These names cover ethnic, cultural, and social lines that divided people in Paul’s world. Paul’s point is that none of these divisions decides a person’s place among Christ’s people.

Bondservants (3:22; 4:1). In the household instructions, “bondservants” refers to enslaved people. Paul speaks to them directly as members of the church, with their own dignity before the Lord. The NKJV uses the same term when Paul calls Epaphras “a bondservant of Christ” (4:12).

The household instructions as one unit (3:18–4:1). The instructions come in pairs: wives and husbands, children and fathers, bondservants and masters. Each side receives duties, and each is placed under the Lord, “as is fitting in the Lord,” “as to the Lord,” and under “a Master in heaven.” The chapter break at 4:1 falls in the middle of the last pair, so the command to masters belongs with the verses before it.

“The handwriting of requirements” (2:14). Paul pictures a written record “that was against us,” which God “wiped out” and “nailed it to the cross.” Readers debate exactly what this record was, but Paul’s point is plain: whatever stood against believers has been cancelled through Christ’s death.

“Of the circumcision” (4:11). When Paul says that Aristarchus, Mark, and Justus are his only fellow workers “who are of the circumcision,” he means they are the only Jewish believers among the coworkers with him.

Christ and Life

Colossians follows God's presence from the Son to His people. The Father was pleased that "in Him all the fullness should dwell" (1:19), and Paul repeats the point even more sharply: "in Him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily" (2:9). Everything God is lives in Jesus Christ. Yet the letter does not leave that fullness at a distance. The mystery God once kept hidden is now made known "among the Gentiles: which is Christ in you, the hope of glory" (1:27). People who were once "alienated and enemies" (1:21) now have the Son Himself living among them.

Paul's words hold together two sides of that presence. "Christ in you" describes Christ present among His people as a gathered church, Gentiles included. It also describes the personal reality of His presence with each believer. His presence carries a promise as well: the Christ who lives among them now is their hope of the glory still to come. That is why Paul can tell the church, "Let the word of Christ dwell in you richly" (3:16). The God who placed His fullness in His Son wants to live with His people through that Son.

Because Christ holds that fullness, believers are "complete in Him" (2:10). Paul shows how that completeness works with the picture of a head and its body. Christ is "the head of the body, the church" (1:18), and He is more than its ruler. He is the Head "from whom all the body, nourished and knit together by joints and ligaments, grows with the increase that is from God" (2:19). The church's life flows from Christ, and its growth is God's own work. That is why the danger Paul names in chapter 2 is "not holding fast to the Head." Believers do not receive more of God by adding something to Christ. They receive what they need by staying joined to Him.

Colossians also places the believer's whole life in Christ. Believers died with Christ (2:20) and were raised with Him (2:12; 3:1), and Paul draws the conclusion plainly: "your life is hidden with Christ in God" (3:3). That life is real now, though its glory cannot yet be seen. Christ's resurrection is the ground of both the present and the future: "When Christ who is our life appears, then you also will appear with Him in glory" (3:4). The Christian life is shared life with the risen Son, and it moves toward the day of His appearing.

The peace Christ made at the cross becomes the pattern for how His people live together. He reconciled those who had been enemies "in the body of His flesh through death, to present you holy, and blameless, and above reproach in His sight" (1:22). God made them alive with Christ, "having forgiven you all trespasses," and wiped out the record that stood against them, "having nailed it to the cross" (2:13–14). At the same cross He disarmed the hostile "principalities and powers" (2:15) and brought them under Christ's rule. That defeat of the powers is a different thing from the forgiveness and reconciliation believers receive. The forgiveness itself does not stay between the believer and God. Paul tells the church to forgive one another, "even as Christ forgave you, so you also must do" (3:13), and to "let the peace of God rule in your hearts, to which also you were called in one body" (3:15). People who have been forgiven become people who forgive, and the peace God made through His Son is meant to govern the life of the church.

Christ is also the pattern for His people's renewal. He is "the image of the invisible God" (1:15), and the new man is "renewed in knowledge according to the image of Him who created him" (3:10). In that renewed humanity "there is neither Greek nor Jew, circumcised nor uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave nor free, but Christ is all and in all" (3:11). Colossians names one person whose life shows this. Onesimus travels with Tychicus as "a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you" (4:9). Read alongside Philemon 10–16, he is the same Onesimus whom Paul asks Philemon to receive as a beloved brother. Shared life in Christ changes how believers receive one another, even across the lines their world kept firmly in place.

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Let Paul describe the teaching he answers. Colossians never names the teachers or gives their system a title. Paul describes what they pressed on the church and answers each point with Christ. Labels such as "the Colossian heresy" can make the teaching sound like a known, organized movement. Trying to reconstruct it in detail can also pull attention away from where Paul puts it: on what believers already have in Christ.

Read the commands in light of what God has done. Paul's instructions keep returning to what is already true: "As you therefore have received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk in Him" (2:6); "If then you were raised with Christ" (3:1); "Therefore, as the elect of God, holy and beloved, put on" (3:12). The instructions in chapters 3 and 4 are not a new set of regulations for earning a place with God, which is the very thing chapter 2 rejects. They describe how people who already belong to Christ now live.

Hold completeness and growth together. Believers are already "complete in Him" (2:10). Yet Paul prays for them to keep "increasing in the knowledge of God" (1:10), labors to "present every man perfect in Christ Jesus" (1:28), and reports Epaphras's prayer that they "stand perfect and complete in all the will of God" (4:12). Being complete in Christ does not end growth. It is the ground growth comes from, just as the body grows from its Head (2:19).

Keep the "if" beside the promise. Christ reconciled the Colossians to present them blameless before God (1:22), "if indeed you continue in the faith, grounded and steadfast" (1:23). Paul means the condition seriously, and it does not cancel what Christ has done. God uses real warnings like this one to keep His people steadfast. Paul holds the promise and the condition together without answering every question later readers bring to the verse.

Remember that the letter was read to a church. Paul wanted it "read among you" and in the church of the Laodiceans (4:16). Its promises and commands concern believers' shared life as one body (3:15–16), even as they speak to each believer personally.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

"What is lacking in the afflictions of Christ" (1:24). Paul says he rejoices in his sufferings and fills up "what is lacking in the afflictions of Christ, for the sake of His body, which is the church." The phrase does not mean that Christ's death left something unfinished. The same letter says Christ has already reconciled believers "through death" (1:22) and nailed the record against them to the cross (2:14). Paul is speaking of the sufferings he bears for Christ's body as he carries out his ministry to the church.

"Which he has not seen" (2:18). The NKJV describes the person Paul warns against as "intruding into those things which he has not seen." Some translations leave out the word "not" and describe someone dwelling on things they claim to have seen, such as visions. The difference comes from differences in the ancient manuscripts behind the translations. Paul's concern is the same in either reading: this person is "vainly puffed up by his fleshly mind" and is "not holding fast to the Head" (2:18–19).

Nymphas and "his house" (4:15). The NKJV reads "Nymphas and the church that is in his house." The ancient manuscripts differ here, so some translations give a woman's name and read "her house." In every reading, the verse shows a church gathering in a believer's home.

The epistle from Laodicea (4:16). Paul tells the Colossians to make sure their letter is read in the church of the Laodiceans, and also to read "the epistle from Laodicea." Colossians does not say what that letter was, and its identity remains unknown. The instruction does show that the churches shared apostolic letters and read them together.
