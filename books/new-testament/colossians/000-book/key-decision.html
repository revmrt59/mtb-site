Book Questions

What was the false teaching Paul warns against in Colossae? (Colossians 2:8–23)

Paul never names the teaching or its teachers. He describes it by its features: “philosophy and empty deceit, according to the tradition of men” (2:8); judgments about food, drink, festivals, new moons, and sabbaths (2:16); “false humility and worship of angels” and claims about visions (2:18); and harsh rules like “Do not touch, do not taste, do not handle” (2:21).

Scholars have proposed several reconstructions, including a mix of Jewish practice and early Gnostic ideas. MTB treats all of these as proposals. The exact system cannot be recovered from the letter.

What is clear is Paul’s answer. Whatever drew believers away from Christ was the problem, because “in Him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily; and you are complete in Him” (2:9–10). MTB treats those verses as the center of the letter.

Colossians 1 · 4 questions

Does “firstborn over all creation” mean the Son was created? (v. 15)

No. “He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn over all creation” (v. 15). “Firstborn” here speaks of rank and supremacy, the position of the heir, not of being created first. Paul explains it in the next verse: “For by Him all things were created... All things were created through Him and for Him” (v. 16). The Son is the Creator of all things, not one of the things created.

Paul uses the same word in verse 18, “the firstborn from the dead,” to show Christ’s supremacy in resurrection and in the church.

Read: Colossians 1:15–19, Psalm 89:27, John 1:1–3, Hebrews 1:2–3

Does “to reconcile all things” mean everyone will be saved? (v. 20)

God was pleased “by Him to reconcile all things to Himself... having made peace through the blood of His cross” (v. 20). The cross brings peace on the scale of all creation.

That peace does not work the same way for everyone. Believers are reconciled to God and forgiven through Christ’s death (vv. 21–22). Hostile spiritual powers are not forgiven. Read with 2:15, they are disarmed, defeated, and brought under Christ’s rule. Defeating the powers is not the same as redeeming them. Verse 20 does not teach universal salvation.

Read: Colossians 1:19–22, Colossians 2:15, Philippians 2:10–11 · Related: Does “if indeed you continue in the faith” mean a believer can lose salvation?

Does “if indeed you continue in the faith” mean a believer can lose salvation? (v. 23)

Paul says Christ will present believers “holy, and blameless, and above reproach in His sight” (v. 22), “if indeed you continue in the faith, grounded and steadfast, and are not moved away from the hope of the gospel which you heard” (v. 23). The condition is real. Continuing in the faith is the mark of those Christ will present.

Who they are. Paul is writing to the professing believers in Colossae, “the saints and faithful brethren in Christ” (v. 2). They “once were alienated and enemies,” and now Christ has reconciled them (v. 21).

What they received. They heard “the word of the truth of the gospel” and the hope “laid up for you in heaven” (v. 5). They “have received Christ Jesus the Lord” (2:6).

What turning away looks like. It means being “moved away from the hope of the gospel” (v. 23). In Colossae the pull came from teaching that led believers away from Christ toward human tradition and rules (2:8, 18–19). Paul is not describing a believer who struggles with sin or doubt while still holding to Christ. He is warning against leaving the hope of the gospel itself.

What consequence God warns of, and what assurance He gives. Paul holds out no promise of being presented holy and blameless to those who abandon the hope of the gospel. Beside that warning stands what Christ has already done: He has reconciled them “in the body of His flesh through death” (v. 22), and God keeps His people (John 10:27–29; Philippians 1:6). God uses real warnings to sustain real endurance. The warning wakes up the drifting, and the promises comfort the fearful. Christians disagree on the larger question of whether a true believer can fall away, and this verse alone does not settle it.

The gospel “preached to every creature under heaven” (v. 23) describes how far it had spread, not a count of every person who had heard.

Read: Colossians 1:1–6, Colossians 1:21–23, Colossians 2:6–8, Colossians 2:18–19, John 10:27–29, Philippians 1:6, Hebrews 3:12–14

What is “lacking in the afflictions of Christ”? (v. 24)

Paul says, “I... fill up in my flesh what is lacking in the afflictions of Christ, for the sake of His body, which is the church” (v. 24). This cannot mean Christ’s death was not enough to save. His atoning work is complete (1:20–22; 2:13–14).

Paul is speaking of his ministry. He suffers for the church as he makes God’s word known and works to “present every man perfect in Christ Jesus” (v. 28). Exactly what “remains” to be suffered is debated. Some see a measure of suffering appointed for Christ’s people; others see the sufferings the risen Christ shares with His body.

Read: Colossians 1:24–29, Acts 9:4–5, 2 Corinthians 1:5–7

Colossians 2 · 5 questions

What does Colossians 2 teach about baptism? (vv. 11–12)

Believers were “circumcised with the circumcision made without hands... buried with Him in baptism, in which you also were raised with Him through faith in the working of God, who raised Him from the dead” (vv. 11–12).

MTB holds that salvation is by grace through faith alone. Water baptism is obedient faith confessing Jesus as Lord and identifying with Him in His death, burial, and resurrection. In that obedience God meets believers and works inwardly by grace, confirming to them the reality of their new life in Christ. Paul sets baptism beside “the circumcision made without hands” (v. 11). That circumcision is God’s work of grace, “putting off the body of the sins of the flesh” (v. 11) and cutting away the power the flesh held over us.

Paul’s words do not spell out all of this; it is MTB’s explanation of them. MTB does not take a position on exactly when or how God does that inward work. Baptism has no saving power of its own apart from Christ or apart from faith. The saving power is in “the working of God” (v. 12). Christians hold other views, from baptism as God’s means of new birth to baptism as a sign only.

Read: Colossians 2:11–13, Romans 6:3–4, Galatians 3:26–27

What is “the handwriting of requirements” nailed to the cross? (v. 14)

God forgave all our trespasses, “having wiped out the handwriting of requirements that was against us... having nailed it to the cross” (v. 14). MTB takes the “handwriting” most likely as the written record of debt that condemned us, like an IOU we could never pay. How directly it refers to the commands of the law of Moses is debated.

The same cross also defeats the hostile powers: “Having disarmed principalities and powers, He made a public spectacle of them” (v. 15). Forgiveness of our guilt and victory over the powers belong together, but they are not the same thing.

Read: Colossians 2:13–15, Ephesians 2:14–16 · Related: Does “to reconcile all things” mean everyone will be saved?

Does verse 16 mean Christians no longer keep a Sabbath? (vv. 16–17)

“So let no one judge you in food or in drink, or regarding a festival or a new moon or sabbaths, which are a shadow of things to come, but the substance is of Christ” (vv. 16–17). Paul forbids anyone to judge believers over these observances, because what they pointed to has come in Christ.

The passage is about freedom from judgment, and conclusions about a weekly Sabbath belong to the wider teaching of Scripture. MTB holds the Sabbath as part of God’s moral law, the fourth commandment. Believers do not keep it to be saved, and a weekly rhythm of ceasing from ordinary work to worship and rest in Christ remains wise and beneficial for His people. Which day is set apart is a matter of freedom, and verse 16 is exactly why believers must not judge one another over it (Romans 14:5–6).

Read: Colossians 2:16–17, Exodus 20:8–11, Mark 2:27–28, Romans 14:5–6, Hebrews 4:9–10

Does verse 18 say “has not seen” or “has seen”? (v. 18)

The NKJV warns against someone “intruding into those things which he has not seen” (v. 18). Many manuscripts and modern translations leave out “not,” so the person is dwelling on visions he has seen, or claims to have seen.

The difference changes how the false teacher’s visions are described, but not Paul’s main charge. Whatever the person claims, he is “not holding fast to the Head” (v. 19), the Christ from whom the whole body grows. The passage does not give enough to rebuild the teachers’ views on angels, or to judge every claimed vision.

Read: Colossians 2:18–19 · Related: What was the false teaching Paul warns against in Colossae?

Are “Do not touch, do not taste, do not handle” commands for Christians? (vv. 20–23)

No. Paul is quoting the false teachers’ rules in order to reject them (vv. 20–21). These human regulations “have an appearance of wisdom” but are “of no value against the indulgence of the flesh” (v. 23).

That does not make self-discipline wrong. Paul goes straight on to command believers to “put to death” sinful practices (3:5). The difference is the source. Human rules promise spiritual power they cannot give. Real holiness flows from having died and been raised with Christ (2:20; 3:1).

Read: Colossians 2:20–23, Colossians 3:1–5

Colossians 3 · 3 questions

Does “Christ is all and in all” erase every difference between people? (v. 11)

In the new humanity “there is neither Greek nor Jew, circumcised nor uncircumcised, barbarian, Scythian, slave nor free, but Christ is all and in all” (v. 11). No ethnic, cultural, religious, or social status gives anyone a higher standing in Christ’s people. It means our basis of unity is not external but internal by the Spirit received by faith of Christ.

Paul is describing the renewed community, not denying that people still live in different circumstances. The household instructions that follow still speak to wives, husbands, children, fathers, servants, and masters (3:18–4:1).

Read: Colossians 3:9–11, Galatians 3:28 · Related: What do the household instructions require, and do they allow abuse?

Is “Let the peace of God rule in your hearts” a test for guidance? (v. 15)

“And let the peace of God rule in your hearts, to which also you were called in one body” (v. 15). In context Paul is speaking about peace among believers in one body. It sits beside forgiving one another, love, thankfulness, and the word of Christ dwelling richly among them (vv. 12–16).

Personal peace has a place within that shared life and the Spirit certainly can and will guide us with inner peace; but the verse is not mainly about an inner feeling for making decisions, and inward calm is not by itself a test of God’s guidance.

Read: Colossians 3:12–17

What do the household instructions require, and do they allow abuse? (3:18–4:1)

Paul speaks to each member of the household in relation to the Lord: wives submit “as is fitting in the Lord” (v. 18), husbands “love your wives and do not be bitter toward them” (v. 19), children obey, fathers do not provoke, bondservants serve “fearing God” (v. 22), and masters give “what is just and fair, knowing that you also have a Master in heaven” (4:1). MTB reads 3:18–4:1 as one unit under Christ’s lordship, and the chapter break does not separate the masters’ duty from the servants’.

These commands are never permission for coercion, abuse, or wrongdoing. “Bondservants” were enslaved people in the Roman world, and Paul addresses that setting without endorsing it. Applying these verses to modern employment requires care, because a job is not enslavement.

Read: Colossians 3:18–4:1, Ephesians 5:21–6:9, Philemon 1:15–16 · Related: Does “Christ is all and in all” erase every difference between people?, Is Onesimus the runaway slave of Philemon?

Colossians 4 · 1 question

Is Onesimus the runaway slave of Philemon? (v. 9)

Paul sends Tychicus “with Onesimus, a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you” (v. 9). MTB identifies him with the Onesimus of Philemon 10–16, whom Paul asks Philemon to receive no longer as a slave but as “a beloved brother.”

He is often called a runaway, but that is inferred; Scripture does not tell us exactly how he came to be with Paul. What Paul says is that he is a faithful and beloved brother. Here the unity of 3:11 takes a name and a face.

Read: Colossians 4:7–9, Philemon 1:10–16 · Related: What do the household instructions require, and do they allow abuse?
