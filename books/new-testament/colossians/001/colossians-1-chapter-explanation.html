Colossians 1

Verse-by-Verse
Colossians 1:1
Colossians 1:1
NKJV

Colossians 1:1 Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God, and Timothy our brother,(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 1:1 This letter is from Paul, chosen by the will of God to be an apostle of Christ Jesus, and from our brother Timothy. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul opens by calling himself "an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God," so his authority to write rests on God's appointment, not on his own initiative. He names "Timothy our brother" beside him as a fellow believer who shares in the greeting.

Verse Explanation

Paul opens by calling himself "an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God," so his authority to write rests on God's appointment, not on his own initiative. He names "Timothy our brother" beside him as a fellow believer who shares in the greeting.

Paul opens the letter with his own name. He is the same man who says later in this chapter, "I, Paul, became a minister" of the gospel (1:23), and who signs the closing greeting "by my own hand" (4:18). From its first word to its last, Colossians comes to the church as a personal letter from a man they could name.

an apostle of Jesus Christ describes the role in which Paul writes. An apostle of Jesus Christ belongs to Christ and is sent to speak for Him, so what follows comes with Christ's authority behind it. Paul is not offering the Colossians private advice. Later in the chapter he describes his calling as "the stewardship from God which was given to me for you" (1:25), a trust God placed in his hands for the good of churches like this one.

by the will of God tells where that role came from. Paul did not appoint himself or earn the title; God willed it. This matters for a church that first heard the gospel from Epaphras (1:7) and that may never have seen Paul's face (2:1). The Colossians can receive his words as a message from someone God Himself appointed, even though Paul did not found their church.

and Timothy our brother names the companion who stands with Paul as he writes. Paul gives Timothy no official title here. He calls him "our brother," a fellow believer, and that family language carries straight into the next verse, where the Colossians themselves are greeted as "faithful brethren." Because Timothy shares in the greeting, the thanksgiving and prayer that follow speak as "we" (1:3, 9). Paul still writes as the apostle, and the authority claimed in this verse belongs to him.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the opening as "chosen by the will of God to be an apostle of Christ Jesus." This rendering spells out what the shorter NKJV wording implies: "by the will of God" describes how Paul came to be an apostle.

Colossians 1:2
Colossians 1:2
NKJV

Colossians 1:2 To the saints and faithful brethren in Christ who are in Colosse: Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 1:2 We are writing to God’s holy people in the city of Colosse, who are faithful brothers and sisters in Christ. May God our Father give you grace and peace. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul addresses the church as "saints and faithful brethren in Christ," calling them God's holy people and His faithful family before he raises any concern. He greets them with grace and peace from "God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ," naming the Father and the Son together as the source of these blessings.

Verse Explanation

Paul addresses the church as "saints and faithful brethren in Christ," calling them God's holy people and His faithful family before he raises any concern. He greets them with grace and peace from "God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ," naming the Father and the Son together as the source of these blessings.

To the saints names the Colossians by what God has made them. Paul uses the word for the whole congregation, not for a few believers of unusual achievement. Saints are God's holy people, set apart to belong to Him. The church's holiness begins with God claiming them as His own.

and faithful brethren adds the language of family. "Brethren" is an older English plural of "brother." Here it takes in the whole church as members of one household of faith. "Faithful" describes them as believers who are holding to the faith they received. Paul says all this before he raises any concern. His later warning, "lest anyone should deceive you with persuasive words" (2:4), is addressed to a church he has already greeted as holy and faithful.

in Christ tells why they are holy and faithful: they belong to Christ and share His life. Their standing rests in Him rather than in anything they add to Him. This short phrase prepares for one of the letter's central claims, that believers are "complete in Him" (2:10).

who are in Colosse gives the church's earthly address. The same believers who are "in Christ" also live in a real city among ordinary neighbors, and their life in Christ is lived out there. Colosse was not isolated, either. The letter later names believers in nearby Laodicea and Hierapolis (4:13) and asks that this letter be read in the church of the Laodiceans as well (4:16).

Grace to you and peace is Paul's greeting and his prayerful wish for the church. Grace is God's undeserved kindness, the favor He gives freely. Peace is the well-being that comes from being at peace with God. Both words carry weight in this letter. Paul soon declares that Christ made peace "through the blood of His cross" (1:20), and he later urges the church, "let the peace of God rule in your hearts" (3:15).

from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ names the source of grace and peace. Paul places the Father and the Son side by side as the One from whom these blessings come. "Our Father" continues the family language of "brethren": the God who gives grace to this church is their Father. Calling Jesus "the Lord" acknowledges His authority, and Paul unfolds that authority within a few verses when he declares that "in all things He may have the preeminence" (1:18).

Study Notes

Translations differ at the end of this verse. The NLT reads "May God our Father give you grace and peace" and does not include the words "and the Lord Jesus Christ," which appear in the NKJV. In either wording, the very next verse gives thanks to "the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1:3), so the letter's opening lines still join the Father to the Lord Jesus Christ.

Grace frames the whole letter. Paul opens with "Grace to you" here and closes in his own hand with "Grace be with you" (4:18).

Colossians 1:3–8 — Thanksgiving for the Gospel's Fruit Among the Colossians

NLT

Colossians 1:3 We always pray for you, and we give thanks to God, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul and Timothy give thanks to "the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ" whenever they pray for the Colossians. Their prayer for this church begins with gratitude for what God has already done among them.

Verse Explanation

Paul and Timothy give thanks to "the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ" whenever they pray for the Colossians. Their prayer for this church begins with gratitude for what God has already done among them.

We give thanks to the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ is spoken by Paul and Timothy together, since Paul named Timothy beside him in the greeting. Before they ask anything for the Colossians, they thank God for them. Paul identifies the God he thanks by His relationship to Jesus: He is the Father of the One the Colossians confess as Lord. The thanks goes to the same Father who, as verses 12–14 will say, rescued His people and brought them into His Son's kingdom.

praying always for you shows that this gratitude belongs to a steady habit of prayer. Whenever Paul and Timothy pray for this church, thanks comes first, and their actual requests wait until verse 9. By starting this way, Paul lets the Colossians know that he already sees God at work among them. The rest of the sentence explains what he has heard.

Study Notes

In the NKJV, verses 3–8 form one long sentence. It begins with thanks to God in verse 3 and does not reach a full stop until Epaphras's report in verse 8. The faith, love, hope, gospel, and messenger described along the way all belong to the same thanksgiving. Verse 9 then opens a new sentence with "For this reason."

Main Idea

The thanksgiving was prompted by news of the Colossians' "faith in Christ Jesus" and their "love for all the saints." Their trust in Christ showed itself in a love that reached every believer, not only those close to them.

Verse Explanation

The thanksgiving was prompted by news of the Colossians' "faith in Christ Jesus" and their "love for all the saints." Their trust in Christ showed itself in a love that reached every believer, not only those close to them.

since we heard of your faith in Christ Jesus gives the news that moved Paul and Timothy to thank God. They know about the Colossians' faith from a report; they did not watch it grow. That faith is "in Christ Jesus," meaning the Colossians have put their trust in Him. Their faith rests on a Person. That matters in a letter that will later tell them, "you are complete in Him" (2:10).

and of your love for all the saints names the evidence that this faith is genuine. "Saints" is the same term Paul used for the Colossians themselves in verse 2. It means God's holy people, everyone who belongs to Him. The Colossians' love did not stop with a favored few. It reached "all the saints," and that broad love showed that trusting Christ had changed how they treated His people.

Study Notes

Paul received this news of the Colossians' faith and love through Epaphras, who "declared to us" their love (1:8). Later Paul writes of his struggle for "as many as have not seen my face in the flesh" (2:1). The letter does not say whether that group includes the Colossians, so whether Paul ever visited Colosse remains an open question.

Colossians 1:5
NKJV

Colossians 1:5 because of the hope which is laid up for you in heaven, of which you heard before in the word of the truth of the gospel,(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 1:5 which come from your confident hope of what God has reserved for you in heaven. You have had this expectation ever since you first heard the truth of the Good News. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Colossians' faith and love grow out of "the hope which is laid up for you in heaven," a future God is keeping safe for them. They first heard of this hope in "the word of the truth of the gospel," so their hope rests on a true message from God and not on wishful thinking.

Verse Explanation

The Colossians' faith and love grow out of "the hope which is laid up for you in heaven," a future God is keeping safe for them. They first heard of this hope in "the word of the truth of the gospel," so their hope rests on a true message from God and not on wishful thinking.

because of the hope which is laid up for you in heaven explains where the Colossians' faith and love come from. In this phrase, "hope" means what is hoped for, not just a hopeful feeling. That hope is "laid up," meaning stored and kept, and it is kept "in heaven," beyond the reach of anything on earth. Faith and love grow from that secure future. People who know what God is keeping for them can trust Christ now and spend themselves in love for His people. Later in the letter Paul ties this hope to Christ Himself: "your life is hidden with Christ in God," and "When Christ who is our life appears, then you also will appear with Him in glory" (3:3–4).

of which you heard before in the word of the truth of the gospel reminds the Colossians when they first learned of this hope. They heard it "before," at the beginning, when the gospel first reached them. Verses 6–7 describe that day. What they heard was "the word of the truth of the gospel": the good news about Christ, a message that is true. Their hope stands on something God has spoken, not on guesswork or a teacher's clever ideas. Paul's stress on truth matters in Colossians, because he will soon write "lest anyone should deceive you with persuasive words" (2:4).

Study Notes

The wording "which you heard" comes back in 1:23, where Paul urges the Colossians not to be "moved away from the hope of the gospel which you heard." The hope that supports their faith and love here is the same hope Paul later calls them to hold without drifting.

Colossians 1:6
NKJV

Colossians 1:6 which has come to you, as it has also in all the world, and is bringing forth fruit, as it is also among you since the day you heard and knew the grace of God in truth;(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 1:6 This same Good News that came to you is going out all over the world. It is bearing fruit everywhere by changing lives, just as it changed your lives from the day you first heard and understood the truth about God’s wonderful grace. (NLT)

Main Idea

The gospel that reached Colosse is the same message spreading "in all the world," and it is "bringing forth fruit" among the Colossians just as it does elsewhere. That fruit has been growing since the day they "heard and knew the grace of God in truth," meaning they understood and received God's grace as it truly is.

Verse Explanation

The gospel that reached Colosse is the same message spreading "in all the world," and it is "bringing forth fruit" among the Colossians just as it does elsewhere. That fruit has been growing since the day they "heard and knew the grace of God in truth," meaning they understood and received God's grace as it truly is.

which has come to you makes the gospel itself the subject of the sentence. Paul speaks of the good news as if it were alive: it arrives, it spreads, and it bears fruit. The gospel came to Colosse. The Colossians did not work it out for themselves; they received it.

as it has also in all the world places that local arrival within a much larger movement. The message taught in this one church is the same message spreading across the world. Paul is describing how far the gospel has spread, not counting everyone who has heard it. For the Colossians, this means they hold the one gospel God is using everywhere, not a local or partial version of it.

and is bringing forth fruit, as it is also among you describes what the gospel does wherever it goes. Like a healthy plant, it produces fruit. In Colosse, that fruit is what Paul has just named: faith in Christ Jesus and love for all the saints (1:4). The words "as it is also among you" assure the church that the growth seen elsewhere is happening in their own fellowship too.

since the day you heard and knew the grace of God in truth traces that fruit back to its beginning. The gospel started working on the day it reached them, and the fruit has been growing ever since. "Heard and knew" means more than hearing words: they understood the message and took it to heart. What they came to know was "the grace of God," His kindness given freely to people who had not earned it. They knew it "in truth," as it really is, without distortion. Paul is telling the Colossians that their beginning was sound.

Study Notes

The words "since the day you heard" find an echo in verse 9: "For this reason we also, since the day we heard it, do not cease to pray for you." Two first hearings are set side by side. From the day the gospel reached Colosse, it bore fruit there. From the day news of their faith reached Paul, he and Timothy prayed for them without stopping.

The picture of fruit returns in Paul's prayer, where he asks that the Colossians be "fruitful in every good work and increasing in the knowledge of God" (1:10). The fruit the gospel produced among them in verse 6 is the fruit Paul prays will keep growing in their daily lives.

Colossians 1:7 You learned about the Good News from Epaphras, our beloved co-worker. He is Christ’s faithful servant, and he is helping us on your behalf. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Colossians learned this gospel from Epaphras, whom Paul calls "our dear fellow servant" and "a faithful minister of Christ on your behalf." By commending Epaphras, Paul confirms that the gospel they first received is the true one.

Verse Explanation

The Colossians learned this gospel from Epaphras, whom Paul calls "our dear fellow servant" and "a faithful minister of Christ on your behalf." By commending Epaphras, Paul confirms that the gospel they first received is the true one.

as you also learned from Epaphras names the man who first taught the Colossians the gospel. "Learned" points to real instruction. Epaphras did more than announce the message; he taught it until they understood it. Later Paul urges them to stay "established in the faith, as you have been taught" (2:7). What Epaphras taught them is the foundation Paul wants them to keep.

our dear fellow servant shows how warmly Paul regards Epaphras. He is "dear," loved by Paul and Timothy, and a "fellow servant," someone who serves the same Lord alongside them. Paul counts the Colossians' own teacher as a partner in his work.

who is a faithful minister of Christ on your behalf adds Paul's formal endorsement. Epaphras serves Christ, and he has served faithfully. His ministry has been "on your behalf," for the good of the Colossians. By speaking of Epaphras this way, Paul confirms that the gospel the church received from him is the true gospel. Paul is not correcting their beginning; he is endorsing it.

Study Notes

Epaphras appears again near the end of the letter. Paul calls him "one of you," a man from the Colossians' own city, and "a bondservant of Christ" who is "always laboring fervently for you in prayers" (4:12). Paul adds that Epaphras has "a great zeal for you, and those who are in Laodicea, and those in Hierapolis" (4:13), so his care reached the neighboring churches as well.

Paul describes Tychicus in similar terms, calling him "a beloved brother, faithful minister, and fellow servant in the Lord" (4:7). Both men are called faithful ministers and fellow servants. Paul gives the same respect to the coworkers who carried the gospel and news between him and the churches.

The NLT ends this verse with "he is helping us on your behalf," which pictures Epaphras serving Paul in the Colossians' place. The NKJV reads "a faithful minister of Christ on your behalf," which describes his ministry as service done for the Colossians. In both translations Epaphras is a trusted servant linked to Paul and to the church.

who also declared to us your love in the Spirit completes the picture of Epaphras. He carried the gospel to Colosse, and he carried news of the church back to Paul. This verse is the only place in the NKJV of Colossians where "Spirit" is capitalized. Elsewhere, "spirit" in 2:5 refers to Paul being present with the church in his thoughts, and the adjective "spiritual" describes understanding (1:9) and songs (3:16).

Colossians 1:9–14 — Prayer for a Worthy Walk and Thanks for the Father's Rescue

Colossians 1:9
Colossians 1:9
NKJV

Colossians 1:9 For this reason we also, since the day we heard it, do not cease to pray for you, and to ask that you may be filled with the knowledge of His will in all wisdom and spiritual understanding;(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 1:9 So we have not stopped praying for you since we first heard about you. We ask God to give you complete knowledge of his will and to give you spiritual wisdom and understanding. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because of Epaphras's report, Paul and Timothy have not stopped praying for the Colossians since the day they heard it. They ask that the church be "filled with the knowledge of His will in all wisdom and spiritual understanding," which means knowing God's will together with the God-given insight to see how to live it.

Verse Explanation

Because of Epaphras's report, Paul and Timothy have not stopped praying for the Colossians since the day they heard it. They ask that the church be "filled with the knowledge of His will in all wisdom and spiritual understanding," which means knowing God's will together with the God-given insight to see how to live it.

For this reason we also, points back to the report Paul has just described. Epaphras had told him of the Colossians' "love in the Spirit" (1:8), and that good news led straight into prayer. The "we" includes Timothy, whom Paul named beside himself in the greeting (1:1). A healthy church did not make Paul feel that prayer was less needed. Good news about God's work moved him to ask God for more of it.

since the day we heard it, do not cease to pray for you, describes prayer that has continued without a break since the report arrived. Paul has already said that he and Timothy give thanks while "praying always for you" (1:3). Now he reveals what they have been asking. These are believers Paul came to know through another man's ministry, yet he carries them before God as faithfully as if he had planted the church himself.

and to ask that you may be filled with the knowledge of His will is the heart of the request. To be filled is to be full, with no corner left empty, so Paul wants knowledge of God's will to reach every part of the church's life. "His will" is what God desires and intends for His people. Later in the letter Paul warns against teaching that has only "an appearance of wisdom" (2:23). Before any warning is given, he prays that the church will be full of the real knowledge God gives, so that nothing else can claim to supply what is missing.

in all wisdom and spiritual understanding; describes the kind of knowledge Paul has in mind. Wisdom is the ability to apply God's truth rightly in real situations, and spiritual understanding is insight that comes from God's Spirit rather than from human cleverness. Paul is asking for more than information about what God wants. He wants the Colossians to see clearly how God's will applies to the choices in front of them, and the next verse shows what that knowledge is for.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the request as "complete knowledge of his will" and "spiritual wisdom and understanding." Its word "complete" expresses the same fullness as the NKJV's "filled," and it applies "spiritual" to both wisdom and understanding. The meaning of the request is the same in both translations.

Colossians 1:10
Colossians 1:10
NKJV

Colossians 1:10 that you may walk worthy of the Lord, fully pleasing Him, being fruitful in every good work and increasing in the knowledge of God;(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 1:10 Then the way you live will always honor and please the Lord, and your lives will produce every kind of good fruit. All the while, you will grow as you learn to know God better and better. (NLT)

Main Idea

Knowing God's will has a purpose: that the Colossians may "walk worthy of the Lord, fully pleasing Him." Paul describes that life as fruitful in every good work and "increasing in the knowledge of God," so faithful living and a deeper knowledge of God grow together.

Verse Explanation

Knowing God's will has a purpose: that the Colossians may "walk worthy of the Lord, fully pleasing Him." Paul describes that life as fruitful in every good work and "increasing in the knowledge of God," so faithful living and a deeper knowledge of God grow together.

that you may walk worthy of the Lord, gives the purpose of the knowledge Paul prayed for in verse 9. To "walk" is a common biblical way of describing a person's everyday conduct, the steady direction of a life. A walk "worthy of the Lord" is a life that fits who the Lord is and what He has done. Paul uses the same picture in 2:6: "As you therefore have received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk in Him." Those words point to Christ as the Lord whose worth the Colossians' conduct should reflect.

fully pleasing Him, names the aim of that walk. Paul wants the Colossians to please the Lord in every area of life, not only in the parts others see. Knowing God's will is never an end in itself. It is given so that God's people can live in a way that delights the One they belong to.

being fruitful in every good work begins Paul's description of that worthy life. The gospel was already "bringing forth fruit" among the Colossians (1:6). Now Paul prays that the fruit will show up in actual deeds, and "every good work" takes in the whole range of ordinary goodness that comes from a life under Christ.

and increasing in the knowledge of God; completes the picture. In verse 9 Paul asked that they be filled with the knowledge of God's will. Here the knowledge goes deeper, to knowing God Himself. Knowledge leads to obedience, and the obedient life in turn grows in its knowledge of God, so the two keep growing together.

Study Notes

In the NKJV, the worthy walk is described by a chain of phrases that runs through verse 12: "being fruitful," "increasing," "strengthened" (1:11), and "giving thanks" (1:12). This chain explains why the thanksgiving of verse 12 belongs to Paul's prayer and does not begin a new subject.

Paul's language of knowing in this prayer reaches back to the Colossians' first hearing of the gospel, when they "knew the grace of God in truth" (1:6). The knowledge he asks God to increase began on that day.

Paul prays that they will be "strengthened with all might, according to His glorious power," so the strength for a worthy life comes from God Himself. Verse Explanation

Paul prays that they will be "strengthened with all might, according to His glorious power," so the strength for a worthy life comes from God Himself. God gives that power "for all patience and longsuffering with joy," so that believers can endure hardship and bear with others without losing their joy.

strengthened with all might, continues the description of the worthy walk. Paul does not expect the Colossians to produce that life by willpower. They need to be strengthened, and the source of that strength is God. "All might" means every kind of strength the worthy life will require.

according to His glorious power, gives the measure of that strength. Believers are strengthened in keeping with God's own power, and not just with a little help from Him. His power is "glorious" because it displays who He is. The God whose glory is beyond measure supplies the strength His people need to live for Him.

for all patience and longsuffering with joy; reveals the surprising purpose of this power. Paul does not pray for spectacular displays. He prays for endurance. Patience is the strength to hold up under hard circumstances without giving way. Longsuffering is the strength to bear with difficult people without striking back, as Paul makes clear in 3:12–13, where "longsuffering" is followed by "bearing with one another." Paul adds "with joy," so the endurance he prays for is not grim. God's power is at work when His people keep going and keep loving, and still have joy.

Study Notes

The two translations place "joy" differently. The NKJV joins it to the preceding words: "patience and longsuffering with joy." The NLT ends verse 11 with "May you be filled with joy," and continues into verse 12 with "always thanking the Father," linking joy with thanksgiving. Either way, joy belongs to the worthy life Paul prays for.

Paul uses similar language about his own ministry at the end of the chapter, where he labors "according to His working which works in me mightily" (1:29). The strength he prays for the Colossians to receive is the same kind of divine working he relies on himself.

Verse Explanation

The worthy life also includes "giving thanks to the Father," because He "has qualified us" to share in "the inheritance of the saints in the light." Believers have a place among God's people because the Father Himself made them fit for it.

giving thanks to the Father is the last mark of the worthy walk in Paul's prayer. Thanksgiving belongs to how the Colossians will live, alongside fruitfulness, growth in knowing God, and God-given endurance. Paul's own prayer began with thanks to the Father (1:3), and now he prays that the church will share that same gratitude.

who has qualified us gives the reason for the thanks. The Father has already made His people fit, and the action is complete. The Colossians did not qualify themselves, and they cannot add to what the Father has done. Paul says "us," placing himself beside the Colossians as one who also received this gift.

to be partakers of the inheritance of the saints tells what they were qualified for. To be a partaker is to have a real share. An inheritance is received as a gift, not earned as wages. "The saints" are God's holy people, the same word Paul used for the Colossians in his greeting (1:2). People who "once were alienated and enemies" (1:21) now have a full share alongside all of God's people.

in the light describes the realm where this inheritance belongs. Light stands for God's truth, purity, and presence, and it prepares for the contrast of the next verse, where the Father rescues His people from "the power of darkness." The inheritance belongs to the side of light, and the Father has placed His people there.

Study Notes

The NLT reads "He has enabled you" where the NKJV reads "who has qualified us." Both translations include the Colossians among those the Father has made fit for the inheritance. The NKJV wording also includes Paul.

He writes in chains (4:3, 18), yet the first thing he says about his suffering is that he rejoices in it. "Now" sets his present hardship beside everything he has just said about Christ's reconciling work, and "for you" names the Colossians as the people his suffering serves. Paul does not delight in pain for its own sake. He rejoices because his hardships are doing good for believers who may never have seen his face (2:1).

and fill up in my flesh — "In my flesh" places the suffering in Paul's own body: the imprisonment, the chains, and the physical cost of his work. To "fill up" is to supply what is still needed to complete a measure. Paul sees his own body as the place where the hardship his calling requires is being borne.

what is lacking in the afflictions of Christ — This phrase needs the most care in the verse. Paul cannot mean that Christ's death left something undone. Just two verses earlier he says believers have already been reconciled "in the body of His flesh through death" (1:22), and in 2:14 he says the record against them was nailed to the cross. Paul is speaking about the sufferings that still fall to him as he serves the church, the share of hardship that belongs to his calling and is not yet complete. Readers debate exactly how these sufferings can be called "the afflictions of Christ," but the safeguard is plain: Christ's atoning death lacks nothing.

for the sake of His body, which is the church — Paul's suffering has a purpose beyond himself. He has already called Christ "the head of the body, the church" (1:18), and now he says his hardships serve that same body. Because the church is Christ's own body, suffering for the church is suffering in Christ's service. The NKJV ends the verse with a comma, and Paul's sentence runs straight on into the account of his calling in verse 25.

Study Notes

Within a few lines Paul uses "flesh" and "body" in two settings. In 1:22 Christ reconciled believers "in the body of His flesh through death," His own physical body on the cross. In 1:24 Paul suffers "in my flesh" for "His body, which is the church." The repeated words link the two verses, while the context keeps Christ's death, which has already reconciled believers, distinct from Paul's continuing service to the church.

The NLT renders the central clause, "I am participating in the sufferings of Christ that continue for his body, the church." That translation reads the phrase as Paul's share in sufferings that continue among Christ's people, where the NKJV keeps the wording "fill up ... what is lacking."

which has been hidden from ages and from generations — "Ages" and "generations" describe long stretches of time and the people who lived through them. Across all those years God's purpose remained hidden, and generation after generation passed without seeing it made known.

but now has been revealed — "But now" marks the turning point. What God kept hidden He has now uncovered, and Paul's stewardship is to make that revelation known. The disclosure is God's act, and Paul's role is to proclaim what God has revealed.

to His saints — The mystery has been revealed to "His saints," the same word Paul used for the Colossians in his greeting (1:2). God's revealed purpose belongs to all His holy people. Him we preach — Because the mystery is "Christ in you," the message Paul proclaims is Christ Himself. In the NKJV "Him" stands first in the sentence, placing Christ at the center of the preaching. The "we" includes those who share the work with Paul, such as Timothy, who is named in the greeting (1:1).

warning every man and teaching every man — Preaching Christ involves two activities. Warning confronts danger and calls people back from wrong paths, which suits a letter that cautions believers against being deceived "with persuasive words" (2:4) or cheated "through philosophy and empty deceit" (2:8). Teaching builds understanding of the truth. Paul does both, and he does both for "every man," meaning every person without distinction.

in all wisdom — Paul's warning and teaching draw on the same wisdom he prayed the Colossians would receive, "all wisdom and spiritual understanding" (1:9). In this letter, true wisdom is found in Christ, "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge" (2:3).

that we may present every man perfect in Christ Jesus — This is the goal of the whole ministry. "Perfect" here means complete or mature, fully grown in Christ. "Present" recalls 1:22, where Christ's death aims "to present you holy, and blameless, and above reproach in His sight." Paul's preaching and teaching serve the same purpose Christ's reconciling death has in view. The threefold "every man" leaves no believer out and reserves maturity for no inner circle. That maturity is "in Christ Jesus": believers grow by remaining joined to Him, the One in whom they are already complete (2:10).

Study Notes

The NKJV's "every man" uses "man" in the sense of each person, and the NLT renders it "everyone." The NLT also renders the goal as "present them to God, perfect in their relationship to Christ," reading "perfect" as maturity in relationship to Christ rather than flawless conduct.

The phrase "in all wisdom" appears again in 3:16, where the whole church is to let the word of Christ dwell in them richly "in all wisdom, teaching and admonishing one another." The teaching Paul describes as his own ministry here is later given to the believers as a work they share with one another.

Epaphras prays toward the same goal in 4:12, "that you may stand perfect and complete in all the will of God." Paul's labor and Epaphras's prayer aim at the Colossians' maturity.

He works hard because God is working powerfully in him, and his striving rests on that power rather than on his own reserves.
