Chapter Study

Colossians 2

Verse-by-Verse
Colossians 2:1
Colossians 2:1
NKJV

Colossians 2:1 For I want you to know what a great conflict I have for you and those in Laodicea, and for as many as have not seen my face in the flesh,(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 2:1 I want you to know how much I have agonized for you and for the church at Laodicea, and for many other believers who have never met me personally. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul wants the Colossians to know “what a great conflict” he has for them, for the believers in Laodicea, and for all who “have not seen my face in the flesh.” This conflict is his earnest struggle in prayer and labor on their behalf. It carries on the striving he described in 1:29 and reaches even churches that have never met him.

Verse Explanation

Paul wants the Colossians to know “what a great conflict” he has for them, for the believers in Laodicea, and for all who “have not seen my face in the flesh.” This conflict is his earnest struggle in prayer and labor on their behalf. It carries on the striving he described in 1:29 and reaches even churches that have never met him.

For I want you to know ties the chapter to what Paul has just said. At the end of chapter 1 he described himself as laboring and “striving according to His working which works in me mightily” (1:29). The word “For” shows that he is now explaining that striving in more personal terms. The phrase “I want you to know” shows that he wants the church to be aware of something it could not see for itself.

what a great conflict I have for you names that unseen effort. The “conflict” is not a quarrel with the Colossians. It is a struggle Paul carries on for them, the earnest labor of prayer and ministry that he spends on their behalf. Paul is in chains (4:3, 18), so the church in Colosse could not watch any of this happen. He wants them to know that a prisoner far away is pouring himself out for their good.

and those in Laodicea widens the circle to the believers in a neighboring city. Paul later greets “the brethren who are in Laodicea” and asks that this letter be read in their church as well (4:15–16), so naming them here is no afterthought. The churches of the area shared news, workers, and letters, and Paul’s struggle takes them in together.

and for as many as have not seen my face in the flesh reaches further still, to every believer who has never met Paul in person. Here “in the flesh” simply means physically, face to face. The Colossians had learned the gospel from Epaphras (1:7), and Paul knew of their faith through what had been reported to him (1:4, 8). Paul does not say whether he had ever visited Colosse himself. What he does make plain is that his care does not depend on knowing people personally. He labors for churches that know him only by name, because they belong to the same Lord.

Study Notes

The wording “and for as many as” can be read in two ways. It may describe a wider group that includes the Colossians and Laodiceans among those who have never seen Paul, or it may add a further group beyond them. The NLT’s “many other believers who have never met me personally” leans toward the first reading. Either way, this verse cannot by itself settle whether Paul had been to Colosse.

Later in the letter Paul says that Epaphras has “a great zeal for you, and those who are in Laodicea, and those in Hierapolis” (4:13). The concern for Laodicea expressed here is therefore shared by the man who first brought the gospel to Colosse, although this verse names only Laodicea.

Colossians 2:2
Colossians 2:2
NKJV

Colossians 2:2 that their hearts may be encouraged, being knit together in love, and attaining to all riches of the full assurance of understanding, to the knowledge of the mystery of God, both of the Father and of Christ,(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 2:2 I want them to be encouraged and knit together by strong ties of love. I want them to have complete confidence that they understand God’s mysterious plan, which is Christ himself. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul names the goal of his struggle: that their hearts may be encouraged and “knit together in love,” and that they may reach the “full assurance of understanding” that comes from knowing “the mystery of God, both of the Father and of Christ.” Encouragement, unity in love, and settled understanding belong together, and the mystery they come to know is God’s once-hidden purpose, now revealed in Christ (1:26–27).

Verse Explanation

Paul names the goal of his struggle: that their hearts may be encouraged and “knit together in love,” and that they may reach the “full assurance of understanding” that comes from knowing “the mystery of God, both of the Father and of Christ.” Encouragement, unity in love, and settled understanding belong together, and the mystery they come to know is God’s once-hidden purpose, now revealed in Christ (1:26–27).

that their hearts may be encouraged states what Paul’s struggle aims at. He moves from “you” in verse 1 to “their” here, so he is speaking of everyone he has just named: the Colossians, the Laodiceans, and all who have never seen him. In Scripture the heart is the inner person, where a believer thinks, chooses, and trusts. Paul wants that inner life strengthened and comforted, because persuasive voices could unsettle them (2:4).

being knit together in love describes how that encouragement comes. To be knit together is to be joined into one, like separate parts fastened into a single whole. Paul does not picture isolated believers each finding strength alone. Their hearts are strengthened as they are bound to one another, and love is the bond that holds them together.

and attaining to all riches of the full assurance of understanding adds a second goal. Paul wants them to reach an understanding that is settled and confident, not hesitant or easily shaken. He calls it “all riches,” the same language of wealth he used for the mystery itself, “the riches of the glory of this mystery” (1:27). A church that clearly understands what it already has in Christ is rich, and that settled understanding gives it firm footing when someone tries to talk it out of the truth.

to the knowledge of the mystery of God, both of the Father and of Christ names what that understanding grasps. In Colossians a mystery is God’s purpose, once hidden and now revealed. Paul has just described it as “the mystery which has been hidden from ages and from generations, but now has been revealed to His saints,” and he has called it “Christ in you, the hope of glory,” made known among the Gentiles (1:26–27). This mystery is not secret information for a spiritual elite, because Paul proclaims it to “every man” (1:28). The words “both of the Father and of Christ” connect the mystery to the Father, who “willed to make known” His purpose (1:27), and to Christ, in whom that purpose is revealed. For the mostly Gentile believers in Colosse, the mystery means that Christ lives among them as His people and within each of them personally. Encouragement, love, and understanding all lead to knowing Him.

Study Notes

The NKJV phrase “knit together” appears again in 2:19, where Christ’s body is “knit together by joints and ligaments.” The repeated wording links the love that joins believers here with the growth of the body that holds fast to its Head there.

The NLT renders the end of the verse as “God’s mysterious plan, which is Christ himself,” identifying the mystery directly with Christ. The NKJV reads “the mystery of God, both of the Father and of Christ.” Both translations tie the mystery closely to Christ, as 1:27 does.

Colossians 2:3
Colossians 2:3
NKJV

Colossians 2:3 in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 2:3 In him lie hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge. (NLT)

Main Idea

Christ is the One “in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge.” These treasures are hidden in the sense that they are stored up in Him, where believers find them. No one has to look beyond Christ for true wisdom.

Verse Explanation

Christ is the One “in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge.” These treasures are hidden in the sense that they are stored up in Him, where believers find them. No one has to look beyond Christ for true wisdom.

in whom are hidden points back to Christ, the last person named in verse 2, and the NLT makes this explicit with “In him.” Here “hidden” does not mean locked away out of reach, since Paul has just said that the mystery “now has been revealed to His saints” (1:26). The treasures are hidden the way valuables are kept in a treasury: stored safely in one place, where anyone who comes there finds them. Believers do not find wisdom by going around Christ or past Him. They find it by knowing Him.

all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge leaves nothing outside Christ. Wisdom is the understanding of how to live rightly before God, and knowledge is the understanding of God and His purposes. Calling them “treasures” continues the language of riches from verse 2 and presents true understanding as real wealth. This verse prepares for what follows. Any teacher who offers a higher wisdom beyond Christ is offering what believers already have in Him, and that is why Paul can immediately warn against “persuasive words” (2:4) and philosophy that is “not according to Christ” (2:8).

Study Notes

Colossians uses “hidden” in more than one way. In 1:26 the mystery was hidden in the sense of not yet disclosed, and it “now has been revealed.” In 2:3 the treasures are hidden in the sense of stored up in Christ. In 3:3 “your life is hidden with Christ in God,” meaning that it is kept safe with Him though not yet visible. The context of each verse shows which sense applies, so 2:3 does not contradict the revelation announced in 1:26.

Wisdom and knowledge run through the letter. Paul prays that the Colossians may be “filled with the knowledge of His will in all wisdom” (1:9). Later he says that human regulations have only “an appearance of wisdom” (2:23). Verse 3 sits between these passages as the place where Paul locates the real thing.

Colossians 2:4
Colossians 2:4
NKJV

Colossians 2:4 Now this I say lest anyone should deceive you with persuasive words.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 2:4 I am telling you this so no one will deceive you with well-crafted arguments. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul says he has written this so that no one will “deceive you with persuasive words.” Since every treasure of wisdom is found in Christ, any smooth argument that draws believers away from Him is deception, however convincing it sounds. This short warning prepares for the fuller warnings in the rest of the chapter.

Verse Explanation

Paul says he has written this so that no one will “deceive you with persuasive words.” Since every treasure of wisdom is found in Christ, any smooth argument that draws believers away from Him is deception, however convincing it sounds. This short warning prepares for the fuller warnings in the rest of the chapter.

Now this I say explains why Paul has written what comes just before. “This” refers most directly to his statement that every treasure of wisdom and knowledge is in Christ (2:3), together with his concern for their understanding in 2:1–2. Paul has told them where wisdom is found so that they will recognize a counterfeit when they hear one.

lest anyone should deceive you states his protective purpose. Paul does not name anyone. Throughout the chapter he describes the teaching by what it says and demands rather than by who taught it (2:8–23). To deceive someone is to lead that person to accept what is false as true. Paul treats the danger as real, even though the church is currently standing firm (2:5).

with persuasive words names the means of deception: speech that sounds convincing and is skillfully argued. Paul does not say this speech is crude or obviously wrong. Its danger is that it is attractive. Because every treasure of wisdom is already in Christ, any argument that leads away from Him is deceptive, however reasonable it seems. This brief warning prepares for the fuller warnings that begin in 2:8.

Study Notes

This verse is the first in a series of warnings that each concern an unnamed person: “lest anyone should deceive you” (2:4), “Beware lest anyone cheat you” (2:8), “let no one judge you” (2:16), and “Let no one cheat you of your reward” (2:18). The first is the shortest. The later warnings describe the specific claims and practices involved.

Colossians 2:5
Colossians 2:5
NKJV

Colossians 2:5 For though I am absent in the flesh, yet I am with you in spirit, rejoicing to see your good order and the steadfastness of your faith in Christ.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 2:5 For though I am far away from you, my heart is with you. And I rejoice that you are living as you should and that your faith in Christ is strong. (NLT)

Main Idea

Although Paul is absent in body, he is with the Colossians “in spirit,” and he rejoices to see their “good order and the steadfastness of your faith in Christ.” His warnings come to a church that is already standing firm, and he writes to keep it that way.

Verse Explanation

Although Paul is absent in body, he is with the Colossians “in spirit,” and he rejoices to see their “good order and the steadfastness of your faith in Christ.” His warnings come to a church that is already standing firm, and he writes to keep it that way.

For though I am absent in the flesh explains how Paul can speak so personally to a church far away. Being absent “in the flesh” means he is not physically there. He writes as a prisoner (4:3, 18) and cannot travel to them.

yet I am with you in spirit insists that distance has not cut him off from them. Paul is present with them in his thoughts, his prayers, and his loving attention. This is how the “great conflict” of verse 1 works: though separated from them, he is fully engaged on their behalf.

rejoicing to see your good order shows what Paul feels as he thinks of them. He cannot see the church with his own eyes. He most likely “sees” it through what Epaphras reported, since Epaphras “declared to us your love in the Spirit” (1:8). “Good order” describes a church whose life is well ordered and holding together as it should. Paul’s warnings are not a rebuke for disorder.

and the steadfastness of your faith in Christ names the second cause of his joy. Their faith is firm and unmoved, and its object is Christ. Paul has already said that believers must “continue in the faith, grounded and steadfast” (1:23), and here he rejoices that the Colossians are doing so. His warning in 2:4 and his appeal in 2:6, “As you therefore have received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk in Him,” both aim to keep a steady church steady.

Study Notes

“In the flesh” opens and closes this section (2:1, 5), and both times it simply means bodily presence. Later in the chapter, “flesh” takes on a moral sense, describing human life ruled by sin, as in “the sins of the flesh” (2:11), “the uncircumcision of your flesh” (2:13), “fleshly mind” (2:18), and “the indulgence of the flesh” (2:23). The context decides which sense applies.

The NKJV prints “spirit” here in lowercase, which shows it is speaking of Paul’s own spirit, unlike “the Spirit” in 1:8. The NLT’s “my heart is with you” reads the phrase the same way.

2. Colossians 2:6–7 — Keep Walking in Christ as You Received Him

Colossians 2:6
Colossians 2:6
NKJV

Colossians 2:6 As you therefore have received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk in Him,(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 2:6 And now, just as you accepted Christ Jesus as your Lord, you must continue to follow him. (NLT)

Main Idea

With the word “therefore,” Paul turns from his own ministry to his central appeal: “As you therefore have received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk in Him.” To “walk” is to conduct one’s everyday life. The Colossians began by receiving Jesus as Lord, and they are to keep living the same way, depending on Him and following Him.

Verse Explanation

With the word “therefore,” Paul turns from his own ministry to his central appeal: “As you therefore have received Christ Jesus the Lord, so walk in Him.” To “walk” is to conduct one’s everyday life. The Colossians began by receiving Jesus as Lord, and they are to keep living the same way, depending on Him and following Him.

As you therefore draws a conclusion from everything Paul has just written. He has described Christ as the One in whom are hidden “all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (2:3), and he has warned against anyone who would deceive the Colossians “with persuasive words” (2:4). Now he stops speaking about his own struggle for them (2:1–5) and speaks directly to them. The word “as” opens a comparison that the verse completes with “so”: the way they began the Christian life is the way they are to go on living it.

have received Christ Jesus the Lord recalls how that life began. What the Colossians received was a Person, and Paul gives Him His full name and title. Calling Him “the Lord” names Jesus as the One with rightful authority over their lives, the same Son Paul has just presented as Creator and Head of the church, “that in all things He may have the preeminence” (1:18). They received Him through the gospel Epaphras brought, “as you also learned from Epaphras” (1:7). Receiving Christ meant welcoming Jesus Himself, the Lord proclaimed in that true message.

so walk in Him gives the command. Paul uses walking as a picture of the whole course of a person’s conduct. He prayed earlier “that you may walk worthy of the Lord” (1:10), and later he reminds the Colossians of sins “in which you yourselves once walked” (3:7) and tells them to “Walk in wisdom toward those who are outside” (4:5). A walk is steady and ongoing, one step after another, day after day. The words “in Him” place that whole way of life inside their union with Christ. They are not to imitate Him from a distance or move on to something beyond Him. They are to keep living in the same dependence with which they first received Him. Every warning that follows in 2:8–23 protects this one appeal, because nothing needs to be added to the Lord they already have.

Study Notes

The NLT renders “Christ Jesus the Lord” as “Christ Jesus as your Lord,” which makes explicit that the Colossians received Jesus as the One who rules their lives. The NKJV keeps the wording as a full title. Both translations point to His lordship as part of what was received.

The NLT gives the meaning of Paul’s picture of walking with “you must continue to follow him.” The NKJV keeps the picture itself, so English readers can follow it through the letter: “walk” in 1:10, “walked” in 3:7, and “Walk” in 4:5.

Colossians 2:7
Colossians 2:7
NKJV

Colossians 2:7 rooted and built up in Him and established in the faith, as you have been taught, abounding in it with thanksgiving.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 2:7 Let your roots grow down into him, and let your lives be built on him. Then your faith will grow strong in the truth you were taught, and you will overflow with thankfulness. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul describes that walk with two pictures, a tree and a building: believers are “rooted and built up in Him” and “established in the faith, as you have been taught.” He confirms the gospel they already learned rather than replacing it, and he wants their faith to overflow, “abounding in it with thanksgiving.”

Verse Explanation

Paul describes that walk with two pictures, a tree and a building: believers are “rooted and built up in Him” and “established in the faith, as you have been taught.” He confirms the gospel they already learned rather than replacing it, and he wants their faith to overflow, “abounding in it with thanksgiving.”

rooted and built up in Him continues the sentence that began in verse 6 and describes what the walk in Christ looks like. A tree’s roots go down into the soil and draw up its life, and a building rises course by course on its foundation. Both pictures are anchored “in Him”: Christ is the soil the believer draws life from and the foundation the believer’s life is built on. A walk that lasts depends on that anchoring. Later in this chapter Paul traces the false teacher’s failure to “not holding fast to the Head,” the One from whom the body “grows with the increase that is from God” (2:19). Growth comes from staying connected to Christ.

and established in the faith adds the idea of being made firm and settled. Here “the faith” is the gospel truth the Colossians believe. Paul has already said that Christ will present them blameless “if indeed you continue in the faith, grounded and steadfast” (1:23), and he has just rejoiced to see “the steadfastness of your faith in Christ” (2:5). He wants that firmness to hold, so that no persuasive argument can move them.

as you have been taught sets the standard for that faith: the gospel the Colossians already learned. Paul is confirming Epaphras’s teaching (1:7), not correcting it or adding a deeper level to it. This matters because of the teaching he is about to expose, which is “not according to Christ” (2:8). New claims are to be tested against the gospel the church already received, and the gospel is not to be judged by the new claims.

abounding in it with thanksgiving describes a faith that overflows. The word “it” refers back to “the faith,” and Paul wants that faith to spill over in thanks to God. Thanksgiving runs through the whole letter (1:3, 12; 3:15, 17; 4:2), and here it fits the argument closely. Believers give thanks for what they have already received, and in the verses that follow Paul shows how much that is: all the fullness of God in Christ and completeness in Him (2:9–10).

Study Notes

The NLT turns these descriptions into commands: “Let your roots grow down into him, and let your lives be built on him.” In the NKJV, verse 6 ends with a comma, and the phrases of verse 7 continue the same sentence as descriptions of what it means to “walk in Him.” The NLT presents them as instructions, while the NKJV presents them as the character of the walk Paul has just commanded.

The phrase “in Him” appears in both verse 6 and verse 7 and keeps recurring in the verses that follow: “in Him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily” (2:9), “you are complete in Him” (2:10), and “In Him you were also circumcised” (2:11). The repetition ties Paul’s appeal to walk in Christ directly to the statements about what believers have in Him.

3. Colossians 2:8–10 — Beware of Empty Philosophy, for You Are Complete in Christ

Colossians 2:8
Colossians 2:8
NKJV

Colossians 2:8 Beware lest anyone cheat you through philosophy and empty deceit, according to the tradition of men, according to the basic principles of the world, and not according to Christ.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 2:8 Don’t let anyone capture you with empty philosophies and high-sounding nonsense that come from human thinking and from the spiritual powers of this world, rather than from Christ. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul warns, “Beware lest anyone cheat you through philosophy and empty deceit,” and he identifies such teaching by where it comes from: “according to the tradition of men, according to the basic principles of the world, and not according to Christ.” The exact meaning of “the basic principles of the world” is debated, but Paul places this whole order of human religion over against Christ, and believers have already died to it with Him (2:20).

Verse Explanation

Paul warns, “Beware lest anyone cheat you through philosophy and empty deceit,” and he identifies such teaching by where it comes from: “according to the tradition of men, according to the basic principles of the world, and not according to Christ.” The exact meaning of “the basic principles of the world” is debated, but Paul places this whole order of human religion over against Christ, and believers have already died to it with Him (2:20).

Beware lest anyone cheat you — Paul now turns the short caution of 2:4 into a direct warning. “Beware” calls for alertness, and “lest anyone” leaves the source unnamed, because Paul describes the danger by what it does rather than by who teaches it. To be cheated is to lose something that already belongs to you. The Colossians have received Christ Jesus the Lord (2:6), and the danger is that someone will talk them out of what they already have in Him.

through philosophy and empty deceit — These words name the means the cheating would use. Paul joins “philosophy” to “empty deceit,” and the phrases that follow define the kind of philosophy he means, so he is not dismissing careful thought in general. He is describing religious teaching that sounds wise but has nothing real to give, and that misleads the people who accept it. Paul has just said that all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden in Christ (2:3). Any wisdom offered apart from Christ is therefore empty, however impressive it sounds.

according to the tradition of men — The first of three phrases identifies where the teaching comes from. A tradition is teaching handed down from one person to another, and this one starts with people, not with God. Its age or its wide acceptance does not change where it came from. Paul makes the same charge at the end of the chapter, where the rules he challenges rest on “the commandments and doctrines of men” (2:22).

according to the basic principles of the world — The second phrase ties this human teaching to the order of the present world. Paul does not define the expression, and its exact meaning is debated. It may refer to elementary religious rules and teachings, or to spiritual powers connected with the present world order. In either case it describes the realm believers have left behind. When Paul uses the phrase again in 2:20, he says that believers “died with Christ” from these basic principles, so whatever the phrase includes, it no longer has any claim on them.

and not according to Christ — The last phrase gives the deciding test. The teaching fits human tradition and the world’s basic principles well enough, but Christ is not its standard. Paul’s objection does not depend on whether the teaching is new or strange. He objects that it measures spiritual life by something other than Christ. This is why the next verse turns straight to who Christ is.

Study Notes

Where the NKJV reads “cheat you,” the NLT reads “capture you,” which pictures the believers being carried off as captives. In the NKJV the believers are robbed of what belongs to them; in the NLT they are taken away. Neither translation says who the person behind the threat was or whether that person belonged to the church.

The NKJV’s “the basic principles of the world” appears in the NLT as “the spiritual powers of this world.” The NLT takes one side of a debated question by reading the phrase as spiritual forces rather than elementary rules or teachings. The NKJV wording leaves both possibilities open. The NLT makes the same choice in 2:20, where it says Christ “has set you free from the spiritual powers of this world.”

Colossians never names the teachers or gives their teaching a title. Paul describes it by its features in 2:8–23: human tradition, judgments over food and holy days, angel worship and visions, and severe bodily rules. Detailed reconstructions of a movement behind these features, such as proposals that combine Jewish and Gnostic elements, go beyond what the letter says and remain proposals.

Colossians 2:9
Colossians 2:9
NKJV

Colossians 2:9 For in Him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily;(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 2:9 For in Christ lives all the fullness of God in a human body. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul gives the reason behind his warning: “in Him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily.” “Godhead” is an older English word for deity. Everything God is lives in Christ, who has a real human body, so no teaching, power, or experience can offer more of God than believers already find in Him.

Verse Explanation

Paul gives the reason behind his warning: “in Him dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily.” “Godhead” is an older English word for deity. Everything God is lives in Christ, who has a real human body, so no teaching, power, or experience can offer more of God than believers already find in Him.

For — This small word joins the verse to the warning of verse 8. Paul does not answer empty philosophy with a better philosophy of his own. He answers it by telling the Colossians who Christ is. Teaching “not according to Christ” is empty because of what is true of Christ.

in Him dwells — “In Him” keeps attention on Christ, as it has throughout this part of the letter. To “dwell” is to live in a place or make one’s home there. Paul speaks in the present tense. He is describing who Christ is now, not only something that was once true of Him.

all the fullness of the Godhead — Earlier Paul wrote that “it pleased the Father that in Him all the fullness should dwell” (1:19). Here he says plainly what that fullness is: the fullness “of the Godhead,” an older English word for deity. “All the fullness” leaves nothing out. Christ is not one spiritual being among many who holds a share of God’s nature. Everything that makes God who He is lives in Christ, so a teaching that leads people away from Christ cannot bring them nearer to God.

bodily — The final word tells how this fullness lives in Christ. It is present in the Son who has a real human body, the body in which He reconciled believers “in the body of His flesh through death” (1:22) and in which God raised Him from the dead (2:12). God’s fullness is not tucked away in an unseen realm that has to be reached through angels or visions (2:18). It lives in Jesus Christ Himself.

Study Notes

In the NKJV, verse 9 ends with a semicolon and verse 10 continues with a lowercase “and.” The two verses form one sentence, so the verse division falls in the middle of Paul’s statement. Christ’s fullness and believers’ completeness are stated together as a single thought.

Colossians 2:10
Colossians 2:10
NKJV

Colossians 2:10 and you are complete in Him, who is the head of all principality and power.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 2:10 So you also are complete through your union with Christ, who is the head over every ruler and authority. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because the fullness of deity is in Christ, believers “are complete in Him” and do not need any other source to supply what they lack. Christ is also “the head of all principality and power,” ruling over every authority, seen or unseen, so no such power can add to what believers already have in Him.

Verse Explanation

Because the fullness of deity is in Christ, believers “are complete in Him” and do not need any other source to supply what they lack. Christ is also “the head of all principality and power,” ruling over every authority, seen or unseen, so no such power can add to what believers already have in Him.

and you are complete in Him — In the same sentence, Paul turns from Christ to the Colossians. “And you” applies the truth of verse 9 directly to them. Christ possesses all the fullness of deity. Believers do not share His deity, but in Him they have received fullness. “Complete” describes their present condition. They are not partly supplied, waiting for another teacher, rule, or experience to finish what Christ began.

The words “in Him” carry the weight of the claim. Believers are complete because they are joined to Christ, the One in whom the fullness dwells, and not because of their own spiritual achievements. This completeness does not mean the Colossians have nothing left to learn, since Paul still prays for them to keep “increasing in the knowledge of God” (1:10). It means nothing essential is missing from what they have in Christ. Their growth comes out of that completeness and is never a search for something Christ has not given.

who is the head of all principality and power — Paul ends by naming Christ’s position over every other authority. “Principality and power” are words for ruling powers, and the letter has already said that such powers, visible and invisible, were created through Christ and for Him (1:16). Here “head” means the One who rules over them. Whatever place the teaching Paul opposes gave to angels or other spiritual powers, none of them stands beside Christ or above Him, and none can supply what believers lack. These same powers come up again in 2:15, where God disarmed the hostile ones at the cross. Believers who belong to the Head over every power have no reason to look to those powers for anything.

Study Notes

The NKJV’s “all principality and power” pairs “all” with singular nouns, an older English way of saying every principality and every power. The NLT makes this explicit with “every ruler and authority.”

Colossians uses “head” in two related ways. In 1:18 and 2:19 Christ is the head of the body, the church, from whom the whole body grows. In 2:10 He is head over “all principality and power,” and the emphasis falls on His authority over them. The powers are under Christ as their ruler; the letter does not describe them as members of His body.

In Him you were also circumcised carries forward the thought of verses 9–10. All the fullness of the Godhead dwells in Christ, and believers are “complete in Him.” The word “also” adds one more part of that completeness, and Paul describes it in the language of circumcision. That language mattered in Paul’s world. In this letter he describes his Jewish coworkers simply as those “of the circumcision” (4:11), so the word marked a person as belonging to the Jewish people. Most of the Colossians were Gentiles. Paul describes their former condition as “the uncircumcision of your flesh” (2:13). Now he tells them that they have been circumcised in Christ. Their union with Him gives them everything that circumcision could have stood for, and no one can treat them as outsiders to God’s people.

with the circumcision made without hands explains what kind of circumcision Paul means. No human hand performed it. Physical circumcision was a rite people carried out on the body, but this circumcision is God’s own work on the person. Paul is not describing a ceremony the Colossians underwent. He is describing a change God made when they came to belong to Christ.

by putting off the body of the sins of the flesh tells what that circumcision accomplished. Here “flesh” does not mean skin and muscle. It means human life under the rule of sin, and “the body of the sins of the flesh” is that whole old life. The physical rite removed only a small piece of the body, but God’s work cut away the old life ruled by sin. This does not mean believers never face sin again. Paul will later command them to “put to death your members which are on the earth” (3:5). That command rests on what God has already done to them in Christ.

by the circumcision of Christ names whose circumcision this is. It comes from Christ and is received in union with Him. It is not a rite added to Christ or a requirement that some teacher sets beside Him. The phrase brings the verse back to where it began, “In Him.” The Gentile believers in Colosse need nothing that physical circumcision could add, because they already have this circumcision in Christ.

Study Notes

Colossians never says that the teachers troubling the church demanded physical circumcision. The pressures Paul names directly are human tradition, food and calendar judgments, angel worship, and bodily rules (2:8, 16–23). The text does not settle whether Paul uses the language of circumcision to answer a specific demand or to assure Gentile believers that they fully belong.

The NKJV phrase “the circumcision of Christ” can be read as the circumcision that Christ performs or as the circumcision that belongs to Christ. The NLT chooses the first meaning, rendering it “Christ performed a spiritual circumcision.” In either reading, Christ is the source of this circumcision.

The NKJV’s language of “putting off” returns in chapter 3. There believers are told that they “have put off the old man with his deeds” (3:9) and “have put on the new man” (3:10). The commands in 3:8 to put off anger, malice, and similar sins build on this earlier statement of what God has done.

buried with Him in baptism continues the same sentence from verse 11. The cutting away of the old life now becomes a burial. Burial follows death and shows that the death is real and final. in which you also were raised with Him carries the picture past the grave. In the NKJV, “in which” points back to baptism, so the same baptism that acts out burial also acts out rising. Paul’s comparison with circumcision in verse 11 also does not require matching every detail of circumcision to baptism.

The phrase “raised with Him” returns at 3:1, “If then you were raised with Christ.” That verse matches “if you died with Christ” in 2:20. Paul states the believers’ resurrection with Christ here and later uses it as the basis for the commands of chapter 3.

And you, Paul begins, turning from Christ’s resurrection at the close of verse 12 to the Colossians themselves. God raised Jesus from the dead, and now Paul shows what the same God did in the lives of his readers. They are placed directly into the account of God’s work.

He does not mean that the Colossians were physically dead. He means they were spiritually lifeless because of their sins and had no power to give themselves the life of God. Someone who is dead cannot help raise himself, and that is why everything else in the verse is God’s doing.

and the uncircumcision of your flesh, adds a second description. Most of the Colossians were probably Gentiles who had never received the physical sign of circumcision given to Israel. Paul’s meaning reaches further than a missing mark on the body, however. To be in “the uncircumcision of your flesh” was to remain in the old, unrenewed condition, before God cut away the life ruled by sin. Their outward status matched their inward distance from God.

He has made alive together with Him, names the One who acted. “He” is God, whom verse 12 describes as the One “who raised Him from the dead,” and “Him” is Christ. God did for people who were dead in sin what He had done for His Son in the grave: He gave them life. The words “together with Him” show that this life was not a separate, private life. They were made alive in union with Christ and now share the life of the risen Christ. This carries forward verse 12, where believers were “raised with Him through faith in the working of God.”

having forgiven you all trespasses, tells what came with that new life. The word that described their death, trespasses, now names what God has forgiven, and Paul adds “all.” No trespass that once held them in death is left unpaid. Paul joins new life and forgiveness in a single statement. People who were dead because of their trespasses could not be made alive while those trespasses still stood against them, so God’s forgiveness belongs with the life He gave. This forgiveness also prepares for Paul’s description in verse 14 of the record God canceled.

Study Notes

The NKJV reads “having forgiven you all trespasses,” while the NLT reads “for he forgave all our sins.” The NKJV keeps the “you” that opens the verse. The NLT already uses the first person, which anticipates the “us” of verse 14. In both translations the forgiveness reaches Paul’s readers.

The NLT renders “the uncircumcision of your flesh” as “because your sinful nature was not yet cut away.” That translation reads the phrase in light of 2:11, where the circumcision made without hands is “putting off the body of the sins of the flesh.” The NKJV keeps Paul’s circumcision language, so the reader can hear in it both the Colossians’ Gentile status and their unrenewed condition.

having wiped out the handwriting of requirements describes what God did with the record against His people. “Wiped out” pictures writing erased so thoroughly that nothing is left to read. “Handwriting” means a handwritten document, and “of requirements” tells what it contained: demands that had not been met. that was against us, which was contrary to us. Paul says the same thing twice to stress how firmly the record stood against them. And He has taken it out of the way, Paul continues, and the subject is still God. The record no longer lies in the path, ready to be produced against believers again. God removed it entirely. The wording “has taken” describes a finished act whose result still stands.

having nailed it to the cross. The record was fastened to the cross where Jesus died, so the charge against believers was settled in His death. God did not overlook the debt or pretend that it was never written. He dealt with it at the cross. This is the same death through which Christ “reconciled” believers “in the body of His flesh through death” (1:21–22), and in Christ they have “the forgiveness of sins” (1:14).

Because God has already done all this in Christ, Paul says, “let no one judge you in food or in drink, or regarding a festival or a new moon or sabbaths.” No one has the right to condemn believers over these food rules and holy days, as if their standing with God depended on keeping them.

So let no one judge you draws a conclusion from everything Paul has just said. The word “So” reaches back to 2:9–15: all the fullness of deity dwells in Christ, believers are complete in Him, God has forgiven all their trespasses, the record against them has been nailed to the cross, and the hostile powers have been disarmed. Since God has already done all of that, no one is in a position to pass a condemning verdict on believers, treating them as if they fall short before God. Paul addresses the command to the Colossians themselves. They are not to let anyone’s verdict on these matters unsettle them or define their standing.

in food or in drink names the first area where this judgment was pressed: what a believer eats and drinks. Paul does not list the particular rules here. Those verses show that rules about the table were part of the pressure the Colossians faced.

or regarding a festival or a new moon or sabbaths moves from the table to the calendar. Together these three terms cover sacred days, from special feasts to regularly recurring days of rest. Paul does not tell the Colossians to keep these days, and he does not forbid them to. His command is about judgment: no one may condemn a believer over these observances. This verse settles that point, and wider questions about Sabbath practice are left to their own treatment in the rest of Scripture.

Study Notes

The NLT reads “don’t let anyone condemn you for what you eat or drink, or for not celebrating certain holy days or new moon ceremonies or Sabbaths.” By adding “for not celebrating,” the NLT presents the criticism as aimed at believers who did not keep these days. This command is the first of two matching warnings. “Let no one judge you” (2:16) is followed by “Let no one cheat you of your reward” (2:18). Each warning rests on its own ground: shadow and substance in 2:17, and holding fast to the Head in 2:19.

Let no one cheat you of your reward. This is the second “let no one” warning in a row. In verse 16 Paul told the Colossians not to let anyone judge them over food and holy days. Now he warns against someone who would rob them of what is rightly theirs. A reward is a prize that belongs to the one who receives it. Paul does not define the reward here, but the chapter has just told believers that they “are complete in Him” (2:10). The loss Paul fears is most naturally that they would be talked out of what they already have in Christ, trading it for something that only looks more spiritual.

taking delight in false humility and worship of angels. The person Paul has in view enjoys these practices and holds them up as the way to deeper spirituality. “False humility” is lowliness put on for display. Paul names it again in 2:23 beside “self-imposed religion” and “neglect of the body,” which suggests strict self-denial that looks humble and devout. What “worship of angels” involved is harder to pin down. Readers debate whether Paul means worship offered to angels or a claim to join in the worship angels offer to God, and the letter does not explain what role the teaching gave such beings. The two readings describe different practices, and this verse does not settle between them. What is clear is the contrast Paul draws: Christ is the One through whom all things “visible and invisible” were created (1:16), and He is “the head of all principality and power” (2:10).

intruding into those things which he has not seen. In the NKJV wording, this person pushes into matters beyond his actual experience. He claims insight into hidden spiritual realities he has never seen, so his authority rests on claims no one can test, not on the faith the church was taught (2:7). The ancient manuscripts differ here, and some leave out “not.” On that reading, the person is dwelling on visions he claims to have seen. Paul does not stop to weigh each claimed experience on either reading. His concern is what these claims do to the man who makes them, and the next phrase names it.

vainly puffed up by his fleshly mind. Here Paul exposes the contradiction. The man who parades humility is actually “puffed up,” swollen with pride. “Vainly” means without any real ground. His pride is empty because its source is “his fleshly mind,” thinking driven by ordinary human nature rather than by God. His talk of angels and unseen things sounds heavenly, but Paul traces it to a merely human source. The sentence does not end at this point. Verse 18 closes with a comma, and Paul carries the thought straight into verse 19, where he names the failure beneath all these claims.

Where the NKJV reads “Let no one cheat you of your reward,” the NLT reads “Don’t let anyone condemn you.” The NKJV keeps the picture of losing a reward. The NLT puts the emphasis on the verdict passed against believers, which brings its wording close to the “let no one judge you” of verse 16.

Verse Explanation

Paul treats it as settled that believers “died with Christ from the basic principles of the world,” and he asks why, “as though living in the world,” they would “subject yourselves to regulations.” Their death with Christ has released them from that old order, so submitting to its rules again makes no sense.

Therefore, if you died with Christ draws a conclusion from everything Paul has said since 2:8. The Colossians are complete in Christ, they were buried and raised with Him (2:12), their debt has been canceled and the hostile powers defeated at the cross (2:13–15), and no one has the right to judge them or cheat them (2:16–18). The “if” does not question whether they died with Christ. Paul states a known fact in order to build on it: since this is true of you, consider what follows. Their death with Christ took place when they were joined to Him, the union Paul has just described as being “buried with Him in baptism” (2:12).

from the basic principles of the world names what that death separated them from. Paul used the same phrase in 2:8 to describe where the deceptive “philosophy” came from, alongside “the tradition of men” and in contrast to Christ. Its exact meaning is debated. It may refer to elementary religious rules and teachings, or to spiritual forces connected with the present world order. Paul’s point holds either way. Death ends a relationship, and believers who died with Christ have been released from that whole order of human religion and its demands.

why, as though living in the world turns that settled fact into a pointed question. The Colossians still lived in Colosse, so “the world” here does not mean their physical surroundings. It means the order Paul has just named, where those basic principles and human traditions hold sway. To live “as though” they still belonged there would be to act as if their death with Christ had never happened.

do you subject yourselves to regulations describes what that contradiction looks like in practice. The wording places the action on the believers themselves. No teacher can bind people who have died with Christ, but believers can choose to put themselves back under such rules, and Paul’s question suggests that some of this pressure was already being felt. The “regulations” are the kind of rules he is about to quote, and the dash at the end of the verse shows that his question is not finished. It runs on through the examples in 2:21 and closes at the end of 2:22.

Study Notes

This verse is matched by the opening of the next chapter. “If you died with Christ” here and “If then you were raised with Christ” in 3:1 begin parallel arguments. The first leads to freedom from human regulations, and the second leads to seeking “those things which are above” and putting off the old life. The chapter break falls between them, but Paul’s argument continues across it.

The NLT translates this verse quite differently: “You have died with Christ, and he has set you free from the spiritual powers of this world.” It states the condition as a plain fact, which matches the settled sense of the NKJV’s “if.” It also takes “the basic principles of the world” to mean spiritual powers. The NKJV keeps the more general wording, which leaves room for either meaning of the phrase.

Verse Explanation

Paul quotes sample rules, “Do not touch, do not taste, do not handle,” as examples of the regulations he is challenging. These short prohibitions show how such teaching measures holiness by what a person avoids.

Do not touch, do not taste, do not handle gives three samples of the regulations Paul has in mind. He sets them off as quotations, presenting the kind of short commands the teaching pressed on believers. None of the three gives a reason or names anything good to pursue. Each one simply forbids contact. “Taste” plainly concerns food and drink, the same area in which believers were being judged in 2:16. “Touch” and “handle” widen the restriction to any contact with whatever the rules declared off-limits. The verse does not say exactly which things were forbidden.

The string of prohibitions reveals the character of this religion. Its holiness is measured by keeping away from things, and its rules keep multiplying around the edges of ordinary life. Paul does not argue with each item. Set beside what believers already have in Christ, including fullness, forgiveness, and victory over the powers, these rules show themselves to be an attempt to supply what Christ has already given.

Study Notes

The NKJV reads “Do not touch, do not taste, do not handle,” and the NLT reads “Don’t handle! Don’t taste! Don’t touch!” The two translations use different English words for the first and third prohibitions, so “touch” and “handle” appear in opposite positions. Both lists forbid the same kinds of contact. The NLT also closes the verse with a question mark. In the NLT, Paul’s question ends here, and 2:22 becomes a separate statement about the rules. The NKJV keeps the question open until the end of 2:22. The two arrangements express the same argument.

They also rest on “the commandments and doctrines of men,” so they carry only human authority and cannot claim to speak for God.

which all concern things which perish with the using gives Paul’s first reason that the rules miss the mark. “Which all” points back to the rules he has just quoted, and every one of them deals with things that are used up in the very act of using them. Food is eaten and gone, and ordinary objects wear out. Rules that center on such passing things treat them as though they decided a person’s standing with God, when they are on their way to disappearing.

according to the commandments and doctrines of men gives his second reason. Whatever spiritual weight the rules claim, their source is human command and human teaching. That places them alongside “the tradition of men” Paul warned against in 2:8. With this phrase, the question that began in 2:20 comes to its close: why would people who died with Christ submit again to rules about perishable things that carry only human authority?

That arrangement suggests that “according to the commandments and doctrines of men” connects back to the “regulations” of 2:20 and identifies where those regulations come from.

Verse Explanation

Paul gives his verdict: such rules “indeed have an appearance of wisdom in self-imposed religion, false humility, and neglect of the body,” because they look devout and demanding. Yet they “are of no value against the indulgence of the flesh.” Rules that people impose on themselves cannot overcome sin, and Paul now turns to the life believers share with the risen Christ (3:1–5).

These things indeed have an appearance of wisdom begins Paul’s verdict on the rules. “Indeed” grants something real: the rules do look wise. Yet Paul’s word is “appearance.” Earlier in the chapter he said that in Christ “are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (2:3). Rules that only look wise are competing with the One in whom true wisdom is actually found.

Paul then names three features that give the rules their impressive look. The phrase in self-imposed religion describes worship and devotion that people devise and lay on themselves rather than receive from God, which fits the “commandments and doctrines of men” of 2:22. The words false humility repeat the very phrase Paul used for the person in 2:18, whose humility was only for show while he was “vainly puffed up by his fleshly mind.” The third phrase, and neglect of the body, refers to harsh treatment of the body through severe restrictions and self-denial. All three look devout and demanding, and that is why such rules can impress sincere believers.

but are of no value against the indulgence of the flesh gives the decisive judgment. “The flesh” here means sinful human nature, the self that insists on its own way, not the physical body as such. Paul has already spoken of “the sins of the flesh” put off in Christ (2:11) and of the “fleshly mind” behind false spirituality (2:18). For all their severity, the rules cannot restrain sinful desire. A person can treat the body harshly and still be ruled by pride, as the false humility of 2:18 shows.

Paul is not treating sin lightly. In the next chapter he commands, “put to death your members which are on the earth” (3:5). The difference lies in where each command comes from. Human rules promise a spiritual power they cannot deliver, while the deliberate putting to death of sin flows from being raised with Christ (3:1). Having shown why believers should refuse these regulations, Paul turns to the life that actually overcomes the flesh.

Study Notes

Paul’s wording in the final clause is difficult, and how its parts fit together is debated. The NKJV says the rules “are of no value against the indulgence of the flesh,” and the NLT similarly says they “provide no help in conquering a person’s evil desires.” Both translations read the clause as Paul’s judgment that the rules fail to restrain sinful desire, which fits the contrast with the “appearance of wisdom” earlier in the verse.
