Chapter Study

Colossians 4

Verse-by-Verse
Colossians 4:1
Colossians 4:1
NKJV

Colossians 4:1 Masters, give your bondservants what is just and fair, knowing that you also have a Master in heaven.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 4:1 Masters, be just and fair to your slaves. Remember that you also have a Master — in heaven. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul completes the instructions to bondservants in 3:22–25 by telling masters to give the enslaved people in their households “what is just and fair.” Masters owe this because they are not the final authority in the house: “you also have a Master in heaven,” and they must answer to the same Lord their bondservants serve.

Verse Explanation

Paul completes the instructions to bondservants in 3:22–25 by telling masters to give the enslaved people in their households “what is just and fair.” Masters owe this because they are not the final authority in the house: “you also have a Master in heaven,” and they must answer to the same Lord their bondservants serve.

Masters — Paul now speaks to the other side of the last household pair. He has just given bondservants a long instruction (3:22–25), and this verse finishes that same instruction even though a chapter break now falls between them. These masters are the “masters according to the flesh” whom bondservants were told to obey in 3:22. That phrase describes authority at the earthly, human level. Paul addresses these masters directly as members of the church who would hear the letter read in the same gathering as the people who served in their homes.

give your bondservants — In the household instructions, “bondservants” means enslaved people, the men and women who belonged to a master’s household and worked under his authority. Paul does not tell masters what they may demand from these people. He tells them what they must give. The bondservants owed obedience and sincere work, and now the masters learn that they have obligations of their own. Duty in the household runs in both directions.

what is just and fair — Paul names the standard that governs what masters give. It is not left to the master’s mood, convenience, or the customs of the day. “Just” points to what is right and actually owed. “Fair” points to even-handed treatment that does not take advantage of someone who has no power to object. Paul presents this treatment as an obligation the master owes, not as a kindness he may choose to show.

knowing that you also have a Master in heaven — Paul now gives the reason, and he gives it as something masters are to keep in mind as they act. The small word “also” puts them in the same position as the people who serve them. Bondservants had just been told, “you serve the Lord Christ” (3:24). Now their masters hear that they serve Him too. The capitalized “Master” is the Lord Himself. Their own authority belongs to the earthly level, “according to the flesh,” while the Lord’s authority is “in heaven” and stands over them. A master who treats bondservants unjustly will answer to the Lord. Paul has just warned that “he who does wrong will be repaid for what he has done,” and that “there is no partiality” (3:25). The whole household, the one who commands as well as the one who obeys, stands under the lordship of Christ.

Study Notes

The words “knowing that” introduce the motive on both sides of this household pair. Bondservants work “knowing that from the Lord you will receive the reward of the inheritance” (3:24), and masters act “knowing that you also have a Master in heaven.” Repeating the same wording links the two commands across the chapter break and grounds each one in what is true about the Lord.

The NLT reads “be just and fair to your slaves.” Its word “slaves” states plainly what the NKJV’s “bondservants” means in this passage. The NKJV keeps the wording “give your bondservants what is just and fair,” so justice appears as something masters hand over to the people under them. The NLT describes how masters are to behave toward them. The obligation is the same in both translations.

The statement “there is no partiality” (3:25) comes immediately before this verse. Its subject, “he who does wrong,” names no particular group. Because it stands between the two commands, its principle sits right beside the reminder that masters themselves have a Master in heaven.

2. Colossians 4:2–4 — Watchful, Thankful Prayer for an Open Door

Colossians 4:2
Colossians 4:2
NKJV

Colossians 4:2 Continue earnestly in prayer, being vigilant in it with thanksgiving;(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 4:2 Devote yourselves to prayer with an alert mind and a thankful heart. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul calls the whole church to “Continue earnestly in prayer,” so that prayer is a settled, persistent practice and not an occasional one. They are to stay alert as they pray and to pray “with thanksgiving,” so that watchfulness and gratitude go together.

Verse Explanation

Paul calls the whole church to “Continue earnestly in prayer,” so that prayer is a settled, persistent practice and not an occasional one. They are to stay alert as they pray and to pray “with thanksgiving,” so that watchfulness and gratitude go together.

Continue earnestly in prayer is spoken to the whole church. Paul has just addressed wives and husbands, children and fathers, bondservants and masters in turn (3:18–4:1). Now he speaks to everyone at once, and the first thing he asks of all of them is prayer. To “continue earnestly” is to keep at something and hold on to it, so Paul is describing a steady habit of the church’s life, not a practice saved for emergencies. Prayer belongs to the new life he has been describing since 3:1. People who were raised with Christ and seek the things above keep turning to God together.

The words being vigilant in it add alertness. To be vigilant is to stay awake and watchful. Paul wants the Colossians to pray attentively, mindful of what they are asking and watching for what God is doing, so that their prayer does not slide into routine words spoken half-asleep. The words “in it” tie this watchfulness to prayer itself: they are to stay alert while they pray.

Paul joins this alert prayer with thanksgiving. Watchful prayer could easily turn into anxious prayer, and thanksgiving keeps it anchored in what God has already done. The Colossians have a great deal to thank Him for. The Father has qualified them to share the inheritance of the saints, delivered them from the power of darkness, and conveyed them into the kingdom of His Son (1:12–13). Gratitude for that rescue gives their requests firm footing. Thanksgiving has run through the whole letter, and Paul will not let the church’s prayer lose it.

Study Notes

In the NKJV, verses 2–4 form one continuous sentence. Verse 2 ends with a semicolon, and verse 3 begins with “meanwhile praying also for us.” The request for Paul is therefore part of the steady, watchful, thankful prayer just described and not a separate subject.

Paul asks the church to do what he has already described himself and Timothy doing for them. The letter opened with “praying always for you” (1:3), and Paul added, “since the day we heard it, do not cease to pray for you” (1:9). Later in this chapter Epaphras is described as “always laboring fervently for you in prayers” (4:12).

The wording “being vigilant in it with thanksgiving” closely resembles 2:7, where believers are “established in the faith, as you have been taught, abounding in it with thanksgiving.” In both verses thanksgiving goes along with the main activity. Thanks to God appears in every chapter of Colossians (1:3, 12; 2:7; 3:15, 17; 4:2).

The NLT reads “Devote yourselves to prayer with an alert mind and a thankful heart,” which highlights the inner attitude behind each phrase. The NKJV’s “in it” keeps the watchfulness explicitly attached to the act of praying.

Colossians 4:3
Colossians 4:3
NKJV

Colossians 4:3 meanwhile praying also for us, that God would open to us a door for the word, to speak the mystery of Christ, for which I am also in chains,(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 4:3 Pray for us, too, that God will give us many opportunities to speak about his mysterious plan concerning Christ. That is why I am here in chains. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul asks the Colossians to pray for him and his companions as well, that God “would open to us a door for the word” so they can “speak the mystery of Christ,” God’s once-hidden purpose now revealed in Christ. Although he is “in chains” because of that very message, his request is about opportunity for the gospel, not about his own release.

Verse Explanation

Paul asks the Colossians to pray for him and his companions as well, that God “would open to us a door for the word” so they can “speak the mystery of Christ,” God’s once-hidden purpose now revealed in Christ. Although he is “in chains” because of that very message, his request is about opportunity for the gospel, not about his own release.

With the words meanwhile praying also for us, Paul asks to be included in that steady prayer. “Meanwhile” means while they pray in the way verse 2 describes. Paul is asking for a place within the prayer they already offer, not giving them a new task. “Us” reaches beyond Paul himself. Timothy shares in the letter’s greeting (1:1), and other coworkers are with Paul as he writes (4:7–14). The apostle who has prayed so faithfully for the Colossians now asks them to pray for him and his companions.

What Paul wants them to ask is that God would open to us a door for the word. “The word” is the gospel message, which the Colossians first heard as “the word of the truth of the gospel” (1:5). A door for the word is an opportunity to speak that message. Paul names God as the One who opens it. He does not plan to force his way into hearts or circumstances. He asks the Lord to create openings that only He can create, and he wants the church’s prayers behind that work.

The open door has a purpose: to speak the mystery of Christ. Earlier Paul described this mystery as “hidden from ages and from generations, but now has been revealed to His saints” (1:26). Its content is “Christ in you, the hope of glory,” made known “among the Gentiles” (1:27). In Colossians a mystery is God’s purpose, once hidden and now disclosed in Christ. It is not secret knowledge kept for an inner circle, and Paul wants to speak it openly to anyone who will listen.

The verse ends on a personal note: for which I am also in chains. Paul is a prisoner because of this very message, and that makes his request striking. A man in chains might be expected to ask the church to pray for his release. Paul says nothing about release here. He asks for an open door for the word, and the gospel that put him in chains is still what he most wants to speak.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the request as “many opportunities to speak about his mysterious plan concerning Christ,” which confirms that the open door stands for opportunity to speak. The NLT also spells out the link between the message and the imprisonment: “That is why I am here in chains.”

The verse shifts from plural to singular. Prayer is requested for “us,” but the chains belong to Paul: “for which I am also in chains.” Verse 4 continues with “I.” Paul is not the only one confined, though, since he calls Aristarchus “my fellow prisoner” (4:10).

Colossians does not say where Paul is imprisoned. The setting that fits best is Rome in the early AD 60s, where Acts describes him living under guard yet welcoming visitors and openly preaching the kingdom of God (Acts 28:16, 30–31). If that is the setting, Paul’s request comes from a man whose movements are restricted but who can still speak to those who come to him.

Paul mentions his chains again in his signature: “Remember my chains” (4:18). That closing request picks up the circumstance he names here.

Colossians 4:4
Colossians 4:4
NKJV

Colossians 4:4 that I may make it manifest, as I ought to speak.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 4:4 Pray that I will proclaim this message as clearly as I should. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul names the goal of that open door: “that I may make it manifest, as I ought to speak.” He wants to make the mystery of Christ plain to his hearers, and he sees speaking it clearly as a duty God has given him.

Verse Explanation

Paul names the goal of that open door: “that I may make it manifest, as I ought to speak.” He wants to make the mystery of Christ plain to his hearers, and he sees speaking it clearly as a duty God has given him.

Verse 4 gives the goal of the open door: that I may make it manifest. “It” is the mystery of Christ from verse 3. To make something manifest is to make it plain and visible. That fits everything Paul has said about the mystery. God has already revealed it to His saints (1:26), and Paul’s part is to bring it into the open for the people who hear him. What he asks for is clarity. The singular “I” narrows the focus to Paul’s own preaching.

The closing phrase, as I ought to speak, shows how Paul views this work. Speaking the mystery clearly is something he owes. Earlier he described his ministry as “the stewardship from God which was given to me for you, to fulfill the word of God” (1:25). A steward manages what belongs to someone else, and Paul handles the gospel as a trust from God. He asks the Colossians to pray that he will speak it the way it should be spoken: plainly, faithfully, and fully. The request also shows how much he depends on prayer. Even an apostle needs the prayers of a church he may never have visited in order to carry out the work God gave him.

Study Notes

Speaking ties verses 2–6 together. Paul asks for prayer so that he may “speak the mystery of Christ” and “make it manifest, as I ought to speak” (4:3–4). In verse 6 he turns to the believers’ own speech toward outsiders, with a matching aim: “that you may know how you ought to answer each one.” The repeated “ought” links Paul’s preaching with the church’s everyday conversation.

3. Colossians 4:5–6 — Wise Conduct and Gracious Speech Toward Outsiders

Colossians 4:5
Colossians 4:5
NKJV

Colossians 4:5 Walk in wisdom toward those who are outside, redeeming the time.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 4:5 Live wisely among those who are not believers, and make the most of every opportunity. (NLT)

Main Idea

Turning from his own preaching to the church’s witness, Paul tells believers to “Walk in wisdom toward those who are outside,” that is, toward people who do not belong to Christ. “Redeeming the time” means making full use of the opportunities those relationships give them, not letting them slip away.

Verse Explanation

Turning from his own preaching to the church’s witness, Paul tells believers to “Walk in wisdom toward those who are outside,” that is, toward people who do not belong to Christ. “Redeeming the time” means making full use of the opportunities those relationships give them, not letting them slip away.

Walk in wisdom — These words turn the focus from Paul’s preaching to the church’s own life. Paul has just asked the Colossians to pray that God would open a door for him to “speak the mystery of Christ” (4:3). Now he speaks about how they themselves live in front of others. To “walk” is to live, describing the steady pattern of a person’s conduct day after day. Paul has used this picture throughout the letter. He prayed that they would “walk worthy of the Lord” (1:10) and urged them, “so walk in Him” (2:6). Here that daily conduct is to be marked by wisdom. In Colossians, true wisdom is found in Christ, “in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (2:3). So Paul means more than general good manners. He means the practical good sense of people who belong to Christ: knowing how to act rightly in the real situations they face.

toward those who are outside — This phrase names the people this wise conduct is aimed at: those who do not belong to Christ. It marks a real line between the church and the people around it, yet Paul does not tell believers to withdraw behind that line. He tells them how to live toward the people on the other side of it. Their neighbors, relatives and fellow workers saw how Christians spoke, worked and treated others. The gospel Paul asked them to pray for (4:3–4) would be heard alongside the lives of the believers who already lived in Colosse.

redeeming the time — These words show what this wisdom looks like in practice. To redeem something is to buy it back or secure it. Paul pictures the time believers spend with outsiders as something valuable that must not be wasted. Every conversation, every shared task, every moment of need in a neighbor’s life is an opportunity that may not come again. Wise believers notice those opportunities and make full use of them instead of letting them slip by. In the life Paul describes, wisdom includes being alert to the moments God places in front of His people.

Study Notes

Wisdom comes up repeatedly in Colossians. Paul prays that the church will be filled “in all wisdom and spiritual understanding” (1:9). He teaches “every man in all wisdom” (1:28), says that all the treasures of wisdom are hidden in Christ (2:3), and dismisses rules that have only “an appearance of wisdom” (2:23). Against that background, “Walk in wisdom” in 4:5 refers to the genuine wisdom found in Christ, not its imitation.

Verses 5 and 6 pair conduct with speech: first how believers walk, then how they talk. The pairing recalls Paul’s earlier instruction, “whatever you do in word or deed, do all in the name of the Lord Jesus” (3:17). These two verses close the larger movement on the new life under Christ’s lordship that began at 3:1.

Colossians 4:6
Colossians 4:6
NKJV

Colossians 4:6 Let your speech always be with grace, seasoned with salt, that you may know how you ought to answer each one.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 4:6 Let your conversation be gracious and attractive so that you will have the right response for everyone. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul wants believers’ speech to “always be with grace, seasoned with salt,” so that it is kind and gracious yet also wholesome and worth hearing, never careless or empty. The purpose is “that you may know how you ought to answer each one,” giving a fitting answer to each person rather than one stock reply for everyone.

Verse Explanation

Paul wants believers’ speech to “always be with grace, seasoned with salt,” so that it is kind and gracious yet also wholesome and worth hearing, never careless or empty. The purpose is “that you may know how you ought to answer each one,” giving a fitting answer to each person rather than one stock reply for everyone.

Let your speech always be with grace — Paul now moves from the way believers live to the way they talk. The word “always” leaves no exceptions. Paul is not speaking only about moments when believers explain the gospel. He includes ordinary conversation, casual remarks and tense disagreements. Speech “with grace” is kind and considerate of the person hearing it. That fits people who “heard and knew the grace of God in truth” (1:6): those who have received God’s undeserved kindness should show the same kindness when they speak. Paul has already told the Colossians to put off “filthy language out of your mouth” (3:8). Here he describes the kind of speech that replaces it.

seasoned with salt — Paul adds a second quality. Salt gives food its flavor, and food without it tastes flat. In the same way, speech seasoned with salt is wholesome and worth hearing, never careless or empty. Grace and salt belong together in this verse. Grace keeps a believer’s words kind, and salt keeps them from becoming bland or pointless. Gracious words do not have to be dull, and words with substance do not have to be harsh.

that you may know how you ought to answer each one — This clause gives the purpose of that gracious, salted speech. The word “answer” pictures real conversations in which outsiders ask questions, raise objections or simply respond, and believers reply. “Ought” means that some answers fit better than others, so believers should look for the right one. “Each one” means that people differ. A skeptic, a grieving neighbor and a curious friend do not need the same words, and Paul does not hand out one stock reply for everyone. Knowing how to answer each person grows out of the habits Paul has just described: walking wisely toward outsiders and speaking with grace.

Study Notes

The NKJV uses the phrase “with grace” in 3:16 as well, where believers sing “with grace in your hearts to the Lord.” In 3:16 the phrase describes worship directed to the Lord, and in 4:6 it describes speech directed to outsiders.

The NLT reads “gracious and attractive” and “the right response for everyone.” It takes the salt image as speech that draws people in, while the NKJV keeps the image itself. The NKJV’s “each one” also keeps the focus on individual people, one at a time.

4. Colossians 4:7–9 — Tychicus and Onesimus Sent to Colosse

Colossians 4:7
Colossians 4:7
NKJV

Colossians 4:7 Tychicus, a beloved brother, faithful minister, and fellow servant in the Lord, will tell you all the news about me.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 4:7 Tychicus will give you a full report about how I am getting along. He is a beloved brother and faithful helper who serves with me in the Lord’s work. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul commends Tychicus as “a beloved brother, faithful minister, and fellow servant in the Lord,” so the church will receive him as someone Paul trusts and loves. Tychicus will tell them all the news about Paul, and the Colossians will hear firsthand how the imprisoned apostle is doing.

Verse Explanation

Paul commends Tychicus as “a beloved brother, faithful minister, and fellow servant in the Lord,” so the church will receive him as someone Paul trusts and loves. Tychicus will tell them all the news about Paul, and the Colossians will hear firsthand how the imprisoned apostle is doing.

Tychicus — With this name the letter moves from teaching to its closing. Paul’s instructions for the new life under Christ’s lordship ended with gracious speech toward outsiders in 4:6. Now he introduces the man he is sending to Colosse. Colossians does not say in so many words that Tychicus carried the letter, but he is traveling to the church with Paul’s news, so he most likely did. Paul names him first because the Colossians are about to meet him in person.

a beloved brother, faithful minister, and fellow servant in the Lord — Paul gives Tychicus three descriptions, and each tells the church something different about him. As a “beloved brother” he belongs to the same family of faith as the Colossians and has a place in Paul’s affection. As a “faithful minister” he has proved trustworthy in service. Here “minister” means someone who serves, which is why the NLT renders the phrase “faithful helper.” As a “fellow servant” he works beside Paul under the same Lord instead of serving Paul as a private assistant. The closing words “in the Lord” place their shared work under Christ, whom both men serve. Together these descriptions recommend Tychicus to the church: he comes with Paul’s full trust and love.

will tell you all the news about me — Paul says little in the letter about his own situation beyond his chains (4:3, 18). He leaves the fuller account to Tychicus, who will speak to the church face to face. The Colossians will receive Paul’s teaching in writing, and they will also hear firsthand how the imprisoned apostle is doing from someone who has been with him.

Study Notes

Two of Paul’s descriptions of Tychicus repeat words he used earlier for Epaphras, whom he called “our dear fellow servant” and “a faithful minister of Christ on your behalf” (1:7). The church first learned the gospel from a man described in these terms, and Paul now commends Tychicus with the same words.

Colossians 4:8
Colossians 4:8
NKJV

Colossians 4:8 I am sending him to you for this very purpose, that he may know your circumstances and comfort your hearts,(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 4:8 I have sent him to you for this very purpose — to let you know how we are doing and to encourage you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul says he is sending Tychicus “for this very purpose,” so that he may know the church’s circumstances and comfort their hearts. Tychicus will bring Paul’s news to Colosse, learn how the church is doing, and strengthen the believers there.

Verse Explanation

Paul says he is sending Tychicus “for this very purpose,” so that he may know the church’s circumstances and comfort their hearts. Tychicus will bring Paul’s news to Colosse, learn how the church is doing, and strengthen the believers there.

I am sending him to you for this very purpose — Paul states plainly why Tychicus is coming. The words “this very purpose” point ahead to the two aims that follow and show that the journey was deliberate. Paul is sending Tychicus to Colosse for the church’s sake.

that he may know your circumstances — The first aim is for Tychicus to learn how the Colossians are doing, so news will travel in both directions. Paul has already heard about the church through Epaphras (1:7–8), and he has written to guard them against teaching that would draw them away from Christ (2:4, 8). Tychicus will see the church’s situation for himself and can bring word back to Paul. Paul’s care for these believers continues beyond the letter.

and comfort your hearts — The second aim is for Tychicus to strengthen the believers inwardly. This purpose echoes Paul’s earlier concern “that their hearts may be encouraged, being knit together in love” (2:2). Paul has worked for that encouragement in prayer and in writing, and now he provides it in person through a brother he trusts.

Study Notes

The NLT words the middle of this verse differently: “to let you know how we are doing and to encourage you.” In the NLT the Colossians learn about Paul and his companions. In the NKJV Tychicus learns about the Colossians. Either way, Paul’s news still reaches the church, because verses 7 and 9 say so directly, and both translations include the encouragement Tychicus brings.

In the NKJV the word “comfort” appears again in 4:11, where Aristarchus, Mark, and Justus “have proved to be a comfort” to Paul. In this closing section comfort goes both ways: Paul sends it to Colosse through Tychicus, and his companions give it to Paul in his imprisonment.

Colossians 4:9
Colossians 4:9
NKJV

Colossians 4:9 with Onesimus, a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you. They will make known to you all things which are happening here.(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 4:9 I am also sending Onesimus, a faithful and beloved brother, one of your own people. He and Tychicus will tell you everything that’s happening here. (NLT)

Main Idea

Onesimus travels with Tychicus. Paul presents him as “a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you,” and he is the same Onesimus whom Paul asks Philemon to receive as a beloved brother (Philemon 10–16). By giving this enslaved man full standing among the Colossian believers, Paul shows the unity in Christ described in 3:11 in a real person, and he sends both men to report everything happening where Paul is.

Verse Explanation

Onesimus travels with Tychicus. Paul presents him as “a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you,” and he is the same Onesimus whom Paul asks Philemon to receive as a beloved brother (Philemon 10–16). By giving this enslaved man full standing among the Colossian believers, Paul shows the unity in Christ described in 3:11 in a real person, and he sends both men to report everything happening where Paul is.

with Onesimus — Tychicus does not travel alone. Paul adds Onesimus to the same sentence about sending Tychicus, so the two men come to Colosse together. Read alongside Philemon 10–16, this is the same Onesimus whom Paul asks Philemon to receive no longer simply as an enslaved man but as a beloved brother. Neither letter spells out exactly how Onesimus came to be separated from his master and to be with Paul. He is often described as a runaway, but that description is an inference. Paul’s own words about him in this verse are the surest guide to how the church should see him.

a faithful and beloved brother — Paul describes Onesimus with the same words he has just used for Tychicus: “faithful” and “beloved brother.” He does not present one man as an honored coworker and the other as a lesser companion. Paul says nothing here about Onesimus’s earlier circumstances. He speaks of who Onesimus is now: a brother whom Paul loves and has found trustworthy.

who is one of you — Onesimus is a Colossian, and Paul presents him to the church as one of its own members. The believers have just heard Paul’s instructions to bondservants and masters (3:22–4:1), and earlier they heard that in the new humanity there is neither “slave nor free, but Christ is all and in all” (3:11). Now this enslaved man from their own city comes to them as a brother Paul calls one of them. The unity Paul described becomes visible in a real person whom the church would soon welcome.

They will make known to you all things which are happening here — “They” includes both men. Onesimus is more than a traveling companion: Paul trusts him with the report about Paul’s situation just as he trusts Tychicus. “Here” is the place where Paul is held in chains (4:3, 18), most likely Rome. The church will hear Paul’s news from two witnesses who have been with him.

Study Notes

Paul uses the words “who is one of you” again in 4:12 for Epaphras, the Colossian who first taught the church the gospel (1:7). The repeated phrase places Onesimus beside a respected member of the congregation as a Colossian whom Paul commends.

Paul’s description of Onesimus also echoes the letter’s opening address to “the saints and faithful brethren in Christ who are in Colosse” (1:2). The words Paul used for the whole church at the beginning of the letter he now applies to this one returning member.

Verses 7 and 9 frame this short section with the promise of news. Tychicus “will tell you all the news about me” (4:7), and the two men together “will make known to you all things which are happening here” (4:9). Verse 8, placed between these two statements, gives the purpose of the visit.

5. Colossians 4:10–14 — Greetings from Paul’s Fellow Workers

Colossians 4:10
Colossians 4:10
NKJV

Colossians 4:10 Aristarchus my fellow prisoner greets you, with Mark the cousin of Barnabas (about whom you received instructions: if he comes to you, welcome him),(NKJV)

NLT

Colossians 4:10 Aristarchus, who is in prison with me, sends you his greetings, and so does Mark, Barnabas’s cousin. As you were instructed before, make Mark welcome if he comes your way. (NLT)

Main Idea

The greetings begin with Aristarchus, whom Paul calls “my fellow prisoner” because he shares Paul’s confinement. Mark “the cousin of Barnabas” also sends greetings, and Paul reminds the church of instructions they have already received about Mark: “if he comes to you, welcome him.”

Verse Explanation

The greetings begin with Aristarchus, whom Paul calls “my fellow prisoner” because he shares Paul’s confinement. Mark “the cousin of Barnabas” also sends greetings, and Paul reminds the church of instructions they have already received about Mark: “if he comes to you, welcome him.”

Aristarchus my fellow prisoner greets you — After commending the two men who will carry news to Colosse (4:7–9), Paul passes along greetings from the companions who are with him, and Aristarchus comes first. Paul calls him “my fellow prisoner” because Aristarchus shares Paul’s confinement. Paul is in chains because of “the mystery of Christ” (4:3), and Aristarchus is bound with him, not simply visiting. His greeting comes from a man who is bearing the same cost for the gospel that Paul bears.

with Mark the cousin of Barnabas — Mark joins the greeting. Paul identifies him through his family tie to Barnabas, which suggests that the Colossians knew Barnabas by name and would place Mark through that connection.

about whom you received instructions — Paul pauses to remind the church that they have already been told how to treat Mark. He does not repeat everything they heard. He points them back to what they already know. if he comes to you, welcome him — The heart of those instructions is simple: if Mark reaches Colosse, the church is to receive him warmly. The “if” leaves his visit uncertain, but Paul wants the welcome settled in advance, so that Mark will find a ready reception whenever he arrives.

Study Notes

In 4:10–14 the greetings come from the men who are with Paul, and the NKJV marks them with “greets you” or “greet you” (4:10, 12, 14). and Jesus who is called Justus — The sentence that began in verse 10 continues here, so Justus is the third man in the same greeting with Aristarchus and Mark. who are of the circumcision — This phrase refers to Jewish believers. Among the coworkers with Paul at that time, these three alone were Jewish. Paul is describing the people around him as he writes. they have proved to be a comfort to me — These men have strengthened Paul during his imprisonment, and the words “have proved to be” point to comfort shown in practice over time. Paul calls Aristarchus, Mark, and Justus his only coworkers “of the circumcision.” That suggests the companions named after them, Epaphras, Luke, and Demas, were not Jewish. The verse does not say so directly. The conclusion comes from the way Paul groups the names.

Epaphras, who is one of you — Epaphras comes from Colosse. The Colossians first learned the gospel from him, and Paul earlier called him “our dear fellow servant” and “a faithful minister of Christ on your behalf” (1:7). He is now with Paul, far from the church he first taught, so this greeting comes from someone the Colossians know well.

a bondservant of Christ — Paul describes Epaphras as a man who belongs wholly to Christ and lives in His service. The rest of the verse shows what that service looks like: steady, demanding prayer for Christ’s people.

greets you, always laboring fervently for you in prayers — Distance has not ended Epaphras’s care for the church. He cannot be with them, so he serves them through prayer, and Paul describes that prayer as hard, persistent work. “Always” shows that it does not stop, and “laboring fervently” shows that it costs him real effort. Epaphras is doing what Paul asked of the whole church a few verses earlier: “Continue earnestly in prayer” (4:2).

that you may stand perfect and complete in all the will of God — Here is what Epaphras asks for. He wants the Colossians to “stand,” holding firm instead of being moved. “Perfect” here means mature, fully grown in faith, and “complete” means fully settled. “In all the will of God” covers everything God wills for them. Paul has already told them that they are “complete in Him” (2:10), so Epaphras is not praying for something Christ has failed to give. He prays that people who already have fullness in Christ will grow to maturity and stand firm in everything God wills.

Study Notes

Paul describes Epaphras with the same words, “who is one of you,” that he used for Onesimus three verses earlier (4:9). Both men belonged to the Colossian church.

Epaphras’s prayer matches Paul’s own aim. Paul labors “that we may present every man perfect in Christ Jesus” (1:28), and Epaphras prays that the Colossians “may stand perfect.”

The NLT gives the request as “asking God to make you strong and perfect, fully confident that you are following the whole will of God.” The NKJV speaks of standing “perfect and complete.” The NLT expresses the second part of the request as settled confidence about doing God’s will.

Greet the brethren who are in Laodicea — Until now the greetings have come from the men with Paul, each one sending word that he “greets you” (4:10–14). Here Paul speaks for himself and gives the Colossians a task: they are to pass his greeting on to the believers in nearby Laodicea. “Brethren” means fellow believers, the brothers and sisters of one family in Christ. By asking Colosse to carry his greeting to the next congregation, Paul treats the two churches as partners who stay in touch and care for one another. Epaphras’s zeal already reaches both of them (4:13).

and Nymphas and the church that is in his house — Paul also greets one believer by name, along with the congregation that gathered in that home. The believers in this region met in homes, and this household opened its doors so the church had a place to worship, pray, and hear letters like this one read aloud. Paul greets the host and the congregation together, because the house was the church’s gathering place as well as a family home.

The NKJV gives a man’s name, Nymphas, and “his house,” following the manuscripts behind its translation. Other ancient manuscripts read Nympha, a woman’s name, and “her house.” Whether the host was a man or a woman depends on which reading is followed. In either reading, the verse shows a congregation meeting in a believer’s home and Paul greeting its host by name.

Laodicea appears earlier in the letter as well. Paul includes “those in Laodicea” in the “great conflict” he has for the believers (2:1), and he names them again among those Epaphras cares for (4:13). Paul tells the Colossians that once “this epistle is read among you,” they should have it read in the church of the Laodiceans too, and they should “likewise read the epistle from Laodicea.” The churches were to share apostolic letters. The words “from Laodicea” say where the Colossians would receive that other letter, not who it was first written to, and the letter itself remains unidentified.

Now when this epistle is read among you — “This epistle” is the letter to the Colossians itself. Paul expects it to be read aloud when the church gathers. The whole congregation would hear his teaching about Christ together, not each person alone. Everything in the letter, from Christ’s fullness to the instructions for the household, was meant to be received by the church as one body.

see that it is read also in the church of the Laodiceans — Paul makes the Colossians responsible for passing the letter on. The words “see that” show that this is not a casual suggestion: they are to make sure the reading happens. The warnings against empty philosophy and human regulations, and the call to live the new life under Christ’s lordship, apply to the neighboring congregation as well.

and that you likewise read the epistle from Laodicea — The sharing goes both ways. Another letter would come to Colosse from Laodicea, and the Colossians were to read it as carefully as the Laodiceans would read theirs. The words “from Laodicea” tell where the Colossians would receive that letter. They do not say to whom it was first sent, and Colossians does not describe it any further. Paul’s instruction shows the churches treating apostolic letters as words for the wider family of believers, to be read and passed from one congregation to the next.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the last clause “you should read the letter I wrote to them,” which presents the letter as one Paul sent to Laodicea. The NKJV’s “the epistle from Laodicea” stays closer to what the verse actually states: it tells where the Colossians would obtain the letter, and it leaves the letter’s original destination open.

Because Colossians does not describe this letter, its identity remains unknown. One proposal is that it was a letter of Paul’s that circulated among several churches, possibly the one known as Ephesians. Another is that it was a letter that has not been preserved. Both remain proposals, and the verse itself does not settle the question.

Paul has the church pass on a personal charge to Archippus: “Take heed to the ministry which you have received in the Lord, that you may fulfill it.” Archippus’s ministry is a trust he received from the Lord, and he hears in front of the whole church that he must carry it through to completion.

And say to Archippus — Paul ends his instructions to the church with a word for one man, but he does not send it privately. The church itself is to deliver Paul’s message, so Archippus hears the charge with his fellow believers listening. Colossians tells us nothing more about who Archippus was. What matters in this verse is the charge he receives.

Take heed to the ministry — To “take heed” is to pay close attention and to keep watch over something. Here the thing to watch over is Archippus’s “ministry,” the service entrusted to him. Paul does not describe what that service involved, but he speaks of it as something that needs Archippus’s careful and continuing attention.

which you have received in the Lord — Archippus received this ministry, and he received it “in the Lord.” His service comes from Christ and belongs within his life with Christ, so he carries it out as someone who answers to the Lord who gave it.

that you may fulfill it — The goal of the charge is completion. Receiving a ministry is only the beginning, and Paul wants Archippus to carry it all the way through. Because the church hears the charge with him, his fellow believers know what he has been entrusted with and what he has been called to finish.

Study Notes

In the NKJV, “fulfill” is the same English verb Paul used for his own calling earlier in the letter, when he described his stewardship from God “to fulfill the word of God” (1:25). In the English wording, at least, the charge to Archippus repeats the language Paul used for his own ministry.

The NLT translates the clause as “the ministry the Lord gave you,” which makes the Lord the giver explicitly. The NKJV’s “received in the Lord” is broader: it can name the Lord as the source of the ministry and can also describe the relationship with Him in which Archippus received it and carries it out. Both renderings fit the verse.

Colossians does not explain why Paul sent this charge or describe Archippus’s circumstances. The verse gives a command and its purpose, but it does not report any failure on Archippus’s part.

Verse Explanation

Paul takes the pen and writes the closing greeting himself, “by my own hand,” which suggests that someone else wrote down the rest of the letter. In his own hand he asks, “Remember my chains,” echoing his mention of his chains in 4:3, and he closes with “Grace be with you,” so the letter ends with grace just as it began (1:2).

This salutation by my own hand—Paul — A salutation is a greeting, and Paul now writes this closing greeting in his own handwriting and signs his name to it. Saying that this part is “by my own hand” suggests that someone else wrote out the rest of the letter while Paul supplied its words. Many believers in the region had never seen Paul’s face (2:1), yet the final lines reach them directly from the apostle himself. His signature puts his personal name to everything the letter has said, and it means that the request that follows comes straight from him.

Remember my chains — Paul writes as a prisoner, probably in Rome. A few verses earlier he spoke of “the mystery of Christ, for which I am also in chains” (4:3), so these chains are the price of the message he preaches. To remember his chains is to keep his imprisonment in mind and not forget the man who bears it. Paul does not spell out everything he means by this short request. In the same chapter, though, he has already asked the church to pray for him and for an open door for the word (4:3), so remembering his chains certainly includes carrying him and his ministry to God in prayer.

Grace be with you — Paul’s last wish for the church is grace, the free and undeserved kindness of God. The letter opened with “Grace to you” (1:2), and it closes the same way, so everything in between is framed by grace. That frame fits the letter’s teaching: the Colossians are “complete in Him” (2:10) because of what God has freely given in Christ, not because of anything they have added. Paul cannot come to them in his chains, but he can ask that God’s grace stay with the believers he has been addressing since 1:2.

Amen — In the NKJV the letter ends with “Amen,” a word of firm agreement that confirms the blessing Paul has just spoken. Paul’s final word is not a demand but a confident yes to the grace he has wished for the church.

Study Notes

The NLT renders “by my own hand” as “IN MY OWN HANDWRITING,” which makes clear that Paul is describing the physical act of writing these words, not only giving his approval to them. Colossians does not name the person who wrote out the rest of the letter.

Paul is not the only prisoner in the chapter’s closing greetings. He calls Aristarchus “my fellow prisoner” (4:10), so at least one of the companions sending greetings shares his confinement.

The NLT reads “May God’s grace be with you,” naming God as the source of the grace. The NKJV’s shorter “Grace be with you” leaves the source unstated in this verse, but the letter’s opening greeting identifies it as grace “from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ” (1:2).

The NKJV ends the verse with “Amen,” while the NLT ends with the blessing and has no “Amen.”
