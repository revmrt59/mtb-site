Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 4
Main Idea:

Christ’s lordship reaches the master in the household and carries into watchful prayer and gracious speech toward outsiders, and the letter closes among faithful coworkers whose relationships show the gospel at work.

Setting:

Paul writes in chains (4:3, 18), most likely from Rome in the early AD 60s. Verses 1–6 complete the teaching on the new life under Christ’s lordship that began at 3:1, and verses 7–18 bring the whole letter to a close.

Chapter Outline:

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Colossians 4 opens in the middle of a thought. The chapter break falls between Paul’s words to bondservants and his words to their masters, so 4:1 completes the instruction begun at 3:22. Those who were “masters according to the flesh” (3:22) are told, “you also have a Master in heaven.” The Lord who rules every power in heaven also sees how a master treats the enslaved people in his household, and He requires what is just and fair.

From the household Paul turns to prayer and speech, which still belong to the new life he has been describing since 3:1. He asks the church for persistent, alert, thankful prayer, and then for prayer on his own behalf. Though he is “in chains” because of “the mystery of Christ,” Paul asks God for an open door for the word and for clarity in speaking it, and he says nothing about his own release. The believers have their own part as well: they are to walk wisely among outsiders and answer each person with grace.

The closing names show the letter’s teaching lived out in real people. Comfort moves in both directions: Paul sends Tychicus, a “faithful minister,” to “comfort your hearts” (4:7–8), while Aristarchus, Mark, and Justus, his only Jewish coworkers, have proved “a comfort” to Paul himself in his imprisonment (4:10–11). Epaphras prays that the Colossians will “stand perfect and complete” (4:12), though Paul has already told them they are “complete in Him” (2:10). Being complete in Christ is where growth begins. Onesimus, read alongside Philemon 10–16, is the enslaved man whom Paul asks Philemon to receive as a beloved brother. Here he returns to Colosse as “a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you,” and the truth that “Christ is all and in all” (3:11) takes a human face.

Grace frames the whole letter. Paul opened with “Grace to you” (1:2), and he now takes the pen himself to close with “Grace be with you” (4:18). Everything he has taught about Christ’s fullness, the believers’ completeness in Him, and their new life rests on what God freely gives, and Paul’s chains do not keep him from offering that grace to the church.

Chapter Sections

Colossians 4:1 — Masters Who Answer to a Master in Heaven

Paul finishes the household pair begun at 3:22 by telling masters to give their bondservants, the enslaved people in their households, “what is just and fair.” Masters are not the final authority in the house, because they answer to the same Lord their bondservants serve.

Colossians 4:2–4 — Watchful, Thankful Prayer for an Open Door

Paul calls the church to steady, alert prayer full of thanksgiving, and he asks them to pray for him and his companions as well. Though he is in chains, he asks that God “would open to us a door for the word,” so that he can make the mystery of Christ plain, “as I ought to speak.”

Colossians 4:5–6 — Wise Conduct and Gracious Speech Toward Outsiders

Turning from his own preaching to the church’s witness, Paul tells believers to live wisely among people who do not belong to Christ and to make full use of the opportunities those relationships give them. Their speech is to be gracious yet also wholesome and worth hearing, “seasoned with salt,” so that they can give a fitting answer to each person.

Colossians 4:7–9 — Tychicus and Onesimus Sent to Colosse

Paul sends Tychicus, a coworker he trusts and loves, to bring news about Paul, learn how the church is doing, and “comfort your hearts.” Onesimus travels with him, and Paul presents this enslaved man to his home church as “a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you.”

Colossians 4:10–14 — Greetings from Paul’s Fellow Workers

Aristarchus, Paul’s fellow prisoner, sends greetings with Mark and Justus, and these three, the only Jewish believers among Paul’s coworkers, have been a comfort to him. Epaphras sends greetings while he labors in prayer for the Colossians’ maturity, and Luke the beloved physician and Demas add their greetings too.

Colossians 4:15–17 — Greetings to Laodicea, an Exchange of Letters, and a Charge to Archippus

Paul sends his own greetings to the believers in Laodicea and to a church that meets in a believer’s home, and he tells the Colossians to have this letter read in Laodicea and to read “the epistle from Laodicea,” a letter whose identity remains unknown. He then has the church pass on a personal charge to Archippus to fulfill the ministry he has “received in the Lord.”

Colossians 4:18 — Paul’s Signature and Final Request

Paul takes the pen and signs the greeting “by my own hand,” which suggests that someone else wrote down the rest of the letter. He asks the church to “Remember my chains,” echoing 4:3, and closes with grace, just as the letter began.

Why This Chapter Matters

Colossians 4 shows that Christ’s lordship has practical consequences for anyone who holds power over others. Masters are measured by justice and fairness because they stand before a Lord who sees what happens inside their homes.

The chapter also places the spread of the gospel in the hands of the whole church. Paul is the apostle, yet he depends on the Colossians’ prayers for an open door. Their own wise conduct and gracious answers carry the message to the people around them in Colosse.

Finally, the chapter shows what Christ’s preeminence produces among His people. Jewish coworkers comfort Paul in his mission among Gentiles, an enslaved man is welcomed as a brother, and neighboring churches share letters, greetings, and prayer. The Christ who holds all things together also holds His people together.

Key Themes to Watch For

Every master under the Master (4:1; 3:22–25). The command to masters completes the household instructions and places those with authority under the same Lord as those who serve them.

Watchful, thankful prayer (4:2–4; 4:12). Paul calls for persistent, alert prayer joined with thanksgiving. Epaphras shows what that looks like as he labors in prayer for the church he first taught.

Speaking the mystery and speaking with grace (4:3–6). Paul prays for clear words to make Christ known, and the church is to answer outsiders with words that are gracious and wise.

Faithful partners in the gospel (4:7–17). Paul’s coworkers are called faithful, beloved, a comfort, and full of zeal. Archippus is reminded that his ministry is a trust he received from the Lord.

Grace from first to last (4:6, 18; 1:2). Believers’ speech is to be “with grace,” and Paul’s final words, “Grace be with you,” answer the “Grace to you” that opened the letter.

Key Words & Concepts

Bondservants (4:1). In the household instructions, “bondservants” means enslaved people. Paul speaks directly to their masters and places them under “a Master in heaven,” so a master’s authority in the house is never the final word.

“A door for the word” and “the mystery of Christ” (4:3). A door for the word is an opportunity to speak the gospel. The mystery of Christ is God’s purpose, once hidden and now made known in Christ, including His presence among Gentile believers (1:26–27). Paul wants to make that purpose plain to everyone; it is not secret knowledge kept for a few.

“Redeeming the time” (4:5). Making full use of the opportunities that relationships with outsiders provide, instead of letting them slip away.

“Seasoned with salt” (4:6). Speech that is wholesome and worth hearing as well as gracious, never careless or empty.

“Of the circumcision” (4:11). Jewish believers. Aristarchus, Mark, and Justus were the only Jewish believers among the coworkers with Paul.

“The epistle from Laodicea” (4:16). A letter the Colossians were to receive from the church in Laodicea. The words “from Laodicea” tell where they would get it. They do not say who it was first written to, and the letter’s identity remains unknown.
