Book Overview

At a Glance

Hebrews
Author

Not named. The writer includes himself among those to whom the Lord’s message “was confirmed to us by those who heard Him” (2:3), and he knew Timothy (13:23).

Audience

A congregation of believers, most likely of Jewish background, who knew Israel’s Scriptures well and had already suffered for their faith (10:32–34). Their location is not stated.

Date

Most likely before AD 70, while the priests were still offering the sacrifices of the law (10:11). The exact year is uncertain.

Historical Setting

Hebrews explains Jesus through Israel’s worship: the tabernacle Moses built, the priesthood of Aaron, the yearly Day of Atonement, and the priest-king Melchizedek. The hearers had endured public reproach and loss, and some were growing weary.

Purpose

To show that God has spoken His final word in His Son and that the Son has opened the way into God’s presence, so that tired believers will draw near to God through Jesus, hold fast their confession, and not turn back.

Central Message

God, who once spoke to the fathers by the prophets, “has in these last days spoken to us by His Son” (1:2). The Son made purification for sins, shared our flesh and blood, and became “a merciful and faithful High Priest” (2:17). He offered Himself once for all, and “He always lives to make intercession” for those who come to God through Him (7:25). Because He has opened “a new and living way” into God’s presence (10:20), His people can come boldly to the throne of grace, hold fast their confession, and run their race “looking unto Jesus” (12:2). The warnings of Hebrews carry the same message from the other side: there is no one else to turn to, so God’s people must not refuse “Him who speaks” (12:25).

Key Verse

“Let us therefore come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need.” (4:16)

Key Themes

God speaking in His Son; Jesus our merciful High Priest; the better covenant and the once-for-all offering; drawing near to God; hearing God “Today” and entering His rest; faith that endures; warnings against drifting and turning away; love and worship in the community of faith.

Book Flow

  • I. God’s Son and the Great Salvation He Brings (1:1–2:18): the Son above the angels, made like His brethren

  • II. Hear God’s Voice and Enter His Rest Through Faith (3:1–4:13): Jesus over Moses; the “Today” of Psalm 95

  • III. Jesus’ Perfect Priesthood and the Better Covenant (4:14–10:18): the sympathetic High Priest, Melchizedek, and the once-for-all offering

  • IV. Draw Near and Endure by Faith (10:19–12:29): boldness to enter, the witnesses of faith, and the unshakable kingdom

  • V. Faithful Conduct and the Closing Appeal (13:1–13:25): love, worship, and the prayer to the God of peace

Go Deeper

Background

Author

Hebrews never names its writer. It opens without a greeting. The writer speaks to his hearers personally along the way (5:11; 6:9–12), but the personal particulars come at the close, where he asks for prayer, mentions “our brother Timothy,” and hopes to see his hearers soon (13:18–19, 23). A few details about him still come through. He includes himself among those to whom the message of salvation “was confirmed to us by those who heard Him” (2:3). This suggests that he received the testimony of people who had heard the Lord Himself. He was also a gifted preacher who knew Israel’s Scriptures deeply and handled them with great care.

Over the centuries several names have been proposed, among them Paul, Barnabas, Luke, and Apollos, but none has won general agreement (see Helpful Notes). The uncertainty takes nothing from the book’s authority. Hebrews begins with God as the One speaking, and the human writer stays in the background so that God’s word in the Son holds the reader’s attention from the first sentence.

Audience

The title To the Hebrews is very old, though it was added to the book rather than written by its author. The contents fit the title well. The writer builds his whole argument from Israel’s Scriptures and assumes his hearers already know the tabernacle, the priesthood, the sacrifices, Moses, Aaron, and Melchizedek. The audience was most likely a congregation of Jewish believers in Jesus, though Gentile believers may have been among them. Where they lived is not stated. The greeting “Those from Italy greet you” (13:24) could mean that the writer was in Italy, or that believers from Italy who were away from home were sending greetings back, perhaps to Rome.

These believers had a history of courage. “After you were illuminated,” the writer reminds them, “you endured a great struggle with sufferings” (10:32). They were publicly shamed, they stood with others who were mistreated, and they “joyfully accepted the plundering of your goods” (10:33–34). They had served other believers and were still doing so (6:10). Yet the same people had become “dull of hearing” (5:11), some had stopped gathering with the church (10:25), and many were weary (12:3, 12). Hebrews speaks to faithful people who were tired, under pressure, and in danger of drifting.

Date

Hebrews was most likely written before AD 70, the year the Romans destroyed the temple in Jerusalem. The writer speaks of the old priestly service as still going on: “every priest stands ministering daily” (10:11), and the law’s sacrifices are offered “continually year by year” (10:1). He also says the old order “is becoming obsolete and growing old” and “is ready to vanish away” (8:13). If the temple had already fallen, he would very likely have pointed to it as evidence for his argument. These features fit a date before AD 70, though they fall short of proof, because Hebrews describes the tabernacle of Moses’ day rather than the temple itself.

Two other details fit that range. Timothy was still active and had recently been “set free” (13:23). A letter written by the church in Rome near the end of the first century, known as 1 Clement, appears to echo Hebrews, which would place the book earlier than that.

Historical Setting

Hebrews explains Jesus through Israel’s worship, so readers need a picture of that worship. At Mount Sinai God gave Israel His law and instructions for a portable sanctuary, the tabernacle. Its first room held the lampstand, the table, and the showbread. Behind a second veil lay “the Holiest of All,” where the ark of the covenant stood beneath “the cherubim of glory” (9:2–5). Priests descended from Aaron served in the first room every day. Only once a year, on the Day of Atonement, did the high priest pass behind the veil, and then “not without blood” (9:7; Leviticus 16). The whole arrangement taught Israel two things at once: God truly dwelt among His people, and sinful people could not simply walk into His presence.

Hebrews also draws on two moments in Israel’s history. The first is Melchizedek, who appears briefly in Genesis as “king of Salem” and “the priest of God Most High.” He blessed Abram and received a tenth of the spoils of battle (Genesis 14:18–20). Centuries later, Psalm 110:4 announced that God’s chosen King would be “a priest forever According to the order of Melchizedek.” The second is the wilderness generation. Israel came out of Egypt, heard God’s voice, and saw His works, yet refused to enter Canaan when the moment came (Numbers 14). Psalm 95 later used that failure to warn God’s people, and Hebrews quotes the psalm at length: “Today, if you will hear His voice, Do not harden your hearts” (3:7–8; Psalm 95:7–11).

Occasion

Hebrews was prompted by spiritual danger rather than by a single event the book records. The writer sees believers who are slipping. “We must give the more earnest heed to the things we have heard,” he warns, “lest we drift away” (2:1). His hearers ought to have been teachers by now, yet they needed someone to teach them “the first principles of the oracles of God” again (5:12). Some were neglecting to meet with the church (10:25), and some were tired enough to give up (12:3, 12–13). He also warns them not to be “carried about with various and strange doctrines,” especially teaching about foods (13:9).

The writer never says exactly what his hearers were tempted to turn back to. His long comparison of Jesus with the angels, Moses, Aaron, and the old sacrifices suggests that some were drawn toward the familiar worship of the first covenant, perhaps to escape the reproach that came with following Jesus (13:13). That reconstruction fits the book well, though it remains a reconstruction. The writer’s answer is clear. He sets Jesus before them in all His greatness, and then urges them on. He calls what he has written “the word of exhortation” (13:22), a sermon of encouragement and warning sent to people he hoped to see again soon.

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Hebrews is about Jesus, the Son in whom God has spoken His final word, and about a people who must decide whether they will keep listening. The book moves back and forth between teaching and appeal. A passage about who Jesus is gives way to a “therefore” and a call to respond, and then the teaching resumes (2:1; 3:1; 4:14; 10:19; 12:1). The appeals are not an add-on to the teaching. They are the reason it was preached.

The book opens with the Son’s greatness. He is “heir of all things,” the One through whom God “made the worlds,” and “the brightness of His glory and the express image of His person” (1:2–3). After He “purged our sins,” He sat down at God’s right hand. Then Hebrews turns to His humility. He “was made a little lower than the angels” so that “by the grace of God” He “might taste death for everyone” (2:9). He shared our flesh and blood to break the devil’s power and to set free those who had lived all their lives in fear of death (2:14–15).

Much of Hebrews explains Jesus as High Priest. He does not come from Aaron’s family but serves “according to the order of Melchizedek,” appointed by God’s oath and holding His office “according to the power of an endless life” (7:16). Hebrews sets the earthly tabernacle beside “the true tabernacle which the Lord erected” (8:2), and the repeated animal sacrifices beside Jesus’ single offering of Himself. At the center of the comparison stands Jeremiah’s promise of a new covenant, in which God writes His laws on His people’s hearts and remembers their sins no more (8:8–12; 10:16–17).

Serious warnings run through the book, each joined to encouragement: against drifting (2:1–4), against hardened and unbelieving hearts (3:7–4:13), against falling away (5:11–6:8), against willful sin after receiving the truth (10:26–31), and against refusing the One who speaks from heaven (12:25–29). Chapter 11 gathers a long line of men and women who lived “by faith,” some rescued and some who suffered and died still trusting God. The final chapter brings that faith into ordinary life: love, hospitality, purity, contentment, worship, and generosity.

Why This Book Matters

Without Hebrews, the church would understand far less about what Jesus is doing now. The Gospels tell of His death and resurrection, and Acts tells of His ascension. Hebrews follows Him into “heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God for us” (9:24). It is the only New Testament book that calls Jesus our High Priest. It shows Him seated at God’s right hand because His sacrifice is finished, and yet still at work, because “He always lives to make intercession” for those who come to God through Him (7:25). Believers come to the Father through a High Priest who knows human weakness from the inside and was still “without sin” (4:15).

Hebrews also teaches the church how to read the Old Testament. The tabernacle, the priesthood, the sacrifices, and the first covenant were all given by God, and Hebrews treats them with respect. It also calls them “the copy and shadow of the heavenly things” (8:5), real but pointing beyond themselves to Christ. Read after Hebrews, the book of Leviticus is no longer a puzzle of rituals. It becomes a long preparation for what Jesus would do.

Behind all of it stands God’s desire to dwell with His people. In the tabernacle, the veil told Israel that “the way into the Holiest of All was not yet made manifest” (9:8). In Hebrews that way stands open. Believers have “boldness to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus” (10:19), and the invitation reaches every one of them, including the weak and the struggling.

Finally, Hebrews speaks to tired believers. It was written to people who had started well and were wearing down, and the writer will neither flatter them nor give up on them. Its warnings are among the most serious in the Bible, and its encouragements among the warmest. Both come from the same concern: that no one would “fall short of the grace of God” (12:15).

Book Flow

I. God’s Son and the Great Salvation He Brings (1:1–2:18)

Hebrews opens without a greeting. God once spoke “at various times and in various ways” through the prophets, but He “has in these last days spoken to us by His Son” (1:1–2). A chain of Old Testament quotations shows the Son far above the angels, who are “ministering spirits sent forth to minister for those who will inherit salvation” (1:14). The first warning follows: if the word spoken through angels was binding, “how shall we escape if we neglect so great a salvation” (2:3)? Hebrews then reads Psalm 8 and sees Jesus, made lower than the angels and “for the suffering of death crowned with glory and honor” (2:9). The movement ends with the reason for His humiliation. He “had to be made like His brethren, that He might be a merciful and faithful High Priest” (2:17), and that title introduces the theme the rest of the book develops.

II. Hear God’s Voice and Enter His Rest Through Faith (3:1–4:13)

The writer calls the “holy brethren” to “consider the Apostle and High Priest of our confession, Christ Jesus” (3:1). Moses was faithful in God’s house “as a servant,” but Christ is faithful “as a Son over His own house, whose house we are if we hold fast” (3:5–6). Then the Holy Spirit’s words in Psalm 95 take over: “Today, if you will hear His voice, Do not harden your hearts” (3:7–8). The generation that left Egypt heard God and saw His works, yet “they could not enter in because of unbelief” (3:19). Because God was still saying “Today” through David long after Joshua, “There remains therefore a rest for the people of God” (4:9), and the hearers are urged to “be diligent to enter that rest” (4:11). The movement closes with the searching power of God’s word, “living and powerful,” before whose eyes “all things are naked and open” (4:12–13).

III. Jesus’ Perfect Priesthood and the Better Covenant (4:14–10:18)

The long central movement begins with Jesus’ compassion. “We do not have a High Priest who cannot sympathize with our weaknesses” (4:15), so His people may “come boldly to the throne of grace” (4:16). Like Aaron, Jesus was called by God, yet He belongs to a greater order, and “He learned obedience by the things which He suffered” (5:8). The explanation pauses for a serious warning about spiritual dullness and falling away (5:11–6:8), followed at once by reassurance and by God’s promise and oath, which give believers hope as “an anchor of the soul” reaching “behind the veil” (6:19). Chapter 7 returns to Melchizedek and shows a priesthood that does not pass from one mortal man to the next, because Jesus “always lives to make intercession” (7:25). Chapters 8–10 place Jeremiah’s new covenant at the center and contrast the earthly copy with the heavenly reality, and the yearly sacrifices with Christ’s single offering. The movement ends where Jeremiah’s promise ends: “Their sins and their lawless deeds I will remember no more,” so “there is no longer an offering for sin” (10:17–18).

IV. Draw Near and Endure by Faith (10:19–12:29)

The “Therefore” of 10:19 is the hinge of the book, turning the teaching into invitation. With “boldness to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus,” the church is called to “draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith,” to hold fast its confession, and to “consider one another in order to stir up love and good works” (10:19–24). A grave warning about willful sin follows (10:26–31). The writer then reminds his hearers of their earlier endurance and urges them, “do not cast away your confidence” (10:35). Chapter 11 shows that kind of faith in Abel, Noah, Abraham, Sarah, Moses, Rahab, and many others, some delivered and some who suffered and died still trusting God (11:33–39). Chapter 12 turns to the race, run with eyes fixed on Jesus, who “endured the cross, despising the shame” (12:2), and explains hardship as the loving discipline of a Father. The movement ends at Mount Zion rather than Sinai, with a final warning not to “refuse Him who speaks” and a call to serve God “with reverence and godly fear” as those receiving “a kingdom which cannot be shaken” (12:25, 28).

V. Faithful Conduct and the Closing Appeal (13:1–13:25)

The last chapter shows persevering faith in ordinary life: brotherly love, hospitality to strangers, care for prisoners, faithfulness in marriage, and contentment that rests on the Lord’s promise, “I will never leave you nor forsake you” (13:5). Over all of it stands the confession, “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever” (13:8). Because Jesus “suffered outside the gate,” His people are called to “go forth to Him, outside the camp, bearing His reproach” (13:12–13), and to offer “the sacrifice of praise” together with doing good and sharing (13:15–16). The book closes with a prayer to “the God of peace who brought up our Lord Jesus from the dead, that great Shepherd of the sheep” (13:20), followed by news about Timothy and final greetings.

Key Themes to Watch For

God speaks in His Son. God’s final word has come in Jesus, and Hebrews quotes the Scriptures as God and the Holy Spirit speaking now (1:1–2; 3:7; 10:15; 12:25).

Better. The word marks what Jesus brings: a more excellent name, a better hope, covenant, promises, and sacrifices, a better country and resurrection, and blood that “speaks better things than that of Abel” (1:4; 7:19, 22; 8:6; 9:23; 11:16, 35; 12:24).

Jesus our High Priest. He is merciful and faithful, able to sympathize with weakness, permanent in His office, and seated at God’s right hand (2:17; 4:14–16; 7:23–28; 8:1).

Once for all. Christ’s offering is complete and never needs repeating (7:27; 9:12, 26–28; 10:10–14).

Drawing near. Through Jesus, believers come to God and into His presence with confidence (4:16; 7:19, 25; 10:19–22; 11:6).

Today and rest. God’s call in Psalm 95 is still open, and His rest remains for those who believe (3:7–4:11).

Hold fast and endure. Faith keeps its confidence to the end and does not draw back (3:6, 14; 4:14; 10:23, 35–39; 12:1–3).

Faith. Faith trusts what God has said about things not yet seen and keeps trusting while it waits (10:38–11:40).

Warning and assurance together. The same people receive serious warnings and strong encouragement, often in the same breath (6:4–12; 10:26–39).

One another. The church helps its members persevere through daily encouragement and mutual care (3:13; 10:24–25; 12:12–15; 13:1–3).

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

High priest and the Day of Atonement. A priest is appointed “for men in things pertaining to God” (5:1), to offer gifts and sacrifices on their behalf. Israel’s high priest came from the family of Aaron. Once a year, on the Day of Atonement, he entered the innermost room of the tabernacle alone, carrying blood for his own sins and for the people’s (9:7; Leviticus 16). Hebrews uses that day as its main picture of Jesus’ work. He entered “heaven itself” with His own blood, once for all (9:12, 24). Readers who keep that ritual in mind will find chapters 8–10 much easier to follow.

Tabernacle, copy, and shadow. Hebrews speaks of the tabernacle Moses built, not the later temple in Jerusalem. God told Moses to make everything “according to the pattern shown you on the mountain” (8:5), so the earthly sanctuary was a copy of a heavenly reality. When Hebrews calls the law “a shadow of the good things to come” (10:1), it does not mean the law was false. A shadow is real, but it points to the substance that casts it.

The veil. The veil was the curtain that separated the tabernacle’s two rooms (9:3). As long as it stood closed, it showed that “the way into the Holiest of All was not yet made manifest” (9:8). Hebrews pictures Jesus going “behind the veil” as our forerunner (6:19–20) and opening “a new and living way” for us “through the veil, that is, His flesh” (10:20).

Melchizedek. Melchizedek was the king of Salem and priest of God Most High who blessed Abram (Genesis 14:18–20). Genesis records no family line, birth, or death for him. Hebrews draws meaning from that silence, describing him as “made like the Son of God” (7:3), a picture of a priesthood that never ends. Hebrews compares Melchizedek with Jesus. It does not say they are the same person.

First covenant and new covenant. A covenant is a binding relationship that God establishes, with promises and obligations. In Hebrews, the “first covenant” is the one given at Sinai, together with its priesthood and sacrifices (9:1, 18). The “new covenant” is the one God promised through Jeremiah “with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah” (8:8; Jeremiah 31:31–34), and Jesus is its Mediator (8:6; 9:15; 12:24). Its promises are God’s laws written on the heart, knowing God, and sins remembered no more. When Hebrews says the first covenant has become “obsolete” (8:13), it is speaking of that covenant and its priestly order.

Perfect and perfection. These words usually mean completion rather than flawlessness. Jesus was always “without sin” (4:15). He was made “perfect through sufferings” (2:10) in the sense that suffering brought Him to the completion of His calling as Savior and High Priest (5:8–9). For believers, Christ’s offering gives a complete standing before God that the old sacrifices could never give (7:19; 10:1, 14). Elsewhere the same idea describes spiritual maturity (5:14; 6:1).

Rest. Hebrews speaks of God’s own rest after creation (4:4), the land the wilderness generation failed to enter, and the rest that still “remains” for God’s people (4:9). Because Psalm 95 was written long after Joshua brought Israel into Canaan, Hebrews concludes that God’s promise reaches beyond the land (4:7–8).

Conscience. The conscience is a person’s inner awareness of guilt before God. The old sacrifices could not make the worshiper perfect “in regard to the conscience” (9:9), but the blood of Christ will “cleanse your conscience from dead works to serve the living God” (9:14). That cleansing explains how Hebrews can invite believers to draw near with boldness instead of fear.

Angels and the law. Scripture associates angels with the giving of the law at Sinai (2:2; Acts 7:53; Galatians 3:19). That background explains why Hebrews begins by comparing the Son with the angels. The message spoken through the Son is greater than the law delivered through angels, so neglecting it is even more serious (2:1–4).

Christ and Life

Hebrews presents Jesus as both God’s final Word and our Brother. The Son who is “the express image of His person” (1:3) “is not ashamed to call them brethren” (2:11). He took on flesh and blood and tasted death in order to destroy the devil’s power and “release those who through fear of death were all their lifetime subject to bondage” (2:15). The God who once spoke from Sinai in fire and darkness has now spoken in One who stands with His people and calls them family.

Because Jesus suffered when He was tempted, “He is able to aid those who are tempted” (2:18). He was “in all points tempted as we are, yet without sin” (4:15), and the throne He gives access to is “the throne of grace” (4:16). Weakness is no reason for believers to stay away from God, since it is the very thing their High Priest understands. Their security rests on Him as well: “He is also able to save to the uttermost those who come to God through Him, since He always lives to make intercession for them” (7:25).

Hebrews also speaks of rest. “We who have believed do enter that rest,” and the one who enters has “ceased from his works as God did from His” (4:3, 10). Hebrews builds that promise from God’s rest at creation, Israel’s failure in the wilderness, and the call of Psalm 95. Read together with what Hebrews later says about Christ’s offering, it points to a rest found by relying on what Christ has already accomplished. His blood cleanses the conscience (9:14), and His single offering has “perfected forever those who are being sanctified” (10:14). Believers can therefore come to God without the shame that makes people hide from Him, and they enjoy His love as they rest in what He has done. The full inheritance still lies ahead, in the city “whose builder and maker is God” (11:10).

In 10:5–10, the body God prepared is Jesus’ own body. He came into the world saying, “Behold, I have come to do Your will, O God,” and by that will “we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (10:9–10). His sacrifice is the center of the passage. Elsewhere the New Testament calls the church the body of Christ (1 Corinthians 12:27; Ephesians 4:12–16), and read in that light the pattern reaches His people too: God prepares a body to do His will. Hebrews points the same direction when it calls believers to “consider one another” (10:24) and prays that God would be “working in you what is well pleasing in His sight” (13:21). The church’s obedience follows the pattern of His obedience and flows from His finished work.

Finally, Jesus goes ahead of His people. He has gone behind the veil, “where the forerunner has entered for us” (6:20), so their hope is fastened in God’s presence like an anchor. He is “the author and finisher of our faith,” who “for the joy that was set before Him endured the cross” (12:2). He also “suffered outside the gate” (13:12), and following Him may mean sharing His reproach. Hebrews gives His people a reason to bear it: “here we have no continuing city, but we seek the one to come” (13:14).

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Teaching and appeal belong together in Hebrews. The writer explains who Jesus is in order to move his hearers to draw near, hold fast, and keep going. Reading the teaching while skipping the appeals, or the reverse, misses the point of the book.

The warnings and the assurances are addressed to the same people. Right after one of his sternest warnings, the writer says, “we are confident of better things concerning you” (6:9). Hebrews gives believers real security in God’s promise and oath, in Christ’s intercession, and in His once-for-all sacrifice. It also warns seriously that turning away from Christ is a real danger. Holding fast to the end is the evidence that a person truly belongs to Christ’s house (3:6, 14). Neither truth should be allowed to cancel the other.

The warnings speak to the whole congregation. They call every hearer to heed God, to encourage one another daily, and to guard against being “hardened through the deceitfulness of sin” and departing from the living God (3:12–13). In this way the warnings help believers persevere. The deliberate rejection of one who has “trampled the Son of God underfoot” (10:29) is different from the ongoing struggle of a believer who keeps returning to Christ in repentance. Believers who are struggling, fearful, or failing can come to the High Priest who sympathizes with their weaknesses and find mercy and help at the throne of grace (4:15–16).

The first covenant was God’s gift. Hebrews calls its sanctuary and sacrifices a shadow, never an error, and shows how they prepared for Christ.

When Hebrews quotes the Old Testament, read the passage in its original setting first, and then follow how Hebrews uses it to speak about Christ.

Hebrews was preached to a congregation. Its many “let us” appeals include the writer and the whole church, so its call to endure is something believers answer together.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

Who wrote Hebrews? Early Christian writers disagreed. Clement of Alexandria held that Paul wrote Hebrews and that Luke translated it into Greek. Origen thought the ideas were Paul’s, reported that some credited Luke or Clement of Rome with the writing, and concluded that only God knows who actually wrote it. Tertullian named Barnabas. Much later, Martin Luther suggested Apollos. One detail weighs against Paul. In 2:3 the writer includes himself among those to whom the message was confirmed by people who had heard the Lord, while Paul insisted that he received the gospel directly “through the revelation of Jesus Christ” (Galatians 1:12).

Hebrews and the Greek Old Testament. Hebrews usually quotes the Old Testament in the wording of the ancient Greek translation that many Jewish communities used. That explains why some quotations differ from our English Old Testament. In Hebrews 10:5 the Son says, “a body You have prepared for Me,” while the Hebrew text of Psalm 40:6 reads, “My ears You have opened.” Both describe a life made ready to obey God.

Covenant or testament (9:16–17). The Greek word for “covenant” can also mean a last will. The NKJV uses “testament” in 9:16–17, where the writer seems to use the second meaning: a will takes effect only after the death of the one who made it. Some interpreters think the writer still means a covenant, which was confirmed through the death of sacrificial animals (9:18–20). Either way, the point is that Christ’s death puts the new covenant into effect.

The warnings in Hebrews 6 and 10. Faithful readers differ on the spiritual standing of the people described in 6:4–8 and 10:26–31. Some believe they had truly believed and then lost their salvation. Some stress a deliberate rejection of salvation once received. Others hold that they shared deeply in the life and blessings of the church without ever having saving faith. Still others read the warnings as describing what would happen if a believer fell away, or as warning about the loss of fruitfulness and reward, though that view must account for the language of judgment in 10:27 and 10:39. The NKJV’s “if they fall away” (6:6) reflects a translation choice; the Greek lists falling away alongside the other experiences in the passage. Whatever view a reader holds, the passage is followed at once by reassurance (6:9–12; 10:32–39).

A textual note on 10:34. The NKJV reads, “you had compassion on me in my chains.” Many early manuscripts say instead that the hearers had compassion on the prisoners. For that reason, this verse cannot firmly establish that the writer had been imprisoned.

Was Melchizedek Christ? Some have identified Melchizedek as an appearance of Christ before His birth. Hebrews uses the language of likeness. Melchizedek was “made like the Son of God” (7:3), and Jesus is a priest “in the likeness of Melchizedek” (7:15). The comparison rests on what Genesis records about him and on what it leaves unsaid.

The foundations of 6:1–2. The writer lists six foundational teachings: repentance, faith toward God, baptisms, laying on of hands (6:1–2), the resurrection of the dead, and eternal judgment. The plural word baptisms may include Jewish ritual washings as well as Christian baptism. Laying on of hands appears in the New Testament in commissioning for service, receiving the Holy Spirit, and imparting spiritual gifts (Acts 6:6; 8:17; 13:3; 1 Timothy 4:14). The writer does not set these teachings aside. He urges his hearers to build on them and “go on to perfection” (6:1).
