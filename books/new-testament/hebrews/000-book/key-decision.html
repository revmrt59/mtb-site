Book Questions

Who wrote Hebrews, and to whom was it written?

Hebrews does not name its writer. It begins without a greeting and closes with personal news: “Know that our brother Timothy has been set free” (Hebrews 13:23). The writer also counts himself among those to whom the message “was confirmed to us by those who heard Him” (Hebrews 2:3), which suggests he was not one of the first eyewitnesses. Over the centuries Paul, Barnabas, Apollos, and others have been proposed. MTB leaves the human author unnamed. The book’s authority rests on God speaking in His Son, not on knowing the writer’s name.

The writer calls the book “the word of exhortation” (Hebrews 13:22), a preached appeal put in writing. The readers knew Israel’s Scriptures well, and they had already endured public insult and the loss of their possessions (Hebrews 10:32–34). MTB’s working view is that they were believers from a Jewish background under pressure to turn back from Christ to their former ways of approaching God. That is a reasonable reconstruction, not something the text states.

The date and place are uncertain. MTB favors a date before AD 70, when the temple in Jerusalem was destroyed. Hebrews speaks of priestly service in the present tense (Hebrews 10:11), though it describes that worship through the tabernacle rather than the temple. “Those from Italy greet you” (Hebrews 13:24) could mean the writer was in Italy, or that Italians with him were greeting friends back home.

Read: Hebrews 2:3, Hebrews 10:11, Hebrews 10:32–34, Hebrews 13:22–25 · Related: Does verse 34 show that the writer had been in prison?

Can a true believer lose salvation? How do the warnings and the assurances fit together? (Hebrews 3:12–19; 6:4–8; 10:26–31; 12:15–29)

MTB teaches the believer’s security in Christ and takes Scripture’s warnings about falling away at full weight. Hebrews holds out some of the strongest assurances in the Bible. God confirmed His promise with an oath so that “we might have strong consolation” (Hebrews 6:18). Jesus “is also able to save to the uttermost those who come to God through Him, since He always lives to make intercession for them” (Hebrews 7:25). By one offering “He has perfected forever those who are being sanctified” (Hebrews 10:14).

Hebrews also gives some of the sternest warnings in the Bible (Hebrews 3:12–19; 6:4–8; 10:26–31; 12:15–17, 25–29). MTB reads each one the same way. It begins with what the passage says about the people involved, what they received, what they turned away from, and what consequence God warns of. Where Scripture says a profession was never genuine, MTB says so plainly, as with the wilderness generation, who “could not enter in because of unbelief” (Hebrews 3:19). Where the person’s prior standing remains open, as in Hebrews 6:4–8 and 10:26–31, MTB leaves it open. Each warning keeps the force the writer gives it.

Holding fast to Christ shows that a person truly belongs to Him (Hebrews 3:6, 14). Each warning stands beside a ground of hope, such as God’s oath, Christ’s intercession, or the readers’ own past faithfulness (Hebrews 6:9–20; 10:32–39).

These warnings concern a settled turning away from Christ Himself. They are not aimed at believers who struggle with sin or lose their sense of assurance and still want Him. Anyone who fears they have gone too far is invited to “come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need” (Hebrews 4:16).

If Jesus offered Himself once for all, what is He doing now? (Hebrews 7:25–27; 9:24–28; 10:11–14)

Hebrews stresses that Jesus’ sacrifice is complete. The earlier priests kept “offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins.” But Jesus, “after He had offered one sacrifice for sins forever, sat down at the right hand of God” (Hebrews 10:11–12). His sitting shows that the offering is finished. Nothing needs to be added to it or repeated.

Yet His priestly work goes on. “He always lives to make intercession” for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25), and He has entered heaven itself, “now to appear in the presence of God for us” (Hebrews 9:24). MTB holds these together. Jesus does not offer Himself again. He represents His people before the Father on the ground of the one sacrifice already given.

He “will appear a second time, apart from sin, for salvation” to those who eagerly wait for Him (Hebrews 9:28). Meanwhile believers answer His finished work by offering “the sacrifice of praise to God” and by doing good and sharing (Hebrews 13:15–16). These sacrifices are thanks, not payment.

Read: Hebrews 7:23–27, Hebrews 9:24–28, Hebrews 10:11–14, Hebrews 13:15–16 · Related: Does “to the uttermost” mean “completely” or “forever”?

What does it mean that Jesus was “made perfect”? Was He ever imperfect? (Hebrews 2:10; 5:8–9; 7:28)

Hebrews says God made “the captain of their salvation perfect through sufferings” (Hebrews 2:10). It also says that Jesus, “having been perfected,” became “the author of eternal salvation” (Hebrews 5:9). MTB holds that this does not mean Jesus had faults that needed fixing. Hebrews insists He was “without sin” (Hebrews 4:15) and “holy, harmless, undefiled” (Hebrews 7:26).

The words Hebrews uses here belong to the same family as teleios, the word Jesus uses in Matthew 5:48. They come from telos, which means end, goal, or purpose. To be “made perfect” is not to move from flawed to flawless. It is to reach the intended end or purpose. The sinless Son walked the whole path of suffering and obedience God gave Him, and through it He reached the goal for which He came: to be the Savior and the sympathetic High Priest of His people.

The same word family appears elsewhere in Hebrews with related meanings. Believers are urged to “go on to perfection,” that is, to maturity (Hebrews 6:1). Christ’s offering “has perfected forever those who are being sanctified,” giving them a standing the old sacrifices never could (Hebrews 10:14). The faithful of old will be “made perfect” together with us (Hebrews 11:40). Read each use in its own setting.

Read: Hebrews 2:9–10, Hebrews 4:15, Hebrews 5:7–10, Hebrews 6:1, Hebrews 7:26–28, Hebrews 10:14, Hebrews 11:39–40 · Related: Does “He learned obedience” mean Jesus was once disobedient?; What did the Old Testament saints not receive? What does “made perfect” with us mean?

I. God’s Son and the Great Salvation He Brings (Hebrews 1:1–2:18)

Hebrews 1 · 3 questions

Hebrews 1:5–6 · The Son and Firstborn Whom Angels Worship

Does “Today I have begotten You” mean the Son had a beginning? (v. 5)

Hebrews quotes Psalm 2:7, “You are My Son, Today I have begotten You,” and joins it to God’s promise to David’s heir, “I will be to Him a Father, And He shall be to Me a Son” (v. 5; 2 Samuel 7:14). In Psalm 2 these words are a royal decree, spoken when God installs His King (Psalm 2:6–8). Hebrews applies them to Jesus as the promised royal Son.

MTB reads “Today” as pointing to that royal declaration, not to the start of the Son’s existence. Hebrews has already said that God made the worlds through the Son (v. 2) and that the Son upholds all things (v. 3). He existed before creation. The “more excellent name” He inherits (v. 4) belongs to His enthronement after He “purged our sins” (v. 3).

Read: Hebrews 1:1–5, Psalm 2:6–8, 2 Samuel 7:12–16 · Related: Does “firstborn” mean the Son was created? And when does God bring Him “into the world”?

Does “firstborn” mean the Son was created? And when does God bring Him “into the world”? (v. 6)

“Firstborn” here names rank, not origin. The Son is “heir of all things” (v. 2), and God says of David’s royal line, “I will make him My firstborn, The highest of the kings of the earth” (Psalm 89:27). The Son through whom God made the worlds was not Himself made.

The timing of the verse is less clear. “When He again brings the firstborn into the world” (v. 6) could refer to Jesus’ birth, His exaltation after the resurrection, or His return. The word “again” may simply introduce another quotation, or it may describe a second entrance. MTB leaves the timing open. On any reading, God commands, “Let all the angels of God worship Him.”

Read: Hebrews 1:2–6, Psalm 89:27 · Related: Does “Today I have begotten You” mean the Son had a beginning?

Hebrews 1:7–12 · Serving Angels and the Eternal, Unchanging Son

Does Hebrews call the Son “God”? (vv. 8–12)

Yes, and MTB affirms it plainly. Hebrews quotes Psalm 45:6–7 as God’s word to the Son: “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever” (v. 8). It then applies Psalm 102:25–27, a psalm about the LORD who made heaven and earth, to the Son: “You, LORD, in the beginning laid the foundation of the earth” (v. 10). The Son is the eternal Creator who remains when creation wears out.

The same passage keeps the Father and the Son distinct: “God, Your God, has anointed You” (v. 9). Hebrews holds both truths. The Son is addressed as God and Lord, and He is distinct from the Father who addresses and anoints Him.

Some have read verse 8 as “God is your throne.” The setting, where the Son is set above the angels and called the unchanging Creator, supports the NKJV’s direct address to the Son as God.

Read: Hebrews 1:7–12, Psalm 45:6–7, Psalm 102:25–27

Hebrews 2 · 2 questions

Hebrews 2:5–9 · We See Jesus Crowned with Glory and Honor

Is Psalm 8 about humanity or about Jesus? (vv. 5–9)

MTB’s answer is both. Psalm 8 marvels that God crowned human beings with glory and honor and put His works under their rule (Psalm 8:4–6). Hebrews quotes it and then admits, “But now we do not yet see all things put under him” (v. 8). Humanity has not fulfilled that calling.

“But we see Jesus” (v. 9). He shared our humanity, was “made a little lower than the angels,” and is now “crowned with glory and honor” because He suffered death for us. In Him the calling of Psalm 8 is fulfilled, and He is “bringing many sons to glory” (v. 10) in the world to come.

Read: Hebrews 2:5–10, Psalm 8:3–6

Hebrews 2:14–16 · Through Death He Destroys the Devil’s Power

Does “the seed of Abraham” mean Jesus helps only Jewish people? (v. 16)

No. MTB reads the phrase as including every believer. Hebrews contrasts the people Jesus came to help with the angels: “He does not give aid to angels, but He does give aid to the seed of Abraham” (v. 16). The phrase identifies those He helps as people who share in God’s promise to Abraham.

The New Testament shows that this family includes Gentiles. “Only those who are of faith are sons of Abraham,” and “if you are Christ’s, then you are Abraham’s seed, and heirs according to the promise” (Galatians 3:7, 29). Jesus aids all who come to God through Him by faith.

Read: Hebrews 2:14–18, Galatians 3:7, Galatians 3:26–29

II. Hear God’s Voice and Enter His Rest Through Faith (Hebrews 3:1–4:13)

Hebrews 3 · 1 question

Hebrews 3:1–6 · Jesus, Faithful as a Son over God’s House

Does “if we hold fast” mean we keep our salvation by holding on? (vv. 6–19)

MTB understands holding fast as the evidence that a person truly belongs to Christ. Endurance does not earn a place in God’s house. It shows that a person already has one. We are God’s house “if we hold fast the confidence and the rejoicing of the hope firm to the end” (v. 6), and “we have become partakers of Christ if we hold the beginning of our confidence steadfast to the end” (v. 14). We have become partakers of Christ, and holding fast shows it.

Who they are. The warning is addressed to “brethren” (v. 12), the “holy brethren, partakers of the heavenly calling” (v. 1). It sets before them the wilderness generation of Psalm 95 (vv. 7–11).

What they received. That generation came “out of Egypt, led by Moses” (v. 16). They heard God’s voice and “saw My works forty years” (v. 9).

What they turned away from. They hardened their hearts and rebelled (vv. 8, 16). The writer warns the church against the same thing: “an evil heart of unbelief in departing from the living God” (v. 12), a heart hardened “through the deceitfulness of sin” (v. 13).

What consequence God warns of. God swore, “They shall not enter My rest” (v. 11), and Scripture names the cause plainly: “they could not enter in because of unbelief” (v. 19). They heard God’s word, but it “did not profit them, not being mixed with faith in those who heard it” (4:2). God’s answer for the church is daily mutual care: “exhort one another daily, while it is called ‘Today’” (v. 13). Others read verses 6 and 14 as conditions a believer must keep to remain in Christ. MTB affirms both the believer’s security in Christ and the real danger of departing from the living God.

Hebrews 4 · 4 questions

Hebrews 4:1–8 · The Promise of Rest Still Stands

What is God’s rest: the land of Canaan, heaven, or something we enter now? (vv. 1–11)

Hebrews builds its answer from three Scriptures. God rested on the seventh day (v. 4; Genesis 2:2). Israel could not enter the promised land “because of unbelief” (3:19). Yet long after Joshua, God still said through David, “Today, if you will hear His voice, Do not harden your hearts” (v. 7; Psalm 95:7–8). “For if Joshua had given them rest, then He would not afterward have spoken of another day” (v. 8). The rest God calls His own reaches beyond Canaan.

MTB understands that believers begin to enter this rest now: “For we who have believed do enter that rest” (v. 3). The one who enters “has himself also ceased from his works as God did from His” (v. 10). We rest by relying on what Christ has finished. He gives forgiveness, a cleansed conscience, and freedom to come to God without hiding in shame (9:14; 10:14–22). The promise also looks ahead to the full inheritance still to come.

Some read the rest as only future, the heavenly home believers enter at the end. Hebrews does keep the promise open: “Let us therefore be diligent to enter that rest” (v. 11). But the present tense of verse 3 leads MTB to stress the rest believers can enjoy today.

Read: Hebrews 3:16–4:11, Genesis 2:2–3, Psalm 95:7–11, Hebrews 9:14, Hebrews 10:14–22 · Related: Does “a rest for the people of God” mean Christians no longer need a weekly Sabbath?

Hebrews 4:9–11 · A Rest Remains for the People of God

Does “a rest for the people of God” mean Christians no longer need a weekly Sabbath? (v. 9)

“There remains therefore a rest for the people of God” (v. 9). The word Hebrews uses for rest here is related to the word Sabbath. Hebrews is explaining the rest believers enter by faith in Christ. It is not giving instructions about a weekly day, either to keep one or to drop it.

MTB holds the Sabbath as part of God’s moral law, the fourth commandment. Believers do not keep it to be saved. A regular weekly rhythm of ceasing from ordinary work to worship, remember God’s deliverance, and receive Christ’s rest remains good for His people. Which day is set apart is a matter of freedom, and believers are not to judge one another over it (Romans 14:5–6; Colossians 2:16–17).

Christians hold different views. Some see the weekly Sabbath fulfilled in Christ with no remaining obligation, and others require a specific day. Hebrews 4 points every reader to the deeper rest found in Christ.

Read: Hebrews 4:9–11, Exodus 20:8–11, Mark 2:27–28, Romans 14:5–6, Colossians 2:16–17 · Related: What is God’s rest: the land of Canaan, heaven, or something we enter now?

Hebrews 4:12–13 · The Living Word and the All-Seeing God

Does verse 12 teach that soul and spirit are distinct parts of a person? (v. 12)

Verse 12 piles up vivid pictures. The word of God is “living and powerful, and sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing even to the division of soul and spirit, and of joints and marrow.” The pictures show how deeply God’s word reaches. It lays bare what is hidden and is “a discerner of the thoughts and intents of the heart.”

MTB understands a human being as body, soul, and spirit. Paul prays that “your whole spirit, soul, and body be preserved blameless” (1 Thessalonians 5:23). The soul is the mind, will, and emotions. The spirit is the immortal, immaterial core of who we are. When we are born again, our spirit is made alive to God and can know Him: “that which is born of the Spirit is spirit” (John 3:6), and “the Spirit Himself bears witness with our spirit that we are children of God” (Romans 8:16). Verse 12 fits this understanding. God’s word reaches deeper than anything else can, even to the place where soul and spirit are divided.

Many Christians see soul and spirit as two ways of speaking about the same inner life, and they read the paired pictures as images of how deeply the word cuts. On any view, the point in context is the urgency of hearing God. His word exposes whether His hearers are trusting Him or following the wilderness generation, and “all things are naked and open to the eyes of Him to whom we must give account” (v. 13). That exposure leads straight to the invitation to draw near to a High Priest who understands us (vv. 14–16).

Read: Hebrews 4:11–16, 1 Thessalonians 5:23, John 3:6, Romans 8:16

Hebrews 4:14–16 · Come Boldly to the Throne of Grace

If Jesus never sinned, were His temptations real? (v. 15)

Yes. MTB holds that His temptations were fully real. Jesus “was in all points tempted as we are, yet without sin” (v. 15). He felt the pull and pressure of temptation as a real human being. Hebrews says He “suffered, being tempted” (2:18). Refusing to give in did not make the struggle smaller.

Because He knows weakness from the inside and stayed sinless, He can “sympathize with our weaknesses” and give real help. So we may “come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need” (v. 16). Our boldness rests on who He is, not on how well we have done.

Read: Hebrews 2:17–18, Hebrews 4:14–16 · Related: Does “He learned obedience” mean Jesus was once disobedient?

III. Jesus’ Perfect Priesthood and the Better Covenant (Hebrews 4:14–10:18)

Hebrews 5 · 3 questions

Hebrews 5:7–10 · Perfected Through Suffering

How was Jesus “heard” if He still died? (v. 7)

Jesus “offered up prayers and supplications, with vehement cries and tears to Him who was able to save Him from death, and was heard because of His godly fear” (v. 7). Many readers think of Gethsemane, though the verse names no single occasion.

Jesus did die, so how was His prayer answered? The Greek wording can mean rescue out of death, not only escape from it. MTB understands that the Father heard Him by carrying Him through death and raising Him from it. Hebrews later speaks of “the God of peace who brought up our Lord Jesus from the dead” (13:20).

Read: Hebrews 5:7–10, Hebrews 13:20

Does “He learned obedience” mean Jesus was once disobedient? (v. 8)

No, and MTB is clear on this. “Though He was a Son, yet He learned obedience by the things which He suffered” (v. 8). The Son did not move from disobedience to obedience. He learned what obedience costs by living it out under real suffering, all the way to the cross.

Hebrews is clear that He was “without sin” (4:15). His obedience through suffering completed His calling as Savior and fitted Him to be a High Priest who understands those He helps.

Read: Hebrews 4:15, Hebrews 5:7–10 · Related: What does it mean that Jesus was “made perfect”? Was He ever imperfect?

Does “to all who obey Him” mean we are saved by our obedience? (v. 9)

Jesus “became the author of eternal salvation to all who obey Him” (v. 9). MTB holds that He is the source of salvation. Obedience is not the price we pay for it.

In Hebrews, trusting Christ and obeying Him belong together. The wilderness generation’s unbelief showed itself as disobedience (3:18–19). Those who truly trust Christ follow Him. Their obedience is the response or fruit of people He has saved.

Read: Hebrews 3:18–19, Hebrews 5:8–9

Hebrews 6 · 3 questions

Hebrews 6:1–3 · Go On to Maturity

What are the “baptisms” and the “laying on of hands” in verse 2? (vv. 1–2)

Hebrews lists six foundations: “repentance from dead works,” “faith toward God,” “the doctrine of baptisms,” “laying on of hands,” “resurrection of the dead,” and “eternal judgment” (vv. 1–2). The writer does not want believers to tear up these foundations. He wants them to build on them and grow to maturity.

MTB holds that Scripture speaks of at least three baptisms, which helps explain the plural. There is baptism in water, by which believers publicly identify with Christ (Matthew 28:19; Acts 2:38). There is baptism in the Holy Spirit, which Jesus Himself gives: “He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire” (Matthew 3:11). And there is a baptism of suffering. Jesus said, “But I have a baptism to be baptized with” (Luke 12:50), speaking of His coming sufferings, and He told His disciples they would share it (Mark 10:38–39). Some readers take the plural more narrowly, as Christian baptism set beside the ritual washings believers from a Jewish background already knew (9:10).

Laying on of hands is not explained in any single verse. It is learned from the examples Scripture gives, and they show God conveying and confirming His blessing through one person to another. Scripture shows three main purposes. The first is impartation: hands were laid on people to bless them (Genesis 48:14; Mark 10:16), to impart gifts and the Holy Spirit (Deuteronomy 34:9; Acts 8:17; 1 Timothy 4:14), and to heal the sick (Mark 16:18; Acts 28:8). The second is commissioning for service, as Moses did with Joshua and the church at Antioch did with Barnabas and Saul (Numbers 27:18–23; Acts 13:1–3). The third is identification and confirmation, as when a worshiper laid his hand on the offering that died in his place (Leviticus 1:4).

That is why it belongs among the foundations. It is a real spiritual ministry, not an empty ceremony, and it calls for care: “Do not lay hands on anyone hastily” (1 Timothy 5:22). It is best practiced with prayer and humility, under the Holy Spirit’s guidance, and usually within the local church under the oversight of its elders.

Read: Hebrews 6:1–3, Matthew 3:11, Luke 12:50, Mark 10:38–39, Acts 2:38, Acts 8:14–17, Numbers 27:18–23, Acts 13:1–3, Leviticus 1:4, 1 Timothy 4:14, 1 Timothy 5:22

Hebrews 6:4–8 · The Warning Against Falling Away

Who are the people in Hebrews 6:4–8? Were they true believers? (vv. 4–8)

Who they are. The passage addresses professing believers in the congregation who have been part of the Christian community and shared in its spiritual privileges. Scripture does not settle their inward, eternal standing beforehand. It describes them by their shared experience and then warns what happens if they fall away, leaving their standing to be shown by whether they endure or turn back.

What they received. They were “once enlightened,” “tasted the heavenly gift,” became “partakers of the Holy Spirit,” and “tasted the good word of God and the powers of the age to come” (vv. 4–5). In the flow of Hebrews these are real spiritual realities experienced within the life of the gospel community: light, the heavenly gift of salvation in Christ, a share in the Spirit’s work, and a real taste of God’s word and the power of His coming kingdom.

What they turned away from. The warning is for those who “fall away” (v. 6). This is not a believer who stumbles into sin or struggles with doubt while still looking to Christ. It is a deliberate, settled turning away from Christ and the gospel after experiencing its light and power.

What consequence God warns of. If they fall away, it is impossible “to renew them again to repentance” (v. 6), because turning from Christ rejects the only sacrifice and grace that can bring repentance and forgiveness. The picture in verses 7–8 drives this home. Ground that drinks in the same rain from God but bears thorns and briers “is rejected and near to being cursed, whose end is to be burned” (v. 8).

MTB holds this severe warning together with the believer’s security in Christ and the writer’s immediate encouragement: “But, beloved, we are confident of better things concerning you, yes, things that accompany salvation” (v. 9). God’s promise and oath stand firm for those who truly belong to Him (vv. 17–20). Any believer who fears falling short is called not to despair, but to hold fast and draw near to Christ (4:16).

Read: Hebrews 4:14–16, Hebrews 6:4–20 · Related: Can a true believer lose salvation? Does “impossible” mean God can never restore someone who has fallen away? (v. 6)

The NKJV says renewal is impossible “since they crucify again for themselves the Son of God, and put Him to an open shame” (v. 6). The Greek can also be read “while they crucify again,” and some translations take it that way. On that reading the impossibility lasts as long as the person keeps rejecting Christ. MTB follows the NKJV’s “since” in its main explanation. On either reading, the person sides with those who put Jesus to shame, and Jesus is the only sacrifice for sins (10:26).

Some also note that the verse speaks of what is impossible for people to bring about, while leaving God’s ability open. The warning concerns a settled turning against Christ. It is not about a believer who has sinned and wants to come back. Anyone troubled by sin and seeking Christ is invited to the throne of grace to find mercy (4:16).

Read: Hebrews 4:14–16, Hebrews 6:4–9, Hebrews 10:26 · Related: Who are the people in Hebrews 6:4–8? Were they true believers?

Hebrews 7 · 4 questions

Hebrews 7:1–3 · Melchizedek, King and Priest

Was Melchizedek Jesus Himself, appearing before His birth? (v. 3)

Hebrews says Melchizedek was “without father, without mother, without genealogy, having neither beginning of days nor end of life, but made like the Son of God” (v. 3). Some have taken this to mean he was the Son of God appearing before His birth, or an angel.

MTB understands Melchizedek as a real, historical man. He was king of Salem, the ancient name for Jerusalem (Psalm 76:2), and “the priest of God Most High” (Genesis 14:18). He was a type of Christ, a real person whose life and office foreshadowed Jesus. The words “made like the Son of God” point to a likeness. To resemble someone is to be distinct from him, so Melchizedek and the Son of God are two different persons.

Genesis records no parents, birth, or death for Melchizedek. Hebrews uses that silence to picture a priesthood that does not end. As king of righteousness and king of peace (v. 2), he joined the offices of king and priest, as Christ does. Jesus’ permanent priesthood rests on something greater: “the power of an endless life” (v. 16).

Read: Genesis 14:18–20, Psalm 76:2, Psalm 110:4, Hebrews 7:1–3, Hebrews 7:15–17

Hebrews 7:4–10 · Greater Than Abraham and Levi

Does Hebrews 7 support tithing for Christians today? (vv. 4–10)

Abraham “gave a tenth part of all” to Melchizedek (v. 2), and Hebrews makes much of it. But the writer’s point is about rank, not giving. The one who receives the tenth and gives the blessing is the greater. Even Levi, whose tribe later received tithes, “paid tithes through Abraham, so to speak” (v. 9). So Melchizedek’s priesthood stands above Levi’s.

MTB believes the passage also supports the Bible’s wider teaching on tithing. Abraham gave the tenth long before the law of Moses (Genesis 14:20), and Jacob vowed to God, “of all that You give me I will surely give a tenth to You” (Genesis 28:22). The law confirmed the tithe (Leviticus 27:30), and Jesus affirmed it even as He rebuked those who neglected “justice and mercy and faith” (Matthew 23:23). Hebrews 7 shows the tithe given to the priest whose order Jesus fulfills. Those who are Abraham’s children by faith honor Christ, their great High Priest, in the same way.

The New Testament gives no direct command to tithe, so faithful Christians differ on whether the tenth still applies. MTB holds it as a biblical principle and a starting point for generous giving, offered willingly and as worship, “for God loves a cheerful giver” (2 Corinthians 9:7).

Read: Genesis 14:18–20, Genesis 28:20–22, Leviticus 27:30, Hebrews 7:4–10, Matthew 23:23, 2 Corinthians 9:6–8

Hebrews 7:11–19 · A New Priest and a Change of Law

If there is “a change of the law,” are the Ten Commandments set aside? (v. 12)

“For the priesthood being changed, of necessity there is also a change of the law” (v. 12). The context shows what changed. The law set out who could serve as priest, and priests came from Levi through Aaron. Jesus came from Judah, “of which tribe Moses spoke nothing concerning priesthood” (v. 14). His appointment set aside “the former commandment” about the priesthood and the order of sacrifice bound up with it (v. 18).

MTB follows the historic Reformed understanding of the law. The ceremonial laws of priesthood and sacrifice were shadows that pointed to Christ, and He has fulfilled them (10:1). God’s moral law, summed up in the Ten Commandments, still reveals His character. Believers are not saved by keeping it, but it guides a life that pleases God, and He writes it on their hearts (8:10).

Hebrews’ concern here is access to God: “for the law made nothing perfect; on the other hand, there is the bringing in of a better hope, through which we draw near to God” (v. 19).

Read: Hebrews 7:11–19, Hebrews 8:10, Hebrews 10:1, Jeremiah 31:33 · Related: Does “obsolete” mean the Old Testament no longer matters?

Hebrews 7:23–25 · He Always Lives to Intercede

Does “to the uttermost” mean “completely” or “forever”? (v. 25)

Jesus “is also able to save to the uttermost those who come to God through Him, since He always lives to make intercession for them” (v. 25). Some translations say “completely,” and others say “forever” or “for all time.” The Greek phrase can carry both ideas.

MTB holds both together. His salvation is complete, leaving nothing undone, and it lasts, because He lives forever and never stops interceding. The earlier priests “were prevented by death from continuing” (v. 23), but Jesus “continues forever” (v. 24). The promise is for “those who come to God through Him.”

Read: Hebrews 7:23–25 · Related: If Jesus offered Himself once for all, what is He doing now?

Hebrews 8 · 3 questions

Hebrews 8:8–12 · God’s Promise of a New Covenant

Jeremiah’s new covenant names Israel and Judah. Does it apply to the church? (vv. 8–12)

Hebrews quotes Jeremiah 31:31–34, where God promises to “make a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah” (v. 8). MTB gives full weight to those named recipients. Hebrews does not erase them.

Hebrews also says plainly that Jesus “is also Mediator of a better covenant, which was established on better promises” (v. 6), and it applies the covenant’s promises to the believers it addresses (10:15–18). God writes His laws on their hearts, makes Himself known to them, and forgives their sins. Through Jesus these promises belong to all who come to God through Him.

Hebrews does not take up the wider question of Israel’s future in God’s plan. Its words about the first covenant do not answer that question.

Read: Jeremiah 31:31–34, Hebrews 8:6–12, Hebrews 10:15–18 · Related: Does “obsolete” mean the Old Testament no longer matters?

If “none of them shall teach his neighbor,” do believers still need teachers? (v. 11)

God promises, “None of them shall teach his neighbor, and none his brother, saying, ‘Know the LORD,’ for all shall know Me, from the least of them to the greatest of them” (v. 11). Under the first covenant, a person could belong to Israel outwardly and still need to be urged to know the LORD. In the new covenant, every true believer already knows God personally and can draw near through Jesus without a Levitical priest.

MTB holds that this does not remove the need for teachers. Hebrews itself scolds its readers because “by this time you ought to be teachers” (5:12). Teachers help believers grow in knowing the God they already know. A fuller knowledge still lies ahead, when “we shall see Him as He is” (1 John 3:2).

Read: Hebrews 5:12–14, Hebrews 8:10–11, 1 Corinthians 13:12, 1 John 3:2

Hebrews 8:13 · The First Covenant Made Obsolete

Does “obsolete” mean the Old Testament no longer matters? (v. 13)

“In that He says, ‘A new covenant,’ He has made the first obsolete. Now what is becoming obsolete and growing old is ready to vanish away” (v. 13). “The first” is the covenant made at Sinai, with its priests and sacrifices. God Himself gave it, and it served His purpose. Its tabernacle followed the pattern God showed Moses on the mountain (v. 5), and its sacrifices were shadows that pointed to Christ (10:1).

MTB’s answer is that the covenant is obsolete, but the Scriptures are not. Hebrews quotes the Old Testament constantly and hears the Holy Spirit still speaking through it (3:7; 10:15). What has passed is the old way of coming to God through Levitical priests and repeated sacrifices, now fulfilled in Jesus.

Read: Hebrews 3:7, Hebrews 8:5–13, Hebrews 10:1, Hebrews 10:15 · Related: Jeremiah’s new covenant names Israel and Judah. Does it apply to the church?; If there is “a change of the law,” are the Ten Commandments set aside?

Hebrews 9 · 3 questions

Hebrews 9:1–5 · The Earthly Tabernacle and Its Furnishings

Did Hebrews put the incense altar in the wrong room? (v. 4)

Hebrews describes the Holiest of All as the room “which had the golden censer and the ark of the covenant” (v. 4). In Exodus the altar of incense stood just outside the inner veil (Exodus 30:6), so some readers think Hebrews has misplaced it.

MTB does not see an error here. The Greek word can mean either a censer or the incense altar. The NKJV’s “golden censer” fits the vessel the high priest carried behind the veil on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:12–13). If the word means the altar, Hebrews is describing it by its close tie to the inner room, since it stood “before the mercy seat” (Exodus 30:6). Either way, the writer does not linger here: “Of these things we cannot now speak in detail” (v. 5).

His real point follows in verses 6–10. While the first tabernacle stood, “the way into the Holiest of All was not yet made manifest” (v. 8).

Read: Exodus 30:1–10, Leviticus 16:12–13, Hebrews 9:1–10

Hebrews 9:11–14 · Christ Entered with His Own Blood

Is “the eternal Spirit” the Holy Spirit or Christ’s own spirit? (v. 14)

Christ “through the eternal Spirit offered Himself without spot to God” (v. 14). MTB’s preferred reading is the Holy Spirit, who empowered and sustained Jesus as He willingly offered Himself. The NKJV’s capital S reflects that reading.

The Greek phrase has no “the,” which leaves room for another reading: Christ’s own eternal nature, in keeping with “the power of an endless life” (7:16). Both readings agree on the main point. His offering has eternal power. He obtained “eternal redemption” (v. 12), and His blood can “cleanse your conscience from dead works to serve the living God” (v. 14).

Read: Hebrews 7:16, Hebrews 9:11–14

Hebrews 9:15–17 · The Mediator’s Death and the Promised Inheritance

Are verses 16–17 about a “testament” (a will) or a “covenant”? (vv. 16–17)

“For where there is a testament, there must also of necessity be the death of the testator” (v. 16). The Greek word here is the same word Hebrews elsewhere uses for covenant, and it can also mean a last will. MTB follows the NKJV’s “testament.” Verse 15 has just spoken of “the promise of the eternal inheritance,” and an inheritance comes through a will that takes effect when the one who made it dies.

Others keep the word “covenant” throughout. On that reading, the death refers to the slain animals that sealed ancient covenants and stood for the one making the covenant (Genesis 15:9–18). Both readings lead to the same place. The real death of Jesus, the Mediator, puts the new covenant into effect and secures what God promised.

Read: Genesis 15:9–18, Hebrews 9:15–22

Hebrews 10 · 4 questions

Hebrews 10:5–10 · A Body Prepared to Do God’s Will

Psalm 40 says “My ears You have opened.” Why does Hebrews say “a body You have prepared”? (v. 5)

Psalm 40:6 in our Old Testament reads, “My ears You have opened.” Hebrews quotes the psalm as it reads in the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament: “But a body You have prepared for Me” (v. 5). Both speak of One whom God made ready to hear and do His will.

In Hebrews the body is Jesus’ own body. He came “to do Your will, O God” (v. 7), and “by that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (v. 10). His obedience and His sacrifice are one act.

The verse itself is about Jesus’ own body. As a spiritual application, MTB also sees a picture of the church, Christ’s body, which God prepares to do His will as its members submit to their Head and build one another up (1 Corinthians 12:12–27; Ephesians 4:11–16). That application grows out of Jesus’ obedience. It does not replace the meaning of the verse.

Read: Psalm 40:6–8, Hebrews 10:5–10, 1 Corinthians 12:12–27, Ephesians 4:11–16

Hebrews 10:19–25 · Let Us Draw Near, Hold Fast, and Consider One Another

Does “our bodies washed with pure water” refer to baptism? (v. 22)

Believers draw near “having our hearts sprinkled from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water” (v. 22). The words recall the washing and sprinkling that prepared Israel’s priests to serve (Exodus 29:4, 21; Leviticus 16:4). They also recall God’s promise, “Then I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean” (Ezekiel 36:25). Christ cleanses the whole person, conscience and all, so that we can come into God’s presence.

Many readers also hear an echo of Christian baptism, the public sign of that cleansing. MTB allows the echo but keeps the priestly washing and Ezekiel’s promise at the center. Our confidence rests on Jesus’ blood and His ongoing priesthood (vv. 19–21), not on a ritual.

Read: Exodus 29:4, Exodus 29:21, Leviticus 16:4, Ezekiel 36:25–27, Hebrews 10:19–25

Hebrews 10:26–31 · No Sacrifice Left for Willful Rejection

Does “if we sin willfully” mean any deliberate sin after salvation cannot be forgiven? (vv. 26–31)

Who they are. The warning addresses professing members of the church who have “received the knowledge of the truth” (v. 26) and are described in terms of the covenant blood “by which he was sanctified” (v. 29). Faithful readers differ on whether “sanctified” here means genuine inward renewal or an outward setting apart within the covenant community. Because the passage does not settle the person’s prior inward standing, MTB leaves that standing open and lets the warning speak directly to the whole congregation.

What they received. They received “the knowledge of the truth” (v. 26), came under “the blood of the covenant” (v. 29), and encountered “the Spirit of grace” (v. 29). These are not outsiders who never heard the gospel, but people who knew Christ’s sacrifice and the grace of His Spirit.

What they turned away from. Verse 29 defines the willful sin in view. It is not a believer stumbling into a known sin and grieving over it. It is a settled, defiant rejection of Christ Himself: trampling “the Son of God underfoot,” counting the blood of the covenant “a common thing,” and insulting “the Spirit of grace.”

What consequence God warns of. “There no longer remains a sacrifice for sins, but a certain fearful expectation of judgment, and fiery indignation which will devour the adversaries” (vv. 26–27). Because Jesus’ sacrifice is the only sacrifice for sins, turning away from Him leaves no other covering and ends in what verse 39 calls drawing back “to perdition.” Readings that soften this warning into a mere loss of reward do not carry the weight of the writer’s words.

MTB holds this severe warning together with the believer’s security in Christ: by one offering “He has perfected forever those who are being sanctified” (v. 14). Right after the warning, the writer encourages the congregation: “Therefore do not cast away your confidence” (v. 35), for “we are not of those who draw back to perdition, but of those who believe to the saving of the soul” (v. 39). Believers who fear they have sinned too much are not the people this warning describes. They are invited to draw near with confidence through Jesus’ blood and ongoing priesthood (vv. 19–22).

Read: Hebrews 10:11–14, Hebrews 10:19–39 · Related: Can a true believer lose salvation? How do the warnings and the assurances fit together?; Who are the people in Hebrews 6:4–8? Were they true believers?

Hebrews 10:32–39 · Do Not Cast Away Your Confidence

Does verse 34 show that the writer had been in prison? (v. 34)

The NKJV reads, “for you had compassion on me in my chains” (v. 34). Many ancient manuscripts read instead, “you had compassion on the prisoners,” and most modern translations follow that reading.

Because of this difference, MTB does not treat the verse as firm ground for conclusions about the writer’s imprisonment or identity. The point is clear on either reading. These believers stood by people who suffered for Christ, even at the cost of their own goods, because they knew they had “a better and an enduring possession” in heaven.

Read: Hebrews 10:32–39, Hebrews 13:3 · Related: Who wrote Hebrews, and to whom was it written?

IV. Draw Near and Endure by Faith (Hebrews 10:19–12:29)

Hebrews 11 · 5 questions

Hebrews 11:1–3 · What Faith Is

Is faith a strong feeling that something will happen? (v. 1)

“Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen” (v. 1). MTB holds that faith is trusting in who God has revealed Himself to be in His word. It is not a feeling we work up or a force that makes things happen. Because faith rests on God’s character and promises, what He has promised carries real weight in how we live now.

The chapter shows faith by what it does. Noah, “divinely warned of things not yet seen,” built an ark (v. 7). Abraham “went out, not knowing where he was going” (v. 8). Each time, faith responds to something God has said. “He who comes to God must believe that He is, and that He is a rewarder of those who diligently seek Him” (v. 6).

Read: Hebrews 11:1–8

Hebrews 11:17–22 · Faith That Looks Beyond Death

Was Isaac raised from the dead? (vv. 17–19)

When God tested him, Abraham offered Isaac, “concluding that God was able to raise him up, even from the dead, from which he also received him in a figurative sense” (v. 19). Abraham trusted that God would keep His promise through Isaac (v. 18), even if it meant raising the dead.

MTB is clear that Isaac did not literally die and rise. God stopped Abraham’s hand and provided a ram (Genesis 22:11–13). Receiving Isaac back was like receiving him from death. Hebrews keeps the focus on Abraham’s tested trust and on God’s power to give life.

Read: Genesis 22:1–19, Hebrews 11:17–19

Hebrews 11:23–28 · The Faith of Moses

How could Moses bear “the reproach of Christ” centuries before Jesus came? (v. 26)

Moses chose to suffer with God’s people rather than enjoy Egypt’s pleasures, “esteeming the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures in Egypt” (v. 26). In Exodus he sided with his oppressed people (Exodus 2:11–15), long before Jesus was born.

MTB reads Moses’ choice as Hebrews does, in the light of Christ. The disgrace Moses accepted for standing with God’s people is the same kind of disgrace Christ’s people bear for Him. Hebrews uses the same words for its readers: “let us go forth to Him, outside the camp, bearing His reproach” (13:13). Moses “endured as seeing Him who is invisible” (v. 27), and believers can do the same.

Read: Exodus 2:11–15, Hebrews 11:24–27, Hebrews 13:12–13

Hebrews 11:32–38 · Faith in Deliverance and in Suffering

Does real faith always bring deliverance? (vv. 32–38)

No, and MTB says so plainly. By faith some “subdued kingdoms,” “stopped the mouths of lions,” and “escaped the edge of the sword” (vv. 33–34). By the same faith others “were tortured, not accepting deliverance, that they might obtain a better resurrection” (v. 35). They were mocked, imprisoned, and killed, people “of whom the world was not worthy” (v. 38).

Hebrews gives deliverance and faithful suffering equal honor. Faith is measured by trust in God and His promise, not by whether hardship is removed. Some refused release because they would not give up their faithfulness, and they looked to the resurrection.

Read: Hebrews 11:32–40 · Related: What did the Old Testament saints not receive? What does “made perfect” with us mean?

Hebrews 11:39–40 · Made Perfect Together

What did the Old Testament saints not receive? What does “made perfect” with us mean? (vv. 39–40)

All these “obtained a good testimony through faith” yet “did not receive the promise” (v. 39). They received real gifts from God, but not the full fulfillment He had promised. “God having provided something better for us, that they should not be made perfect apart from us” (v. 40).

MTB understands the “something better” as what God has now done through Christ. The faithful of old belong to the same saving purpose, and they will share in its completion together with believers today, as one people of God. Their example then turns the readers toward Jesus (12:1–2).

Read: Hebrews 11:13–16, Hebrews 11:39–12:2 · Related: What does it mean that Jesus was “made perfect”? Was He ever imperfect?; Does real faith always bring deliverance?

Hebrews 12 · 6 questions

Hebrews 12:1–3 · Run the Race Looking unto Jesus

Is the “cloud of witnesses” watching us from heaven? (v. 1)

“We are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses” (v. 1). The witnesses are the people of faith in chapter 11. MTB reads them mainly as witnesses who testify, not spectators who watch. Their lives give evidence that God’s promises are worth enduring for.

Some readers picture them as a crowd in the stands cheering us on. The text puts its weight elsewhere. The witnesses point us away from themselves to “Jesus, the author and finisher of our faith” (v. 2).

Read: Hebrews 11:39–12:3

What was “the joy that was set before Him”? (v. 2)

Jesus, “for the joy that was set before Him endured the cross, despising the shame” (v. 2). MTB reads this joy with His redeemed people in view. At the cross Jesus looked directly at people He was dying for as well as ahead to the people He was saving and would bring to glory. Hebrews has already said that God is “bringing many sons to glory” through Him, and that He “is not ashamed to call them brethren” (2:10–11).

Isaiah says the same of the suffering Servant: “He shall see the labor of His soul, and be satisfied” (Isaiah 53:11). The joy also includes the victory that followed, for He “has sat down at the right hand of the throne of God.” Others stress that exaltation, or His return to the Father’s presence. These belong together, but MTB puts His people at the center.

Read: Hebrews 2:10–13, Hebrews 12:1–3, Isaiah 53:10–12

Hebrews 12:4–13 · The Father’s Loving Discipline

Does God send hardship to punish His children? (vv. 5–11)

Hebrews quotes Proverbs 3:11–12: “For whom the LORD loves He chastens” (v. 6). Chastening here means training, the way a loving father raises a child. God disciplines His children “for our profit, that we may be partakers of His holiness” (v. 10).

MTB holds that this passage does not teach that every hardship is a penalty for a particular sin. Christ was “offered once to bear the sins of many” (9:28). The Father uses hardship to train His children. It hurts at the time, “nevertheless, afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it” (v. 11).

The response is to strengthen one another: “strengthen the hands which hang down, and the feeble knees,” so that what is lame may “be healed” (vv. 12–13).

Read: Proverbs 3:11–12, Hebrews 9:28, Hebrews 12:4–13

Hebrews 12:14–17 · Watch That No One Falls Short of Grace

What is the “root of bitterness”? (v. 15)

The church is to watch over one another, “lest anyone fall short of the grace of God; lest any root of bitterness springing up cause trouble, and by this many become defiled” (v. 15). The picture comes from Deuteronomy 29:18, where Moses warns against anyone “whose heart turns away today from the LORD our God,” which would be “a root bearing bitterness or wormwood.” In the text, the root is a person or attitude that turns from God and spreads poison through the community.

As a spiritual application, MTB sees how resentment often grows the same way. A hurt, resentment, or an offense that is not forgiven and yielded to God can take root quietly and later harm many people. Falling short of grace can mean missing the enjoyment of God’s favor in the very place resentment took hold. The answer is to bring the actual hurt to God and receive His grace there. This is an application. The verse itself warns the church against anyone who turns away from the LORD and spreads that turning to others.

Read: Deuteronomy 29:18, Hebrews 12:14–17

Could Esau not repent even though he wanted to? (v. 17)

Who they are. The warning is for “anyone” in the congregation (v. 15). Esau is the example, and Scripture names him plainly as the “profane person” of verse 16, set beside the “fornicator.” Both trade what God has made holy for the appetite of the moment.

What he received. As Isaac’s firstborn, Esau held the birthright, his place as heir in the family that carried God’s promise to Abraham and Isaac (Genesis 25:29–34).

What he turned away from. He sold that birthright “for one morsel of food” (v. 16). Genesis says, “Thus Esau despised his birthright” (Genesis 25:34). To be profane is to treat what is holy as common. Esau valued a single meal above God’s promise.

What consequence God warns of. “Afterward, when he wanted to inherit the blessing, he was rejected, for he found no place for repentance, though he sought it diligently with tears” (v. 17). MTB reads the “it” Esau sought with tears as the blessing (Genesis 27:30–38). He could not undo his choice or reverse the blessing Isaac had given. Some choices cannot be called back.

The verse does not describe a person who longs to turn back to God and is turned away. It warns the church not to trade what God gives for momentary relief. Anyone turning to God in repentance will find mercy through Christ (4:16).

Read: Genesis 25:29–34, Genesis 27:30–38, Hebrews 4:16, Hebrews 12:15–17

Hebrews 12:25–29 · A Kingdom That Cannot Be Shaken

Does “See that you do not refuse Him who speaks” warn believers? (vv. 25–29)

Who they are. The warning is addressed to the congregation that has “come to Mount Zion” (v. 22), and the writer includes himself: “much more shall we not escape” (v. 25). It describes no one’s prior standing. It speaks to everyone who has heard God speak in His Son.

What they received. They have come “to Jesus the Mediator of the new covenant, and to the blood of sprinkling that speaks better things than that of Abel” (v. 24), and they are “receiving a kingdom which cannot be shaken” (v. 28). Israel heard God on earth at Sinai. These hearers are addressed by Him from heaven.

What they turned away from. The danger is refusing “Him who speaks” (v. 25), turning away from God’s voice in His Son, as Israel at Sinai “begged that the word should not be spoken to them anymore” (v. 19).

What consequence God warns of. “If they did not escape who refused Him who spoke on earth, much more shall we not escape if we turn away from Him who speaks from heaven” (v. 25). God has promised to shake heaven and earth so that only what cannot be shaken remains (vv. 26–27; Haggai 2:6), “for our God is a consuming fire” (v. 29). The warning carries the full weight of the God who speaks.

MTB holds this last warning together with the hope it rests on. Those who belong to Christ receive a kingdom that cannot be shaken. The right response is gratitude and reverent worship: “let us have grace, by which we may serve God acceptably with reverence and godly fear” (v. 28).

Read: Haggai 2:6, Hebrews 12:18–29 · Related: Can a true believer lose salvation?

V. Faithful Conduct and the Closing Appeal (Hebrews 13:1–25)

Hebrews 13 · 2 questions

Hebrews 13:7–9 · Remember Your Leaders; Jesus Christ Is the Same

Does “obey those who rule over you” mean church members must obey leaders without question? (vv. 7, 17)

Hebrews speaks of leaders twice. Verse 7 most likely looks back to former leaders “who have spoken the word of God to you.” Readers are to consider “the outcome of their conduct” and follow their faith. Verse 17 speaks of present leaders: “Obey those who rule over you, and be submissive, for they watch out for your souls, as those who must give account.”

MTB holds that the call to respond to leaders is real, and it stands beside the leaders’ own accountability to God. They are to watch over souls, speak God’s word, and live a faith worth following. The passage gives no leader authority above God’s word, and it does not ask the church to follow anyone into what God forbids, for “we ought to obey God rather than men” (Acts 5:29). The fixed point is Christ: “Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever” (v. 8).

Read: Hebrews 13:7–9, Hebrews 13:17–18, Acts 5:29

Hebrews 13:10–14 · Outside the Camp

What is the altar Christians have? Is it the Lord’s Supper? (v. 10)

“We have an altar from which those who serve the tabernacle have no right to eat” (v. 10). MTB understands the altar as Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice on the cross. Hebrews explains with the Day of Atonement. The bodies of the animals whose blood was brought into the sanctuary were burned outside the camp and were not eaten (v. 11; Leviticus 16:27). In the same way Jesus, “that He might sanctify the people with His own blood, suffered outside the gate” (v. 12).

To eat from this altar means to share in the benefits of His finished sacrifice. Those who still look to the old tabernacle as their way to God have no share in it. Some readers see a reference to the Lord’s Supper. MTB allows a secondary echo of Communion but keeps the focus on grace and on Christ’s sacrifice, since verse 9 has just said the heart is established “by grace, not with foods.”

The response is to “go forth to Him, outside the camp, bearing His reproach,” for “here we have no continuing city, but we seek the one to come” (vv. 13–14).

Read: Leviticus 16:27, Hebrews 13:9–16
