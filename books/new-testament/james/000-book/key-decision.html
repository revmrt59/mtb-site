Important questions in the text, and how MTB understands them.

Book Questions

Who wrote James, and to whom? (James 1:1)

The writer calls himself “James, a bondservant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ” (James 1:1). MTB understands him most likely to be James the Lord’s brother, who led the church in Jerusalem (Galatians 1:19; Acts 15:13–21). The risen Jesus appeared to him (1 Corinthians 15:7). It is telling that he introduces himself only as a servant, not as Jesus’ brother.

Other men named James appear in the New Testament, and some scholars question whether the Lord’s brother wrote the letter. The letter itself names no more than “James.”

“The twelve tribes which are scattered abroad” (James 1:1) most likely means Jewish believers in Jesus living away from their homeland (compare James 2:1). A date around AD 48–50, written from Jerusalem, is a reasonable historical judgment, not something the letter states.

Read: James 1:1, Galatians 1:19, Acts 15:13–21, 1 Corinthians 15:7

Are “the rich” in James believers or unbelievers? (James 1:9–11; 2:1–7; 5:1–6)

It depends on the passage. In James 1:9–11 MTB reads the rich person as a believer. James tells “the lowly brother” to glory in his exaltation, “but the rich in his humiliation” (James 1:9–10). In Greek the command to glory carries over to both. The wealthy believer is to rejoice in the humbling truth that he also depends wholly on God, because life and riches fade like a flower (Isaiah 40:6–8).

In James 2:6–7 and 5:1–6, James speaks of rich oppressors who drag believers into court, blaspheme the name of Christ, withhold wages, and condemn the innocent. Many read James 1:10 in light of those passages and see an unbeliever there too. MTB holds the believer reading in chapter 1 because of its close parallel with verse 9.

Wealth itself is not proof of condemnation, and poverty does not earn salvation. James condemns the conduct he names: partiality, fraud, luxury at others’ expense, and violence.

Read: James 1:9–11, James 2:1–7, James 5:1–6, Isaiah 40:6–8 · Related: Does God favor the poor?

What is “the perfect law of liberty”? (James 1:25; 2:8–12)

James speaks of “the perfect law of liberty” (James 1:25) and of “the royal law,” “You shall love your neighbor as yourself” (James 2:8; Leviticus 19:18). MTB reads the law of liberty together with “the implanted word” (James 1:21). It is God’s word received into the heart, which frees His people to live as He intends and holds their speech and actions accountable.

This fits MTB’s settled understanding of the law. Believers are not saved by keeping it, but God’s moral law remains a guide to a life that pleases Him, written on the heart by His Spirit (Jeremiah 31:33). Love for neighbor is “the fulfillment of the law” (Romans 13:8–10).

Freedom and obedience belong together. The law of liberty is not freedom from accountability, for James says we “will be judged by the law of liberty” (James 2:12).

Read: James 1:21–25, James 2:8–12, Leviticus 19:18, Jeremiah 31:33, Romans 13:8–10 · Related: Does breaking one command make all sins equal?

What “death” does sin bring a believer? (James 1:14–15; 5:19–20)

James traces a progression: desire “gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death” (James 1:15). He ends the letter on the same theme. Whoever turns a wandering believer back “will save a soul from death” (James 5:20).

MTB understands this death, for a believer, as the destructive course of sin. Sin begins its deadly work as soon as it is given room, damaging joy, relationships, spiritual vitality, and integrity. Left unchecked, it can end in premature physical death (Romans 6:23; 1 Corinthians 11:30–32). The warning is serious and calls for a real turning from sin.

Faithful readers see it differently. Some take the death as eternal death for someone whose faith was never genuine. Others see eternal death as a real danger for a genuine believer who turns away. Still others limit it to physical death as God’s discipline. MTB’s emphasis is the damage sin itself brings, together with the hope that God forgives and restores those who turn back (1 John 1:7–9).

Read: James 1:14–15, James 5:19–20, Romans 6:23, 1 Corinthians 11:30–32, 1 John 1:7–9 · Related: Who is the wanderer?

James 1 · 5 questions

James 1:2–4 · Joy in Trials That Produce Patience

Does “count it all joy” mean we should feel happy about suffering? (vv. 2–4)

No. James calls believers to joy because of what God can bring about through testing: “the testing of your faith produces patience” (v. 3), and patience leads to being “perfect and complete, lacking nothing” (v. 4). The joy rests on God’s purpose, not on the pain itself.

This is not a command to deny grief; Scripture gives believers room to lament. Nor does “perfect” mean sinless. It describes faith matured through endurance, and James himself says, “we all stumble in many things” (3:2).

Read: James 1:2–4, James 3:2

James 1:5–8 · Asking God for Wisdom in Faith

Does a single doubt mean God will not answer my prayer? (vv. 5–8)

No. James says to “ask in faith, with no doubting,” and describes the doubter as “a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways” (vv. 6, 8). The picture is divided loyalty: a person tossed back and forth between trusting God and trusting something else (compare 4:8).

This is not a warning against honest questions or a moment of fear. The God “who gives to all liberally and without reproach” (v. 5) invites anyone who lacks wisdom to come to Him. The request for wisdom, especially wisdom to meet trials faithfully, stays at the center.

Read: James 1:5–8, James 4:8

James 1:12–15 · Enduring Temptation and the Birth of Sin

Is “the crown of life” a special reward for exceptional Christians? (v. 12)

MTB reads the crown of life mainly as eternal life itself, the life of God, pictured as a victor’s wreath and promised “to those who love Him” (v. 12; compare Revelation 2:10). Believers begin to experience that life now, including freedom from the destructive fruit of sin, and will receive it in full in the future. It is God’s gift, not a special class of salvation for unusually successful believers.

Endurance plays a part in that present experience. As tested faith grows toward maturity (vv. 3–4), believers enter more fully into the abundant life Jesus came to give (John 10:10).

Read: James 1:12, Revelation 2:10, John 10:10

If God tests His people, does He also tempt them to sin? (vv. 13–14)

No. “God cannot be tempted by evil, nor does He Himself tempt anyone” (v. 13). James moves from trials (vv. 2, 12) to temptation (vv. 13–14), and the same Greek word can mean either. A hardship can become an occasion for sin, but the enticement comes from a person’s “own desires” (v. 14).

James is stressing personal responsibility. He is not denying that Satan tempts people, as Scripture teaches elsewhere. He also does not call every human desire sinful or treat the first pull of temptation as sin already committed.

Read: James 1:12–15 · Related: What “death” does sin bring a believer?

James 1:19–21 · Receiving the Implanted Word with Meekness

If believers are already saved, why does James speak of the word that can “save your souls”? (v. 21)

James writes to people the Father has already “brought us forth by the word of truth” (v. 18). Yet they are to “receive with meekness the implanted word, which is able to save your souls” (v. 21).

MTB holds present deliverance and final salvation together. The word rescues believers now from sin’s destructive work and keeps them toward the completion of their salvation (Romans 13:11; 1 Peter 1:5–9). “Souls” here means the whole person, including present conduct and final destiny. Good conduct is the fruit of life received, not the price of it.

Read: James 1:18–21, Romans 13:11, 1 Peter 1:5–9

James 2 · 5 questions

Does God favor the poor? Are the poor automatically saved? (vv. 1–7)

James asks, “Has God not chosen the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom which He promised to those who love Him?” (v. 5). Favoring a well-dressed visitor contradicts faith in “the Lord of glory” (v. 1), who overturns the world’s ranking.

Poverty does not earn election or salvation, and wealth alone does not prove unbelief. The kingdom is promised “to those who love Him.” James condemns the partiality he describes, and he does not assume that every rich person in the assembly is an oppressor.

Read: James 2:1–7 · Related: Are “the rich” in James believers or unbelievers?

Does breaking one command make all sins equal? (vv. 10–11)

“Whoever shall keep the whole law, and yet stumble in one point, he is guilty of all” (v. 10). The commands come from one God. Breaking any of them makes a person a transgressor of His law, so obedience in some areas cannot excuse partiality in another (vv. 9–11).

This does not mean every sin is equally serious, or that one offense amounts to committing every sin. James is removing the excuse of selective obedience.

Read: James 2:8–11 · Related: What is “the perfect law of liberty”?

Does verse 13 mean a believer who fails to show mercy will be condemned? (v. 13)

“Judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy. Mercy triumphs over judgment” (v. 13). MTB reads this warning as pointing ultimately to final judgment on a merciless person whose profession of faith is not genuine. The next verses make it concrete with a brother or sister left hungry and cold (vv. 14–17).

Because James is writing to the church, the warning also shakes genuine believers out of favoritism and calls them to repent and show mercy. Settled, unrepentant mercilessness exposes a dead profession; a believer’s failure calls for repentance, not despair. Showing mercy does not purchase salvation. God’s mercy in Christ is the ground of our confidence, and the mercy we show is its evidence (Matthew 5:7; 18:21–35).

Read: James 2:12–17, Matthew 5:7, Matthew 18:21–35 · Related: Do James and Paul contradict each other?

Do James and Paul contradict each other about faith and works? (vv. 14–26)

No. Paul explains how God declares the ungodly righteous through faith, apart from works (Romans 4:1–5). James answers a different problem: someone who “says he has faith but does not have works” (v. 14), like a person who sends a hungry brother or sister away with kind words (vv. 15–16). Such faith “is dead” (v. 17).

Abraham shows the order. He “believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness” (v. 23; Genesis 15:6) long before he offered Isaac (Genesis 22:1–18). His obedience did not earn that standing. It showed his faith to be real and brought it to maturity: “by works faith was made perfect” (v. 22). So when James says “a man is justified by works, and not by faith only” (v. 24), MTB understands him to mean that genuine faith is shown to be real through obedience, before God and not merely before people.

Ephesians 2:8–10 puts the two together: we are saved by grace through faith, and created for the good works God prepared. Interpreters differ on how James’s wording relates to God’s final verdict. MTB holds that works never earn salvation, and that faith which never acts is not saving faith.

Read: James 2:14–26, Romans 4:1–5, Genesis 15:6, Genesis 22:1–18, Ephesians 2:8–10 · Related: Does Rahab’s example approve her lie?

Does James approve of Rahab’s lie? (v. 25)

No. James commends what he names: Rahab “received the messengers and sent them out another way” (v. 25). Her welcome and protection of them expressed the faith she confessed in Israel’s God (Joshua 2:8–13).

James does not give blanket approval to every action in her story. His point is that living faith acts, whether in Abraham the patriarch or in “Rahab the harlot.”

Read: James 2:25–26, Joshua 2

James 3 · 2 questions

James 3:1–2 · Teachers and the Stricter Judgment

What is the “stricter judgment” for teachers? (vv. 1–2)

James warns, “let not many of you become teachers, knowing that we shall receive a stricter judgment” (v. 1). MTB reads this mainly as the final accounting before God, which fits James’s picture of the Judge standing at the door (5:9). God also disciplines His children now as a Father (Hebrews 12:5–11), and misleading or wounding words from a teacher can bring heavier consequences because they affect others.

James includes himself and adds, “we all stumble in many things” (v. 2). The warning concerns accountability for what God has entrusted (Luke 12:48). It does not make a teacher’s salvation depend on performance, and it does not mean every hardship a teacher faces is punishment for a particular mistake.

Read: James 3:1–2, James 5:9, Hebrews 12:5–11, Luke 12:48

James 3:3–6 · The Tongue’s Small Size and Great Power

Do our words have creative power? (vv. 3–6)

James shows how much power words have. Like a bit or a rudder, the small tongue steers much larger things, and like a spark it “sets on fire the course of nature” (v. 6), most likely meaning the whole course of a human life.

James’s point is moral: speech can direct and defile the whole person. The passage gives no basis for treating spoken words as a force that creates reality, for diagnosing the spiritual source of every careless word, or for claiming sinless maturity, since “we all stumble” (v. 2).

Read: James 3:2–6

James 4 · 5 questions

Does “you murder” mean literal murder? (v. 2)

The NKJV reads, “You lust and do not have. You murder and covet and cannot obtain” (v. 2). The word carries its full force. James follows selfish craving to the point where it can lead to actual killing, the same progression he described in 1:14–15. He names no particular murder.

Many read “murder” here as a figure for hatred. Jesus does hold people accountable for anger and contempt (Matthew 5:21–22), and that brings the warning home to every heart. But MTB does not soften James’s word into a figure of speech. In vv. 2–3 James also separates two problems with prayer: not asking at all, and asking for selfish ends.

Read: James 4:1–3, James 1:14–15, Matthew 5:21–22

Who “yearns jealously” in verse 5? (v. 5)

The NKJV reads, “The Spirit who dwells in us yearns jealously” (v. 5). MTB gives priority to God’s jealous desire for His people’s wholehearted love, expressed through the Spirit He has made to dwell in them. It follows James’s charge of spiritual unfaithfulness and “friendship with the world” (v. 4), and it echoes the LORD “whose name is Jealous” (Exodus 34:14).

The Greek can also be read as describing the human spirit’s tendency toward envy, and some translations take it that way. Either way, the answer follows at once: “But He gives more grace” (v. 6). God’s jealousy is His rightful claim on His people’s love.

Read: James 4:4–6, Exodus 34:14, 1 Corinthians 3:16

Does “resist the devil” describe a method of deliverance ministry? (v. 7)

James writes, “submit to God. Resist the devil and he will flee from you” (v. 7). Resisting the devil is part of submitting to God. It sits within a call to repentance that begins with God’s grace to the humble (v. 6) and continues: draw near, cleanse your hands, purify your hearts, mourn over sin, and humble yourselves (vv. 8–10).

Teaching on deliverance should be built on the passages that address it directly. Here the focus is a wholehearted return to God, with His promise to draw near and lift up the humble.

Read: James 4:6–10

Does “who are you to judge another?” forbid correcting anyone? (vv. 11–12)

No. James forbids speaking evil of a brother or sister and setting oneself up as their judge (v. 11). That breaks the royal law of neighbor love (2:8). God alone is the Lawgiver, “who is able to save and to destroy” (v. 12).

James is not banning truthful correction or the recognition of wrongdoing. He ends the letter by urging believers to turn back a wandering brother or sister (5:19–20). What he condemns is contempt, slander, and condemnation.

Read: James 4:11–12, James 2:8, James 5:19–20

Is it wrong to make plans? Must we always say “if the Lord wills”? (vv. 13–17)

No. James confronts traders who speak as though tomorrow, their lives, and their profits were in their own hands. “What is your life? It is even a vapor” (v. 14). The sin is arrogant boasting (v. 16), not planning or trade.

“If the Lord wills” (v. 15) is not a formula to recite. It describes plans made in real dependence on God, whether or not the words are spoken. James then states the principle broadly: “to him who knows to do good and does not do it, to him it is sin” (v. 17).

Read: James 4:13–17

James 5 · 6 questions

Who is “the just” whom the rich have murdered? (v. 6)

James charges the rich: “You have condemned, you have murdered the just; he does not resist you” (v. 6). MTB reads “the just” as standing for the innocent people the rich have harmed, such as the laborers whose wages they kept back by fraud (v. 4; Leviticus 19:13). James leaves the individual identity open.

Some see Jesus Himself here. Christ’s unjust suffering does illuminate the pattern (1 Peter 2:21–23), but MTB does not make Jesus the only person James means or weaken the force of the charges against these oppressors.

Read: James 5:1–6, Leviticus 19:13, 1 Peter 2:21–23 · Related: Are “the rich” in James believers or unbelievers?

Does James forbid all oaths, even in court? (v. 12)

“Do not swear, either by heaven or by earth or with any other oath. But let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes,’ and your ‘No,’ ‘No’” (v. 12). Following Jesus (Matthew 5:33–37), James forbids everyday oath-taking used to prop up a doubtful word or slip out of responsibility for it. A believer’s plain word should carry its own weight.

MTB understands formal legal oaths to be permissible. Jesus answered when the high priest put Him under oath (Matthew 26:63–64), Paul called God as witness in serious matters (2 Corinthians 1:23; Galatians 1:20), and God confirmed His promise with an oath (Hebrews 6:13–17). Some Christians refuse all oaths on the basis of this verse. The same standard of complete truthfulness applies in court and out of it.

Read: James 5:12, Matthew 5:33–37, Matthew 26:63–64, Hebrews 6:13–17

Is anointing with oil for today? Is the oil medicine or a sign? (v. 14)

James gives the church a continuing practice: “Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord” (v. 14).

Oil could be used practically, as in the Samaritan’s care for the wounded man (Luke 10:34). In this passage, though, MTB understands the anointing as symbolic: a tangible sign of the Holy Spirit’s power coming upon the sick person to heal through the prayer of faith (compare Mark 6:13). The Greek verb simply describes applying oil; the setting gives it meaning. Some read the oil as medicine, and others hold that this practice belonged to the early church. MTB does not set aside medical care, but that is not the point of the oil here.

Read: James 5:13–15, Luke 10:34, Mark 6:13 · Related: Does the prayer of faith guarantee healing?

Does the prayer of faith guarantee healing? If a person is not healed, is it a lack of faith? (v. 15)

“The prayer of faith will save the sick, and the Lord will raise him up” (v. 15). In this setting “save” and “raise up” describe recovery from sickness, and the Lord is the one who heals. MTB understands the prayer of faith as prayer the Holy Spirit can prompt and empower for a particular healing (compare the gift of faith in 1 Corinthians 12:9). The church prays expectantly, under the Lord’s will (4:15; Luke 22:42).

The Spirit may withhold specific faith for an immediate healing while the church keeps praying and caring for the person. When sickness continues, that gives no grounds for blaming the sick person or their family. And every believer has the hope of full and lasting healing in the resurrection (Romans 8:23; 1 Corinthians 15:42–44).

Read: James 5:14–16, 1 Corinthians 12:9, Luke 22:42, Romans 8:23, 1 Corinthians 15:42–44 · Related: Is sickness caused by sin?

Is sickness caused by sin? (vv. 15–18)

Not always. Jesus made clear that a man’s blindness was not caused by his own sin or his parents’ (John 9:1–3). Scripture also shows that God can use sickness to discipline, as when the LORD struck King Uzziah with leprosy (2 Chronicles 26:16–21) and King Asa’s severe disease followed his rejection of the LORD’s warning (2 Chronicles 16:7–12). Satan can also be a source of sickness and affliction (Job 2:7; Luke 13:16). James says, “if he has committed sins, he will be forgiven” (v. 15). The “if” leaves room for sickness with no personal sin involved. Where the Spirit exposes sin connected with suffering, believers are to “confess your trespasses to one another, and pray for one another, that you may be healed” (v. 16).

Confession calls for humility, appropriate confidentiality, and ongoing care. Where spiritual bondage is discerned, the church responds with prayer and pastoral care. The power lies with the Lord who answers: Elijah “was a man with a nature like ours,” yet God answered his earnest prayer (vv. 17–18; 1 Kings 17:1; 18:41–45).

Read: James 5:15–18, 1 Kings 17:1, 1 Kings 18:41–45, John 9:1–3, 2 Chronicles 26:16–21, 2 Chronicles 16:7–12, Job 2:7, Luke 13:16

Who is the wanderer, and whose sins are covered? (vv. 19–20)

“If anyone among you wanders from the truth, and someone turns him back” (v. 19). MTB takes the wanderer to be a genuine believer. The one who turns him back “will save a soul from death” (v. 20), interrupting the destructive course of sin that can end in premature physical death (1:14–15).

MTB takes the “multitude of sins” covered as chiefly the wanderer’s. God forgives the repentant through Christ (1 John 1:7–9). Love also covers in another sense. It deals with wrongdoing truthfully and protects a repentant person from gossip and needless exposure (Proverbs 10:12; 1 Peter 4:8), while still supporting accountability and protecting those who were harmed. Other views of this death are set out under Book Questions.

Read: James 5:19–20, James 1:14–15, 1 John 1:7–9, Proverbs 10:12, 1 Peter 4:8 · Related: What “death” does sin bring a believer?
