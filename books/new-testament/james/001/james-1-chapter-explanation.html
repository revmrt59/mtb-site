Chapter Study

James 1

Verse-by-Verse
James 1:1
James 1:1
NKJV

James 1:1 James, a bondservant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ, To the twelve tribes which are scattered abroad: Greetings.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:1 This letter is from James, a slave of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ. I am writing to the “twelve tribes” — Jewish believers scattered abroad. Greetings! (NLT)

Main Idea

James introduces himself as “a bondservant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ,” a servant who belongs to God and acknowledges Jesus as Lord. He writes to “the twelve tribes which are scattered abroad,” best understood as Jewish believers in Jesus living away from their homeland.

Verse Explanation

James introduces himself as “a bondservant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ,” a servant who belongs to God and acknowledges Jesus as Lord. He writes to “the twelve tribes which are scattered abroad,” best understood as Jewish believers in Jesus living away from their homeland.

“James, a bondservant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ,”

The writer gives his name and nothing more about his family or position. Several men named James appear in the New Testament, and the one most likely meant here is James the Lord’s brother, whom Paul calls “James, the Lord’s brother” (Galatians 1:19). The risen Jesus appeared to him (1 Corinthians 15:7), and he became a leader of the church in Jerusalem (Acts 15:13).

James could have opened with that relationship or that standing. Instead he calls himself “a bondservant,” a slave who belongs to a master. He belongs to “God” and to “the Lord Jesus Christ.” He names God and Jesus side by side as the ones he serves, and he calls his own brother “Lord.” A man who grew up in the same household with Jesus now speaks of Him as his Master.

“To the twelve tribes which are scattered abroad:”

James writes to “the twelve tribes,” the name for the whole people of Israel. These readers are “scattered abroad,” living away from the land of Israel. The phrase is best understood as Jewish believers in Jesus living outside their homeland. James later speaks of their “assembly” and of “the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ” (2:1–2), so he is writing to believers.

“Greetings.”

The single word was the ordinary opening of a Greek letter. James wastes no time. After this one word he moves straight to trials and joy.

Study Notes

The Greek word behind “scattered abroad” is the word from which English gets “Diaspora,” the usual term for Jews living outside the land of Israel. Peter uses the same word in addressing “the pilgrims of the Dispersion” (1 Peter 1:1).

The Greek word translated “Greetings” is the verb for rejoicing. In James 1:2 the next word James uses is the related noun “joy,” so the letter moves from a greeting of joy to a call to joy.

Mark 6:3 names James first among the brothers of Jesus. Jude 1:1 calls its writer “brother of James,” most likely the same James.

James 1:2–4 — Joy in Trials That Produce Patience

James 1:2
James 1:2
NKJV

James 1:2 My brethren, count it all joy when you fall into various trials,(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:2 Dear brothers and sisters, when troubles of any kind come your way, consider it an opportunity for great joy. (NLT)

Main Idea

James tells his brethren to “count it all joy” when they fall into various trials. Hardship of many kinds will come, and believers can meet it with joy because of what God is able to accomplish through it, as the next verse explains.

Verse Explanation

James tells his brethren to “count it all joy” when they fall into various trials. Hardship of many kinds will come, and believers can meet it with joy because of what God is able to accomplish through it, as the next verse explains.

“My brethren,”

James uses this family word often in the letter. His commands may be blunt, but they come from someone who counts himself part of the same family of faith.

“count it all joy when you fall into various trials,”

“Count it” means to consider, to regard something in a certain way. James is not telling his readers to feel happy about pain. He is telling them how to evaluate their trials in light of what God can do through them. “All joy” means pure, full joy, nothing less.

The word “when” assumes that trials will come. James does not say “if.” To “fall into” trials pictures being surrounded by them, encountering them without choosing them. The trials are “various,” many-colored, of every kind: sickness, poverty, mistreatment, loss, and pressure to give up. James does not name a single kind of hardship. He speaks to all of them.

The reason for this joy is not the trial itself. It is what the trial can produce, which James explains in the next two verses. This call to joy does not forbid grief. Believers may lament and weep. James is giving them a way to see their hardship that keeps hope alive inside the pain.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “trials” can mean either an outward test or an inward temptation to sin. In 1:2–3 the context points to hardships that test faith. In 1:13–14 the related verb refers to enticement toward evil.

James 1:3
James 1:3
NKJV

James 1:3 knowing that the testing of your faith produces patience.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:3 For you know that when your faith is tested, your endurance has a chance to grow. (NLT)

Main Idea

The ground for that joy is knowledge: “the testing of your faith produces patience.” Trials put faith to the test, and faith that is tested grows steady endurance.

Verse Explanation

The ground for that joy is knowledge: “the testing of your faith produces patience.” Trials put faith to the test, and faith that is tested grows steady endurance.

“knowing that the testing of your faith produces patience.”

Joy rests on knowledge. James says believers can count trials as joy “knowing” something about them. They know that “the testing of your faith produces patience.”

“Testing” is the process of proving something genuine, as metal is proved in a fire. Trials put faith under pressure, and the pressure shows whether the faith is real and makes it stronger. The faith being tested is “your faith,” trust in God. Hardship tests whether that trust will hold.

The result of this testing is “patience.” The word means steady endurance, the strength to keep standing under a load without collapsing or quitting. It is not passive waiting. It is active staying power. Faith that is tested grows steady endurance, the way a muscle grows stronger under resistance.

Study Notes

Paul describes a similar chain: “tribulation produces perseverance; and perseverance, character; and character, hope” (Romans 5:3–4). Peter speaks of “the genuineness of your faith” being tested by fire (1 Peter 1:7).

The Greek word translated “patience” here is translated “perseverance” in 5:11 (“the perseverance of Job”). James begins and ends the letter with endurance under trial.

James 1:4
James 1:4
NKJV

James 1:4 But let patience have its perfect work, that you may be perfect and complete, lacking nothing.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:4 So let it grow, for when your endurance is fully developed, you will be perfect and complete, needing nothing. (NLT)

Main Idea

Believers must let patience finish its work, so that they may be “perfect and complete, lacking nothing.” Endurance carried through to the end produces mature believers whose faith has been developed in every part.

Verse Explanation

Believers must let patience finish its work, so that they may be “perfect and complete, lacking nothing.” Endurance carried through to the end produces mature believers whose faith has been developed in every part.

“But let patience have its perfect work,”

James adds a command. Endurance has a work to do in the believer, and it must be allowed to finish that work. The command is not about how long a hardship lasts. James calls believers to remain faithful to God through testing and let endurance do its work. A person can cut that work short by giving up, turning against God, or looking for a way out that abandons faithfulness. Remaining faithful does not mean hiding grief or refusing to lament. Believers can bring their sorrow to God and still keep trusting Him (1:2).

“that you may be perfect and complete, lacking nothing.”

The goal is maturity. “Perfect” here means mature, full-grown, brought to the end God has in view. It does not mean sinless, for James will soon say “we all stumble in many things” (3:2). “Complete” means whole in every part, with nothing missing. “Lacking nothing” says the same thing from the other side.

Endurance carried through to the end produces believers whose faith has been developed in every part. God is not only getting His people through trials. He is using trials to make them whole.

Study Notes

James uses the word “perfect” twice in this verse: patience is to have its “perfect” work so that believers may be “perfect.” The same word describes the “perfect gift” from above (1:17), the “perfect law of liberty” (1:25), and the “perfect man” who does not stumble in word (3:2).

James 1:5–8 — Asking God for Wisdom in Faith

James 1:5
James 1:5
NKJV

James 1:5 If any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all liberally and without reproach, and it will be given to him.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:5 If you need wisdom, ask our generous God, and he will give it to you. He will not rebuke you for asking. (NLT)

Main Idea

Anyone who lacks the wisdom to walk faithfully through trials should ask God for it. God “gives to all liberally and without reproach,” so the one who asks can be sure that wisdom will be given.

Verse Explanation

Anyone who lacks the wisdom to walk faithfully through trials should ask God for it. God “gives to all liberally and without reproach,” so the one who asks can be sure that wisdom will be given.

“If any of you lacks wisdom,”

James has just spoken of believers “lacking nothing” (1:4). Now he names something they may lack: “wisdom.” The link is deliberate. In the middle of trials, believers need wisdom to know how to respond faithfully. In James, wisdom is practical. It is knowing how to live in a way that pleases God, especially under pressure (3:13, 17).

“let him ask of God,”

The answer is simple. Wisdom is God’s gift. The person who lacks it does not need to work harder to produce it. He needs to pray.

“who gives to all liberally and without reproach,”

James describes the God to whom believers pray. He “gives to all liberally.” “Liberally” means generously, simply, without holding back. God is not a reluctant giver.

He also gives “without reproach.” He does not scold the one who asks or make him feel foolish for needing help. A person may hesitate to ask because he feels he should already know what to do, or because he has failed before. God does not answer requests for wisdom with a lecture.

“and it will be given to him.”

The verse ends with a promise. The one who asks God for wisdom can be sure that God will give it.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “liberally” can mean either generously or with single-minded sincerity. Both ideas fit this verse, and both contrast with the “double-minded” person in 1:8.

James 1:6
James 1:6
NKJV

James 1:6 But let him ask in faith, with no doubting, for he who doubts is like a wave of the sea driven and tossed by the wind.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:6 But when you ask him, be sure that your faith is in God alone. Do not waver, for a person with divided loyalty is as unsettled as a wave of the sea that is blown and tossed by the wind. (NLT)

Main Idea

The request must be made “in faith, with no doubting.” The one who doubts is like a wave of the sea, driven and tossed by the wind and never settled in trust toward God.

Verse Explanation

The request must be made “in faith, with no doubting.” The one who doubts is like a wave of the sea, driven and tossed by the wind and never settled in trust toward God.

“But let him ask in faith, with no doubting,”

The promise of 1:5 has a condition. The one who asks must ask “in faith,” trusting God to give what He has promised. He must ask “with no doubting.” In this setting, doubting means being divided in trust toward God, wavering between relying on Him and relying on something else.

“for he who doubts is like a wave of the sea driven and tossed by the wind.”

James paints the doubter as “a wave of the sea.” A wave has no direction of its own. It is “driven and tossed by the wind,” pushed one way and then another by whatever force is acting on it. The person who asks God for wisdom while his trust wavers is just as unsettled. Today he trusts God. Tomorrow he trusts his own schemes or the opinions of others. He never settles.

James is not describing a believer who brings honest questions to God or who feels fear in a hard moment. The Psalms are full of honest questions. James is describing divided trust, a heart that will not settle on God.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “doubting” can mean to be divided or to dispute with oneself. It describes inward wavering rather than a single question.

James 1:7
James 1:7
NKJV

James 1:7 For let not that man suppose that he will receive anything from the Lord;(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:7 Such people should not expect to receive anything from the Lord. (NLT)

Main Idea

The person who asks God for wisdom while his trust wavers between God and doubt should not suppose that he will receive anything from the Lord. James warns against divided trust in God, not against an honest question or a passing moment of fear.

Verse Explanation

The person who asks God for wisdom while his trust wavers between God and doubt should not suppose that he will receive anything from the Lord. James warns against divided trust in God, not against an honest question or a passing moment of fear.

“For let not that man suppose that he will receive anything from the Lord;”

The consequence is serious. “That man,” the one whose trust wavers between God and doubt, should not “suppose that he will receive anything from the Lord.” He should not expect God to answer his request for wisdom.

The problem is not that God is unwilling. James has just said that God gives generously (1:5). The problem lies in the person who asks. He comes to God while keeping his trust divided, and that divided heart cannot receive what God offers. James warns against divided trust in God, not against an honest question or a passing moment of fear.

Study Notes

None.

James 1:8
James 1:8
NKJV

James 1:8 he is a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:8 Their loyalty is divided between God and the world, and they are unstable in everything they do. (NLT)

Main Idea

James calls the doubter “a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways.” A heart divided toward God does not stay confined to prayer; it unsettles the whole of a person’s life.

Verse Explanation

James calls the doubter “a double-minded man, unstable in all his ways.” A heart divided toward God does not stay confined to prayer; it unsettles the whole of a person’s life.

“he is a double-minded man,”

James names the doubter: “a double-minded man.” The phrase means a person with two souls or two minds. Part of him leans toward God, and part of him leans away. He wants God’s help but also wants to keep his options open.

“unstable in all his ways.”

His instability does not stay inside his prayers. It spreads into every part of his life: his decisions, his relationships, his priorities. A heart divided toward God unsettles the whole of a person’s life. James will return to this word in 4:8: “purify your hearts, you double-minded.”

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “double-minded” joins “two” and “soul.” James uses it here and in 4:8, and these are its only two occurrences in the New Testament.

James 1:9–11 — The Lowly Brother and the Rich Who Fade

James 1:9
James 1:9
NKJV

James 1:9 Let the lowly brother glory in his exaltation,(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:9 Believers who are poor have something to boast about, for God has honored them. (NLT)

Main Idea

The lowly brother is to “glory in his exaltation,” rejoicing in the high standing God has given him. A believer with little in this world has real reason for joy in what he has received from God.

Verse Explanation

The lowly brother is to “glory in his exaltation,” rejoicing in the high standing God has given him. A believer with little in this world has real reason for joy in what he has received from God.

“Let the lowly brother glory in his exaltation,”

James turns to believers at opposite ends of the economic scale. First comes “the lowly brother,” a believer who is poor and of low standing in society. He is a “brother,” a member of the family of faith.

This lowly brother is to “glory in his exaltation.” To “glory” means to boast or rejoice with confidence. His “exaltation” is the high standing God has given him. In the eyes of the world he may count for little, but God has made him a child of God and an heir of the kingdom (2:5). A believer with little in this world has real reason for joy in what he has received from God.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “lowly” can mean poor, humble, or of low social position. In this context it is set against the “rich” of 1:10.

James 1:10
James 1:10
NKJV

James 1:10 but the rich in his humiliation, because as a flower of the field he will pass away.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:10 And those who are rich should boast that God has humbled them. They will fade away like a little flower in the field. (NLT)

Main Idea

The rich believer, in turn, is to glory “in his humiliation,” rejoicing in the humbling truth that he depends on God as fully as the lowly brother does, “because as a flower of the field he will pass away.” His life and his wealth fade as quickly as a wildflower, so his security must rest in God.

Verse Explanation

The rich believer, in turn, is to glory “in his humiliation,” rejoicing in the humbling truth that he depends on God as fully as the lowly brother does, “because as a flower of the field he will pass away.” His life and his wealth fade as quickly as a wildflower, so his security must rest in God.

“but the rich in his humiliation,”

The opposite case follows. The rich person here is best understood as a believer. The Greek grammar carries the command to “glory” forward from 1:9, so James is giving parallel instructions to two believers. The lowly brother glories in being lifted up. The rich believer glories in being brought low.

The rich believer’s “humiliation” is the humbling truth that he depends on God as fully as the lowly brother does. His money does not give him standing before God or secure his future. When he recognizes this, he can rejoice, because he has found his security in the right place.

“because as a flower of the field he will pass away.”

James gives the reason. A wildflower is beautiful for a short time and then fades. The rich person’s life and wealth will pass away just as quickly. His security must rest in God, not in what he owns.

Study Notes

Some interpreters read the rich person in 1:10–11 as an unbeliever, since James later denounces rich oppressors (2:6–7; 5:1–6). In 1:9–10, however, the command to glory carries over from the lowly brother to the rich, which supports reading the two as parallel believers. James does not repeat the word “brother” in 1:10, so the rich person’s identity is drawn from the parallel rather than stated.

The image echoes Isaiah 40:6–8: “All flesh is grass, And all its loveliness is like the flower of the field.”

James 1:11
James 1:11
NKJV

James 1:11 For no sooner has the sun risen with a burning heat than it withers the grass; its flower falls, and its beautiful appearance perishes. So the rich man also will fade away in his pursuits.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:11 The hot sun rises and the grass withers; the little flower droops and falls, and its beauty fades away. In the same way, the rich will fade away with all of their achievements. (NLT)

Main Idea

James develops the picture: the sun rises with burning heat, the grass withers, the flower falls, and its beauty perishes. In the same way “the rich man also will fade away in his pursuits,” showing how quickly earthly life and wealth come to an end.

Verse Explanation

James develops the picture: the sun rises with burning heat, the grass withers, the flower falls, and its beauty perishes. In the same way “the rich man also will fade away in his pursuits,” showing how quickly earthly life and wealth come to an end.

“For no sooner has the sun risen with a burning heat than it withers the grass;”

James develops the picture. “The sun” rises “with a burning heat,” and the heat “withers the grass.” In the dry lands of Israel, a hot wind or scorching sun could wither green growth in a single day. What looked lush in the morning was brown by afternoon.

“its flower falls, and its beautiful appearance perishes.”

The “flower falls,” and “its beautiful appearance perishes.” Whatever beauty it had disappears. James lingers on the image to make the brevity of earthly things vivid.

“So the rich man also will fade away in his pursuits.”

The application is direct. “Pursuits” are his journeys, his business, his busy activities. He will fade while he is still in the middle of them. Earthly life and wealth come to an end quickly, often without warning.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “burning heat” can refer either to the sun’s heat or to a hot east wind. Both were familiar in the land of Israel.

James 1:12–15 — Enduring Temptation and the Birth of Sin

James 1:12
James 1:12
NKJV

James 1:12 Blessed is the man who endures temptation; for when he has been approved, he will receive the crown of life which the Lord has promised to those who love Him.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:12 God blesses those who patiently endure testing and temptation. Afterward they will receive the crown of life that God has promised to those who love him. (NLT)

Main Idea

The man “who endures temptation” is blessed, and when he has been approved he “will receive the crown of life” that the Lord has promised to those who love Him. The crown is eternal life itself, pictured as a victor’s wreath, which believers begin to experience now, including freedom from the destructive fruit of sin, and will receive in full in the future.

Verse Explanation

The man “who endures temptation” is blessed, and when he has been approved he “will receive the crown of life” that the Lord has promised to those who love Him. The crown is eternal life itself, pictured as a victor’s wreath, which believers begin to experience now, including freedom from the destructive fruit of sin, and will receive in full in the future.

“Blessed is the man who endures temptation;”

James returns to trials. “Blessed” means truly happy, favored by God. The blessed person is “the man who endures temptation.” The word translated “temptation” is the same word translated “trials” in 1:2. Here it refers to testing that a person stands up under. “Endures” means to remain steady under the pressure, not giving way.

“for when he has been approved,”

James gives the reason for the blessing. “Approved” means tested and proven genuine. After the test, the one who has endured is shown to have real faith.

“he will receive the crown of life”

The “crown” is a victor’s wreath, the prize given to the winner of an athletic contest. The “crown of life” is best understood as eternal life itself, pictured as the victor’s wreath. The life is the crown.

This promise looks forward to the future, when believers will receive eternal life in full. Yet those who belong to God begin to experience that life now. Each time a believer endures a trial faithfully, he tastes the life God gives, including freedom from the destructive fruit of sin (1:15). The full reward comes later. The foretaste comes now.

“which the Lord has promised to those who love Him.”

The crown is something “the Lord has promised.” It is His gift, not a wage earned by unusually successful believers. It is promised “to those who love Him.” Endurance in trials grows out of love for God. Those who love Him keep trusting Him under pressure.

Study Notes

The same phrase appears in Revelation 2:10: “Be faithful until death, and I will give you the crown of life.”

The Greek word translated “approved” is related to the noun translated “testing” in 1:3.

James 1:13
James 1:13
NKJV

James 1:13 Let no one say when he is tempted, "I am tempted by God"; for God cannot be tempted by evil, nor does He Himself tempt anyone.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:13 And remember, when you are being tempted, do not say, “God is tempting me.” God is never tempted to do wrong, and he never tempts anyone else. (NLT)

Main Idea

No one who is tempted should say, “I am tempted by God,” because God cannot be tempted by evil and He Himself tempts no one. God’s holiness rules out blaming Him for the pull toward sin.

Verse Explanation

No one who is tempted should say, “I am tempted by God,” because God cannot be tempted by evil and He Himself tempts no one. God’s holiness rules out blaming Him for the pull toward sin.

“Let no one say when he is tempted, ‘I am tempted by God’;”

James now addresses the inward side of testing. In 1:2 and 1:12 he used the noun translated “trials” and “temptation.” Here he uses the related Greek verb, and its sense shifts from outward testing to enticement toward evil. When a person “is tempted” in this sense, he must not say, “I am tempted by God.” People have always looked for someone else to blame for their sin. Blaming God is the ultimate excuse.

“for God cannot be tempted by evil,”

James gives two reasons. First, evil has no pull on God. Nothing in Him responds to it or desires it.

“nor does He Himself tempt anyone.”

Second, God tests His people to prove and strengthen their faith (1:3), but He never entices anyone to sin. The pull toward evil does not come from Him. God’s holiness rules out blaming Him for temptation.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “cannot be tempted” appears only here in the New Testament. It describes God as untouched by evil, unable to be drawn toward it.

James does not deny that Satan tempts people. He will tell his readers to “Resist the devil” (4:7). His point here is that people cannot shift responsibility for their sin onto God.

James 1:14
James 1:14
NKJV

James 1:14 But each one is tempted when he is drawn away by his own desires and enticed.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:14 Temptation comes from our own desires, which entice us and drag us away. (NLT)

Main Idea

Each person is tempted “when he is drawn away by his own desires and enticed.” James places the source of enticement within the person, picturing desire as something that lures and baits a person away from what is right.

Verse Explanation

Each person is tempted “when he is drawn away by his own desires and enticed.” James places the source of enticement within the person, picturing desire as something that lures and baits a person away from what is right.

“But each one is tempted when he is drawn away by his own desires and enticed.”

James locates the source of temptation: “each one is tempted when he is drawn away by his own desires.” The problem is inside the person. “His own desires” are the cravings that pull him toward what is wrong.

James uses two vivid words. “Drawn away” pictures being lured out of a safe place, as a fish is drawn out from its hiding spot. “Enticed” pictures being baited, as a fish is caught by a baited hook. Desire dangles something attractive, and the person who bites is caught.

This verse does not make every human desire sinful. God made people with desires. The problem comes when desire turns toward what God forbids and draws a person away from what is right.

Study Notes

The Greek words translated “drawn away” and “enticed” come from the language of hunting and fishing. The second is used in 2 Peter 2:14 for false teachers “enticing unstable souls.”

James 1:15
James 1:15
NKJV

James 1:15 Then, when desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:15 These desires give birth to sinful actions. And when sin is allowed to grow, it gives birth to death. (NLT)

Main Idea

Once desire has conceived, it “gives birth to sin,” and sin, when it is full-grown, “brings forth death.” James traces a deadly course from inner desire to sinful act to the ruin sin brings, which in a believer’s life damages joy, relationships, and spiritual vitality and can end in physical death.

Verse Explanation

Once desire has conceived, it “gives birth to sin,” and sin, when it is full-grown, “brings forth death.” James traces a deadly course from inner desire to sinful act to the ruin sin brings, which in a believer’s life damages joy, relationships, and spiritual vitality and can end in physical death.

“Then, when desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin;”

James pictures temptation as a birth. “Desire” is like a mother. When it has “conceived,” when a person welcomes and embraces the evil desire, it “gives birth to sin.” Desire by itself is not yet the act of sin, but when it is welcomed and carried, it brings sin into the world.

“and sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death.”

The cycle continues. “Sin, when it is full-grown,” when it has grown to maturity, “brings forth death.” Sin is not a harmless act that stays small. It grows, and its end is death.

In a believer’s life, death here describes the ruin sin brings. Sin damages joy, relationships, integrity, and spiritual vitality as it takes hold. Left unchecked, its course can end in physical death (compare 5:19–20; 1 Corinthians 11:30). James’s warning calls for a real turning from sin.

This birth is the opposite of the birth James describes in 1:18. Desire gives birth to sin and death. The Father gives birth to new life through His word.

James 1:16–18 — The Father of Lights Gives New Birth

None.

James 1:16
James 1:16
NKJV

James 1:16 Do not be deceived, my beloved brethren.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:16 So don’t be misled, my dear brothers and sisters. (NLT)

Main Idea

James warns his beloved brethren, “Do not be deceived.” The warning turns them from the false idea that God tempts people toward the truth about His goodness that follows.

Verse Explanation

James warns his beloved brethren, “Do not be deceived.” The warning turns them from the false idea that God tempts people toward the truth about His goodness that follows.

“Do not be deceived, my beloved brethren.”

James gives a warning: “Do not be deceived.” The deception he has in mind is the false idea that God tempts people to sin (1:13). If believers think God is behind their temptations, they will misunderstand Him and lose their confidence in His goodness. James calls them “my beloved brethren.” The warning comes from love. James turns them from a false idea of God toward the truth about His goodness that follows.

Study Notes

Deception is a recurring concern in James. He warns against hearers who deceive themselves (1:22) and those who deceive their own hearts (1:26).

James 1:17
James 1:17
NKJV

James 1:17 Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and comes down from the Father of lights, with whom there is no variation or shadow of turning.(NKJV)

NLT

James 1:17 Whatever is good and perfect is a gift coming down to us from God our Father, who created all the lights in the heavens. He never changes or casts a shifting shadow. (NLT)

Main Idea

“Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above,” coming down from the Father of lights. Unlike the heavenly lights He made, which move and change, the Father has “no variation or shadow of turning,” so His goodness can always be trusted.

Verse Explanation

“Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above,” coming down from the Father of lights. Unlike the heavenly lights He made, which move and change, the Father has “no variation or shadow of turning,” so His goodness can always be trusted.

“Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above,”

Far from tempting people to evil, God is the source of every good thing. Whatever is truly good and complete in a believer’s life comes “from above,” from God in heaven.

“and comes down from the Father of lights,”

These gifts come “from the Father of lights.” God created the sun, moon, and stars, the lights of the heavens (Genesis 1:14–18). He is their Father, their Maker.

“with whom there is no variation or shadow of turning.”

The lights God made change. The sun rises and sets, the moon waxes and wanes, and their shadows shift through the day. But with God there is “no variation or shadow of turning.” He does not change. His goodness does not rise and fall. He will never turn from giving good gifts to enticing people to evil. His goodness can always be trusted.

Study Notes

The words “variation” and “shadow of turning” may draw on the language used for the movements of heavenly bodies. The contrast stresses God’s steady goodness rather than describing astronomy.

The two Greek words for “gift” in this verse differ slightly. The first describes the act of giving, the second the gift itself. Together they cover every good thing God gives.

He gave this new birth “by the word of truth,” the message of the gospel. When people hear and receive God’s saving message, God brings them to new life. “that we might be a kind of firstfruits of His creatures.”

In Israel, the firstfruits were the first part of the harvest, given to God as a sign that the whole harvest belonged to Him. Believers are the first part of a larger harvest. They belong to God now, and they point ahead to the renewal of all that He has made.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “brought us forth” is the same verb translated “brings forth” in 1:15. James contrasts sin bringing forth death with God bringing forth new life.

The phrase “a kind of firstfruits” uses a qualifier that shows James is speaking by comparison. Believers are like the firstfruits offered to God (Exodus 23:19).

Because God has given new life through His word, every believer must be “swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath.” Eager listening, together with restraint in speech and anger, prepares a person to receive what God says.

“So then, my beloved brethren,”

“So then” draws a conclusion. Because God has given new birth through His word (1:18), believers must respond to that word rightly. James again calls them “my beloved brethren.”

“let every man be swift to hear,”

First, every believer must be “swift to hear.” This means being eager to listen, quick to pay attention, especially to God’s word. The context points first to hearing God, though the same attitude shapes how believers listen to one another.

“slow to speak,”

Second, believers should think before they talk. A person who is always talking is seldom listening. James will have much more to say about the tongue in chapter 3.

“slow to wrath;”

Third, believers must not flare up quickly in anger. Anger closes the ears and opens the mouth. Eager listening, together with restraint in speech and anger, prepares a person to receive what God says.

Proverbs gives similar counsel: “He who is slow to wrath has great understanding” (Proverbs 14:29).

“Therefore lay aside all filthiness and overflow of wickedness,”

“Therefore” draws a conclusion. Believers must “lay aside” certain things, as a person takes off dirty clothes. They must remove “all filthiness,” moral uncleanness, and “overflow of wickedness,” the evil that still remains and spills over in their lives. Before they can receive God’s word well, they must put away what is opposed to it.

“and receive with meekness the implanted word,”

“Meekness” is humble teachability, a willingness to be corrected without arguing. It is the opposite of the anger of 1:20.

The word is “implanted.” God has planted it in believers, like a seed in soil. It is the same word of truth by which He gave them new birth (1:18). Those who already have the word must keep receiving it, letting it take deeper root.

“which is able to save your souls.”

“Souls” here means the whole person, the life a person lives. The word rescues believers now from sin’s destructive work, the course of death described in 1:15. It also carries them on to the day when God completes their salvation. Present deliverance and final salvation belong together. Good conduct is the fruit of this received life, not a way to purchase it.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “overflow” means excess or abundance. It describes wickedness that remains and still shows itself.

The Greek word translated “souls” (psyche) can mean life, person, or the inner self.

Main Idea

Anyone who “thinks he is religious” but does not bridle his tongue deceives his own heart, and his religion is useless. Uncontrolled speech exposes the emptiness of a claim to devotion.

Anyone who “thinks he is religious” but does not bridle his tongue deceives his own heart, and his religion is useless. Uncontrolled speech exposes the emptiness of a claim to devotion.

“If anyone among you thinks he is religious,”

James gives practical tests of genuine devotion. The first concerns someone who “thinks he is religious.” “Religious” here describes outward devotion to God, the practices of worship and piety. This person believes his devotion is real.

“and does not bridle his tongue but deceives his own heart,”

Yet he “does not bridle his tongue.” A “bridle” controls a horse. This person does not control what he says. He speaks carelessly, harshly, or deceitfully. In doing so, he “deceives his own heart.” He fools himself about his spiritual condition.

“this one’s religion is useless.”

James’s verdict is blunt. Whatever religious activity he practices is empty and accomplishes nothing before God. Uncontrolled speech exposes the emptiness of a claim to devotion. James will develop this theme in chapter 3.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “religious” refers to the outward practice of worship. The related noun is translated “religion” here and in 1:27.

The Greek word translated “useless” is used in 1 Corinthians 15:17 (“your faith is futile”) and 1 Peter 1:18 (“aimless conduct”).

NKJV

Main Idea

Religion that is “pure and undefiled” before God the Father visits “orphans and widows in their trouble” and keeps oneself unspotted from the world. James joins practical mercy toward the vulnerable with moral faithfulness as the marks of genuine devotion.

Verse Explanation

Religion that is “pure and undefiled” before God the Father visits “orphans and widows in their trouble” and keeps oneself unspotted from the world. James joins practical mercy toward the vulnerable with moral faithfulness as the marks of genuine devotion.

“Pure and undefiled religion before God and the Father is this:”

James now describes devotion that God accepts. The standard is not human opinion but God’s own verdict. The phrase “before God and the Father” reminds readers that God sees and evaluates their devotion.

“to visit orphans and widows in their trouble,”

The first mark of genuine devotion concerns the vulnerable. In the ancient world, orphans and widows had no one to protect or provide for them. They were among the most vulnerable people in society. God repeatedly commanded Israel to care for them (Deuteronomy 10:18). To “visit” means more than a social call. It means to look after them, to care for their needs “in their trouble,” in their distress.

“and to keep oneself unspotted from the world.”

The second mark concerns holiness. “The world” here means the values and ways of life that are opposed to God. Believers must guard themselves from being stained by them. James will say more about this in 4:4.

James joins practical mercy toward the vulnerable with moral faithfulness as the marks of genuine devotion. Both belong together. Mercy without holiness drifts into compromise. Holiness without mercy becomes cold.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “visit” is used for God visiting His people to help them (Luke 1:68) and for visiting the sick and imprisoned (Matthew 25:36).

These verses do not give a full description of Christian worship. They give concrete tests that show whether hearing God’s word has entered ordinary life.
