Chapter Study

James 2

Verse-by-Verse
James 2:1
James 2:1
NKJV

James 2:1 My brethren, do not hold the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with partiality.(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:1 My dear brothers and sisters, how can you claim to have faith in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ if you favor some people over others? (NLT)

Main Idea

James tells his brethren not to hold “the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with partiality.” Faith in the glorious Lord cannot sit alongside favoritism based on wealth or outward rank.

Verse Explanation

James tells his brethren not to hold “the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with partiality.” Faith in the glorious Lord cannot sit alongside favoritism based on wealth or outward rank.

“My brethren, do not hold the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with partiality.”

James begins a new subject with a command. Believers must not hold their faith “with partiality.” Partiality means favoritism, treating people differently because of outward appearance, wealth, or social rank.

The faith they hold is “the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ.” James calls Jesus “the Lord of glory.” All glory belongs to Him. Compared with His glory, the differences between rich and poor shrink to nothing. To honor someone because of gold rings and fine clothes while trusting the Lord of glory is a contradiction. Faith in the glorious Lord cannot sit alongside favoritism based on wealth or outward rank.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “partiality” is formed from words meaning to receive a face. It describes judging people by outward appearance. Paul uses the same word for God, who shows no partiality (Romans 2:11).

The Greek construction behind “the Lord of glory” can also be read as our glorious Lord Jesus Christ. Either way, the glory belongs to Jesus.

James 2:2
James 2:2
NKJV

James 2:2 For if there should come into your assembly a man with gold rings, in fine apparel, and there should also come in a poor man in filthy clothes,(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:2 For example, suppose someone comes into your meeting dressed in fancy clothes and expensive jewelry, and another comes in who is poor and dressed in dirty clothes. (NLT)

Main Idea

James pictures two visitors coming into the assembly: a man with gold rings and fine apparel, and a poor man in filthy clothes. The scene sets up a test of how the church receives people.

Verse Explanation

James pictures two visitors coming into the assembly: a man with gold rings and fine apparel, and a poor man in filthy clothes. The scene sets up a test of how the church receives people.

“For if there should come into your assembly a man with gold rings, in fine apparel,”

James gives an example. A visitor comes “into your assembly,” the gathering of believers. He is “a man with gold rings, in fine apparel.” His jewelry and clothing announce his wealth and status.

“and there should also come in a poor man in filthy clothes,”

Another visitor comes: “a poor man in filthy clothes.” His clothing is shabby and dirty. Everything about his appearance signals poverty. The scene sets up a test of how the church receives people.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “assembly” is the word for “synagogue.” James uses it for the gathering of Jewish believers in Jesus. In 5:14 he uses the usual word for “church.”

James 2:3
James 2:3
NKJV

James 2:3 and you pay attention to the one wearing the fine clothes and say to him, "You sit here in a good place," and say to the poor man, "You stand there," or, "Sit here at my footstool,"(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:3 If you give special attention and a good seat to the rich person, but you say to the poor one, “You can stand over there, or else sit on the floor” — well, (NLT)

Main Idea

If the church pays special attention to the well-dressed man and says, “You sit here in a good place,” while telling the poor man, “You stand there,” or to sit at someone’s footstool, it honors wealth and slights poverty. The seating itself announces who the church thinks matters.

Verse Explanation

If the church pays special attention to the well-dressed man and says, “You sit here in a good place,” while telling the poor man, “You stand there,” or to sit at someone’s footstool, it honors wealth and slights poverty. The seating itself announces who the church thinks matters.

“and you pay attention to the one wearing the fine clothes”

The church gives the rich man special treatment. They notice him, look at him with favor, and go out of their way to welcome him. Their attention is drawn by his clothing.

“and say to him, ‘You sit here in a good place,”

They tell him, “You sit here in a good place.” He is given a comfortable, honored seat.

“and say to the poor man, ‘You stand there,’ or, ‘Sit here at my footstool,”

The poor man receives different treatment. He is told, “You stand there,” or “Sit here at my footstool.” He gets no seat at all, or he is sent to the floor beside someone’s feet. The seating itself announces who the church thinks matters.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “pay attention” means to look upon with favor or regard. It describes more than noticing someone.

James 2:4
James 2:4
NKJV

James 2:4 have you not shown partiality among yourselves, and become judges with evil thoughts?(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:4 doesn’t this discrimination show that your judgments are guided by evil motives? (NLT)

Main Idea

Giving the rich man the good seat and sending the poor man to stand or sit on the floor shows partiality among believers and makes them “judges with evil thoughts.” Believers who rank people by wealth become judges whose verdicts rest on corrupt reasoning.

Verse Explanation

Giving the rich man the good seat and sending the poor man to stand or sit on the floor shows partiality among believers and makes them “judges with evil thoughts.” Believers who rank people by wealth become judges whose verdicts rest on corrupt reasoning.

“have you not shown partiality among yourselves,”

James asks a question that answers itself, and the answer is yes. They have made distinctions that contradict their faith.

“and become judges with evil thoughts?”

They have taken the place of judges, deciding who deserves honor and who does not. Their verdicts rest on “evil thoughts,” corrupt reasoning that measures people by wealth. Believers who rank people by wealth become judges whose verdicts rest on corrupt reasoning.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “shown partiality” is the same verb translated “doubting” in 1:6. It can mean to make distinctions or to be divided. Some take James to mean that partiality shows a divided mind, the same instability he condemned in 1:6–8.

James 2:5
James 2:5
NKJV

James 2:5 Listen, my beloved brethren: Has God not chosen the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom which He promised to those who love Him?(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:5 Listen to me, dear brothers and sisters. Hasn’t God chosen the poor in this world to be rich in faith? Aren’t they the ones who will inherit the Kingdom he promised to those who love him? (NLT)

Main Idea

James calls his beloved brethren to listen: God has “chosen the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom” He promised to those who love Him. Treating the poor with contempt sets the church against God’s own choice.

Verse Explanation

James calls his beloved brethren to listen: God has “chosen the poor of this world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom” He promised to those who love Him. Treating the poor with contempt sets the church against God’s own choice.

“Listen, my beloved brethren:”

James calls for attention. “Listen,” he says to his “beloved brethren.” What follows exposes how badly they have misjudged.

“Has God not chosen the poor of this world to be rich in faith”

James asks another question that expects a yes. “Has God not chosen the poor of this world?” God has often chosen people the world overlooks. The poor are “poor” in the eyes of “this world,” but God has made them “rich in faith.” Their real wealth is their trust in Him.

“and heirs of the kingdom which He promised to those who love Him?”

God has also made them “heirs of the kingdom.” They will inherit God’s kingdom, the kingdom “He promised to those who love Him.” The same promise was attached to the crown of life in 1:12.

God honors the poor whom the church has dishonored. Treating the poor with contempt sets the church against God’s own choice. Poverty does not earn salvation, and wealth does not prove unbelief. But God’s choice exposes how foolish it is to rank people by money.

Study Notes

Jesus said, “Blessed are you poor, For yours is the kingdom of God” (Luke 6:20). Paul writes that God has chosen “the foolish things of the world to put to shame the wise” (1 Corinthians 1:27).

James 2:6
James 2:6
NKJV

James 2:6 But you have dishonored the poor man. Do not the rich oppress you and drag you into the courts?(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:6 But you dishonor the poor! Isn’t it the rich who oppress you and drag you into court? (NLT)

Main Idea

“But you have dishonored the poor man,” James says, even though it is the rich who oppress believers and drag them into the courts. The church is courting the favor of people who mistreat it.

Verse Explanation

“But you have dishonored the poor man,” James says, even though it is the rich who oppress believers and drag them into the courts. The church is courting the favor of people who mistreat it.

“But you have dishonored the poor man.”

“But” brings a sharp contrast. God has honored the poor, “but you have dishonored the poor man.” The church has done the opposite of what God did. By sending the poor man to stand or sit on the floor, they treated him with contempt.

“Do not the rich oppress you and drag you into the courts?”

James points out another irony. “Do not the rich oppress you?” The rich, as a group, were the ones mistreating believers. They “drag you into the courts,” using their power and the legal system against the poor. The church is courting the favor of people who mistreat it.

James is speaking of the rich as a class known to his readers, not of every wealthy person. He does not describe a particular lawsuit.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “oppress” means to exercise power over someone harshly. It is used in Acts 10:38 of those “oppressed by the devil.”

NLT

James 2:7 Aren’t they the ones who slander Jesus Christ, whose noble name you bear? (NLT)

Main Idea

The rich oppressors James has just described “blaspheme that noble name by which you are called.” Showing them favor means honoring those who speak against the name of the Lord that believers bear.

Verse Explanation

The rich oppressors James has just described “blaspheme that noble name by which you are called.” Showing them favor means honoring those who speak against the name of the Lord that believers bear.

“Do they not blaspheme that noble name by which you are called?”

James adds a third question. “Do they not blaspheme that noble name?” The rich oppressors speak against “that noble name,” the name of Jesus Christ. It is the name “by which you are called,” the name believers bear because they belong to Him.

Showing favor to those who speak against the name of the Lord dishonors the Lord whose name believers carry.

Study Notes

The phrase “by which you are called” reads literally which was called upon you. In the Old Testament, having God’s name called over someone marked that person as belonging to Him (for example, Amos 9:12, where nations are “called by My name”).

James 2:8–13 — The Royal Law and Mercy at Judgment

James 2:8
James 2:8
NKJV

James 2:8 If you really fulfill the royal law according to the Scripture, "You shall love your neighbor as yourself," you do well;(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:8 Yes indeed, it is good when you obey the royal law as found in the Scriptures: “Love your neighbor as yourself.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Believers do well when they really fulfill the royal law of Scripture, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” Love for one’s neighbor is the standard that partiality breaks.

Verse Explanation

Believers do well when they really fulfill the royal law of Scripture, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” Love for one’s neighbor is the standard that partiality breaks.

“If you really fulfill the royal law according to the Scripture,”

James turns from what believers must not do to what they must do. He speaks of “the royal law.” It is royal because it comes from God the King and because it rules over all the other commands that concern how people treat one another. It is found “according to the Scripture,” in God’s written word.

“You shall love your neighbor as yourself,’ you do well;”

The royal law is “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” James quotes Leviticus 19:18. Jesus called this the second great commandment (Matthew 22:39). If believers “really fulfill” this law, they “do well.” Love for one’s neighbor is the standard that partiality breaks. The poor man in filthy clothes was a neighbor, and he should have been loved as the church loved itself.

Study Notes

Leviticus 19:15, a few verses before the command to love one’s neighbor, forbids partiality in judgment: “You shall not be partial to the poor, nor honor the person of the mighty.”

The Greek word translated “royal” means belonging to a king.

James 2:9
James 2:9
NKJV

James 2:9 but if you show partiality, you commit sin, and are convicted by the law as transgressors.(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:9 But if you favor some people over others, you are committing a sin. You are guilty of breaking the law. (NLT)

Main Idea

Showing partiality is sin, and those who do it are “convicted by the law as transgressors.” Favoritism breaks God’s law, and the law itself exposes the offender.

Verse Explanation

Showing partiality is sin, and those who do it are “convicted by the law as transgressors.” Favoritism breaks God’s law, and the law itself exposes the offender.

“but if you show partiality, you commit sin,”

James states the opposite case plainly. Favoritism is not a social mistake or a matter of manners. It is sin.

“and are convicted by the law as transgressors.”

The law itself exposes them as lawbreakers. The same Scripture that commands love for one’s neighbor condemns partiality (Leviticus 19:15). Favoritism breaks God’s law, and the law itself exposes the offender.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “convicted” means to expose or prove guilty. It is used in John 16:8 for the Spirit who “will convict the world of sin.”

James 2:10
James 2:10
NKJV

James 2:10 For whoever shall keep the whole law, and yet stumble in one point, he is guilty of all.(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:10 For the person who keeps all of the laws except one is as guilty as a person who has broken all of God’s laws. (NLT)

Main Idea

Whoever keeps the whole law yet stumbles in one point “is guilty of all.” God’s law stands as a single whole, so a person cannot excuse partiality by pointing to the commands he keeps.

Verse Explanation

Whoever keeps the whole law yet stumbles in one point “is guilty of all.” God’s law stands as a single whole, so a person cannot excuse partiality by pointing to the commands he keeps.

“For whoever shall keep the whole law,”

James explains why partiality is so serious. He imagines someone who “shall keep the whole law,” obeying every command but one.

“and yet stumble in one point, he is guilty of all.”

The law is not a list of separate rules that a person can pick from. It is a single whole, given by one God. Breaking any part of it makes a person a lawbreaker.

This does not mean that every sin is equally serious, or that breaking one command is the same as breaking every command. It means that a person cannot excuse partiality by pointing to all the commands he keeps. God’s law stands as a single whole.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “stumble” is used again in 3:2: “For we all stumble in many things.”

James 2:11
James 2:11
NKJV

James 2:11 For He who said, "Do not commit adultery," also said, "Do not murder." Now if you do not commit adultery, but you do murder, you have become a transgressor of the law.(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:11 For the same God who said, “You must not commit adultery,” also said, “You must not murder.” So if you murder someone but do not commit adultery, you have still broken the law. (NLT)

Main Idea

The same God who said, “Do not commit adultery,” also said, “Do not murder.” A person who avoids adultery but commits murder still becomes a transgressor of the law, because every command comes from the one God who gave them all.

Verse Explanation

The same God who said, “Do not commit adultery,” also said, “Do not murder.” A person who avoids adultery but commits murder still becomes a transgressor of the law, because every command comes from the one God who gave them all.

“For He who said, ‘Do not commit adultery,’ also said, ‘Do not murder.”

James gives an example. The God who commanded against adultery also commanded against murder. Both commands come from the same Lawgiver (Exodus 20:13–14).

“Now if you do not commit adultery, but you do murder, you have become a transgressor of the law.”

A person might avoid adultery but commit murder. He cannot claim to be law-abiding because of the one command he kept. He has “become a transgressor of the law.” Every command comes from the one God who gave them all. The same is true of partiality. A person cannot excuse favoritism by pointing to the commands he keeps.

Study Notes

James names adultery before murder. Jesus and Paul also list these two commands in that order (Luke 18:20; Romans 13:9).

James 2:12
James 2:12
NKJV

James 2:12 So speak and so do as those who will be judged by the law of liberty.(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:12 So whatever you say or whatever you do, remember that you will be judged by the law that sets you free. (NLT)

Main Idea

Believers are to “speak and so do as those who will be judged by the law of liberty.” Their words and actions answer to God’s word, which frees them for obedient love and holds them accountable.

Verse Explanation

Believers are to “speak and so do as those who will be judged by the law of liberty.” Their words and actions answer to God’s word, which frees them for obedient love and holds them accountable.

“So speak and so do as those who will be judged by the law of liberty.”

James draws a conclusion. Believers must “speak” and “do,” in both words and actions, “as those who will be judged by the law of liberty.” They will give account to God for how they have lived.

“The law of liberty” is the same law James described in 1:25: God’s word, which frees His people for obedient love. It frees them, but it also holds them accountable. Their words and actions answer to God’s word. Freedom does not mean exemption from judgment.

Study Notes

The phrase “speak and so do” brings together the two areas James addresses throughout the letter: speech (1:19, 26; 3:1–12) and action (1:22–25; 2:14–26).

James 2:13
James 2:13
NKJV

James 2:13 For judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy. Mercy triumphs over judgment.(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:13 There will be no mercy for those who have not shown mercy to others. But if you have been merciful, God will be merciful when he judges you. (NLT)

Main Idea

“Judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy,” yet “mercy triumphs over judgment.” A settled refusal to show mercy exposes a profession of faith that will not stand at the final judgment, while mercy shown to others gives evidence of a life transformed by God and confidence before Him.

Verse Explanation

“Judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy,” yet “mercy triumphs over judgment.” A settled refusal to show mercy exposes a profession of faith that will not stand at the final judgment, while mercy shown to others gives evidence of a life transformed by God and confidence before Him.

“For judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy.”

James explains why the coming judgment matters. A person who has refused to show mercy to others will face God’s judgment without mercy.

This is a severe warning. The person James describes has a settled pattern of mercilessness. A settled refusal to show mercy exposes a profession of faith that will not stand at the final judgment. James addresses the church, so the warning also calls genuine believers who have shown favoritism to repent and act mercifully.

“Mercy triumphs over judgment.”

James ends with a ringing statement. Mercy wins. The immediate emphasis falls on the mercy believers show to others. Such mercy gives evidence of a life transformed by God and gives confidence before Him on the day of judgment. Showing mercy does not purchase salvation. God’s mercy in Christ is the ground of salvation, and the mercy His people show is the evidence that they have received it.

Study Notes

Jesus said, “Blessed are the merciful, For they shall obtain mercy” (Matthew 5:7). His parable of the unforgiving servant warns against refusing mercy after receiving it (Matthew 18:21–35).

The Greek verb translated “triumphs” means to boast over or prevail against.

James 2:14–17 — A Claimed Faith That Leaves the Needy Unhelped

James 2:14
James 2:14
NKJV

James 2:14 What does it profit, my brethren, if someone says he has faith but does not have works? Can faith save him?(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:14 What good is it, dear brothers and sisters, if you say you have faith but don’t show it by your actions? Can that kind of faith save anyone? (NLT)

Main Idea

James asks what profit there is if someone “says he has faith but does not have works,” and whether that faith can save him. The implied answer is no: a claim to faith that produces no action cannot save, because genuine faith shows itself in mercy and obedience.

Verse Explanation

James asks what profit there is if someone “says he has faith but does not have works,” and whether that faith can save him. The implied answer is no: a claim to faith that produces no action cannot save, because genuine faith shows itself in mercy and obedience.

“What does it profit, my brethren,”

James begins a new section with a question: “What does it profit?” What good is it? He is about to describe a faith that accomplishes nothing.

“if someone says he has faith but does not have works?”

James describes “someone” who “says he has faith.” The key word is “says.” This person claims to have faith. But he “does not have works.” His claim is not matched by any action.

“Can faith save him?”

The Greek wording expects the answer no. The faith in question is the kind just described, a faith that is claimed but produces no works. Such a faith cannot save. James is not saying that faith in Christ is insufficient for salvation. He is saying that a faith that never acts is not genuine saving faith at all. Genuine faith shows itself in mercy and obedience.

Study Notes

The Greek question “Can faith save him?” uses a form that expects a negative answer. Many translations bring out the reference to the faith just described by asking whether that faith can save him.

James imagines a brother or sister who is “naked and destitute of daily food.” The example sets the argument within a situation of real and urgent need in the church family.

“and one of you says to them, ‘Depart in peace, be warmed and filled,”

James 2:18–20 — Faith Shown by Works, Not Mere Belief

James 2:18
James 2:18
NKJV

James 2:18 But someone will say, "You have faith, and I have works." Show me your faith without your works, and I will show you my faith by my works.(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:18 Now someone may argue, “Some people have faith; others have good deeds.” But I say, “How can you show me your faith if you don’t have good deeds? I will show you my faith by my good deeds.” (NLT)

Main Idea

James introduces someone who says, “You have faith, and I have works,” and the verse presses the challenge: “Show me your faith without your works, and I will show you my faith by my works.” Faith that is genuine can be seen in what a person does.

Verse Explanation

James introduces someone who says, “You have faith, and I have works,” and the verse presses the challenge: “Show me your faith without your works, and I will show you my faith by my works.” Faith that is genuine can be seen in what a person does.

“But someone will say, ‘You have faith, and I have works.”

James introduces an imagined speaker who says, “You have faith, and I have works.” This speaker seems to suggest that faith and works can be separated, as if some people have the gift of faith and others the gift of works.

“Show me your faith without your works, and I will show you my faith by my works.”

James answers the challenge. “Show me your faith without your works.” It cannot be done. Faith is invisible unless it acts. Then James says, “I will show you my faith by my works.” Faith that is genuine can be seen in what a person does.

Study Notes

The Greek text had no quotation marks, and interpreters disagree about where the imagined speaker’s words end in this verse. The NKJV ends the quotation after “I have works.” The difficulty does not change James’s main point: genuine faith cannot be separated from its expression in conduct.

James 2:19
James 2:19
NKJV

James 2:19 You believe that there is one God. You do well. Even the demons believe"”and tremble!(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:19 You say you have faith, for you believe that there is one God. Good for you! Even the demons believe this, and they tremble in terror. (NLT)

Main Idea

“You believe that there is one God. You do well.” Yet “even the demons believe—and tremble,” shaking with dread before the God they oppose, so correct belief about God can exist without trusting allegiance to Him.

Verse Explanation

“You believe that there is one God. You do well.” Yet “even the demons believe—and tremble,” shaking with dread before the God they oppose, so correct belief about God can exist without trusting allegiance to Him.

“You believe that there is one God. You do well.”

James addresses someone who believes “that there is one God.” This is the most basic confession of Israel’s faith: “Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one!” (Deuteronomy 6:4). James affirms this belief. “You do well.” It is true.

“Even the demons believe—and tremble!”

But correct belief is not enough. “Even the demons believe.” They know there is one God. They know it better than many people do. And they “tremble,” shaking with dread before the God they oppose. Yet they remain His enemies.

Correct belief about God can exist without trusting allegiance to Him. Saving faith includes knowing the truth about God, but it goes further. It trusts Him, loves Him, and obeys Him.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “tremble” means to shudder or bristle with fear. It appears only here in the New Testament.

James 2:20
NKJV

James rebukes the one who claims faith without works as “O foolish man” and asks whether he wants to know “that faith without works is dead.” The question introduces the proof from Scripture that follows in Abraham and Rahab.

“But do you want to know, O foolish man,”

James addresses his imagined opponent directly: “O foolish man.” “Foolish” means empty, hollow. The person who claims faith without works is as empty as the faith he claims.

“that faith without works is dead?”

James is about to give the proof from Scripture, in the examples of Abraham and Rahab.

Study Notes

Some Greek manuscripts read “dead” in this verse, while many others read a word meaning “useless” or “idle.” The NKJV follows the reading “dead,” which matches 2:17 and 2:26.

“Was not Abraham our father justified by works when he offered Isaac his son on the altar?”

James turns to “Abraham our father,” the father of the Jewish people and the great example of faith. James asks a question that expects a yes: was not Abraham “justified by works when he offered Isaac his son on the altar?”

The event is recorded in Genesis 22:1–18. God told Abraham to offer Isaac as a sacrifice. Abraham obeyed, and God stopped him at the last moment and provided a ram instead.

Abraham’s costly obedience showed that the faith by which God had already counted him righteous was genuine. Years earlier, God had declared Abraham righteous because he believed (Genesis 15:6). When Abraham offered Isaac, his obedience proved that his faith was real. He was justified by works in the sense that his works demonstrated the genuineness of his faith.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “offered” describes bringing something up to the altar. Abraham placed Isaac on the altar and took the knife, but God stopped him and provided a ram in Isaac’s place (Genesis 22:9–13).

Verse Explanation

Abraham’s faith “was working together with his works, and by works faith was made perfect.” His obedience expressed the faith he already had and brought it to maturity.

“Do you see that faith was working together with his works,”

James asks, “Do you see?” Abraham’s faith “was working together with his works.” Faith and works were not separate or opposed. They worked as partners. Abraham’s faith led to his obedience, and his obedience expressed his faith.

“and by works faith was made perfect?”

“Perfect” means complete or mature. Abraham’s obedience brought his faith to maturity. His faith was genuine before he offered Isaac, but the test brought it to its full expression. His obedience expressed the faith he already had and brought it to maturity.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “was made perfect” is related to the word “perfect” in 1:4. Tested faith, carried through, is brought to maturity.

“And the Scripture was fulfilled which says, ‘Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness.”

When Abraham offered Isaac, “the Scripture was fulfilled.” The Scripture James quotes is Genesis 15:6: “Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness.” God declared Abraham righteous because he believed. That declaration came years before the offering of Isaac.

When Abraham offered Isaac, that earlier Scripture was “fulfilled.” The offering of Isaac displayed the reality of the faith God had counted as righteousness long before Abraham went to the altar. The faith God had credited was shown to be real.

“And he was called the friend of God.”

The Old Testament gives Abraham this title (2 Chronicles 20:7; Isaiah 41:8). His trust and obedience showed a relationship of intimate friendship with God.

Study Notes

Paul quotes the same verse, Genesis 15:6, in Romans 4:3.

Main Idea

James concludes, “You see then that a man is justified by works, and not by faith only.” In context he means that genuine faith is shown to be real through obedience, and a claim to faith that never acts is not the faith that justifies.

Verse Explanation

James concludes, “You see then that a man is justified by works, and not by faith only.” In context he means that genuine faith is shown to be real through obedience, and a claim to faith that never acts is not the faith that justifies.

“You see then that a man is justified by works, and not by faith only.”

James concludes: “a man is justified by works, and not by faith only.” This statement can seem to contradict Paul, who wrote, “a man is justified by faith apart from the deeds of the law” (Romans 3:28).

In context, James means that genuine faith is shown to be real through obedience. The “faith only” he rejects is the kind he has been describing: a faith that is claimed but never acts (2:14), a faith that offers empty words to the hungry (2:16), a faith like the demons’ belief (2:19). Such a faith does not justify. Paul and James agree that people are saved by grace through faith, and that genuine faith produces good works (Ephesians 2:8–10). Paul addresses those who try to earn salvation by works. James addresses those who claim faith but do not live it.

Main Idea

Rahab the harlot was likewise “justified by works when she received the messengers and sent them out another way.” Her protective actions gave visible expression to the allegiance to Israel’s God that she had confessed.

Verse Explanation

Rahab the harlot was likewise “justified by works when she received the messengers and sent them out another way.” Her protective actions gave visible expression to the allegiance to Israel’s God that she had confessed.

“Likewise, was not Rahab the harlot also justified by works”

James gives a second example: “Rahab the harlot.” She was a Canaanite woman in Jericho, a pagan by background and a prostitute by occupation. Abraham was the honored father of Israel. Rahab was an outsider with a shameful past. Yet both show the same truth.

Rahab was “also justified by works.” Like Abraham, her actions showed that her faith was real.

“when she received the messengers and sent them out another way?”

When Joshua sent spies into Jericho, Rahab hid them, protected them from the king’s men, and helped them escape (Joshua 2:1–21). She had confessed faith in Israel’s God: “the LORD your God, He is God in heaven above and on earth beneath” (Joshua 2:11). Her protective actions gave visible expression to the allegiance to Israel’s God that she had confessed. James commends the actions he names. He does not approve every detail of her story.

Study Notes

Hebrews 11:31 also commends Rahab: “By faith the harlot Rahab did not perish with those who did not believe, when she had received the spies with peace.”

NKJV

James 2:26 For as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is dead also.(NKJV)

NLT

James 2:26 Just as the body is dead without breath, so also faith is dead without good works. (NLT)

Main Idea

“For as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is dead also.” James closes the argument by showing that faith and its works belong together as surely as body and spirit in a living person.

Verse Explanation

“For as the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without works is dead also.” James closes the argument by showing that faith and its works belong together as surely as body and spirit in a living person.

“For as the body without the spirit is dead,”

James closes the argument with a picture. When the spirit leaves the body, the body dies. It may still look like a person, but there is no life in it.

“so faith without works is dead also.”

A faith that produces no works has no life in it. It may look like faith, use the language of faith, and claim the name of faith, but it is lifeless. James closes the argument by showing that faith and its works belong together as surely as body and spirit in a living person. The comparison does not mean that works create faith. It means that genuine faith and its works cannot be separated.

Study Notes

This is the third time in the chapter that James calls faith without works “dead” (2:17, 20, 26).
