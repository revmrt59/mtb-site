Chapter Study

James 4

Verse-by-Verse
James 4:1
James 4:1
NKJV

James 4:1 Where do wars and fights come from among you? Do they not come from your desires for pleasure that war in your members?(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:1 What is causing the quarrels and fights among you? Don’t they come from the evil desires at war within you? (NLT)

Main Idea

James asks where the “wars and fights” among them come from, and answers that they arise from “your desires for pleasure that war in your members.” Conflict in the community springs from selfish desire at war within each person.

Verse Explanation

James asks where the “wars and fights” among them come from, and answers that they arise from “your desires for pleasure that war in your members.” Conflict in the community springs from selfish desire at war within each person.

“Where do wars and fights come from among you?”

James asks a direct question. “Wars” suggests ongoing conflict. “Fights” suggests individual quarrels and battles. Both were happening “among you,” within the community of believers. James has just spoken of peace and peacemakers (3:18). Now he confronts the conflict in the church.

“Do they not come from your desires for pleasure that war in your members?”

James answers with another question that expects a yes. These conflicts “come from your desires for pleasure.” The root is selfish craving, the desire to get what one wants. These desires “war in your members,” in the parts of each person. The outward conflict among believers springs from an inward conflict within each one. Conflict in the community springs from selfish desire at war within each person.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “desires for pleasure” is the word from which English gets “hedonism.” It appears again in 4:3 (“your pleasures”).

Peter uses similar language: “fleshly lusts which war against the soul” (1 Peter 2:11).

James 4:2
James 4:2
NKJV

James 4:2 You lust and do not have. You murder and covet and cannot obtain. You fight and war. Yet you do not have because you do not ask.(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:2 You want what you don’t have, so you scheme and kill to get it. You are jealous of what others have, but you can’t get it, so you fight and wage war to take it away from them. Yet you don’t have what you want because you don’t ask God for it. (NLT)

Main Idea

“You lust and do not have. You murder and covet and cannot obtain. You fight and war.” Unchecked craving can drive people to actual murder and open strife, and still they do not get what they desire, partly “because you do not ask” God at all.

Verse Explanation

“You lust and do not have. You murder and covet and cannot obtain. You fight and war.” Unchecked craving can drive people to actual murder and open strife, and still they do not get what they desire, partly “because you do not ask” God at all.

“You lust and do not have.”

James traces how selfish desire works. People crave something, but they do not get it. Desire remains unsatisfied.

“You murder and covet and cannot obtain.”

Unsatisfied desire leads to worse things. James follows selfish craving to its extreme end: it can lead to actual murder. This follows the progression in 1:14–15, where desire gives birth to sin and sin brings forth death. James does not name a particular murder, but he does not soften the word. Jesus also warned that anger and contempt in the heart are the roots from which murder grows (Matthew 5:21–22).

Even when people “covet” intensely, they “cannot obtain.” Their desires are never satisfied.

“You fight and war.”

Unsatisfied desire breaks out in open conflict among people.

“Yet you do not have because you do not ask.”

James points to a simple reason for their lack. They have been trying to get what they want by fighting rather than by asking God. They do not pray. Unchecked craving can drive people to actual murder and open strife, and still they do not get what they desire, partly because they do not ask God at all.

Study Notes

Some interpreters have suggested changing the Greek word for “murder” to a similar word meaning “envy,” but no manuscript supports that change. The NKJV, following the Greek text, reads “murder.”

James 4:3
James 4:3
NKJV

James 4:3 You ask and do not receive, because you ask amiss, that you may spend it on your pleasures.(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:3 And even when you ask, you don’t get it because your motives are all wrong — you want only what will give you pleasure. (NLT)

Main Idea

The people whose desires have caused these quarrels do sometimes ask God, yet they do not receive “because you ask amiss, that you may spend it on your pleasures.” James names their selfish aim as the reason these requests go unanswered: they want only to spend what they receive on their own pleasures.

Verse Explanation

The people whose desires have caused these quarrels do sometimes ask God, yet they do not receive “because you ask amiss, that you may spend it on your pleasures.” James names their selfish aim as the reason these requests go unanswered: they want only to spend what they receive on their own pleasures.

“You ask and do not receive,”

James anticipates an objection. Some do ask God, yet their prayers go unanswered.

“because you ask amiss, that you may spend it on your pleasures.”

James explains why. “You ask amiss.” They ask wrongly, with the wrong motive. They ask “that you may spend it on your pleasures.” They want God to supply their selfish desires. Their prayers are another way of feeding the craving that caused the conflict in the first place. James names their selfish aim as the reason these requests go unanswered.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “pleasures” is the same word translated “desires for pleasure” in 4:1. The selfish desires that cause conflict also corrupt prayer.

James distinguishes two failures in 4:2–3: not asking at all, and asking for selfish ends.

James 4:4–6 — Friendship with the World and God’s Greater Grace

James 4:4
James 4:4
NKJV

James 4:4 Adulterers and adulteresses! Do you not know that friendship with the world is enmity with God? Whoever therefore wants to be a friend of the world makes himself an enemy of God.(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:4 You adulterers! Don’t you realize that friendship with the world makes you an enemy of God? I say it again: If you want to be a friend of the world, you make yourself an enemy of God. (NLT)

Main Idea

James calls them “Adulterers and adulteresses,” because “friendship with the world is enmity with God.” Giving loyalty to desires and values opposed to God is spiritual unfaithfulness that makes a person His enemy.

Verse Explanation

James calls them “Adulterers and adulteresses,” because “friendship with the world is enmity with God.” Giving loyalty to desires and values opposed to God is spiritual unfaithfulness that makes a person His enemy.

“Adulterers and adulteresses!”

James uses strong language. He is not speaking mainly of sexual sin. He is speaking of spiritual unfaithfulness. In the Old Testament, God often described His relationship with His people as a marriage. When Israel worshiped other gods, the prophets called it adultery (for example, Jeremiah 3:20; Hosea 2:2). James’s readers have been unfaithful to God by giving their loyalty to the world.

“Do you not know that friendship with the world is enmity with God?”

“The world” here means the values, desires, and ways of life opposed to God (1:27). “Friendship with the world” means embracing those values and living by them. Such friendship “is enmity with God.” It makes a person God’s enemy. A person cannot be friends with both.

“Whoever therefore wants to be a friend of the world makes himself an enemy of God.”

James draws the conclusion. The word “wants” shows that this is a choice. A person who chooses the world’s friendship has chosen to oppose God. Giving loyalty to desires and values opposed to God is spiritual unfaithfulness that makes a person His enemy.

Study Notes

Many Greek manuscripts read only “Adulteresses,” the feminine form. The feminine form fits the Old Testament picture of God’s people as an unfaithful wife.

John gives a similar warning: “If anyone loves the world, the love of the Father is not in him” (1 John 2:15).

James 4:5
James 4:5
NKJV

James 4:5 Or do you think that the Scripture says in vain, "The Spirit who dwells in us yearns jealously"?(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:5 Do you think the Scriptures have no meaning? They say that God is passionate that the spirit he has placed within us should be faithful to him. (NLT)

Main Idea

James appeals to Scripture: “The Spirit who dwells in us yearns jealously.” God jealously desires the wholehearted love of His people, a longing the NKJV presents as expressed through the Spirit who dwells in them.

Verse Explanation

James appeals to Scripture: “The Spirit who dwells in us yearns jealously.” God jealously desires the wholehearted love of His people, a longing the NKJV presents as expressed through the Spirit who dwells in them.

“Or do you think that the Scripture says in vain,”

James asks whether his readers think “the Scripture says in vain” what it says. “In vain” means emptily, without meaning. God’s word is never empty. What it says matters.

“The Spirit who dwells in us yearns jealously’?”

James appeals to Scripture. God jealously desires the wholehearted love of His people. The NKJV presents that desire as expressed through the Spirit who dwells in them.

God’s jealousy is not petty or insecure. It is the rightful claim of a husband on his wife’s love. God made His people and redeemed them. He has every right to their whole hearts, and He will not share them with the world. In the Old Testament, God says, “the LORD, whose name is Jealous, is a jealous God” (Exodus 34:14). James applies that truth to believers in whom the Spirit dwells (compare 1 Corinthians 3:16). When believers give their love to the world, God’s jealous love is grieved.

Study Notes

No single Old Testament verse contains the exact words James quotes. He may be summarizing a theme of Scripture, such as God’s jealousy for His people (Exodus 20:5; 34:14), rather than quoting one passage.

The NKJV reads “The Spirit who dwells in us.” Other manuscripts read a verb form meaning caused to dwell, with God understood as the one who caused the spirit to dwell in us. The difference in the verb leaves the identity of “spirit” open.

The Greek of this verse can be read in more than one way. The NKJV presents the Spirit dwelling in believers as yearning jealously for them. Other translations read it as describing the human spirit, which God made to live in us, as tending toward envy. The NKJV reading fits the charge of spiritual adultery in 4:4 and the promise of grace in 4:6.

James 4:6
James 4:6
NKJV

James 4:6 But He gives more grace. Therefore He says: "God resists the proud, But gives grace to the humble."(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:6 And he gives grace generously. As the Scriptures say, “God opposes the proudbut gives grace to the humble.” (NLT)

Main Idea

“But He gives more grace,” and Scripture says, “God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble.” God meets divided hearts with greater grace, which the humble receive while He sets Himself against the proud.

Verse Explanation

“But He gives more grace,” and Scripture says, “God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble.” God meets divided hearts with greater grace, which the humble receive while He sets Himself against the proud.

“But He gives more grace.”

“But” brings hope. God’s jealous claim could crush people who have been unfaithful. Instead, “He gives more grace.” God’s grace is greater than their sin and greater than the pull of the world. Whatever God demands, He also supplies the grace to obey.

“Therefore He says: ‘God resists the proud, But gives grace to the humble.”

James supports this with Scripture. He quotes Proverbs 3:34: “God resists the proud, But gives grace to the humble.” “Resists” means to oppose, to set oneself against, like an army in battle formation. God opposes the proud. They want to live their own way, and God stands against them. But God “gives grace to the humble.” Those who admit their need and turn to Him receive His grace. God meets divided hearts with greater grace, which the humble receive while He sets Himself against the proud.

Study Notes

James quotes the Greek translation of Proverbs 3:34. The NKJV of Proverbs 3:34 reads, “Surely He scorns the scornful, But gives grace to the humble.” Peter quotes the same verse in 1 Peter 5:5.

James 4:7–10 — Submitting to God and Drawing Near

James 4:7
James 4:7
NKJV

James 4:7 Therefore submit to God. Resist the devil and he will flee from you.(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:7 So humble yourselves before God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because God gives grace to the humble, believers are to submit to Him and resist the devil, who “will flee from you.” Resisting the devil is part of yielding to God, and it carries a sure promise.

Verse Explanation

Because God gives grace to the humble, believers are to submit to Him and resist the devil, who “will flee from you.” Resisting the devil is part of yielding to God, and it carries a sure promise.

“Therefore submit to God.”

“Therefore” draws a conclusion. Because God gives grace to the humble, believers must “submit to God.” “Submit” means to place oneself under God’s authority, to yield to Him. This is what humility looks like in practice.

“Resist the devil and he will flee from you.”

Submitting to God includes resisting His enemy. “Resist the devil.” The word “resist” means to stand against, to refuse to give ground. Believers must not cooperate with the devil’s temptations or schemes.

The result is a promise: “he will flee from you.” The devil cannot stand against a believer who is submitted to God. Resisting the devil is part of yielding to God, and it carries a sure promise.

Study Notes

The Greek verbs translated “resists” in 4:6 and “resist” here are different verbs, but both begin with the prefix anti- and carry the idea of opposition. God resists the proud, and believers resist the devil.

Peter gives the same command: “Resist him, steadfast in the faith” (1 Peter 5:9).

James 4:8
James 4:8
NKJV

James 4:8 Draw near to God and He will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, you sinners; and purify your hearts, you double-minded.(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:8 Come close to God, and God will come close to you. Wash your hands, you sinners; purify your hearts, for your loyalty is divided between God and the world. (NLT)

Main Idea

“Draw near to God and He will draw near to you.” Sinners must cleanse their hands and the double-minded purify their hearts, turning from outward wrongdoing and from divided loyalty.

Verse Explanation

“Draw near to God and He will draw near to you.” Sinners must cleanse their hands and the double-minded purify their hearts, turning from outward wrongdoing and from divided loyalty.

“Draw near to God and He will draw near to you.”

James gives another command with a promise. “Draw near to God.” Come close to Him in prayer, worship, and trust. “He will draw near to you.” God responds to those who seek Him. He does not keep His distance from people who come to Him humbly.

“Cleanse your hands, you sinners;”

Drawing near to God requires cleansing. “Hands” represent actions. Those who have sinned must clean up their outward conduct.

“and purify your hearts, you double-minded.”

“Hearts” represent the inner life, thoughts, motives, and desires. The “double-minded” are those whose loyalty is divided between God and the world (compare 1:8). They must purify their hearts, turning from divided loyalty to wholehearted devotion. Sinners must turn from outward wrongdoing and from divided loyalty.

Study Notes

The language of drawing near and washing hands comes from the Old Testament priests, who washed before approaching God (Exodus 30:19–21). Psalm 24:4 describes the one who may stand in God’s holy place as having “clean hands and a pure heart.”

James 4:9
James 4:9
NKJV

James 4:9 Lament and mourn and weep! Let your laughter be turned to mourning and your joy to gloom.(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:9 Let there be tears for what you have done. Let there be sorrow and deep grief. Let there be sadness instead of laughter, and gloom instead of joy. (NLT)

Main Idea

James calls them to “Lament and mourn and weep,” letting their laughter turn to mourning and their joy to gloom. Genuine repentance grieves over sin instead of treating it lightly.

Verse Explanation

James calls them to “Lament and mourn and weep,” letting their laughter turn to mourning and their joy to gloom. Genuine repentance grieves over sin instead of treating it lightly.

“Lament and mourn and weep!”

James calls for grief over sin. These three words describe deep sorrow, expressed openly. James is not calling for gloom as a way of life. He is calling for genuine repentance, the kind that grieves over sin instead of treating it lightly.

“Let your laughter be turned to mourning and your joy to gloom.”

James wants their “laughter” to be “turned to mourning” and their “joy to gloom.” The laughter and joy he has in mind are the careless enjoyment of people who are friends with the world and untroubled by their sin. Genuine repentance grieves over sin instead of treating it lightly.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “gloom” describes a downcast face, eyes lowered in shame. It recalls the tax collector who “would not so much as raise his eyes to heaven” (Luke 18:13).

James 4:10
James 4:10
NKJV

James 4:10 Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and He will lift you up.(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:10 Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will lift you up in honor. (NLT)

Main Idea

“Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and He will lift you up.” The call to repent ends with a promise that the Lord Himself raises those who humble themselves before Him.

Verse Explanation

“Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and He will lift you up.” The call to repent ends with a promise that the Lord Himself raises those who humble themselves before Him.

“Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord,”

James ends the call to repentance. Believers must bow low before God, acknowledging their sin and their need. They do this “in the sight of the Lord,” in His presence, before His eyes.

“and He will lift you up.”

The promise follows. God exalts those who humble themselves. The same grace promised to the humble in 4:6 now lifts them up. The call to repent ends with a promise that the Lord Himself raises those who humble themselves before Him. The timing and the form of that lifting are His to decide.

Study Notes

Jesus taught the same principle: “he who humbles himself will be exalted” (Matthew 23:12).

James 4:11–12 — The One Lawgiver and Judge

James 4:11
James 4:11
NKJV

James 4:11 Do not speak evil of one another, brethren. He who speaks evil of a brother and judges his brother, speaks evil of the law and judges the law. But if you judge the law, you are not a doer of the law but a judge.(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:11 Don’t speak evil against each other, dear brothers and sisters. If you criticize and judge each other, then you are criticizing and judging God’s law. But your job is to obey the law, not to judge whether it applies to you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Believers must not speak evil of one another, for whoever speaks evil of a brother and judges him “speaks evil of the law and judges the law.” Since the royal law of James 2:8 commands believers to love their neighbor, the one who slanders a brother treats that command as not worth obeying and sets himself above God’s law as its judge.

Verse Explanation

Believers must not speak evil of one another, for whoever speaks evil of a brother and judges him “speaks evil of the law and judges the law.” Since the royal law of James 2:8 commands believers to love their neighbor, the one who slanders a brother treats that command as not worth obeying and sets himself above God’s law as its judge.

“Do not speak evil of one another, brethren.”

James turns to a related sin. “Do not speak evil of one another.” “Speak evil” means to slander, to speak against someone, to tear them down with words. James addresses “brethren,” fellow believers. This is a family sin.

“He who speaks evil of a brother and judges his brother,”

Slander usually involves judging. The slanderer decides that his brother is guilty and condemns him with words.

“speaks evil of the law and judges the law.”

The law here is the royal law of 2:8: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.” The slanderer does not love his neighbor. By ignoring that command, he treats it as not worth obeying. He sets himself above the law as its judge.

“But if you judge the law, you are not a doer of the law but a judge.”

James explains. The proper role of believers is to obey God’s law, to be doers of it (1:22). The slanderer has taken a different role. He has made himself a judge of the law, deciding which parts are worth keeping.

This does not forbid truthful correction. James himself calls believers to turn back a brother who wanders (5:19–20). What James forbids is the contempt and condemnation that tear down a brother.

Study Notes

Leviticus 19:16, just before the command to love one’s neighbor, forbids going about “as a talebearer among your people.”

James 4:12
NKJV

James 4:12 There is one Lawgiver, who is able to save and to destroy. Who are you to judge another?(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:12 God alone, who gave the law, is the Judge. He alone has the power to save or to destroy. So what right do you have to judge your neighbor? (NLT)

Main Idea

“There is one Lawgiver, who is able to save and to destroy.” Since that authority belongs to God alone, James asks, “Who are you to judge another?”

Verse Explanation

“There is one Lawgiver, who is able to save and to destroy.” Since that authority belongs to God alone, James asks, “Who are you to judge another?”

“There is one Lawgiver, who is able to save and to destroy.”

James reminds his readers who the true judge is. “There is one Lawgiver.” Only God makes the law, and only God has the authority to judge by it. He is “able to save and to destroy.” He holds the power of life and death.

“Who are you to judge another?”

The slanderer has taken a place that belongs only to God. Since that authority belongs to God alone, no believer has the right to sit in judgment on another and condemn him.

Study Notes

Many Greek manuscripts add and Judge after Lawgiver. The NKJV follows a text that reads “one Lawgiver.”

James 4:13–17 — Planning Under the Lord’s Will

James 4:13
James 4:13
NKJV

James 4:13 Come now, you who say, "Today or tomorrow we will go to such and such a city, spend a year there, buy and sell, and make a profit";(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:13 Look here, you who say, “Today or tomorrow we are going to a certain town and will stay there a year. We will do business there and make a profit.” (NLT)

Main Idea

“Come now,” James says to those who plan to go to a city, spend a year there, buy and sell, and make a profit. He confronts merchants who speak of the future as if it were theirs to control.

Verse Explanation

“Come now,” James says to those who plan to go to a city, spend a year there, buy and sell, and make a profit. He confronts merchants who speak of the future as if it were theirs to control.

“Come now, you who say,”

James begins a new section with “Come now.” The phrase calls for attention, with a note of rebuke. He addresses “you who say” certain things.

“Today or tomorrow we will go to such and such a city, spend a year there, buy and sell, and make a profit’;”

These people make confident plans. “Today or tomorrow we will go to such and such a city.” They choose the time and the place. They plan to “spend a year there, buy and sell, and make a profit.” They are merchants planning a business trip. They assume everything is under their control: when they will go, where they will go, how long they will stay, what they will do, and what they will gain. James will show in 4:14 how wrong that assumption is.

Planning is not wrong. Trade is not wrong. What James confronts is the way these merchants speak of the future as if it were theirs to control.

Study Notes

James uses “Come now” again in 5:1 to address the rich oppressors.

James 4:14
James 4:14
NKJV

James 4:14 whereas you do not know what will happen tomorrow. For what is your life? It is even a vapor that appears for a little time and then vanishes away.(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:14 How do you know what your life will be like tomorrow? Your life is like the morning fog — it’s here a little while, then it’s gone. (NLT)

Main Idea

The merchants do not know what will happen tomorrow, for life “is even a vapor that appears for a little time and then vanishes away.” The brevity and uncertainty of life expose the folly of confident plans that leave God out.

Verse Explanation

The merchants do not know what will happen tomorrow, for life “is even a vapor that appears for a little time and then vanishes away.” The brevity and uncertainty of life expose the folly of confident plans that leave God out.

“whereas you do not know what will happen tomorrow.”

James exposes the flaw in their plans. They spoke of a whole year, but they cannot even see one day ahead. Proverbs warns, “Do not boast about tomorrow, For you do not know what a day may bring forth” (Proverbs 27:1).

“For what is your life?”

James asks a searching question. What is it really? How secure is it?

“It is even a vapor that appears for a little time and then vanishes away.”

He answers: “It is even a vapor.” A “vapor” is a puff of smoke or a morning mist. It “appears for a little time and then vanishes away.” Human life is that brief and that fragile. The brevity and uncertainty of life expose the folly of confident plans that leave God out.

Study Notes

Psalm 39:5 says, “Certainly every man at his best state is but vapor.”

James 4:15
James 4:15
NKJV

James 4:15 Instead you ought to say, "If the Lord wills, we shall live and do this or that."(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:15 What you ought to say is, “If the Lord wants us to, we will live and do this or that.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Rather than boasting about their plans, the merchants ought to say, “If the Lord wills, we shall live and do this or that.” Their very lives, and not only their plans, depend on the Lord’s will.

Verse Explanation

Rather than boasting about their plans, the merchants ought to say, “If the Lord wills, we shall live and do this or that.” Their very lives, and not only their plans, depend on the Lord’s will.

“Instead you ought to say, ‘If the Lord wills, we shall live and do this or that.”

James tells them what they should say instead. “If the Lord wills, we shall live and do this or that.” Notice the order. The first thing that depends on the Lord’s will is “we shall live.” Their very lives depend on Him. Only after that comes “do this or that,” their plans and activities.

Saying “If the Lord wills” is not a magic phrase. It expresses a real attitude of dependence on God. Paul often spoke this way, telling the Ephesians, “I will return again to you, God willing” (Acts 18:21). Their very lives, and not only their plans, depend on the Lord’s will.

None.

James 4:16
NKJV

James 4:16 But now you boast in your arrogance. All such boasting is evil.(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:16 Otherwise you are boasting about your own pretentious plans, and all such boasting is evil. (NLT)

Main Idea

“But now you boast in your arrogance. All such boasting is evil.” Proud confidence in their own plans is itself sinful.

Verse Explanation

“But now you boast in your arrogance. All such boasting is evil.” Proud confidence in their own plans is itself sinful.

“But now you boast in your arrogance.”

James returns to their actual attitude. They do not say, “If the Lord wills.” They boast. Their “arrogance” is a proud confidence in their own abilities and plans.

James 4:17
James 4:17
NKJV

James 4:17 Therefore, to him who knows to do good and does not do it, to him it is sin.(NKJV)

NLT

James 4:17 Remember, it is sin to know what you ought to do and then not do it. (NLT)

Main Idea

“Therefore, to him who knows to do good and does not do it, to him it is sin.” Those who know they should reckon with the Lord’s will and fail to do so sin, and the principle reaches any good a person knows to do and leaves undone.

Verse Explanation

“Therefore, to him who knows to do good and does not do it, to him it is sin.” Those who know they should reckon with the Lord’s will and fail to do so sin, and the principle reaches any good a person knows to do and leaves undone.

“Therefore, to him who knows to do good and does not do it, to him it is sin.”

James concludes with a principle. The word “Therefore” ties the principle to what came before. The merchants now know they should reckon with the Lord’s will. If they continue to plan as though God did not matter, they sin.

The principle reaches further. Sin is not only doing what is wrong. It is also failing to do what one knows is right. Those who know they should reckon with the Lord’s will and fail to do so sin, and the principle reaches any good a person knows to do and leaves undone.

Study Notes

None.
