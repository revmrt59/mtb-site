Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 5
Main Idea:

James warns rich oppressors that the Lord of hosts hears the cries of those they cheated, then calls believers to wait patiently for the Lord’s coming, to speak truthfully, to pray in every circumstance, to confess and pray for one another, and to turn back anyone who wanders from the truth.

Setting:

James 5:1–6 completes the “Come now” warnings that began in 4:13. At 5:7 James turns back to “brethren,” and the letter ends with the church caring for its suffering, sick, and straying members, with no conventional farewell.

Chapter Outline:

  • James 5:1–6 — Judgment on Rich Oppressors

  • James 5:7–11 — Patient Endurance Until the Lord Comes

  • James 5:12 — Let Your Yes Be Yes

  • James 5:13–15 — Prayer for the Suffering and the Sick

  • James 5:16–18 — Confession, Prayer, and Elijah’s Example

  • James 5:19–20 — Turning Back the Wanderer

Key Verse:

“Confess your trespasses to one another, and pray for one another, that you may be healed. The effective, fervent prayer of a righteous man avails much.” (James 5:16)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

The chapter opens with judgment. Rich landowners have hoarded wealth that is already rotting, lived in luxury, and cheated the workers who harvested their fields. Those withheld wages “cry out; and the cries of the reapers have reached the ears of the Lord of Sabaoth” (5:4). The oppressors may think no one notices, but the Lord of hosts hears every wronged worker.

Then James turns to the believers who suffer such wrongs. “Therefore be patient, brethren, until the coming of the Lord” (5:7). Like a farmer waiting for the early and latter rain, they must establish their hearts and refuse to turn their pressure against one another, because “the Judge is standing at the door” (5:9). The prophets suffered patiently, and Job’s story shows “that the Lord is very compassionate and merciful” (5:11). Their speech must be plain and trustworthy, a simple “Yes” or “No” with no oaths to prop it up (5:12).

The letter ends with a church that prays together. The suffering pray, the cheerful sing, and the sick call for the elders, who pray and anoint with oil in the Lord’s name. “The prayer of faith will save the sick, and the Lord will raise him up” (5:15), as believers pray in the faith the Holy Spirit gives for a particular healing under the Lord’s will. Believers confess their sins to one another and pray for one another, and Elijah, “a man with a nature like ours” (5:17), shows what God does through earnest prayer. Last of all, when someone wanders from the truth, another believer goes after him, turning him back from the path of death that James first described in 1:15.

Chapter Sections

James 5:1–6 — Judgment on Rich Oppressors

James announces coming misery on rich oppressors whose hoarded wealth is already decaying. They withheld the wages of their harvesters, lived in self-indulgence, and condemned and killed innocent people who did not resist, and the Lord of hosts has heard the cries of those they wronged.

James 5:7–11 — Patient Endurance Until the Lord Comes

Believers must wait patiently for the Lord’s coming, like a farmer waiting for rain, and must not grumble against one another, for the Judge is near. The prophets and Job show the blessing of endurance and the Lord’s compassion and mercy.

James 5:12 — Let Your Yes Be Yes

Believers are not to back up their words with oaths by heaven or earth. Their plain “Yes” and “No” should be trustworthy, so that they do not fall under judgment.

James 5:13–15 — Prayer for the Suffering and the Sick

The suffering should pray and the cheerful sing praise, while the sick should call the church’s elders to pray over them and anoint them with oil in the Lord’s name. The prayer of faith is prayer offered in the faith the Holy Spirit can give for a particular healing under the Lord’s will; through it the Lord Himself raises the sick person up, and if he has committed sins, he will be forgiven.

James 5:16–18 — Confession, Prayer, and Elijah’s Example

Believers are to confess their sins to one another and pray for one another so that they may be healed, because the earnest prayer of a righteous person accomplishes much. Elijah was an ordinary man, yet God answered his prayers by withholding rain and then sending it.

James 5:19–20 — Turning Back the Wanderer

When a believer wanders from the truth and another turns him back, that rescue saves him from the path of death that sin produces. It also brings the returning wanderer to God’s forgiveness, covering a multitude of sins.

Why This Chapter Matters

James 5 shows that God sees injustice and hears those who are wronged (5:4). That truth gives suffering believers reason to wait for the Lord’s coming with patience rather than bitterness, and it warns anyone who uses power to cheat others.

The chapter also pictures a church that carries its people together. No one faces suffering, sickness, sin, or wandering alone. James calls the church to pray, to anoint the sick, to confess sin honestly, and to go after those who stray. The Lord is the one who heals and forgives, and He works through a praying, caring community (5:13–20).

Key Themes to Watch For

The Lord hears the oppressed. Withheld wages cry out, and the Lord of hosts hears (5:1–6).

Patience until the Lord comes. The farmer, the prophets, and Job show endurance grounded in hope (5:7–11).

Truthful speech. A believer’s simple word should be trustworthy (5:12).

The prayer of faith. Believers pray in suffering and sickness, and the Lord raises the sick through prayer offered in Spirit-given faith (5:13–18).

Restoring the wanderer. Turning a straying believer back saves a soul from death (5:19–20).

Key Words & Concepts

The Lord of Sabaoth (5:4). “Sabaoth” is a Hebrew word meaning “hosts” or “armies.” The title presents God as the Lord of hosts, who hears the cry of defrauded workers.

The early and latter rain (5:7). The autumn and spring rains on which farmers in Israel depended (Deuteronomy 11:14). The farmer cannot hurry them, so he waits in hope.

The Judge is standing at the door (5:9). A picture of the Lord’s nearness as Judge. It warns believers not to grumble against one another and assures them that wrongs will be set right.

Elders and anointing with oil (5:14). The elders were the recognized spiritual leaders of the congregation. Their anointing in the Lord’s name is best understood as a tangible sign of the Holy Spirit’s power coming upon the sick person to heal through the prayer of faith (compare Mark 6:13). Medical care is not set aside, but it is not the point of the oil here.

“If he has committed sins” (5:15). The condition shows that sickness is not always caused by personal sin (compare John 9:1–3). When healing does not come, the outcome gives no reason to blame the sick person or the family.

The wanderer (5:19–20). A believer who has strayed from the truth. The death James warns of is the destructive course of sin he described in 1:15, which can reach even physical death, and turning the person back interrupts it.
