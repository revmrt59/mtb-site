John 10:32

NKJV

John 10:32 Jesus answered them, "Many good works I have shown you from My Father. For which of those works do you stone Me?"(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:32 Jesus said, “At my Father’s direction I have done many good works. For which one are you going to stone me?” (NLT)

Main Idea


As His opponents prepare to stone Him, Jesus confronts the contradiction in their response: they have witnessed many good works from the Father, yet they now intend to kill the One through whom those works were done.

Verse Explanation


“Jesus answered them” — Verse 31 ended with the Jews taking up stones to stone Jesus after His declaration, “I and My Father are one.” Jesus does not retreat or attempt to escape the issue. He directly challenges those preparing to kill Him and forces them to consider what they have actually witnessed.

“Many good works I have shown you from My Father.” — Jesus points to the works He has performed openly among them. These were not merely impressive miracles; they were “good works” revealing the compassionate and life-giving activity of the Father. Throughout John, Jesus’ works function as testimony to His identity and His relationship with the Father. Jesus has already told them, “The works that I do in My Father’s name, they bear witness of Me” (John 10:25). Their own experience therefore provides evidence they must account for.

“For which of those works do you stone Me?” — Jesus exposes the contradiction in their actions. They have seen works characterized by goodness and originating from the Father, yet their response is to prepare an execution. Jesus knows that their accusation concerns His claim about His relationship with God, which they will state in the next verse. His question forces them to acknowledge that His works themselves provide no basis for condemning Him.

Study Notes


“Good” translates kalos (G2570), which can describe something good, noble, beautiful, or excellent. The expression emphasizes the character and quality of Jesus’ works, not merely their supernatural nature.

“Works” translates erga, from ergon (G2041). “Works” is an important theme in John’s Gospel. Jesus repeatedly appeals to His works as evidence of His relationship with the Father (John 5:36; 10:25, 37–38; 14:11).

“I have shown you” translates a form of deiknymi (G1166), meaning to show, demonstrate, or make known. Jesus’ works have visibly displayed the Father’s activity before them.

Jesus’ question prepares directly for verse 33. His opponents will admit that they are not attempting to stone Him “for a good work” but because they believe His claims about Himself amount to blasphemy. The controversy therefore centers on Jesus’ identity and His unique relationship with the Father.

Spirit & Life


Jesus points His opponents back to what the Father has been doing directly in front of them. They have seen His works, but their settled resistance to Jesus prevents them from responding properly to what those works reveal.

That is the tragedy developing in this passage. The evidence is present, yet their hearts remain opposed to the One to whom the evidence points.

Jesus consistently refuses to separate His works from the Father: “Many good works I have shown you from My Father.” What He does reveals something about where He comes from and who He is. His works are signs pointing beyond themselves to the Father working through the Son.

John 10 therefore keeps pressing the same central question: What will we do with Jesus? His words and His works belong together. Those standing before Him had seen what He did and heard what He claimed, yet they were preparing to stone Him. The proper response to the revelation of the Father in the Son is not resistance, but belief.

John 10:1

NKJV

John 10:1 "Most assuredly, I say to you, he who does not enter the sheepfold by the door, but climbs up some other way, the same is a thief and a robber.(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:1 “I tell you the truth, anyone who sneaks over the wall of a sheepfold, rather than going through the gate, must surely be a thief and a robber! (NLT)

Main Idea


Jesus begins the shepherd discourse by contrasting the rightful shepherd who enters God’s flock legitimately with intruders who seek access for selfish and destructive purposes.

Verse Explanation


“Most assuredly, I say to you” — Jesus introduces His words with solemn emphasis. The statement continues the confrontation of chapter 9, where the Pharisees had rejected the man Jesus healed and cast him out (John 9:34–41). Jesus now turns from exposing their spiritual blindness to describing true and false shepherds.

“he who does not enter the sheepfold by the door” — A sheepfold was an enclosed place where sheep could be gathered and protected. Jesus uses this familiar picture to speak about God’s people and those who claim authority over them. The issue is not merely whether someone is among the sheep, but whether that person has entered in the proper, authorized way.

“but climbs up some other way” — The picture is deliberately suspicious. Someone avoiding the legitimate entrance and climbing over the enclosure is acting without rightful access. In the immediate context, Jesus is beginning to expose religious leaders who claim spiritual authority while failing to recognize and follow what God is doing through Him.

“the same is a thief and a robber” — Jesus gives the intruder two forceful descriptions. A “thief” takes what does not belong to him, while a “robber” carries the idea of one who seizes or plunders. Both terms emphasize illegitimate access and harmful intent. Jesus will develop this contrast throughout the chapter, especially when He identifies Himself as both the door of the sheep and the Good Shepherd.

Study Notes


“Most assuredly” translates amen, amen, literally “truly, truly.” John frequently records this doubled expression when Jesus introduces a statement of particular importance and authority.

“Sheepfold” translates aule (G833), referring here to an enclosed courtyard or fold where sheep were kept.

“Thief” (kleptes, G2812) and “robber” (lestes, G3027) overlap but are not identical. Kleptes commonly emphasizes stealing, while lestes can describe a robber or bandit who takes by force.

The chapter division can obscure the connection with John 9. Jesus has just condemned the spiritual blindness of the Pharisees (9:39–41). The shepherd imagery beginning in 10:1 develops that confrontation by addressing the legitimacy and character of those who claim to lead God’s people.

Spirit & Life


Jesus cares not only that His sheep are gathered, but also about who leads them and how they are treated. John 9 has just shown a healed man being driven out by religious leaders, while Jesus sought him, received him, and brought him into deeper faith. John 10 now gives that contrast the imagery of shepherding.

This opening verse therefore prepares us to recognize something essential about Jesus: He does not approach God’s people as someone seeking to use them for His own purposes. He comes with rightful authority, and everything that follows will reveal His heart toward the sheep. As the chapter develops, the One exposing thieves and robbers will identify Himself as the Good Shepherd who gives His own life for the sheep.

John 10:2

NKJV

John 10:2 But he who enters by the door is the shepherd of the sheep.(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:2 But the one who enters through the gate is the shepherd of the sheep. (NLT)

Main Idea


Unlike the thief who seeks illegitimate access to the sheep, the true shepherd comes openly and rightfully because the sheep belong under his care.

Verse Explanation


“But he who enters by the door” — Jesus immediately contrasts the true shepherd with the thief and robber of verse 1. The intruder avoids the door and climbs in “some other way,” but the shepherd has no reason to hide or force his way into the fold. He enters through the proper entrance because his relationship to the sheep is legitimate.

“is the shepherd of the sheep” — The shepherd is the one who rightfully belongs with the sheep and bears responsibility for their care. In the immediate context, this contrast follows Jesus’ confrontation with the Pharisees at the end of chapter 9. They claimed to be spiritual guides, yet their treatment of the man born blind exposed the failure of their leadership. Jesus is beginning to distinguish false spiritual leadership from the shepherding that truly comes from God.

The statement also prepares for Jesus’ fuller revelation of Himself later in the chapter. He will declare, “I am the good shepherd” (John 10:11). Verse 2 introduces the shepherd; the following verses will reveal how the true shepherd relates to his sheep—he knows them, calls them, leads them, and ultimately lays down his life for them.

Study Notes


“Shepherd” translates poimen (G4166), the ordinary Greek term for one who tends and cares for sheep. Scripture frequently develops shepherd imagery for spiritual leadership, especially for God’s care of His people and for leaders entrusted with their welfare.

Verses 1–2 are constructed as a direct contrast: the thief “climbs up some other way,” while the shepherd “enters by the door.” The manner of entrance reveals whether the person’s relationship to the sheep is legitimate.

The shepherd imagery also carries significant Old Testament background. Israel’s leaders were repeatedly described as shepherds, and Ezekiel 34 sharply condemns shepherds who exploit the flock rather than care for it. God promises that He Himself will seek His sheep and appoint His servant David as shepherd over them (Ezekiel 34:11–16, 23). That background becomes especially important as Jesus identifies Himself as the Good Shepherd in John 10:11.

Spirit & Life


Jesus places rightful authority beside genuine care. The true shepherd does not seize the sheep, manipulate his way into their lives, or treat them as something to exploit. He comes as the one who rightfully cares for them.

The contrast with John 9 is striking. The religious leaders cast the healed man out; Jesus went and found him. They interrogated and rejected him; Jesus revealed Himself to him and received his worship. Before Jesus even says, “I am the good shepherd,” His actions have already begun to show what His shepherding looks like.

For those who belong to Jesus, this means we are not merely members of a flock—we are under the personal care of the rightful Shepherd. John 10 will progressively open that truth: He comes for His sheep, knows His sheep, leads His sheep, protects His sheep, and gives His life for His sheep.

John 10:3

NKJV

John 10:3 To him the doorkeeper opens, and the sheep hear his voice; and he calls his own sheep by name and leads them out.(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:3 The gatekeeper opens the gate for him, and the sheep recognize his voice and come to him. He calls his own sheep by name and leads them out. (NLT)

Main Idea


The true shepherd is recognized and admitted, and his relationship with the sheep is personal: they recognize his voice, he knows them individually, and he leads them out under his care.

Verse Explanation


“To him the doorkeeper opens” — Unlike the thief who must climb into the sheepfold another way, the shepherd is freely admitted through the proper entrance. The doorkeeper’s response confirms that the shepherd has legitimate access to the flock. Jesus continues the contrast begun in verses 1–2 between those who wrongfully approach the sheep and the one who rightfully belongs with them.

“and the sheep hear his voice” — The shepherd’s relationship with the sheep is marked by recognition. The sheep have become familiar with his voice and respond when they hear it. Jesus is moving beyond the question of rightful access to describe the relationship between the shepherd and his sheep. His voice is known among them.

“and he calls his own sheep by name” — The language becomes distinctly personal. They are not simply “the sheep” but “his own sheep,” and he calls them “by name.” The shepherd knows which sheep belong to him and addresses them individually. Jesus presents His shepherding as personal knowledge rather than distant supervision.

“and leads them out” — The shepherd does not merely call the sheep; he takes responsibility for where they go. He brings them out of the fold in order to lead them, a movement Jesus continues in verse 4. The sheep are not left to find their own way. They move under the direction of the shepherd whose voice they know.

Study Notes


“Doorkeeper” translates thuroros (G2377), referring to one responsible for guarding or opening a doorway or gate. Within the figure, his opening of the door reinforces the legitimate entrance of the shepherd in contrast with the intruder of verse 1.

“His own” translates idios (G2398), emphasizing what belongs particularly to someone. Its use strengthens the personal relationship expressed in the phrase “his own sheep.”

The sequence of the verse is significant: the shepherd is admitted, the sheep hear his voice, he calls his own sheep by name, and he leads them out. Verse 4 continues the movement by explaining that the shepherd goes before them and the sheep follow because they know his voice.

Spirit & Life


Jesus is beginning to reveal the deeply personal nature of His relationship with those who belong to Him. His sheep are “his own,” He calls them “by name,” and He “leads them.” His care is neither impersonal nor distant.

This also gives important meaning to hearing the Shepherd’s voice. In this verse, hearing is rooted in belonging and relationship. The sheep recognize the shepherd because they are his sheep and have learned his voice. As Jesus develops the picture, hearing His voice will be joined with following Him.

For the believer, the security of this verse begins with the Shepherd Himself. He knows His own personally, speaks so that they may follow, and takes the lead rather than leaving them to determine the way alone. The Christian life is therefore not merely knowing truths about the Shepherd; it is belonging to Jesus and learning to follow the One who knows His sheep by name.

John 10:4

NKJV

John 10:4 And when he brings out his own sheep, he goes before them; and the sheep follow him, for they know his voice.(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:4 After he has gathered his own flock, he walks ahead of them, and they follow him because they know his voice. (NLT)

Main Idea


The true shepherd leads from the front, and his sheep willingly follow because they have learned to recognize and trust his voice.

Verse Explanation


“And when he brings out his own sheep” — Jesus continues the movement begun in verse 3, where the shepherd calls his own sheep by name and leads them out. The emphasis remains on ownership and relationship: these are “his own sheep.” The shepherd is not gathering strangers for his own advantage; he is taking responsibility for sheep that belong to him.

“he goes before them” — The shepherd does not simply send the sheep in a direction and remain behind. He takes the lead and goes ahead of them. The image emphasizes personal leadership: the shepherd himself is present on the path he expects the sheep to travel. The sheep’s direction is determined by where their shepherd goes.

“and the sheep follow him” — Their response to the shepherd is to follow. Jesus is beginning to establish a central characteristic of His sheep: they do not merely hear the shepherd; they respond to his voice by going where he leads. This becomes explicit later when Jesus says, “My sheep hear My voice, and I know them, and they follow Me” (John 10:27).

“for they know his voice” — The reason the sheep follow is recognition. They have become familiar with the shepherd’s voice and can distinguish it as the voice of the one who belongs with them and leads them. Verse 5 will sharpen the contrast: they will not follow a stranger because they do not know the voice of strangers. Jesus is describing a relationship in which hearing, recognizing, and following belong together.

Study Notes


“Goes before” translates emprosthen (G1715), meaning “before” or “in front of.” The expression reinforces the shepherding picture: the shepherd leads from ahead rather than merely driving the flock from behind.

“Follow” translates akoloutheo (G190), a common New Testament verb meaning to follow or accompany someone. John uses it repeatedly for following Jesus (John 1:37–43; 8:12; 12:26). Within the shepherd imagery, the ordinary action of sheep following their shepherd also carries clear significance for discipleship.

Verses 3–4 contain a deliberate progression: the sheep hear the shepherd’s voice, he calls them, he leads them out, he goes before them, and they follow him. The emphasis is relational movement under the shepherd’s personal leadership.

Spirit & Life


Jesus does not merely call His sheep and then leave them to determine the path. “He goes before them.” The Shepherd Himself takes the lead.

That makes “they know his voice” especially important. The purpose of recognizing the Shepherd’s voice is not simply receiving information; it is following the Shepherd. Hearing and following belong together in Jesus’ picture.

This gives discipleship a deeply personal center. We are following Jesus Himself. He calls, He goes before, and His sheep follow. The security of the path is not that the sheep can see everything ahead, but that the Shepherd who knows them is already in front of them.

John 10:5

NKJV

John 10:5 Yet they will by no means follow a stranger, but will flee from him, for they do not know the voice of strangers."(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:5 They won’t follow a stranger; they will run from him because they don’t know his voice.” (NLT)

Main Idea


Jesus’ sheep recognize the difference between their shepherd and a stranger, so they refuse to follow an unfamiliar voice and move away from it.

Verse Explanation


“Yet they will by no means follow a stranger” — Jesus now gives the opposite side of the relationship described in verse 4. The sheep follow their shepherd because they know his voice, but they refuse to follow someone who is foreign to them. The wording is emphatic: “by no means” stresses their refusal. Recognition of the true shepherd produces not only following but also discernment about whom not to follow.

“but will flee from him” — The sheep do more than remain where they are; they move away from the stranger. Within Jesus’ illustration, an unfamiliar voice signals someone who does not have the established relationship with the sheep that their shepherd has. This continues the contrast with the thief and robber introduced in verse 1.

“for they do not know the voice of strangers” — Jesus gives the reason for their response. The issue is recognition. The sheep know the shepherd’s voice because they belong to him and have learned to recognize him; the stranger’s voice lacks that familiarity. Verses 4–5 therefore present two opposite responses: they follow the shepherd whose voice they know, and they flee from the stranger whose voice they do not know.

Study Notes


“By no means” reflects an emphatic Greek construction using ou me, a strong form of negation. Jesus is describing a decisive refusal to follow the stranger.

“Stranger” translates allotrios (G245), meaning one who belongs to another or is foreign to the person or group in view. In the shepherding picture, it describes someone who does not have the shepherd’s recognized relationship with the sheep.

The contrast between verses 4 and 5 is carefully balanced: “they know his voice” (v. 4) versus “they do not know the voice of strangers” (v. 5). Recognition determines response—following in one case and fleeing in the other.

Spirit & Life


Jesus’ picture shows that knowing the Shepherd’s voice also produces discernment about other voices. His sheep do not follow indiscriminately. They have learned whose voice belongs to the Shepherd who calls them by name and goes before them.

The emphasis is therefore first on knowing Jesus, not on becoming preoccupied with every false voice. The sheep recognize what does not belong because they know the voice they are meant to follow.

This gives spiritual discernment a relational center. Jesus’ sheep hear His voice, follow Him, and refuse voices that would draw them away from their Shepherd. The deeper our familiarity with Jesus through His Word and our life with Him, the more clearly we learn to recognize what is consistent with the Shepherd we know and what is not.

John 10:6

NKJV

John 10:6 Jesus used this illustration, but they did not understand the things which He spoke to them.(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:6 Those who heard Jesus use this illustration didn’t understand what he meant, (NLT)

Main Idea


Jesus used the familiar picture of shepherds and sheep to reveal spiritual truth, but His hearers failed to recognize what He was saying about themselves and His relationship to God’s people.

Verse Explanation


“Jesus used this illustration” — This refers to the shepherding picture Jesus has just given in verses 1–5: the sheepfold, the door, the shepherd, the sheep, the thief and robber, and the stranger. Jesus was using familiar realities from everyday life to communicate spiritual truth. The illustration also continues His confrontation with the Pharisees from the end of John 9, where their treatment of the man born blind exposed the character of their spiritual leadership.

“but they did not understand” — Jesus’ hearers could understand the ordinary details of sheep and shepherding, but they did not grasp what those details meant spiritually. This continues the theme of spiritual blindness from John 9:39–41. The problem was not that Jesus’ words were meaningless; His hearers failed to perceive the reality to which His words pointed.

“the things which He spoke to them” — Jesus had not yet explicitly identified each part of the illustration. He had described the true shepherd, the sheep who know his voice, and those who approach the flock illegitimately. Because His hearers did not understand, beginning in verse 7 Jesus will explain the imagery more directly, declaring, “I am the door of the sheep,” and later, “I am the good shepherd.”

Study Notes


“Illustration” translates paroimia (G3942), which can refer to a figurative saying, proverb, or veiled form of speech. John uses the term rather than the more familiar Synoptic term parabole (“parable”). The same word appears in John 16:25, 29 for figurative or indirect speech.

The pronoun “they” is best understood in connection with the Pharisees addressed at the end of John 9 (9:40–41). The chapter division between John 9 and 10 does not mark a clear change of scene.

Verse 6 functions as a transition. Verses 1–5 present the shepherding figure; verses 7 and following begin Jesus’ more explicit explanation, centered particularly on His identity as the door and the good shepherd.

Spirit & Life


It was possible to hear Jesus’ words and still miss what He was revealing. These listeners heard about the shepherd, his voice, and the sheep who followed him, yet “they did not understand.”

That is especially significant after Jesus’ words about spiritual blindness at the end of chapter 9. The religious leaders believed they could see, yet they were failing to recognize the One standing before them.

John 10 will now move from the picture to Jesus Himself. The answer to the illustration is not merely better knowledge about shepherding; it is revelation of who Jesus is. He is about to declare, “I am the door” and “I am the good shepherd.” Understanding the picture ultimately means seeing the Person to whom the picture points.

NLT

John 10:7 so he explained it to them: “I tell you the truth, I am the gate for the sheep. (NLT)

Main Idea


Because His hearers did not understand the shepherding illustration, Jesus explains it more directly by identifying Himself as the door through whom the sheep have rightful access and security.

Verse Explanation


“Then Jesus said to them again” — Verse 6 tells us that Jesus’ hearers did not understand what He had been saying. Rather than abandoning the shepherding picture, Jesus continues and begins explaining its meaning more directly. What had been presented figuratively in verses 1–5 now becomes centered explicitly on His own identity.

“Most assuredly, I say to you” — Jesus again uses this solemn expression to emphasize the importance and certainty of what follows. He is not merely explaining an interesting feature of shepherding; He is making a significant declaration about Himself and His relationship to God’s people.

“I am the door of the sheep” — Jesus now places Himself at the center of the illustration. The door is the legitimate point of entrance into the sheepfold. In verse 1, the thief and robber were identified by their attempt to enter some other way; now Jesus reveals that He Himself is the door. The imagery will be developed further in verse 9, where Jesus explains that the one who enters through Him will be saved and will “go in and out and find pasture.”

This is the first of two major “I am” declarations in this chapter. Jesus is “the door” (vv. 7, 9), and He is “the good shepherd” (vv. 11, 14). The images are different but complementary: as the door, Jesus is the necessary entrance for the sheep; as the Good Shepherd, He is the One who knows, leads, protects, and gives His life for them.

Study Notes


“Most assuredly” translates the doubled amen, amen. John repeatedly records this expression when Jesus introduces an authoritative statement of particular significance.

“Door” translates thura (G2374), the ordinary word for a door, gate, or entrance. Its meaning here is controlled by the sheepfold imagery of verses 1–5 and Jesus’ own explanation in verse 9.

The phrase “I am” translates ego eimi. John records a series of significant metaphorical declarations in which Jesus identifies Himself through this construction, including “the bread of life” (6:35), “the light of the world” (8:12), “the good shepherd” (10:11), “the resurrection and the life” (11:25), “the way, the truth, and the life” (14:6), and “the true vine” (15:1).

Spirit & Life


Jesus does not merely point toward the entrance; He says, “I am the door.” The focus moves from understanding the illustration to recognizing the Person standing before them.

That makes Jesus central to everything the sheep need. The sheep do not find their place in God’s flock by discovering another entrance or creating their own way in. Their access is bound up with Jesus Himself.

This also sharpens the contrast running through the passage. There are those who try to approach the sheep “some other way,” but Jesus declares that He is “the door.” Our security is therefore not found in religious position, human leadership, or another pathway to God. It is found in Christ Himself. Verse 9 will make the implication unmistakable: “If anyone enters by Me, he will be saved.”

NLT

John 10:8 All who came before me were thieves and robbers. But the true sheep did not listen to them. (NLT)

Main Idea


Jesus contrasts Himself with those who came seeking to lead God’s people illegitimately: they acted as thieves and robbers, but the true sheep did not ultimately recognize their voice as the voice of their shepherd.

Verse Explanation


“All who ever came before Me” — Jesus continues explaining His declaration, “I am the door of the sheep” (v. 7). He is not condemning every prophet or faithful leader who lived before Him. Scripture presents men such as Moses, David, and the prophets as servants sent by God. In the immediate shepherding context, Jesus is speaking about those who claimed access to and authority over God’s people apart from the rightful Shepherd and His purposes. The religious leaders confronting Jesus provide the immediate example.

“are thieves and robbers” — Jesus returns to the language of verse 1. A thief or robber does not rightfully own the sheep; he approaches them for what he can take from them. The description exposes false spiritual leadership that treats God’s people as something to possess, exploit, or control rather than a flock belonging to God. This is especially pointed after John 9, where the religious leaders cast out the man whom Jesus had healed.

“but the sheep did not hear them” — “Hear” carries the sense established in verses 3–5 of recognizing and responding to a shepherd’s voice. Jesus is not saying that false leaders never gained followers. Rather, within His shepherding illustration, His sheep do not recognize such voices as the voice of their true shepherd. Their distinguishing mark is that they respond to the Shepherd who rightfully knows and leads them.

Study Notes


The phrase “all who ever came before Me” requires interpretation within its context. It cannot naturally include God’s faithful Old Testament prophets, since Jesus elsewhere affirms that the Scriptures and prophets testify concerning Him (John 5:39, 46). The statement concerns illegitimate claimants to spiritual leadership rather than every person who chronologically preceded Jesus.

“Thieves” (kleptai, from kleptes, G2812) and “robbers” (lestai, from lestes, G3027) repeat the categories introduced in verse 1, tying Jesus’ explanation directly back to the opening shepherd illustration.

“Hear” translates akouo (G191). In this passage, hearing involves recognition and response: “the sheep hear his voice” (v. 3), know the shepherd’s voice (v. 4), and refuse the voice of strangers (v. 5).

Spirit & Life


Jesus draws a sharp distinction between those who want something from the sheep and the One who truly comes for the sheep. Thieves and robbers approach the flock for their own purposes; Jesus will soon reveal that the Good Shepherd does exactly the opposite—He “gives His life for the sheep” (v. 11).

The verse also continues the emphasis on recognizing the Shepherd’s voice. Jesus’ sheep are not defined merely by proximity to religious leadership. They belong to Him, hear Him, and follow Him.

That makes Jesus Himself the measure of true spiritual leadership. Those entrusted with God’s people are never owners of the flock. The sheep belong to Christ. Every legitimate spiritual voice must therefore lead people toward the Shepherd rather than compete with Him for the allegiance that belongs to Him alone.

John 10:9

John 10:9

NKJV

John 10:9 I am the door. If anyone enters by Me, he will be saved, and will go in and out and find pasture.(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:9 Yes, I am the gate. Those who come in through me will be saved. They will come and go freely and will find good pastures. (NLT)

Main Idea


Jesus explains what it means that He is the door: those who enter through Him are saved, brought into His secure care, and provided what they need as His sheep.

Verse Explanation


“I am the door” — Jesus repeats and strengthens the declaration He made in verse 7. He is not merely showing people where the entrance is; He Himself is the entrance. Within the shepherding picture, access to the safety and provision of the flock is inseparably connected to Jesus.

“If anyone enters by Me” — The invitation is personal and open—“if anyone.” Yet the means of entrance is specific—“by Me.” Jesus presents Himself as the necessary point of access. The contrast with verse 1 remains important: thieves attempt to enter “some other way,” but the legitimate entrance is through Jesus Himself.

“he will be saved” — Jesus now explains the spiritual reality represented by entering through the door. To enter through Him is to experience salvation. In John’s Gospel, salvation is centered in believing in Jesus and receiving the eternal life God gives through His Son. The sheep are not merely admitted into a religious community; through Jesus they are rescued and brought into life.

“and will go in and out” — In the shepherding imagery, this describes the freedom and security of sheep living under the shepherd’s care. The expression does not mean moving in and out of salvation. The picture is of sheep able to move under the protection and oversight of the shepherd rather than living under the threat of thieves and robbers.

“and find pasture” — Pasture represents the provision the sheep receive under the shepherd’s care. Jesus does more than bring His sheep through the door; He provides what sustains them. This prepares for verse 10, where Jesus contrasts the destructive purposes of the thief with His own purpose: “I have come that they may have life, and that they may have it more abundantly.”

Study Notes


“Saved” translates sozo (G4982), meaning to save, rescue, or deliver. Here Jesus moves the sheepfold imagery directly into the language of salvation.

The expression “go in and out” is an idiomatic description of secure and unhindered movement. Similar Old Testament language can describe living and moving safely under God-appointed leadership (Numbers 27:17; Deuteronomy 28:6).

“Pasture” translates nome (G3542), referring to grazing or pasture where animals find food. Within the shepherd metaphor, it expresses the provision available to sheep under the shepherd’s care.

The verse has a clear progression: enter by Christ → be saved → go in and out → find pasture. Jesus is both the exclusive means of entrance and the source of the secure, provided-for life described by the imagery.

Spirit & Life


Jesus says, “If anyone enters by Me.” Salvation is not ultimately entrance through a religious system, spiritual achievement, or human leader. The door is a Person. We come into the life and keeping of God through Jesus Himself.

But Jesus does not stop with entrance. His sheep “will go in and out, and find pasture.” The One who saves also becomes the place of security and provision for those who belong to Him. The Christian life is not simply beginning with Jesus and then learning to sustain ourselves.

The sheep enter through Him, live under His care, and find pasture through what He provides. Jesus is not merely the doorway at the beginning of our life with God; He remains central to the entire life of His sheep.

John 10:10

NKJV

John 10:10 The thief does not come except to steal, and to kill, and to destroy. I have come that they may have life, and that they may have it more abundantly.(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:10 The thief’s purpose is to steal and kill and destroy. My purpose is to give them a rich and satisfying life. (NLT)

Main Idea


Jesus draws the sharpest contrast between false shepherds and Himself: the thief takes life from the sheep, but Jesus came to give them life in its overflowing fullness.

Verse Explanation


“The thief does not come except to steal, and to kill, and to destroy” — Jesus brings together the destructive purpose of the thief introduced in verse 1. The three verbs intensify the picture: the thief takes what belongs to another, brings death, and causes destruction. Within the immediate shepherding context, Jesus is contrasting those who approach God’s people for their own purposes with His own purpose toward the sheep. False shepherds take from the flock; Jesus gives Himself for the flock.

“I have come that they may have life” — Against the thief’s destructive purpose stands Jesus’ life-giving mission. He does not come to take life from the sheep but to give life to them. In John’s Gospel, this “life” is more than physical existence. It is the eternal life found in the Son—the life of God received through believing in Jesus and knowing Him.

“and that they may have it more abundantly” — Jesus does not merely rescue His sheep from destruction; He gives them life in abundance. “Abundantly” carries the idea of something exceeding what is merely sufficient. This should not be reduced to material prosperity, comfort, or freedom from suffering. The abundance is attached directly to “life.” Jesus gives the fullness of the life that comes through relationship with Him—a life that begins now and reaches its fullness in eternity.

Study Notes


“Life” translates zoe (G2222), a major term in John’s Gospel for the life God gives through His Son. John has already connected this life with believing in Jesus (John 3:15–16, 36; 5:24; 6:40).

“More abundantly” translates perissos (G4053), carrying the sense of exceeding the usual measure, more than sufficient, or abundant. Grammatically, it describes the fullness of the life Jesus gives; the text itself does not define abundance primarily in terms of possessions or circumstances.

The structure of the verse is deliberately contrasting: steal — kill — destroy versus life — abundance. Verse 11 will deepen the contrast further by revealing the cost at which Jesus gives this life: “The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep.”

Spirit & Life


Jesus tells us plainly what is in His heart toward His sheep: “I have come that they may have life.” He does not approach us to consume, exploit, or diminish us. He comes as the life-giving Shepherd.

And the life He gives is “more abundantly.” This does not mean a life untouched by hardship—the Good Shepherd Himself will speak immediately about laying down His life. It means that what Jesus gives cannot be measured merely by outward circumstances. His sheep possess the life that comes from Him.

There is also a striking movement into verse 11. Jesus came that the sheep might have life, and then He tells us how far He will go to give it: “The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep.” The sheep receive life because the Shepherd is willing to lay down His own. The abundant life of John 10:10 is therefore inseparable from Jesus Himself and the self-giving love of the Good Shepherd.

NLT

John 10:11 “I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd sacrifices his life for the sheep. (NLT)

Main Idea


Jesus identifies Himself as the Good Shepherd whose defining act is willingly giving His own life to save the sheep.

Verse Explanation


“I am the good shepherd.” — Jesus now moves beyond the shepherd imagery of verses 1–10 and directly identifies Himself as the Shepherd. “Good” means more than competent or kind; Jesus is the true and ideal Shepherd, perfectly fulfilling what a shepherd of God’s people was meant to be. Against the thieves and robbers who take from the sheep, Jesus is the Shepherd who gives Himself for them.

“The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep.” — Jesus explains what makes His shepherding uniquely good: He gives His own life for the sheep. The contrast is striking. The thief comes to take life; Jesus gives His life. His death will not be an accidental end to His ministry but a deliberate act of self-giving on behalf of those who belong to Him. The language anticipates the cross and introduces a theme Jesus will develop in verses 15, 17, and 18—His life is willingly laid down.

Study Notes


“Good” translates kalos (G2570), which can describe what is good, noble, excellent, or worthy. In this context it emphasizes the quality and character of Jesus as the Shepherd—the Shepherd who perfectly embodies what shepherding should be.

“Gives His life” uses tithemi (G5087), literally “to lay down, put, or place,” with psyche (G5590), “life.” The expression “lay down His life” becomes a repeated phrase in this section (John 10:11, 15, 17–18), emphasizing the voluntary nature of Jesus’ death.

“For the sheep” expresses the life of the Shepherd being given on behalf of the sheep. The shepherd does not merely risk danger while protecting them; Jesus points forward to actually surrendering His life for them.

Spirit & Life


The heart of the Good Shepherd is revealed by what He is willing to give for His sheep. Jesus does not preserve Himself at their expense. He gives Himself for their sake.

This makes the Shepherd imagery deeply personal. The One who leads, protects, and knows His sheep is also the One who goes to the cross for them. His care is measured not merely by comforting words but by His surrendered life. Before Jesus speaks later in this chapter about His sheep hearing His voice and following Him, He establishes the foundation beneath that relationship: the Shepherd loves His sheep enough to lay down His life for them.

NLT

John 10:12 A hired hand will run when he sees a wolf coming. He will abandon the sheep because they don’t belong to him and he isn’t their shepherd. And so the wolf attacks them and scatters the flock. (NLT)

Main Idea


Jesus contrasts the Good Shepherd with the hired worker, who abandons the sheep when danger comes because the sheep do not truly belong to him.

Verse Explanation


“But a hireling, he who is not the shepherd, one who does not own the sheep” — Jesus introduces a sharp contrast with the Good Shepherd of verse 11. A hireling is a hired worker who may care for the flock as part of his employment, but he does not have the shepherd’s personal relationship to the sheep. Jesus emphasizes this distinction: he is “not the shepherd,” and the sheep are not his own. His commitment is therefore tested when caring for them becomes costly.

“sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep and flees” — The approaching wolf represents real danger to the flock. Instead of standing between the danger and the sheep, the hireling protects himself. His departure exposes what was already true about his relationship with the flock: when his own safety is threatened, preserving himself matters more to him than protecting the sheep.

“and the wolf catches the sheep and scatters them.” — The hireling’s abandonment leaves the flock exposed. The wolf can now attack, seize, and scatter the sheep. Jesus’ emphasis is not only on the danger posed by the wolf but also on the failure of the person who should have remained with the flock. This prepares for Jesus’ explanation in verse 13 and strengthens the contrast with the Good Shepherd, who does not flee from danger but lays down His life for the sheep.

Study Notes


“Hireling” translates misthotos (G3411), referring to someone who works for wages. The term itself does not necessarily describe a dishonest worker; the emphasis here is on the limited commitment of someone whose relationship to the flock is based on employment rather than ownership.

“Catches” translates harpazo (G726), which can carry the sense of seizing, snatching, or taking by force. The same verb appears in John 10:28–29, where Jesus declares that no one will be able to “snatch” His sheep from His hand or the Father’s hand.

The sequence is deliberate: the hireling sees, leaves, and flees; the wolf then catches and scatters. The danger to the flock exposes the difference between temporary service and genuine shepherding.

Spirit & Life


Danger reveals the character of the shepherd. The hireling stays while staying is safe; Jesus stays when staying costs Him His life.

That contrast deepens what Jesus has just said about Himself. The sheep belong to Him, and He does not abandon them when the wolf comes. The cross will demonstrate the difference completely: when danger comes against His sheep, the Good Shepherd does not run to save Himself. He gives Himself to save them.

Jesus then returns in verse 14 to the contrasting character of the Good Shepherd.

Jesus wants His sheep to understand the kind of Shepherd they have. He is not connected to them merely by responsibility or obligation. He genuinely cares for them.

The contrast with the hireling makes verse 11 even stronger. His care is demonstrated by His willingness to remain with His sheep and ultimately lay down His life for them.

Main Idea


Jesus describes Himself as the Good Shepherd whose relationship with His sheep is marked by personal, mutual knowledge.

Verse Explanation


“I am the good shepherd” — Jesus repeats the declaration from verse 11, returning to the central contrast after describing the hireling. The hireling abandons the sheep because he does not truly care for them; Jesus is the Good Shepherd whose character and actions are completely different. His goodness is seen not only in laying down His life but also in the relationship He has with His sheep.

“and I know My sheep” — Jesus’ care for His sheep is personal. They are not an anonymous flock to Him. “Know” speaks of genuine recognition and relationship, not merely awareness that the sheep exist. Jesus knows those who belong to Him, and this knowledge stands behind the shepherding care He gives them.

“and am known by My own.” — The relationship is mutual. Jesus knows His sheep, and His sheep know Him. This develops the earlier picture of the sheep recognizing the shepherd’s voice and following him (John 10:3–4). Their response to Jesus grows out of knowing the Shepherd to whom they belong. Verse 15 will deepen this language of mutual knowledge by connecting it with the relationship between Jesus and the Father.

Study Notes


“Know” translates ginosko (G1097), a common New Testament verb meaning to know, recognize, understand, or become acquainted with. Here its significance is determined by the shepherd-and-sheep relationship Jesus is describing: the Shepherd knows His sheep, and they know Him.

“My sheep” and “My own” reinforce the language of belonging that has shaped this section. The hireling of verse 12 does not own the sheep; Jesus explicitly identifies them as His.

Verses 14–15 form one connected statement. The mutual knowledge between Jesus and His sheep in verse 14 is immediately compared with the knowledge shared between the Father and the Son in verse 15, before Jesus again declares that He lays down His life for the sheep.

Spirit & Life


The Good Shepherd does more than protect a flock; He knows His sheep. Jesus’ care is rooted in a real relationship with those who belong to Him.

This also gives greater depth to hearing His voice and following Him. His sheep are not following a stranger whose voice they have merely learned to recognize. They are responding to the Shepherd who knows them and whom they are coming to know. Life with the Good Shepherd is therefore deeply relational: “I know My sheep, and am known by My own.”

Verse Explanation


“As the Father knows Me, even so I know the Father” — Jesus deepens the statement of verse 14, where He said, “I know My sheep, and am known by My own.” He now points to His relationship with the Father as the comparison. The Father knows the Son, and the Son knows the Father. Jesus’ shepherding of His people therefore flows from the profound relationship He has with His Father. The sheep are being brought into a relationship with the Shepherd whose life is rooted in intimate knowledge of the Father.

“and I lay down My life for the sheep.” — Jesus returns to the defining action of the Good Shepherd first stated in verse 11. His knowledge of the sheep is not distant or passive; it leads to self-giving love. “Lay down” emphasizes that Jesus gives His life willingly. He does not merely face the possibility of death while protecting the sheep—He deliberately gives His life on their behalf. Verses 17–18 will make this voluntary character even more explicit.

Study Notes


“As” translates kathos (G2531), introducing the comparison that connects verses 14 and 15. The flow is: Jesus knows His sheep and His sheep know Him, as the Father knows Jesus and Jesus knows the Father. The verse should therefore be read as the continuation of the thought begun in verse 14.

“Lay down” translates tithemi (G5087), the same verb used in verse 11. Its repetition becomes important throughout this section: Jesus “lays down” His life for the sheep (vv. 11, 15), lays it down that He may take it again (v. 17), and declares that He lays it down of Himself (v. 18).

“For the sheep” again identifies those on whose behalf Jesus gives His life. The repeated wording of verses 11 and 15 keeps His coming death at the center of the Good Shepherd discourse.

Spirit & Life


Jesus connects two remarkable realities in this verse: His relationship with the Father and His sacrifice for the sheep. The Shepherd who knows us is the Son who perfectly knows the Father, and He willingly lays down His life for us.

This means the cross is not separate from Jesus’ shepherding care; it is at the very heart of it. The Good Shepherd knows His sheep, remains committed to them, and gives Himself for them. His words “I lay down My life for the sheep” reveal how far His shepherding love will go.

Jesus reveals that His saving work will reach sheep beyond Israel, bringing them together under one Shepherd as one flock.

Verse Explanation


“And other sheep I have which are not of this fold” — Jesus expands the scope of His shepherding mission. The immediate setting of His ministry is among Israel, pictured here as the present “fold,” but His sheep will not come from Israel alone. The “other sheep” points to Gentiles who will also belong to Him. Jesus speaks of them as already His because they are included within the saving purpose He is carrying out.

“them also I must bring” — Their inclusion is not an afterthought. “Must” expresses the necessity of Jesus’ mission: He will bring these other sheep into the people who belong to Him. The Shepherd who lays down His life for the sheep will gather people beyond the boundaries of Israel.

“and they will hear My voice” — The other sheep will respond to Jesus in the same way His sheep have been described earlier in the chapter: they hear the Shepherd’s voice. Their different background does not create a different relationship with Him. They recognize and respond to the same Shepherd.

“and there will be one flock and one shepherd.” — Jesus’ purpose is not to maintain separate peoples under separate shepherds. Those He gathers become “one flock” under Him. Their unity is centered in the Shepherd Himself. Jesus does not say there will be one fold but one flock—one people belonging to and following one Shepherd.

Study Notes


“Fold” translates aule (G833), which can refer to an enclosed courtyard or sheepfold. In the shepherd imagery, it describes the enclosure from which Jesus distinguishes the “other sheep.”

“Must” translates dei (G1163), a term John regularly uses for something that is necessary or must take place. Here it emphasizes the necessity of Jesus bringing the other sheep as part of His mission.

There is an important distinction between “fold” and “flock.” “Fold” is aule (G833), while “flock” is poimne (G4167). Jesus does not say that all His sheep will be brought into one enclosure; He says they will become one flock under one Shepherd.

Spirit & Life


Jesus sees sheep beyond the boundaries of the fold standing before Him. His heart and His saving work reach outward to gather them.

Yet His goal is not merely to gather more sheep. It is “one flock and one shepherd.” What joins His sheep together is not where they came from but whom they now hear and follow. Jesus Himself is the center of their unity. Different sheep are gathered by the same voice, brought by the same Shepherd, and made part of the same flock under Him.

“Therefore My Father loves Me” — Jesus connects the Father’s love with what He is about to describe: His willing surrender of His life. This does not mean the Father begins to love Jesus because He dies. The Father’s love for the Son is already established throughout John’s Gospel. Here Jesus emphasizes the Father’s delight in the Son as He willingly carries out the mission before Him.

“because I lay down My life” — Jesus again uses the language that has defined the Good Shepherd in this passage. His death will not simply happen to Him. He will “lay down” His life. “that I may take it again.” — Jesus’ laying down of His life is not the end of the story. He speaks of both His death and His resurrection. The same One who willingly lays down His life will “take it again.” The cross and resurrection therefore belong together within Jesus’ mission. The Good Shepherd will die for His sheep, but death will not permanently hold the Shepherd.

Study Notes


“Therefore” translates dia touto, literally carrying the sense “because of this” or “for this reason.” It connects the Father’s love specifically with the statement that follows concerning Jesus laying down His life and taking it again.

“Lay down” is tithemi (G5087), continuing the repeated language of John 10:11 and 10:15. Its repetition emphasizes the deliberate self-giving of the Good Shepherd.

“Take it again” uses lambano (G2983), meaning to take or receive. The same expression appears again in verse 18, where Jesus explains that He has authority both to lay down His life and to take it again.

Spirit & Life


Jesus speaks about His coming death with complete purpose. He is not a Shepherd who will be unexpectedly overcome by the danger facing Him. He knows He will lay down His life, and He knows He will take it again.

This gives the sheep a Shepherd whose care reaches through death itself. The cross reveals His willing self-giving, while His taking up His life again means the sheep will not be left without their Shepherd. The One who lays down His life for them is also the living Shepherd who will rise again.

Jesus makes clear that His death will be a willing act of self-surrender carried out under His own authority and in obedience to His Father.

Verse Explanation


“No one takes it from Me” — Jesus removes any possibility of viewing His coming death merely as something His enemies will do to Him. Human beings will certainly arrest, condemn, and crucify Him, but they will not ultimately take His life against His will. Jesus goes to the cross knowingly and willingly. What will appear outwardly to be His enemies overpowering Him is, at a deeper level, the Good Shepherd voluntarily giving His life for the sheep.

“but I lay it down of Myself.” — Jesus emphasizes His willing participation in His death. He is not a helpless victim caught in circumstances beyond His control. The Good Shepherd chooses to give Himself. This completes the thought repeated throughout the passage: He “gives His life for the sheep” (v. 11), “lays down” His life for them (v. 15), and now declares that He does so “of Myself.”

“I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again.” — Jesus possesses authority over both the laying down and taking up of His life. Verse 17 introduced the resurrection with the words “that I may take it again”; here Jesus explicitly declares His authority to do so. His death and resurrection are therefore presented together as parts of the mission He knowingly carries out.

“This command I have received from My Father.” — Jesus’ authority does not mean independence from the Father. His voluntary action and His obedience to the Father operate together. The Son willingly lays down His life while carrying out what the Father has given Him to accomplish. The Good Shepherd’s sacrifice is therefore both freely given and perfectly aligned with the Father’s will.

Study Notes

The Feast of Dedication, later known as Hanukkah, commemorated the rededication of the Jerusalem temple after it had been desecrated under Antiochus IV Epiphanes in the second century BC. Study Notes

Unlike Passover, Pentecost, and Tabernacles, the Feast of Dedication was not one of the pilgrimage feasts established in the Law of Moses. It arose from events during the Maccabean period and was celebrated for eight days.

The transition from verse 21 to verse 22 likely marks a change in time. The preceding events were associated with the aftermath of the healing in John 9, while verse 22 introduces the setting for the confrontation recorded in John 10:22–39.

Spirit & Life


John places Jesus in Jerusalem during a feast centered on the dedication of the temple to God. Yet the verses that follow will turn attention from the temple and its history to Jesus Himself and the question everyone must answer: Who is He?

The setting therefore prepares us to listen closely. As the chapter continues, Jesus will make clear that His sheep hear His voice, He gives them eternal life, and He and the Father are one.

“Snatch” translates harpazō (G726), meaning to seize, take forcibly, or carry away. The same verb appeared in John 10:12, where the wolf “catches” the sheep. The contrast is significant: the hired servant cannot protect the flock from the wolf, but no one can snatch Jesus’ sheep from His hand.

Spirit & Life


The security in this verse rests first in what Jesus does: “I give them eternal life.” The sheep do not create this life, and they do not hold themselves in the Shepherd’s hand. Jesus gives, Jesus holds, and Jesus keeps.

This promise becomes even stronger when we remember how Jesus has identified Himself: “I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep” (John 10:11). The hand holding the sheep belongs to the Shepherd who was willing to lay down His own life for them.

Jesus does not say His sheep will never face danger. He says they will “never perish” and no one can “snatch them out of My hand.” The emphasis is not on the strength of the sheep but on the strength of the Shepherd. Those who hear His voice, are known by Him, and follow Him are held securely in the hand of the One who gives them eternal life.

“Again” (palin, G3825) is significant within John’s narrative. In John 8:59, Jesus’ opponents “took up stones to throw at Him” following His declaration, “before Abraham was, I AM.” John 10:31 records another attempted stoning following another major statement concerning Jesus’ identity.

Verse 33 provides the stated legal and theological reason for their action: they accuse Jesus of blasphemy because they believe that He, “being a Man,” has made Himself God. Verse 31 should therefore be read together with verses 30 and 33.

Spirit & Life


The stones reveal how seriously Jesus’ words were heard. “I and My Father are one” was not received as vague spiritual language. His opponents understood that Jesus was saying something extraordinary about who He is.

Their response also exposes the central decision running through John’s Gospel. The same Jesus whose sheep hear His voice is rejected by others who hear the very same words. One group hears the Shepherd; another reaches for stones.

John 10:31 therefore presses us back toward Jesus Himself. His claims do not allow Him to be reduced merely to a wise teacher or admirable religious leader. The question is whether His words about Himself are true. If they are, then the One speaking is the Good Shepherd who gives eternal life, holds His sheep securely, and shares a unique divine unity with the Father.

“Make Yourself God” translates poieis seauton theon. The accusation should be read in the immediate context of John 10:28–30. Jesus has claimed authority to give eternal life, described His sheep as secure in both His hand and His Father’s hand, and declared His unity with the Father.

The conflict has now reached the central issue: Who is Jesus?

His opponents cannot dismiss His works as evil. Instead, they stumble over the identity those works and His words reveal. They see a man standing before them and conclude that His humanity makes His claims impossible.

But John’s Gospel began by preparing us for precisely this mystery: “the Word was God,” and then “the Word became flesh” (John 1:1, 14). The One standing before them truly is a man, but He is not merely a man.

This makes their accusation tragically ironic. They say, in effect, You are a man making Yourself God. John reveals the opposite movement: the One who is divine has come among us as man.

The incarnation confronts us with the same question faced by those standing before Jesus. We cannot adequately explain Him merely as a teacher, miracle worker, or religious leader. His words demand a decision about His identity. John is steadily revealing the answer: in Jesus Christ, God has truly come near to us.

“Your law” does not mean Jesus is distancing Himself from Scripture or denying its authority. Since He quotes from Psalms, the expression demonstrates that nomos (“law”) can refer more broadly to the authoritative Scriptures.

Verses 34–36 form one argument and should be interpreted together. Verse 34 introduces Psalm 82; verse 35 explains its relevance; verse 36 applies the argument directly to Jesus and the accusation of blasphemy.

Jesus does something important when His identity is challenged: He takes His opponents back to Scripture.

Yet Jesus is doing more than winning a technical argument. The discussion is moving toward the enormous contrast of verse 36. Scripture could use elevated language for those who received God’s word, but Jesus is the One whom the Father sanctified and sent into the world.

His opponents are preparing to stone Him for speaking too highly of Himself when, in reality, they have not yet understood how high His identity truly is.

John keeps pressing us beyond superficial conclusions about Jesus. His claims must be heard carefully, His works must be considered, and Scripture must be allowed to testify concerning Him. The question remains at the center of this passage: Will we receive Jesus according to who the Father reveals Him to be?

This keeps the focus of the passage where John intends it—on the identity of Jesus. We cannot decide who He is merely by fitting Him into categories we already understand. We must receive Him according to the revelation the Father has given.

Jesus is not a man trying to make Himself the Son of God; He is the Son whom the Father sent into the world.

Verses 37–38 belong together. Verse 37 presents the negative test: if Jesus does not perform the Father’s works, they should not believe Him. Verse 38 presents the opposite conclusion: if He does perform them, they should believe the works even if they struggle to believe His words.

Spirit & Life


Jesus does not separate His words from His life. What He says about His relationship with the Father is confirmed by what the Father is visibly doing through Him.

His opponents have heard His claims, but they have also watched Him heal, restore, deliver, and give life. Jesus tells them to put those two things together. His works are pointing toward the truth they are resisting.

This reveals something important about Jesus: He is completely confident that a careful examination of His life will confirm His words. There is no contradiction between what He claims and what He does.

John wants us to follow the evidence where it leads. The signs are not meant merely to make us marvel at miracles; they are meant to reveal the Son. The works of Jesus bear witness that the Father is at work through Him.

The works point beyond themselves to Jesus, and through Jesus they reveal the Father.

John 10:38

NKJV

John 10:38 but if I do, though you do not believe Me, believe the works, that you may know and believe that the Father is in Me, and I in Him."(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:38 But if I do his work, believe in the evidence of the miraculous works I have done, even if you don’t believe me. Then you will know and understand that the Father is in me, and I am in the Father.” (NLT)

Main Idea


Jesus urges those who struggle to accept His words to consider His works, because those works reveal the profound mutual relationship between the Father and the Son.

Verse Explanation


“but if I do” — Jesus now gives the other side of the test introduced in verse 37. If He truly is doing the works of His Father—and His opponents have witnessed those works—then they must deal honestly with what those works reveal. They cannot dismiss the evidence simply because they reject His claims.

“though you do not believe Me, believe the works” — Jesus distinguishes between their immediate response to His words and the evidence provided by His works. If they are unwilling to accept His testimony about Himself, they should at least begin with what they have actually seen. His works provide an independent witness pointing toward His identity. Jesus is not asking them merely to admire the miracles; He wants the works to lead them to the truth about His relationship with the Father.

“that you may know and believe” — Jesus desires more than momentary acknowledgment. The works are intended to bring His hearers into a settled recognition of who He is. Their resistance does not cause Jesus to stop giving them reasons to believe. Even while they have stones in their hands, He continues calling them toward faith.

“that the Father is in Me, and I in Him” — This is the truth toward which the works point. Jesus describes a mutual indwelling between Himself and the Father: the Father is in the Son, and the Son is in the Father. This explains the unity Jesus declared in verse 30—“I and My Father are one.” The Father and Son are distinct, yet their relationship involves a unity far deeper than merely sharing the same goals or cooperating in the same work.

Study Notes


“Believe the works” continues an important theme in John. Jesus’ works function as testimony concerning His identity and divine mission (John 5:36; 10:25, 32, 37–38). The miracles are signs because they point beyond the event itself to who Jesus is.

There is a textual variation in “that you may know and believe.” Many early manuscripts read a form meaning “that you may know and continue to know” rather than “know and believe.” The central point remains that Jesus wants His works to produce a settled understanding of His relationship with the Father.

“The Father is in Me, and I in Him” expresses reciprocal indwelling. Similar language becomes especially prominent in Jesus’ teaching in John 14–17 (John 14:10–11; 17:21–23).

The statement should be read with John 10:30, “I and My Father are one.” Verses 31–38 do not retreat from that claim. Jesus answers the charge of blasphemy and then returns to an exceptionally strong description of His unity with the Father.

Spirit & Life


Jesus’ opponents are not lacking evidence. They have heard His words and witnessed His works. Yet even after they prepare to stone Him, Jesus continues giving them a pathway toward belief: “believe the works.”

The works are meant to lead somewhere—to Jesus, and through Jesus to the Father.

At the heart of the verse is Jesus’ remarkable declaration: “the Father is in Me, and I in Him.” When people encounter Jesus, they are not encountering someone operating independently from God. The Father’s works are being revealed through the Son because of the unique unity between them.

This is why knowing Jesus brings us into the revelation of the Father. Jesus does not merely tell us information about God from a distance. His words, works, character, and life reveal the Father because the Father is in Him and He is in the Father.

The works are evidence, but they are meant to lead beyond the evidence itself: to recognize Jesus for who He truly is and, through Him, to know the Father.

John 10:39

NKJV

John 10:39 Therefore they sought again to seize Him, but He escaped out of their hand.(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:39 Once again they tried to arrest him, but he got away and left them. (NLT)

Main Idea


Jesus’ opponents again attempt to seize Him, but He escapes their grasp because their hostility cannot determine the timing or outcome of His mission.

Verse Explanation


“Therefore they sought again to seize Him” — Jesus’ explanation does not soften their opposition. After He declares that the Father is in Him and He is in the Father, they again attempt to take Him into custody. “Again” connects this moment with their earlier attempts to arrest Jesus. Rather than responding to the evidence of His works, they remain determined to remove Him.

“but He escaped out of their hand.” — Their attempt fails. John gives no details about how Jesus escapes, keeping the emphasis simply on the fact that they cannot take Him. This fits a repeated pattern in John: Jesus’ enemies are genuinely hostile and actively seek His death, yet they cannot take control of His life before the appointed time. Jesus will eventually be arrested and crucified, but it will occur within the fulfillment of His mission rather than because His opponents finally overpower Him.

Study Notes


“Seize” translates piazo (G4084), meaning to take, catch, or arrest. John uses the word repeatedly for attempts to apprehend Jesus (John 7:30, 32, 44; 8:20; 10:39).

“Escaped” translates exerchomai (G1831), the common verb meaning to go out or depart. The additional phrase “out of their hand” emphasizes that Jesus gets beyond their attempt to take Him.

The verse continues a recurring Johannine pattern. Earlier attempts to seize Jesus failed because His “hour had not yet come” (John 7:30; 8:20). John 10:39 does not repeat that explanation explicitly, but it occurs within the same developing narrative in which Jesus’ death takes place according to the appointed course of His mission.

The expression “out of their hand” also creates an interesting contextual contrast with John 10:28–29. Jesus had just declared that no one can snatch His sheep from His hand or the Father’s hand; now His opponents themselves are unable to hold Jesus in their hand.

Spirit & Life


The opposition against Jesus is real. His enemies are not merely discussing Him; they are actively trying to seize Him. Yet even their determined hostility cannot take control of His mission.

Jesus had already explained that His life would not ultimately be taken from Him against His will. He would lay it down according to the authority given Him by the Father (John 10:17–18). Verse 39 shows that reality continuing to unfold. His opponents reach for Him, but they cannot yet hold Him.

This keeps the focus on Jesus’ authority even in the face of rejection. He is not moving helplessly toward the cross as circumstances close around Him. He is walking toward the hour appointed for Him, and until that hour arrives, His enemies cannot force the outcome.

The same Jesus who declared that His sheep are secure in His hand is Himself moving securely within the Father’s purpose.

John 10:40

NKJV

John 10:40 And He went away again beyond the Jordan to the place where John was baptizing at first, and there He stayed.(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:40 He went beyond the Jordan River near the place where John was first baptizing and stayed there awhile. (NLT)

Main Idea


After escaping His opponents in Jerusalem, Jesus withdraws across the Jordan to the place where John the Baptist had first ministered and remains there for a time.

Verse Explanation


“And He went away again beyond the Jordan” — After another attempt to seize Him in verse 39, Jesus leaves the immediate hostility of Jerusalem and crosses to the eastern side of the Jordan River. John marks a significant geographical change. Jesus moves away from the religious center where opposition has intensified and returns to an area associated with the beginning of His public ministry.

“to the place where John was baptizing at first” — The location deliberately recalls John the Baptist. Earlier in the Gospel, John had ministered beyond the Jordan and directed people’s attention toward Jesus. He identified Jesus as the Lamb of God, testified that the Spirit remained upon Him, and declared Him to be the Son of God. Jesus now returns to a place where testimony concerning His identity had been given from the beginning.

“and there He stayed.” — Jesus remains in this region rather than immediately returning to Jerusalem. The contrast with the preceding scene is striking. In Jerusalem, leaders attempted to seize Him; beyond the Jordan, the following verses will describe people coming to Him and believing. John moves the narrative from determined rejection to growing faith.

Study Notes


“Beyond the Jordan” translates peran tou Iordanou, referring generally to the region on the eastern side of the Jordan River.

The reference to the place where John was baptizing “at first” points back to the opening chapters of the Gospel. John 1:28 locates John’s baptizing activity at Bethabara beyond the Jordan in the NKJV; many manuscripts and modern translations read Bethany beyond the Jordan.

The return to John the Baptist’s former ministry location creates a literary connection with John 1. John the Baptist himself has left the narrative, but his testimony about Jesus will be remembered and evaluated in verses 41–42.

The verb translated “stayed” is meno (G3306), meaning to remain, stay, or abide. It is a common and important word in John, although here its immediate sense is simply that Jesus remained in that location.

Spirit & Life


Jesus returns to a place where faithful testimony about Him had already been planted. John the Baptist is no longer there, but his words remain in the memories of the people.

That becomes important in the next verse. The people will remember what John said about Jesus, compare his testimony with what they now see, and conclude that John’s words about Him were true.

John’s ministry therefore continues bearing fruit even after he is gone. He had faithfully pointed away from himself toward Christ, and now people come to Jesus because that testimony prepared them to recognize Him.

The movement of the passage is also striking. Jesus leaves a place where people tried to seize Him and enters a place where people will believe in Him. Rejection in one place does not stop the Father’s work. Jesus continues His mission, and testimony faithfully given earlier is still preparing people to receive Him.

John 10:41

NKJV

John 10:41 Then many came to Him and said, "John performed no sign, but all the things that John spoke about this Man were true."(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:41 And many followed him. “John didn’t perform miraculous signs,” they remarked to one another, “but everything he said about this man has come true.” (NLT)

Main Idea


Many people come to Jesus and recognize that although John the Baptist performed no miraculous signs, everything he had testified about Jesus proved to be true.

Verse Explanation


“Then many came to Him and said” — Jesus’ departure beyond the Jordan does not leave Him isolated. Many people come to Him there. This creates a sharp contrast with the hostility He has just encountered in Jerusalem. While some are trying to seize Him, others are coming near and considering the testimony concerning Him.

“John performed no sign” — The people remember John the Baptist’s ministry and recognize that he did not authenticate his message by performing miraculous signs. John’s God-given assignment was different. He was sent as a witness who prepared the way for Jesus and directed people toward Him.

“but all the things that John spoke about this Man were true.” — Time has confirmed John’s testimony. The people can now compare what John had said about Jesus with what they have heard and seen from Jesus Himself. John had identified Jesus as the Lamb of God, testified to His unique greatness, and pointed to Him as the Son of God. The evidence before them confirms that John had faithfully pointed them to the right Person.

Study Notes


“Sign” translates semeion (G4592), an important term throughout John for miraculous works that reveal something about Jesus’ identity. John the Baptist performed no such sign, distinguishing his ministry from the signs performed by Jesus.

The statement that John “performed no sign” is the Gospel’s only explicit evaluation of John’s ministry in these terms. His prophetic credibility did not depend upon miracles but upon the truthfulness of his testimony concerning Jesus.

“Were true” uses alethes (G227), meaning true, truthful, or reliable. The people evaluate John’s earlier testimony in light of what they now know about Jesus and conclude that his witness was accurate.

The verse reaches back especially to John 1:19–34, where John the Baptist repeatedly directs attention away from himself and toward Jesus. John 10:41 shows that this testimony continued bearing fruit after John’s ministry had ended.

Spirit & Life


John the Baptist performed no signs, yet his ministry accomplished exactly what God had called him to do: he faithfully pointed people to Jesus.

Now, sometime later, people stand in the place where John once ministered and realize that what he told them about Jesus was true. John is no longer there, but his testimony is still working. His words prepared people to recognize Christ when they encountered Him.

There is an important picture of faithful ministry here. Our calling is not to make people impressed with us but to help them see Jesus clearly. John had said, “He must increase, but I must decrease” (John 3:30), and this verse shows that happening. John has disappeared from the scene, while people are coming to Christ because John faithfully told them who He was.

The lasting fruit of John’s ministry was not that people remembered how remarkable John was. They remembered that what John said about Jesus was true.

John 10:42

NKJV

John 10:42 And many believed in Him there.(NKJV)

NLT

John 10:42 And many who were there believed in Jesus. (NLT)

Main Idea


John 10 closes with the fruit of faithful testimony and convincing evidence: many people in the region beyond the Jordan place their faith in Jesus.

Verse Explanation


“And many believed in Him there.” — The chapter ends with a simple but significant statement of faith. The people have remembered John the Baptist’s testimony, compared what he said with what they now know of Jesus, and concluded that his testimony was true. Their response goes beyond recognizing that John was correct—they believe in Jesus Himself.

“there” — John draws attention to the location. This is the place beyond the Jordan where John the Baptist had previously ministered and testified about Jesus. John had performed no miraculous signs there, but he had faithfully prepared people to recognize Christ. Now the seed of that earlier testimony produces fruit as many believe.

The location also creates a striking contrast with Jerusalem. There Jesus’ opponents had taken up stones and attempted to seize Him. Here, people respond to Him with faith. The same Jesus who is rejected by some is received by others.

Study Notes


“Believed in Him” uses pisteuō (G4100) with eis, literally expressing belief “into” Him. This construction occurs frequently throughout John to describe faith directed personally toward Jesus, not merely agreement with facts about Him.

The statement completes the movement of verses 40–42. Jesus returns to the region of John’s earlier ministry; people remember John’s testimony; they recognize that everything John said about Jesus was true; and many consequently believe in Jesus.

John 10 therefore closes by returning to the witness of John the Baptist introduced near the beginning of the Gospel. His testimony continued accomplishing its purpose after his public ministry had ended.

Spirit & Life


John the Baptist had faithfully pointed people toward Jesus, and now the fruit appears: many believe in Christ.

John is absent from the scene. He performs no sign. He receives no attention here. Yet what he said about Jesus remains, and people discover that his testimony was true. His ministry succeeded because it led people beyond himself to Christ.

There is also a powerful contrast at the end of the chapter. Some people had seen Jesus’ works and still reached for stones; others remembered faithful testimony, considered what they saw in Jesus, and believed.

John brings the chapter to its proper destination. Jesus has been revealing Himself as the Good Shepherd, the giver of eternal life, the One who is one with the Father, and the Son whom the Father sent into the world. The question throughout the chapter has been how people will respond to Him.

Here the answer is beautifully simple: many believed in Him.
