Book Overview

At a Glance

Jude
Author

The letter comes from “Jude, a bondservant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James” (v. 1). The James he names is best identified as James the Lord’s brother, the recognized leader of the Jerusalem church (Acts 15:13–21; Galatians 1:19; 2:9). The Gospels name a Judas among Jesus’ brothers alongside James (Matthew 13:55; Mark 6:3). Jude is therefore best identified as Jesus’ half-brother: Mary was the mother of both, while Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit (Matthew 1:18–25). This identification comes from comparing Scripture with Scripture; Jude 1:1 does not itself call him the Lord’s brother. He is distinct from Judas Iscariot and from the apostle “Judas the son of James” (Luke 6:16).

Audience

Jude writes “to those who are called, sanctified by God the Father, and preserved in Jesus Christ” (v. 1), and he addresses them as “beloved” (vv. 3, 17, 20). They were a Christian community, or possibly a group of churches, who had heard the apostles’ teaching (vv. 17–18). His use of Israel’s history and of Jewish traditions about Enoch and Michael suggests readers familiar with that material. The letter does not say where they lived or whether they were mainly Jewish or Gentile believers.

Date

Jude gives no date or place of writing. Because the author is the brother of Jesus and James, the letter belongs to the first century, and a date in its second half is a reasonable approximate estimate. The letter’s message does not depend on a more precise date.

Historical Setting

Early believers shared common meals, which Jude calls “your love feasts” (v. 12; compare 1 Corinthians 11:20–22). These meals expressed the church’s fellowship, and the intruders took part in them. That closeness is what made them dangerous: their influence worked from inside the fellowship, at the table where believers met together.

Purpose

Jude had been eager to write about “our common salvation” (v. 3). Instead he found it necessary to urge his readers “to contend earnestly for the faith which was once for all delivered to the saints.” The reason was that “certain men have crept in unnoticed” (v. 4). Jude charges that they turn the grace of God into lewdness and deny the Lord Jesus Christ. They shared in the church’s love feasts (v. 12) and caused divisions (v. 19). The danger came from within the fellowship, so Jude set aside the letter he had planned and wrote this urgent appeal.

Central Message

Jude urges God’s called and kept people to contend earnestly for the faith once for all delivered to the saints, because ungodly intruders are turning the grace of God into lewdness and denying the Lord Jesus Christ. He exposes their certain judgment and calls believers to remain in God’s love through faith, prayer in the Holy Spirit, and merciful rescue, resting in the God who is able to keep them from stumbling and present them faultless with exceeding joy.

Key Themes

  • The entrusted faith. The faith “once for all delivered to the saints” is to be contended for and built upon (vv. 3, 20).

  • Grace and lordship. Turning grace into lewdness is a denial of Jesus as Lord (v. 4).

  • Keeping. Believers are preserved in Jesus Christ, keep themselves in God’s love, and are kept from stumbling by God, while rebellious angels and the intruders are reserved for judgment (vv. 1, 6, 13, 21, 24).

  • The certainty of judgment. God’s past judgments are examples of what awaits the ungodly (vv. 5–7, 14–15).

  • Self-willed religion. The intruders follow their own desires, serve themselves, seek gain, and cause division while remaining in the fellowship (vv. 8–12, 16, 19).

  • Remembering. Jude calls his readers to remember what God has done and what the apostles said (vv. 5, 17).

  • Mercy that rescues. Those who look for Christ’s mercy show mercy to others and pull them from the fire (vv. 2, 21–23).

  • Joyful worship. The letter ends with praise to the God who presents His people faultless “with exceeding joy” (vv. 24–25).

Book Flow

Jude 1:1–4: Called, Kept, and Urged to Contend

Jude introduces himself as “a bondservant of Jesus Christ” and greets those who are called, sanctified by God the Father, and “preserved in Jesus Christ,” praying that mercy, peace, and love be multiplied to them. He explains that he set aside a letter about their common salvation to urge them “to contend earnestly for the faith.” Verse 4 gives the reason: ungodly men have “crept in unnoticed,” turning grace into lewdness and denying Christ. These verses state the letter’s purpose, and everything that follows develops it.

Jude 1:5–16: The Certain Judgment of the Ungodly Intruders

Jude reminds his readers of three judgments they already know: the destruction of those who did not believe after the exodus, the angels who did not keep their proper domain, and Sodom and Gomorrah, “set forth as an example.” He then applies these examples with a repeated “these”: the intruders defile the flesh, reject authority, and speak evil of what they do not understand, unlike Michael, who would not bring a reviling accusation against the devil but said, “The Lord rebuke you!” (v. 9). They have gone in the way of Cain, the error of Balaam, and the rebellion of Korah, and Jude exposes their emptiness with images from nature: clouds without water, fruitless trees, raging waves, and wandering stars. Enoch’s prophecy that “the Lord comes with ten thousands of His saints” to execute judgment confirms their end (vv. 14–15).

Jude 1:17–23: But You, Beloved: Remain in God’s Love and Rescue Others

“But you, beloved” (v. 17) turns the letter from the intruders to the believers. Jude reminds them that the apostles foretold mockers in the last time, people “who cause divisions, not having the Spirit” (v. 19). He then gives the positive shape of contending: build yourselves up on your most holy faith, pray in the Holy Spirit, “keep yourselves in the love of God,” and look for the mercy of the Lord Jesus Christ unto eternal life (vv. 20–21). This faithfulness reaches outward in compassion for some and rescue for others, “pulling them out of the fire,” while “hating even the garment defiled by the flesh” (vv. 22–23).

Jude 1:24–25: Glory to the God Who Is Able to Keep

The letter ends in worship. God “is able to keep you from stumbling, and to present you faultless before the presence of His glory with exceeding joy” (v. 24). To God our Savior belong “glory and majesty, dominion and power, both now and forever” (v. 25). This closing praise brings the letter to its conclusion: the people called and preserved in verse 1 are entrusted to the God who keeps them to the end.

Go Deeper

Background

Author

He is distinct from Judas Iscariot and from the apostle “Judas the son of James” (Luke 6:16).

Jude does not appeal to his family tie with Jesus. He calls himself Christ’s bondservant. Jesus’ brothers once did not believe in Him (John 7:5), yet after the resurrection they were gathered with the disciples in prayer (Acts 1:14). Scripture does not narrate Jude’s conversion, but his opening words show a man who serves Jesus as Lord.

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Jude is a short letter of warning and encouragement. After a greeting and a statement of purpose, it recalls God’s past judgments and applies them to ungodly people who have entered the church, describing them through Old Testament figures, images from nature, and Enoch’s prophecy. It then turns to the believers with instruction for their own faithfulness and their care for others in danger, and it closes in praise of God.

The letter joins sharp warning with deep pastoral care. The same Jude who describes the intruders as “wandering stars for whom is reserved the blackness of darkness forever” (v. 13) calls his readers “beloved” and closes with one of Scripture’s great expressions of praise to God.

Why This Book Matters

Jude is one of the shortest books in the New Testament, yet it addresses a danger every generation of the church faces: people who belong to the fellowship outwardly while corrupting it from within. It teaches that the gospel is not open to revision. The faith has been “once for all delivered to the saints” (v. 3), and God’s people are responsible to know it, live it, and guard it.

Jude’s distinctive contribution is to show that grace can be misused as well as denied. The intruders did not reject grace outright; they turned it into license (v. 4). Jude teaches believers to recognize this danger by its fruit: self-will, irreverence, greed, and division within a fellowship that still looks outwardly sound (vv. 8–16, 19).

Jude also holds together what believers are tempted to keep apart: firm opposition to error and mercy toward people caught in it, sober warning and joyful confidence in God’s keeping. In this letter, faithfulness to the truth and compassion for people belong to the same calling.

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

  • Bondservant. A servant who wholly belongs to a master. Jude uses the word to express his surrendered allegiance to Jesus Christ (v. 1).

  • Common salvation. The salvation all believers share in Christ (v. 3). “Common” means shared, not ordinary or inferior; there is no higher saving truth reserved for a spiritual elite.

  • The faith once for all delivered. The truth about Christ taught by the apostles and entrusted to the whole church (vv. 3, 17). “Once for all” means it is complete and is not to be replaced. Later understanding grows from this faith rather than adding a new gospel to it (Galatians 1:8–9).

  • Lewdness. Open, unrestrained sensuality (v. 4). The intruders treated grace as permission for it.

  • The angels who did not keep their proper domain. These angels left their own abode and are kept “in everlasting chains under darkness” for judgment (v. 6). Some connect them with the “sons of God” in Genesis 6:1–4; others think of a broader angelic rebellion. The two views need not exclude each other, and Jude does not explain the connection. His point is clear: rebellion against God’s appointed order brings judgment.

  • Sodom and Gomorrah. Genesis 19:1–11 records the men of Sodom attempting to violate Lot’s visitors, who were angels; their sin included same-sex assault and violence. Jude describes these cities as “having given themselves over to sexual immorality and gone after strange flesh” (v. 7). How the phrase “in a similar manner to these” relates them to the angels of verse 6 is debated.

  • Dignitaries. Literally “glories” or glorious ones (v. 8). The word may refer to angelic beings or to authority more broadly. Either way, the intruders speak with contempt about what they do not understand.

  • Michael the archangel. A chief angel also named in Daniel 10:13; 12:1 and Revelation 12:7. When he disputed with the devil about the body of Moses, he “dared not bring against him a reviling accusation,” but appealed to the Lord: “The Lord rebuke you!” (v. 9). His restraint contrasts with the intruders, who “speak evil of whatever they do not know” (v. 10).

  • Cain, Balaam, and Korah. Cain murdered his brother after God accepted Abel’s offering and not his (Genesis 4:1–8; Hebrews 11:4; 1 John 3:12). Balaam was a prophet who sought payment and later advised the course of action that led Israel into sin (Numbers 22–24; 31:16; Revelation 2:14). Korah, a Levite, rebelled against Moses and Aaron and sought a priesthood God had not given him (Numbers 16:8–11). Together they picture service to God corrupted by envy, greed, and self-seeking ambition.

  • Love feasts. Shared meals of the early church that were part of its fellowship (v. 12). Jude does not describe them further.

  • Enoch, the seventh from Adam. Counting Adam as the first, Enoch is the seventh in the line of Genesis 5. He “walked with God” and was taken by God (Genesis 5:24).

  • The last time. The present age between Christ’s first coming and His return, in which the apostles said mockers would appear (v. 18; compare Acts 2:17; 1 John 2:18).

  • Sensual persons. The NKJV word translates a term meaning natural or merely human: people living by ordinary instinct rather than by the Spirit (v. 19; compare 1 Corinthians 2:14).

Christ and Life

Jude belongs to Jesus Christ as His bondservant, and he writes to people who are “preserved in Jesus Christ” (v. 1). To belong to Christ is to yield one’s will to Him. That is why the intruders’ error is so serious. By turning grace into lewdness they “deny the only Lord God and our Lord Jesus Christ” (v. 4). They may speak His name, but their lives refuse His rule. The grace that forgives also teaches believers to deny ungodliness and live for God (Titus 2:11–14). In Jude, grace and the lordship of Jesus cannot be separated.

Believers’ security rests in Christ. They are preserved in Him, and they look for “the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ unto eternal life” (v. 21). The wider New Testament shows the same keeping in Christ’s own ministry. He prayed that the Father would keep His people (John 17:11–15), and “He always lives to make intercession for them” (Hebrews 7:25). Jude praises the God who is able to present His people “faultless before the presence of His glory” (v. 24); Paul describes Christ giving Himself for the church so that it might be “holy and without blemish” (Ephesians 5:25–27; compare Colossians 1:22). Believers take His warnings seriously while resting in the One who keeps them.

Enoch’s prophecy speaks of the Lord coming “with ten thousands of His saints, to execute judgment on all” (vv. 14–15). Read with the rest of the New Testament, it looks ahead to the return of Christ, when the Son of Man comes “in His glory, and all the holy angels with Him” (Matthew 25:31) and the Lord Jesus is “revealed from heaven with His mighty angels” (2 Thessalonians 1:7). The prophecy itself does not name Jesus, but the day it announces is the day believers await. The coming that brings judgment on the ungodly brings mercy to those who belong to Him (v. 21).

Life in Christ is also life in the Spirit. The intruders are “not having the Spirit” (v. 19), while believers pray in the Holy Spirit, keep themselves in the love of God, and look for the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ (vv. 20–21). The Spirit, the Father’s love, and the Son’s mercy are joined in this one call. The life Jude describes is more than correct religious language; it is living dependence on God in prayer, love, and hope.

Those who receive mercy extend it. Believers who await Christ’s mercy show compassion and pull others “out of the fire” (vv. 22–23). The picture resembles Zechariah 3:1–5, where Joshua the high priest is “a brand plucked from the fire”; in Zechariah’s vision his filthy garments are removed and he is clothed with rich robes. Jude’s own emphasis is rescue that refuses defilement: believers pursue the endangered person with mercy while “hating even the garment defiled by the flesh.”

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Read Jude as a pastor’s letter to people he loves. Jude exposes the intruders to protect the people he calls beloved. Receive his warnings as a call to faithful vigilance, and his closing praise as assurance of God’s keeping. Hold the two together as Jude does, without needing to settle every question about how they fit.

Remember that contending for the faith is not quarrelsomeness. Look to verses 20–23 for the shape Jude himself gives it.

Do not let the letter’s unusual details, such as the angels of verse 6, Michael’s dispute about Moses’ body, or the quotation of Enoch, become the center of attention. Each serves Jude’s main point. And read to the end. The letter closes with the God who is able to keep you.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

  • Verse 1. The NKJV reads “sanctified by God the Father.” Other manuscripts read “beloved,” and the NLT reflects that reading: “called by God the Father, who loves you.” Both readings describe believers as belonging to God.

  • Verse 4. The NKJV reads “deny the only Lord God and our Lord Jesus Christ.” Other manuscripts lack “God,” as reflected in the NLT: “our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ.” In that reading both titles belong to Jesus.

  • Verse 5. The NKJV reads “the Lord, having saved the people out of the land of Egypt.” Some early manuscripts read “Jesus,” which the NLT follows. In that reading Jude identifies Jesus as the One who delivered Israel from Egypt (compare 1 Corinthians 10:4, 9).

  • Verse 9. Scripture does not record Michael’s dispute about Moses’ body elsewhere, and Jude does not explain its purpose. The early Christian writer Origen linked the account with a Jewish work he called the Ascension of Moses (On First Principles 3.2.1); the incomplete surviving text usually identified with that work does not contain the episode. Jude’s point is Michael’s reverent restraint.

  • Verse 12. The NKJV reads “spots in your love feasts.” The Greek word can also mean hidden reefs, as in the NLT: “like dangerous reefs.” Either image describes people who defile or endanger the fellowship while sharing in it.

  • Verses 14–15. Jude’s quotation of Enoch closely parallels 1 Enoch 1:9, a Jewish writing known in Jude’s day. Jude affirms the truth of the prophecy he quotes; this does not make the whole of 1 Enoch Scripture. Paul likewise quotes Greek writers without endorsing everything they wrote (Acts 17:28; Titus 1:12).

  • Verses 22–23. The manuscripts differ here. The NKJV describes two groups: “on some have compassion, making a distinction; but others save with fear.” The NLT, following other manuscripts, describes three. In either form the call is to discerning mercy and urgent rescue.

  • Verse 25. The NKJV reads “To God our Savior, Who alone is wise.” Other manuscripts, followed by the NLT, lack “wise,” add “through Jesus Christ our Lord,” and include “before all time.”

  • Jude and 2 Peter. Much of Jude 4–18 closely resembles 2 Peter 2:1–3:3. Whether one letter drew on the other, and in which direction, remains uncertain, and Jude’s message does not depend on the answer. Reading the two together can shed light on both.

  • References. Jude has one chapter, so verses may be cited as Jude 1:6 or simply as verse 6.
