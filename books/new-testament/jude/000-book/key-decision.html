Important questions in the text, and how MTB understands them.

Book Questions

Who is Jude? (Jude 1:1)

Jude calls himself “a bondservant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James” (Jude 1:1). MTB understands him to be the Judas named among Jesus’ brothers in Matthew 13:55 and Mark 6:3, and his brother to be James, the Lord’s brother who led the church in Jerusalem (Acts 15:13; Galatians 1:19). He is not Judas Iscariot, and MTB does not identify him with the apostle “Judas the son of James” (Luke 6:16).

That family tie comes from comparing these passages; Jude himself does not state it. MTB understands Jude as Jesus’ half-brother, a later son of Mary, while affirming that Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit (Matthew 1:18–25). Some church traditions understand Jesus’ “brothers” as cousins or as sons of Joseph by an earlier marriage.

What Jude does say is telling. He could have claimed family privilege. Instead he calls himself Christ’s bondservant. During Jesus’ ministry His brothers did not believe in Him (John 7:5), yet after the resurrection they were praying with the disciples (Acts 1:14). Scripture does not tell the story of Jude’s own conversion.

Read: Jude 1:1, Matthew 13:55, Mark 6:3, Galatians 1:19, Acts 15:13, Luke 6:16, Matthew 1:18–25, John 7:5, Acts 1:14

If God keeps His people, why does Jude warn them so strongly? (Jude 1:1, 5, 21, 24)

Jude begins and ends with God’s keeping. His readers are “preserved in Jesus Christ” (Jude 1:1), and God “is able to keep you from stumbling” (Jude 1:24). In between, Jude reminds them that the Lord saved Israel out of Egypt and “afterward destroyed those who did not believe” (Jude 1:5). He also commands, “keep yourselves in the love of God” (Jude 1:21).

MTB holds both truths with their full weight. God’s keeping is real, and true believers can rest in it (John 10:27–29; 1 Peter 1:5). His warnings are also real. Unbelief brings real loss and judgment, and turning away from Christ carries genuine spiritual peril, even where faithful believers disagree on how such warnings relate to eternal security (Hebrews 3:12–14).

Jude does not write to settle that debate. The warning is meant to wake up faith and endurance; the promise is meant to give rest. Keeping ourselves in God’s love does not earn His love. It is remaining where His love has placed us, while trusting the One who is able to keep us.

Read: Jude 1:1, Jude 1:5, Jude 1:20–25, John 10:27–29, 1 Peter 1:5, Hebrews 3:12–14 · Related: Does verse 5 say “the Lord” or “Jesus”?, Does “faultless” mean believers will stop sinning?

Does Jude quote books that are not in the Bible? (Jude 1:9, 14–15)

Enoch’s prophecy in Jude 1:14–15 closely matches a line in 1 Enoch 1:9, an ancient Jewish writing outside the Bible. The dispute between Michael and the devil over Moses’ body (Jude 1:9) is not found in the Old Testament either. Its original source has not survived in full.

MTB receives what Jude says as true, because Jude’s letter is inspired Scripture. Quoting a true statement does not make the whole book it comes from Scripture. It also does not prove that the historical Enoch wrote everything later attached to his name, or confirm every story told about angels in those writings.

A similar line surviving outside the Protestant Bible is no reason to dismiss the prophecy. Jude applies Enoch’s prophecy of judgment to the men troubling his readers; he does not claim that Enoch named them.

Read: Jude 1:9, Jude 1:14–15, Genesis 5:21–24 · Related: Is Enoch’s prophecy about the return of Jesus?, What was the dispute over the body of Moses?

Did Jude borrow from 2 Peter, or did Peter borrow from Jude? (Jude 1:4–18)

Much of Jude 1:4–18 closely parallels 2 Peter 2:1–3:3: intruders who deny the Lord, angels held for judgment, Sodom and Gomorrah, Balaam, waterless clouds, and the apostles’ warning about mockers in the last days. The two letters are clearly related.

Scholars disagree about which came first, or whether both drew on shared teaching. Many think 2 Peter used Jude. Others, in a view MTB finds reasonable, place Jude later in the first century and see Jude drawing on 2 Peter. MTB does not build its teaching on either answer. Reading the letters side by side can shed light on both, but Jude’s message is clear without settling the historical question.

Read: Jude 1:4–18, 2 Peter 2, 2 Peter 3:1–3

Jude 1:3–4 · The Urgent Call to Contend for the Faith

What does it mean to “contend earnestly for the faith”? (v. 3)

“The faith” here is the apostolic gospel, the truth about Christ “once for all delivered to the saints” (v. 3). It is a received message with moral claims, not a private religious feeling. Believers are to know it, live it, and defend it with conviction and love.

“Once for all” means no later teaching or claimed revelation may revise this gospel (Galatians 1:8–9). It does not mean the Holy Spirit no longer gives fresh understanding or revelation in line with the inerrant Word of God. His light serves the truth already received; it never replaces it.

Contending is not a mandate to divide over every secondary disagreement. Jude shows what faithful contending includes: building one another up, praying, keeping in God’s love, and showing mercy to those in danger (vv. 20–23).

Read: Jude 1:3, Jude 1:20–23, Galatians 1:8–9

Did God predestine these men to be ungodly? (v. 4)

The NKJV says these men “long ago were marked out for this condemnation” (v. 4). MTB reads this as their judgment being announced beforehand. Jude then shows that judgment in the examples and prophecy that follow (vv. 5–16). Exactly which earlier words Jude has in mind is debated.

The clause does not by itself explain a decree of God that caused their ungodliness. What Jude condemns is their own conduct: they “turn the grace of our God into lewdness and deny the only Lord God and our Lord Jesus Christ.”

Read: Jude 1:4–16

Does grace give freedom to keep sinning? (v. 4)

No. These men “turn the grace of our God into lewdness” (v. 4). They treat grace as permission for immorality, and in doing so they deny Jesus Christ as Lord, whatever they profess (Titus 1:16).

Grace frees us from sin; it does not authorize sin. Belonging to Jesus as Lord and a changed life go together, and the call to holiness grows out of grace rather than replacing it.

Read: Jude 1:4, Titus 1:16

Jude 1:5–7 · Three Reminders of God’s Judgment

Does verse 5 say “the Lord” or “Jesus”? (v. 5)

The NKJV reads, “the Lord, having saved the people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed those who did not believe” (v. 5). Some important Greek manuscripts read “Jesus” instead, and several modern translations, including the NLT, follow them.

In the NKJV, “the Lord” is God as the deliverer and judge of the Exodus. The “Jesus” reading would name Christ as active in the Exodus before His incarnation. It does not refer to Joshua. Christ’s existence before His birth is taught elsewhere in Scripture and does not depend on this disputed reading.

Either way, the warning stands: being part of a rescued people gives no shelter for unbelief (Numbers 14; 1 Corinthians 10:1–12; Hebrews 3:7–19).

Read: Jude 1:5, Numbers 14, 1 Corinthians 10:1–12, Hebrews 3:7–19 · Related: If God keeps His people, why does Jude warn them?

Who are the angels in verse 6? Is this Genesis 6? (v. 6)

Jude speaks of “the angels who did not keep their proper domain, but left their own abode,” now held “in everlasting chains under darkness for the judgment of the great day” (v. 6).

Many connect this with Genesis 6:1–4, where “the sons of God” took wives from “the daughters of men.” MTB leans toward this view. Jude’s move straight to Sodom, which “in a similar manner to these” went “after strange flesh” (v. 7), and the parallel in 2 Peter 2:4–6 support it. Others see a broader angelic rebellion against God, and the two views need not exclude each other.

What Jude teaches is clear: rebellion against God and leaving the place He has appointed bring judgment. These verses do not support detailed theories about angelic reproduction, the origin of every demon, or when Satan fell.

Read: Jude 1:6–7, Genesis 6:1–4, 2 Peter 2:4–6 · Related: What was Sodom’s sin?

What was Sodom’s sin? (v. 7)

Jude says Sodom and Gomorrah had “given themselves over to sexual immorality and gone after strange flesh” (v. 7). Genesis 19 shows what that looked like: the men of the city surrounded Lot’s house and demanded his guests, who were in fact angels, so that they might “know them carnally” (Genesis 19:1–11).

MTB understands “strange flesh” as sexual union against the order God created: angels with women, men with men, and women with women (compare Romans 1:26–27). In Sodom it took the form of men seeking to violate other men, joined with violence. Sodom’s sin was more than a failure of hospitality. Scripture also names its pride and neglect of the poor (Ezekiel 16:49), but Jude’s focus is sexual sin.

The cities are “set forth as an example” of God’s judgment. They warn against rebellion; they are not a timeline of the end.

Read: Jude 1:7, Genesis 19:1–11, Ezekiel 16:49, Romans 1:26–27 · Related: Who are the angels in verse 6?

Jude 1:8–10 · Dreamers Who Revile What They Do Not Know

Who are the “dignitaries” these men speak evil of? (v. 8)

The word translated “dignitaries” means glorious ones. MTB understands them most likely as angelic beings, because the next verse sets these men beside Michael the archangel, who would not insult even the devil (v. 9). Whether Jude means good angels, fallen ones, or both is disputed, and some read the word as human leaders.

The sin itself is clear. These men “reject authority” and “speak evil of whatever they do not know” (vv. 8, 10). As a spiritual application, the passage calls believers to honor, reverence and humility before what God has established, including human leadership, together with honest and responsible accountability.

Read: Jude 1:8–10 · Related: Should believers not rebuke the devil?

What was the dispute over the body of Moses? Is Michael Jesus? (v. 9)

Jude affirms a real dispute between Michael the archangel and the devil “about the body of Moses” (v. 9). He does not say what caused it or how it ended. Scripture says only that the LORD buried Moses and “no one knows his grave to this day” (Deuteronomy 34:5–6). The popular suggestion that the devil wanted Moses’ body to become an object of worship is a guess, not what the text reveals.

Michael is called “the archangel” (v. 9) and “one of the chief princes” (Daniel 10:13). These titles mark him as a high-ranking created angel, not Christ. Some identify Michael with Christ, but that view is clearly unscriptural and borders on heresy, especially since Michael appeals to “the Lord” rather than speaking with divine authority of his own (v. 9; Zechariah 3:2).

Read: Jude 1:9, Deuteronomy 34:5–6, Daniel 10:13, Zechariah 3:2 · Related: Does Jude quote books that are not in the Bible?

Does Michael’s example mean believers should not rebuke the devil? (vv. 8–10)

No. Michael “dared not bring against him a reviling accusation, but said, ‘The Lord rebuke you!’” (v. 9; compare Zechariah 3:2). His restraint exposes the arrogant, insulting speech of men who “speak evil of whatever they do not know” (v. 10). Michael left the judgment to the Lord.

This is not a ban on resisting evil in the authority of Christ. It warns against stepping outside the sphere God has given us. Even an archangel did not go beyond his assigned place; he appealed to the Lord. Believers are not to pray, act, or exercise spiritual authority beyond what God has entrusted to them (compare 2 Corinthians 10:13). Jude shows the same danger elsewhere: angels who “did not keep their proper domain” (v. 6), and Korah, who grasped for a ministry not given to him (v. 11). Michael’s example points us to reverent dependence on God, and to prayer and action within the sphere He has assigned.

Read: Jude 1:8–10, Zechariah 3:2, Jude 1:6, Jude 1:11, 2 Corinthians 10:13 · Related: Who are the “dignitaries”?

Jude 1:14–16 · Enoch’s Prophecy of the Lord’s Coming Judgment

Is Enoch’s prophecy about the return of Jesus? (vv. 14–15)

Enoch says, “Behold, the Lord comes with ten thousands of His saints, to execute judgment on all” (vv. 14–15). The saying itself does not name Jesus. Read with the rest of the New Testament, it fits the coming of Christ in glory to judge (Matthew 25:31; 2 Thessalonians 1:7–10).

MTB draws that connection from the wider teaching of Scripture, not from a claim that Enoch spoke the name of Jesus. Either way, the point is sure: the Lord will judge the ungodly for their deeds and for “all the harsh things which ungodly sinners have spoken against Him” (v. 15).

Read: Jude 1:14–16, Matthew 25:31, 2 Thessalonians 1:7–10 · Related: Does Jude quote books that are not in the Bible?

Jude 1:20–23 · Keep Yourselves in the Love of God and Rescue Others

What does “praying in the Holy Spirit” mean? Does it include praying in tongues? (v. 20)

To pray in the Holy Spirit is to pray as the Spirit enables and directs. Jude sets it against those who are “not having the Spirit” (v. 19). The call is to living dependence on the Spirit, not merely correct religious language.

MTB understands this to include praying in tongues. Paul says the one who speaks in a tongue “edifies himself” (1 Corinthians 14:4), which fits Jude’s call to be “building yourselves up” (v. 20). Jude does not limit Spirit-led prayer to tongues, and he does not exclude tongues from it. Many faithful Christians read the phrase more generally and do not connect it with tongues.

Read: Jude 1:19–21, 1 Corinthians 14:4

Does “hating even the garment” mean keeping our distance from sinners? (v. 23)

No. The fire pictures urgent danger, and “the garment defiled by the flesh” pictures the contaminating power of sin (v. 23). Jude calls believers to pursue the person with mercy while refusing the evil. The “fear” is a sober sense of how serious sin is and how easily it spreads.

Hating the stained garment never permits hating or humiliating the person. Zechariah 3:1–5 offers a striking connection: Joshua the high priest is called “a brand plucked from the fire,” and his filthy garments are removed. Jude does not explain his words that way, but the picture fits.

Read: Jude 1:23, Zechariah 3:1–5

Jude 1:24–25 · Glory to the God Who Is Able to Keep

Does “faultless” mean believers will stop sinning in this life? (v. 24)

No. Jude praises the God who “is able to keep you from stumbling” and to present His people “faultless” before His glory “with exceeding joy” (v. 24). The promise rests on God’s power, not on our self-sufficiency, and it looks ahead to our presentation before Him (Colossians 1:21–23; Ephesians 5:25–27).

The verse does not promise that believers will never commit a sin now. It is confidence that God is fully able to finish what He has begun, and it calls us to worship Him for it.

Read: Jude 1:24–25, Colossians 1:21–23, Ephesians 5:25–27 · Related: If God keeps His people, why does Jude warn them?
