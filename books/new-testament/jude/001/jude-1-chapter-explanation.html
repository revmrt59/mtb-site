Chapter Study

Jude 1

Verse-by-Verse
Jude 1:1
Jude 1:1
NKJV

Jude 1:1 Jude, a bondservant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James, To those who are called, sanctified by God the Father, and preserved in Jesus Christ:(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:1 This letter is from Jude, a slave of Jesus Christ and a brother of James. I am writing to all who have been called by God the Father, who loves you and keeps you safe in the care of Jesus Christ. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jude introduces himself by his surrender rather than his family tie to Jesus, as “a bondservant of Jesus Christ,” adding “brother of James” to identify himself. He addresses his readers by what God has done for them: they are “called,” “sanctified by God the Father,” and “preserved in Jesus Christ,” so a letter full of warnings begins with God’s keeping hold on His people.

Verse Explanation

Jude introduces himself by his surrender rather than his family tie to Jesus, as “a bondservant of Jesus Christ,” adding “brother of James” to identify himself. He addresses his readers by what God has done for them: they are “called,” “sanctified by God the Father,” and “preserved in Jesus Christ,” so a letter full of warnings begins with God’s keeping hold on His people.

Jude opens the letter with his own name. He does not tell us here that he grew up in the same household as Jesus, but the Gospels name a Judas among the brothers of Jesus, alongside James (Matthew 13:55; Mark 6:3). Jude was Jesus’ half-brother: they shared a mother, Mary, while Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit. During Jesus’ ministry His brothers did not believe in Him (John 7:5). After the resurrection they are found praying with the disciples in the upper room (Acts 1:14). Scripture does not narrate the moment Jude came to faith, but this letter shows where it led him.

a bondservant of Jesus Christ is the first thing Jude wants his readers to know about him. A bondservant belonged to a master and lived to do that master’s will. Jude could have opened with the most impressive credential any early Christian could claim, a family tie to the Lord. Instead he introduces himself by his surrender. The brother he once doubted is now his Master, and Jude gives Him His full title, Jesus Christ, the Messiah.

and brother of James identifies Jude by a name his readers would recognize. This James is the Lord’s brother who became a leading figure in the church at Jerusalem (Acts 15:13–21; Galatians 1:19; 2:9), not James the son of Zebedee. Even James, in his own letter, calls himself “a bondservant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ” (James 1:1). Both brothers chose the same humble title.

To those who are called describes the readers by what God has done, not by where they live or who they are. To be called is to be summoned by God to Himself through the gospel. Jude never names a city or a congregation, and the precise identity of his first readers is not known. What he does make plain is that the people about to receive his warnings belong to God because God took the initiative.

sanctified by God the Father means set apart as holy, made God’s own possession. The Father is named as the One who has done this. In a letter that will describe men who “defile the flesh” (v. 8), Jude first reminds his readers that God has already claimed them for Himself.

and preserved in Jesus Christ completes the description. To be preserved is to be kept and guarded. Jude describes something God has done that continues to hold true: these believers have been kept, and they remain kept, in Jesus Christ. The letter will ask much of them, and it will warn them soberly, but it begins with God’s keeping hold on His people. When the letter ends, it will praise the God “who is able to keep you from stumbling” (v. 24). Jude places his readers between God’s keeping at the beginning and God’s keeping at the end.

Study Notes

The Greek name behind “Jude” is Ioudas, the same name as the patriarch Judah and as Judas Iscariot. English translations use the shorter form “Jude” for the writer of this letter, which helps readers keep him distinct from the betrayer and from “Judas the son of James,” one of the Twelve (Luke 6:16).

The NKJV reads “sanctified,” following the Greek text behind the King James Version. Many older manuscripts read “beloved” instead, and the NLT reflects that reading with “who loves you.” This is a difference between manuscripts, not a difference in translation. Both readings describe what God the Father has done for His people.

The Greek verb translated “preserved” (tēreō, to keep or guard) appears in a form that describes a completed action with lasting results. Jude uses the same verb when the angels “did not keep” their domain and God “has reserved” them (v. 6), when darkness is “reserved” for the intruders (v. 13), and in the command “keep yourselves” (v. 21).

The words “in Jesus Christ” translate a Greek phrase without a preposition. It can be understood as kept in Christ, kept by Christ, or kept for Christ, that is, kept safe until the day He comes. Translations differ here because the grammar allows more than one of these senses.

Jude 1:2
Jude 1:2
NKJV

Jude 1:2 Mercy, peace, and love be multiplied to you.(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:2 May God give you more and more mercy, peace, and love. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jude prays, “Mercy, peace, and love be multiplied to you.” He asks God to increase these gifts among people who will need every one of them to stand against what has crept in, and mercy and love return at the letter’s close (vv. 21–22).

Verse Explanation

Jude prays, “Mercy, peace, and love be multiplied to you.” He asks God to increase these gifts among people who will need every one of them to stand against what has crept in, and mercy and love return at the letter’s close (vv. 21–22).

Mercy comes first in Jude’s prayer. Mercy is God’s compassion toward people in need who cannot help themselves. Jude’s readers will need it, and they will also be asked to show it. Mercy returns at the end of the letter, where believers wait for “the mercy of our Lord Jesus Christ” (v. 21) and are told to “have compassion” on others (v. 22).

peace is the settled wholeness that comes from being right with God. In a fellowship troubled by people who “cause divisions” (v. 19), peace with God and peace among believers were not small things to pray for.

and love completes the prayer. Jude means God’s love for His people, the love in which he will later urge them to keep themselves (v. 21). He prays that love would grow among them at the very time some are using the language of grace to excuse sin.

be multiplied to you asks for more than a single gift. Jude prays that these blessings would increase and keep increasing. He does not pray that the danger would disappear. He prays that God’s mercy, peace and love would grow larger than the danger.

Study Notes

Most New Testament letters greet the readers with “grace and peace.” Jude’s greeting of mercy, peace and love is found only here. The form “be multiplied to you” also opens 1 Peter and 2 Peter (1 Peter 1:2; 2 Peter 1:2), where grace and peace are multiplied.

Jude often groups his thoughts in threes. Verse 1 describes the readers in three ways, verse 2 names three blessings, verses 5–7 give three examples of judgment, verse 8 lists three charges, and verse 11 names three men from the Old Testament.

Jude 1:3–4 — The Urgent Call to Contend for the Faith

Jude 1:3
Jude 1:3
NKJV

Jude 1:3 Beloved, while I was very diligent to write to you concerning our common salvation, I found it necessary to write to you exhorting you to contend earnestly for the faith which was once for all delivered to the saints.(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:3 Dear friends, I had been eagerly planning to write to you about the salvation we all share. But now I find that I must write about something else, urging you to defend the faith that God has entrusted once for all time to his holy people. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jude had been eager to write about “our common salvation,” the salvation all believers share, but the danger facing them made it necessary instead to urge them “to contend earnestly for the faith which was once for all delivered to the saints.” The faith is the apostolic gospel God has entrusted to His people, complete and not open to revision, and they are to hold it, live it, and defend it.

Verse Explanation

Jude had been eager to write about “our common salvation,” the salvation all believers share, but the danger facing them made it necessary instead to urge them “to contend earnestly for the faith which was once for all delivered to the saints.” The faith is the apostolic gospel God has entrusted to His people, complete and not open to revision, and they are to hold it, live it, and defend it.

Beloved is the first word Jude speaks directly to his readers, and he uses it again when he turns from the intruders to the church (vv. 17, 20). The warnings that follow are written by someone who loves the people he warns.

while I was very diligent to write to you concerning our common salvation tells us what Jude had first intended. He was eager to write about the salvation all believers share. “Common” here means shared, not ordinary. The same salvation belongs to every believer, with no higher or secret version reserved for a spiritual elite.

I found it necessary to write to you explains why the letter took a different shape. Something had happened that would not let Jude write the letter he planned. Verse 4 gives the reason.

exhorting you shows the tone of what follows. Jude urges and encourages his readers rather than simply informing them.

to contend earnestly is a strong expression for an intense, sustained struggle. Jude does not call believers to be quarrelsome or to fight over every secondary difference. He calls them to hold the faith firmly, to live by it and to defend it when it is under attack.

for the faith here means the content of what Christians believe, the apostolic gospel, rather than an individual’s personal trust. Jude will later speak of “your most holy faith” (v. 20) as the foundation on which believers build.

which was once for all delivered to the saints tells how that faith came to God’s people. It was delivered, handed over as a trust, and it was delivered once for all. The gospel is complete. The Holy Spirit continues to illuminate it and make it alive in believers, but no later teaching or claimed revelation may revise it. “The saints” are God’s holy people, the whole church to whom this trust was given.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “contend earnestly” (epagōnizomai) occurs only here in the New Testament. It is built on the word for an athletic contest, the root behind the English word “agony,” with a prefix that intensifies it.

The word “delivered” translates a Greek verb regularly used for handing on teaching from one person to another. Paul uses it of the gospel he passed on to the Corinthians: “For I delivered to you first of all that which I also received” (1 Corinthians 15:3).

Jude 1:4
Jude 1:4
NKJV

Jude 1:4 For certain men have crept in unnoticed, who long ago were marked out for this condemnation, ungodly men, who turn the grace of our God into lewdness and deny the only Lord God and our Lord Jesus Christ.(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:4 I say this because some ungodly people have wormed their way into your churches, saying that God’s marvelous grace allows us to live immoral lives. The condemnation of such people was recorded long ago, for they have denied our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ. (NLT)

Main Idea

The urgency has a reason: “certain men have crept in unnoticed,” ungodly men who “turn the grace of our God into lewdness and deny the only Lord God and our Lord Jesus Christ.” They treat grace as permission to sin, and their lives reject Christ’s lordship; their condemnation was announced long ago, as the examples that follow will show.

Verse Explanation

The urgency has a reason: “certain men have crept in unnoticed,” ungodly men who “turn the grace of our God into lewdness and deny the only Lord God and our Lord Jesus Christ.” They treat grace as permission to sin, and their lives reject Christ’s lordship; their condemnation was announced long ago, as the examples that follow will show.

For certain men gives the reason for Jude’s urgency. He does not name them. The vague word “certain” keeps attention on what they do rather than on who they are.

have crept in unnoticed shows where the danger came from. These men did not attack the church from outside. They slipped into its fellowship quietly, and they were now present at its meals (v. 12).

who long ago were marked out for this condemnation means their judgment was announced beforehand. The examples Jude is about to give (vv. 5–7, 11) and the prophecy of Enoch (vv. 14–15) show that God has always judged this kind of ungodliness. The phrase tells us that their condemnation was written in advance. It does not say that God caused them to be ungodly.

ungodly men is Jude’s summary of their character. To be ungodly is to live without reverence for God, and it shows in conduct and in speech. The word will return again and again in the letter (vv. 15, 18).

who turn the grace of our God into lewdness describes the first mark of these men. They take God’s free, undeserved kindness and change it into an excuse for open, shameless sensuality. Lewdness means sin without restraint or shame. Grace is meant to free people from sin, but these men treated it as permission to sin.

and deny the only Lord God and our Lord Jesus Christ describes the second mark. They may not have denied Christ in words, but they denied Him by refusing His rule over their lives. The word “only” matters here: there is one Lord, and they would not bow to Him. Jude, the bondservant of Jesus Christ (v. 1), stands in sharp contrast to men who claim His grace while rejecting His lordship.

Study Notes

The Greek verb behind “crept in unnoticed” (pareisdyō) occurs only here in the New Testament. It pictures slipping in through a side way, entering where one has not been openly received.

“Marked out” translates a Greek verb meaning “written beforehand.” Paul uses the same verb in Romans 15:4, “For whatever things were written before were written for our learning.” Whether Jude means the Old Testament examples, the prophecy of Enoch, the warnings of the apostles (v. 17), or all of these together is debated.

The Greek verb translated “turn” means to change or transfer something from one thing to another. Paul uses it in Galatians 1:6 for believers “turning away so soon” from the One who called them.

The NKJV includes the word “God” in “the only Lord God,” following the Greek text behind the King James Version. Many manuscripts lack it, which gives a reading like the NLT’s “our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ.” In that reading both titles belong to Jesus. The first word translated “Lord” here (despotēs) means master or owner, the word used for the owner of a household’s servants. Peter uses it of the false teachers “denying the Lord who bought them” (2 Peter 2:1).

Jude 1:5–7 — Three Reminders of God’s Judgment

Jude 1:5
Jude 1:5
NKJV

Jude 1:5 But I want to remind you, though you once knew this, that the Lord, having saved the people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed those who did not believe.(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:5 So I want to remind you, though you already know these things, that Jesus first rescued the nation of Israel from Egypt, but later he destroyed those who did not remain faithful. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jude reminds his readers of what they already know: “the Lord, having saved the people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed those who did not believe.” Being part of a rescued people did not shelter those who refused to trust Him, so belonging to the fellowship gives no cover for unbelief. Some early manuscripts read Jesus in place of the Lord here, naming Him as the deliverer in the exodus.

Verse Explanation

Jude reminds his readers of what they already know: “the Lord, having saved the people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed those who did not believe.” Being part of a rescued people did not shelter those who refused to trust Him, so belonging to the fellowship gives no cover for unbelief. Some early manuscripts read Jesus in place of the Lord here, naming Him as the deliverer in the exodus.

But I want to remind you, though you once knew this shows that Jude is not bringing new teaching. His readers already know the history he is about to recall. They need to remember it and see how it applies to them.

that the Lord, having saved the people out of the land of Egypt recalls the exodus. The Lord delivered the whole nation of Israel from slavery. That rescue was real, and every Israelite who walked out of Egypt shared in it.

afterward destroyed those who did not believe recalls what happened next. When Israel reached the edge of the Promised Land, the people refused to trust God and enter it. The Lord declared that the adults of that generation who had complained against Him would die in the wilderness. Only Caleb and Joshua, who had trusted Him, would enter the land (Numbers 14:29–30). The same Lord who rescued them also judged their unbelief. Jude’s point is sobering: having been part of a delivered people did not protect those who refused to trust the God who delivered them. Belonging to the fellowship of believers gives no cover for unbelief.

Study Notes

The NKJV reads “the Lord,” and so do many Greek manuscripts. Other early and important manuscripts read “Jesus,” and several modern translations follow that reading, including the NLT: “Jesus first rescued the nation of Israel from Egypt.” If that reading is original, Jude names Jesus as the deliverer in the exodus.

The Greek word translated “afterward” literally means “the second time.” Jude presents two acts of the same Lord: first He saved His people, and then He destroyed those among them who did not believe.

Jude 1:6
NKJV

Jude 1:6 And the angels who did not keep their proper domain, but left their own abode, He has reserved in everlasting chains under darkness for the judgment of the great day;(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:6 And I remind you of the angels who did not stay within the limits of authority God gave them but left the place where they belonged. God has kept them securely chained in prisons of darkness, waiting for the great day of judgment. (NLT)

Main Idea

The second example is “the angels who did not keep their proper domain, but left their own abode,” whom God “has reserved in everlasting chains under darkness for the judgment of the great day.” Even heavenly beings who abandoned the place God assigned them are held for judgment; the most likely connection is with the “sons of God” in Genesis 6:1–4, though Jude’s warning stands either way.

Verse Explanation

The second example is “the angels who did not keep their proper domain, but left their own abode,” whom God “has reserved in everlasting chains under darkness for the judgment of the great day.” Even heavenly beings who abandoned the place God assigned them are held for judgment; the most likely connection is with the “sons of God” in Genesis 6:1–4, though Jude’s warning stands either way.

And the angels who did not keep their proper domain is Jude’s second example. God had given these angels a position and a sphere of authority. They did not keep it.

but left their own abode adds that they deliberately abandoned the dwelling place God had appointed for them. Their sin was rebellion against God’s order, a refusal to stay where He had placed them.

He has reserved in everlasting chains under darkness describes their present condition. The angels who would not keep their place are now kept by God, held in darkness as prisoners are held in chains. Jude does not describe these chains or say where this place is. His point is that their rebellion did not escape God’s control.

for the judgment of the great day shows that their present confinement is not the end. They are held for the final judgment. Jude does not say when these angels sinned. The most likely connection is with the “sons of God” in Genesis 6:1–4: the sexual sin of Sodom in the next verse and the parallel in 2 Peter 2:4–5 point that way. A broader angelic rebellion is the main alternative, and the two need not exclude each other. Either way, if God holds rebellious angels for judgment, people who reject His authority will not escape it either.

Study Notes

Jude plays on the Greek verb for “keep” (tēreō) in this verse. The angels “did not keep” their domain, so God “has reserved,” or kept, them in chains.

“Proper domain” translates a Greek word (archē) that can mean beginning, rule, or position of authority. Here it refers to the rank and sphere God had assigned to these angels. The word translated “abode” refers to a dwelling place; Paul uses it in 2 Corinthians 5:2 for “our habitation which is from heaven.”

The Greek word translated “everlasting” in “everlasting chains” (aidios) is not the usual New Testament word for eternal. It appears elsewhere in the New Testament only in Romans 1:20, of God’s “eternal power.” In verses 7 and 21 Jude uses the more common Greek word (aiōnios).

Peter makes the same point in 2 Peter 2:4: “God did not spare the angels who sinned, but cast them down to hell and delivered them into chains of darkness, to be reserved for judgment.”

Jude 1:7
Jude 1:7
NKJV

Jude 1:7 as Sodom and Gomorrah, and the cities around them in a similar manner to these, having given themselves over to sexual immorality and gone after strange flesh, are set forth as an example, suffering the vengeance of eternal fire.(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:7 And don’t forget Sodom and Gomorrah and their neighboring towns, which were filled with immorality and every kind of sexual perversion. Those cities were destroyed by fire and serve as a warning of the eternal fire of God’s judgment. (NLT)

Main Idea

Sodom, Gomorrah, and the cities around them, “having given themselves over to sexual immorality and gone after strange flesh,” are “set forth as an example, suffering the vengeance of eternal fire.” Their destruction followed the men of Sodom’s violent attempt at a same-sex assault on Lot’s guests, angels who had come as men (Genesis 19:4–11), and it stands as a visible warning that persistent sexual rebellion brings God’s judgment.

Verse Explanation

Sodom, Gomorrah, and the cities around them, “having given themselves over to sexual immorality and gone after strange flesh,” are “set forth as an example, suffering the vengeance of eternal fire.” Their destruction followed the men of Sodom’s violent attempt at a same-sex assault on Lot’s guests, angels who had come as men (Genesis 19:4–11), and it stands as a visible warning that persistent sexual rebellion brings God’s judgment.

as Sodom and Gomorrah, and the cities around them brings Jude’s third example. Genesis 19 tells how God destroyed these cities of the plain with fire from heaven. The cities around them included Admah and Zeboiim (Deuteronomy 29:23).

in a similar manner to these most likely links Sodom with the angels of verse 6. Like those angels, the people of Sodom crossed the boundaries God had set.

having given themselves over to sexual immorality describes their sin. They surrendered themselves to it as a way of life, not as a single fall.

and gone after strange flesh names its particular form. In Genesis 19:4–11 the men of Sodom surrounded Lot’s house and demanded that he bring out his two guests so they could abuse them sexually. The guests were angels who had come in the form of men. Sodom’s sin was not merely a failure of hospitality. It included an attempted same-sex assault, carried out with violence, against messengers sent by God.

are set forth as an example means Sodom and Gomorrah stand on display as a warning. The destruction of those cities was a visible, lasting sign of what God does with persistent rebellion.

suffering the vengeance of eternal fire describes the penalty. The fire that fell on Sodom (Genesis 19:24) pointed beyond itself to the eternal judgment of God. Vengeance here does not mean God losing His temper. It means the just penalty a holy God gives to sin.

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “these” (toutois) has the same form in the masculine and the neuter, so its form alone does not settle what it refers to. The Greek words describing the cities’ sin, “having given themselves over” and “gone after,” are feminine and agree with the cities, so “these” points to someone the cities resembled rather than to the cities themselves. Many take it to mean the angels of verse 6, the group Jude has just named. Others think of the unbelieving Israelites and the angels together, or of the intruders of verse 4.

Genesis 19:20–23 shows that one nearby city, Zoar, was spared at Lot’s request. Deuteronomy 29:23 names the others destroyed with Sodom and Gomorrah: “Admah, and Zeboiim, which the LORD overthrew in His anger and His wrath.”

The Greek verb behind “given themselves over to sexual immorality” is an intensified form of the ordinary verb for sexual immorality. It occurs only here in the New Testament.

“Strange flesh” translates a Greek phrase meaning “other flesh” or “different flesh.” The NLT renders the verse “filled with immorality and every kind of sexual perversion.”

The Greek word translated “example” (deigma) means a sample or specimen put on display. The verb “set forth” pictures something lying exposed in public view.

Jude 1:8–10 — Dreamers Who Revile What They Do Not Know

Jude 1:8
Jude 1:8
NKJV

Jude 1:8 Likewise also these dreamers defile the flesh, reject authority, and speak evil of dignitaries.(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:8 In the same way, these people — who claim authority from their dreams — live immoral lives, defy authority, and scoff at supernatural beings. (NLT)

Main Idea

With “Likewise also these dreamers,” Jude applies the examples to the intruders: they “defile the flesh, reject authority, and speak evil of dignitaries.” Jude does not describe their dreams, though the label suggests men who appealed to visions or private insight to excuse what they did; his three charges match the old patterns of sexual corruption, rebellion against the Lord’s rule, and contempt for glorious angelic beings.

Verse Explanation

With “Likewise also these dreamers,” Jude applies the examples to the intruders: they “defile the flesh, reject authority, and speak evil of dignitaries.” Jude does not describe their dreams, though the label suggests men who appealed to visions or private insight to excuse what they did; his three charges match the old patterns of sexual corruption, rebellion against the Lord’s rule, and contempt for glorious angelic beings.

Likewise also these dreamers turns from the ancient examples to the intruders. Throughout the middle of the letter, Jude follows each example or prophecy with “these,” applying it to the men in the church (vv. 10, 12, 16, 19). Jude does not describe their dreams, but the word suggests men who appealed to visions or private spiritual experiences to justify what they did. The law of Moses had warned about “a dreamer of dreams” who used signs to lead Israel away from the Lord (Deuteronomy 13:1–5).

defile the flesh is the first charge. Like Sodom, these men pursued sexual corruption.

reject authority is the second. They refused to submit to the Lord’s rule, just as the unbelieving Israelites and the rebellious angels had refused it. This is what Jude meant when he said they deny the Lord (v. 4).

and speak evil of dignitaries is the third charge. “Dignitaries” here most likely refers to glorious angelic beings. These men spoke with contempt about spiritual powers far above them, things they did not understand (v. 10).

Study Notes

The Greek word translated “dignitaries” literally means “glories.” The NLT renders it “supernatural beings.” Most interpreters understand it of angels, but whether Jude means holy angels or fallen ones is debated.

The Greek word translated “authority” is related to the Greek word for “lord” and means lordship or dominion. It may refer to the lordship of Christ Himself, which these men rejected (v. 4).

The NLT expands “dreamers” as “who claim authority from their dreams.” That is an interpretation of the word rather than a separate reading in the Greek text.

Jude 1:9
NKJV

Jude 1:9 Yet Michael the archangel, in contending with the devil, when he disputed about the body of Moses, dared not bring against him a reviling accusation, but said, "The Lord rebuke you!"(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:9 But even Michael, one of the mightiest of the angels, did not dare accuse the devil of blasphemy, but simply said, “The Lord rebuke you!” (This took place when Michael was arguing with the devil about Moses’ body.) (NLT)

Main Idea

Michael the archangel, even while contending with the devil over the body of Moses, “dared not bring against him a reviling accusation, but said, ‘The Lord rebuke you!’” A mighty archangel left judgment to the Lord, which exposes how reckless these men are when they slander what is far above them.

Verse Explanation

Michael the archangel, even while contending with the devil over the body of Moses, “dared not bring against him a reviling accusation, but said, ‘The Lord rebuke you!’” A mighty archangel left judgment to the Lord, which exposes how reckless these men are when they slander what is far above them.

Yet Michael the archangel provides a striking contrast. Michael appears in Daniel as a great angelic prince who stands guard over God’s people (Daniel 10:13, 21; 12:1), and in Revelation 12:7 he leads God’s angels in battle. An archangel is a chief angel. If anyone could speak with authority against the devil, Michael could.

in contending with the devil, when he disputed about the body of Moses refers to an event the Old Testament does not record. Deuteronomy says only that Moses died in the land of Moab, that the Lord buried him, and that “no one knows his grave to this day” (Deuteronomy 34:5–6). Jude affirms that Michael and the devil disputed over Moses’ body, but he does not tell us what the dispute was about or how it ended. It is wiser not to guess.

dared not bring against him a reviling accusation is the heart of the verse. Michael would not pronounce an abusive judgment even against the devil. The devil did not deserve respect, but judgment belongs to God, and Michael would not take God’s place.

but said, “The Lord rebuke you!” shows what Michael did instead. He left the rebuke to the Lord. In Zechariah’s vision, when Satan stood to accuse Joshua the high priest, “the LORD said to Satan, ‘The LORD rebuke you, Satan!’” (Zechariah 3:1–2). The contrast with the intruders is plain. If a mighty archangel would not speak with contempt even toward the devil, men who mock glorious beings they do not understand are reckless indeed. Michael’s restraint does not forbid believers to resist evil in Christ’s authority. It forbids presumption and slander.

Study Notes

The word “archangel” appears only twice in the New Testament: here and in 1 Thessalonians 4:16, where “the voice of an archangel” accompanies the Lord’s return.

Early Christian writers, including Clement of Alexandria and Origen, said this account was also found in a Jewish writing known as the Assumption of Moses. The part of that work which would have contained the story has not survived. Jude’s reference affirms the event he describes. It does not make that writing Scripture or confirm its other contents.

The phrase “a reviling accusation” translates Greek words meaning “a judgment of slander” or “a slanderous verdict.” Michael refused to pass that kind of sentence on the devil himself.

Jude 1:10
Jude 1:10
NKJV

Jude 1:10 But these speak evil of whatever they do not know; and whatever they know naturally, like brute beasts, in these things they corrupt themselves.(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:10 But these people scoff at things they do not understand. Like unthinking animals, they do whatever their instincts tell them, and so they bring about their own destruction. (NLT)

Main Idea

“But these speak evil of whatever they do not know,” Jude continues, and what they do understand by instinct, “like brute beasts,” becomes the very thing that ruins them. Lacking Michael’s reverence, they mock spiritual realities beyond their grasp while their own appetites destroy them.

Verse Explanation

“But these speak evil of whatever they do not know,” Jude continues, and what they do understand by instinct, “like brute beasts,” becomes the very thing that ruins them. Lacking Michael’s reverence, they mock spiritual realities beyond their grasp while their own appetites destroy them.

But these speak evil of whatever they do not know draws out the contrast with Michael. The intruders mock spiritual realities they have never understood. Their confidence is the confidence of ignorance.

and whatever they know naturally, like brute beasts describes the one kind of knowledge they do have. They understand what animals understand: hunger, appetite and physical desire. Like animals without reason, they follow instinct.

in these things they corrupt themselves is the bitter irony. The very appetites they understand and follow become the means of their own ruin. They despise the spiritual realm they cannot see and are destroyed by the physical appetites they serve.

Study Notes

Jude uses two different Greek verbs for knowing in this verse. The first (oidasin, from oida) is the ordinary word for knowing. The second (epistantai) means to understand or be acquainted with something. The second verb does not by itself mean instinct. The word “naturally” and the comparison with animals show what kind of understanding Jude means: what these men grasp by bodily instinct, in contrast to the spiritual realities they do not know at all.

“Brute beasts” translates a Greek phrase meaning “unreasoning animals.” Peter uses the same phrase in his parallel description of false teachers (2 Peter 2:12).

The Greek verb translated “corrupt themselves” can also mean “are destroyed.” The NLT takes it that way: “they bring about their own destruction.”

Woe to them! is the language of the prophets announcing judgment and grief together. Jesus pronounced woes on the hypocrites of His day in the same way. Jude speaks it once, and it falls on men who follow three ancient patterns.

For they have gone in the way of Cain recalls the first murderer. Cain brought an offering God did not accept, and when God accepted his brother’s offering, Cain grew angry and killed him (Genesis 4:3–8). John writes that Cain “was of the wicked one and murdered his brother” (1 John 3:12). The way of Cain is religion without faith, ending in envy and hatred.

have run greedily in the error of Balaam for profit recalls the prophet who was hired to curse Israel (Numbers 22–24). God would not let him curse them, but Balaam later gave the counsel that led Israel into sexual immorality and idolatry at Peor (Numbers 25:1–3; 31:16; Revelation 2:14). Balaam sold spiritual service for money. The intruders, Jude says, rushed headlong into the same error.

and perished in the rebellion of Korah recalls the Levite who, with others, rose up against Moses and Aaron. Korah already served at the tabernacle, but he wanted the priesthood God had given to Aaron (Numbers 16:8–11). The earth opened and swallowed the rebels (Numbers 16:31–33). Jude speaks of the intruders as having already “perished” in Korah’s rebellion. Their end is so certain that he describes it as accomplished.

Study Notes

All three verbs in this verse share the same Greek past tense, but they do not all describe the same thing. The first two, “have gone” and “have run greedily,” describe the course the intruders have chosen. The third, “perished,” describes their end, and Jude speaks of it as already accomplished because it is certain.

The Greek verb translated “run greedily” literally means “were poured out.” It pictures men abandoning themselves completely to Balaam’s course.

The Greek word translated “error” (planē) also means wandering. It belongs to the same word family as the “wandering stars” in verse 13.

The Greek word translated “rebellion” literally means “speaking against” or “contradiction.” Korah’s rebellion began with words against Moses and Aaron (Numbers 16:3), which fits Jude’s repeated concern with the intruders’ speech (vv. 8, 10, 15–16).

Now Enoch, the seventh from Adam introduces a prophecy from before the flood. Counting Adam as the first generation, Enoch was the seventh in the line through Seth (Genesis 5:3–18). Genesis says he “walked with God; and he was not, for God took him” (Genesis 5:24). Jude shows that God’s judgment on the ungodly was announced from the earliest generations of human history.

prophesied about these men also, saying shows how Jude applies the prophecy. Enoch did not name these first-century men. He prophesied about ungodly people and their judgment, and Jude, writing by the Spirit, applies that prophecy to the intruders because they fit its description.

Behold, the Lord comes with ten thousands of His saints opens the prophecy. “Ten thousands” means a vast, uncountable host. “Saints” means holy ones, which in this setting may include the angels who accompany the Lord as well as His people. Read with the rest of the New Testament, the prophecy looks toward the return of Christ, who will come “with all His saints” (1 Thessalonians 3:13).

Study Notes

Genesis also names an Enoch in Cain’s line, Cain’s son (Genesis 4:17). The words “the seventh from Adam” identify the Enoch of Seth’s line, the man who walked with God.

A close parallel to this prophecy appears in 1 Enoch 1:9, part of an ancient Jewish collection preserved in full in Ethiopic, with Aramaic fragments found among the Dead Sea Scrolls. Jude receives the prophecy he quotes as true. His use of it does not make the whole collection Scripture, and the history of how the saying was passed down is not fully known.

The Greek verb behind “comes” is actually in a past tense, “came.” Prophets sometimes described future events as if they had already happened because they were certain.

Moses used similar language when he described the Lord coming from Sinai to give the law: “He came with ten thousands of saints” (Deuteronomy 33:2).

Verse Explanation

Others must be saved “with fear, pulling them out of the fire,” rescued urgently as from flames, while believers go on “hating even the garment defiled by the flesh.” Love moves toward the endangered person yet refuses the sin that stains, with a sober awareness of how easily it contaminates.

but others save with fear calls for more urgent action toward people in greater danger. Believers are not the source of salvation; God saves. But He uses His people as His instruments to bring others back. The fear Jude mentions is a sober awareness of how serious sin is and how easily it contaminates those who come near it.

pulling them out of the fire pictures a rescue from flames. The fire recalls the judgment of verse 7. Some people are already close to it, and there is no time for slow persuasion. They must be snatched away.

hating even the garment defiled by the flesh sets the boundary for the rescue. The garment is clothing stained by the sinful nature. Believers move toward the endangered person with love, but they hate the sin that stains, and they refuse to be contaminated by it. Hating the garment does not mean hating or humiliating the person who wears it.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “pulling them out” means to snatch or seize quickly. The picture resembles Zechariah 3:2, where the Lord calls Joshua the high priest “a brand plucked from the fire,” and Zechariah 3:3–4, where Joshua’s filthy garments are taken away. The same chapter contains the words “The Lord rebuke you!” (Zechariah 3:2), quoted in verse 9.

The Greek word translated “garment” refers to the tunic worn next to the body. Clothing in direct contact with defiled skin makes a vivid picture of how near and how contaminating sin can be.

The NLT, following the three-group manuscript tradition, reads: “Rescue others by snatching them from the flames of judgment. Show mercy to still others, but do so with great caution, hating the sins that contaminate their lives.”

Jude 1:24–25 — Glory to the God Who Is Able to Keep

Jude 1:24
Jude 1:24
NKJV

Jude 1:24 Now to Him who is able to keep you from stumbling, And to present you faultless Before the presence of His glory with exceeding joy,(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:24 Now all glory to God, who is able to keep you from falling away and will bring you with great joy into his glorious presence without a single fault. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jude closes in praise “to Him who is able to keep you from stumbling, And to present you faultless Before the presence of His glory with exceeding joy.” A letter of sober warning ends with God’s power: He is able to guard His people now and bring them blameless and rejoicing into His presence.

Verse Explanation

Jude closes in praise “to Him who is able to keep you from stumbling, And to present you faultless Before the presence of His glory with exceeding joy.” A letter of sober warning ends with God’s power: He is able to guard His people now and bring them blameless and rejoicing into His presence.

Now to Him who is able turns the letter to praise. After all the warnings, Jude rests the hope of his readers on God’s ability, not on their own strength.

to keep you from stumbling describes what God is able to do now. He can guard His people so that they do not fall away. The promise does not mean believers will never sin. It means God is able to keep them standing to the end, even in a church troubled by corruption.

And to present you faultless describes what God will do at the end. He will bring His people before Himself without fault, without blemish. The intruders were blemishes on the church’s fellowship (v. 12); God will present His people with no blemish at all.

Before the presence of His glory names where they will stand. They will come into the full presence of God’s glory, and they will stand there because God has made them fit to be there.

with exceeding joy describes the gladness of that day. The meeting that would bring terror to the ungodly (vv. 14–15) will bring overflowing joy to those God has kept.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “keep” in this verse is not tēreō, used in verses 1, 6, 13 and 21. It is a different Greek verb (phylassō) meaning to guard or protect, as a sentry guards what is entrusted to him.

The Greek word translated “faultless” (amōmos) means without blemish. It was used for sacrificial animals that had no defect, and Peter uses it of Christ, “a lamb without blemish and without spot” (1 Peter 1:19).

Jude 1:25
Jude 1:25
NKJV

Jude 1:25 To God our Savior, Who alone is wise, Be glory and majesty, Dominion and power, Both now and forever. Amen.(NKJV)

NLT

Jude 1:25 All glory to him who alone is God, our Savior through Jesus Christ our Lord. All glory, majesty, power, and authority are his before all time, and in the present, and beyond all time! Amen. (NLT)

Main Idea

To “God our Savior, Who alone is wise,” belong “glory and majesty, Dominion and power, Both now and forever.” Jude gives the last word to worship, ascribing every honor to the God who saves and keeps, and seals it with “Amen.”

Verse Explanation

To “God our Savior, Who alone is wise,” belong “glory and majesty, Dominion and power, Both now and forever.” Jude gives the last word to worship, ascribing every honor to the God who saves and keeps, and seals it with “Amen.”

To God our Savior identifies the One who receives the praise. God Himself is the Savior of His people. Paul uses the same title in his letters to Timothy and Titus (1 Timothy 1:1; Titus 1:3).

Who alone is wise declares that God alone possesses perfect wisdom. Against men who mocked what they did not understand (v. 10), Jude sets the God who understands all things and needs no one’s instruction.

Be glory and majesty, Dominion and power ascribes to God four things that belong to Him. Glory is the splendor of who He is. Majesty is His greatness. Dominion is His rule over all things. Power is His ability to do all that He wills. The intruders rejected authority (v. 8); Jude gladly gives all authority to God.

Both now and forever. Amen. extends that praise through all time. The letter that began with God’s keeping ends with God’s glory, and Jude seals it with “Amen,” a Hebrew word meaning “truly” or “so be it.”

Study Notes

The NKJV follows the Greek text behind the King James Version, which gives the praise to the only wise God our Savior. Many other manuscripts lack the word “wise” and add “through Jesus Christ our Lord” and “before all time.” The NLT reflects those manuscripts: “All glory to him who alone is God, our Savior through Jesus Christ our Lord. All glory, majesty, power, and authority are his before all time, and in the present, and beyond all time! Amen.”

A similar doxology closes Paul’s letter to the Romans: “to God, alone wise, be glory through Jesus Christ forever. Amen” (Romans 16:27).
