Book Overview

At a Glance

Matthew
Author

The Gospel does not name its writer. Church tradition from the second century credits it to Matthew, the tax collector Jesus called to follow Him (9:9; 10:3).

Audience

Not named. The genealogy from Abraham, the steady appeal to Israel’s Scriptures, and Jewish customs mentioned with little explanation suggest readers at home in Israel’s faith, possibly congregations with many Jewish believers, whom the Gospel also teaches to welcome the nations.

Date

Not stated and uncertain. Many place it in the later decades of the first century, but the date depends on debated questions about Matthew’s relation to Mark and to the temple’s destruction in AD 70.

Historical Setting

Galilee and Judea under Roman rule, from the days of Herod the Great to the governorship of Pontius Pilate, with the Jerusalem temple and its leaders at the center of Israel’s religious life.

Purpose

To show that Jesus is the promised King who fulfills Israel’s Scriptures, and to train His disciples to obey His teaching and carry it to all the nations.

Central Message

Jesus is the promised King who fulfills Israel’s Scriptures, gives His life to redeem His people, and rises with all authority. He gathers disciples from Israel and all nations to live under His reign in every aspect of life as they await His return.

Key Verse

“All authority has been given to Me in heaven and on earth. Go therefore and make disciples of all the nations” (28:18–19).

Key Themes

The kingdom of heaven (4:17; 6:10; 13:1–52); Jesus the Son of David and Son of God (1:1; 16:16); Scripture fulfilled (1:22–23; 5:17); a righteousness of the heart (5:20; 6:1–18); the authority of Jesus (7:29; 9:6; 28:18); the cost of following Him (16:24; 20:26–28); Israel and the nations (10:6; 28:19); readiness for His return (24:42–25:46); God with us (1:23; 28:20).

Book Flow

  • I. The Promised King Comes (Matthew 1:1–4:16) — Jesus’ ancestry, birth, baptism, and testing reveal who He is.

  • II. The King Announces the Kingdom in Word and Deed (Matthew 4:17–11:1) — The Sermon on the Mount, mighty works, and the sending of the Twelve.

  • III. The King Meets Rejection and Recognition (Matthew 11:2–16:20) — Opposition grows, the parables explain it, and Peter confesses the Christ.

  • IV. The King Teaches the Way of the Cross (Matthew 16:21–20:34) — Jesus foretells His death and teaches His disciples humility, forgiveness, and service.

  • V. The King Confronts Jerusalem (Matthew 21:1–23:39) — The King enters the city, cleanses the temple, and exposes its leaders.

  • VI. The King Promises His Return and Calls for Readiness (Matthew 24:1–25:46) — The temple’s fall, the Son of Man’s coming, and parables of readiness.

  • VII. The King Gives His Life and Rises with All Authority (Matthew 26:1–28:20) — The covenant meal, the cross, the resurrection, and the commission.

Go Deeper

Background

Author

Like the other Gospels, Matthew does not name its writer. From the second century the church has credited it to Matthew, one of the Twelve. Only this Gospel calls him “Matthew the tax collector” (10:3), and only here is his call told under that name: Jesus saw him “sitting at the tax office” and said, “Follow Me” (9:9). Mark and Luke tell the same story about a man they call Levi (Mark 2:14; Luke 5:27).

Right after Matthew’s call, Jesus sits at table with “many tax collectors and sinners” (9:10). When the Pharisees object, He answers, “I did not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance” (9:13). The scene shows Jesus welcoming the very people others kept at a distance, and it places a tax collector among His closest followers.

Audience

Matthew does not name his first readers, but the book leaves clues. It opens with a genealogy that runs from Abraham through David, quotes Israel’s Scriptures again and again, and mentions “the tradition of the elders” about washing hands (15:2) with less explanation than Mark gives for the same custom (Mark 7:3–4). These features suggest readers at home in Israel’s Scriptures and customs, perhaps congregations with many Jewish believers, though the book does not describe them directly.

Yet this same Gospel looks beyond Israel from its first pages, when wise men from the East come to worship the newborn King (2:1–12), and it ends with the command to make disciples “of all the nations” (28:19). Whoever its first readers were, Matthew teaches them to see Jesus as Israel’s promised King and as the Lord whose reign reaches every people.

Date

Matthew gives no date, and none can be fixed with certainty. Many interpreters place the book in the later decades of the first century. Much depends on two debated questions: how Matthew relates to Mark’s Gospel, which many scholars think Matthew used, and whether it was written before or after the Romans destroyed the temple in AD 70, the event Jesus foretells in 24:2. The book’s message does not depend on settling a precise year.

Historical Setting

Jesus is born “in the days of Herod the king” (2:1), the king Rome had set over the Jews, who died around 4 BC. After Herod’s death his son Archelaus ruled Judea (2:22), and another son, Herod Antipas, ruled Galilee. Matthew calls him “Herod the tetrarch” (14:1), and he is the ruler who imprisons and executes John the Baptist (14:1–12). By the time of Jesus’ trial Judea is governed directly by a Roman official, and the chief priests and elders hand Jesus over “to Pontius Pilate the governor” (27:1–2) to be put to death.

Most of Jesus’ ministry takes place in Galilee, in towns around its lake, with Capernaum as His base (4:13). Isaiah had called the region “Galilee of the Gentiles” (4:15), and in Jesus’ day it still had a mixed population of Jews and Gentiles. Jerusalem, in Judea, was the center of Israel’s worship. Its temple was the place of sacrifice and pilgrimage, especially at Passover, when the city filled with worshipers. Jesus’ final week falls at that feast (26:2, 17), so His entry into the city and His actions in the temple take place amid the Passover crowds.

Many in Israel were longing for God to send the promised Son of David to restore the kingdom. When Jesus heals, the crowds ask, “Could this be the Son of David?” (12:23). Matthew shows that Jesus is that King, but His reign comes by a road no one expected: through mercy to the outcast, service, and a cross.

Occasion

Matthew does not say why he wrote, but the shape of the book points to its purposes. First, it answers the question of whether Jesus really is the Messiah Israel’s Scriptures promised. Matthew traces His ancestry, records repeatedly that events happened “that it might be fulfilled,” and shows the leaders rejecting Him. He also reports that the story of the disciples stealing Jesus’ body “is commonly reported among the Jews until this day” (28:15), so the question was still being argued when he wrote.

Second, Matthew writes to form disciples. The risen Jesus sends His followers out, “teaching them to observe all things that I have commanded you” (28:20), and Matthew gathers that teaching into five large blocks. The book itself supplies what the church needed in order to obey that command.

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Matthew tells the story of Jesus’ birth, ministry, death, and resurrection as the story of a King. The first verse calls Him “the Son of David” (1:1). Wise men come looking for “He who has been born King of the Jews” (2:2). He enters Jerusalem as the King who comes “Lowly, and sitting on a donkey” (21:5), dies under the charge “THE KING OF THE JEWS” (27:37), and rises to declare, “All authority has been given to Me in heaven and on earth” (28:18). Kingship is the thread that ties the whole book together.

The King brings a kingdom. Jesus’ first message is “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand” (4:17), and His healings and deliverances show that God’s reign has arrived in Him. When He frees a man who was blind and mute (12:22), He explains it as binding “the strong man” and plundering his house (12:29), and He declares, “If I cast out demons by the Spirit of God, surely the kingdom of God has come upon you” (12:28). The Pharisees call that rescue the work of Beelzebub (12:24), and Jesus warns that blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven (12:31–32). In that scene the accusation deliberately turns the Spirit’s testimony to Jesus upside down, calling His rescue the devil’s work. Blasphemy against the Spirit is the willful, conscious, and final rejection of the Spirit’s revelation of Christ, not a careless or fearful remark. Grief over sin and a desire to turn to Christ are grounds for reassurance, and those who feel them are invited to come to Him for forgiveness. This kingdom does not overthrow Rome. It claims the hearts, bodies, relationships, and possessions of those who receive it.

A large share of the book is Jesus’ own teaching, gathered into five blocks: the Sermon on the Mount (5–7), the sending of the Twelve (10), the parables of the kingdom (13), instruction for life together as His disciples (18), and His teaching on the Mount of Olives about the future (24–25). He speaks “as one having authority” (7:29) and calls for a righteousness that exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees (5:20), the Father’s will carried into anger, desire, speech, prayer, money, and love for enemies.

All along the way people respond to Jesus. Crowds marvel, the sick and the outcast come to Him, His disciples follow and falter, and opposition grows, first from the Pharisees and scribes and finally from the chief priests and elders in Jerusalem (12:14; 21:45–46; 26:3–4). The story reaches its goal in Jerusalem, where the King gives “His life a ransom for many” (20:28), pours out His blood “for the remission of sins” (26:28), and rises again. The book that began with “God with us” (1:23) ends with the risen Jesus’ promise, “I am with you always” (28:20).

Why This Book Matters

Matthew stands at the head of the New Testament, and it binds the two Testaments together. Its first line reaches back to Abraham and David, the two men to whom God made His great promises: through Abraham’s Seed all nations would be blessed (Genesis 22:18), and from David’s line would come a King whose throne would last forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16). Matthew shows that God kept His word in Jesus, and that the same God who spoke through Israel’s prophets has now acted in His Son.

Matthew also gives the church much of the teaching it lives by. The Sermon on the Mount, the Lord’s Prayer (6:9–13), the Great Commandment (22:37–40), and the Great Commission (28:18–20) all come to us through this book, many of them in their fullest form. Among the Gospels only Matthew records Jesus using the word “church” (16:18; 18:17), and chapter 18 shows Him forming a community that practices humility, seeks out the straying, corrects with the aim of restoration, and forgives from the heart.

Finally, Matthew shows that the King’s reign leaves no part of life untouched. Jesus speaks to the motives behind actions, the use of money (6:19–24), marriage (19:3–12), worry (6:25–34), and the way disciples treat “the least of these My brethren” (25:40). The kingdom is not a private religious corner of life. It is the rule of a gracious King over everything He has redeemed.

Book Flow

I. The Promised King Comes (Matthew 1:1–4:16)

Matthew opens with a genealogy that presents Jesus as “the Son of David, the Son of Abraham” (1:1). The angel announces that He “will save His people from their sins” (1:21), and Matthew identifies His birth as the fulfillment of Isaiah’s word about Immanuel (1:22–23). The Father’s voice at the Jordan, Jesus’ victory over the devil in the wilderness, and His move to Capernaum prepare for His public ministry.

II. The King Announces the Kingdom in Word and Deed (Matthew 4:17–11:1)

“From that time Jesus began to preach” (4:17) marks the first great turning point of the book. Jesus calls His first disciples, teaches the Sermon on the Mount, and then shows the same authority in deeds: healing, calming the storm, forgiving sins, and calling sinners (8–9). Moved with compassion for crowds “like sheep having no shepherd” (9:36), He sends the Twelve to Israel with His message and power.

III. The King Meets Rejection and Recognition (Matthew 11:2–16:20)

John the Baptist’s question from prison, “Are You the Coming One, or do we look for another?” (11:3), opens a movement in which Jesus divides His hearers. The Pharisees plot to destroy Him (12:14), while the Father reveals the truth to “babes” (11:25). The parables of chapter 13 explain these different responses, and the movement closes with Peter’s confession, “You are the Christ, the Son of the living God” (16:16).

IV. The King Teaches the Way of the Cross (Matthew 16:21–20:34)

“From that time Jesus began to show to His disciples that He must go to Jerusalem” (16:21) marks the second great turning point. Jesus repeatedly foretells His suffering, death, and resurrection, and He calls His followers to “deny himself, and take up his cross” (16:24). On the road to Jerusalem He teaches about humility, forgiveness, marriage, wealth, and service, for He came “to serve, and to give His life a ransom for many” (20:28).

V. The King Confronts Jerusalem (Matthew 21:1–23:39)

Jesus enters Jerusalem to the crowd’s “Hosanna to the Son of David!” (21:9), drives the merchants from the temple, and answers the chief priests and elders who challenge His authority with parables of judgment (21:23). He warns that “the kingdom of God will be taken from you and given to a nation bearing the fruits of it” (21:43), and the chief priests and Pharisees perceive “that He was speaking of them” (21:45). He pronounces woes on the scribes and Pharisees and laments over Jerusalem: “See! Your house is left to you desolate” (23:38).

VI. The King Promises His Return and Calls for Readiness (Matthew 24:1–25:46)

Leaving the temple, Jesus foretells that “not one stone shall be left here upon another” (24:2). That warning was fulfilled historically when Jerusalem fell to Rome in AD 70, and that judgment foreshadows the final judgment. Jesus prepares His disciples for deception and persecution and describes the coming of the Son of Man, best read as still future, though its timing in relation to Jerusalem’s fall is left open (24:29–31). Parables of the faithful servant, the ten virgins, and the talents call for readiness, and the scene of the King judging the nations closes the last of Jesus’ great discourses.

VII. The King Gives His Life and Rises with All Authority (Matthew 26:1–28:20)

The final movement tells of the Last Supper, Gethsemane, Jesus’ arrest and trials, His crucifixion, and His burial. He cries out, “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” (27:46), and a Roman centurion confesses, “Truly this was the Son of God!” (27:54). On the third day the angel announces, “He is risen, as He said” (28:6), and the risen King sends His disciples to make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them and teaching them to observe all He commanded, with the promise of His presence (28:19–20).

Key Themes to Watch For

The kingdom of heaven. God’s reign, already at work in Jesus and awaiting its full revelation, is the subject of His preaching, prayer, and parables (4:17; 6:10; 12:28; 13:1–52; 25:31–34).

The King and His identity. Jesus is the Son of David, the Son of God, the Son of Man, and Immanuel, and the question “Who is this?” runs through the book (1:1, 23; 16:13–16; 21:10; 26:63–64).

Scripture fulfilled. Events in Jesus’ life happen “that it might be fulfilled,” and His teaching brings the Law and the Prophets to their purpose (1:22; 2:15; 5:17; 8:17; 26:56).

A righteousness of the heart. The Father’s will reaches anger, desire, speech, prayer, and money, not just outward actions (5:20–48; 6:1–34; 15:8–20).

The authority of Jesus. He teaches, heals, forgives, commands storms and spirits, and finally holds all authority in heaven and on earth (7:29; 8:27; 9:6; 21:23; 28:18).

Faith and little faith. Outsiders show great faith while disciples are rebuked for little faith, and both are met by Jesus’ mercy (8:10, 26; 14:31; 15:28; 17:20).

Mercy. Jesus quotes “I desire mercy and not sacrifice” twice and shows mercy to the sick, the blind, and sinners (9:13; 12:7; 9:27; 20:30–34).

Israel and the nations. The King goes first to “the lost sheep of the house of Israel” (10:6; 15:24). His warning in 21:43 is aimed at the chief priests and Pharisees, who perceive that He is speaking of them (21:45); the fruit-bearing people are those Jesus gathers around Himself, beginning within Israel and welcoming the nations (8:11). The commission to all the nations includes Israel and fulfills God’s promise to bless all nations through Abraham (Genesis 22:18; 28:19).

Readiness for His return. Disciples are called to watch and to serve faithfully until the King comes (24:42–44; 25:1–13, 14–30).

God with us. Jesus’ presence frames the book and stays with His gathered people (1:23; 18:20; 28:20).

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

The kingdom of heaven. Matthew uses this phrase more than thirty times. It names the same reign as “the kingdom of God” (19:23–24). The word heaven in that phrase was a reverent Jewish way of referring to God. God rules over all things, yet His kingdom is also present wherever Jesus’ authority is at work and willingly received (12:28), and its glory will be revealed openly when He returns (13:43; 25:31–34). Readers will meet sayings about the kingdom’s present work and its future glory, and both are true.

Christ, Messiah, and Son of David. The title Christ comes from a Greek word meaning anointed one, the same meaning as the Hebrew word Messiah. It became the title for the promised King from David’s family line (2 Samuel 7:12–16). That is why “Son of David” is a royal title in Matthew, and why the blind and the needy cry out to Jesus by that name (9:27; 15:22; 20:30).

Son of Man. This is the title Jesus uses for Himself more than any other. It recalls Daniel’s vision of “One like the Son of Man” coming with the clouds of heaven to receive an everlasting kingdom (Daniel 7:13–14). Jesus uses it for His present authority to forgive sins (9:6), for His suffering (20:28), and for His future coming, when all will “see the Son of Man coming on the clouds of heaven with power and great glory” (24:30). On trial before the high priest He says that His judges will see Him “sitting at the right hand of the Power, and coming on the clouds of heaven” (26:64), claiming both a place of rule beside God and His return as Judge.

“That it might be fulfilled” (1:22). Matthew often introduces a Scripture with this formula. Fulfillment comes in more than one kind. Some passages foretell an event directly, such as Micah’s prophecy of a Ruler from Bethlehem (2:5–6; Micah 5:2). Others describe a pattern in Israel’s story that Jesus completes, such as Hosea’s words about God calling Israel out of Egypt (2:15; Hosea 11:1). It helps to read the passage Matthew cites in its own setting and then ask how Jesus fulfills it.

The Law and the Prophets. This is Matthew’s name for the Scriptures of Israel, our Old Testament (5:17; 7:12; 22:40). Jesus did not come “to destroy but to fulfill” them (5:17). A command is first understood in its own setting. Jesus then brings its purpose to completion in His person, His saving work, and His authoritative teaching, as when He carries the command against murder to the anger of the heart (5:21–22). The obedience He calls for is enabled by the Spirit, as the new covenant promised (Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 36:26–27).

Righteousness. In Matthew this usually means doing the will of God the Father, from the heart and in conduct (3:15; 5:6, 20; 6:1, 33).

Israel’s leaders. The Pharisees were a movement devoted to strict keeping of the Law and of “the tradition of the elders” (15:2). The Sadducees, linked with the priestly families, “say there is no resurrection” (22:23). Scribes were experts in the Law. The chief priests and elders led the temple and sat on the council that tried Jesus (26:59). Matthew names these groups precisely, and the distinctions matter when Jesus rebukes them.

Tax collectors. Tax collectors gathered tolls for the ruling powers and were widely despised as collaborators and cheats. Jesus’ welcome of them (9:10–11; 11:19; 21:31) showed the reach of His mercy.

Parables. Jesus’ parables are stories from ordinary life that reveal the kingdom to those who receive His word and conceal it from those who refuse it (13:10–17). Begin with each story’s setting and main point. Jesus Himself explains some of them (13:18–23, 36–43), and their images can also carry further symbolic meaning that rewards careful study.

The five teaching blocks. Five times Matthew closes a long stretch of Jesus’ teaching with nearly the same words: “when Jesus had ended these sayings” or a similar phrase (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1). The last time he writes “all these sayings” (26:1), and the story turns to the cross.

Key people. John the Baptist prepares the way and calls Israel to repent; his imprisonment and death foreshadow the cost of faithful witness (3:1–12; 14:1–12). Jesus gives the Twelve real authority (10:1), and they also fail Him. Peter confesses Jesus as the Christ, resists the way of the cross, and later denies Him (16:16, 22; 26:69–75).

Christ and Life

Matthew begins and ends with the presence of God. Jesus is named Immanuel, “God with us” (1:23), and the risen Lord promises, “I am with you always, even to the end of the age” (28:20). Between those two promises Jesus says that where two or three gather in His name, “I am there in the midst of them” (18:20). God’s desire to dwell with His people is fulfilled in a Person, and that Person goes with His disciples into every nation they are sent to reach.

The King of Matthew’s Gospel rules by giving Himself. He “did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life a ransom for many” (20:28). At the Last Supper He gives the cup as His blood “of the new covenant, which is shed for many for the remission of sins” (26:28). On the cross He cries, “My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” (27:46). These are the opening words of Psalm 22, and His anguish and forsakenness are real as He bears judgment in our place. The psalm itself moves from that cry to trust and vindication, and the resurrection is God’s answer: the forsaken One is raised and given all authority.

Matthew also shows the reach of Christ’s saving work into the body. When Jesus heals the sick, Matthew writes that this fulfilled Isaiah’s word, “He Himself took our infirmities And bore our sicknesses” (8:17). His healings showed the Servant already lifting the burdens He came to bear. The Holy Spirit still gives gifts of healings as He wills (1 Corinthians 12:9–11), so believers can pray with bold expectation, while complete and lasting bodily restoration awaits the resurrection (Romans 8:23).

Entering the kingdom and living in it are both the Spirit’s work. Matthew shows the Spirit throughout Jesus’ own life: He is conceived by the Holy Spirit (1:18, 20), anointed by the Spirit at His baptism (3:16), led by the Spirit into the wilderness (4:1), and casts out demons “by the Spirit of God” (12:28). John promised that He would “baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire” (3:11). John’s Gospel adds that no one can see or enter the kingdom of God without being born of the Spirit (John 3:3–8), and that new birth is where life under Matthew’s King begins. The same Spirit then enables the obedience Jesus calls for. The demands of the Sermon on the Mount match the new covenant promises of a changed heart and God’s Spirit within His people (Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 36:26–27), and Jesus tells His persecuted disciples that the Spirit of their Father will speak through them (10:20). The King who commands also gives rest: “Take My yoke upon you and learn from Me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart” (11:29).

Jesus builds His church on the truth Peter confessed, which the Father revealed (16:16–18). He entrusts the keys of the kingdom to His people: authority to act on His behalf in matters of access to the kingdom (16:19). In proclamation the church announces the King’s forgiveness to all who repent and trust Him and warns that those who reject Him remain in their sins (John 20:21–23). In discipline it acts under His word in matters of fellowship, beginning with a private appeal and aiming at repentance and restoration (18:15–20).

Final judgment belongs to the King. When He comes in glory, He says that what was done “to one of the least of these My brethren” was done to Him (25:40). In Matthew His brethren are His disciples (12:49–50; 28:10), and those sent in His name are received as He is received (10:40–42). The nations’ treatment of these vulnerable messengers makes their response to the King Himself visible. Scripture’s wider call to show mercy to all in need also stands: “He who has pity on the poor lends to the LORD” (Proverbs 19:17).

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

The kingdom has two sides that belong together. It is already at work through Jesus and among those who obey Him, and it still awaits its public glory when He returns. Reading only one side flattens the book’s message.

The Sermon on the Mount is neither a ladder for earning the kingdom nor an ideal no one is meant to live. It begins with blessing for “the poor in spirit” (5:3) and describes the life of those who belong to the King and are enabled by His Spirit.

Matthew’s warnings are addressed to people who call Jesus “Lord, Lord” (7:21–23; 24:45–51). They keep their full force. Obedience and endurance show true allegiance, but not every warning is meant to settle every question about who will persevere.

Jesus’ sharpest words are aimed at particular leaders, and Matthew names them (21:45; 23:2). Jesus, His disciples, and the first church were Jewish, and the mission to all nations includes Israel. Passages such as 21:43 and 27:25 must not be turned into a verdict on the Jewish people as a whole.

Forgiveness from the heart is something a disciple gives even if the offender never repents (18:21–35). Reconciliation and restored trust require repentance and changed conduct (18:15). Turning the other cheek concerns personal insult and humiliation (5:38–39); it does not forbid protecting people from violence.

When healing does not come, Matthew gives no basis for concluding that the sick person lacks faith or hides some sin. Its picture of Jesus leaves room both for expectant prayer and for patient, faithful care.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

Matthew and the other Gospels. Matthew, Mark, and Luke share a great deal of material and are often read side by side. Many scholars think Matthew drew on Mark, though the exact relationship is debated. Some of Matthew’s material appears in no other Gospel, including the visit of the wise men, the parables of the hidden treasure, the pearl, the unforgiving servant, the laborers in the vineyard, and the ten virgins, the guard at the tomb, and the commission on the mountain in Galilee.

An early report. Eusebius, the fourth-century church historian, quotes Papias, an early second-century church leader, as saying that Matthew put together the oracles in the Hebrew language (Church History 3.39.16). The word translated oracles may refer to Jesus’ sayings or to something broader. What Papias was describing, and how it relates to the Greek Gospel we have, remains uncertain.

Verses missing from some early copies. The closing words of the Lord’s Prayer, “For Yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever. Amen” (6:13), are absent from many early copies of Matthew, as are 17:21 and 18:11. Similar sayings appear elsewhere (Mark 9:29; Luke 19:10), and no teaching in Matthew depends on these verses alone.

Binding and loosing. In 16:19 and 18:18 the wording can also be translated to say that what the church binds or looses on earth will already have been bound or loosed in heaven. Either way, the church acts under the King’s word and does not decide heaven’s verdict on its own.

Questions that remain open. Matthew says the saying “He shall be called a Nazarene” was spoken “by the prophets” (2:23) without naming a single passage, and its exact source is uncertain. In 10:23 Jesus tells the Twelve, “you will not have gone through the cities of Israel before the Son of Man comes”; which coming He means is left open, while the urgency of witness and endurance is plain. In 24:34 “this generation” is best taken in its ordinary sense of Jesus’ own contemporaries, though how that statement relates to the timing of the events described in 24:29–31 remains debated.
