Book Questions

Are “the kingdom of heaven” and “the kingdom of God” different? (Matthew 19:23–24)

No. Matthew usually says “kingdom of heaven,” while Mark and Luke say “kingdom of God,” and Matthew himself uses both for the same reign in back-to-back sentences (19:23–24). “Heaven” was a reverent Jewish way of referring to God.

God rules everywhere. His kingdom is also already at work through Jesus and is willingly received by those who turn to Him: “if I cast out demons by the Spirit of God, surely the kingdom of God has come upon you” (12:28). Its glory will be publicly revealed when the King returns (25:31–34). These belong together: His unbroken sovereignty, His saving rule received now, and the kingdom’s future fullness.

Jesus’ reign reaches the whole person and every sphere of life. Praying for His kingdom to come includes asking Him to reign in our motives, conduct, relationships, possessions, and service (6:9–13, 33). His power to heal and free captives, His care for His people, and His mission to the nations also express that reign (10:1–8; 28:18–20).

Read: Matthew 19:23–24, Matthew 6:9–13, Matthew 6:33, Matthew 10:1–8, Matthew 12:28, Matthew 13:43, Matthew 25:31–34, Matthew 28:18–20

Did Matthew, the tax collector, write this Gospel?

The Gospel does not name its author, but the early church consistently attributed it to Matthew, the tax collector Jesus called (9:9; 10:3). MTB holds to that traditional attribution, though many modern scholars question it. The Gospel is deeply rooted in Israel’s Scriptures: Matthew presents Jesus as “the Son of David, the Son of Abraham” (1:1), Israel’s Messiah and King.

Read: Matthew 1:1, Matthew 9:9, Matthew 10:2–3

Do Jesus’ warnings to disciples mean a believer can lose salvation? (Matthew 7:21–23; 24:45–51)

Jesus warns professing disciples directly: “Not everyone who says to Me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ shall enter the kingdom of heaven, but he who does the will of My Father in heaven” (7:21). Some will be told, “I never knew you” (7:23). Other warnings speak of those who fall away under pressure (13:20–21) and the unfaithful servant (24:45–51).

Believers are secure in Christ, and Jesus’ warnings call professing disciples to real obedience and endurance. In 7:23 He explicitly identifies people He never knew. That conclusion does not automatically describe everyone in another warning. Where a passage leaves a person’s earlier standing unclear, the warning still calls us to repentance and perseverance. Obedience grows from grace and expresses allegiance to the King; it does not purchase His acceptance (Ephesians 2:8–10).

Read: Matthew 7:21–27, Matthew 13:20–23, Matthew 24:45–51, Matthew 10:22, Ephesians 2:8–10 · Related: The oil in the ten virgins

Is entering the kingdom the same as adopting a better way of life?

Entering the kingdom begins with life received from God. John announces the Messiah who gives the Spirit, and Jesus calls people to repent and follow Him (3:1–12; 4:17–22). John 3:3–8 explains the new birth through which a person sees and enters God’s kingdom. The Spirit gives life; improved behavior or religious association cannot supply it.

That new life grows into willing obedience under Jesus’ reign. The Word, prayer, obedience, and life among His people nourish growth (Colossians 2:6–7; Ephesians 4:11–16). Acceptance in Christ is received by grace, and His life bears fruit in conduct. Growth follows the gift of life rather than earning it (Ephesians 2:8–10).

Read: Matthew 3:1–12, Matthew 4:17–22, John 3:3–8, Colossians 2:6–7, Ephesians 4:11–16, Ephesians 2:8–10 · Related: The kingdom of heaven and the kingdom of God · Jesus’ warnings and assurance

I. The Promised King Comes (Matthew 1:1–4:16)

Matthew 1 · 1 question

Why does Matthew’s genealogy skip generations and include four women? (vv. 1–17)

Matthew arranges Jesus’ family line in three sets of fourteen generations: from Abraham to David, from David to the exile in Babylon, and from the exile to the Christ (v. 17). In Jewish genealogies, “begot” can trace a line of descent without naming every generation. Matthew gives a structured royal lineage, not a complete list.

Unusually for an ancient genealogy, Matthew names four women before Mary: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and Bathsheba, “her who had been the wife of Uriah” (vv. 3, 5–6). Each shows God’s faithful grace working through a broken human history and reaching beyond Israel. Rahab was a Canaanite and Ruth a Moabite, both welcomed into Israel’s story. The line leads to the One who “will save His people from their sins” (v. 21).

Read: Matthew 1:1–17, Matthew 1:21, Genesis 22:18, 2 Samuel 7:12–16, Ruth 4:13–22

Matthew 2 · 2 questions

How is “Out of Egypt I called My Son” fulfilled in Jesus? (v. 15)

Matthew says Jesus’ return from Egypt fulfilled the words, “Out of Egypt I called My Son” (v. 15). In Hosea 11:1 those words look back to Israel’s exodus. They were not a prediction about the Messiah.

MTB reads Matthew’s “fulfillment” in more than one way. Some passages are direct predictions; others, like this one, are patterns. Jesus relives and completes Israel’s story: God’s Son comes out of Egypt, passes through water, and is tested in the wilderness (3:13–4:11), and where Israel failed, He is faithful. Matthew’s fulfillment passages are not all the same kind.

Read: Matthew 2:13–15, Hosea 11:1, Matthew 3:13–17, Matthew 4:1–11

Where do the prophets say, “He shall be called a Nazarene”? (v. 23)

Matthew says Jesus’ home in Nazareth fulfilled what “was spoken by the prophets” (v. 23). Elsewhere in chapters 1–2 he names a single prophet and quotes him. Here he speaks of the prophets, plural, so he is summing up a theme rather than quoting one verse.

MTB reads two strands together. Growing up in obscure Nazareth marked Jesus as the lowly, despised One the prophets foretold (Isaiah 53:2–3; Psalm 22:6). Even Nathanael asked, “Can anything good come out of Nazareth?” (John 1:46). The town’s name also echoes netser, the Hebrew word for “Branch” in Isaiah 11:1: the shoot that grows from the cut-down stump of David’s family line.

Read: Matthew 2:19–23, Isaiah 11:1, Isaiah 53:2–3, Psalm 22:6–7, John 1:45–46

Matthew 3 · 1 question

Is the fire in John’s promise a blessing or a warning? (vv. 11–12)

In this setting the fire warns of judgment. John says that the Messiah baptizes with the Holy Spirit and fire, then describes Him gathering wheat and burning chaff with unquenchable fire (vv. 11–12). The surrounding warning about unfruitful trees being burned reinforces that meaning (v. 10).

The Messiah gives His Spirit to His people and brings judgment on persistent unrepentance.

Read: Matthew 3:7–12

II. The King Announces the Kingdom in Word and Deed (Matthew 4:17–11:1)

Matthew 5 · 2 questions

Did Jesus abolish the Old Testament law? (vv. 17–20)

“Do not think that I came to destroy the Law or the Prophets. I did not come to destroy but to fulfill” (v. 17). Jesus brings Scripture to its intended fulfillment through His person, perfect obedience, sacrifice, saving work, and authoritative teaching. Fulfillment includes more than explaining the commands correctly.

MTB reads a command by asking what it required in its original setting, how Jesus fulfills its purpose, and what obedience to Him now requires. His teaching reaches anger as well as murder, and lust as well as adultery (vv. 21–30). God promised to write His law on the heart and put His Spirit within His people (Jeremiah 31:31–34; Ezekiel 36:26–27). The risen King commissions disciples to teach obedience to all He commanded (28:18–20).

Read: Matthew 5:17–48, Matthew 22:34–40, Jeremiah 31:31–34, Ezekiel 36:26–27, Matthew 28:18–20

Does turning the other cheek mean we may never protect ourselves or others? (vv. 38–42)

“Whoever slaps you on your right cheek, turn the other to him also” (v. 39). MTB reads this as Jesus’ teaching on how a disciple responds to personal insult, put-downs, and humiliation: yield the impulse to retaliate.

It does not forbid protecting people from physical violence. A disciple can refuse revenge and still take steps to keep people safe.

Read: Matthew 5:38–48, Romans 12:17–21 · Related: Must I forgive someone who never repents, and does forgiving mean trusting them again?

Matthew 6 · 1 question

Does deliverance from the evil one include help for believers? (v. 13)

MTB’s preferred reading of the prayer is rescue from the evil one, Satan, and the demonic forces working with him. Jesus’ actual deliverances reveal the King freeing captives (8:28–34; 12:22–29). The disciples’ prayer includes protection from temptation and rescue where the enemy has gained influence.

A believer belongs to Christ and can still need deliverance from demonic oppression or bondage. Belonging to Christ and being subjected to hostile influence are different matters (Ephesians 4:27; James 4:7; 1 Peter 5:8–9). Deliverance belongs with repentance, resistance to evil, and a life increasingly filled with the things of God.

The flesh, entrenched habits, wounds, and demonic oppression are distinct. An illness, emotional difficulty, or recurring struggle does not by itself prove demonization. Christ’s authority and the Father’s care give disciples confidence to seek freedom.

Read: Matthew 6:9–13, Matthew 8:28–34, Matthew 12:22–29, Ephesians 4:27, James 4:7, 1 Peter 5:8–9 · Related: The unpardonable sin and the strong man

Matthew 7 · 1 question

Does “Judge not” mean Christians should never call anything wrong? (vv. 1–5)

“Judge not, that you be not judged” (v. 1). Jesus forbids harsh, hypocritical judgment that ignores one’s own sin. He tells us first to remove “the plank from your own eye” and then, “you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother’s eye” (v. 5). The goal is humble help, not silence about sin.

In the same chapter Jesus calls for discernment: do not give what is holy to dogs (v. 6), and “by their fruits you will know them” (v. 20).

Read: Matthew 7:1–6, 15–20, John 7:24, Galatians 6:1

Matthew 8 · 1 question

Is there healing in the atonement? (vv. 16–17)

Matthew says Jesus “healed all who were sick, that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Isaiah the prophet, saying: ‘He Himself took our infirmities and bore our sicknesses’” (vv. 16–17; Isaiah 53:4). Jesus’ healings before the cross showed the Servant already lifting the burdens He came to bear, and they reveal that His atoning work reaches the body.

MTB teaches believers to pray for healing with bold expectation; the Spirit continues to give gifts of healings as He wills (1 Corinthians 12:9–11). Immediate healing is not guaranteed, and persistent illness is no proof of weak faith or hidden sin. Paul left Trophimus sick, and Timothy had recurring infirmities (2 Timothy 4:20; 1 Timothy 5:23). Complete and permanent bodily restoration awaits the resurrection (Romans 8:23; 1 Corinthians 15:42–54).

Faith is the persuasion that God is who He has revealed Himself to be in His Word and that He is faithful to perform all He has promised. The centurion’s confidence rests on Jesus’ authority (vv. 5–13). Faith relies on the King; it is not a method that controls His response. God’s invitation to ask gives confidence to people who feel unworthy, while His care continues when healing is delayed.

Read: Matthew 8:5–17, Isaiah 53:4–5, 1 Corinthians 12:9–11, 2 Timothy 4:20, 1 Timothy 5:23, Romans 8:23, 1 Corinthians 15:42–54

Matthew 10 · 1 question

What coming does Jesus mean by “before the Son of Man comes”? (v. 23)

“You will not have gone through the cities of Israel before the Son of Man comes” (v. 23). From verse 16 on, Jesus looks beyond the Twelve’s first tour: floggings in synagogues, trials before governors and kings, testimony to the Gentiles, and enduring “to the end” (vv. 17–22). None of that happened on the first trip.

MTB reads the coming in two stages. The first, decisive stage is Jesus’ resurrection, when He comes in royal authority: “All authority has been given to Me in heaven and on earth” (28:18). That coming opens the mission from Israel alone (10:5–6) to all the nations (28:19) before the disciples could finish Israel’s towns. The final horizon is His return in glory. Until then the mission to Israel remains unfinished, and His messengers must endure.

Read: Matthew 10:5–6, Matthew 10:16–23, Matthew 28:18–20, Matthew 24:14 · Related: Who saw “the Son of Man coming in His kingdom” before they died?

III. The King Meets Rejection and Recognition (Matthew 11:2–16:20)

Matthew 11 · 2 questions

Does the kingdom suffer violent opposition or advance forcefully? (v. 12)

MTB favors the reading that the kingdom suffers violent opposition and its messengers face hostility. John’s imprisonment makes that opposition concrete (vv. 2–3), and his death later shows the cost of faithful witness (14:1–12).

The wording has also been understood as the kingdom advancing powerfully or people responding with urgent determination. The opposition surrounding John best fits the reading favored here.

Read: Matthew 11:2–15, Matthew 14:1–12

Is the rest Jesus promises a gift, or something we learn? (vv. 28–30)

“Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest. Take My yoke upon you and learn from Me, for I am gentle and lowly in heart, and you will find rest for your souls” (vv. 28–29). MTB sees two sides of this rest. Jesus gives rest to those who come to Him, lifting the burden of sin through His forgiveness. We also find rest as we take His yoke and learn from Him, becoming gentle and lowly like Him.

Pride, self-reliance, comparison, and the need to prove ourselves weigh the soul down. His yoke is His rule and way of life, carried in relationship with Him, and living under it lifts that weight. The rest is inward; it does not promise that outward circumstances will become easy.

Read: Matthew 11:25–30

Matthew 12 · 1 question

What is the unpardonable sin? (vv. 22–32)

Jesus healed a demon-possessed man who was blind and mute, and the Pharisees said He did it by Beelzebub. Jesus answered that He was binding the strong man and plundering his house by the Spirit of God (vv. 28–29). Then He warned: “the blasphemy against the Spirit will not be forgiven men” (v. 31).

MTB understands this as the willful, conscious, and final rejection of the Spirit’s revelation of Christ. In this scene the Pharisees deliberately turn the testimony of Jesus’ rescue upside down by calling the Spirit’s work Satan’s. The hardened rejection refuses the testimony that leads a person to the Savior.

Grief over sin and a desire to turn to Christ are grounds for reassurance and an invitation to receive forgiveness (1 John 1:9). An intrusive thought, fearful question, or careless remark does not by itself establish this final rejection. Jesus’ warning is serious, and His offer of forgiveness is broad (v. 31).

Read: Matthew 12:22–32, Mark 3:28–30, 1 John 1:9

Matthew 13 · 5 questions

Can different soils describe different areas of one person’s heart? (vv. 1–23)

Jesus first explains different responses to the word of the kingdom: it can be snatched away, received without depth, choked by cares and riches, or understood and made fruitful (vv. 18–23). His explanation addresses real differences among hearers.

As a spiritual application, MTB also reads the soils as conditions that can coexist in different areas of one disciple’s heart. A person may receive the King’s instruction in one matter while resisting forgiveness, generosity, or obedience in another. This extends the parable’s pattern into honest examination of our own reception of His word.

Growing from Christ’s love and resources helps explain fruitful life (Colossians 2:6–7; Ephesians 3:17–19).

Read: Matthew 13:1–23, Colossians 2:6–7, Ephesians 3:17–19 · Related: Entering the kingdom and new birth · Jesus’ warnings and assurance

Does leaving the tares until harvest rule out church discipline? (vv. 24–30, 36–43)

No. Jesus identifies the field as the world, the good seed as the sons of the kingdom, the tares as the sons of the wicked one, the harvest as the end of the age, and the reapers as angels (vv. 37–39). The Son of Man and His angels carry out the final separation and judgment.

The church does not take over that final judgment. Jesus nevertheless commands correction and restorative discipline among His disciples in 18:15–20. Patient coexistence in the world and accountable care within the church serve different purposes.

Read: Matthew 13:24–30, Matthew 13:36–43, Matthew 18:15–20 · Related: The rock and the keys

What do the birds in the mustard tree mean? (vv. 31–32)

The mustard seed pictures the kingdom’s surprising growth, from “the least of all the seeds” to a tree, “so that the birds of the air come and nest in its branches” (v. 32). The kingdom began with Jesus and a handful of Galilean disciples, and it grows to reach the world.

The birds echo the Old Testament picture of a great tree sheltering birds of every kind, an image of nations finding shelter (Ezekiel 17:22–24; Daniel 4:12). MTB reads them as the nations gathering under Christ’s reign. Birds in the earlier soils parable picture the evil one (vv. 4, 19), but each parable sets its own picture. Scripture can use a lion for both Satan and Christ (1 Peter 5:8; Revelation 5:5).

Read: Matthew 13:31–32, Ezekiel 17:22–24, Daniel 4:10–12

Is the leaven in Jesus’ parable good or evil? (v. 33)

“The kingdom of heaven is like leaven, which a woman took and hid in three measures of meal till it was all leavened” (v. 33). Leaven often pictures corruption elsewhere in Scripture (16:6; 1 Corinthians 5:6–8), so some read this as evil spreading in the church.

MTB’s preferred reading is that the leaven pictures the kingdom’s positive influence: Christ’s reign works from within, at first hidden and then evident throughout a life, home, and wider community. Its influence reaches thought, affection, conduct, marriage, and work. In this parable Jesus uses leaven for the kingdom itself.

Read: Matthew 13:31–33, Matthew 16:6–12, 1 Corinthians 5:6–8

Who sells everything in the parables of the treasure and the pearl? (vv. 44–46)

In context, the one who sells everything is the person who discovers the kingdom. The kingdom is worth more than everything else, and “for joy over it he goes and sells all that he has” (v. 44). Wholehearted surrender is the response to discovered worth, not the price of salvation.

As a wider application, the disciple’s self-giving mirrors the King’s own. Christ gave everything at the cross to purchase His people (1 Corinthians 6:20; Ephesians 5:25).

Read: Matthew 13:44–46, Philippians 3:7–8, 1 Corinthians 6:19–20, Ephesians 5:25–27

Matthew 15 · 1 question

Why did Jesus seem to refuse the Canaanite woman and speak of “little dogs”? (vv. 21–28)

When the Canaanite woman pleads for her daughter, Jesus states His earthly assignment: “I was not sent except to the lost sheep of the house of Israel” (v. 24). He then tests her faith with a household picture: “It is not good to take the children’s bread and throw it to the little dogs” (v. 26). The word He uses means small household dogs beneath the family table, not wild street dogs.

Jesus is drawing out her faith, not insulting her worth. She accepts Israel’s priority in God’s saving plan and appeals to the abundance of the Master’s table: “Yes, Lord, yet even the little dogs eat the crumbs which fall from their masters’ table” (v. 27). Jesus answers, “O woman, great is your faith!” and heals her daughter (v. 28), a foretaste of the gospel’s welcome to all nations (28:19).

Read: Matthew 15:21–28, Matthew 8:5–13, Matthew 10:5–6, Matthew 28:18–20, Romans 15:8–12

Matthew 16 · 3 questions

What is the “rock,” and what are “the keys of the kingdom”? (vv. 18–19)

“You are Peter, and on this rock I will build My church” (v. 18). Christians have understood the rock as Peter himself, as Peter’s confession, or as Christ. MTB reads the rock as the truth Peter confessed, revealed by the Father: “You are the Christ, the Son of the living God” (vv. 16–17).

“I will give you the keys of the kingdom of heaven” (v. 19). The keys are authority entrusted by the King to act on His behalf in matters of access to the kingdom. Through the gospel, His servants announce forgiveness to those who repent and believe and warn those rejecting Him that they remain in their sins (John 20:21–23). Binding and loosing also concern restorative discipline and fellowship (18:15–20). The authority is accountable to His word, and final judgment belongs to Him. The Greek wording of verse 19 can be read as heaven having already bound or loosed, which underscores that the church does not invent its own terms but announces what heaven has already decided in God’s revealed word.

Read: Matthew 16:13–20, Matthew 18:15–20, John 20:21–23, Ephesians 2:20

Does denying yourself mean hating yourself? (v. 24)

“If anyone desires to come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow Me” (v. 24). To deny yourself is to stop making self-preservation, self-advancement, comfort, or your own agenda the center of life, and to put Christ there instead. Taking up the cross means obeying Him even when obedience brings suffering, loss, or misunderstanding. Luke records that it is daily (Luke 9:23), lived out in ordinary acts that confront pride: apologizing, forgiving, serving, speaking truth, and yielding control.

Self-denial is not self-hatred or the loss of the person God made you to be, and it never means submitting to another person’s sin. Jesus promises that whoever loses his life for His sake will find it (v. 25).

Read: Matthew 16:21–28, Luke 9:23–24

Who saw “the Son of Man coming in His kingdom” before they died? (v. 28)

Verse 27 describes the final coming in glory and judgment. Verse 28 promises that “some standing here” will see the Son of Man coming in His kingdom before they die. Matthew then writes, “Now after six days,” and Jesus takes Peter, James, and John up a high mountain, where He is transfigured before them (17:1–2).

MTB reads the transfiguration as the preview: three disciples see the King’s glory unveiled before they taste death. Peter later says they were “eyewitnesses of His majesty” (2 Peter 1:16). The preview is fulfilled in history when Jesus rises and declares, “All authority has been given to Me in heaven and on earth” (28:18).

Read: Matthew 16:27–17:9, Matthew 28:18, 2 Peter 1:16–18 · Related: What coming does Jesus mean by “before the Son of Man comes”?

IV. The King Teaches the Way of the Cross (Matthew 16:21–20:34)

Matthew 17 · 1 question

Why do some Bibles leave out Matthew 17:21? (v. 21)

In the NKJV, Jesus explains why the disciples could not cast out the demon: “However, this kind does not go out except by prayer and fasting” (v. 21). Several of the earliest Greek manuscripts of Matthew do not include this verse, so translations such as the ESV and NIV place it in a footnote. Copyists likely brought the wording over from the parallel account in Mark 9:29, where Jesus’ words about prayer are firmly in the text.

The teaching does not rest on a disputed verse. Mark records it in the same scene, and Jesus’ answer in Matthew points the same way: the disciples failed “because of your unbelief” (v. 20). He calls them away from self-reliance to humble, dependent prayer to God, whose authority alone sets the oppressed free.

Read: Matthew 17:14–21, Mark 9:14–29

Matthew 18 · 1 question

Must I forgive someone who never repents, and does forgiving mean trusting them again? (vv. 15–35)

Jesus told Peter to forgive “up to seventy times seven” (v. 22) and warned that the Father will deal with those who do not forgive “from his heart” (v. 35).

MTB distinguishes forgiveness from reconciliation. Forgiveness from the heart is one-sided: even if the offender never repents, the disciple gives up personal revenge and entrusts justice to the King. Reconciliation takes two: restoring fellowship and trust requires the offender’s repentance and changed conduct (vv. 15–17). Safety must stay in view wherever there is danger.

Heart forgiveness can involve a process of bringing pain and resentment into Christ’s care. A recurring memory or renewed grief does not by itself prove that someone has refused to forgive. The call is to relinquish revenge sincerely while allowing the Lord to heal the wound.

Read: Matthew 18:15–35, Matthew 6:14–15, Romans 12:17–19, Luke 17:3–4 · Related: Does turning the other cheek mean we may never protect ourselves or others?

Matthew 19 · 1 question

What does Jesus allow regarding divorce and remarriage? (5:31–32; 19:3–12)

Jesus begins with creation: “the two shall become one flesh” (19:5–6). He names one exception: “whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another, commits adultery” (19:9). Paul addresses abandonment by an unbelieving spouse (1 Corinthians 7:15).

Matthew names sexual immorality; Paul addresses an unbelieving spouse’s departure. MTB understands covenant abandonment to include a refusal of marital obligations even within a shared home, and treats persistent, unrepentant physical abuse as a form of that abandonment. Jesus does not list abuse as a separate exception; MTB applies the broader biblical teaching on abandonment. Protection from danger comes first. Loss of affection, incompatibility, hardship, or a spouse’s unbelief alone does not justify divorce.

A divorce actually dissolves the marriage, while its moral justification is a separate question. MTB permits remarriage after widowhood or justified divorce. After wrongful divorce, repentance includes acknowledging the wrong, seeking forgiveness, and seeking reconciliation while possible. When reconciliation is no longer possible, including a former spouse’s remarriage, MTB permits remarriage after genuine repentance (Romans 7:2–3; 1 Corinthians 7:10–15; 1 Timothy 5:14).

A wrongfully entered remarriage can involve an act of adultery against the former covenant. MTB does not treat that present marriage as perpetual adultery: after repentance, the couple should honor their present covenant rather than create another divorce to undo the past (Deuteronomy 24:1–4). Forgiveness does not erase the original wrong, and present faithfulness matters.

Read: Matthew 5:31–32, Matthew 19:3–12, 1 Corinthians 7:10–16, Romans 7:2–3, 1 Timothy 5:14, Deuteronomy 24:1–4, Malachi 2:14–16

Matthew 20 · 1 question

What does the parable of the vineyard workers teach? (vv. 1–16)

A landowner pays the workers hired at the eleventh hour the same as those who worked all day, and the early workers grumble (vv. 1–12). The point is grace: even the opportunity to serve is a gift. The landowner’s generosity exposes envy and entitlement and calls for service out of gratitude. “So the last will be first, and the first last” (v. 16).

As a prophetic interpretation, MTB also reads the workday as gospel labor through the ages and the eleventh-hour workers as those called shortly before Christ returns. This further picture encourages participation in the harvest and welcome for workers called late. The immediate challenge to envy and entitlement remains.

Read: Matthew 20:1–16, Matthew 19:27–30

V. The King Confronts Jerusalem (Matthew 21:1–23:39)

Matthew 21 · 1 question

Does “the kingdom of God will be taken from you” mean God rejected Israel? (v. 43)

“The kingdom of God will be taken from you and given to a nation bearing the fruits of it” (v. 43). Jesus says this to the chief priests and Pharisees who rejected Him, and “they perceived that He was speaking of them” (v. 45). It is a rebuke of those leaders.

Jesus first comes to Israel in covenant faithfulness (10:5–6). He embodies Israel’s calling and gathers a fruit-bearing people around Himself, beginning within Israel and welcoming the nations. The all-nations mission fulfills Abraham’s promised blessing and includes Israel (Genesis 22:18; Matthew 28:19). MTB understands this as Israel’s story reaching its worldwide purpose through her King, rather than a Gentile church taking the place of a discarded Jewish people.

Read: Matthew 21:33–46, Matthew 10:5–6, Genesis 22:18, Matthew 28:18–20, Romans 11:1–2

Matthew 22 · 1 question

What does the wedding garment represent? (vv. 1–14)

MTB’s preferred reading is the righteousness of Christ received by grace. The king’s generous invitation is real, yet outward attendance alone is not a fitting response. Isaiah 61:10 describes salvation and righteousness as clothing God provides.

The gift of righteousness belongs with a life that expresses it. Revelation 19:7–9 connects the wedding clothing with the righteous acts of God’s people. Church attendance, baptism by itself, or good deeds cannot buy admission; genuine reception of the King bears fruit in conduct.

Read: Matthew 22:1–14, Isaiah 61:10, Revelation 19:7–9 · Related: Jesus’ warnings and assurance

Matthew 23 · 1 question

Does Jesus forbid calling anyone “father” or “teacher”? (vv. 8–12)

“Do not call anyone on earth your father; for One is your Father, He who is in heaven” (v. 9). Jesus is confronting scribes and Pharisees who loved “greetings in the marketplaces” and honored titles (v. 7). He forbids using titles to claim the status and spiritual authority that belong to God alone. His disciples are brothers under one Father and one Teacher, and “he who is greatest among you shall be your servant” (v. 11).

The issue is self-exalting status, not the words themselves. Paul calls himself the Corinthians’ father in the gospel (1 Corinthians 4:15), and Christ gives teachers to His church (Ephesians 4:11). Every teaching role stays under the one Master and serves others.

Read: Matthew 23:1–12, 1 Corinthians 4:15, Ephesians 4:11–12

VI. The King Promises His Return and Calls for Readiness (Matthew 24:1–25:46)

Matthew 24 · 2 questions

Was Jesus describing Jerusalem’s fall or His second coming? (vv. 1–36)

Jesus’ disciples asked about the temple’s destruction and “the sign of Your coming, and of the end of the age” (v. 3). Jesus answers both, and MTB reads His answer on two horizons.

The near horizon is AD 70. “This generation will by no means pass away till all these things take place” (v. 34) keeps its plain meaning: Jesus’ contemporaries saw the deception, wars, persecution, and Jerusalem’s destruction He described (vv. 4–28; Luke 21:20–24).

That first-century crisis is also a pattern that recurs across this age and intensifies into a final tribulation. The generation that sees those signs come together at the end will see “the Son of Man coming on the clouds of heaven with power and great glory” (v. 30). Verses 29–31 describe Christ’s future return. “But of that day and hour no one knows” (v. 36), so Jesus commands, “Watch therefore, for you do not know what hour your Lord is coming” (v. 42).

Read: Matthew 24:1–44, Luke 21:20–24 · Related: Is the one “taken” in verses 40–41 rescued or judged?

Is the one “taken” in verses 40–41 rescued or judged? (vv. 37–42)

Jesus compares His coming to Noah’s day, when “the flood came and took them all away” in judgment (v. 39). Then: “Two men will be in the field: one will be taken and the other left” (v. 40). The flood comparison points toward being taken in judgment.

But in verses 40–41 Matthew uses a different verb, one that means to take alongside or receive. Jesus uses it in John 14:3: “I will come again and receive you to Myself.” That points toward being gathered to Christ.

MTB leaves the exact identity of those taken and left open. Jesus presses sudden separation and readiness: “Watch therefore, for you do not know what hour your Lord is coming” (v. 42).

Read: Matthew 24:36–44, Luke 17:26–37, John 14:1–3 · Related: Was Jesus describing Jerusalem’s fall or His second coming?

Matthew 25 · 3 questions

What is the oil in the parable of the ten virgins? (vv. 1–13)

The foolish virgins “took their lamps and took no oil with them” (v. 3), and when the bridegroom came after a long delay, they were shut out. The oil is what kept them ready through the delay. The warning is plain: readiness cannot be borrowed at the last moment.

As a spiritual interpretation, MTB reads the lamps as visible life, profession, and witness, and the oil as the righteousness God provides and the supply of His Spirit sustaining the inner life. The wise possess inward life from God; the foolish have outward profession without that reality (2 Timothy 3:5; Titus 1:16). Life with Christ cannot be borrowed from a parent, pastor, spouse, or congregation at the last moment.

All ten sleep, so sleep is not their failure; the difference is the oil. Each person’s readiness depends on his own life with the King.

Read: Matthew 25:1–13, Matthew 24:42–44, 2 Timothy 3:5, Titus 1:16 · Related: Jesus’ warnings and assurance

Does the parable of the talents promise a return on what we give or invest? (vv. 14–30)

The master entrusts his servants with different amounts, “to each according to his own ability” (v. 15). The two who put the money to work receive the same commendation: “Well done, good and faithful servant” (vv. 21, 23). The one who buried his is judged severely (vv. 26–30).

The money pictures everything God entrusts: time, gifts, abilities, material resources, opportunities, and responsibilities. MTB teaches a law of increase: using what God gives develops capacity, and neglect can lead to loss. Faithfulness means acting now with what we have, not waiting for more.

The parable does not promise a fixed financial return, and productivity does not measure a person’s worth. Faithfulness is measured by what each servant was given, and reward remains the King’s gracious gift (20:1–16).

Read: Matthew 25:14–30, Matthew 20:1–16

Who are “the least of these My brethren”? (vv. 31–46)

“Inasmuch as you did it to one of the least of these My brethren, you did it to Me” (v. 40). Many read “the least of these” as anyone in need. In Matthew, Jesus uses “brethren” for His disciples (12:48–50; 28:10), and He has said that whoever receives His messengers receives Him (10:40–42).

MTB reads the King here as identifying especially with His vulnerable disciples sent in His name. How the nations treat them shows how they respond to Him. The wider teaching of Scripture still calls believers to show mercy to all in need (Proverbs 19:17; Galatians 6:10).

The scene ends in everlasting punishment and eternal life (v. 46). Deeds of mercy make allegiance to Christ visible; they do not buy salvation. His identification with His messengers gives ordinary acts of care lasting significance.

Read: Matthew 25:31–46, Matthew 10:40–42, Matthew 12:48–50, Matthew 28:10, Proverbs 19:17, Galatians 6:10

VII. The King Gives His Life and Rises with All Authority (Matthew 26:1–28:20)

Matthew 26 · 1 question

Are the bread and cup really Jesus’ body and blood? (vv. 26–29)

At Passover Jesus gives bread and says, “Take, eat; this is My body” (v. 26). He identifies the cup with His covenant blood shed for forgiveness (vv. 27–28) and promises to drink with His disciples in His Father’s kingdom (v. 29). The meal joins His saving death, covenant fellowship, and future hope.

Christians understand the meal in different ways. MTB holds that the bread and cup are symbols and that Christ is truly present spiritually through the Holy Spirit as believers receive in faith. Communion is a means of grace: Christ communicates life, assurance of forgiveness, freedom from guilt and shame, and renewed surrender. Remembering His once-for-all death and receiving from Him belong together; the meal does not repeat or supplement His sacrifice.

Communion also concerns the one body sharing His life (1 Corinthians 10:16–17). Self-examination and discerning the body involve love, forgiveness, peace, and care for one another (1 Corinthians 11:23–32). The meal is personal fellowship with Christ within the people He has gathered.

Read: Matthew 26:17–29, 1 Corinthians 10:16–17, 1 Corinthians 11:23–32

Matthew 27 · 2 questions

Was Jesus really forsaken by the Father on the cross? (v. 46)

“My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?” (v. 46). Jesus expresses real anguish and forsakenness as He bears judgment for sin in our place. He came “to give His life a ransom for many” (20:28).

His cry quotes Psalm 22:1. That psalm moves from forsakenness to trust and God’s vindication (Psalm 22:24), and MTB reads Jesus’ cry in that light. The Son did not stop being God’s beloved Son, and the Trinity was not broken; He truly bore what sin deserves.

Read: Matthew 27:45–50, Psalm 22:1–24, Matthew 20:28, 2 Corinthians 5:21

How was Jesus in the tomb “three days and three nights” if He rose on Sunday? (12:40; 27:62; 28:1)

Jesus said the Son of Man would be “three days and three nights in the heart of the earth” (12:40). MTB holds that Jesus was crucified on Thursday, the Day of Preparation (Nisan 14), followed by two back-to-back Sabbaths: Friday, the annual High Sabbath that opened the Feast of Unleavened Bread (Nisan 15; Leviticus 23:6–7; John 19:31), and Saturday, the weekly Sabbath (Nisan 16).

Jesus ate the Passover with His disciples on Wednesday evening, as Nisan 14 began (26:17–20). He was crucified and buried on Thursday before sundown, lay in the tomb Thursday, Friday, and Saturday nights and days, and rose bodily at dawn on Sunday, the first day of the week (28:1). The Greek word for “Sabbath” in 28:1 is plural. This timeline honors Matthew 12:40 directly and fits the Feast of Firstfruits and the fifty-day count to Pentecost (Leviticus 23:10–16).

Other faithful Christians hold a Friday crucifixion, counting parts of days as whole days in the Jewish manner. Whichever reconstruction a reader holds, the saving center is Christ’s atoning death and bodily resurrection.

Read: Matthew 12:38–40, Matthew 26:17–20, Matthew 27:57–66, Matthew 28:1–6, Leviticus 23:5–16, John 19:31

Matthew 28 · 2 questions

What does Jesus’ command to baptize mean for believers today? (vv. 18–20)

“Go therefore and make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all things that I have commanded you” (vv. 19–20). Baptism and teaching obedience are part of making disciples.

MTB teaches immersion after a person receives the gospel, repents, and believes. Baptism expresses surrender and identification with Christ’s death, burial, and resurrection. Through faith in God’s work, it also concerns freedom from sin’s old dominion, spiritual circumcision, a cleansed conscience, and a walk of new life (Romans 6:3–7; Colossians 2:11–12; 1 Peter 3:21). God works inwardly as the believer responds in faith.

The Spirit gives new birth (John 3:3–8). MTB rejects infant baptism and the idea that the water rite mechanically produces saving life apart from personal faith. Baptism and continued teaching belong together: the commission calls for growing disciples who learn to obey Jesus, not only an initial profession (vv. 19–20).

Read: Matthew 28:16–20, Romans 6:3–7, Colossians 2:11–12, 1 Peter 3:21, John 3:3–8, Acts 2:38–41 · Related: Entering the kingdom and new birth · Should believers be baptized in the name of Jesus, or of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit?

Should believers be baptized in the name of Jesus, or of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit? (v. 19)

Jesus commands baptism “in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit” (v. 19). “Name” is singular: one divine name and authority shared by the three Persons of the Godhead. All authority has been given to the risen Christ (v. 18), and in Him “dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily” (Colossians 2:9). The triune name is revealed and entered through Him.

The apostles baptized “in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins” (Acts 2:38; compare 8:12; 19:5), and “as many of you as were baptized into Christ have put on Christ” (Galatians 3:27).

MTB’s practice follows that apostolic wording and honors the triune God revealed in Jesus: “I baptize you into the name of the Lord Jesus Christ for the remission of sins and the circumcision of the heart.” MTB affirms all Christian baptisms performed in the triune name and requires no single spoken formula for validity.

Read: Matthew 28:18–20, Colossians 2:9, Acts 2:38, Acts 8:12, Acts 19:5, Galatians 3:27
