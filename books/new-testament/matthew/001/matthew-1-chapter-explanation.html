Verse Explanation

Matthew opens his Gospel by naming Jesus as the Christ, the Son of David, and the Son of Abraham. Jesus is the promised King who will sit on David's throne and the promised descendant through whom God will bless all the nations of the earth.

“The book of the genealogy”

The word translated “genealogy” means “origin” or “beginning,” and it is the word from which the book of Genesis takes its name. The expression echoes Genesis 2:4 and 5:1, where the same wording introduces the account of the heavens and the earth and the family line of Adam. Matthew begins the New Testament the way Genesis began the Old, with an account of origins. With the coming of Jesus, God is making a new beginning in His dealings with humanity.

“of Jesus Christ,”

“Jesus” is His personal name, and the angel will explain its meaning in verse 21. “Christ” is a title. It comes from the Greek word for “anointed one,” which translates the Hebrew word for Messiah. In the Old Testament, kings, priests, and at times prophets were anointed with oil to set them apart for their work. By Jesus' day, “the Messiah” had come to mean the King God promised to send from David's family to deliver His people. Matthew states this claim in his first line, and the rest of the Gospel shows why it is true.

“the Son of David,”

God promised David that one of his descendants would rule on his throne forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16). The prophets developed this hope, speaking of a coming King from David's line who would reign in righteousness (Isaiah 9:6–7; 11:1–10; Jeremiah 23:5–6). “Son of David” therefore became a way of saying “the Messiah.” “Son” here means descendant, since David lived about a thousand years before Jesus. Matthew uses the title more than any other Gospel writer. Blind men, a Canaanite mother, and the Jerusalem crowds will all call out to Jesus by this name (9:27; 15:22; 21:9).

“the Son of Abraham:”

Abraham is the father of the Jewish people. When God called him, He promised, “In you all the families of the earth shall be blessed” (Genesis 12:3; see also 22:18). As the Son of Abraham, Jesus is the true heir of that promise, and through Him its blessing reaches the nations. Matthew's Gospel begins with Abraham and ends with Jesus sending His disciples to “make disciples of all the nations” (28:19).

Matthew names David before Abraham, even though Abraham lived nearly a thousand years earlier. The order highlights kingship. Jesus is first of all the promised King. The genealogy that follows then moves in historical order from Abraham to David and on to Christ.

Study Notes

People in this verse.

David: Israel's second king, to whom God promised an everlasting throne (2 Samuel 7:16).

Abraham: father of the Jewish people, called by God and promised that all nations would be blessed through him (Genesis 12:1–3).

“The book of the genealogy.” The Greek phrase biblos geneseōs appears in exactly this form in the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) at Genesis 2:4 and 5:1. Interpreters debate whether Matthew intends it as a heading for the genealogy alone (1:2–17), for the birth narrative (chapters 1–2), or for the entire Gospel. It most naturally introduces the genealogy, while the echo of Genesis gives the whole Gospel a sense of new beginning. The same Greek word reappears in verse 18 (“birth”), linking the genealogy and the birth account.

Genealogies in the ancient world. Jewish genealogies established identity, inheritance, and the right to hold certain offices. After the exile, men who could not prove their priestly descent from the records were excluded from the priesthood (Ezra 2:59–63). The historian Josephus, writing later in the first century, records his own priestly descent from public registers. For anyone presented as the Messiah, descent from David was essential.

“Son of David” in Matthew. Matthew uses the title about ten times, more than Mark and Luke combined. Apart from 1:20, where the angel addresses Joseph, it always refers to Jesus.

Verse Explanation

The promise God gave Abraham passes through Isaac and Jacob to Judah, the son from whom Israel's kings would come (Genesis 49:10). By adding “and his brothers,” Matthew keeps the whole family of Israel in view as the line moves toward the Messiah.

“Abraham begot Isaac,”

“Begot” means “became the father of.” The NKJV keeps the traditional word, which Matthew repeats through verse 16 like a steady drumbeat. Isaac was the child of promise, born when Abraham and Sarah were far past the age of having children (Genesis 18:11–14; 21:1–3). From the first step, the Messiah's line depended on God's power. God chose Isaac rather than Ishmael, Abraham's older son, as the heir of the promise (Genesis 17:19–21).

“Isaac begot Jacob,”

Jacob was the younger of Isaac's twin sons. Before the twins were born, God declared that the older would serve the younger (Genesis 25:23). God later renamed Jacob “Israel” (Genesis 32:28), and the nation descended from him bears that name.

“and Jacob begot Judah and his brothers.”

Judah was Jacob's fourth son by Leah (Genesis 29:35). Reuben, the firstborn, lost his privileged place because of his sin (Genesis 49:3–4; 1 Chronicles 5:1–2). On his deathbed Jacob prophesied that “the scepter shall not depart from Judah” (Genesis 49:10), marking Judah as the tribe of Israel's kings. David and every king of Judah came from this tribe.

“And his brothers” refers to the twelve sons of Jacob, the fathers of Israel's twelve tribes. The line of the Messiah narrows to one tribe, but Matthew keeps the whole family of Israel in view. The King who comes from Judah comes for all of God's people.

Study Notes

People in this verse.

Isaac: son of Abraham and Sarah, born in their old age as the child of promise (Genesis 21:1–3).

Jacob: Isaac's younger twin son, renamed Israel; his twelve sons became the fathers of the twelve tribes (Genesis 32:28; 35:22–26).

Judah: Jacob's fourth son by Leah, ancestor of Israel's royal tribe (Genesis 29:35; 49:10).

“Begot.” The Greek verb gennaō appears in the active form thirty-nine times in verses 2–16. In biblical genealogies it can describe descent across more than one generation, so that “A begot B” may mean “A was the ancestor of B.” This usage becomes important in verses 8 and 11. In verse 16 the pattern changes.

God's choice of younger sons. Again and again the line passes to someone other than the eldest: Isaac rather than Ishmael, Jacob rather than Esau, Judah rather than Reuben, Perez rather than Zerah (Genesis 38:27–30), David the youngest of Jesse's sons (1 Samuel 16:11), and Solomon rather than his older brothers (1 Kings 1–2). Paul draws on this pattern to show that God's purposes rest on His choice and promise, not on human birthright (Romans 9:6–13).

Verse Explanation

Matthew names Tamar, the first of four women in the genealogy, and with her recalls the troubled episode in Judah's family recorded in Genesis 38. From its earliest generations, the line of the Messiah shows God carrying His promise forward through human failure.

“Judah begot Perez and Zerah by Tamar,”

Tamar was Judah's daughter-in-law. After her first two husbands, Judah's sons Er and Onan, died under God's judgment, Judah promised her his third son, Shelah, and then failed to keep his word. Tamar disguised herself as a prostitute, and Judah, not recognizing her, fathered twins by her. When the truth came out, Judah confessed, “She has been more righteous than I” (Genesis 38:26). Judah's own confession places the greater blame on himself.

Ancient genealogies normally traced descent through fathers, and Matthew could easily have left Tamar's name out. He includes her, the first of four women named before Mary. Matthew records the troubling parts of Israel's history openly. The Messiah's family tree includes deception, sin, and broken promises, and God still carried His purpose forward through it.

Perez and Zerah were twins. At their birth Perez broke through ahead of his brother, and his name means “breach” or “breaking through” (Genesis 38:27–30). The line continues through Perez, whose descendants became the leading clan of Judah. When the elders of Bethlehem blessed Boaz and Ruth, they prayed that their house would be “like the house of Perez” (Ruth 4:12).

“Perez begot Hezron, and Hezron begot Ram.”

Hezron was among the members of Jacob's family who went down to Egypt (Genesis 46:12). From this point through verse 6, Matthew follows the family record of Judah preserved in Ruth 4:18–22 and 1 Chronicles 2:5–15.

Study Notes

People in this verse.

Tamar: Judah's daughter-in-law and the mother of his twin sons (Genesis 38).

Perez: Judah's son through whom the royal line continued (Ruth 4:18).

Zerah: Perez's twin brother, ancestor of the Zerahite clan of Judah (Numbers 26:20).

Hezron: son of Perez, among Jacob's family who went down to Egypt (Genesis 46:12).

Ram: son of Hezron, known from the genealogies of Judah (Ruth 4:19; 1 Chronicles 2:9).

The four women. Matthew names Tamar (v. 3), Rahab (v. 5), Ruth (v. 5), and “her who had been the wife of Uriah” (v. 6), and later Mary (v. 16). He does not state a single reason for including them, and interpreters have noted several features. Three of the four stories involve sin or scandal: Judah's relations with Tamar, Rahab's life as a prostitute before she came to faith, and David's adultery with Bathsheba. Several of the women also had connections with the Gentile world: Rahab was a Canaanite and Ruth a Moabitess; Tamar's nationality is not stated, though the setting of Genesis 38 suggests she was a Canaanite; and Bathsheba was married to Uriah the Hittite. Their histories differ widely. Tamar was wronged and responded with deception; Rahab was a prostitute who came to faith; Ruth was a woman of loyal and godly character; and Bathsheba was taken by a king who then had her husband killed. Mary's place in verse 16 stands apart from all of them, since her Son was conceived by the Holy Spirit. Together these names display God's grace and His faithfulness in carrying His promise forward through unexpected histories.

Matthew's sources. For Abraham to David, Matthew's list agrees with the Old Testament genealogies in Ruth 4:18–22 and 1 Chronicles 1:34; 2:1–15. The Greek spellings of several names follow the Septuagint.

The line of Judah continues through the generations of Israel's slavery in Egypt, its wandering in the wilderness, and its entry into Canaan. Nahshon, who led the tribe of Judah in the wilderness (Numbers 2:3), shows the royal tribe already taking its place at the front of God's people.

“Ram begot Amminadab,”

Little is known about Ram. His son Amminadab is mentioned in Exodus 6:23, where his daughter Elisheba marries Aaron, Moses' brother. The family of Judah's future kings was joined by marriage to the family of Israel's high priests.

“Amminadab begot Nahshon,”

Nahshon was the leader of the tribe of Judah during the wilderness years (Numbers 1:7; 2:3). When the tabernacle was dedicated, he was the first of the tribal leaders to bring an offering (Numbers 7:12). When Israel broke camp, Judah's standard went first, with Nahshon at its head (Numbers 10:14). Long before Israel had a king, the tribe of Judah was already leading God's people.

“and Nahshon begot Salmon.”

Salmon belongs to the generation that entered Canaan under Joshua. Verse 5 connects him with Rahab of Jericho, placing him at the time of Israel's first victory in the land.

Study Notes

People in this verse.

Amminadab: son of Ram and father-in-law of Aaron the high priest (Exodus 6:23).

Nahshon: leader of the tribe of Judah in the wilderness (Numbers 2:3).

Salmon: son of Nahshon, of the generation that entered Canaan (Ruth 4:20–21).

Gaps in the early generations. Israel lived in Egypt for 430 years (Exodus 12:40), yet only a few generations are named between Hezron, who entered Egypt, and Nahshon, who left it. Likewise, only four names (Salmon, Boaz, Obed, and Jesse) span the long period from the conquest to David. Biblical genealogies often skip generations and focus on key ancestors. Matthew is following the Old Testament lists here; his own deliberate omissions come later (vv. 8, 11).

“Salmon.” First Chronicles 2:11 spells the name “Salma,” and Ruth 4:20–21 uses both “Salmon” and “Salmah” in the Hebrew. These are variant spellings of the same name.

Verse Explanation

Two women from outside Israel enter the line of the Messiah: Rahab, a Canaanite of Jericho, and Ruth, a Moabitess. Both turned to the God of Israel, and God gave them a place in the family that would produce David and, in time, the Christ.

“Salmon begot Boaz by Rahab,”

Rahab was the prostitute of Jericho who hid Israel's spies and confessed, “The LORD your God, He is God in heaven above and on earth beneath” (Joshua 2:11). She belonged to a Canaanite city under God's judgment, yet because she trusted the God of Israel, she and her household were spared when Jericho fell (Joshua 6:22–25). The New Testament honors her for both her faith (Hebrews 11:31) and her actions (James 2:25). The Old Testament does not record her marriage, but Matthew tells us she married Salmon and became the mother of Boaz.

“Boaz begot Obed by Ruth,”

Ruth was a widow from Moab, a nation that descended from Lot and had long been hostile to Israel. Moabites were barred from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3). Yet Ruth bound herself to her mother-in-law Naomi and to Naomi's God: “Your people shall be my people, and your God, my God” (Ruth 1:16). Boaz, a man of Bethlehem, acted as her kinsman-redeemer, married her, and preserved the family line (Ruth 4:9–13). The story of Ruth ends by naming her son Obed as the grandfather of David (Ruth 4:17).

Boaz was himself the son of a woman from outside Israel. The man who welcomed and redeemed a Moabite widow had grown up in a home where his own mother had once been a stranger to Israel's God.

“Obed begot Jesse,”

Jesse lived in Bethlehem (1 Samuel 16:1), the town of Boaz and Ruth. Isaiah prophesied that “a Rod from the stem of Jesse” would come forth, a descendant on whom the Spirit of the LORD would rest (Isaiah 11:1–2). The mention of Jesse prepares the way for David in verse 6 and, more distantly, for the birth of Jesus in Bethlehem (2:1).

Study Notes

People in this verse.

Rahab: the woman of Jericho who hid Israel's spies and was spared for her faith (Joshua 2; 6:25).

Boaz: landowner of Bethlehem who redeemed and married Ruth (Ruth 2–4).

Ruth: Moabite widow who joined Naomi and took Israel's God as her own (Ruth 1:16).

Obed: son of Boaz and Ruth, grandfather of David (Ruth 4:17).

Jesse: man of Bethlehem, the father of David (1 Samuel 16:1).

Rahab's identity. Matthew is the only biblical writer who connects Rahab with Salmon. His Greek spelling of her name (Rhachab) differs slightly from the spelling in Hebrews 11:31 and James 2:25 (Rhaab), but both represent the same Hebrew name. Nothing in the text suggests a different woman. Matthew's chronology is compressed here, since Salmon lived at the conquest while Boaz lived in the period of the judges.

Ruth and the law concerning Moab. Deuteronomy 23:3 excluded Moabites from the assembly “even to the tenth generation.” Later Jewish teaching applied the restriction to Moabite men but not women (Mishnah, Yevamot 8:3). Scripture itself presents Ruth's inclusion as the fruit of her faith and loyalty, and God honored her by placing her in the ancestry of David.

Verse Explanation

The genealogy reaches David, the king to whom God promised an enduring throne (2 Samuel 7:12–16). Matthew then names Solomon's mother only as the one “who had been the wife of Uriah,” openly recalling David's sin within the very line of the Messiah.

“and Jesse begot David the king.”

David is the only person in the genealogy called “the king,” even though many of the names that follow belonged to kings. The title marks David as the high point of the first period of Israel's history (v. 17). He was the youngest of Jesse's sons, a shepherd overlooked even by his own father (1 Samuel 16:11), yet God chose him and promised that his house and kingdom would be established forever (2 Samuel 7:16).

“David the king begot Solomon”

Matthew repeats “David the king,” keeping the royal theme in view. God chose Solomon to succeed David and to build the temple (1 Chronicles 22:9–10; 28:5–6).

“by her who had been the wife of Uriah.”

Matthew does not name Bathsheba. He identifies her by her first husband. Uriah the Hittite was one of David's mighty men (2 Samuel 23:39). David committed adultery with Uriah's wife and then arranged for Uriah to die in battle (2 Samuel 11). Scripture places the responsibility on David: “The thing that David had done displeased the LORD” (2 Samuel 11:27). By naming Uriah, Matthew brings David's sin into the record of the Messiah's ancestry.

Yet God's mercy is also in view. After David repented, God gave him and Bathsheba another son, Solomon, and “the LORD loved him” (2 Samuel 12:24). The line of the Messiah continued through the child of a marriage that began in sin, after that sin had been confessed and forgiven.

Study Notes

People in this verse.

Solomon: David's son and successor, who built the first temple (1 Kings 1–8).

Bathsheba: “her who had been the wife of Uriah,” later David's wife and Solomon's mother (2 Samuel 11–12).

Uriah: Hittite soldier among David's mighty men, killed at David's order (2 Samuel 11; 23:39).

“The wife of Uriah.” The phrase keeps Uriah, a faithful Gentile soldier, in view. Nathan's rebuke centered on David's wrong against him (2 Samuel 12:9–10). Bathsheba herself was the daughter of Eliam (2 Samuel 11:3), probably an Israelite. Her connection with the Gentile world comes through her marriage to Uriah.

Solomon or Nathan? Luke traces Jesus' ancestry through Nathan, another son of David and Bathsheba (Luke 3:31; 1 Chronicles 3:5), rather than through Solomon. Matthew follows the royal line of kings who actually reigned. The relationship between the two genealogies is discussed under verse 16.

Verse Explanation

Solomon's son Rehoboam begins the line of kings who ruled Judah after the kingdom divided, followed by Abijah and Asa. The Messiah's ancestry runs through the actual royal house of David, with its record of both failure and faithfulness.

“Solomon begot Rehoboam,”

Rehoboam's harsh answer to the people's request for relief split the kingdom (1 Kings 12:1–20). Ten tribes followed Jeroboam and formed the northern kingdom of Israel. Only Judah and Benjamin remained loyal to David's house, which God preserved “for the sake of My servant David” (1 Kings 11:13). From this point on, Matthew's list follows the kings of Judah.

“Rehoboam begot Abijah,”

Abijah “walked in all the sins of his father” (1 Kings 15:3). Yet Scripture adds, “for David's sake the LORD his God gave him a lamp in Jerusalem, by setting up his son after him” (1 Kings 15:4). The line continued because of God's promise to David.

“and Abijah begot Asa.”

Asa “did what was right in the eyes of the LORD, as did his father David” (1 Kings 15:11). He removed idols and even deposed his grandmother for her idolatry. Later in his reign, however, he relied on the king of Syria rather than on the LORD and, when diseased, sought physicians rather than God (2 Chronicles 16:7–12). The problem was not that Asa received medical care, but that in his illness he relied on human help without seeking the LORD. Like many in this list, Asa's life combined genuine faith with serious failure.

Study Notes

People in this verse.

Rehoboam: Solomon's son, in whose reign the kingdom divided (1 Kings 12).

Abijah: Rehoboam's son, also called Abijam, whose short reign followed his father's sins (1 Kings 15:1–8).

Asa: Abijah's son, a reforming king who reigned forty-one years (1 Kings 15:9–24).

“Asa” or “Asaph.” Several of the oldest Greek manuscripts of Matthew read “Asaph” (the name of the psalmist) in verses 7–8, while most manuscripts read “Asa.” The NKJV follows “Asa.” Either way, the king of Judah is meant. “Asaph” may reflect an alternate spelling or an early copying error. A similar variant appears in verse 10.

A lamp for David. The phrase “a lamp” for David appears several times in Kings (1 Kings 11:36; 15:4; 2 Kings 8:19). It describes God's commitment to keep a descendant of David ruling in Jerusalem even when the kings themselves were unfaithful.

Verse Explanation

The kingly line continues through Jehoshaphat, Joram, and Uzziah. Matthew moves directly from Joram to Uzziah, passing over three kings. His genealogy is an ordered line of descent that does not list every generation.

“Asa begot Jehoshaphat,”

Jehoshaphat was a godly king who sent officials and Levites throughout Judah to teach the Book of the Law (2 Chronicles 17:7–9). He made one costly mistake: he allied himself with Ahab, the wicked king of Israel, and sealed the alliance by marriage (2 Chronicles 18:1; 19:2).

“Jehoshaphat begot Joram,”

Joram (also called Jehoram) married Ahab's daughter Athaliah and “walked in the way of the kings of Israel, just as the house of Ahab had done” (2 Kings 8:18). Yet Scripture again records that “the LORD would not destroy Judah, for the sake of His servant David” (2 Kings 8:19). The consequences of this marriage nearly ended David's line. After Joram's son died, Athaliah seized the throne and tried to destroy the entire royal family. Only one infant, Joash, survived, hidden in the temple for six years (2 Kings 11:1–3).

“and Joram begot Uzziah.”

Uzziah was not Joram's son but his great-great-grandson. Between them stood three kings: Ahaziah, Joash, and Amaziah (1 Chronicles 3:11–12). Matthew passes over them and uses “begot” in the sense of “was the ancestor of.” Matthew is arranging Judah's history according to the pattern he describes in verse 17.

Uzziah, also called Azariah, reigned fifty-two years and prospered while he sought the LORD. But when he became strong, his heart was lifted up, and he entered the temple to burn incense, a task reserved for the priests. God struck him with leprosy, and he lived in isolation until his death (2 Chronicles 26:16–21). It was “in the year that King Uzziah died” that Isaiah saw the Lord high and lifted up (Isaiah 6:1).

Study Notes

People in this verse.

Jehoshaphat: Asa's son, a godly king who had the Law taught throughout Judah (2 Chronicles 17).

Joram: Jehoshaphat's son, also called Jehoram, who married Ahab's daughter Athaliah (2 Kings 8:16–24).

Uzziah: also called Azariah, a long-reigning king struck with leprosy for his pride (2 Chronicles 26).

The three omitted kings. Matthew does not explain why Ahaziah, Joash, and Amaziah are left out. The clearest reason is structural: the omission keeps the second period at fourteen generations (v. 17). Some interpreters add that these three were the descendants of Athaliah, Ahab's daughter, and suggest an allusion to God's visiting iniquity “to the third and fourth generations” (Exodus 20:5). This is possible but not stated. All three kings died violently (2 Kings 9:27; 12:20–21; 14:19).

Uzziah and Azariah. The two names refer to the same king (2 Kings 15:1–7; 2 Chronicles 26:1–23). Both are built on Hebrew words for the LORD's help or strength.

Verse Explanation

The line passes through Ahaz, one of Judah's most unfaithful kings, and Hezekiah, one of its most faithful. Ahaz is the king to whom Isaiah first gave the sign of Immanuel (Isaiah 7:10–14), the promise Matthew will declare fulfilled in verse 23.

“Uzziah begot Jotham,”

Jotham “did what was right in the sight of the LORD,” though the people of Judah “still acted corruptly” (2 Chronicles 27:2). He served as regent while his father Uzziah lived in isolation (2 Kings 15:5).

“Jotham begot Ahaz,”

Ahaz was one of the worst kings in Judah's history. He worshiped the gods of the nations, made his son pass through the fire in pagan sacrifice (2 Kings 16:3), and eventually shut the doors of the temple (2 Chronicles 28:24).

Ahaz is especially significant for Matthew 1. When the kings of Syria and Israel threatened Jerusalem, Isaiah came to Ahaz with God's promise of protection and invited him to ask for a sign. Ahaz refused, hiding his unbelief behind pious words. Isaiah then addressed the whole royal family: “Hear now, O house of David!… The Lord Himself will give you a sign: Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a Son, and shall call His name Immanuel” (Isaiah 7:13–14). Instead of trusting the LORD, Ahaz sent silver and gold to the king of Assyria for help (2 Kings 16:7–8). The sign first given to the house of David in the days of its faithless king finds its fulfillment in that same house, in the birth of Jesus (v. 23).

“and Ahaz begot Hezekiah.”

Hezekiah stands in sharp contrast to his father. He reopened and cleansed the temple, restored the Passover, and tore down the high places. Scripture says of him, “He trusted in the LORD God of Israel, so that after him was none like him among all the kings of Judah, nor who were before him” (2 Kings 18:5). Study Notes

Counting the fourteens. The first period (Abraham through David) contains fourteen names. If David is counted again as the first name of the second period, David through Josiah gives fourteen, and Jeconiah through Jesus gives fourteen. Other ways of counting have been proposed, such as counting Jeconiah twice or counting Mary as a generation. Matthew does not explain his method. His purpose is to present an ordered structure, not to supply a complete list of every ancestor.

Why fourteen? The explanation that best fits Matthew's emphasis is the value of David's name. Hebrew letters also served as numbers, and the consonants of David's name (D-W-D) have the value 4 + 6 + 4 = 14, so the repeated number may reinforce Matthew's presentation of Jesus as the Son of David. A secondary possibility is that Matthew saw significance in fourteen as twice seven, a number associated in Scripture with completeness. Matthew does not explicitly identify either explanation. Either way, the structure keeps David, the king to whom the throne was promised, at the center of the genealogy that leads to Jesus.

Schematic genealogies. Arranging genealogies in symmetrical patterns was not unusual. Genesis 5 and 11 each list ten generations, from Adam to Noah and from Shem to Abraham. Such patterns aided memory and highlighted significant figures without claiming to name every generation.

“After His mother Mary was betrothed to Joseph,”

Jewish marriage took place in two stages. The first was betrothal, a formal and legally binding commitment made before witnesses. A betrothed couple were called husband and wife, and the relationship could be ended only by divorce. Unfaithfulness during betrothal was treated as adultery (Deuteronomy 22:23–24). The bride, however, normally remained in her father's home, often for about a year. The second stage came when the husband brought his bride into his home, the marriage was celebrated, and the couple began living together. Mary and Joseph were in the first stage.

“before they came together,”

The phrase refers to the couple beginning their life together, including sexual union. Matthew states plainly that Mary's pregnancy began before this. Joseph was not the father.

“she was found with child of the Holy Spirit.”

“Was found” means her pregnancy became evident. Matthew states at once what Joseph does not yet know: the child is “of the Holy Spirit.” Jesus' conception was a creative act of God. Luke records the angel's words to Mary: “The Holy Spirit will come upon you, and the power of the Highest will overshadow you; therefore, also, that Holy One who is to be born will be called the Son of God” (Luke 1:35). The Spirit who moved over the waters at creation (Genesis 1:2) now brings into being the life of the One through whom God will make all things new.

The virgin conception is essential to who Jesus is. He is truly human, “born of a woman” (Galatians 4:4), and He is the eternal Son of God who took on human flesh (John 1:14). Scripture affirms both, and Matthew's account shows both from the very beginning.

“Of the Holy Spirit.” The Greek ek pneumatos hagiou (“from the Holy Spirit”) identifies the Spirit as the source of the conception. The same phrase appears in verse 20. The Holy Spirit is active at every stage of Jesus' life in Matthew: His conception, His anointing at baptism (3:16), His ministry of casting out demons (12:28), and the promise that He will baptize others with the Holy Spirit (3:11).

“Was minded” means he had made up his mind. “Put her away” refers to divorce, the legal means of ending a betrothal. “Secretly” means quietly, without a public accusation. Matthew does not describe the procedure Joseph planned, but his intent was to end the betrothal with as little shame to Mary as possible.

Study Notes

Joseph's options under the law. Deuteronomy 22:23–24 prescribed death for a betrothed woman who willingly lay with another man. By the first century, Roman rule restricted the power of Jewish courts to carry out executions (John 18:31), and the death penalty for adultery seems rarely to have been enforced. A public divorce with formal charges, however, would still have brought lasting disgrace. Later Jewish sources indicate that a divorce could be handled quietly, with a written certificate given before witnesses.

Righteousness and mercy in Matthew. Joseph's conduct anticipates a theme Jesus will teach throughout Matthew. Twice Jesus quotes Hosea 6:6, “I desire mercy and not sacrifice” (9:13; 12:7), and He rebukes the Pharisees for neglecting “justice and mercy and faith” (23:23). Joseph's righteousness held to God's standards while seeking the good of the person who appeared to have wronged him.

Jesus and Joshua. In the Septuagint, Joshua's name is spelled Iēsous, the same Greek name given to Jesus. The KJV twice reads “Jesus” where Joshua is meant (Acts 7:45; Hebrews 4:8). Matthew's fulfillment formula. About ten times Matthew introduces Old Testament passages with similar words: “that it might be fulfilled which was spoken.” These statements reflect his purpose of showing that Jesus is the Messiah the Scriptures promised. Who is speaking? Verses 22–23 could be read as the angel's words continuing, but Matthew uses similar fulfillment statements elsewhere to explain how events fulfill Scripture. These verses are best understood the same way.

“Behold, the virgin shall be with child, and bear a Son,”

Matthew quotes Isaiah 7:14. “The virgin” translates the Greek word parthenos, which refers to a young woman who has not had sexual relations with a man. Matthew has already made Mary's virginity clear (vv. 18, 20) and will state it again in verse 25. The birth of Jesus is the sign God promised.

Isaiah first spoke these words to the house of David in the days of King Ahaz (see v. 9). Jerusalem was under threat, and Ahaz refused to trust the LORD. God gave a sign that carried both a warning and a promise. Isaiah said that before the child would know to refuse evil and choose good, the two kings Ahaz feared would be gone (Isaiah 7:15–16). The prophecy spoke to Ahaz's own time.

Yet Isaiah's message about Immanuel does not end in chapter 7. In the following chapters the land of Judah is called “your land, O Immanuel” (Isaiah 8:8), and the prophet announces a Child who will sit on David's throne and be called “Wonderful, Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace” (Isaiah 9:6–7). No ordinary child of Ahaz's day could bear those names. Matthew sees the full meaning of Isaiah's sign in Jesus. He is the only child ever conceived by a virgin, and He is the only one who is truly “God with us.”

“and they shall call His name Immanuel,”

Jesus was not called Immanuel as a personal name. His name, given by the angel, is Jesus. Immanuel describes who He is. “They shall call” refers to those who recognize Him for who He is and confess it. His name, Jesus, tells what He does: He saves. Immanuel tells who He is: God present with His people.

“which is translated, ‘God with us.’”

Matthew translates the Hebrew name into Greek. In Jesus, God Himself has come to dwell among His people.

Throughout Scripture God reveals His desire to dwell with His people. He walked with Adam in the garden, filled the tabernacle and the temple with His glory, and promised through the prophets to dwell among His people forever (Ezekiel 37:27). In Jesus that desire reaches its fulfillment. God came Himself, took on human flesh, and lived among His people (John 1:14).

Matthew's Gospel begins and ends with this truth. Here Jesus is “God with us.” In the middle of the Gospel He promises, “Where two or three are gathered together in My name, I am there in the midst of them” (18:20). In the final verse He tells His disciples, “I am with you always, even to the end of the age” (28:20).

Study Notes

Almah and parthenos. Isaiah 7:14 uses the Hebrew word almah, meaning a young woman of marriageable age. In the Old Testament it describes unmarried young women such as Rebekah (Genesis 24:43) and Miriam (Exodus 2:8), and no passage clearly uses it of a sexually experienced woman. The Jewish translators of the Septuagint, working about two centuries before Christ, rendered it parthenos, the ordinary Greek word for a virgin. Some argue that Isaiah would have used betulah if he meant “virgin,” but betulah is not always a precise technical term either, and its use in passages such as Joel 1:8 is debated, so the argument does not settle the question. Matthew follows the Septuagint, and his own account states plainly that Mary conceived without sexual relations (vv. 18, 25). Jesus is the fulfillment of Isaiah's sign.

The sign in Ahaz's day. Interpreters differ on whether Isaiah 7:14 had an initial fulfillment in a child born in Ahaz's time. Proposals include Isaiah's own son (Isaiah 8:3–4) or a child of the royal family. Hezekiah is sometimes suggested, though the chronology of his reign makes this difficult. Others take the prophecy as speaking directly of the Messiah alone. Whatever view is taken of Ahaz's day, Matthew's statement is clear: the virgin conception of Jesus fulfills the sign, and Jesus is Immanuel.

“They shall call.” The Hebrew text of Isaiah 7:14 reads “she shall call,” and the Septuagint reads “you shall call.” Matthew's “they shall call” points to the people who will recognize and confess who Jesus is.

Bookends of the Gospel. The promise of God's presence in 1:23 and Jesus' promise in 28:20 frame the entire Gospel. Writers sometimes used this technique, called an inclusio, to mark a central theme.
