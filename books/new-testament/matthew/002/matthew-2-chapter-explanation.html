Matthew 2:1
Matthew 2:1
NKJV

Matthew 2:1 Now after Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judea in the days of Herod the king, behold, wise men from the East came to Jerusalem,(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 2:1 Jesus was born in Bethlehem in Judea, during the reign of King Herod. About that time some wise men from eastern lands arrived in Jerusalem, asking, (NLT)

Main Idea

After Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judea during Herod’s reign, wise men from the East arrived in Jerusalem. The promised King has been born in David’s town, and the first people Matthew shows seeking Him are foreigners.

Verse Explanation

After Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judea during Herod’s reign, wise men from the East arrived in Jerusalem. The promised King has been born in David’s town, and the first people Matthew shows seeking Him are foreigners.

“Now after Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judea”

Matthew 1 closed with Joseph naming the child Jesus (1:25). Matthew does not describe the birth itself but moves to what happened afterward. He names the town carefully. Bethlehem lay about six miles south of Jerusalem and was David’s hometown (1 Samuel 16:1, 18). “Of Judea” distinguishes it from a Bethlehem in Galilee (Joshua 19:15) and places Jesus’ birth in the territory of Judah, the tribe from which the promised king would come (Genesis 49:10). Matthew has already called Jesus “the Son of David” (1:1), and His birthplace confirms it.

“in the days of Herod the king,”

This is Herod the Great, who ruled Judea under Roman authority from 37 BC until his death in 4 BC. He was a gifted builder and a shrewd politician, but he was also suspicious and cruel, ready to kill anyone he saw as a threat, including members of his own family. Matthew calls him “the king,” and the title matters. This chapter is about two kings: the one who held the throne and the One who was born to it.

“behold, wise men from the East came to Jerusalem,”

“Behold” draws attention to something unexpected. The “wise men” were scholars and royal advisers from the lands east of Israel, known for studying the stars, interpreting dreams, and preserving ancient writings. “From the East” most likely means Persia or Babylon, though some suggest Arabia. Matthew does not say how many there were, and he does not call them kings. They were Gentiles, outsiders to Israel, yet they are the first people in Matthew’s Gospel to come looking for Jesus. They went to Jerusalem because the capital was the natural place to look for a newborn king.

Study Notes

Herod died in 4 BC. Since Jesus was born before Herod’s death, and Herod later targeted boys up to two years old (2:16), Jesus was probably born sometime between about 7 and 4 BC. The oddity of Jesus being born “BC” comes from a miscalculation by Dionysius Exiguus, the sixth-century monk who set up the calendar dating from Christ’s birth.

The Greek word magoi was used for priests, astrologers, dream interpreters, and sometimes sorcerers. Herodotus describes the magi as a priestly tribe among the Medes. Matthew gives neither their number nor their names and does not call them kings. The tradition of three visitors reflects the three gifts in 2:11.

Herod was an Idumean, descended from the Edomites, whom the Jewish ruler John Hyrcanus had forced to adopt Judaism around 125 BC. Many Jews never accepted Herod as a legitimate king. The Roman Senate gave him the title “king of the Jews” in 40 BC.

Matthew 2:2
Matthew 2:2
NKJV

Matthew 2:2 saying, "Where is He who has been born King of the Jews? For we have seen His star in the East and have come to worship Him."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 2:2 “Where is the newborn king of the Jews? We saw his star as it rose, and we have come to worship him.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The wise men ask, “Where is He who has been born King of the Jews?” and explain that they have seen His star and have come to worship Him. They recognize Jesus as a King by birth, and God has guided them by His star so that they come seeking to worship.

Verse Explanation

The wise men ask, “Where is He who has been born King of the Jews?” and explain that they have seen His star and have come to worship Him. They recognize Jesus as a King by birth, and God has guided them by His star so that they come seeking to worship.

“saying, ‘Where is He who has been born King of the Jews?”

The wise men do not ask whether a king has been born. They are sure He has; they only ask where He is. Their wording is significant. He “has been born” King. Herod had been appointed king of the Jews by Rome, but this Child is King by birth.

The title “King of the Jews” does not appear again in Matthew until Jesus’ trial and crucifixion (27:11, 29, 37), where it is again spoken by Gentiles. At the beginning and the end of His earthly life, outsiders give Jesus the title His own nation’s leaders refused to give Him.

“For we have seen His star in the East”

They call it “His star,” a star that belonged to this King and announced His birth. Matthew does not explain what the star was, and nothing in the passage endorses astrology. God used a sign in the heavens to announce His Son, and He would soon lead the wise men past the star to the Scriptures (2:5–6). Many Jews knew Balaam’s prophecy, “A Star shall come out of Jacob; A Scepter shall rise out of Israel” (Numbers 24:17), which linked a star with a coming ruler. Matthew does not quote it, but it stands in the background of the wise men’s words.

“and have come to worship Him.”

“Worship” translates a Greek word meaning to bow down or lie prostrate before someone. It could describe the honor given to a king, but Matthew uses it repeatedly for the reverence given to Jesus (8:2; 14:33; 28:9, 17). It appears three times in this chapter (2:2, 8, 11). The wise men had traveled hundreds of miles for one purpose: to bow before this King.

Study Notes

Proposals for the star include a conjunction of Jupiter and Saturn in 7 BC, a comet, a nova, and a supernatural light. Matthew does not identify an astronomical event. The account describes the star going before the visitors and stopping over the place where the Child was (2:9).

The Greek phrase here (en tē anatolē) is singular, while “from the East” in 2:1 is plural. The singular was often used for the rising of a heavenly body, so the phrase may also be translated we saw His star when it rose or at its rising (as in the ESV).

The Roman historians Suetonius (Vespasian 4) and Tacitus (Histories 5.13) mention a widespread belief in the East that someone coming from Judea would rule the world. Both connect it with events of the Jewish War (AD 66–70), but they describe it as an old and established belief.

Matthew 2:3
NKJV

Matthew 2:3 When Herod the king heard this, he was troubled, and all Jerusalem with him.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 2:3 King Herod was deeply disturbed when he heard this, as was everyone in Jerusalem. (NLT)

Main Idea

When King Herod hears of a newborn King of the Jews, he is troubled, and all Jerusalem with him. The news that brings worshipers from far away unsettles the ruler who wants no rival to his throne.

Verse Explanation

When King Herod hears of a newborn King of the Jews, he is troubled, and all Jerusalem with him. The news that brings worshipers from far away unsettles the ruler who wants no rival to his throne.

“When Herod the king heard this, he was troubled,”

Matthew again calls Herod “the king,” setting him against the King the wise men are seeking. “Troubled” means shaken or thrown into turmoil. Herod had spent decades guarding his throne. He had executed a wife and three of his sons, along with many others he suspected of plotting against him. A report of a royal birth he knew nothing about was, to him, a threat.

“and all Jerusalem with him.”

The city shared his unrest. Jerusalem knew how Herod reacted to threats, and its people feared what he might do. Their response is striking. Jerusalem, the city of the temple and the Scriptures, should have greeted news of the Messiah with hope. Instead it was troubled, while Gentile strangers arrived eager to worship. This anticipates the end of Matthew’s Gospel, when the whole city is stirred at Jesus’ arrival (21:10) and its leaders reject Him.

Study Notes

The Jewish historian Josephus records that Herod executed his wife Mariamne (29 BC), her two sons Alexander and Aristobulus (7 BC), and his eldest son Antipater only days before his own death. A saying attributed to the emperor Augustus played on the similar Greek words for pig (hus) and son (huios): it was safer to be Herod’s pig than his son, since Herod kept the Jewish food laws and would not kill a pig (Macrobius, Saturnalia 2.4.11).

The same verb (tarassō) describes the disciples’ terror when they saw Jesus walking on the sea (14:26).

Matthew 2:4 And when he had gathered all the chief priests and scribes of the people together, he inquired of them where the Christ was to be born.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 2:4 He called a meeting of the leading priests and teachers of religious law and asked, “Where is the Messiah supposed to be born?” (NLT)

Main Idea

Herod gathers the chief priests and scribes of the people and asks where the Christ is to be born. The king who fears the Messiah turns to Israel’s religious leaders for the answer Scripture provides.

Verse Explanation

Herod gathers the chief priests and scribes of the people and asks where the Christ is to be born. The king who fears the Messiah turns to Israel’s religious leaders for the answer Scripture provides.

“And when he had gathered all the chief priests and scribes of the people together,”

The chief priests included the high priest, former high priests, and leading members of the priestly families who oversaw the temple. The scribes were experts in the Law of Moses who copied, studied, and taught the Scriptures. Herod went to the people most likely to know what the Scriptures said about the Messiah. Later in Matthew these same two groups stand together in opposing Jesus (16:21; 20:18; 21:15).

“he inquired of them where the Christ was to be born.”

Herod translates the wise men’s “King of the Jews” into Israel’s own term, “the Christ,” meaning the Anointed One, the Messiah. He understood at once that the wise men were speaking of the promised King. His question is precise. He does not ask whether the Christ will come, but where He is to be born.

Study Notes

The Greek christos translates the Hebrew mashiach, “anointed.” Kings, priests, and occasionally prophets were anointed with oil when they were set apart for their office. By Jesus’ day the title had come to mean the expected Son of David who would deliver and restore Israel (Psalm 2:2; Daniel 9:25–26). Matthew has already used it of Jesus (1:1, 16–18).

Matthew’s wording does not require a formal meeting of the Sanhedrin, the Jewish ruling council. Herod’s relationship with the council was hostile; Josephus reports that he had many of its members killed when he came to power (Antiquities 14.175). Herod may simply have summoned the leading experts he needed.

Main Idea

The leaders answer that the Christ will be born “In Bethlehem of Judea,” because the prophet has written it. They correctly identify the place from the Scriptures.

Verse Explanation

The leaders answer that the Christ will be born “In Bethlehem of Judea,” because the prophet has written it. They correctly identify the place from the Scriptures.

“So they said to him, ‘In Bethlehem of Judea,”

The answer came immediately. The leaders did not need to debate the question. Micah’s prophecy was well known, and others in Jesus’ day also expected the Christ to come “from the town of Bethlehem, where David was” (John 7:42).

“for thus it is written by the prophet:”

“It is written” appeals to Scripture as settled authority. The prophet is Micah, whose words are given in the next verse. The leaders’ certainty came from God’s Word, and Matthew wants his readers to see that Jesus’ birthplace was announced centuries before He was born.

Study Notes

The Greek verb is in the perfect tense, it has been written and still stands written. Jesus uses the same expression three times to answer Satan in the wilderness (4:4, 7, 10).

Matthew 2:6
Matthew 2:6
NKJV

Matthew 2:6 "˜But you, Bethlehem, in the land of Judah, Are not the least among the rulers of Judah; For out of you shall come a Ruler Who will shepherd My people Israel.' "(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 2:6 ‘And you, O Bethlehem in the land of Judah,are not least among the ruling cities of Judah,for a ruler will come from youwho will be the shepherd for my people Israel.’ ” (NLT)

Main Idea

The chief priests and scribes join Micah 5:2 to the shepherding words God spoke to David: Bethlehem is not least among Judah’s rulers, for from it will come “a Ruler Who will shepherd My people Israel.” The child is David’s promised heir, the King who will shepherd God’s people.

Verse Explanation

The chief priests and scribes join Micah 5:2 to the shepherding words God spoke to David: Bethlehem is not least among Judah’s rulers, for from it will come “a Ruler Who will shepherd My people Israel.” The child is David’s promised heir, the King who will shepherd God’s people.

“But you, Bethlehem, in the land of Judah,”

The prophecy addresses Bethlehem directly. Micah’s original wording is “Bethlehem Ephrathah” (Micah 5:2), using the old name for the area (Genesis 35:19; Ruth 4:11). The quotation as given here reads “in the land of Judah,” keeping the focus on Judah, the royal tribe.

“Are not the least among the rulers of Judah;”

Micah said Bethlehem was little among the thousands of Judah, a small town among Judah’s many clans. The quotation here stresses the result of God’s choice: because of who comes from it, Bethlehem is by no means the least. Both statements are true. Bethlehem was small in itself, and God’s purpose made it great. “Rulers” refers to the leading clans of Judah and the chiefs who represented them.

“For out of you shall come a Ruler”

This is why Bethlehem is honored. A Ruler will come from it. Micah’s full verse adds that this Ruler’s “goings forth are from of old, From everlasting” (Micah 5:2), pointing to someone far greater than an ordinary descendant of David.

“Who will shepherd My people Israel.”

This line echoes God’s words to David: “You shall shepherd My people Israel” (2 Samuel 5:2). David was a shepherd before he was a king, and God described his reign in a shepherd’s terms. The Ruler from Bethlehem will rule in the same way, leading and caring for the people who belong to God. “My people” reminds the reader that Israel belongs to the LORD, and the Messiah rules them on His behalf.

The contrast with Herod is plain. Herod ruled by fear and was about to kill children to protect his throne. The true King would shepherd.

Study Notes

Matthew’s wording does not exactly match either the Hebrew text or the Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) of Micah 5:2. It combines Micah 5:2 with 2 Samuel 5:2 and words the prophecy so its meaning stands out. Jewish teachers of the time commonly quoted Scripture this way, joining related texts and drawing out their sense.

The Hebrew word in Micah 5:2 (alphe, “thousands” or “clans”) is written with the same consonants as a word for “chiefs” or “leaders” (alluphe). Reading it the second way gives “rulers,” which matches Matthew’s wording.

The Targum, an ancient Aramaic paraphrase used in the synagogues, renders Micah 5:2 (5:1 in the Hebrew Bible) as from you shall come forth before Me the Messiah. This confirms that Jews read the verse as a prophecy of the Messiah.

Matthew 2:8
NKJV

Matthew 2:8 And he sent them to Bethlehem and said, "Go and search carefully for the young Child, and when you have found Him, bring back word to me, that I may come and worship Him also."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 2:8 Then he told them, “Go to Bethlehem and search carefully for the child. And when you find him, come back and tell me so that I can go and worship him, too!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Herod sends the wise men to Bethlehem to search carefully for the child and report back, “that I may come and worship Him also.” His claim to want to worship hides his intention to destroy the child.

Verse Explanation

Herod sends the wise men to Bethlehem to search carefully for the child and report back, “that I may come and worship Him also.” His claim to want to worship hides his intention to destroy the child.

“And he sent them to Bethlehem and said,”

Herod passes on what he learned from the chief priests and scribes. He sends the wise men to the right town, but he intends to use them as his agents.

“Go and search carefully for the young Child,”

“Search carefully” calls for an exact, thorough inquiry. “Young Child” translates a Greek word for a little child (paidion), not the word for a newborn infant that Luke uses of the baby in the manger (Luke 2:12, 16). Together with “the house” in 2:11 and Herod’s two-year limit in 2:16, this suggests that some time had passed since Jesus’ birth, perhaps several months or more.

“and when you have found Him, bring back word to me, that I may come and worship Him also.”

Herod uses the same word for “worship” the wise men used in 2:2. He presents himself as a fellow seeker, but 2:13 and 2:16 expose his real purpose. He meant to use the wise men to locate the Child so he could destroy Him.

Study Notes

Many nativity scenes place the wise men at the manger on the night of Jesus’ birth, but Matthew’s details point to a later visit. Luke records that Jesus was presented at the temple forty days after His birth (Luke 2:22–38). This most likely took place before the wise men arrived, since after their visit the family fled directly from Bethlehem to Egypt.

The word paidion appears nine times in this chapter (2:8, 9, 11, 13 twice, 14, 20 twice, 21). Matthew keeps attention on the Child at every stage of the story.

“When they heard the king, they departed;”

The wise men set out at once for Bethlehem. They now had what the star alone had not given them: the place named in Scripture.

“and behold, the star which they had seen in the East went before them,”

“Behold” marks another surprise. The same star they had seen in the East appears again and now leads them. Matthew does not say the star guided them all the way from their homeland; their decision to go to Jerusalem may have been based on what they first observed. On the road to Bethlehem, though, the star went ahead of them like a guide.

“till it came and stood over where the young Child was.”

The star stopped over the specific place where Jesus was. No ordinary star or planet can single out one house in a town, and Matthew presents this as God’s direct guidance. The sign that announced the King now led the seekers to His door.

Verse Explanation

When they see the star, the wise men rejoice “with exceedingly great joy.” Their long search is ending, and the prospect of finding the King fills them with overflowing gladness.

“When they saw the star,”

The reappearance of the star assured them that they were on the right road and that God was leading them to the King they had come to worship.

“they rejoiced with exceedingly great joy.”

Matthew piles up words to describe their joy. The Greek reads literally, they rejoiced a great joy exceedingly. Their joy answers Herod’s troubled reaction and that of all Jerusalem (2:3). The same news produced alarm in the palace and overflowing joy on the road to Bethlehem.

Study Notes

The Greek joins the verb for rejoicing with its related noun, then adds words meaning “great” and “exceedingly.” The wording intensifies the description of their joy.

Matthew 2:11 And when they had come into the house, they saw the young Child with Mary His mother, and fell down and worshiped Him. And when they had opened their treasures, they presented gifts to Him: gold, frankincense, and myrrh.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 2:11 They entered the house and saw the child with his mother, Mary, and they bowed down and worshiped him. Then they opened their treasure chests and gave him gifts of gold, frankincense, and myrrh. (NLT)

Main Idea

Entering the house, the wise men see the young Child with Mary His mother, fall down and worship Him, and present gifts of gold, frankincense, and myrrh. Verse Explanation

Entering the house, the wise men see the young Child with Mary His mother, fall down and worship Him, and present gifts of gold, frankincense, and myrrh. Jerusalem’s leaders supplied the answer from Scripture, but these foreign visitors are the ones Matthew shows coming to give the King of Israel honor and treasure fit for royalty.

“And when they had come into the house,”

By now the family was living in a house in Bethlehem rather than in the place where Jesus was born (Luke 2:7). Matthew’s account fits a visit some time after the birth.

“they saw the young Child with Mary His mother,”

Matthew mentions Mary but not Joseph. The phrase “the young Child and His mother” is repeated through the chapter (2:13, 14, 20, 21), always naming the Child first. Joseph’s role is to protect them, and the focus stays on Jesus.

“and fell down and worshiped Him.”

The wise men bowed to the ground before Jesus. “Worshiped” is the same word used in 2:2 and 2:8. They worshiped Him, not His mother. Learned men of standing knelt before a small child in an ordinary house because they recognized who He was.

“And when they had opened their treasures,”

“Treasures” refers to the chests or boxes in which valuables were carried. They opened what they had guarded over the long road.

“they presented gifts to Him: gold, frankincense, and myrrh.”

These were costly gifts fit for royalty. Gold was the most precious metal and the natural gift for a king. Frankincense was a fragrant resin burned as incense and was an ingredient in the holy incense used in Israel’s worship (Exodus 30:34). Myrrh was an aromatic resin used in perfume (Psalm 45:8), in the holy anointing oil (Exodus 30:23), and in preparing bodies for burial (John 19:39).

Isaiah foresaw nations coming to Israel’s light, bringing “gold and incense” and proclaiming the praises of the LORD (Isaiah 60:3, 6; see also Psalm 72:10–11, 15). Gentiles bowing before the Child and offering Him gold and incense show those hopes beginning to come true.

Study Notes

The gifts may have helped support the family during their sudden flight to Egypt and their stay there (2:13–15). Matthew does not say so, but the timing is notable.

Warned by God in a dream not to return to Herod, the wise men go home another way. God protects His Son by turning the visitors away from the king’s plot.

“Then, being divinely warned in a dream”

“Divinely warned” translates a Greek word used for a message or instruction from God. God spoke to these Gentiles in a dream, just as He had spoken to Joseph (1:20). The dream revealed what they could not see for themselves: Herod’s real intention.

“that they should not return to Herod,”

Herod had told them to bring back word (2:8). God’s instruction overruled the king’s. Obeying God meant defying a powerful ruler, and the wise men chose to obey God.

“they departed for their own country another way.”

They returned home by a different route, avoiding Jerusalem. “Departed” (literally “withdrew”) becomes a recurring word in this chapter. Joseph will “depart” for Egypt (2:13–14) and “turn aside” into Galilee (2:22), each time withdrawing from danger at God’s direction.

Study Notes

The verb (chrēmatizō) is used for God’s instruction to Moses (Hebrews 8:5) and His warning to Noah (Hebrews 11:7). Matthew uses it again for Joseph in 2:22.

The verb anachōreō, translated “departed,” recurs in 2:12–14 and 22 for withdrawal from danger.

Main Idea

After the wise men leave, an angel of the Lord tells Joseph in a dream to take the Child and His mother and flee to Egypt, because Herod will seek to destroy Him. God warns Joseph in time to preserve the life of His Son.

Verse Explanation

After the wise men leave, an angel of the Lord tells Joseph in a dream to take the Child and His mother and flee to Egypt, because Herod will seek to destroy Him. God warns Joseph in time to preserve the life of His Son.

“Now when they had departed, behold, an angel of the Lord appeared to Joseph in a dream,”

As soon as the wise men left, God acted again. This is the second of four dreams in which God guides Joseph (1:20; 2:13, 19, 22). Matthew presents Joseph as a man who receives God’s direction and acts on it.

“saying, ‘Arise, take the young Child and His mother, flee to Egypt,”

The command is urgent and specific. Joseph is told what to do, whom to take, and where to go. Egypt lay to the southwest, a trip of several days, and it was outside Herod’s control. It had a large Jewish population, and it had served as a place of refuge for God’s people before (Genesis 12:10; 46:3–4; 1 Kings 11:40; Jeremiah 43:7).

“and stay there until I bring you word;”

Joseph is given no date for the return. He is to stay until God speaks again. His next move will depend on God’s next word.

“for Herod will seek the young Child to destroy Him.”

The angel exposes Herod’s purpose. “Destroy” means to kill. The Ruler who will shepherd God’s people (2:6) is now the target of a king who will kill to keep his throne. Matthew uses the same verb later when the Pharisees plot “how they might destroy Him” (12:14) and when the chief priests persuade the crowd to ask that Jesus be destroyed (27:20). The hostility that greeted Jesus at His birth will meet Him again at the end.

Study Notes

Egypt had been under Roman rule since 30 BC, but it was not part of Herod’s kingdom. The first-century Jewish writer Philo estimated that a million Jews lived there (Flaccus 43), with a large community in Alexandria. A Jewish family from Judea could find a home among them.

Coptic Christian tradition traces a route the family followed through several places in Egypt. Scripture gives no details about where they lived or for how long, beyond the limit set by Herod’s death (2:15).

Matthew 2:15 and was there until the death of Herod, that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet, saying, "Out of Egypt I called My Son."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 2:15 and they stayed there until Herod’s death. This fulfilled what the Lord had spoken through the prophet: “I called my Son out of Egypt.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The family stays in Egypt until Herod’s death, fulfilling the Lord’s word through the prophet, “Out of Egypt I called My Son.” Hosea spoke of God calling Israel out of Egypt, and Matthew shows Jesus, God’s true Son, taking up and fulfilling Israel’s story.

Verse Explanation

The family stays in Egypt until Herod’s death, fulfilling the Lord’s word through the prophet, “Out of Egypt I called My Son.” Hosea spoke of God calling Israel out of Egypt, and Matthew shows Jesus, God’s true Son, taking up and fulfilling Israel’s story.

“and was there until the death of Herod,”

Matthew looks ahead to 2:19. The stay in Egypt was temporary, limited by Herod’s life. Since Herod died in 4 BC, the family may have been in Egypt anywhere from a few months to a year or two.

“that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet, saying,”

This is Matthew’s second fulfillment statement (see 1:22). The Lord is the speaker, and the prophet is His instrument. “Fulfilled” means more than a prediction coming true. It means filled full: an Old Testament word, pattern, or promise reaches its intended completion in Christ.

“Out of Egypt I called My Son.”

The quotation is from Hosea 11:1: “When Israel was a child, I loved him, And out of Egypt I called My son.” In Hosea the words look back to the Exodus. God had called Israel His “son” when He brought the nation out of Egypt (Exodus 4:22–23). Hosea was remembering God’s love for Israel, not predicting the Messiah’s stay in Egypt.

Matthew sees that the story of God’s son Israel finds its fullest meaning in the story of God’s Son Jesus. Israel went down into Egypt and was brought out by God, and Jesus does the same. Hosea goes on to say that Israel turned away from God after He called them (Hosea 11:2). Jesus is the Son who will be faithful where Israel failed. The pattern continues in the chapters that follow. Israel passed through the sea and was tested forty years in the wilderness; Jesus will pass through the waters of baptism (3:13–17) and be tested forty days in the wilderness (4:1–11).

Matthew places the quotation here, before the return in 2:21, because Egypt is the place from which God will call His Son.

Study Notes

The Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) translates Hosea 11:1 as I called his children, which would not support Matthew’s point. Matthew’s “My Son” follows the Hebrew text, showing that he was working closely with the original wording.

Matthew 2:16–18 — Herod Kills the Boys of Bethlehem

NLT

Matthew 2:16 Herod was furious when he realized that the wise men had outwitted him. He sent soldiers to kill all the boys in and around Bethlehem who were two years old and under, based on the wise men’s report of the star’s first appearance. (NLT)

Main Idea

When Herod realizes the wise men have outwitted him, he is furious and has all the boys two years old and under in Bethlehem and its region killed, based on the time he had learned. His rage against a rival King falls on Bethlehem’s children.

Verse Explanation

When Herod realizes the wise men have outwitted him, he is furious and has all the boys two years old and under in Bethlehem and its region killed, based on the time he had learned. His rage against a rival King falls on Bethlehem’s children.

“Then Herod, when he saw that he was deceived by the wise men,”

The word translated “deceived” means mocked or made a fool of. Herod took the wise men’s failure to return as an insult. They had not set out to trick him; they had obeyed God’s warning. Herod, who had planned to deceive them, now saw himself as the one deceived.

“was exceedingly angry;”

Herod’s fury is the dark counterpart of the wise men’s “exceedingly great joy” (2:10). Both responses overflowed, in opposite directions. Herod’s rage was the reaction of a man who could not stand to lose control.

“and he sent forth and put to death all the male children who were in Bethlehem and in all its districts,”

Herod sent soldiers to kill every boy in Bethlehem and the countryside around it. Bethlehem was a small town, and many scholars estimate that the number of boys killed was somewhere between ten and thirty. The number does not lessen the horror. Each one was a son, and each was mourned.

“from two years old and under, according to the time which he had determined from the wise men.”

Herod based the age limit on what he had learned in 2:7, likely adding a margin to make sure the Child could not escape. The order is ruthless, and it fits what history records of Herod’s character.

Study Notes

No other ancient source mentions this event, which is not surprising. Josephus, the main historian of Herod’s reign, records many larger atrocities, and the killing of a few children in a small village would not have stood out among them. The order fits Herod’s conduct near the end of his life. Josephus reports that, as he lay dying, Herod had leading men from across the nation shut in the hippodrome at Jericho and ordered them killed at his death, so the nation would mourn when he died (Antiquities 17.174–179). The order was not carried out.

The verb (empaizō) usually means to mock or ridicule. Matthew uses it later for the soldiers, priests, and scribes who mocked Jesus at His trial and crucifixion (20:19; 27:29, 31, 41).

Main Idea

Matthew states that the word spoken by Jeremiah the prophet was fulfilled in this tragedy. He sets Bethlehem’s grief within God’s unfolding Word without excusing Herod’s guilt.

Verse Explanation

Matthew states that the word spoken by Jeremiah the prophet was fulfilled in this tragedy. He sets Bethlehem’s grief within God’s unfolding Word without excusing Herod’s guilt.

“Then was fulfilled what was spoken by Jeremiah the prophet, saying:”

This is the third fulfillment statement in the chapter, but its wording is different. In 2:15 and 2:23 Matthew writes “that it might be fulfilled,” language that expresses purpose. Here he writes, “Then was fulfilled,” the same form he uses when he describes the potter’s field bought with Judas’s betrayal money (27:9). The difference is worth noticing. In both places the fulfillment comes through human wickedness, and the wording keeps attention on the event rather than on a purpose behind the crime. Jeremiah’s words of grief found a new fulfillment in the grief of Bethlehem, but the guilt for the killing belonged to Herod.

Study Notes

Matthew is the only New Testament writer who mentions Jeremiah by name (2:17; 16:14; 27:9).

Main Idea

Jeremiah’s words describe a voice heard in Ramah, “Rachel weeping for her children,” refusing to be comforted because they are gone. Israel’s ancient lament over exile is heard again in Bethlehem’s mothers, and Jeremiah’s surrounding promise of restoration offers hope beyond the sorrow.

Verse Explanation

Jeremiah’s words describe a voice heard in Ramah, “Rachel weeping for her children,” refusing to be comforted because they are gone. Israel’s ancient lament over exile is heard again in Bethlehem’s mothers, and Jeremiah’s surrounding promise of restoration offers hope beyond the sorrow.

“A voice was heard in Ramah,”

The quotation is from Jeremiah 31:15. Ramah was a town about five miles north of Jerusalem in the territory of Benjamin. In Jeremiah’s day it was the place where captives from Judah were gathered before being taken to Babylon (Jeremiah 40:1).

“Lamentation, weeping, and great mourning,”

Three words for grief are piled together to describe loud, unrestrained mourning.

“Rachel weeping for her children,”

Rachel was Jacob’s beloved wife and the mother of Joseph and Benjamin. She died giving birth to Benjamin and was buried “on the way to Ephrath (that is, Bethlehem)” (Genesis 35:19). In Jeremiah, Rachel represents the mothers of Israel weeping as their children were carried off into exile. Her burial near Bethlehem makes the connection to Bethlehem’s mothers especially fitting.

“Refusing to be comforted, Because they are no more.”

The grief is beyond consolation, because the children are gone. Matthew lets the sorrow stand without softening it.

Yet Jeremiah’s next words bring hope: “Refrain your voice from weeping, And your eyes from tears” (Jeremiah 31:16–17). Readers who knew Jeremiah 31 would hear that hope behind the weeping. The Child who escaped Herod’s soldiers is the One through whom God will bring it to pass.

Study Notes

Genesis 35:19 and Genesis 48:7 place Rachel’s tomb on the road to Ephrath, which is Bethlehem. First Samuel 10:2 places it in the territory of Benjamin at Zelzah, north of Jerusalem. The difference may reflect uncertainty about the exact site, or a burial place near the border between the two tribal areas. Either way, Rachel was associated with both Ramah’s region and Bethlehem, which fits Matthew’s use of the passage.

After Herod dies, an angel of the Lord appears again to Joseph in a dream in Egypt. The tyrant who tried to destroy the child is gone, and God speaks to guide the family once more.

“Now when Herod was dead,”

Herod died in 4 BC after a painful illness. The king who tried to kill the Child King was gone, and the Child was alive. Matthew states it plainly and without comment.

“behold, an angel of the Lord appeared in a dream to Joseph in Egypt,”

This is Joseph’s third dream. God had told him to stay in Egypt “until I bring you word” (2:13), and now that word comes. “In Egypt” reminds the reader that Joseph had stayed exactly where he was told.

Study Notes

Josephus describes Herod’s final illness, including fever, itching, intestinal pain, swelling, convulsions, and bodily decay (Antiquities 17.168–169; Jewish War 1.656). Herod died at Jericho shortly before Passover.

Verse Explanation

The angel tells Joseph to take the Child and His mother back to the land of Israel, because those who sought the Child’s life are dead. The words echo God’s message to Moses to return to Egypt after his enemies had died.

“saying, ‘Arise, take the young Child and His mother,”

The command repeats the wording of 2:13. The same instruction that sent the family into Egypt now brings them out.

“and go to the land of Israel,”

“The land of Israel” appears in the New Testament only here and in 2:21. The phrase recalls the land God promised to Abraham and gave to Israel after the Exodus. Having been called out of Egypt (2:15), God’s Son returns to the land of God’s people.

“for those who sought the young Child’s life are dead.”

The plural “those” may include others who served Herod’s purpose, or it may simply be a general way of referring to Herod. The wording closely echoes what God told Moses in Midian: “Go, return to Egypt; for all the men who sought your life are dead” (Exodus 4:19). Like Moses, Jesus was spared from a king who killed Hebrew boys, and He returns when the threat is gone. Moses came back to deliver Israel from Egypt. Jesus has come to “save His people from their sins” (1:21).

Study Notes

Matthew’s Greek closely resembles the Greek wording of Exodus 4:19, where Moses is told that those seeking his life have died.

Main Idea

Hearing that Herod’s son Archelaus rules Judea, Joseph is afraid to go there, and after a warning from God in a dream he turns aside to Galilee. God continues to direct the family’s path to protect the Child.

Verse Explanation

Hearing that Herod’s son Archelaus rules Judea, Joseph is afraid to go there, and after a warning from God in a dream he turns aside to Galilee. God continues to direct the family’s path to protect the Child.

“But when he heard that Archelaus was reigning over Judea instead of his father Herod,”

At Herod’s death his kingdom was divided among three of his sons. Archelaus received Judea, Samaria, and Idumea. He quickly proved as brutal as his father. Early in his rule his soldiers killed about three thousand people in the temple courts during Passover. Rome removed him from power in AD 6 after complaints from his own subjects.

“he was afraid to go there.”

Joseph’s fear was reasonable. Archelaus had his father’s character, and Judea, including Bethlehem, was under his control. Joseph seems to have intended to settle in Judea, possibly in Bethlehem, the town of David.

“And being warned by God in a dream,”

This is Joseph’s fourth and final dream in Matthew. God did not rebuke Joseph’s fear; He gave him direction. “Warned by God” is the same word used of the wise men’s warning in 2:12.

“he turned aside into the region of Galilee.”

Galilee, in the north, was ruled by another son of Herod, Herod Antipas, and was safer for the family. “Turned aside” is the same word for withdrawing used in 2:12, 13, and 14. Galilee also matters for Matthew’s larger story. Isaiah foretold that a great light would dawn on “Galilee of the Gentiles” (Isaiah 9:1–2), and Matthew will quote that prophecy when Jesus begins His ministry there (4:12–16).

Study Notes

Augustus gave Archelaus the title ethnarch (ruler of a people) rather than king, though Matthew’s “reigning” accurately describes his rule. Herod Antipas became tetrarch of Galilee and Perea; he is the Herod who later beheaded John the Baptist (14:1–12) and before whom Jesus stood at His trial (Luke 23:7–11). Philip became tetrarch of the regions northeast of the Sea of Galilee (Luke 3:1). Josephus records the Passover massacre under Archelaus (Antiquities 17.213–218) and his removal and banishment to Gaul (Antiquities 17.342–344).

Luke reports that Joseph and Mary lived in Nazareth before Jesus was born and returned there afterward (Luke 1:26–27; 2:4, 39). Matthew does not mention their earlier home, but the two accounts fit together. Matthew explains why the family did not settle in Judea, and Luke explains why Nazareth was the natural place to go.

Main Idea

Joseph settles in a town called Nazareth, fulfilling what was spoken by the prophets, “He shall be called a Nazarene.” Matthew sums up the prophets’ witness: Nazareth’s obscurity marks Jesus as the lowly, despised One they foretold, and its name echoes the Branch from Jesse’s stump in Isaiah 11:1.

Verse Explanation

Joseph settles in a town called Nazareth, fulfilling what was spoken by the prophets, “He shall be called a Nazarene.” Matthew sums up the prophets’ witness: Nazareth’s obscurity marks Jesus as the lowly, despised One they foretold, and its name echoes the Branch from Jesse’s stump in Isaiah 11:1.

“And he came and dwelt in a city called Nazareth,”

Nazareth was a small village in the hills of lower Galilee. It is never mentioned in the Old Testament, in Josephus, or in other early Jewish writings. Its reputation was poor; Nathanael asked, “Can anything good come out of Nazareth?” (John 1:46). The Son of David, born in the royal town of Bethlehem, grew up in a place most people overlooked.

“that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophets,”

Matthew says “the prophets,” plural. In his other fulfillment quotations he refers to a single prophet. Here he is summing up a theme found in several prophets rather than quoting one verse.

“He shall be called a Nazarene.”

No Old Testament verse contains these exact words. Two connections with the prophets are especially meaningful, and both may be in view.

First, “Nazarene” sounds like the Hebrew word netser, “branch,” used in Isaiah 11:1: “There shall come forth a Rod from the stem of Jesse, And a Branch shall grow out of his roots.” Other prophets also call the Messiah the Branch, using a related word (Jeremiah 23:5; Zechariah 3:8; 6:12). The town’s name may echo the prophets’ title for the coming King.

Second, coming from Nazareth meant being looked down on. The prophets foretold that the Messiah would be despised and rejected (Psalm 22:6; Isaiah 53:2–3). To be called “a Nazarene” was to be dismissed as a nobody, and Jesus’ followers were later called “the sect of the Nazarenes” with the same contempt (Acts 24:5). Either way, Jesus’ humble hometown fits what the prophets said about Him.

Study Notes

Some have connected “Nazarene” with “Nazirite,” a person under a special vow of separation to God (Numbers 6:1–21; Judges 13:5). This is unlikely. The words are spelled differently in both Hebrew and Greek, and Jesus did not live as a Nazirite. He drank wine (11:19) and touched the dead (9:25).

Excavations show Nazareth as a small farming village, home to perhaps a few hundred people. It lay about four miles from Sepphoris, a larger city that Herod Antipas rebuilt as his capital during Jesus’ youth.

The name stayed with Jesus throughout His ministry (Matthew 26:71), at His crucifixion (John 19:19), and after His resurrection (Mark 16:6; Acts 2:22).
