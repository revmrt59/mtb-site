Matthew 3:1
Matthew 3:1
NKJV

Matthew 3:1 In those days John the Baptist came preaching in the wilderness of Judea,(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 3:1 In those days John the Baptist came to the Judean wilderness and began preaching. His message was, (NLT)

Main Idea

In those days John the Baptist appears, preaching in the wilderness of Judea. Matthew moves from Jesus’ childhood to the public ministry of the messenger who prepares Israel for its King.

Verse Explanation

In those days John the Baptist appears, preaching in the wilderness of Judea. Matthew moves from Jesus’ childhood to the public ministry of the messenger who prepares Israel for its King.

“In those days”

Matthew moves from Jesus’ childhood in Nazareth (2:23) to the beginning of John’s ministry, roughly thirty years later (Luke 3:23). “In those days” is a familiar biblical way of opening a new and important stage in God’s work. While Jesus was still living quietly in Nazareth, God began preparing Israel to receive Him.

“John the Baptist came preaching”

Matthew introduces John without explaining his background. Luke tells of his miraculous birth to the elderly priest Zechariah and his wife Elizabeth (Luke 1:5–25, 57–80). “The Baptist” names John by the act most associated with his ministry: he immersed people in water as a sign of repentance. “Preaching” describes the work of a herald who publicly announces a king’s message. John did not come to offer opinions or start debates. He came with an announcement from God.

“in the wilderness of Judea”

The wilderness of Judea is the dry, rugged country that falls from the Judean hills down to the Dead Sea and the lower Jordan Valley. John’s location carried meaning for Israel. The nation had been formed in the wilderness after the exodus, and God had met His people there. The prophets also spoke of a coming day when God would again speak to Israel in the wilderness and restore her (Isaiah 40:3; Hosea 2:14). John stood outside Jerusalem and its temple and called the nation to come out and meet God in the place where its history with Him began.

Study Notes

Luke dates the start of John’s ministry to the fifteenth year of Tiberius Caesar (Luke 3:1–2), around AD 28–29, depending on how Tiberius’ reign is counted. The Jewish historian Josephus also writes about John (Antiquities 18.116–119). He describes him as a good man who called the Jews to righteousness and baptism, and he records his execution by Herod Antipas. This agrees with Matthew 14:1–12.

The Greek verb comes from kēryx, “herald,” the official who announced a ruler’s decree in public. The word emphasizes proclamation with authority rather than discussion or instruction.

Matthew 3:2
Matthew 3:2
NKJV

Matthew 3:2 and saying, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand!"(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 3:2 “Repent of your sins and turn to God, for the Kingdom of Heaven is near. ” (NLT)

Main Idea

John’s message is, “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand!” “Heaven” is a reverent way of referring to God, so the kingdom of heaven is God’s reign; because that reign is drawing near in the coming King, people must turn from their sins to God now.

Verse Explanation

John’s message is, “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand!” “Heaven” is a reverent way of referring to God, so the kingdom of heaven is God’s reign; because that reign is drawing near in the coming King, people must turn from their sins to God now.

“and saying, ‘Repent”

The Greek word for “repent” literally means to change one’s mind, but in Scripture it reaches far deeper than a change of opinion. It carries the Old Testament call to “turn” or “return” to the LORD, a change of direction that involves the heart, the will, and the whole life. In John’s preaching, repentance means turning from sin to God. It is expressed in open confession (3:6) and proven by its fruit (3:8).

John addressed this call to Israel as a whole, including its most religious people. They had the Scriptures, the temple, and the sacrifices, yet John called them back to God as people who had drifted far from Him.

“for the kingdom of heaven”

The kingdom of heaven is God’s reign, His rule established over His people and ultimately over all creation. Israel had long waited for it. The prophets promised that God Himself would come to reign (Isaiah 52:7), that a Son of David would sit on the throne forever (2 Samuel 7:12–16; Isaiah 9:6–7), and that the God of heaven would set up a kingdom that would never be destroyed (Daniel 2:44). Matthew’s “kingdom of heaven” means the same as “kingdom of God” in Mark and Luke. “Heaven” points to the source and character of this kingdom. It comes from God and carries His authority, unlike the kingdoms of Herod and Caesar.

“is at hand!”

“At hand” means the kingdom has drawn near. Its arrival is close and pressing, and in the next scene the King Himself steps forward (3:13–17). The kingdom draws near because the King draws near, and that is why repentance cannot wait.

Study Notes

Matthew uses “kingdom of heaven” more than thirty times, and the phrase appears nowhere else in the New Testament. Writing for readers steeped in Jewish practice, Matthew follows the reverent custom of speaking of “heaven” in place of God’s name (compare Luke 15:18). Parallel passages show the meaning is identical to “kingdom of God” (Matthew 4:17; Mark 1:15), and Matthew uses that phrase himself a few times (12:28; 19:24; 21:31, 43).

The Greek ēngiken is a perfect-tense form meaning has drawn near. The same wording occurs in Jesus’ announcement in 4:17 and the instruction to the Twelve in 10:7.

Main Idea

Matthew identifies John as the voice Isaiah foretold, crying in the wilderness, “Prepare the way of the LORD; Make His paths straight.” John’s ministry readies hearts for the LORD Himself, who is arriving in Jesus.

Verse Explanation

Matthew identifies John as the voice Isaiah foretold, crying in the wilderness, “Prepare the way of the LORD; Make His paths straight.” John’s ministry readies hearts for the LORD Himself, who is arriving in Jesus.

“For this is he who was spoken of by the prophet Isaiah”

Matthew explains John’s ministry by pointing to Scripture. John did not invent his role. Isaiah had spoken of him more than seven centuries earlier. Matthew continues the pattern of chapters 1 and 2, where each step of Jesus’ early life unfolds as the prophets said it would.

“saying: ‘The voice of one crying in the wilderness”

Isaiah 40 opens a message of comfort for God’s people after judgment: their warfare is ended and their iniquity pardoned (Isaiah 40:1–2). A voice then cries out that the LORD is coming to His people. John identified himself as that voice (John 1:23). He is described as a voice and nothing more, because the voice matters only for the message it carries and the One it announces. “Crying” means calling out loudly, the urgent announcement of a herald.

“Prepare the way of the LORD”

When an ancient king planned to travel, workers went ahead of him to prepare the road by filling holes, removing obstacles, and leveling rough ground. Isaiah uses this picture for the coming of the LORD Himself. In Isaiah 40:3, “the LORD” translates God’s personal name, Yahweh, and the next line calls Him “our God.” A few verses later Isaiah cries, “Behold your God! Behold, the Lord GOD shall come with a strong hand” (Isaiah 40:9–10). Isaiah was announcing the coming of God Himself.

Matthew applies this prophecy to John as the forerunner of Jesus. If John prepares the way of the LORD, and the One who comes after him is Jesus, then the coming of Jesus is the coming of the LORD to His people. Matthew has already told us that Jesus is “Immanuel,” meaning “God with us” (1:23). Here he shows that Israel’s long-awaited hope of Yahweh’s coming is fulfilled in the arrival of Jesus Christ.

“Make His paths straight.”

The second line repeats the thought in different words. A straight path is level and clear, with nothing in the way. John’s ministry shows where that road had to be prepared: in human hearts. Pride, self-confidence, and unconfessed sin were the obstacles, and repentance cleared the way for the King.

Study Notes

Isaiah 40:3 ends “Make straight in the desert a highway for our God.” Matthew, Mark, and Luke render the line “Make His paths straight.”

The community associated with the Dead Sea Scrolls also lived in the Judean wilderness and applied Isaiah 40:3 to itself. Some have suggested that John had contact with them, but Scripture gives no evidence of it, and the differences are significant. Qumran withdrew from the nation to guard its own purity. John called the whole nation, including tax collectors and soldiers (Luke 3:12–14), to repent and prepare for the Messiah.

Main Idea

John wears camel’s hair with a leather belt and eats locusts and wild honey. His rough clothing and wilderness diet mark him as a prophet in the pattern of Elijah, set apart from comfortable society.

Verse Explanation

John wears camel’s hair with a leather belt and eats locusts and wild honey. His rough clothing and wilderness diet mark him as a prophet in the pattern of Elijah, set apart from comfortable society.

“Now John himself was clothed in camel’s hair, with a leather belt around his waist”

Matthew pauses to describe John. A garment woven from camel’s hair was coarse and rough, the clothing of a poor man of the wilderness. The description deliberately recalls Elijah, whom King Ahaziah’s messengers described as “a hairy man wearing a leather belt around his waist” (2 Kings 1:8). Rough garments seem to have become associated with the prophetic calling (Zechariah 13:4).

This detail mattered to Matthew’s readers. The Old Testament closes with a promise: “Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD. And he will turn the hearts of the fathers to the children, and the hearts of the children to their fathers” (Malachi 4:5–6). John’s appearance connected him with that promise before he said a word. Gabriel had told Zechariah that his son would go before the Lord “in the spirit and power of Elijah” (Luke 1:17), and Jesus later confirms it: “If you are willing to receive it, he is Elijah who is to come” (Matthew 11:14; see also 17:11–13). John was not Elijah returned in person, but he came in Elijah’s spirit and power and fulfilled the role God promised.

“and his food was locusts and wild honey”

Locusts were among the insects the Law allowed Israel to eat (Leviticus 11:22), and poor people in desert regions still eat them. Wild honey came from wild bees nesting in rocks and trees (Deuteronomy 32:13; 1 Samuel 14:25–27). John lived on what the wilderness provided. His simple life was part of his message. He stood apart from the comfort, wealth, and religious power of Jerusalem, and his independence from them gave weight to his call to repentance.

Study Notes

When the priests and Levites asked John whether he was Elijah, he answered, “I am not” (John 1:21). This does not contradict Jesus. John denied being Elijah in the literal sense many expected, the ancient prophet himself returning from heaven. Jesus affirmed that John fulfilled the role Malachi promised. Luke 1:17 shows how both statements are true: John came “in the spirit and power of Elijah.”

Some have suggested that “locusts” refers to the pods of the carob tree, sometimes called St. John’s bread. The Greek word (akris) refers to the insect, and the Law’s permission to eat locusts makes the plain meaning natural.

Main Idea

People from Jerusalem, all Judea, and the whole region around the Jordan go out to John. His call stirs a widespread movement among the people.

Verse Explanation

People from Jerusalem, all Judea, and the whole region around the Jordan go out to John. His call stirs a widespread movement among the people.

“Then Jerusalem, all Judea, and all the region around the Jordan”

Matthew names the places to describe the people who lived there. Jerusalem was the capital and the center of worship. Judea was the surrounding province. The region around the Jordan included the river valley on both sides. “All” describes the remarkable breadth of the response, with people coming from every part of the land.

“went out to him”

The people “went out.” Jerusalem and its temple stood at the center of Israel’s religious life, yet the crowds left the city to hear a prophet in the wilderness. The trip from Jerusalem to the Jordan near Jericho covered more than twenty miles and dropped over three thousand feet in elevation. Coming to John took real effort. The people sensed that God was speaking again.

Study Notes

First Maccabees 9:27 describes distress such as had not been since the time that prophets ceased to appear among them. Other passages in First Maccabees also refer to waiting for a prophet. These texts provide evidence of Jewish expectations before John’s ministry.

Matthew 3:6
Matthew 3:6
NKJV

Matthew 3:6 and were baptized by him in the Jordan, confessing their sins.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 3:6 And when they confessed their sins, he baptized them in the Jordan River. (NLT)

Main Idea

Those who come are baptized by John in the Jordan as they confess their sins. Their baptism gives public expression to a real turning from sin toward God.

Verse Explanation

Those who come are baptized by John in the Jordan as they confess their sins. Their baptism gives public expression to a real turning from sin toward God.

“and were baptized by him in the Jordan”

“Baptized” comes from a Greek word meaning to dip or immerse. John immersed people in the Jordan River. The Jordan held deep meaning in Israel’s history. Israel crossed it to enter the land God had promised (Joshua 3), and Naaman was cleansed when he washed in it (2 Kings 5:14). Going down into the Jordan, the people acted out a fresh beginning, as though the nation were once again preparing to enter what God had promised.

“confessing their sins”

Their baptism was joined to open confession. To confess sin is to acknowledge it plainly and agree with God about it. These people did not hide behind religious practice. They named their sin. John’s baptism did not remove sin by itself. It was the outward sign of an inward turning to God. The forgiveness it pointed toward would come through the One John announced, who would give His blood “for many for the remission of sins” (26:28).

Study Notes

The Law required washings for ceremonial cleansing (Leviticus 15; Numbers 19), and immersion pools have been found near the temple mount. Later Jewish sources describe immersion by Gentile converts, although the date of that practice is disputed. John administered baptism to Israelites who came confessing their sins.

Matthew 3:7 But when he saw many Pharisees and Sadducees coming to watch him baptize, he denounced them. “You brood of snakes!” he exclaimed. “Who warned you to flee the coming wrath? (NLT)

Main Idea

When John sees many Pharisees and Sadducees coming to his baptism, he calls them a “Brood of vipers!” and asks who warned them to flee from the wrath to come. His sharp question warns these religious leaders that coming to be baptized cannot rescue them from judgment unless they truly turn to God.

Verse Explanation

When John sees many Pharisees and Sadducees coming to his baptism, he calls them a “Brood of vipers!” and asks who warned them to flee from the wrath to come. His sharp question warns these religious leaders that coming to be baptized cannot rescue them from judgment unless they truly turn to God.

“But when he saw many of the Pharisees and Sadducees coming to his baptism”

Matthew now introduces Israel’s two most influential religious groups. The Pharisees were a movement devoted to careful observance of the Law and the traditions of the elders, and they held great influence in the synagogues and among ordinary people. The Sadducees came largely from the priestly and wealthy families connected with the temple. They rejected the Pharisees’ traditions and denied the resurrection, angels, and spirits (Acts 23:8). The two groups often disagreed sharply, yet Matthew pairs them here and later shows them joining to test Jesus (16:1).

“Coming to his baptism” may mean they came to be baptized or that they came to observe what John was doing. Matthew does not say which. Whatever brought them, John saw what lay behind their religious standing and addressed it directly.

“he said to them, ‘Brood of vipers!”

A brood is a parent’s offspring. Vipers were poisonous snakes common in the wilderness. These men took pride in their descent from Abraham (3:9), but John said their character resembled the serpent. It was a stinging rebuke, and Jesus Himself later uses the same words for the religious leaders (12:34; 23:33). John’s sharpness was not personal anger. It was a prophet’s exposure of religion that keeps its outward forms while the heart remains poisoned by pride.

“Who warned you to flee from the wrath to come?”

The picture may come from a wilderness brush fire, when snakes slither out ahead of the flames. John’s question carries an edge. The leaders came to the place of repentance as if baptism could shelter them from judgment, while their trust still rested in their standing. “The wrath to come” is God’s righteous judgment against sin, which the prophets linked with the Day of the LORD (Zephaniah 1:14–18; Malachi 4:1). The coming of the Messiah would bring that day near, and no religious office or reputation could protect anyone from it. Only genuine repentance would.

Study Notes

Josephus places the number of Pharisees at about six thousand in Herod’s time (Antiquities 17.42). The Sadducees were associated with the priestly leadership. Matthew names both groups here, while Luke addresses the warning to the crowds (Luke 3:7).

Matthew 3:8
NKJV

Matthew 3:8 Therefore bear fruits worthy of repentance,(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 3:8 Prove by the way you live that you have repented of your sins and turned to God. (NLT)

Main Idea

John commands the Pharisees and Sadducees to “bear fruits worthy of repentance.” Genuine repentance becomes visible in a changed life.

Verse Explanation

John commands the Pharisees and Sadducees to “bear fruits worthy of repentance.” Genuine repentance becomes visible in a changed life.

“Therefore bear fruits worthy of repentance”

“Therefore” ties the command to the warning before it. If they truly wanted to escape the wrath to come, their lives had to show it. Fruit is the visible result of what is growing inside a tree, and “worthy of” means fitting or in keeping with. A turned heart produces a changed life. Luke records what such fruit looked like for John’s hearers: sharing clothing and food with those in need, collecting no more than what was right, and refusing extortion and false accusation (Luke 3:10–14). John did not set aside baptism or confession. He insisted that the outward sign be matched by an inner reality that shows itself in action.

The image of fruit runs through the rest of John’s message and prepares for 3:10, where trees that bear no good fruit are cut down. Jesus continues the same teaching: “Every good tree bears good fruit” (7:17), and “a tree is known by its fruit” (12:33).

Study Notes

None.

Matthew 3:9
Matthew 3:9
NKJV

Matthew 3:9 and do not think to say to yourselves, "˜We have Abraham as our father.' For I say to you that God is able to raise up children to Abraham from these stones.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 3:9 Don’t just say to each other, ‘We’re safe, for we are descendants of Abraham.’ That means nothing, for I tell you, God can create children of Abraham from these very stones. (NLT)

Main Idea

John warns these religious leaders not to rely on having Abraham as their father, since God could raise up children for Abraham from the very stones. Family descent and religious heritage cannot take the place of personally turning to God.

Verse Explanation

John warns these religious leaders not to rely on having Abraham as their father, since God could raise up children for Abraham from the very stones. Family descent and religious heritage cannot take the place of personally turning to God.

“and do not think to say to yourselves, ‘We have Abraham as our father.”

John moves from their actions to their thinking, naming the inner reasoning that made them feel secure. God had made great promises to Abraham and his descendants (Genesis 12:1–3; 17:7), and many in Israel believed that being born into Abraham’s family guaranteed a share in those promises. John cuts that confidence away. Physical descent did not secure a right relationship with God. Jesus confronts the same thinking when He tells those who claim Abraham as their father that Abraham’s true children do the works of Abraham (John 8:39). Paul later explains that “they are not all Israel who are of Israel” and that Abraham’s true children are those who share his faith (Romans 9:6–8; Galatians 3:7).

“For I say to you that God is able to raise up children to Abraham from these stones.”

John points to the stones lying along the Jordan. God is not limited by human descent. If He wished, He could form children for Abraham out of lifeless rock. Isaiah had used similar language: “Look to the rock from which you were hewn” (Isaiah 51:1–2). God first made a nation from one man and a barren wife, and He could create a people again.

John’s words also anticipate what God was about to do. Through Christ, God would raise up children of Abraham from among the Gentiles, people with no natural claim to Abraham at all (Galatians 3:7–9, 29). God’s people have always been His creation, formed by His grace and power rather than their pedigree.

Study Notes

In Hebrew and Aramaic, the words for “sons” (banim, benayya) and “stones” (abanim, abnayya) sound alike. If John spoke these words in Aramaic, the wordplay would have been sharp and memorable.

When Israel crossed the Jordan, Joshua set up twelve stones from the riverbed as a memorial representing the twelve tribes (Joshua 4:1–9, 20–24). If John was preaching near that area, his words about stones becoming children of Abraham would carry added force, though the text does not say whether he had those stones in view.

Main Idea

The ax is already laid at the root of the trees, and every tree that does not bear good fruit will be cut down and thrown into the fire. Judgment is near, which makes the call to repent urgent.

Verse Explanation

The ax is already laid at the root of the trees, and every tree that does not bear good fruit will be cut down and thrown into the fire. Judgment is near, which makes the call to repent urgent.

“And even now the ax is laid to the root of the trees.”

“Even now” stresses how close judgment is. The ax is not being sharpened somewhere in the distance. It is already resting at the base of the tree, positioned for the first swing. Striking at the root means complete removal, not trimming a branch. The prophets also pictured God’s judgment as the felling of trees (Isaiah 10:33–34).

“Therefore every tree which does not bear good fruit is cut down and thrown into the fire.”

The Greek describes the cutting and burning in the present tense, presenting the judgment as so certain that it can be spoken of as already happening. “Every tree” makes judgment personal and universal. No one is exempt because of family, position, or religious reputation. The standard is “good fruit,” the evidence of genuine repentance (3:8). The fire here is the fire of judgment, and it prepares for the fire John speaks of in the next two verses.

Study Notes

Matthew 7:19 repeats the image of a tree without good fruit being cut down and thrown into the fire.

Matthew 3:11
NKJV

Matthew 3:11 I indeed baptize you with water unto repentance, but He who is coming after me is mightier than I, whose sandals I am not worthy to carry. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 3:11 “I baptize with water those who repent of their sins and turn to God. But someone is coming soon who is greater than I am — so much greater that I’m not worthy even to be his slave and carry his sandals. He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and with fire. (NLT)

Main Idea

John baptizes with water for repentance, but the One coming after him is mightier, and John is not worthy even to carry His sandals. That One will baptize “with the Holy Spirit and fire,” giving His Spirit to His people and bringing judgment on those who refuse to repent.

Verse Explanation

John baptizes with water for repentance, but the One coming after him is mightier, and John is not worthy even to carry His sandals. That One will baptize “with the Holy Spirit and fire,” giving His Spirit to His people and bringing judgment on those who refuse to repent.

“I indeed baptize you with water unto repentance”

John describes his own ministry humbly and accurately. “With water” names its element, and “unto repentance” names its purpose: it expressed a turning to God and called for it. John’s baptism could picture cleansing, but water could not change a heart.

“but He who is coming after me is mightier than I”

“He who is coming” was a way of speaking about the Messiah (Psalm 118:26; Malachi 3:1; Matthew 11:3). He would come after John in time, yet He would be far greater. “Mightier” speaks of strength and authority. John was mighty in word, but the One coming would have the power to do what no prophet could do.

“whose sandals I am not worthy to carry”

Carrying a master’s sandals was the work of the lowest household slave. Later Jewish tradition held that a disciple should do every service for his teacher except this one, because it was too demeaning. John, a prophet honored by the nation, said he was not worthy to do even that for the One coming after him. The crowds were drawn to John, and John turned every eye toward Jesus.

“He will baptize you with the Holy Spirit and fire.”

Here John names the great difference between his ministry and the Messiah’s. John immersed people in water. The Messiah will immerse them in the Holy Spirit. The prophets had promised a day when God would pour out His Spirit (Isaiah 44:3; Joel 2:28–29), and Ezekiel joined cleansing water with a new heart and God’s own Spirit placed within His people (Ezekiel 36:25–27). John’s baptism could picture the cleansing. The Messiah would bring the reality and place God’s own life within His people. John is announcing that the Coming One will do what only God can do: give the Spirit of God.

John joins fire to the Spirit. The verses on either side explain the fire. In 3:10 the unfruitful tree is thrown into the fire, and in 3:12 the chaff is burned with unquenchable fire. The Messiah who gives His Spirit to those who turn to Him will also bring decisive judgment on those who persist in refusing Him. John was speaking to a mixed crowd, and the Coming One’s work would meet each person according to their response to Him.

Jesus Himself later repeated the promise of the Spirit: “John truly baptized with water, but you shall be baptized with the Holy Spirit not many days from now” (Acts 1:5). The gift of the Spirit and the warning of judgment both belong to the Messiah John announces.

Study Notes

One Greek preposition, en, governs both “Holy Spirit” and “fire.” Interpreters differ over whether the fire refers to judgment, purification, or both. Fire is used for judgment in the adjoining verses (3:10, 12). The shared preposition alone does not settle the interpretation.

The Greek preposition en can be translated “with” or “in.” The verb baptizō ordinarily refers to immersion. These features account for differences in English renderings of the phrase.

Matthew records John saying he is not worthy to “carry” the Messiah’s sandals. Mark and Luke record that he is not worthy to “loose” their strap (Mark 1:7; Luke 3:16). Both describe a slave’s task. The wording differs, but each Gospel conveys the same point of John’s unworthiness.

Verse Explanation

The coming One holds a winnowing fan to clear His threshing floor, gathering the wheat into the barn and burning the chaff with unquenchable fire. The harvest picture explains the fire of the previous verse as the Messiah’s final judgment, which will separate those who turn to God in repentance from those who persist in refusing Him.

“His winnowing fan is in His hand”

John’s picture comes from the harvest. After grain was cut, it was spread on a hard, flat threshing floor and crushed so the kernels came loose from their husks. The farmer then tossed the mixture into the air with a wooden winnowing fork. The wind carried the light chaff away, and the heavier grain fell back to the floor. The tool is already “in His hand,” and the work is ready to begin. “His” makes clear that this sorting belongs to the Messiah. The Father has committed all judgment to the Son (John 5:22).

“and He will thoroughly clean out His threshing floor”

“Thoroughly” stresses completeness. Nothing on the threshing floor will be left unsorted. It is “His” threshing floor, and the harvest belongs to Him.

“and gather His wheat into the barn”

The wheat represents those who belong to Him, who repent and receive Him. He gathers them and keeps them safe. Jesus later uses the same picture in His parable of the wheat and tares: “gather the wheat into my barn” (13:30). The Messiah’s work at the harvest includes gathering and protecting what is precious to Him.

“but He will burn up the chaff with unquenchable fire.”

Chaff is the worthless husk, light and useless. The psalmist describes the ungodly as “like the chaff which the wind drives away” (Psalm 1:4). “Unquenchable” means the fire cannot be put out. Isaiah’s prophecy closes with a fire that is not quenched (Isaiah 66:24), and Jesus speaks of “the fire that shall never be quenched” (Mark 9:43). John’s preaching ends on the seriousness of final judgment. The Messiah who gives the Spirit is also the Judge, and the choice John set before the crowds was urgent.

Study Notes

The Greek word refers to a winnowing shovel or fork used to throw threshed grain into the wind. The older English word “fan” describes its purpose, not a modern fan.

Matthew 3:13–17 — The Baptism of Jesus and the Father’s Declaration

Matthew 3:13
Matthew 3:13
NKJV

Matthew 3:13 Then Jesus came from Galilee to John at the Jordan to be baptized by him.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 3:13 Then Jesus went from Galilee to the Jordan River to be baptized by John. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus comes from Galilee to the Jordan to be baptized by John. The One John has been announcing now steps forward in person.

Verse Explanation

Jesus comes from Galilee to the Jordan to be baptized by John. The One John has been announcing now steps forward in person.

“Then Jesus came from Galilee to John at the Jordan”

“Then” moves from John’s announcement of the Coming One to His actual coming. The One John described with the winnowing fork in His hand now arrives, quietly, among the people coming to the river. He came from Galilee, the northern region where He grew up in Nazareth (2:22–23; Mark 1:9).

“to be baptized by him.”

Matthew states Jesus’ purpose plainly. He did not come to watch or to begin by preaching. He came to be baptized. Jesus’ first act as an adult in Matthew’s Gospel is to step into the water where sinners were confessing their sins. This sets up John’s objection in the next verse.

Study Notes

Luke 3:23 places Jesus’ age at about thirty when His ministry began. John 1:28 locates the Baptist’s activity at “Bethabara beyond the Jordan” in the NKJV; the exact site is uncertain.

The Greek uses an infinitive of purpose (tou baptisthēnai), in order to be baptized. Jesus came with this specific intention.

Matthew 3:14
Matthew 3:14
NKJV

Matthew 3:14 And John tried to prevent Him, saying, "I need to be baptized by You, and are You coming to me?"(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 3:14 But John tried to talk him out of it. “I am the one who needs to be baptized by you,” he said, “so why are you coming to me?” (NLT)

Main Idea

John tries to stop Him, saying, “I need to be baptized by You, and are You coming to me?” John recognizes that Jesus needs no repentance and is far greater than he is.

Verse Explanation

John tries to stop Him, saying, “I need to be baptized by You, and are You coming to me?” John recognizes that Jesus needs no repentance and is far greater than he is.

“And John tried to prevent Him”

The Greek tense suggests John kept trying to stop Him. John recognized who stood before him and could not bring himself to baptize Him.

“saying, ‘I need to be baptized by You”

John had just said that the Coming One would baptize with the Holy Spirit (3:11). Now he confesses that he himself needs that baptism. The greatest prophet of his generation sensed his own need in Jesus’ presence. John’s baptism was a baptism of repentance, and John knew Jesus had nothing to repent of.

“and are You coming to me?”

The question expresses amazement. The order seemed backward: the lesser should come to the greater. John could not understand why the sinless Messiah would come to a baptism meant for sinners. Only Matthew records this exchange, and it answers the question his readers would naturally ask: if John’s baptism was for repentance, why was Jesus baptized?

Study Notes

In John’s Gospel, John the Baptist says, “I did not know Him,” and explains that the Spirit’s descent was the sign God gave him to identify the Messiah (John 1:31–34). John and Jesus were relatives (Luke 1:36), and John clearly recognized Jesus’ greatness before the baptism. The descent of the Spirit gave him God’s own confirmation, allowing him to testify publicly that Jesus was the One. “Know” in John 1 refers to that God-given recognition of Jesus as the Messiah.

Matthew 3:15
Matthew 3:15
NKJV

Matthew 3:15 But Jesus answered and said to him, "Permit it to be so now, for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness." Then he allowed Him.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 3:15 But Jesus said, “It should be done, for we must carry out all that God requires. ” So John agreed to baptize him. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus answers, “Permit it to be so now, for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness,” and John consents. The sinless Son submits to baptism to carry out everything God’s saving plan requires, identifying Himself with the people He has come to save.

Verse Explanation

Jesus answers, “Permit it to be so now, for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness,” and John consents. The sinless Son submits to baptism to carry out everything God’s saving plan requires, identifying Himself with the people He has come to save.

“But Jesus answered and said to him”

These are Jesus’ first recorded words in Matthew’s Gospel. They concern righteousness and obedience to His Father’s purpose.

“Permit it to be so now”

Jesus does not deny John’s point. He acknowledges that the order seems reversed, but He asks John to allow it “now,” for this moment in God’s plan. The word implies that the present humility is temporary. The time would come when Jesus’ authority would be fully revealed (28:18).

“for thus it is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness.”

“Fitting” means proper, in keeping with God’s purpose. “Us” includes John. Both had roles to fulfill in this moment. In Matthew, “righteousness” often refers to doing God’s will and living in a way that conforms to what He requires (5:6, 20; 6:33). “Fulfill” is one of Matthew’s key words, used repeatedly for Scripture coming to pass in Jesus (1:22; 2:15, 17, 23). Jesus is saying that He and John must do what God requires of them at this point in His saving plan. The sinless Son obeys His Father. He identifies Himself with the people He came to save, who were coming to John to be baptized. His sonship is already His (3:17), and His baptism marks the public beginning of His ministry.

Jesus had no sin to confess, yet by being baptized He fully identified with the people He came to save. He stood where they stood, in the river of repentance, and took His place among sinners. This points ahead to Isaiah’s Servant, who “was numbered with the transgressors” and “bore the sin of many” (Isaiah 53:12). His baptism was also obedience. John’s baptism came “from heaven” (21:25), God’s call to Israel in that hour, and Jesus answered it as Israel’s representative and faithful Son. Where Israel had failed, Jesus obeyed.

“Then he allowed Him.”

John yields. His humility now takes the form of obedience, and he submits to Jesus’ word before he fully understands it.

Study Notes

The noun dikaiosynē, “righteousness,” recurs in Matthew 5:6, 10, 20; 6:33; and 21:32.

Matthew 3:16
Matthew 3:16
NKJV

Matthew 3:16 When He had been baptized, Jesus came up immediately from the water; and behold, the heavens were opened to Him, and He saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and alighting upon Him.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 3:16 After his baptism, as Jesus came up out of the water, the heavens were opened and he saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and settling on him. (NLT)

Main Idea

As Jesus comes up out of the water, the heavens open and He sees the Spirit of God descending like a dove and resting on Him. The Spirit anoints Jesus for the mission He now begins publicly.

Verse Explanation

As Jesus comes up out of the water, the heavens open and He sees the Spirit of God descending like a dove and resting on Him. The Spirit anoints Jesus for the mission He now begins publicly.

“When He had been baptized, Jesus came up immediately from the water”

Matthew moves quickly past the baptism itself. Jesus came up out of the river, and “immediately” heaven responded.

“and behold, the heavens were opened to Him”

“Behold” calls attention to what follows. Opened heavens signal divine revelation. Ezekiel saw visions of God when the heavens opened (Ezekiel 1:1). Israel had long prayed, “Oh, that You would rend the heavens! That You would come down!” (Isaiah 64:1). At the Jordan, the heavens opened, and God answered that prayer in the person of His Son.

“and He saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove”

The Spirit descended in a visible form; Luke adds “in bodily form like a dove” (Luke 3:22). John the Baptist also saw it and testified to it (John 1:32). The dove carries meaningful associations from Israel’s Scriptures. At creation, the Spirit of God hovered over the waters (Genesis 1:2). After the flood, Noah’s dove returned over the waters with an olive leaf, the sign of a new beginning (Genesis 8:8–11). At the Jordan, the Spirit comes over the waters again as God begins His new creation in Christ.

“and alighting upon Him.”

The Spirit came to rest on Jesus. Isaiah had promised that the Spirit of the LORD would rest upon the Branch from Jesse’s root (Isaiah 11:2), that God would put His Spirit upon His Servant (Isaiah 42:1), and that the Anointed One would say, “The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon Me, because the LORD has anointed Me” (Isaiah 61:1). “Christ” means “Anointed One,” and here the Christ is anointed for His public ministry.

This is not the moment Jesus became the Son of God. Matthew has already shown that He was conceived by the Holy Spirit (1:18, 20) and that God calls Him “My Son” (2:15). The Spirit’s descent is His anointing and public revelation as Messiah. From this point forward, Jesus carries out His ministry in the power of the Spirit. The Spirit leads Him into the wilderness (4:1), and He casts out demons “by the Spirit of God” (12:28). Peter later summarizes His ministry this way: “God anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power, who went about doing good and healing all who were oppressed by the devil, for God was with Him” (Acts 10:38). The One who receives the Spirit here is the One who will baptize His people with the Spirit (3:11).

Study Notes

Matthew and Luke say the heavens were “opened.” Mark uses a verb meaning “torn open” (Mark 1:10).

Matthew 3:17
NKJV

NLT

Matthew 3:17 And a voice from heaven said, “This is my dearly loved Son, who brings me great joy.” (NLT)

Main Idea

A voice from heaven declares, “This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.” The Father publicly affirms Jesus as His beloved Son, echoing the royal Son of Psalm 2 and the Servant of Isaiah 42, with Father, Son, and Spirit all present together.

Verse Explanation

A voice from heaven declares, “This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.” The Father publicly affirms Jesus as His beloved Son, echoing the royal Son of Psalm 2 and the Servant of Isaiah 42, with Father, Son, and Spirit all present together.

“And suddenly a voice came from heaven, saying”

For the first time in Matthew’s Gospel, God the Father speaks directly. Angels and prophets have spoken about Jesus. Now the Father Himself testifies to who He is.

“This is My beloved Son”

The Father’s words echo Psalm 2, where the LORD says to the King He has set on Zion, “You are My Son, today I have begotten You” (Psalm 2:7). Psalm 2 describes the anointed King whom God installs to rule the nations. The Father identifies Jesus as that King, the Son of David whom Matthew introduced in his opening line (1:1).

“Beloved” expresses the Father’s love for the Son. It also recalls the words God spoke to Abraham: “Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love” (Genesis 22:2). The Greek translation of Genesis used in Jesus’ day calls Isaac Abraham’s “beloved” son. Isaac was the beloved son laid on the altar. Jesus is the beloved Son whom the Father will give.

“in whom I am well pleased.”

These words echo Isaiah 42:1: “Behold! My Servant whom I uphold, My Elect One in whom My soul delights! I have put My Spirit upon Him.” Isaiah’s Servant receives God’s Spirit, carries out God’s mission with gentleness (Isaiah 42:2–3), and ultimately suffers for the sins of many (Isaiah 53). Matthew later quotes Isaiah 42:1–4 in full and applies it to Jesus: “My Beloved in whom My soul is well pleased! I will put My Spirit upon Him” (12:18). The Father’s words at the Jordan join two great Old Testament portraits: the royal Son who reigns and the Servant who suffers. Jesus will reign as King, and He will do it by serving and giving His life.

The whole scene reveals Father, Son, and Holy Spirit together. The Son stands in the water, the Spirit descends upon Him, and the Father speaks from heaven. At the start of Jesus’ ministry, the triune God is openly revealed.

Study Notes

Matthew records “This is My beloved Son,” while Mark and Luke record “You are My beloved Son” (Mark 1:11; Luke 3:22). Matthew’s wording also occurs in the account of the transfiguration (17:5).
