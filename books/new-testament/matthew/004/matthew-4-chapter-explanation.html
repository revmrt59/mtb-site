Verse-by-Verse
Matthew 4:1
Matthew 4:1
NKJV

Matthew 4:1 Then Jesus was led up by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:1 Then Jesus was led by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted there by the devil. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Spirit leads Jesus into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil. The beloved Son just declared at His baptism now faces the enemy, and His testing takes place within the Father’s purpose.

Verse Explanation

The Spirit leads Jesus into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil. The beloved Son just declared at His baptism now faces the enemy, and His testing takes place within the Father’s purpose.

“Then Jesus was led up by the Spirit into the wilderness”

“Then” ties this scene directly to the baptism. The heavens had opened, the Spirit had descended on Jesus like a dove, and the Father had declared, “This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased” (3:16–17). The same Spirit now leads Him away from the crowds at the Jordan. Jesus does not wander into the wilderness or go looking for a fight. He goes where the Spirit leads.

“Led up” reflects the geography. The Jordan Valley near Jericho lies far below sea level, and the barren Judean wilderness rises steeply to the west. The wilderness also carries history. Israel passed through the waters of the Red Sea and then spent forty years in the wilderness, where God humbled and tested them (Deuteronomy 8:2). Jesus has passed through the waters of baptism, and He now enters the wilderness as the true Son who will remain faithful where Israel failed.

“to be tempted by the devil.”

The Spirit’s purpose and the devil’s purpose meet in one event. The Greek word translated “tempted” can mean either “tested” or “tempted,” and both meanings are present here. God tests in order to prove and strengthen what is true, while the devil tempts in order to draw a person into sin (James 1:13). The Father allows His Son to be tested so that His faithfulness will be shown. The devil comes hoping to make Him fall.

“Devil” means “slanderer” or “accuser.” Matthew introduces him without explanation, as a real, personal enemy who opposes God and His purposes. Jesus will face him directly.

Study Notes

Each Gospel makes clear that the Spirit initiated this encounter. Mark says the Spirit “drove” Jesus into the wilderness (Mark 1:12), stressing urgency. Luke says Jesus returned from the Jordan “filled with the Holy Spirit” and “was led by the Spirit into the wilderness” (Luke 4:1).

The same Greek verb appears in the Greek Old Testament when “God tested Abraham” (Genesis 22:1). Context decides whether the emphasis falls on testing or tempting. Here God’s testing and the devil’s tempting occur in the same event, but with opposite aims.

Matthew does not name the place. Later tradition located the temptation on a mountain west of Jericho, still called the Mount of Temptation, but the identification rests on tradition rather than on the text.

Matthew 4:2
Matthew 4:2
NKJV

Matthew 4:2 And when He had fasted forty days and forty nights, afterward He was hungry.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:2 For forty days and forty nights he fasted and became very hungry. (NLT)

Main Idea

After fasting forty days and forty nights, Jesus is hungry. His forty days recall Israel’s forty years in the wilderness, and His hunger shows that His humanity and His need were real.

Verse Explanation

After fasting forty days and forty nights, Jesus is hungry. His forty days recall Israel’s forty years in the wilderness, and His hunger shows that His humanity and His need were real.

“And when He had fasted forty days and forty nights,”

Jesus went without food for the full forty days (Luke 4:2). The number recalls Moses, who fasted forty days and forty nights on the mountain when he received God’s word (Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 9:9), and Elijah, who traveled forty days and forty nights to Horeb in the strength of food an angel provided (1 Kings 19:8). It also recalls Israel’s forty years in the wilderness, which Moses described as God humbling them, testing them, and letting them hunger (Deuteronomy 8:2–3). Jesus enters that same history as the faithful Son.

The fast is a time set apart to God. Jesus is not struggling against hard circumstances. He is giving Himself to dependence on His Father before His public ministry begins.

“afterward He was hungry.”

Matthew states this plainly. Jesus is the eternal Son of God, and He was also fully human. Forty days without food left Him truly hungry and physically weak. The tempter comes at that moment. The temptations that follow are not abstract tests of theology. They press on a real body with a real need.

Study Notes

Matthew includes “and forty nights,” wording also found in the accounts of Moses on Sinai (Exodus 24:18; 34:28). Mark 1:13 and Luke 4:2 mention forty days.

Matthew 4:3
Matthew 4:3
NKJV

Matthew 4:3 Now when the tempter came to Him, he said, "If You are the Son of God, command that these stones become bread."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:3 During that time the devil came and said to him, “If you are the Son of God, tell these stones to become loaves of bread.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The tempter comes and says, “If You are the Son of God, command that these stones become bread.” He urges Jesus to use His sonship to meet His own need apart from the Father’s direction.

Verse Explanation

The tempter comes and says, “If You are the Son of God, command that these stones become bread.” He urges Jesus to use His sonship to meet His own need apart from the Father’s direction.

“Now when the tempter came to Him,”

Matthew calls the devil “the tempter,” naming him by what he does. He approaches Jesus at the moment of hunger.

“he said, ‘If You are the Son of God,”

The devil’s first words echo the Father’s declaration at the Jordan: “This is My beloved Son” (3:17). The “if” does not deny that Jesus is the Son of God. The devil assumes it for the sake of argument, as if to say, Since You are the Son of God, act like it. The temptation concerns how Jesus will live as the Son. Will He trust His Father’s provision, or use His own power for Himself?

“command that these stones become bread.”

The Judean wilderness is covered with loose limestone rocks. The request seems small. Jesus had the power to do it, and He would later multiply bread for thousands (14:13–21). Hunger is not sin, and bread is not evil. The temptation lies in acting independently of the Father, who had led Him into the wilderness and had not told Him to end the fast. Israel in the wilderness complained for bread and doubted God’s care (Exodus 16:2–3). Jesus faces the same pressure and refuses to provide for Himself apart from God.

Study Notes

The Greek construction presents the condition as true for the sake of argument. The devil is not questioning whether Jesus is God’s Son so much as pressing Him to use that status on His own terms.

The Greek is a participle, the one who tempts. Paul uses the same description (1 Thessalonians 3:5).

Matthew 4:4
Matthew 4:4
NKJV

Matthew 4:4 But He answered and said, "It is written, "˜Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God.' "(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:4 But Jesus told him, “No! The Scriptures say, ‘People do not live by bread alone,but by every word that comes from the mouth of God.’ ” (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus answers from Deuteronomy 8:3: “Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God.” As the faithful Son, He trusts His Father’s word and provision rather than seizing bread on His own terms.

Verse Explanation

Jesus answers from Deuteronomy 8:3: “Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God.” As the faithful Son, He trusts His Father’s word and provision rather than seizing bread on His own terms.

“But He answered and said, ‘It is written,”

Jesus answers the devil with Scripture. “It is written” carries the sense of it stands written. God’s word has been spoken, it remains in force, and it settles the matter. Jesus does not debate, and He does not answer with a display of divine power. He answers as a man living under the authority of God’s word.

“Man shall not live by bread alone,”

Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 8:3. Moses reminded Israel that God had let them hunger and then fed them with manna “that He might make you know that man shall not live by bread alone.” The manna taught Israel that their life came from God, not from their own resources. By saying “Man,” Jesus identifies with all humanity. He faces this temptation as a man, relying on what is available to every believer.

“Alone” matters. Bread is necessary, and God provides it. But physical provision is not the whole of life, and it is not its deepest source.

“but by every word that proceeds from the mouth of God.”

True life comes from God’s word. “Every word” includes all that God says, not a selected portion. “Proceeds from the mouth of God” stresses that the word comes from God Himself. Jesus lives by what comes from His Father’s mouth, and in this moment that meant continuing to trust Him rather than turning stones into bread. Later Jesus would say, “My food is to do the will of Him who sent Me” (John 4:34).

Study Notes

All three replies quote Deuteronomy: 8:3 in 4:4, 6:16 in 4:7, and 6:13 in 4:10.

The Greek perfect tense describes something written in the past that stands with continuing authority.

The Greek is a present participle, describing the word as it goes out from God’s mouth. The emphasis falls on God as the One who speaks.

Matthew 4:5
Matthew 4:5
NKJV

Matthew 4:5 Then the devil took Him up into the holy city, set Him on the pinnacle of the temple,(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:5 Then the devil took him to the holy city, Jerusalem, to the highest point of the Temple, (NLT)

Main Idea

The devil takes Jesus to the holy city and sets Him on the pinnacle of the temple. The second test moves to the center of Israel’s worship.

Verse Explanation

The devil takes Jesus to the holy city and sets Him on the pinnacle of the temple. The second test moves to the center of Israel’s worship.

“Then the devil took Him up into the holy city,”

The second temptation moves from the wilderness to Jerusalem. Matthew calls it “the holy city” (also 27:53) because it was the site of the temple and the center of Israel’s worship. The devil brings his temptation into the holiest setting in the land.

Matthew does not explain how the devil “took” Jesus there. It may have been a physical movement or a visionary experience. Either way, the temptation was real.

“set Him on the pinnacle of the temple,”

“Pinnacle” translates a word meaning “little wing,” a projecting edge or high point of the temple complex. Its exact location is uncertain. Many suggest the southeast corner of the temple courts, where the wall rose high above the Kidron Valley. From there a fall would be fatal, and a rescue would be seen by all.

Study Notes

The Greek word pterygion refers to a wing-like projection. Josephus describes the royal colonnade along the temple’s southern side and says that anyone looking down from its roof into the ravine below would grow dizzy (Antiquities 15.411–412). This fits the traditional identification with the southeast corner, though it does not prove it.

Matthew uses hieron, the whole temple complex with its courts and colonnades, rather than naos, the sanctuary building itself.

Luke places the temple temptation last (Luke 4:5–12). Matthew places it before the mountain scene. The accounts arrange the same three temptations differently.

Main Idea

The devil challenges Him to throw Himself down and quotes Psalm 91, “He shall give His angels charge over you,” promising that angels will catch Him. The tempter twists a promise of God’s care into a dare to force God’s hand.

Verse Explanation

The devil challenges Him to throw Himself down and quotes Psalm 91, “He shall give His angels charge over you,” promising that angels will catch Him. The tempter twists a promise of God’s care into a dare to force God’s hand.

“and said to Him, ‘If You are the Son of God, throw Yourself down.”

The devil again appeals to Jesus’ sonship. This time he does not urge Jesus to act independently of the Father but to put the Father’s care on display. A dramatic leap and a miraculous rescue in front of the temple would seem to confirm Jesus’ identity before all Jerusalem.

“For it is written:”

Jesus has answered with Scripture, so the devil now quotes Scripture. He uses the same words Jesus used, “it is written,” but he twists their purpose.

“He shall give His angels charge over you,’ and, ‘In their hands they shall bear you up, Lest you dash your foot against a stone.”

The devil quotes Psalm 91:11–12. The psalm promises God’s protection to the one who “dwells in the secret place of the Most High” and trusts in Him (Psalm 91:1–2). It describes God’s care in the dangers that come to a person who walks with Him. It does not invite anyone to create danger in order to test that care. The devil takes a promise meant to strengthen trust and turns it into a dare.

Study Notes

The quotation omits “to keep you in all your ways” from Psalm 91:11 and ends before the following verse about trampling dangerous animals. The text does not explain the omissions.

Matthew 4:7
Matthew 4:7
NKJV

Matthew 4:7 Jesus said to him, "It is written again, "˜You shall not tempt the LORD your God.' "(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:7 Jesus responded, “The Scriptures also say, ‘You must not test the LORD your God.’ ” (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus answers, “It is written again, ‘You shall not tempt the LORD your God.’” Scripture rightly understood governs the use of its promises, and the Son refuses to put His Father to the test.

Verse Explanation

Jesus answers, “It is written again, ‘You shall not tempt the LORD your God.’” Scripture rightly understood governs the use of its promises, and the Son refuses to put His Father to the test.

“Jesus said to him, ‘It is written again,”

“Again” shows that Scripture must be read alongside Scripture. Jesus does not reject Psalm 91. He answers the devil’s misuse of it with another passage that shows what Psalm 91 cannot mean. The whole of God’s word guards the meaning of each part.

“You shall not tempt the LORD your God.”

Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 6:16, which continues, “as you tempted Him in Massah.” At Massah, Israel demanded water and asked, “Is the LORD among us or not?” (Exodus 17:1–7). Their testing came from doubt. They wanted God to prove His presence on their terms. Throwing Himself from the temple would have been the same kind of demand. Jesus already knew the Father’s presence and approval, and He did not need a miracle to confirm it.

To “tempt” God here means to put Him to the test, pressuring Him to act in order to prove Himself. Jesus will trust the Father’s care without trying to force it.

Study Notes

This is a strengthened form of the verb used in 4:1. It appears in the Greek Old Testament at Deuteronomy 6:16, and Paul uses it when he warns believers not to “tempt Christ, as some of them also tempted” in the wilderness (1 Corinthians 10:9).

Some interpreters suggest that Jesus’ words, spoken to the devil, also carry a warning for him not to test the Lord who stands before him. The primary meaning remains Jesus’ own refusal to test His Father.

Matthew 4:8
Matthew 4:8
NKJV

Matthew 4:8 Again, the devil took Him up on an exceedingly high mountain, and showed Him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:8 Next the devil took him to the peak of a very high mountain and showed him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory. (NLT)

Main Idea

The devil takes Jesus to an exceedingly high mountain and shows Him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory. The final test offers worldwide dominion.

Verse Explanation

The devil takes Jesus to an exceedingly high mountain and shows Him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory. The final test offers worldwide dominion.

“Again, the devil took Him up on an exceedingly high mountain,”

The third temptation is set on a mountain. Matthew does not name it, and no mountain on earth offers a literal view of every kingdom. The scene likely included a supernatural vision, though Matthew’s concern is the temptation, not how it was experienced. Mountains are significant in Matthew. Jesus will teach on a mountain (5:1), be transfigured on a mountain (17:1), and give the Great Commission on a mountain (28:16).

“and showed Him all the kingdoms of the world and their glory.”

The devil displays the power, wealth, splendor, and influence of earthly kingdoms. Jesus is the promised King, the Son of David (1:1), and the nations are His rightful inheritance (Psalm 2:7–8). The devil offers Him what the Father has promised, but by a different road.

Study Notes

Luke says the devil showed Jesus the kingdoms “in a moment of time” (Luke 4:5), which supports a visionary element. Neither Gospel suggests that the temptation was imaginary or merely an inner struggle.

Matthew 4:9
Matthew 4:9
NKJV

Matthew 4:9 And he said to Him, "All these things I will give You if You will fall down and worship me."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:9 “I will give it all to you,” he said, “if you will kneel down and worship me.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The devil promises, “All these things I will give You if You will fall down and worship me.” He offers a crown without the cross, at the price of the Son’s allegiance.

Verse Explanation

The devil promises, “All these things I will give You if You will fall down and worship me.” He offers a crown without the cross, at the price of the Son’s allegiance.

“And he said to Him, ‘All these things I will give You”

Satan exercises genuine authority over the fallen world. Jesus Himself calls him “the ruler of this world” (John 12:31), and Paul calls him “the god of this age” (2 Corinthians 4:4). Luke records Satan’s claim that this authority had been delivered to him and that he could give it to whomever he wished (Luke 4:6). Yet his dominion remains subject to God’s ultimate sovereignty. Satan offers Jesus authority over the kingdoms of the world, but only at the price of worshiping him. Jesus refuses to receive from Satan what the Father has promised to give Him.

“if You will fall down and worship me.”

The devil’s real aim now comes into the open. Behind every temptation stood this one demand. “Fall down and worship” describes bowing to the ground before a superior. The same words describe the wise men, who “fell down and worshiped” the child Jesus (2:11). The One whom wise men from the east worshiped is now asked to worship the devil.

Study Notes

The Greek verb proskyneō refers to bowing down or prostrating oneself. Matthew also uses it in 2:2, 11; 8:2; 14:33; and 28:9, 17.

Matthew 4:10
Matthew 4:10
NKJV

Matthew 4:10 Then Jesus said to him, "Away with you, Satan! For it is written, "˜You shall worship the LORD your God, and Him only you shall serve.' "(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:10 “Get out of here, Satan,” Jesus told him. “For the Scriptures say, ‘You must worship the LORD your Godand serve only him.’ ” (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus commands, “Away with you, Satan!” and answers with Deuteronomy 6:13: “You shall worship the LORD your God, and Him only you shall serve.” The Son gives worship and service to God alone and rejects every shortcut to His kingdom.

Verse Explanation

Jesus commands, “Away with you, Satan!” and answers with Deuteronomy 6:13: “You shall worship the LORD your God, and Him only you shall serve.” The Son gives worship and service to God alone and rejects every shortcut to His kingdom.

“Then Jesus said to him, ‘Away with you, Satan!”

Jesus now dismisses the devil with authority. “Satan” means “adversary,” and Jesus names him for what he is. Jesus will later use nearly the same words to rebuke Peter when Peter tries to turn Him away from the cross: “Get behind Me, Satan!” (16:23). In both moments the temptation is the same, a kingdom without suffering.

“For it is written, ‘You shall worship the LORD your God, and Him only you shall serve.”

Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 6:13, from the passage that calls Israel to love the LORD with all their heart (Deuteronomy 6:4–5) and warns them not to follow other gods (6:14). Worship and service belong together. The One we bow before is the One we will obey. “Him only” excludes every rival. Jesus will receive the kingdom, but only by way of undivided worship of His Father and obedience to Him.

Study Notes

The word is a simple command, “Go!” Jesus uses the same verb to Peter in 16:23 (“Get behind Me”).

Deuteronomy 6:13 reads fear the LORD your God and serve Him. The standard Greek Old Testament wording also uses fear. Matthew records Jesus saying worship and adding only to serve Him. Jesus applies the verse to the devil’s demand in 4:9 that He fall down and worship him. Reverent fear of God is expressed in worship, and that worship belongs to God alone. The word only draws out the warning of the next verse in Deuteronomy against going after other gods (Deuteronomy 6:14). No rival, however much he offers, may receive what belongs to God.

The Greek verb latreuō normally refers to religious service.

Matthew 4:11
NKJV

NLT

Matthew 4:11 Then the devil went away, and angels came and took care of Jesus. (NLT)

Main Idea

The devil leaves Jesus, and angels come and minister to Him. Jesus stands victorious where Israel failed, and the Father’s care meets Him at the end of the trial.

Verse Explanation

The devil leaves Jesus, and angels come and minister to Him. Jesus stands victorious where Israel failed, and the Father’s care meets Him at the end of the trial.

“Then the devil left Him,”

The devil’s departure confirms Jesus’ victory. He came three times and failed three times. Jesus was truly tempted, and He remained entirely without sin. Luke adds that he departed “until an opportune time” (Luke 4:13). The conflict was not over, but this contest was decided.

“and behold, angels came and ministered to Him.”

“Behold” marks a striking turn. In the second temptation the devil urged Jesus to force angels to catch Him (4:6). Jesus refused, and now angels come at the Father’s direction. “Ministered” can include supplying food, as an angel fed Elijah in the wilderness (1 Kings 19:5–8), along with caring for Jesus after His long ordeal. What Jesus would not grasp for Himself, the Father gave freely.

Study Notes

The verb diakoneō can include serving food, as in Matthew 8:15. The imperfect tense here presents the service as continuing.

Matthew 4:12–17 — The Great Light Dawns in Galilee

Matthew 4:12
Matthew 4:12
NKJV

Matthew 4:12 Now when Jesus heard that John had been put in prison, He departed to Galilee.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:12 When Jesus heard that John had been arrested, he left Judea and returned to Galilee. (NLT)

Main Idea

When Jesus hears that John has been put in prison, He withdraws to Galilee. John’s preparatory ministry is closing, and Jesus’ public ministry is about to begin.

Verse Explanation

When Jesus hears that John has been put in prison, He withdraws to Galilee. John’s preparatory ministry is closing, and Jesus’ public ministry is about to begin.

“Now when Jesus heard that John had been put in prison,”

Matthew mentions John’s arrest briefly here and explains it later. Herod Antipas, ruler of Galilee and Perea, imprisoned John because John had rebuked him for taking his brother’s wife (14:3–4). The Greek word translated “put in prison” literally means “handed over.” Matthew later uses the same verb for Jesus being handed over to His enemies (17:22; 26:2). The forerunner’s path anticipates the path of the One he announced.

“He departed to Galilee.”

“Departed” translates a word that means to withdraw. Matthew used it for the wise men’s return home and for Joseph’s moves with the child (2:12, 13, 14, 22). Each time, a withdrawal in the face of danger led to the fulfillment of God’s purposes. Jesus is not retreating in fear. He moves at the Father’s pace, and His withdrawal places Him where Scripture said the light would shine (4:14–16).

Study Notes

Galilee was itself ruled by Herod Antipas, the same ruler who imprisoned John. Jesus’ move is not an escape from Herod’s reach but a deliberate step into the region where His ministry would center. According to Josephus, John was held at Machaerus, a fortress east of the Dead Sea (Antiquities 18.116–119).

John’s Gospel records an earlier period when Jesus ministered in Judea while John the Baptist was still free (John 1:35–4:3; note John 3:24). Matthew, like Mark, moves directly from the temptation to the Galilean ministry, which suggests that some months passed between 4:11 and 4:12.

The verb paradidōmi means “hand over” or “deliver up.” It is translated “put in prison” here and is also used for arrest and betrayal elsewhere in Matthew.

Matthew 4:13
Matthew 4:13
NKJV

Matthew 4:13 And leaving Nazareth, He came and dwelt in Capernaum, which is by the sea, in the regions of Zebulun and Naphtali,(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:13 He went first to Nazareth, then left there and moved to Capernaum, beside the Sea of Galilee, in the region of Zebulun and Naphtali. (NLT)

Main Idea

Leaving Nazareth, Jesus settles in Capernaum by the sea, in the region of Zebulun and Naphtali. Matthew names the tribal territories to prepare for the prophecy that follows.

Verse Explanation

Leaving Nazareth, Jesus settles in Capernaum by the sea, in the region of Zebulun and Naphtali. Matthew names the tribal territories to prepare for the prophecy that follows.

“And leaving Nazareth,”

Nazareth was the town where Jesus grew up (2:23). Matthew does not explain why He left. Luke records His rejection there after He announced Himself as the fulfillment of Isaiah’s prophecy (Luke 4:16–30). Matthew later records a similar rejection (13:53–58).

“He came and dwelt in Capernaum, which is by the sea,”

“Dwelt” means He made His home there. Capernaum was a fishing town on the northwest shore of the Sea of Galilee. It sat near a major road, and it had a customs station (9:9) and a Roman centurion stationed nearby (8:5). Capernaum would become “His own city” (9:1), the base for much of His Galilean ministry, and it was where Peter had a house (8:14).

“in the regions of Zebulun and Naphtali,”

Matthew identifies the area by the names of two Israelite tribes whose territory once covered Galilee (Joshua 19:10–16, 32–39). Capernaum lay in what had been Naphtali, near its border with Zebulun. These were old names by Jesus’ day, and Matthew uses them to prepare for the prophecy he is about to quote.

Study Notes

The name likely means village of Nahum. Excavations at the site have uncovered a later limestone synagogue built over earlier basalt foundations, which many believe belong to the synagogue of Jesus’ day (Mark 1:21; Luke 7:5). Nearby is a house that early Christians honored as Peter’s home.

Matthew 4:14
NKJV

Matthew 4:14 that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Isaiah the prophet, saying:(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:14 This fulfilled what God said through the prophet Isaiah: (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus’ move fulfills what Isaiah the prophet spoke. Even the place where He makes His home follows God’s announced plan.

Verse Explanation

Jesus’ move fulfills what Isaiah the prophet spoke. Even the place where He makes His home follows God’s announced plan.

“that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Isaiah the prophet, saying:”

This is one of Matthew’s fulfillment statements, which show that events in Jesus’ life took place according to God’s word (1:22; 2:15, 17, 23). Even something as ordinary as a change of residence stands within God’s plan. Isaiah spoke the words, but the message came from God through him. A prophecy given more than seven hundred years earlier now finds its fulfillment in Jesus’ coming to Galilee.

Study Notes

Interpreters commonly count ten fulfillment formulas in Matthew (1:22; 2:15, 17, 23; 4:14; 8:17; 12:17; 13:35; 21:4; 27:9). Half of them appear in the first four chapters, where Matthew establishes that Jesus is the promised Messiah.

The verb means “to fill” or “to complete.” In Matthew, fulfillment can mean the direct accomplishment of a prediction, as here, or the completion of a pattern in Israel’s history, as at 2:15.

Matthew 4:15
Matthew 4:15
NKJV

Matthew 4:15 "The land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali, By the way of the sea, beyond the Jordan, Galilee of the Gentiles:(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:15 “In the land of Zebulun and of Naphtali,beside the sea, beyond the Jordan River,in Galilee where so many Gentiles live, (NLT)

Main Idea

Isaiah names “The land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali,” the way of the sea beyond the Jordan, and “Galilee of the Gentiles.” This northern region, long exposed to invasion and foreign influence, is where the promised light appears.

Verse Explanation

Isaiah names “The land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali,” the way of the sea beyond the Jordan, and “Galilee of the Gentiles.” This northern region, long exposed to invasion and foreign influence, is where the promised light appears.

“The land of Zebulun and the land of Naphtali,”

Matthew quotes Isaiah 9:1–2. In Isaiah’s day these northern tribal lands were the first to suffer when Assyria invaded Israel. In 733–732 B.C., the Assyrian king Tiglath-Pileser captured the region and carried many of its people into exile (2 Kings 15:29). Isaiah said God had “lightly esteemed” these lands but would later make them glorious (Isaiah 9:1).

“By the way of the sea, beyond the Jordan,”

These phrases describe the surrounding territory. “The way of the sea” refers to a road through the region, likely running near the Sea of Galilee toward the Mediterranean coast. “Beyond the Jordan” most likely refers to land east of the river. Isaiah names the whole northern and eastern area that had been overrun.

“Galilee of the Gentiles:”

Galilee was surrounded by Gentile peoples and, after the Assyrian conquest, had a mixed population. By Jesus’ day many Jews lived there, but Gentile influence remained strong, and people in Jerusalem tended to look down on the region (John 7:41, 52). The phrase points ahead to the gospel reaching the nations (28:19).

Study Notes

In the Hebrew Bible, Isaiah 9:1 is numbered 8:23, and Isaiah 9:2 is numbered 9:1. Matthew’s quotation is abbreviated and does not follow either the Hebrew or the Greek Old Testament word for word.

Some identify this with the major international trade route later called the Via Maris, though the exact road Isaiah meant is uncertain.

NKJV

Matthew 4:16 The people who sat in darkness have seen a great light, And upon those who sat in the region and shadow of death Light has dawned."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:16 the people who sat in darknesshave seen a great light.And for those who lived in the land where death casts its shadow,a light has shined.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Isaiah declares that the people who sat in darkness have seen a great light, and light has dawned on those in the shadow of death. In Jesus, the light of God’s salvation rises over people living in spiritual darkness, Jew and Gentile alike.

Verse Explanation

Isaiah declares that the people who sat in darkness have seen a great light, and light has dawned on those in the shadow of death. In Jesus, the light of God’s salvation rises over people living in spiritual darkness, Jew and Gentile alike.

“The people who sat in darkness have seen a great light,”

Darkness describes the condition of the people: spiritual ignorance, oppression, sin, and distress. “Sat” pictures people settled in that condition and unable to leave it on their own. The light is “great” because it is the light of God’s salvation. They “have seen” it because the light has come to them.

“And upon those who sat in the region and shadow of death”

The second line deepens the first. “The shadow of death” describes a deep darkness overshadowed by death (Job 10:21–22; Psalm 23:4). The people lacked light, and they also lived under death’s shadow.

“Light has dawned.”

The light “dawned” like the rising sun. In Jesus’ arrival, God’s promised salvation began to rise over Galilee. Zechariah used similar language when he prophesied that “the Dayspring from on high has visited us; to give light to those who sit in darkness and the shadow of death” (Luke 1:78–79). Jesus would later say, “I am the light of the world” (John 8:12), and He would tell His disciples, “You are the light of the world” (Matthew 5:14).

Study Notes

Isaiah 9:2 says the people “walked” in darkness and “dwell” in the land of the shadow of death. Matthew uses “sat” in both lines. His wording echoes Psalm 107:10, which describes those who “sat in darkness and in the shadow of death,” bound in affliction until the LORD brought them out.

The Hebrew word (tsalmaveth) can mean “deep darkness” or “death’s shadow.” The same word appears in Psalm 23:4.

Matthew 4:17
Matthew 4:17
NKJV

Matthew 4:17 From that time Jesus began to preach and to say, "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:17 From then on Jesus began to preach, “Repent of your sins and turn to God, for the Kingdom of Heaven is near. ” (NLT)

Main Idea

From that time Jesus begins to preach, “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.” Since “heaven” is a reverent way of referring to God, this is the announcement of God’s reign, and the King Himself now calls people to turn and willingly receive it.

Verse Explanation

From that time Jesus begins to preach, “Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.” Since “heaven” is a reverent way of referring to God, this is the announcement of God’s reign, and the King Himself now calls people to turn and willingly receive it.

“From that time Jesus began to preach”

“From that time” marks a turning point. Matthew uses the same phrase at 16:21, when Jesus begins to show His disciples that He must suffer, die, and rise again. These two markers divide Jesus’ ministry: first He proclaims the kingdom, and then He sets His face toward the cross. “Preach” means to proclaim publicly as a herald, announcing a message on behalf of a king.

“and to say, ‘Repent,”

To “repent” means to change one’s mind in a way that changes the whole direction of one’s life. It involves turning from sin and turning to God. Jesus’ first recorded public word is a call to respond.

“for the kingdom of heaven is at hand.”

“For” gives the reason to repent. The “kingdom of heaven” is God’s reign, His rule breaking into the world through His King. Many Jews of Jesus’ day avoided speaking God’s name out of reverence and spoke of “heaven” instead, so “kingdom of heaven” means the same as “kingdom of God” in the other Gospels. “At hand” means it has come near. In Jesus the King has arrived and His kingdom is breaking in, though it still awaits its full coming at His return.

Jesus’ words repeat John the Baptist’s message exactly (3:2). The message is the same, but the messenger has changed. John announced the kingdom’s approach. Jesus is the King who brings it.

Study Notes

“From that time Jesus began” appears only here and at 16:21, and many interpreters see these phrases as the hinges of Matthew’s Gospel. The outline for Matthew begins its second movement here (4:17–16:20) and its third at 16:21.

Matthew uses this expression more than thirty times and “kingdom of God” only a few times (12:28; 19:24; 21:31, 43). The Greek reads literally kingdom of the heavens, reflecting Hebrew idiom.

The perfect form ēngiken means has drawn near. Interpreters differ over whether this announcement emphasizes nearness or an arrival already taking place.

The word literally refers to a change of mind or purpose. In Jesus’ preaching it carries the force of the Old Testament prophets’ call to “turn” or “return” to the LORD.

Matthew 4:18–22 — Jesus Calls Four Fishermen to Follow Him

Matthew 4:18
Matthew 4:18
NKJV

Matthew 4:18 And Jesus, walking by the Sea of Galilee, saw two brothers, Simon called Peter, and Andrew his brother, casting a net into the sea; for they were fishermen.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:18 One day as Jesus was walking along the shore of the Sea of Galilee, he saw two brothers — Simon, also called Peter, and Andrew — throwing a net into the water, for they fished for a living. (NLT)

Main Idea

Walking by the Sea of Galilee, Jesus sees two brothers, Simon called Peter and Andrew, casting a net into the sea, for they are fishermen. The King’s call reaches ordinary working men in the middle of their daily labor.

Verse Explanation

Walking by the Sea of Galilee, Jesus sees two brothers, Simon called Peter and Andrew, casting a net into the sea, for they are fishermen. The King’s call reaches ordinary working men in the middle of their daily labor.

“And Jesus, walking by the Sea of Galilee,”

The Sea of Galilee is a freshwater lake in northern Israel, about thirteen miles long and seven or eight miles wide, lying roughly seven hundred feet below sea level. Fishing was a major industry in the towns along its shore, including Capernaum, where Jesus had just settled.

“saw two brothers, Simon called Peter, and Andrew his brother,”

Jesus takes the initiative. He sees them before they follow Him. Matthew calls Simon by the name Jesus gave him, Peter (John 1:42; Matthew 16:18), meaning “rock.” Peter will become the leading figure among the Twelve. Andrew had earlier been a disciple of John the Baptist and had brought Simon to Jesus (John 1:35–42). This meeting at the shore is not the brothers’ first encounter with Jesus, but it is His call to leave everything and follow Him.

“casting a net into the sea; for they were fishermen.”

They were at work. The net was a circular casting net, thrown by hand from the shore or in shallow water. Matthew notes their occupation because Jesus is about to use it to describe their new calling.

Study Notes

The Sea of Galilee is also called the Lake of Gennesaret (Luke 5:1) and the Sea of Tiberias (John 6:1; 21:1). In the Old Testament it is the Sea of Chinnereth (Numbers 34:11; Joshua 12:3).

Matthew uses a specific word here (amphiblēstron) for a circular net cast by one person. It differs from the large dragnet of 13:47 and from the general word for nets in 4:20.

Luke 5:1–11 describes a miraculous catch of fish followed by Jesus’ words that Simon would “catch men.” Some interpreters see Luke’s account as a fuller description of this same call; others see a separate occasion. Matthew and Mark focus on the call and the response.

John says Andrew and Peter were from Bethsaida (John 1:44), though by this time Peter had a home in Capernaum (Mark 1:21, 29).

Matthew 4:19
NKJV

Matthew 4:19 Then He said to them, "Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:19 Jesus called out to them, “Come, follow me, and I will show you how to fish for people!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus says to Simon Peter and Andrew, “Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.” He calls them to Himself and promises to make them fruitful in gathering people into God’s kingdom.

Verse Explanation

Jesus says to Simon Peter and Andrew, “Follow Me, and I will make you fishers of men.” He calls them to Himself and promises to make them fruitful in gathering people into God’s kingdom.

“Then He said to them, ‘Follow Me,”

The Greek reads literally, “Come after Me.” In Jesus’ day students usually chose a rabbi and attached themselves to his teaching. Jesus reverses the pattern. He chooses His disciples and calls them to Himself (John 15:16). “Follow Me” is a call to personal attachment to Jesus, learning His words, His ways, and His life by walking with Him.

“and I will make you fishers of men.”

Jesus connects their new calling to their old one. They had gathered fish; now they will gather people into God’s kingdom. “I will make you” places the work of transformation in Jesus’ hands. They are to follow, and He will do the making. The promise looks ahead to their mission after His resurrection, when He sends them to make disciples of all nations (28:19).

Study Notes

“After Me” is the language of following behind a master. Jesus uses the same expression when He says, “If anyone desires to come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow Me” (16:24).

Matthew 4:20
NKJV

Matthew 4:20 They immediately left their nets and followed Him.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:20 And they left their nets at once and followed him. (NLT)

Main Idea

Simon and Andrew immediately leave their nets and follow Jesus. Their prompt response shows the claim Jesus’ call places on livelihood and plans.

Verse Explanation

Simon and Andrew immediately leave their nets and follow Jesus. Their prompt response shows the claim Jesus’ call places on livelihood and plans.

“They immediately left their nets”

“Immediately” stresses the readiness of their response. They did not negotiate terms or ask for time. Their nets represented their livelihood, their skill, and their security. Leaving them meant trusting Jesus with their future.

“and followed Him.”

To follow Jesus meant going where He went, listening to His teaching, and sharing His life. Their step on the shore began a life lived with Him.

Study Notes

After the resurrection, Peter and others went fishing again (John 21:3). Leaving their nets meant that following Jesus came first, not that they could never touch a net again.

Main Idea

Going on, Jesus sees James and John, the sons of Zebedee, mending their nets in the boat with their father, and He calls them. A second pair of brothers receives the same summons.

Verse Explanation

Going on, Jesus sees James and John, the sons of Zebedee, mending their nets in the boat with their father, and He calls them. A second pair of brothers receives the same summons.

“Going on from there, He saw two other brothers, James the son of Zebedee, and John his brother,”

The scene repeats the pattern of 4:18. Jesus is moving, He sees, and He calls. James and John, with Peter, will become the inner circle among the disciples (17:1; 26:37). John is traditionally understood to be the author of the Gospel of John, the three letters of John, and Revelation. James will become the first of the apostles to be martyred (Acts 12:2).

“in the boat with Zebedee their father, mending their nets.”

Peter and Andrew were casting. James and John were mending, repairing torn nets and preparing them for the next use. The detail shows a family fishing business. Mark adds that Zebedee had hired servants (Mark 1:20), which suggests a business of some size.

“He called them,”

Matthew does not record Jesus’ words this time. The call itself is enough. The same Lord who called Peter and Andrew now calls these two.

Study Notes

The verb katartizō means to put in order, restore, or make complete. Here it describes repairing nets.

Luke notes that James and John were partners with Simon (Luke 5:10). Their mother later asks Jesus for places of honor for her sons (20:20–21), and she is among the women who followed Jesus from Galilee and stood near the cross (27:55–56).

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

James and John immediately leave the boat and their father and follow Jesus. Following Him takes priority even over family business and family ties.

“and immediately they left the boat and their father,”

Like Peter and Andrew, James and John respond at once. Their leaving goes further. They leave the boat, which represented the family business, and their father, which meant stepping away from family obligations that were deeply honored in that culture. Matthew does not suggest that they abandoned their father irresponsibly, and Zebedee was not left alone in the work (Mark 1:20). But their first loyalty now belonged to Jesus.

“and followed Him.”

The paragraph closes as the previous one did (4:20). Four fishermen now follow Jesus. They will witness His teaching, His miracles, His death, and His resurrection, and they will carry His message to the nations.

Matthew 4:23–25 — Jesus Teaches, Preaches, and Heals throughout Galilee

Study Notes

None.

Matthew 4:23
Matthew 4:23
NKJV

Matthew 4:23 And Jesus went about all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues, preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing all kinds of sickness and all kinds of disease among the people.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:23 Jesus traveled throughout the region of Galilee, teaching in the synagogues and announcing the Good News about the Kingdom. And he healed every kind of disease and illness. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus goes throughout Galilee teaching in the synagogues, preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every kind of sickness and disease. His teaching, His proclamation, and His healing work together to reveal the King’s reign and His compassion.

Verse Explanation

Jesus goes throughout Galilee teaching in the synagogues, preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every kind of sickness and disease. His teaching, His proclamation, and His healing work together to reveal the King’s reign and His compassion.

“And Jesus went about all Galilee,”

Jesus did not wait for people to come to Him. He traveled throughout the region, taking His ministry to its many towns and villages.

“teaching in their synagogues,”

Synagogues were local gathering places where Jews met on the Sabbath to read and explain the Scriptures and to pray. Visiting teachers could be invited to speak (Luke 4:16–17; Acts 13:15). Jesus used these gatherings to teach God’s word. “Teaching” focuses on explaining God’s truth and its meaning for life, as Jesus will do in the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5–7).

“preaching the gospel of the kingdom,”

“Preaching” means announcing, as a herald does. “Gospel” means good news. The good news is that God’s kingdom has come near in Jesus (4:17). Matthew uses the phrase “gospel of the kingdom” here, at 9:35, and at 24:14, where Jesus says this gospel will be preached in all the world before the end comes.

“and healing all kinds of sickness and all kinds of disease among the people.”

Healing accompanied Jesus’ preaching. “All kinds” stresses that no illness was beyond His power. The healings were acts of compassion and signs that the kingdom had come. Where God’s rule breaks in, sickness and the devil’s oppression are driven back. Isaiah had foretold that in the day of God’s salvation the blind would see, the deaf would hear, and the lame would leap (Isaiah 35:5–6). Matthew later presents Jesus’ healings as fulfilling Isaiah 53:4: “He Himself took our infirmities and bore our sicknesses” (8:17).

Study Notes

Matthew 9:35 repeats this verse almost word for word. The two summaries frame the Sermon on the Mount (chapters 5–7), which displays Jesus’ authority in teaching, and the miracles of chapters 8–9, which display His authority in action.

The two Greek words (nosos and malakia) overlap in meaning. Nosos is a general word for illness, while malakia suggests weakness or infirmity. Together they cover the whole range of human suffering.

Josephus, writing a generation later, counts more than two hundred towns and villages in Galilee (Life 235). Even if the figure is generous, it shows how much ground “all Galilee” covers.

Matthew 4:24

NKJV

Matthew 4:24 Then His fame went throughout all Syria; and they brought to Him all sick people who were afflicted with various diseases and torments, and those who were demon-possessed, epileptics, and paralytics; and He healed them.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 4:24 News about him spread as far as Syria, and people soon began bringing to him all who were sick. And whatever their sickness or disease, or if they were demon possessed or epileptic or paralyzed — he healed them all. (NLT)

Main Idea

News of Jesus spreads throughout Syria, and people bring Him all who are suffering, including the demon-possessed, epileptics, and paralytics, and He heals them. Matthew distinguishes disease from demonic oppression, and Jesus has authority over both.

Verse Explanation

News of Jesus spreads throughout Syria, and people bring Him all who are suffering, including the demon-possessed, epileptics, and paralytics, and He heals them. Matthew distinguishes disease from demonic oppression, and Jesus has authority over both.

“Then His fame went throughout all Syria;”

“Syria” here most likely refers to the Roman province north of Galilee, a largely Gentile region that included Damascus and the Phoenician coast. Reports about Jesus spread beyond Jewish territory, a first sign of how far His message would reach.

“and they brought to Him all sick people who were afflicted with various diseases and torments,”

People carried or led the sick to Jesus. “Torments” describes severe pain and suffering. Matthew emphasizes both the range and the severity of what people brought to Him.

“and those who were demon-possessed, epileptics, and paralytics;”

Matthew names three groups. The demon-possessed were under the control of evil spirits. Epileptics suffered seizures. Paralytics could not move parts of their bodies. Matthew lists demonic oppression separately from physical illness. Scripture does not attribute every sickness to demons, and it does not deny that some afflictions are demonic (17:15–18).

“and He healed them.”

The sentence ends simply. Whatever the condition, Jesus healed. The One who resisted the devil in the wilderness now sets people free from the devil’s oppression.

Study Notes

The word literally means “moonstruck,” reflecting an ancient belief that the moon influenced seizures. Older English versions translate it “lunatick.” Matthew uses the word again at 17:15, where a boy’s seizures are tied to a demon. Here the demonized and the epileptic are listed separately, so Matthew does not treat the two as identical.

The noun basanos first referred to a touchstone for testing metals and came to be used for torture or severe pain.

The Roman province of Syria was large, and its governor oversaw much of the eastern Mediterranean, including at times the affairs of Judea. Here “all Syria” most naturally refers to the territory north of Galilee.

NLT

Matthew 4:25 Large crowds followed him wherever he went — people from Galilee, the Ten Towns, Jerusalem, from all over Judea, and from east of the Jordan River. (NLT)

Main Idea

Great multitudes follow Him from Galilee, Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea, and beyond the Jordan. People from every region gather around Jesus, setting the scene for the Sermon on the Mount.

Verse Explanation

Great multitudes follow Him from Galilee, Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea, and beyond the Jordan. People from every region gather around Jesus, setting the scene for the Sermon on the Mount.

“Great multitudes followed Him—”

The crowds “followed” Jesus, the same word used of the four disciples (4:20, 22). Following as part of a crowd is different from following as a disciple. The crowds were drawn by His teaching and healing, and whether they would commit themselves to Him remained to be seen. Jesus will speak in the hearing of both groups in the Sermon on the Mount (5:1; 7:28).

“from Galilee, and from Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea, and beyond the Jordan.”

Matthew lists the regions. Galilee was the center of Jesus’ ministry. The Decapolis was a league of Greek cities, mostly east of the Jordan and the Sea of Galilee, with a largely Gentile population. Jerusalem and Judea lay to the south. “Beyond the Jordan” refers to Perea, the region east of the river. People came from nearly every part of the land, along with Gentile regions. Samaria is not mentioned.

Study Notes

The name means “ten cities.” Lists of the cities vary, but they include Scythopolis (Beth Shean), the only one west of the Jordan, along with Gadara (compare 8:28), Gerasa, Hippos, Pella, and Philadelphia (modern Amman). Later, a man Jesus delivered from demons proclaimed what Jesus had done for him throughout the Decapolis (Mark 5:20).
