Chapter Study

Matthew 6

Verse-by-Verse
Matthew 6:1
Matthew 6:1
NKJV

Matthew 6:1 "Take heed that you do not do your charitable deeds before men, to be seen by them. Otherwise you have no reward from your Father in heaven.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:1 “Watch out! Don’t do your good deeds publicly, to be admired by others, for you will lose the reward from your Father in heaven. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus warns His disciples not to do their charitable deeds before people in order to be seen, or they will have no reward from their Father in heaven. The same good deed can be offered to the Father or performed for an audience, and only the first receives His reward.

Verse Explanation

Jesus warns His disciples not to do their charitable deeds before people in order to be seen, or they will have no reward from their Father in heaven. The same good deed can be offered to the Father or performed for an audience, and only the first receives His reward.

“Take heed”

Jesus calls for watchfulness. The danger He describes is subtle. It does not tempt people to abandon devotion but to corrupt it, and it can affect sincere people who are doing the right things.

In chapter 5 Jesus declared that His disciples’ righteousness must exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees (5:20), and He showed that God’s commands reach beneath outward conduct to the heart. Now He applies the same searching standard to three practices at the center of Jewish devotion: giving to the poor, prayer, and fasting. Matthew 6:1 states the principle that governs all three.

“that you do not do your charitable deeds before men, to be seen by them”

“Charitable deeds” refers to acts of mercy and devotion done in obedience to God. The problem is not that others see them. Jesus has just told His disciples to let their light shine so that people see their good works (5:16). The difference is purpose. In 5:16 good works are seen so that people will glorify the Father. Here they are done so that people will admire the one doing them. The phrase “to be seen by them” translates a Greek word related to our word “theater.” Jesus is describing devotion performed for an audience.

“Otherwise you have no reward from your Father in heaven.”

Jesus assumes that the Father rewards what is done for Him, and He does not treat that expectation as unworthy. But a person who performs for human approval has chosen a different paymaster. The phrase “your Father in heaven” also introduces the theme of the whole passage. The Father is named ten times in 6:1–18. A disciple’s devotion is meant to be the expression of a real relationship with Him.

Study Notes

The NKJV follows the majority of Greek manuscripts, which read “charitable deeds” (eleēmosynē, the word used for giving to the poor in 6:2–4). The oldest manuscripts read “righteousness” (dikaiosynē). With that reading, 6:1 serves as a heading over all three practices that follow and links directly to the “righteousness” of 5:20. The overall meaning is the same either way.

Giving to the poor, prayer, and fasting were familiar Jewish practices. Tobit 12:8 commends prayer, fasting, righteousness, and almsgiving together.

Matthew 6:2
Matthew 6:2
NKJV

Matthew 6:2 Therefore, when you do a charitable deed, do not sound a trumpet before you as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and in the streets, that they may have glory from men. Assuredly, I say to you, they have their reward.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:2 When you give to someone in need, don’t do as the hypocrites do — blowing trumpets in the synagogues and streets to call attention to their acts of charity! I tell you the truth, they have received all the reward they will ever get. (NLT)

Main Idea

When giving to the needy, disciples are not to sound a trumpet as the hypocrites do in synagogues and streets to win praise from people; such givers already have their reward. Giving designed for applause receives applause and nothing more.

Verse Explanation

When giving to the needy, disciples are not to sound a trumpet as the hypocrites do in synagogues and streets to win praise from people; such givers already have their reward. Giving designed for applause receives applause and nothing more.

“Therefore, when you do a charitable deed”

Jesus says “when,” not “if.” Giving to the poor is an expected part of a disciple’s life. The Law commanded open-handed generosity toward the needy (Deuteronomy 15:7–11), and Jesus takes that obedience for granted.

“do not sound a trumpet before you”

The picture is of someone announcing his own generosity with fanfare. Whether Jesus is describing an actual practice or using a vivid figure of speech, the point is clear. The giver is calling attention to himself.

“as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and in the streets”

“Hypocrite” originally described an actor on a stage, someone who played a role behind a mask. These hypocrites perform their generosity where the largest audience will see it: in the synagogues, where the community gathered for worship, and in the streets, where people passed by.

“that they may have glory from men”

Their goal is honor for themselves. The glory that good works are meant to bring to the Father (5:16) is redirected to the giver.

“Assuredly, I say to you, they have their reward.”

“Assuredly” translates the word “amen,” which Jesus uses to introduce solemn, authoritative statements. “They have their reward” uses a word common in business receipts of the time, meaning “paid in full.” The hypocrites wanted human praise, and they received it. Nothing more is owed them.

Study Notes

The verb apechō appears in ancient business papyri as a technical term for receiving full payment and issuing a receipt. Jesus uses the same expression for the hypocrites who give (6:2), pray (6:5), and fast (6:16).

No ancient source describes trumpets being blown to announce gifts to the poor. Some have suggested a connection with the temple’s collection chests, which were called “trumpets” because of their horn-like openings, or with trumpets sounded at public fasts. Most likely Jesus is using a figure of speech much like the modern expression blowing your own horn.

The Greek hypokritēs was the ordinary word for a stage actor. Matthew uses it more than any other Gospel writer, especially in Jesus’ woes against the scribes and Pharisees in chapter 23. It describes the gap between outward appearance and inward reality.

Main Idea

When giving, a disciple should not let the left hand know what the right hand is doing. The vivid picture calls for generosity so unselfconscious that it seeks no notice, even from oneself.

Verse Explanation

When giving, a disciple should not let the left hand know what the right hand is doing. The vivid picture calls for generosity so unselfconscious that it seeks no notice, even from oneself.

“But when you do a charitable deed”

Jesus now gives His disciples the alternative to the hypocrites’ display.

“do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing”

This is a vivid figure of speech. A person’s two hands work together closely, yet Jesus pictures one hand acting without the other noticing. Giving should be done so quietly that even those closest to the giver may not know about it.

The expression also reaches inward. A person can avoid public display and still keep a private record of his own generosity, admiring what he has done. Jesus calls His disciples to give without dwelling on it.

None.

Matthew 6:4
Matthew 6:4
NKJV

Matthew 6:4 that your charitable deed may be in secret; and your Father who sees in secret will Himself reward you openly.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:4 Give your gifts in private, and your Father, who sees everything, will reward you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Giving done in secret stays hidden from others, and the Father who sees in secret will Himself reward it openly. The Father notices what no one else sees, and His reward is the one worth having.

Verse Explanation

Giving done in secret stays hidden from others, and the Father who sees in secret will Himself reward it openly. The Father notices what no one else sees, and His reward is the one worth having.

“that your charitable deed may be in secret”

The purpose of the hidden giving in 6:3 is that the gift remain between the giver and God.

“and your Father who sees in secret”

What is hidden from people is never hidden from God (Psalm 139:1–4; Hebrews 4:13). That truth can be a warning, since no motive escapes Him. Here it is a comfort. The gift no one else noticed did not go unnoticed. The Father saw it.

“will Himself reward you openly.”

“Himself” is emphatic. The reward comes from the Father personally. “Openly” stands in contrast to “in secret.” The gift was hidden, but the Father’s reward will not be. Jesus does not describe the reward in detail, and He does not present it as a wage earned. It comes from a generous Father who delights to honor what is done for Him. Proverbs states the same principle: “He who has pity on the poor lends to the LORD, and He will pay back what he has given” (Proverbs 19:17).

Study Notes

The word “openly” appears in the majority of Greek manuscripts at 6:4, 6, and 18, and the NKJV includes it. The oldest manuscripts do not have it. Without it, the promise is simply that the Father “will reward you.” The certainty of the reward remains the same.

Matthew 6:5–15 — Prayer and the Lord’s Prayer

Matthew 6:5
Matthew 6:5
NKJV

Matthew 6:5 "And when you pray, you shall not be like the hypocrites. For they love to pray standing in the synagogues and on the corners of the streets, that they may be seen by men. Assuredly, I say to you, they have their reward.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:5 “When you pray, don’t be like the hypocrites who love to pray publicly on street corners and in the synagogues where everyone can see them. I tell you the truth, that is all the reward they will ever get. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus warns against praying like the hypocrites, who love to pray standing in synagogues and on street corners to be seen by people; they already have their reward. Prayer turned into a public display is no longer directed to God.

Verse Explanation

Jesus warns against praying like the hypocrites, who love to pray standing in synagogues and on street corners to be seen by people; they already have their reward. Prayer turned into a public display is no longer directed to God.

“And when you pray, you shall not be like the hypocrites.”

Again Jesus says “when.” Prayer is assumed to be a normal part of a disciple’s life. The warning concerns the way the hypocrites pray.

Jesus now applies the same principle to prayer. This is the longest of the three sections, because Jesus not only corrects false prayer but also gives His disciples a pattern for true prayer (6:9–13).

“For they love to pray standing in the synagogues and on the corners of the streets”

Standing was a common posture for prayer (1 Samuel 1:26; Mark 11:25). Devout Jews observed set times of prayer during the day, and the hour of prayer could easily find a person in a public place. The hypocrites made sure it found them where they would be noticed. The key word is “love.” They loved to pray, but what they loved was the setting and the audience.

“that they may be seen by men.”

Their prayer was addressed to God in words but directed toward people in purpose. Jesus does not condemn public prayer. He prayed publicly Himself (11:25; 14:19), and the early church often prayed together (Acts 2:42; 4:24). He condemns prayer performed for an audience.

“Assuredly, I say to you, they have their reward.”

As with those who give for show (6:2), the hypocrites receive what they sought. They are seen and admired, and that is all their prayer accomplishes.

Study Notes

Many Jews prayed at fixed times each day, commonly morning, afternoon, and evening (Psalm 55:17; Daniel 6:10). The afternoon prayer coincided with the evening sacrifice at the temple, “the hour of prayer, the ninth hour” (Acts 3:1). The principal prayer of later Jewish worship came to be called the Amidah, meaning “standing,” because it was prayed standing.

Matthew 6:6
Matthew 6:6
NKJV

Matthew 6:6 But you, when you pray, go into your room, and when you have shut your door, pray to your Father who is in the secret place; and your Father who sees in secret will reward you openly.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:6 But when you pray, go away by yourself, shut the door behind you, and pray to your Father in private. Then your Father, who sees everything, will reward you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Instead, a disciple should go into his room, shut the door, and pray to the Father who is in the secret place, and the Father who sees in secret will reward him openly. Prayer is personal communion with the Father, who welcomes His children into His presence.

Verse Explanation

Instead, a disciple should go into his room, shut the door, and pray to the Father who is in the secret place, and the Father who sees in secret will reward him openly. Prayer is personal communion with the Father, who welcomes His children into His presence.

“But you, when you pray”

The Greek shifts to the singular “you.” Having described the hypocrites as a group, Jesus now speaks to each disciple individually.

“go into your room”

The word translated “room” referred to an inner room or storeroom. In an ordinary home it was often the only room with a door that could be closed. Jesus chooses an everyday place. Private prayer does not require a special building or a holy site.

“and when you have shut your door”

Shutting the door closes out both the audience and the distractions of the household. It is a deliberate decision to be alone with God.

“pray to your Father who is in the secret place”

The disciple does not enter the room to find a God who is absent. The Father is already there. The secret place is where He meets His children.

“and your Father who sees in secret will reward you openly.”

The hypocrites had their reward from people (6:5). The disciple who prays in secret receives his reward from the Father, who sees what no one else sees.

Study Notes

The noun tameion refers to an inner room or storeroom. It is also used for “inner rooms” in Matthew 24:26. The Greek wording resembles Isaiah 26:20, which speaks of entering rooms and shutting doors.

Matthew 6:7
Matthew 6:7
NKJV

Matthew 6:7 And when you pray, do not use vain repetitions as the heathen do. For they think that they will be heard for their many words.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:7 “When you pray, don’t babble on and on as the Gentiles do. They think their prayers are answered merely by repeating their words again and again. (NLT)

Main Idea

Disciples are not to use vain repetitions as the Gentiles do, who think they will be heard for their many words. Jesus rejects empty, mechanical words used to pressure God, not sincere prayer that returns to the same request.

Verse Explanation

Disciples are not to use vain repetitions as the Gentiles do, who think they will be heard for their many words. Jesus rejects empty, mechanical words used to pressure God, not sincere prayer that returns to the same request.

“And when you pray, do not use vain repetitions”

Having warned against praying to impress people, Jesus now warns against a wrong way of approaching God. “Vain repetitions” refers to empty, babbling speech, words strung together without meaning or heart. Jesus does not forbid all repetition. He prayed the same words three times in Gethsemane (26:44), and some psalms repeat a refrain throughout (Psalm 136). What He forbids is words used as a technique.

“as the heathen do”

“Heathen” refers to Gentiles who did not know the God of Israel. Their prayers often piled up the names and titles of their gods and repeated set formulas in hope of securing a hearing. The prophets of Baal cried “O Baal, hear us!” from morning until noon (1 Kings 18:26).

“For they think that they will be heard for their many words.”

This exposes the belief behind the practice. Pagan prayer assumed that the gods had to be persuaded, pressured, or worn down. The more words, the better the chance of being heard. Jesus’ disciples know a different God.

Study Notes

The Greek verb battalogeō appears nowhere else in the New Testament and is rare outside it. It may imitate the sound of meaningless chatter, much like the English word “babble.” The emphasis falls on empty speech, not on repetition as such.

Matthew 6:8
Matthew 6:8
NKJV

Matthew 6:8 "Therefore do not be like them. For your Father knows the things you have need of before you ask Him.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:8 Don’t be like them, for your Father knows exactly what you need even before you ask him! (NLT)

Main Idea

Disciples must not be like the people who think many words will win a hearing, because “your Father knows the things you have need of before you ask Him.” Prayer rests on a relationship with a Father who already knows and cares, not on persuading a reluctant God.

Verse Explanation

Disciples must not be like the people who think many words will win a hearing, because “your Father knows the things you have need of before you ask Him.” Prayer rests on a relationship with a Father who already knows and cares, not on persuading a reluctant God.

“Therefore do not be like them.”

Jesus draws the conclusion directly. His disciples must not adopt the pagan approach to prayer.

“For your Father knows the things you have need of before you ask Him.”

The reason is the character of the One they pray to. He is “your Father,” and He already knows what His children need. Prayer does not inform God of facts He has overlooked.

This naturally raises a question: if the Father already knows, why pray? Jesus answers by immediately teaching His disciples how to pray (6:9). The Father’s knowledge does not make prayer unnecessary. It makes prayer confident. God has chosen to act in response to the prayers of His people (7:7–11; James 4:2), and He invites them to bring their needs to Him as children, not as strangers trying to get His attention.

Study Notes

None.

Matthew 6:9
Matthew 6:9
NKJV

Matthew 6:9 In this manner, therefore, pray: Our Father in heaven, Hallowed be Your name.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:9 Pray like this: Our Father in heaven,may your name be kept holy. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus gives the pattern: “Our Father in heaven, Hallowed be Your name.” Prayer begins with God, addressing Him as Father in heaven and asking that His name be honored as holy, including in the lives of those who pray.

Verse Explanation

Jesus gives the pattern: “Our Father in heaven, Hallowed be Your name.” Prayer begins with God, addressing Him as Father in heaven and asking that His name be honored as holy, including in the lives of those who pray.

“In this manner, therefore, pray”

“In this manner” means “like this.” Jesus gives a model that shows the priorities and character of true prayer. Disciples may pray these words directly, as Christians have done from the earliest days, and they may also pray along the lines the prayer lays out. “Therefore” ties the prayer to what precedes it. In contrast to the many words of the pagans, this prayer is brief, ordered, and addressed to a Father who already knows.

“Our Father in heaven”

“Our” makes this a family prayer. Even when a disciple prays alone behind a closed door, he prays as one of the Father’s children alongside others. “Father” is the heart of the prayer. In the Old Testament God is occasionally called the Father of Israel (Deuteronomy 32:6; Isaiah 63:16), but Jesus speaks to God as His own Father with a closeness that is new (11:27). Now He brings His disciples into that relationship and teaches them to call God “Father” too.

“In heaven” holds the other side of the truth. The One who is near as Father is also the exalted Lord who rules over all. True prayer combines intimate trust with reverent awe.

“Hallowed be Your name.”

“Hallowed” means regarded and treated as holy. God’s “name” represents His revealed character, all that He has made known about Himself. This first request asks God to act so that His name is honored as holy throughout the world, and it includes the desire that His people honor it in their own lives. The prophets looked for the day when God would sanctify His great name among the nations (Ezekiel 36:23). Before the prayer asks for anything for ourselves, it asks for God’s glory.

Study Notes

After the address, the prayer contains three requests concerning God (“Your name,” “Your kingdom,” “Your will”) followed by requests concerning the disciples’ needs (“give us,” “forgive us,” “do not lead us,” “deliver us”). God’s honor comes first, and human need follows.

Luke records a shorter form of the prayer, given when a disciple asked Jesus to teach them to pray (Luke 11:1–4). Jesus likely taught this prayer on more than one occasion.

Several phrases resemble the Kaddish, a Jewish prayer whose early form may go back to the time of Jesus. It asks that God’s great name be magnified and sanctified and that He establish His kingdom. Jesus’ prayer stands out for its brevity and for addressing God simply as “Father.”

“Hallowed be” is a passive verb form: the petition asks that God’s name be treated as holy.

Matthew 6:10
Matthew 6:10
NKJV

Matthew 6:10 Your kingdom come. Your will be done On earth as it is in heaven.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:10 May your Kingdom come soon.May your will be done on earth,as it is in heaven. (NLT)

Main Idea

The prayer continues, “Your kingdom come. Your will be done On earth as it is in heaven.” Disciples ask that God’s rule be willingly received and His will be done, beginning with their own surrender and reaching their homes, churches, and the world.

Verse Explanation

The prayer continues, “Your kingdom come. Your will be done On earth as it is in heaven.” Disciples ask that God’s rule be willingly received and His will be done, beginning with their own surrender and reaching their homes, churches, and the world.

“Your kingdom come.”

The kingdom of heaven is the central theme of Jesus’ preaching in Matthew (4:17). It refers to God’s reign, His rule breaking into the world through Jesus. In one sense the kingdom has already come. Jesus’ authority over demons shows that “the kingdom of God has come upon you” (12:28). In another sense it awaits its full arrival when the Son of Man comes in glory (25:31–34). This prayer asks for both: that God’s rule would advance now and that it would come in its fullness.

“Your will be done”

This request asks that what God desires would actually take place. It expresses both longing and surrender. Jesus Himself prayed these exact words in Gethsemane as He faced the cross: “O My Father, if this cup cannot pass away from Me unless I drink it, Your will be done” (26:42).

“On earth as it is in heaven.”

In heaven God’s will is done completely and gladly. The prayer asks that earth would become like heaven in this respect. The phrase may apply to all three of the first requests: that God’s name be hallowed, His kingdom come, and His will be done on earth as in heaven.

Study Notes

Matthew usually writes “kingdom of heaven” where the other Gospels write “kingdom of God,” likely reflecting the Jewish practice of reverently avoiding direct references to God. The two expressions mean the same thing, as Jesus’ use of both side by side shows (19:23–24).

Matthew 6:11
Matthew 6:11
NKJV

Matthew 6:11 Give us this day our daily bread.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:11 Give us today the food we need, (NLT)

Main Idea

Disciples pray, “Give us this day our daily bread.” They depend on the Father for the provision of each day and receive it with gratitude.

Verse Explanation

Disciples pray, “Give us this day our daily bread.” They depend on the Father for the provision of each day and receive it with gratitude.

“Give us this day”

The prayer now turns to the disciples’ own needs. Even people who work hard for their food are taught to ask God for it, because every provision ultimately comes from His hand (Psalm 145:15–16). “This day” keeps the request focused on the present.

“our daily bread.”

Bread was the basic food of daily life, and it stands here for the necessities a person needs to live. The word translated “daily” is extremely rare, and its exact meaning is uncertain. It likely means the bread needed for today or for the day ahead. Either way, the request is modest. Disciples ask for what they need, not for abundance to store up. The request recalls the manna God gave Israel in the wilderness. Each day the people gathered what they needed for that day, and what they tried to hoard spoiled (Exodus 16:4, 16–20).

Study Notes

The adjective epiousios occurs in the New Testament only here and in Luke 11:3. Proposed meanings include necessary for existence, “for today,” and for the coming day. Jerome reports a reading meaning “tomorrow” in a Hebrew-language gospel; his Latin Matthew renders the word “supersubstantial.”

Matthew 6:12
Matthew 6:12
NKJV

Matthew 6:12 And forgive us our debts, As we forgive our debtors.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:12 and forgive us our sins,as we have forgiven those who sin against us. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus teaches His disciples to pray, “forgive us our debts, As we forgive our debtors.” Every disciple needs the Father’s forgiveness, and those who receive it are to release others from what they owe.

Verse Explanation

Jesus teaches His disciples to pray, “forgive us our debts, As we forgive our debtors.” Every disciple needs the Father’s forgiveness, and those who receive it are to release others from what they owe.

“And forgive us our debts”

Jesus describes sin as a debt owed to God. Every failure to love and obey Him leaves us owing what we cannot repay. The parable of the unforgiving servant pictures the size of that debt (18:23–27). Because this request belongs to a prayer meant for regular use, it assumes that disciples will continually need forgiveness and should regularly ask for it (1 John 1:9).

“As we forgive our debtors.”

Those who wrong us are pictured as people who owe us a debt. The disciple asks for forgiveness as one who also releases others. “As” does not mean that God’s forgiveness is earned by our forgiving others or measured by it. It means that the person asking for mercy must also be a person who extends it. Asking God to cancel our debts while holding others’ debts against them is a contradiction Jesus will not allow. He returns to this in 6:14–15.

Study Notes

In Aramaic, the language Jesus commonly spoke, the same word could mean both “debt” and “sin.” Luke’s version reads, “forgive us our sins, for we also forgive everyone who is indebted to us” (Luke 11:4), showing that the two ideas belong together.

The NKJV follows manuscripts that read the present tense, “as we forgive.” The oldest manuscripts read a past tense, “as we have forgiven,” picturing a disciple who comes to God having already released those who owe him.

Matthew 6:13
Matthew 6:13
NKJV

Matthew 6:13 And do not lead us into temptation, But deliver us from the evil one. For Yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever. Amen.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:13 And don’t let us yield to temptation,but rescue us from the evil one. (NLT)

Main Idea

The praying disciples ask, “And do not lead us into temptation, But deliver us from the evil one,” then close with praise to God for the kingdom, power, and glory forever. The prayer humbly admits weakness and asks the Father to keep His children from trials in which they might fall and to rescue them from Satan’s schemes and power.

Verse Explanation

The praying disciples ask, “And do not lead us into temptation, But deliver us from the evil one,” then close with praise to God for the kingdom, power, and glory forever. The prayer humbly admits weakness and asks the Father to keep His children from trials in which they might fall and to rescue them from Satan’s schemes and power.

“And do not lead us into temptation”

God does not tempt anyone to sin (James 1:13). The Greek word for “temptation” can also mean “testing” or “trial,” and God does allow His people to be tested. Jesus Himself “was led up by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil” (4:1). This request asks the Father not to bring us into a trial where we would be overcome, and to keep us from yielding when temptation comes. It is a prayer of humility from people who know their own weakness. In Gethsemane Jesus told His disciples, “Watch and pray, lest you enter into temptation” (26:41).

“But deliver us from the evil one.”

“Deliver” means to rescue. “The evil one” is Satan, the tempter Jesus faced in the wilderness, who also snatches away the word sown in people’s hearts (13:19). Disciples live in a world where spiritual opposition is real, and they pray for the Father’s protection from it. The prayer also asks for rescue where the enemy has already gained influence or a foothold in a believer’s life. Belonging to Christ is secure, yet a believer can still need deliverance from the enemy’s oppression, and the Father answers this prayer.

“For Yours is the kingdom and the power and the glory forever. Amen.”

The prayer ends in praise. These words echo David’s prayer in 1 Chronicles 29:11: “Yours, O LORD, is the greatness, The power and the glory.” They gather up the whole prayer. The kingdom the disciples asked to come belongs to God. The power to provide, forgive, and deliver is His. The glory of His hallowed name is His forever. “Amen” affirms all that has been prayed: So be it.

Study Notes

The noun peirasmos can mean testing or temptation. Both senses occur in the New Testament; the adjoining request asks for deliverance from evil or the evil one.

The Greek phrase can be read as “evil” in general or as “the evil one.” Matthew uses the same expression for Satan in 13:19 and 13:38, where the NKJV translates it “the wicked one,” and Jesus prays that the Father would keep His disciples “from the evil one” (John 17:15).

The closing doxology is present in the majority of Greek manuscripts and in the NKJV, but absent from several of the oldest manuscripts. A shorter form appears in Didache 8.2. Its presence in early prayer practice is one proposed explanation of the manuscript variation.

Matthew 6:14
Matthew 6:14
NKJV

Matthew 6:14 "For if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:14 “If you forgive those who sin against you, your heavenly Father will forgive you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus tells His disciples that if they forgive others their trespasses, their heavenly Father will also forgive them. Forgiving others belongs to the life of those who have received the Father’s mercy.

Verse Explanation

Jesus tells His disciples that if they forgive others their trespasses, their heavenly Father will also forgive them. Forgiving others belongs to the life of those who have received the Father’s mercy.

“For if you forgive men their trespasses”

“For” connects these words to 6:12. Of all the requests in the prayer, forgiveness is the only one Jesus returns to and explains. “Trespasses” are false steps or offenses, the ways people wrong one another.

“your heavenly Father will also forgive you.”

Jesus is speaking to disciples who already know God as their heavenly Father. Forgiving others does not purchase God’s forgiveness. Paul states the order clearly: “forgiving one another, even as God in Christ forgave you” (Ephesians 4:32). God’s mercy comes first. But a heart that has truly received that mercy becomes a forgiving heart, and the Father continues to forgive children who walk in His mercy toward others.

Study Notes

In 6:12 Jesus uses “debts” (opheilēmata). In 6:14–15 He uses “trespasses” (paraptōmata), a word for false steps or offenses. The change in vocabulary shows that the same reality is in view from two angles. Mark records a similar teaching connected with prayer (Mark 11:25).

Matthew 6:15
Matthew 6:15
NKJV

Matthew 6:15 But if you do not forgive men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses.(NKJV)

NLT

Main Idea

“Moreover, when you fast”

Once again Jesus says “when.” The Law required fasting on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:29), and God’s people fasted in times of repentance, mourning, and seeking His help (Ezra 8:21; Joel 2:12). Jesus later said that His disciples would fast after He was taken from them (9:15). He assumes fasting will be part of their lives.

“do not be like the hypocrites, with a sad countenance.”

A “sad countenance” is a gloomy, downcast face put on for display.

“For they disfigure their faces that they may appear to men to be fasting.”

The hypocrites made their faces look neglected, likely by not washing, leaving their hair unkempt, or putting ashes on their heads, so that everyone would know they were fasting. Their fast was designed to be noticed.

“Assuredly, I say to you, they have their reward.”

For the third time Jesus pronounces the same verdict. Those who fast for recognition receive it, and that is all they receive.

Study Notes

The Greek contains a play on words that is difficult to reproduce in English. The hypocrites make their faces disappear (aphanizō, “disfigure”) so that they may “appear” (phainō) to people as fasting.

Some Jews fasted twice a week (Luke 18:12), traditionally on Mondays and Thursdays. Didache 8.1 directs Christians to fast on Wednesdays and Fridays instead.

“Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth”

The Greek repeats the same root: do not treasure up treasures. The phrase “for yourselves” points to self-centered accumulation. Jesus does not forbid owning property, saving wisely, or providing for one’s family (Proverbs 6:6–8; 1 Timothy 5:8). He forbids storing up wealth as one’s security and the goal of one’s life.

Jesus turns from acts of devotion to possessions, but the underlying issue is the same. In 6:1–18 the question was whose approval a person seeks. Now the question is what a person treasures and trusts.

“where moth and rust destroy”

In the ancient world wealth was often kept in fine clothing, stored grain, and precious metals. Moths ate fabric. “Rust” translates a word meaning “eating,” which may describe corrosion or the pests that consumed stored goods. Over time, earthly treasure wears away.

“and where thieves break in and steal.”

“Break in” literally means “dig through.” Many homes had walls of mud brick, which a thief could dig through. What decay does not slowly destroy, theft can suddenly take.

Study Notes

Fine garments could be stored as wealth. Joshua 7:21 lists a Babylonian garment alongside silver and gold among the valuables Achan took.

“You cannot serve God and mammon.”

“Mammon” is an Aramaic word for wealth and possessions. Jesus personifies it as a rival master that competes with God for a person’s service. Jesus does not say “should not” but “cannot.” Divided allegiance is impossible.

Matthew 6:33
Matthew 6:33
NKJV

Matthew 6:33 But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things shall be added to you.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:33 Seek the Kingdom of God above all else, and live righteously, and he will give you everything you need. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus commands, “But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things shall be added to you.” Making the King’s rule and righteousness life’s first priority frees disciples to trust the Father for the needs Jesus has named.

Verse Explanation

Jesus commands, “But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things shall be added to you.” Making the King’s rule and righteousness life’s first priority frees disciples to trust the Father for the needs Jesus has named.

“But seek first”

“But” sets the disciples apart from the Gentiles of 6:32, who seek material things. “Seek” is a continuing command: keep on seeking. “First” refers to priority. The kingdom is to come first in importance, desire, and decision.

“the kingdom of God”

To seek God’s kingdom is to seek His rule: to submit to it, pray for it (6:10), live under it, and work for its advance in the world.

“and His righteousness”

“His righteousness” is the right living that God requires and produces in His people, the righteousness Jesus has been describing throughout the Sermon on the Mount (5:6, 20). It is God’s own way of life worked into those who belong to Him.

“and all these things shall be added to you.”

“All these things” are the necessities Jesus has been discussing: food, drink, and clothing (6:31–32). “Added” means given in addition. As disciples pursue God’s kingdom, the Father adds what they need. This is a promise of provision for life’s necessities, not a formula for gaining wealth, and it rests on the character of the Father who knows what His children need.

Study Notes

Some early manuscripts read simply “the kingdom” rather than “the kingdom of God.” The meaning is unchanged, since “His righteousness” refers to God in either case. Matthew usually writes “kingdom of heaven” and uses “kingdom of God” only a few times (12:28; 19:24; 21:31, 43).

Matthew 6:34
Matthew 6:34
NKJV

Matthew 6:34 Therefore do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will worry about its own things. Sufficient for the day is its own trouble.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 6:34 “So don’t worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will bring its own worries. Today’s trouble is enough for today. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus concludes, “Therefore do not worry about tomorrow,” for tomorrow will bring its own concerns, and each day has enough trouble of its own. Disciples meet each day’s trouble with the grace God gives for that day, rather than carrying tomorrow’s burdens in advance.

Verse Explanation

Jesus concludes, “Therefore do not worry about tomorrow,” for tomorrow will bring its own concerns, and each day has enough trouble of its own. Disciples meet each day’s trouble with the grace God gives for that day, rather than carrying tomorrow’s burdens in advance.

“Therefore do not worry about tomorrow”

Jesus closes the section with a final application. Worry is usually about the future, about what may or may not happen.

“for tomorrow will worry about its own things.”

Jesus speaks of tomorrow as if it were a person with its own concerns. Tomorrow’s needs will be dealt with when tomorrow comes. Disciples do not need to carry them today.

“Sufficient for the day is its own trouble.”

“Trouble” here means hardship or difficulty. Jesus is realistic. He does not promise days without problems. Each day has enough difficulty of its own. Just as disciples ask for bread “this day” (6:11), they meet each day’s trouble on that day, trusting the Father who provides for it.

Study Notes

The noun kakia usually refers to wickedness, but here it denotes trouble or hardship.
