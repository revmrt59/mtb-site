Chapter Study

Matthew 7

Verse-by-Verse
Matthew 7:1
Matthew 7:1
NKJV

Matthew 7:1 "Judge not, that you be not judged.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 7:1 “Do not judge others, and you will not be judged. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus commands His disciples, “Judge not, that you be not judged.” He warns against condemning another person while excusing our own faults, and He reminds the one who judges this way that he too will face judgment. He does not forbid every kind of evaluation, since He soon requires discernment about what is holy and about false prophets.

Verse Explanation

Jesus commands His disciples, “Judge not, that you be not judged.” He warns against condemning another person while excusing our own faults, and He reminds the one who judges this way that he too will face judgment. He does not forbid every kind of evaluation, since He soon requires discernment about what is holy and about false prophets.

Matthew 7 brings the Sermon on the Mount to its close. In chapter 6 Jesus taught His disciples to live before their Father in secret and to trust Him instead of worrying. Now He turns to how they treat other people, beginning with a sin that religious people fall into easily: sitting in judgment over those around them.

Judge not is a command addressed to Jesus’ disciples. The word “judge” can mean anything from evaluating something to condemning someone, so the context has to show which Jesus means. He is not forbidding moral discernment. In this same chapter He tells His disciples to use discernment with what is holy (v. 6) and to recognize false prophets by their fruit (vv. 15–20). What He forbids is the condemning spirit that looks down on others, pronounces verdicts that belong to God, and excuses in ourselves what it condemns in someone else. Verses 3–5 show that this spirit is usually blind to its own faults.

that you be not judged gives the reason. The person who takes the judge’s seat over others does not escape judgment himself. He will stand before God, and the next verse explains that the way he has judged others will matter when he does. Jesus is not denying that there is right and wrong. He is warning the one who judges that he too is accountable.

Study Notes

The Greek verb translated “judge” (krinō) covers a wide range of meaning, from evaluating to condemning. Paul uses it for the church’s proper evaluation of its own members (1 Corinthians 5:12) and for condemning others (Romans 2:1; 14:13). Luke’s parallel sets the saying beside “Condemn not, and you shall not be condemned” (Luke 6:37), which supports the reading that Jesus has condemnation in view.

In the words “that you be not judged,” Jesus does not say who will do the judging. The context of final accountability points to God as the Judge. Jesus uses the same kind of wording in the Beatitudes, where God is plainly the One who acts: “they shall be comforted” (5:4) and “they shall be filled” (5:6).

Matthew 7:2
Matthew 7:2
NKJV

Matthew 7:2 For with what judgment you judge, you will be judged; and with the measure you use, it will be measured back to you.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 7:2 For you will be treated as you treat others. The standard you use in judging is the standard by which you will be judged. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus gives the reason for His command: the standard a person uses in judging others is the standard that will be applied to him. Whoever measures others severely invites the same measure in return.

Verse Explanation

Jesus gives the reason for His command: the standard a person uses in judging others is the standard that will be applied to him. Whoever measures others severely invites the same measure in return.

For with what judgment you judge, you will be judged explains the command of verse 1. The judgment a person passes on others becomes the standard applied to him. Someone who condemns harshly and makes no allowance for weakness cannot expect to be treated more gently than he treats others. Paul makes the same point: “in whatever you judge another you condemn yourself” (Romans 2:1).

and with the measure you use, it will be measured back to you repeats the warning with a picture from the marketplace, where grain and other goods were measured out in containers. The measure a person uses on others is the measure that comes back to him. The verse echoes what Jesus has already taught: “Blessed are the merciful, For they shall obtain mercy” (5:7). In the Lord’s Prayer He also tied the Father’s forgiveness of us to our forgiveness of others (6:12, 14–15).

Study Notes

The Mishnah, a collection of Jewish teaching written down around AD 200, records a similar saying: the measure a man uses is the measure used for him (Sotah 1:7). The Mishnah is later than the Gospels, so the parallel shows a similar way of speaking, not where Jesus’ saying came from.

Luke’s parallel shows the generous side of the same principle. A generous measure returns “pressed down, shaken together, and running over” (Luke 6:38).

Matthew 7:3
Matthew 7:3
NKJV

Matthew 7:3 And why do you look at the speck in your brother's eye, but do not consider the plank in your own eye?(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 7:3 “And why worry about a speck in your friend’s eye when you have a log in your own? (NLT)

Main Idea

With a deliberately exaggerated picture, Jesus asks why someone looks at the speck in his brother’s eye but does not consider the plank in his own. The question exposes how readily we magnify another person’s small fault while ignoring our own far greater one.

Verse Explanation

With a deliberately exaggerated picture, Jesus asks why someone looks at the speck in his brother’s eye but does not consider the plank in his own. The question exposes how readily we magnify another person’s small fault while ignoring our own far greater one.

And why do you look at the speck in your brother’s eye opens a deliberately exaggerated picture. A “speck” is a tiny bit of straw, chaff, or wood, the kind of thing that blows into someone’s eye. The person Jesus describes notices it quickly and keeps his attention on it. The word “brother” shows that Jesus is speaking about relationships among God’s people, the very people we see most closely and are most tempted to examine.

but do not consider the plank in your own eye? completes the picture. A plank is a beam, the kind of heavy timber used in building. The scene is meant to be absurd: a man with a beam in his eye studying a speck in someone else’s. To “consider” is to notice and think carefully about something. The critic studies his brother’s small fault in detail and does not even notice his own far larger one. Jesus does not say the speck is imaginary. The brother has a real fault. The problem is the critic’s blindness about himself.

Study Notes

The Greek word behind “speck” (karphos) describes a small dry particle, such as a bit of straw or wood. The word behind “plank” (dokos) is the word for a large beam of timber, which shows how great the contrast is meant to be.

A later rabbinic text records a similar exchange. When one person tells another to remove a small splinter from his eye, the reply is to remove the beam from his own (Babylonian Talmud, Arakhin 16b, paraphrased). The Talmud was compiled centuries after Jesus, so this shows a similar image, not how widely the saying was known in His day.

Matthew 7:4 How can you think of saying to your friend, ‘Let me help you get rid of that speck in your eye,’ when you can’t see past the log in your own eye? (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus presses the absurdity further by picturing a man with a plank in his own eye offering to remove the speck from his brother’s eye. Help offered from such blindness is presumptuous, however kindly it is worded.

Verse Explanation

Jesus presses the absurdity further by picturing a man with a plank in his own eye offering to remove the speck from his brother’s eye. Help offered from such blindness is presumptuous, however kindly it is worded.

Or how can you say to your brother, ‘Let me remove the speck from your eye’ moves from noticing to acting. The critic’s words sound kind. He presents himself as someone who wants to help. Jesus’ question is not whether the speck should come out, but whether this person is in any condition to remove it.

and look, a plank is in your own eye? calls attention back to the scene. The man leaning in to do delicate work on his brother’s eye has a beam sticking out of his own. He cannot see well enough to help, and his efforts are more likely to injure than to heal. Correction offered while one’s own sin goes unrecognized is presumptuous, however gently it is worded.

None.

Matthew 7:5
Matthew 7:5
NKJV

Matthew 7:5 Hypocrite! First remove the plank from your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother's eye.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 7:5 Hypocrite! First get rid of the log in your own eye; then you will see well enough to deal with the speck in your friend’s eye. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus names this person a hypocrite and gives the right order: first remove the plank from your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother’s eye. Honest self-examination comes first, and it clears the way for genuine help to a brother.

Verse Explanation

Jesus names this person a hypocrite and gives the right order: first remove the plank from your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother’s eye. Honest self-examination comes first, and it clears the way for genuine help to a brother.

Hypocrite! is a sharp word, and Jesus uses it deliberately. He has already used it for those who give, pray, and fast to be seen by others (6:2, 5, 16). Here the hypocrite acts the part of the righteous helper. He shows great concern for his brother’s small fault while overlooking a far greater sin in himself.

First remove the plank from your own eye sets the order. Jesus does not tell His disciples to ignore sin in others. He tells them where to begin. Facing one’s own sin honestly, with confession and repentance, comes before addressing anyone else’s.

and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother’s eye shows that Jesus is not forbidding all correction. He wants the speck removed. The brother’s fault is real, and love will want to help him. But only a person who has humbly faced his own sin can see clearly enough to help well. These verses do not end with “leave your brother alone.” They end with a brother helped by someone who has first been humbled. Paul gives the same order when he tells spiritual believers to restore the fallen “in a spirit of gentleness, considering yourself lest you also be tempted” (Galatians 6:1).

Study Notes

The word “hypocrite” returns repeatedly in Jesus’ woes against the scribes and Pharisees in chapter 23, where the charge is an outward show of righteousness that hides an inward condition (23:27–28).

Jesus later gives practical steps for going to a brother who sins (18:15–17), which shows that the correction allowed in 7:5 is real correction, not only private self-examination.

Matthew 7:6
Matthew 7:6
NKJV

Matthew 7:6 "Do not give what is holy to the dogs; nor cast your pearls before swine, lest they trample them under their feet, and turn and tear you in pieces.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 7:6 “Don’t waste what is holy on people who are unholy. Don’t throw your pearls to pigs! They will trample the pearls, then turn and attack you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus warns against giving what is holy to the dogs or casting pearls before swine. What is holy and precious may be trampled underfoot, and the person who offers it may be attacked, so alongside humble correction He requires discernment about offering holy things to those who meet them with contempt.

Verse Explanation

Jesus warns against giving what is holy to the dogs or casting pearls before swine. What is holy and precious may be trampled underfoot, and the person who offers it may be attacked, so alongside humble correction He requires discernment about offering holy things to those who meet them with contempt.

Verse 6 balances verses 1–5. Disciples must not condemn others, but they must not be naive either. Refusing to judge harshly does not mean refusing to discern.

Do not give what is holy to the dogs uses a shocking picture. “What is holy” is something set apart to God. The Law guarded such things carefully. For example, holy food reserved for the priests was not to be eaten by an outsider (Leviticus 22:10). Against that background, throwing something set apart to God to dogs would have been unthinkable. Jesus uses the picture without saying exactly what the holy thing is. The dogs in view are not household pets but the scavengers that roamed the streets and refuse heaps of ancient towns.

nor cast your pearls before swine adds a second picture. Pearls were among the most valuable possessions in the ancient world. Jesus later compares the kingdom of heaven to a pearl worth everything a merchant owned (13:45–46). Pigs were unclean under the Law (Leviticus 11:7), and a pig sees no value in a pearl.

lest they trample them under their feet, and turn and tear you in pieces names two dangers. What is holy and precious may be treated with contempt, and the person who offers it may be attacked. Jesus does not say exactly what the holy things and pearls are. In light of verses 1–5, wise correction is a natural application. The kingdom message itself is another reasonable application, especially since Jesus later tells His disciples to shake the dust off their feet when a town refuses to receive them (10:14). Either way, the verse calls for discernment about offering holy things to people who meet them with contempt. It does not label any group of people as dogs or swine, and it does not treat honest questions as contempt. Jesus ate with tax collectors and sinners (9:10–13) and sends His disciples to all nations (28:19).

Study Notes

Some interpreters see a crossed pattern in the verse, with the swine trampling the pearls and the dogs turning to attack. Others think the swine do both. The text does not settle the question, and the warning is the same either way.

Proverbs gives similar counsel about speaking to those who mock: “Do not correct a scoffer, lest he hate you” (Proverbs 9:8).

Matthew 7:7–12 — Ask, Seek, Knock, and the Golden Rule

Matthew 7:7
Matthew 7:7
NKJV

Matthew 7:7 "Ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 7:7 “Keep on asking, and you will receive what you ask for. Keep on seeking, and you will find. Keep on knocking, and the door will be opened to you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus invites His disciples to ask, seek, and knock, and He attaches a promise to each: it will be given, they will find, and it will be opened to them. The threefold invitation urges earnest and persevering prayer to the Father.

Verse Explanation

Jesus invites His disciples to ask, seek, and knock, and He attaches a promise to each: it will be given, they will find, and it will be opened to them. The threefold invitation urges earnest and persevering prayer to the Father.

Jesus now turns to prayer. He does not state a link with verses 1–6, but the connection fits. Disciples who need clear sight to help a brother (v. 5) and wisdom to know when to hold back holy things (v. 6) will soon find they lack what they need. Jesus invites them to ask their Father.

Ask, and it will be given to you is the simplest form of prayer: a request made to someone able to give. The giver is not named here, but verse 11 names Him. It is the Father who gives.

seek, and you will find adds desire and effort. Jesus has already told His disciples what to seek first: “the kingdom of God and His righteousness” (6:33). The Old Testament promises that those who seek God with all their heart will find Him (Deuteronomy 4:29; Jeremiah 29:13).

knock, and it will be opened to you pictures someone standing at a closed door and asking to be let in. Each of the three commands comes with a promise, and together they describe earnest, active prayer. In Luke the same saying follows a parable about persistence in asking (Luke 11:5–10), so persevering prayer is a fitting application of these invitations.

Study Notes

In Greek all three commands are in the present tense, a form that can describe ongoing or repeated action. Many interpreters hear them as “keep on asking,” and the NLT renders them, “Keep on asking … Keep on seeking … Keep on knocking.” The form allows this reading but does not require it by itself.

In Luke this saying follows a parable about a man who goes to a friend at midnight to ask for bread. Though the friend will not get up because of friendship, Jesus says that “because of his persistence he will rise and give him as many as he needs” (Luke 11:8).

Main Idea

Jesus widens the promise to include everyone who prays this way: everyone who asks receives, he who seeks finds, and to him who knocks it will be opened. No disciple who comes to the Father asking, seeking, and knocking is left out.

Verse Explanation

Jesus widens the promise to include everyone who prays this way: everyone who asks receives, he who seeks finds, and to him who knocks it will be opened. No disciple who comes to the Father asking, seeking, and knocking is left out.

For everyone who asks receives gives the reason disciples can take up the invitations of verse 7 with confidence. Verse 7 gave commands; verse 8 gives assurance. “Everyone” is the important word. Answered prayer is not reserved for prophets, priests, or especially devout people. The promise belongs to all who come to the Father this way.

and he who seeks finds, and to him who knocks it will be opened repeats the other two actions as sure statements. The repetition is meant to reassure. The word “finds” will appear again in verse 14, where only a few find the way that leads to life.

Or if he asks for a fish, will he give him a serpent? adds a second example that goes further than the first. Fish was a common food in Galilee and was often eaten with bread (14:17). A serpent is not only useless as food but dangerous. The first question pictured a gift that would not help. This one pictures a gift that would harm. Again the expected answer is no, and both questions prepare for the comparison with the Father in the next verse.

Matthew 7:11
Matthew 7:11
NKJV

Matthew 7:11 If you then, being evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask Him!(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 7:11 So if you sinful people know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your heavenly Father give good gifts to those who ask him. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus reasons from the lesser to the greater: if His hearers, being evil, know how to give good gifts to their children, how much more will their Father in heaven give good things to those who ask Him. The disciple’s confidence in prayer rests on the Father’s goodness and on His wisdom in giving what is truly good.

Verse Explanation

Jesus reasons from the lesser to the greater: if His hearers, being evil, know how to give good gifts to their children, how much more will their Father in heaven give good things to those who ask Him. The disciple’s confidence in prayer rests on the Father’s goodness and on His wisdom in giving what is truly good.

If you then, being evil states plainly that His hearers are sinful. Jesus does not mean that every human act is wicked, since He goes on to say they give good gifts. He means that human nature is fallen, and even the best father is not good the way God is good.

know how to give good gifts to your children acknowledges that even sinful parents know how to give their children what they need. Love for one’s children survives in fallen people.

how much more will your Father who is in heaven is the heart of Jesus’ argument. If imperfect fathers give good gifts, the Father whose goodness has no flaw will give far more. Throughout the sermon Jesus has spoken of God as the disciples’ Father in heaven (5:16, 45; 6:1, 9). His disciples come to God as children come to a father.

give good things to those who ask Him! promises that the Father gives what is truly good. He is wiser than we are about what is good, so He may not give everything in the form we ask for it. But He does not answer His children with harm in place of good. “Those who ask Him” ties the promise back to verse 7.

Study Notes

Jesus reasons from the lesser to the greater. Even imperfect fathers give good gifts to their children, so disciples can be all the more confident in the goodness of their Father in heaven. He uses the same kind of reasoning for God’s care in 6:30 (“will He not much more clothe you”) and for the value of a person in 12:12.

Luke’s parallel has “give the Holy Spirit to those who ask Him” where Matthew has “good things” (Luke 11:13).

Matthew 7:12
NKJV

Matthew 7:12 Therefore, whatever you want men to do to you, do also to them, for this is the Law and the Prophets.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 7:12 “Do to others whatever you would like them to do to you. This is the essence of all that is taught in the law and the prophets. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus draws a conclusion with “Therefore”: whatever you want men to do to you, do also to them, for this is the Law and the Prophets. Following His teaching on the Father’s generosity, Jesus calls disciples to take the initiative in treating others as they would want to be treated, and He presents this as the sum of what the Law and the Prophets require in how people treat one another.

Verse Explanation

Jesus draws a conclusion with “Therefore”: whatever you want men to do to you, do also to them, for this is the Law and the Prophets. Following His teaching on the Father’s generosity, Jesus calls disciples to take the initiative in treating others as they would want to be treated, and He presents this as the sum of what the Law and the Prophets require in how people treat one another.

Therefore ties this command to what comes before. The most likely immediate link is the Father’s goodness in verses 7–11: those who receive good things from the Father are to do good to others. The verse also gathers up the sermon’s teaching on how disciples are to treat other people.

whatever you want men to do to you, do also to them is positive and active. Jesus does not simply say, “Do not harm others.” He tells His disciples to think about what they would want from others, such as patience, honesty, mercy, fairness, and help in a time of need, and then to take the first step in giving it. The command does not wait for others to treat us well first. It calls for love even when kindness is not returned.

for this is the Law and the Prophets explains why the command matters so much. “The Law and the Prophets” means the Old Testament Scriptures. At the start of His teaching on the Law, Jesus said He did not come to destroy the Law or the Prophets but “to fulfill” them (5:17). Here He sums up what they require in how people treat one another. Later He says that all the Law and the Prophets hang on loving God and loving one’s neighbor as oneself (22:37–40).

Study Notes

Similar sayings existed in Jewish tradition, usually in a negative form. Tobit 4:15, written before Jesus’ time, says not to do to anyone what you hate. The Babylonian Talmud (Shabbat 31a), compiled centuries later, attributes a comparable saying to Hillel, a teacher of the generation before Jesus. Matthew records the positive form.

The sermon refers to “the Law or the Prophets” in 5:17 and to “the Law and the Prophets” here in 7:12. These two references frame the main body of the sermon. What follows in 7:13–27 is the conclusion, a series of contrasts that call for a decision.

Matthew 7:13–14 — The Narrow and Wide Gates

Matthew 7:13
Matthew 7:13
NKJV

Matthew 7:13 "Enter by the narrow gate; for wide is the gate and broad is the way that leads to destruction, and there are many who go in by it.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 7:13 “You can enter God’s Kingdom only through the narrow gate. The highway to hell is broad, and its gate is wide for the many who choose that way. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus commands, “Enter by the narrow gate,” and warns that the gate is wide and the way broad that leads to destruction, with many going in by it. The easy and crowded way ends in ruin, so no one should take it simply because many others do.

Verse Explanation

Jesus commands, “Enter by the narrow gate,” and warns that the gate is wide and the way broad that leads to destruction, with many going in by it. The easy and crowded way ends in ruin, so no one should take it simply because many others do.

With verse 13 the sermon moves into its conclusion. From here to the end, Jesus sets pairs before His hearers: two gates and two ways (vv. 13–14), good and bad trees (vv. 15–20), those who only say “Lord, Lord” and those who do the Father’s will (vv. 21–23), and two builders (vv. 24–27). Each pair calls for a decision.

Enter by the narrow gate is a command. Jesus has described life in the kingdom of heaven, and now He calls His hearers to enter it. A gate is the way into a city or a walled place, and verse 14 shows that this gate leads to life. Each hearer is called to enter personally.

for wide is the gate and broad is the way that leads to destruction describes the other option. The gate is wide and the road broad, and verse 14 sets them against a narrow gate and a difficult way. Jesus does not describe the journey in detail. What matters is where the broad way leads. Its end is “destruction,” the final ruin that stands opposite to “life” in verse 14.

and there are many who go in by it warns that the broad way is crowded. The number of travelers can make it feel safe, but the crowd does not change where the road ends. No one should take it simply because many others do.

Study Notes

The Greek word for “destruction” (apōleia) is used elsewhere in the New Testament for the final ruin of those who oppose God (Philippians 1:28; 3:19; 2 Peter 3:7).

Matthew 7:14
Matthew 7:14
NKJV

Matthew 7:14 Because narrow is the gate and difficult is the way which leads to life, and there are few who find it.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 7:14 But the gateway to life is very narrow and the road is difficult, and only a few ever find it. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus explains why the narrow gate must be entered: it and the difficult way lead to life, and there are few who find it. The way of life under Jesus’ teaching is demanding, and the contrast between the few and the many presses each hearer to enter that gate.

Verse Explanation

Jesus explains why the narrow gate must be entered: it and the difficult way lead to life, and there are few who find it. The way of life under Jesus’ teaching is demanding, and the contrast between the few and the many presses each hearer to enter that gate.

Because narrow is the gate and difficult is the way which leads to life explains why the narrow gate must be entered. The way beyond it is “difficult,” a word that means pressed or confined. Those who walk this way meet opposition and the cost of following Jesus, as He has already said (5:10–12) and will say again: “If anyone desires to come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow Me” (16:24). The narrow way takes shape in living under Jesus’ authority even when obedience is hard. But it leads to “life,” which in Matthew is the life of God’s kingdom, eternal life (19:16–17, 29; 25:46).

and there are few who find it completes the contrast with the many of verse 13. “Find” recalls the promise of verse 8: “he who seeks finds.” Jesus is not giving a count of the saved or a forecast of numbers. He is warning each hearer not to assume that following the majority will bring him to life. When someone once asked Him, “Lord, are there few who are saved?” He answered, “Strive to enter through the narrow gate” (Luke 13:23–24). The question of how many becomes the question of whether you will enter.

Study Notes

The Greek verb behind “difficult” (thlibō) means to press, crowd, or confine, so the way is pictured as pressed in. The related Greek noun thlipsis is translated “tribulation” in Matthew 13:21 and 24:9, 21, 29.

The NKJV reads “Because narrow is the gate,” following Greek manuscripts that have the word for “because” here. Other Greek manuscripts have a word that can mean “how,” which turns the line into an exclamation. Young’s Literal Translation renders it “how strait [is] the gate.” The warning is the same either way.

Matthew 7:15–20 — Recognizing False Prophets by Their Fruit

Matthew 7:15
Matthew 7:15
NKJV

Matthew 7:15 "Beware of false prophets, who come to you in sheep's clothing, but inwardly they are ravenous wolves.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 7:15 “Beware of false prophets who come disguised as harmless sheep but are really vicious wolves. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus warns His disciples to beware of false prophets, who come to them in sheep’s clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves. They look as though they belong to the flock, yet like wolves among sheep they are a danger to it.

Verse Explanation

Jesus warns His disciples to beware of false prophets, who come to them in sheep’s clothing but inwardly are ravenous wolves. They look as though they belong to the flock, yet like wolves among sheep they are a danger to it.

Having called His hearers to the narrow way, Jesus warns them about people who would lead them away from it.

Beware of false prophets calls disciples to be on guard. A false prophet claims to speak for God when God has not sent him (Jeremiah 14:14; 23:16, 21), or leads people away from the LORD (Deuteronomy 13:1–5). Israel’s history was full of such voices, often promising peace without repentance: “Saying, ‘Peace, peace!’ When there is no peace” (Jeremiah 6:14). Jesus warns that false prophets will continue to arise (24:11, 24).

who come to you in sheep’s clothing describes how they appear. They look like sheep. They seem to belong to the flock, speak its language, and share its practices. They “come to you,” moving among God’s people. Their danger lies in how closely they resemble the real thing.

but inwardly they are ravenous wolves reveals what they are underneath. A wolf among sheep is a danger to the flock, and “ravenous” pictures one that snatches and devours. Whatever such prophets seem to offer, the flock is harmed by them. Paul used the same picture when he warned the Ephesian elders that “savage wolves will come in among you, not sparing the flock” (Acts 20:29).

This warning does not make all prophetic ministry suspect. Elsewhere believers are told, “Do not despise prophecies. Test all things; hold fast what is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:20–21). Jesus warns against false prophets because true prophecy is real and valuable.

Study Notes

Some interpreters see an allusion to the rough garment that marked a prophet (2 Kings 1:8; Matthew 3:4), which false prophets also wore to deceive (Zechariah 13:4). The contrast with wolves, however, suggests the simpler picture: they look like members of the flock.

The Greek word for “ravenous” (harpax) is related to the verb used in John 10:12, where the wolf “catches” the sheep. It describes greedy seizing.

Jesus declares that not everyone who says to Him, “Lord, Lord,” shall enter the kingdom of heaven, but he who does the will of His Father in heaven. Speaking as the Son who calls God “My Father,” Jesus makes obedience to the Father’s will, and not words of devotion alone, the mark of those who enter the kingdom.

Jesus moves from false prophets to a wider warning addressed to everyone who claims to follow Him. For the first time in the sermon He also speaks of His own role at the final judgment.

Not everyone who says to Me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ describes people who address Jesus as Lord. In this setting the title means far more than polite respect. These people speak to Jesus as the One with authority over their eternal destiny. The doubled “Lord, Lord” expresses earnestness and familiarity. They speak as people who know Him and confess Him openly.

shall enter the kingdom of heaven refers to entering God’s kingdom in its final, complete form (5:20; 18:3; 19:23–24). Jesus has just called His hearers to enter by the narrow gate (v. 13). Now He shows that some who appear to be on the way will not enter.

but he who does the will of My Father in heaven names the one who enters. Jesus has spent three chapters describing the Father’s will: a righteousness from the heart that goes beyond outward religion (5:20). Words of devotion without that obedience are empty. Jesus also calls God “My Father.” He has spoken of “your Father” and taught His disciples to pray “Our Father” (6:9). Here, for the first time in Matthew, He speaks of God as His own Father. The One who will stand as Judge is the Son.

This obedience does not earn the kingdom. The wider New Testament teaches that we are saved by grace through faith and created in Christ Jesus “for good works” (Ephesians 2:8–10), and the prophets promised that God would put His Spirit within His people and cause them to walk in His ways (Ezekiel 36:26–27). Jesus’ point here is that a life of obedience shows real allegiance to Him. A profession without it does not.

Study Notes

Luke records the same concern as a pointed question: “But why do you call Me ‘Lord, Lord,’ and not do the things which I say?” (Luke 6:46).
