Chapter Study

Matthew 8

Verse-by-Verse
Matthew 8:1
Matthew 8:1
NKJV

Matthew 8:1 When He had come down from the mountain, great multitudes followed Him.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:1 Large crowds followed Jesus as he came down the mountainside. (NLT)

Main Idea

When Jesus comes down from the mountain, great crowds follow Him. The One who taught with authority on the mountain now displays that authority in His deeds.

Verse Explanation

When Jesus comes down from the mountain, great crowds follow Him. The One who taught with authority on the mountain now displays that authority in His deeds.

“When He had come down from the mountain,”

Jesus had gone up on the mountain to teach His disciples (5:1), and the crowds listened in astonishment, “for He taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes” (7:28–29). Now He comes down. Matthew’s wording recalls Moses coming down from Mount Sinai with the word of God (Exodus 34:29). Moses, however, delivered a message he had received. Jesus spoke with His own authority: “But I say to you” (5:22, 28, 34, 39, 44).

Chapters 8 and 9 show that same authority in action. Matthew gathers ten miracles in these two chapters, revealing Jesus’ power over disease, demons, nature, sin, and death. The words of chapters 5–7 and the works of chapters 8–9 belong together, and Matthew frames both with nearly identical summaries of Jesus teaching, preaching, and healing (4:23; 9:35).

“great multitudes followed Him.”

The crowds that heard the Sermon on the Mount follow Jesus down the mountain. “Followed” becomes an important word in this chapter (8:10, 19, 22, 23). Some follow out of curiosity or need, one man offers to follow without counting the cost, and the disciples follow Jesus straight into a storm. Matthew lets the reader see that walking behind Jesus in a crowd is not yet the same as following Him as a disciple.

Study Notes

Matthew groups the miracles in three sets of three, with teaching on discipleship between the sets. The first set is the leper, the centurion’s servant, and Peter’s mother-in-law (8:1–15). The second is the storm, the two demon-possessed men, and the paralytic (8:23–9:8). The third is the ruler’s daughter together with the woman healed on the way, two blind men, and a mute man (9:18–34). The discipleship sections are 8:18–22 and 9:9–17. Counting the woman, there are ten miracles.

Matthew arranges this material by theme rather than strict sequence. Mark places the healing of Peter’s mother-in-law before the cleansing of the leper (Mark 1:29–45) and the calming of the storm much later (Mark 4:35–41). Both accounts are true to what happened. Matthew groups the events to display the breadth of Jesus’ authority.

Matthew 8:2
Matthew 8:2
NKJV

Matthew 8:2 And behold, a leper came and worshiped Him, saying, "Lord, if You are willing, You can make me clean."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:2 Suddenly, a man with leprosy approached him and knelt before him. “Lord,” the man said, “if you are willing, you can heal me and make me clean.” (NLT)

Main Idea

A leper comes, worships Jesus, and says, “Lord, if You are willing, You can make me clean.” He has no doubt about Jesus’ power; his only question is whether Jesus is willing.

Verse Explanation

A leper comes, worships Jesus, and says, “Lord, if You are willing, You can make me clean.” He has no doubt about Jesus’ power; his only question is whether Jesus is willing.

“And behold, a leper came”

“Behold” calls attention to something startling. A leper approached Jesus in the middle of a crowd. Under the law of Moses, a person with leprosy was unclean. He had to tear his clothes, cover his mouth, cry out “Unclean! Unclean!” and live alone outside the camp (Leviticus 13:45–46). The disease damaged the body, but the uncleanness cut the person off from worship, from family life, and from ordinary human contact. For this man even to come near was a bold act.

“and worshiped Him,”

The man knelt or bowed low before Jesus. The Greek word can describe the honor given to any superior, but Matthew uses it again and again for people who come before Jesus in reverence (2:11; 14:33; 28:9, 17). Whatever this man fully understood, he approached Jesus as far more than an ordinary teacher or healer.

“saying, ‘Lord, if You are willing, You can make me clean.”

The man had no doubt about Jesus’ power: “You can.” His question was whether Jesus would want to help someone like him: “if You are willing.” He did not ask only to be healed but to be made “clean.” He wanted his disease removed and his place restored among God’s people.

Study Notes

The Hebrew term tsara’at in Leviticus 13–14 covers several skin conditions and is also applied to affected clothing and houses. It should not be equated in every occurrence with modern Hansen’s disease.

The Greek proskyneō refers to bowing down or prostrating oneself. The word occurs frequently in Matthew for people approaching Jesus.

Matthew 8:3
Matthew 8:3
NKJV

Matthew 8:3 Then Jesus put out His hand and touched him, saying, "I am willing; be cleansed." Immediately his leprosy was cleansed.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:3 Jesus reached out and touched him. “I am willing,” he said. “Be healed!” And instantly the leprosy disappeared. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus reaches out and touches him, saying, “I am willing; be cleansed,” and immediately the leprosy is gone. Jesus touches the untouchable, and instead of becoming unclean Himself, He makes the man clean.

Verse Explanation

Jesus reaches out and touches him, saying, “I am willing; be cleansed,” and immediately the leprosy is gone. Jesus touches the untouchable, and instead of becoming unclean Himself, He makes the man clean.

“Then Jesus put out His hand and touched him,”

Jesus could have healed this man with a word, as He would heal the centurion’s servant (8:13). Instead, He reached out and touched him. Under the law, touching a leper made a person unclean (Leviticus 5:3), and people kept their distance. This man may not have felt a human hand in years. Jesus’ touch was deliberate. It met the man’s need for healing and his need to be received.

“saying, ‘I am willing; be cleansed.”

Jesus answers the man’s exact words. The man said, “If You are willing”; Jesus replies, “I am willing.” Then He speaks a command: “be cleansed.” He does not pray for the man or ask God to act. He speaks with His own authority, doing what Israel’s king had confessed only God could do (2 Kings 5:7).

“Immediately his leprosy was cleansed.”

The cleansing was immediate and complete. Normally, contact with a leper carried uncleanness to the one who touched him. Here the direction is reversed. Jesus was not made unclean by touching the leper; the leper was made clean by the touch of Jesus.

Study Notes

Jesus’ reply is two words in Greek: thelō, katharisthēti (I will; be cleansed). The first echoes the leper’s own verb (“if You are willing”). Mark records that Jesus was “moved with compassion” before He touched him (Mark 1:41).

Matthew 8:4
Matthew 8:4
NKJV

Matthew 8:4 And Jesus said to him, "See that you tell no one; but go your way, show yourself to the priest, and offer the gift that Moses commanded, as a testimony to them."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:4 Then Jesus said to him, “Don’t tell anyone about this. Instead, go to the priest and let him examine you. Take along the offering required in the law of Moses for those who have been healed of leprosy. This will be a public testimony that you have been cleansed.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus tells the man to tell no one, but to show himself to the priest and offer the gift Moses commanded as a testimony to them. The healed man is to follow the law’s procedure, and his restoration will bear witness to the priests about what God is doing through Jesus.

Verse Explanation

Jesus tells the man to tell no one, but to show himself to the priest and offer the gift Moses commanded as a testimony to them. The healed man is to follow the law’s procedure, and his restoration will bear witness to the priests about what God is doing through Jesus.

“And Jesus said to him, ‘See that you tell no one;”

Jesus often limited publicity about His miracles (9:30; 12:16). Many people expected a Messiah who would win political freedom, and miracle stories spread quickly could stir crowds with the wrong expectations. Mark reports that this man did tell everyone, with the result that Jesus could no longer openly enter a town (Mark 1:45). The instruction also had an immediate purpose. Before the man told anyone else, he needed to go to the priest.

“but go your way, show yourself to the priest,”

The law required a person healed of leprosy to be examined by a priest, who alone could declare him clean and restore him to the community (Leviticus 14:1–3). Jesus had cleansed the man’s body, and He also cared about his restoration to worship, family, and community life.

“and offer the gift that Moses commanded,”

Leviticus 14 describes the offerings required of a cleansed leper. By sending the man to obey these instructions, Jesus honored the law of Moses. He had just said that He came not to destroy the Law but to fulfill it (5:17).

“as a testimony to them.”

“Them” most naturally refers to the priests, and through them to the people. A cleansed leper standing before the priest would be clear evidence that God was at work in Jesus. If the religious leaders received that evidence, it would confirm faith. If they refused Him, the same evidence would stand as a witness against their unbelief.

Study Notes

The priest went outside the camp to examine the healed person. One bird was killed over running water, and a living bird was dipped in its blood and released into the open field (Leviticus 14:3–7). After washing, shaving, and waiting seven days, the person brought guilt, sin, burnt, and grain offerings on the eighth day, and the priest applied blood and oil to the right ear, right thumb, and right big toe (Leviticus 14:8–20). The law made provision for those who could not afford the full offering (Leviticus 14:21–32).

The expression “a testimony to them” also occurs in Matthew 10:18; compare 24:14. The phrase can refer to evidence or witness presented to others.

Matthew 8:5–13 — The Centurion’s Faith and the Healing of His Servant

Matthew 8:5
Matthew 8:5
NKJV

Matthew 8:5 Now when Jesus had entered Capernaum, a centurion came to Him, pleading with Him,(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:5 When Jesus returned to Capernaum, a Roman officer came and pleaded with him, (NLT)

Main Idea

When Jesus enters Capernaum, a centurion comes to Him pleading for help. A Gentile officer of the occupying army comes to Israel’s King as a humble petitioner.

Verse Explanation

When Jesus enters Capernaum, a centurion comes to Him pleading for help. A Gentile officer of the occupying army comes to Israel’s King as a humble petitioner.

“Now when Jesus had entered Capernaum,”

Capernaum, on the northern shore of the Sea of Galilee, had become Jesus’ home base for His ministry (4:13). It was a fishing town on a trade route, with a customs office (9:9) and soldiers stationed to keep order.

“a centurion came to Him, pleading with Him,”

A centurion was a military officer in charge of about a hundred soldiers. Galilee was ruled by Herod Antipas, so this officer may have served in Herod’s forces, which were organized on the Roman pattern. Either way, he was a Gentile and a representative of foreign power. Luke adds that he loved the Jewish nation and had built their synagogue (Luke 7:5).

This man held real authority, yet he came to Jesus “pleading.” The verb describes earnest, urgent appeal. A man used to giving orders now stood before Jesus as a petitioner.

Study Notes

Luke reports that the centurion sent Jewish elders and then friends to speak for him (Luke 7:3–6), while Matthew presents the centurion speaking directly. In the ancient world, a request made through representatives was counted as the act of the one who sent them. Matthew shortens the account and focuses on the centurion’s own words and faith. A similar compression appears when Matthew says the mother of James and John made a request that Mark attributes to the brothers themselves (20:20; Mark 10:35).

Matthew 8:6
Matthew 8:6
NKJV

Matthew 8:6 saying, "Lord, my servant is lying at home paralyzed, dreadfully tormented."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:6 “Lord, my young servant lies in bed, paralyzed and in terrible pain.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The centurion says, “Lord, my servant is lying at home paralyzed, dreadfully tormented.” He brings Jesus the suffering of someone under his care.

Verse Explanation

The centurion says, “Lord, my servant is lying at home paralyzed, dreadfully tormented.” He brings Jesus the suffering of someone under his care.

“saying, ‘Lord, my servant is lying at home paralyzed,”

“Lord” can be a respectful “sir,” but the centurion’s words that follow show that he sees far more in Jesus than an ordinary man. The word for “servant” can mean either a servant or a child. Luke uses the ordinary word for a slave and adds that he was “dear to him” (Luke 7:2). The servant was “lying at home,” unable to move, and could not be brought to Jesus.

“dreadfully tormented.”

The servant’s condition was severe. “Tormented” describes intense suffering, and “dreadfully” makes it stronger. Luke reports that he was “ready to die” (Luke 7:2).

The centurion describes the need without making a specific request. He lays the situation before Jesus and trusts Him to respond.

Study Notes

Matthew’s word (pais) can mean “boy,” “child,” or “servant.” Luke’s use of doulos (“slave”) confirms that the sick man was a servant in the household (Luke 7:2, 10).

The verb basanizō originally described testing metals with a touchstone and came to mean torture or severe distress. The demons use the same word in 8:29: “Have You come here to torment us?” The servant suffered torment from his affliction; the demons fear torment from Jesus.

Matthew 8:7
Matthew 8:7
NKJV

Matthew 8:7 And Jesus said to him, "I will come and heal him."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:7 Jesus said, “I will come and heal him.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus answers, “I will come and heal him.” He responds readily to the request of this Gentile.

Verse Explanation

Jesus answers, “I will come and heal him.” He responds readily to the request of this Gentile.

“And Jesus said to him, ‘I will come and heal him.”

Jesus responds without hesitation. He offers to go in person. Many devout Jews avoided entering a Gentile home because they considered it unclean (Acts 10:28; John 18:28). Jesus does not raise that concern. Just as He touched the leper, He is ready to go under a Gentile’s roof.

The “I” is emphatic in the Greek, as if Jesus said, I Myself will come. He promises both His presence and His healing.

Study Notes

Some interpreters read Jesus’ words as a question: Shall I come and heal him? On that reading, Jesus would be testing the centurion’s faith in a way similar to His answer to the Canaanite woman (15:24–26). The Greek allows either reading. The NKJV and most translations render it as a statement, which fits Jesus’ immediate willingness in 8:3.

Matthew 8:8
Matthew 8:8
NKJV

Matthew 8:8 The centurion answered and said, "Lord, I am not worthy that You should come under my roof. But only speak a word, and my servant will be healed.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:8 But the officer said, “Lord, I am not worthy to have you come into my home. Just say the word from where you are, and my servant will be healed. (NLT)

Main Idea

The centurion replies that he is not worthy for Jesus to come under his roof, and asks Him only to speak a word so that his servant will be healed. He combines humility about himself with complete confidence in the power of Jesus’ word.

Verse Explanation

The centurion replies that he is not worthy for Jesus to come under his roof, and asks Him only to speak a word so that his servant will be healed. He combines humility about himself with complete confidence in the power of Jesus’ word.

“The centurion answered and said, ‘Lord, I am not worthy that You should come under my roof.”

The centurion may have been aware of Jewish concerns about entering a Gentile home, and he may have wanted to spare Jesus any difficulty. But his words go deeper than custom. He recognized the greatness of the One standing before him and his own unworthiness to receive Him. Luke records that the Jewish elders said of him, “The one for whom He should do this was deserving” (Luke 7:4). The centurion did not see himself that way.

“But only speak a word, and my servant will be healed.”

The centurion believed that Jesus’ presence was not required for His power to work. A word from Jesus would be enough, whatever the distance. Scripture says of God, “He sent His word and healed them” (Psalm 107:20). The centurion attributes that kind of authority to the word of Jesus.

Study Notes

The adjective hikanos means sufficient or qualified. John uses the same adjective when saying he is not worthy to carry Jesus’ sandals (3:11).

Literally, only say with a word. The centurion’s confidence anticipates 8:16, where Jesus casts out spirits “with a word.”

Main Idea

The centurion explains that he too is a man under authority, with soldiers who go and come at his command. He recognizes that Jesus holds authority over sickness the way an officer commands his soldiers, so a word from Him is enough.

Verse Explanation

The centurion explains that he too is a man under authority, with soldiers who go and come at his command. He recognizes that Jesus holds authority over sickness the way an officer commands his soldiers, so a word from Him is enough.

“For I also am a man under authority,”

The word “also” is important. The centurion compares himself with Jesus. His own authority did not come from himself. He could command soldiers because he stood under the authority of his superiors and, ultimately, the emperor. He recognized that Jesus likewise acted under the authority of God, and that God’s authority stood behind Jesus’ commands.

“having soldiers under me. And I say to this one, ‘Go,’ and he goes; and to another, ‘Come,’ and he comes; and to my servant, ‘Do this,’ and he does it.”

In the army, orders are obeyed without the officer needing to be present at the place where they are carried out. The centurion reasoned that sickness would obey Jesus the same way his soldiers obeyed him. Jesus did not need to come to the house. He needed only to give the command.

Study Notes

None.

Matthew 8:10
Matthew 8:10
NKJV

Matthew 8:10 When Jesus heard it, He marveled, and said to those who followed, "Assuredly, I say to you, I have not found such great faith, not even in Israel!(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:10 When Jesus heard this, he was amazed. Turning to those who were following him, he said, “I tell you the truth, I haven’t seen faith like this in all Israel! (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus marvels and tells His followers that He has not found such great faith, “not even in Israel!” This outsider’s trust in Jesus’ authority surpasses what Jesus has found among His own people.

Verse Explanation

Jesus marvels and tells His followers that He has not found such great faith, “not even in Israel!” This outsider’s trust in Jesus’ authority surpasses what Jesus has found among His own people.

“When Jesus heard it, He marveled,”

The Gospels record only two occasions when Jesus marveled. Here He marvels at a Gentile’s faith. In Nazareth He marvels at His own townspeople’s unbelief (Mark 6:6). Jesus was genuinely moved by what He heard from this man.

“and said to those who followed,”

Jesus turns to the crowd that had followed Him from the mountain (8:1). The centurion’s faith becomes a lesson for those who were following Jesus.

“Assuredly, I say to you, I have not found such great faith, not even in Israel!”

“Assuredly” introduces a solemn statement. Israel had the Scriptures, the promises, the temple, and the prophets. Yet the greatest faith Jesus had yet encountered came from a Gentile soldier. The comparison does not deny that there was faith in Israel. Jesus’ own disciples were Jewish. It shows that faith, not privilege, is what receives from Jesus. The centurion’s faith was great because he trusted the authority of Jesus’ word without needing anything more.

Study Notes

The Greek is amēn, the Hebrew word for “truly” or so be it. People normally said “amen” to affirm what someone else had said. Jesus uniquely used it at the beginning of His own statements to underline their certainty and authority. Matthew records this usage more than thirty times.

Matthew contrasts the “great faith” of Gentiles (here and the Canaanite woman in 15:28) with the “little faith” of the disciples (8:26; 14:31; 16:8).

Matthew 8:11
Matthew 8:11
NKJV

Matthew 8:11 And I say to you that many will come from east and west, and sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:11 And I tell you this, that many Gentiles will come from all over the world — from east and west — and sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob at the feast in the Kingdom of Heaven. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus declares that many will come from east and west to sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven. The centurion’s faith anticipates people from all nations sharing in the feast of God’s promised kingdom.

Verse Explanation

Jesus declares that many will come from east and west to sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob in the kingdom of heaven. The centurion’s faith anticipates people from all nations sharing in the feast of God’s promised kingdom.

“And I say to you that many will come from east and west,”

The prophets spoke of people being gathered to God “from the east” and “from the west” (Isaiah 43:5–6; 49:12; Psalm 107:3; Malachi 1:11). In several of these passages, scattered Israel is gathered home. In this setting, prompted by a Gentile’s faith, Jesus includes the nations among the “many.” God promised Abraham that all the families of the earth would be blessed through him (Genesis 12:3), and Matthew opens by calling Jesus “the Son of Abraham” (1:1).

“and sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob”

“Sit down” means to recline at a meal. Isaiah foresaw a great feast that the LORD would prepare “for all people” on His mountain (Isaiah 25:6–8). Gentiles who come by faith will share table fellowship with Israel’s patriarchs, the fathers of the promise.

“in the kingdom of heaven.”

The feast belongs to the kingdom in its full and final form. The kingdom Jesus announced (4:17) is already breaking in through His ministry, and it will be completed when He returns.

Study Notes

Guests at formal meals commonly reclined on couches around a low table, supported on the left elbow.

Some interpreters understand the “many” as Jews scattered among the nations. Others understand them as Gentiles, pointing to the centurion’s identity and the contrast with the “sons of the kingdom” in the next verse.

Matthew 8:12
Matthew 8:12
NKJV

Matthew 8:12 But the sons of the kingdom will be cast out into outer darkness. There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth."(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:12 But many Israelites — those for whom the Kingdom was prepared — will be thrown into outer darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.” (NLT)

Main Idea

But “the sons of the kingdom” will be cast into outer darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth. Those who presume on their heritage without trusting the King will be shut out, so inherited privilege cannot replace faith.

Verse Explanation

But “the sons of the kingdom” will be cast into outer darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth. Those who presume on their heritage without trusting the King will be shut out, so inherited privilege cannot replace faith.

“But the sons of the kingdom”

“Sons of” is a Hebrew way of describing those who belong to something or seem to be its natural heirs. Here it refers to those within Israel who assumed the kingdom was theirs by birth and religious privilege. John the Baptist had warned against this kind of confidence: “Do not think to say to yourselves, ‘We have Abraham as our father’” (3:9). Jesus is not condemning Israel as a people. Jesus Himself, His apostles, and the first believers were Jewish. The warning is directed at those who rely on heritage rather than faith.

“will be cast out into outer darkness.”

The feast of 8:11 is pictured as a brightly lit banquet hall. Outside is darkness. To be “cast out” is to be shut out of the kingdom’s celebration and fellowship. Jesus uses this same picture for final judgment in His later parables (22:13; 25:30).

“There will be weeping and gnashing of teeth.”

Weeping expresses grief and regret. Gnashing of teeth expresses anguish and bitter frustration. Here the warning falls on those who relied on inherited privilege. They assumed their place at the feast was secure, yet they will find themselves outside, grieving what they have lost while others from far away sit down with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Jesus speaks plainly and soberly so that no one will trust in heritage instead of faith in Him.

Study Notes

In 13:38 the same phrase describes the good seed, the true people of the kingdom. The meaning depends on context. Here it describes those who appeared to be heirs; there it describes those who truly are.

Matthew 8:13
Matthew 8:13
NKJV

Matthew 8:13 Then Jesus said to the centurion, "Go your way; and as you have believed, so let it be done for you." And his servant was healed that same hour.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:13 Then Jesus said to the Roman officer, “Go back home. Because you believed, it has happened.” And the young servant was healed that same hour. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus tells the centurion, “Go your way; and as you have believed, so let it be done for you,” and the servant is healed that very hour. Jesus’ word heals from a distance, just as the centurion trusted it would.

Verse Explanation

Jesus tells the centurion, “Go your way; and as you have believed, so let it be done for you,” and the servant is healed that very hour. Jesus’ word heals from a distance, just as the centurion trusted it would.

“Then Jesus said to the centurion, ‘Go your way;”

Jesus grants the request by sending the centurion home. He does not go to the house. The centurion asked for a word, and he receives one.

“and as you have believed, so let it be done for you.”

The centurion’s faith did not produce the healing. Jesus’ authority did. But faith received what Jesus gave. Jesus links the outcome to the centurion’s trust, as He will again with two blind men (9:29) and the Canaanite woman (15:28).

“And his servant was healed that same hour.”

The healing took place at the moment Jesus spoke, though Jesus was not present in the house. The timing confirms that the word of Jesus accomplished it. The centurion’s confidence in 8:8 was proven true.

Study Notes

Matthew uses this kind of time note to show that healing came at Jesus’ word, not by gradual natural recovery (9:22; 15:28; 17:18). John records a similar healing at a distance for a royal official in Capernaum, where the father later confirmed that his son recovered at the very hour Jesus spoke (John 4:46–53). The two accounts describe different events.

Matthew 8:14–17 — Healings at Peter’s House

Matthew 8:14
Matthew 8:14
NKJV

Matthew 8:14 Now when Jesus had come into Peter's house, He saw his wife's mother lying sick with a fever.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:14 When Jesus arrived at Peter’s house, Peter’s mother-in-law was sick in bed with a high fever. (NLT)

Main Idea

Entering Peter’s house, Jesus sees Peter’s mother-in-law lying sick with a fever. The King’s compassion reaches into the ordinary home of one of His disciples.

Verse Explanation

Entering Peter’s house, Jesus sees Peter’s mother-in-law lying sick with a fever. The King’s compassion reaches into the ordinary home of one of His disciples.

“Now when Jesus had come into Peter’s house,”

Peter’s house was in Capernaum (Mark 1:21, 29). Peter, also called Simon, was married, and his wife later traveled with him in ministry (1 Corinthians 9:5). The house appears to have served as a home base for Jesus while He ministered in Capernaum.

“He saw his wife’s mother lying sick with a fever.”

Matthew emphasizes that Jesus saw her. Mark and Luke mention that others told Jesus about her (Mark 1:30; Luke 4:38), but Matthew keeps the focus on Jesus’ own attention and initiative. Fevers in the ancient world could be dangerous and even fatal. Luke, the physician, describes it as a “high fever” (Luke 4:38). She was “lying” in bed, unable to carry out her normal responsibilities in the household.

Study Notes

Mark and Luke place this healing on a Sabbath, immediately after Jesus taught and cast out a demon in the Capernaum synagogue (Mark 1:21–31; Luke 4:31–39). Matthew omits the synagogue scene and places this healing as the third of his first set of three miracles.

Excavations in Capernaum have uncovered a first-century house beneath the remains of an octagonal fifth-century church. Early Christians appear to have venerated the house as Peter’s, and it was later converted into a place of worship. The identification cannot be proven, but it is supported by early tradition and the archaeological evidence.

Matthew 8:15
Matthew 8:15
NKJV

Matthew 8:15 So He touched her hand, and the fever left her. And she arose and served them.(NKJV)

NLT

Matthew 8:15 But when Jesus touched her hand, the fever left her. Then she got up and prepared a meal for him. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus touches the hand of Peter’s mother-in-law, the fever leaves, and she rises and serves them. Her healing is complete and immediate, and she responds by serving Jesus.

Verse Explanation

Jesus touches the hand of Peter’s mother-in-law, the fever leaves, and she rises and serves them. Her healing is complete and immediate, and she responds by serving Jesus.

“So He touched her hand,”

Once again Jesus heals through touch, as He did with the leper (8:3). Taking the hand of a sick woman in her own house was a personal and tender act. Jesus does not stand at a distance.

“and the fever left her.”

The fever departed immediately. Luke says Jesus “rebuked the fever” (Luke 4:39), using the same word Jesus uses when He rebukes the wind and sea (8:26). Sickness responds to Jesus’ authority.

“And she arose and served them.”

She did not need a period of recovery. A fever normally leaves a person weak, but she rose immediately and began to serve. Her strength was fully restored. Her serving also expressed gratitude. Having been touched by Jesus, she turned to care for Him and those with Him.

Study Notes

He calls Jesus “Lord,” but his request places something else “first.” Burying one’s father was among the most sacred duties in Jewish life, connected to the command to honor father and mother (Exodus 20:12). It is unlikely that the man’s father had just died. Burial normally took place on the day of death, and a grieving son would have been at home with the family, not standing among the crowd with Jesus. The man was most likely asking to remain at home until his father died and was buried, which might be months or years away. Either way, he wanted to follow Jesus later, after fulfilling his family obligations.

Study Notes

Jewish burial usually took place within a day of death. In some cases, the bones were gathered about a year later and placed in a stone box called an ossuary, a practice known as secondary burial. Some interpreters suggest the man was asking to wait until this second burial. Both possibilities involve an indefinite delay.

The Mishnah exempts a man whose dead relative lies before him from reciting the daily Shema and other prayers until the burial is complete (Berakhot 3:1). This shows how seriously burial obligations were taken.

The herd of pigs in 8:30 fits this predominantly Gentile setting, though it does not tell us the identity of everyone who lived there. Matthew’s focus, like that of the parallel accounts, is on the men Jesus delivered (Mark 5:1–2; Luke 8:26–27).

“there met Him two demon-possessed men,”

“coming out of the tombs,”

Tombs were often caves or chambers cut into hillsides. To Jews, they were places of ritual uncleanness (Numbers 19:16). These men lived among the dead, separated from normal life. Isaiah describes rebellious people who “sit among the graves” and “eat swine’s flesh” (Isaiah 65:4), and this scene shows that kind of defilement.

“exceedingly fierce, so that no one could pass that way.”

The men were violent and dangerous. Mark adds that no one could bind the man even with chains, and that he cried out day and night and cut himself with stones (Mark 5:3–5). The demonic power controlling them had made a whole road impassable. People avoided the area entirely.

Study Notes

The manuscripts vary in all three Gospels. The NKJV follows the majority of manuscripts in reading “Gergesenes” here, while many older manuscripts read “Gadarenes” in Matthew and “Gerasenes” in Mark and Luke. Gadara was a major city about six miles southeast of the lake, and its territory may have extended to the shore. Gerasa was a city more than thirty miles away. Gergesa is often identified with the site of Kursi on the eastern shore, where a steep slope comes near the water. The differences most likely reflect different ways of naming the same general area, by a nearby village, a regional city, or the wider district.

Matthew records two men, while Mark 5:2 and Luke 8:27 concentrate on one. A similar difference occurs in the accounts of the blind men near Jericho (Matthew 20:30; Mark 10:46; Luke 18:35).

The demons beg Jesus that if He casts them out, He will let them go into the herd of swine. Even the demons must ask His permission, showing they are entirely under His authority.

“So the demons begged Him, saying,”

The demons begged. The same word described the centurion pleading with Jesus (8:5). These powers that had terrorized a whole region now had to plead with Jesus.

“If You cast us out,”

The demons did not question whether Jesus could expel them. They assumed He would. Their only question was where they would go.

“permit us to go away into the herd of swine.”

The demons asked permission. They could not act without it. Spirits seek a place to dwell (12:43–45), and these demons wanted a body to inhabit rather than be sent away entirely. Luke records that they begged Jesus not to command them to go into the abyss (Luke 8:31).

Verse Explanation

Jesus says, “Go,” and the demons go into the swine, and the whole herd rushes down the steep slope into the sea and dies. A single word from Jesus frees the men, and the herd’s destruction gives visible proof of the spirits’ departure and their destructive nature.

“And He said to them, ‘Go.”

One word was enough. Jesus did not argue or struggle. Earlier, He cast out spirits “with a word” (8:16); here the word is a single command.

“So when they had come out, they went into the herd of swine.”

The men were set free. The demons left them and entered the pigs, as they had asked.

“And suddenly the whole herd of swine ran violently down the steep place into the sea, and perished in the water.”

The result revealed the nature of the demons. They destroyed what they inhabited. What they did to the pigs in a moment, they had been doing to the men for years. The stampede also gave visible proof that the men had truly been delivered and showed the strength of the forces that had controlled them.

Scripture does not explain fully why Jesus permitted this. What it does make clear is that two human lives were worth more to Jesus than a herd of animals (12:12), and that the demons’ destructive nature was openly exposed.

Study Notes

The command hupagete comes from the same verb used in Jesus’ instructions to the leper in 8:4 and the centurion in 8:13.

Verse Explanation

Those tending the swine run to the city and report everything, including what happened to the demon-possessed men. News of the deliverance spreads through the region.

“Then those who kept them fled;”

The herdsmen had watched their entire herd rush into the sea. Frightened and responsible for the loss, they ran.

“and they went away into the city and told everything,”

They reported what had happened in the town. Their first concern was the herd.

“including what had happened to the demon-possessed men.”

Matthew’s wording places the men last, as if they were an addition to the main report. For the herdsmen, the main story was the loss of the pigs. The deliverance of two human beings came after.

Verse Explanation

After hearing the herdsmen’s report, the whole city comes out to meet Jesus, and when they see Him, they beg Him to leave their region. The very One who has just set the two men free is asked to go away.

“And behold, the whole city came out to meet Jesus.”

“Behold” marks a surprising turn. The entire town came out to Jesus. The word for “meet” often describes a group going out formally to receive an important visitor. The reader might expect a welcome.

“And when they saw Him, they begged Him to depart from their region.”

Instead, they begged Jesus to leave. Luke explains that they were seized with great fear (Luke 8:37). They had seen power they could not control, and they had suffered a costly loss. Rather than receive the One who had set two of their own free, they wanted Him gone.

The chapter that opened with a leper asking to be made clean ends with a city begging Jesus to depart. Jesus respected their request. In the next verse, He gets into a boat and leaves (9:1).

Study Notes

The noun hupantēsis can refer to going out to meet someone, including a visiting official. A related word occurs in Matthew 25:1.

The word parakaleō (“beg,” “plead”) links three requests in this chapter: the centurion asks Jesus to heal (8:5), the demons ask Jesus to let them enter the swine (8:31), and the town asks Jesus to leave (8:34). Jesus grants all three.
