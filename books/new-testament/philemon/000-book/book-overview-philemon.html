Book Overview

At a Glance

Philemon
Author

The letter comes from “Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy our brother” (v. 1). Timothy is named in the greeting, but the body of the letter is in Paul’s own voice, and Paul writes verse 19 with his own hand: “I, Paul, am writing with my own hand. I will repay.”

Audience

Paul writes to “Philemon our beloved friend and fellow laborer,” to “the beloved Apphia,” to “Archippus our fellow soldier,” and to “the church in your house” (vv. 1–2). The house is most likely Philemon’s. Apphia may have been Philemon’s wife and Archippus a member of his household, but the letter does not say. Most of the “you” forms in the letter are singular and addressed to Philemon, yet the whole church hears the appeal. Colossians mentions both Onesimus and Archippus (Colossians 4:9, 17), so Philemon most likely lived in Colossae.

Date

Philemon gives no date or place. Paul writes as a prisoner (vv. 1, 9–10, 13, 23), and the same companions appear in Colossians (Colossians 4:7–17), so the two letters were likely written and sent together. If Paul wrote from Rome, the date is about AD 60–62. Some propose an earlier imprisonment in Ephesus in the mid-50s, or Caesarea. The message of the letter does not depend on the place or date.

Historical Setting

Slavery was woven into the economic and household life of the Roman world. Enslaved people could be household workers, laborers, or skilled managers, and their conditions varied widely, but they were legally under the power of their owners and could be bought, sold, and punished. Slavery in this world was not usually based on race, but it was coercive and deeply unequal. Paul writes into this setting, and the NKJV rightly calls Onesimus a “slave” (v. 16).

Purpose

Onesimus, a slave belonging to Philemon, had come to Paul during his imprisonment and had become a believer through him: “my son Onesimus, whom I have begotten while in my chains” (v. 10). Now Paul is sending him back. The letter does not explain how Onesimus came to Paul. The traditional view is that he ran away; others suggest he sought Paul out as a friend of his master to plead for him, or that Philemon had sent him. Paul’s need to ask Philemon’s consent (v. 14) and his offer to cover any wrong or debt (v. 18) fit best with Onesimus having left without his master’s permission, but the letter says only that he “departed for a while” (v. 15). Paul writes so that Philemon will receive him as a brother.

Central Message

Paul appeals to Philemon, for love’s sake and on the ground of their shared life in Christ, to receive Onesimus no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother, as he would receive Paul himself, while Paul takes on whatever wrong or debt may stand against him. The fellowship Philemon already shares in Christ is to become visible in welcome, costly responsibility, and a freely given response.

Key Themes

  • Love as the ground of appeal. Philemon’s love for the saints becomes the basis for Paul’s request (vv. 5, 7, 9).

  • Fellowship and partnership. Shared life in Christ carries obligations toward one another (vv. 6, 17).

  • A new relationship in Christ. Onesimus returns as “a beloved brother” (v. 16).

  • Transformation. The one once unprofitable is now profitable (v. 11).

  • Identification and substitution. Paul stands in Onesimus’s place: “receive him as you would me” and “put that on my account” (vv. 17–18).

  • Refreshing hearts. Philemon has refreshed the saints and can now refresh Paul by receiving Onesimus (vv. 7, 20).

  • Willing obedience. Paul seeks a good deed that is “voluntary,” not by compulsion (vv. 14, 21).

Book Flow

Philemon 1:1–7: Greeting and Thanksgiving for Philemon’s Love

Paul, “a prisoner of Christ Jesus,” writes with Timothy to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, and the church in Philemon’s house. He thanks God for Philemon’s “love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints” (v. 5) and prays that the sharing of his faith may become effective. Philemon’s known ministry, “the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you” (v. 7), prepares for the appeal that follows.

Philemon 1:8–16: The Appeal for Onesimus, a Beloved Brother

Though Paul might be “very bold in Christ to command,” he appeals “for love’s sake” (vv. 8–9). Only then does he name Onesimus, “whom I have begotten while in my chains,” once unprofitable and now profitable (vv. 10–11). Paul sends him back as “my own heart” and will do nothing without Philemon’s consent (vv. 12–14). Paul then reflects that “perhaps” Onesimus was parted from Philemon for a while so that Philemon might receive him forever (v. 15). Verse 16, the heart of the letter, names the new relationship: “no longer as a slave but more than a slave—a beloved brother.”

Philemon 1:17–22: Receive Him as You Would Me

Paul states the request plainly: “If then you count me as a partner, receive him as you would me” (v. 17). Whatever wrong or debt there may be, “put that on my account,” and Paul signs his promise to repay with his own hand (vv. 18–19). He asks Philemon to “refresh my heart in the Lord” (v. 20), writes with confidence that Philemon “will do even more than I say” (v. 21), and asks him to prepare a guest room in hope of release.

Philemon 1:23–25: Greetings and Grace

Epaphras, Paul’s “fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus,” sends greetings, as do Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke, Paul’s fellow laborers. The letter ends with a blessing on the whole church: “The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit” (v. 25).

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Philemon is a short personal letter with a single request. Paul greets Philemon and the church in his house, thanks God for Philemon’s love and faith, and recalls how he has refreshed the hearts of the saints. He then appeals, for love’s sake rather than by command, on behalf of Onesimus, now his son in the faith. He asks Philemon to receive Onesimus “no longer as a slave but more than a slave—a beloved brother” (v. 16), and to receive him as he would receive Paul. Paul offers to take on any wrong or debt himself, expresses confidence that Philemon will do even more than he asks, and closes with greetings and grace.

Why This Book Matters

Philemon shows the gospel at work in a single, difficult relationship. A master and his slave, now brothers in Christ, must face each other again. Paul does not treat their faith as a private matter that leaves the relationship unchanged. Onesimus is to be received “no longer as a slave but more than a slave” (v. 16), “both in the flesh and in the Lord.” Shared life in Christ reaches into the household, the church, and the ordinary structures of life.

The letter also shows how spiritual authority can be used. Paul could command, but he appeals “for love’s sake” (vv. 8–9) and wants Philemon’s response to be “voluntary” (v. 14). He applies real moral weight, yet seeks a willing heart. Philemon is a model of reconciliation pursued through love, personal cost, and trust in the work of God in another believer.

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

  • Slave. Onesimus was legally his master’s property, with little power over his own life. Paul’s request that he be received “more than a slave” (v. 16) does not command his release, but it treats him as a brother whom Philemon must receive as he would receive Paul.

  • The church in your house (v. 2). Early believers gathered in homes; this congregation met in one, most likely Philemon’s.

  • Onesimus. The name means “useful” or “profitable.” Paul plays on it when he says Onesimus “once was unprofitable to you, but now is profitable to you and to me” (v. 11).

  • Begotten in my chains (v. 10). Onesimus came to faith through Paul while Paul was imprisoned, making him Paul’s spiritual son.

  • Heart (vv. 7, 12, 20). The word Paul uses refers to the inner person and deep affection. Calling Onesimus “my own heart” (v. 12) shows how fully Paul identifies with him.

  • Partner (v. 17). One who shares a common life. The word is related to “sharing” in verse 6. If Philemon shares life in Christ with Paul, he must receive the one Paul now calls his own heart.

  • Put that on my account (v. 18). Paul offers to take on any loss Philemon has suffered. The verse is conditional (“if he has wronged you or owes anything”); it does not state that Onesimus had stolen or owed money.

  • You owe me even your own self (v. 19). Philemon most likely came to faith through Paul or his ministry. How and where is not stated.

Christ and Life

Paul asks Philemon, “Receive him as you would me” (v. 17). Onesimus is to be welcomed on the strength of Paul’s standing. This is a picture of how believers are received by God, not on their own merit but in Christ. God has “made us accepted in the Beloved” (Ephesians 1:6).

Paul then offers to bear whatever Onesimus may owe: “put that on my account” (v. 18). The picture points to a greater reality. Christ “Himself bore our sins in His own body on the tree” (1 Peter 2:24), and God “made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in Him” (2 Corinthians 5:21). Paul’s promise illustrates this exchange; only Christ’s sacrifice atones, and it is complete.

Onesimus also returns with a new standing. He is “a beloved brother” (v. 16). In Christ, those who were far off are brought near and become “members of the household of God” (Ephesians 2:13, 19), and in His family there is “neither slave nor free” (Galatians 3:28; compare Colossians 3:11). The change is real: the one once unprofitable is now profitable (v. 11), a glimpse of the new creation in Christ (2 Corinthians 5:17).

Finally, the grace Philemon has received is to be extended. Paul’s appeal rests on what Christ has already done in Philemon’s life, “you owe me even your own self besides” (v. 19). Those who have been welcomed in Christ welcome others: “Receive one another, just as Christ also received us, to the glory of God” (Romans 15:7). The letter invites every believer to let the fellowship of faith become visible in costly, willing love.

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Read Philemon as a real letter about real people in a hard situation. It is not an allegory with every person standing for someone else, and it does not tell us how Philemon responded. Let the open ending stay open.

Remember that Paul’s request is reception as a beloved brother, made in love and seeking a willing response. Neither read Paul’s silence as approval of slavery nor read the letter as a command for emancipation. Let the gospel’s claim on the relationship between Philemon and Onesimus speak with its own force.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

  • Verse 6. “Your faith” is singular, addressed to Philemon, while “in you” is plural, including the church; other manuscripts read “in us.” The NLT follows that reading: “all the good things we have in Christ.”

  • Verse 9. The NKJV reads “Paul, the aged.” The NLT similarly has “an old man.” Some interpreters suggest “ambassador,” a word very similar in Greek, but the NKJV reading is followed here.

  • Verse 12. The NKJV includes “You therefore receive him.” Other manuscripts lack these words, as does the NLT: “I am sending him back to you, and with him comes my own heart.” The central appeal to receive Onesimus comes in verse 17 in either case.

  • Verse 21. “Even more than I say” may hint at freeing Onesimus or at returning him to serve Paul (compare v. 13). Paul does not specify.

  • Philemon and Colossians. Onesimus is described as “one of you” in Colossians 4:9, and Archippus is addressed in Colossians 4:17. Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke appear in both letters (Colossians 4:10–14).

  • Bishop Onesimus. Around AD 110, Ignatius wrote of a bishop of Ephesus named Onesimus (To the Ephesians 1.3). He may have been the same man, but the name was common, and the identification cannot be confirmed.

  • Names. Onesimus is not Onesiphorus (2 Timothy 1:16), and Epaphras of Colossae is not Epaphroditus of Philippi.

  • References. Philemon has one chapter, so verses may be cited as Philemon 1:17 or simply as verse 17.
