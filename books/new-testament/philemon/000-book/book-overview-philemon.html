Philemon: Book Overview

Philemon: Book Overview - Book Overview

Background

Author

Paul names himself as the author three times (vv. 1, 9, 19) and includes Timothy in the opening greeting (v. 1). The body of the letter, however, is written in Paul's own voice ("I appeal," "I am sending him back," "I will repay"), and in verse 19 he takes the pen himself to guarantee his promise. Paul's authorship has been accepted with near-universal agreement from the early church to the present.

Audience

The letter is addressed to Philemon, "our beloved friend and fellow laborer" (v. 1), along with Apphia, Archippus, and "the church in your house" (v. 2). Apphia may have been Philemon's wife and Archippus a member of the household, but the letter does not say.

While the letter speaks to Philemon personally, Paul made sure the congregation meeting in his home would hear the request as well. Philemon was a believer known for his love and generosity toward the saints (vv. 5, 7), appears to have come to faith through Paul (v. 19), and was the master of Onesimus.

Date

Paul wrote as a prisoner (vv. 1, 9, 10, 13). The coworkers who send greetings here (Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke, vv. 23–24) also send greetings in Colossians 4:10–14, and Onesimus travels with that letter to Colossae (Colossians 4:9). The two letters were very likely written and sent together.

Most interpreters place them during Paul's first imprisonment in Rome, around AD 60–62 (Acts 28:16, 30–31). Some propose an earlier imprisonment in Ephesus in the mid-50s, which Acts does not directly record, partly because Ephesus lay much closer to Colossae and Paul hoped to visit soon (v. 22). Others suggest Caesarea (Acts 23–26). The Roman setting best fits the traditional evidence, but the date cannot be fixed with certainty.

The date matters less than the pairing. Because Philemon was written alongside Colossians, Paul's teaching there on new life in Christ and on masters and slaves (Colossians 3:11; 3:22–4:1) is the immediate background for what he asks of one household here.

Historical Setting

Philemon most likely lived in Colossae, a small city in the Lycus River valley of the Roman province of Asia (in modern western Turkey), near Laodicea and Hierapolis (Colossians 4:13). The letter itself does not name the city; the link comes from Colossians, which calls Onesimus "one of you" and sends a message to Archippus (Colossians 4:9, 17). Paul apparently had not visited Colossae personally (Colossians 2:1). The church there had come to faith through the ministry of Epaphras (Colossians 1:7), who is now with Paul (v. 23).

Early Christians met in homes (Romans 16:5; Colossians 4:15). A household large enough to host a congregation suggests Philemon was a man of some means. The household itself was the basic unit of Roman society, joining family members, slaves, and dependents under the authority of its head.

Slavery was woven into that world. People were enslaved through capture in war, birth to an enslaved mother, abandonment as infants, kidnapping, and sale, and some sold themselves to escape poverty. Debt bondage of Roman citizens had been abolished centuries earlier, though people in some parts of the empire could still fall into servitude through debt. Roman slavery was not organized around the racial categories associated with later Atlantic slavery, yet a person's origin and ethnic identity could influence both enslavement and treatment. Slaves served as household workers, laborers, craftsmen, managers, and even tutors, and some were eventually freed. Yet a slave was legally property, under the owner's power, with little protection from abuse, and could be punished severely. A slave who left without permission faced harsh penalties, and anyone who sheltered him could be held liable to the owner for lost labor. Law and custom gave Philemon full rights over Onesimus.

Paul's imprisonment allowed him visitors and helpers (vv. 13, 23–24; compare Acts 28:30–31), which explains how coworkers could remain with him and how Onesimus could serve him.

Occasion

Onesimus, a slave in Philemon's household, had "departed for a while" (v. 15) and come into contact with Paul in prison. There he came to faith in Christ (v. 10) and proved so useful that Paul wished to keep him (vv. 11, 13). Verse 18 raises the possibility that Onesimus had wronged Philemon or caused him loss, though the letter does not say so directly. How he came to be with Paul is debated (see Helpful Notes).

Paul would not keep Onesimus without Philemon's consent (v. 14). He sent him back (v. 12) with this letter, asking Philemon to receive him as a beloved brother (v. 16) and as he would receive Paul himself (v. 17), and promising to repay whatever Onesimus owed (vv. 18–19).

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Philemon is a personal letter of appeal, the shortest of Paul's letters. It concerns one request: that a Christian master welcome back a slave who has become his brother in Christ. Around that request Paul gathers thanksgiving for Philemon's love, a description of Onesimus's change, a pledge to cover any loss himself, and confidence that Philemon will do even more than he asks.

Paul could have commanded, but he chooses to appeal, and he wants Philemon's response to be free and willing. Through one household matter, the letter shows how the gospel changes the way believers treat one another when status, offense, and debt stand between them.

Central Message

The gospel transforms how we see and receive one another. Those who belong to Christ are no longer defined merely by their past, their failures, or their position in society, but by their new relationship in Him. Paul's appeal to Philemon calls him to demonstrate the love he has shown the saints by receiving Onesimus, once his slave and now his beloved brother, even though forgiveness may cost him and his rights as a master would permit a very different response. Through Paul's willingness to bear Onesimus's debt, the letter also reveals something of the reconciling love of Christ.

Why This Book Matters

Most of Paul's letters teach whole churches. Philemon shows that teaching tested in one relationship. It asks a single believer to act toward a single person at real cost to his rights and possibly his finances. The authenticity of our love for Christ is tested not merely by our general affection for believers, but by how we treat the particular person who has wronged us or whom society considers beneath us.

The letter also shows the dignity the gospel gives every believer. A householder and his slave now share one Lord. Paul's explicit request is that Philemon receive Onesimus as "a beloved brother… both in the flesh and in the Lord" (v. 16); he does not command his release. Yet the relationship Paul describes carries implications beyond this one household. It sets out a principle that works against slavery itself: a brother in Christ cannot finally be treated as property.

Finally, Philemon shows how spiritual influence should be used. Paul does not set aside his authority. He reminds Philemon that he could be "very bold in Christ" to command him, and he writes with confidence in his "obedience" (vv. 8, 21). Yet he deliberately appeals "for love's sake" (v. 9), because he wants Philemon's good deed to be voluntary rather than compelled (v. 14). Authority can secure outward compliance, but Paul seeks a willing response from a transformed heart, and he leads through love, honesty, and personal sacrifice (vv. 18–19). The goal of spiritual leadership is not simply right behavior but right behavior that flows from love and inward transformation.

Key Themes to Watch For

  • Love that refreshes. Philemon's love has refreshed the hearts of the saints, and Paul asks for the same refreshment now (vv. 5, 7, 20).

  • Fellowship and partnership. A shared life in Christ binds believers together and becomes the basis of Paul's request (vv. 6, 17).

  • Appeal rather than command. Paul seeks a willing response arising from love rather than one secured merely through command (vv. 8–9, 14, 21).

  • Transformation in Christ. Onesimus, once unprofitable, is now profitable to Philemon and to Paul (vv. 10–11).

  • A new relationship. A slave is to be received as a beloved brother (v. 16).

  • Receiving one another. Philemon is to receive Onesimus as he would receive Paul (vv. 12, 17).

  • Standing in another's place. Paul takes any wrong or debt onto his own account (vv. 18–19).

  • God's purpose in the separation. A temporary parting may have served a lasting purpose (v. 15).

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

Prisoner of Christ Jesus (vv. 1, 9). Paul calls himself Christ's prisoner rather than Rome's. He sees his chains as part of his service to Christ and the gospel (vv. 10, 13).

Spiritual fatherhood (vv. 10, 19). When Paul calls Onesimus "my son… whom I have begotten while in my chains," he means he led Onesimus to faith (compare 1 Corinthians 4:15). He reminds Philemon that he owes Paul "even your own self," which implies Philemon also came to faith through Paul's ministry.

Onesimus's name (vv. 11, 20). Onesimus means "useful" or "profitable" and was a common name for slaves. Paul plays on it: once "unprofitable," now "profitable." The phrase "let me have joy from you" (v. 20) uses a verb related to the name, a second, gentler play on it.

Heart (vv. 7, 12, 20). The Greek word refers to a person's inner being, the seat of deep affection. When Paul calls Onesimus "my own heart," he identifies himself closely with him, so that receiving Onesimus means receiving Paul.

Fellowship and partner (vv. 6, 17). "Sharing" (v. 6) translates koinonia, and "partner" (v. 17) translates a related word. Both describe a common life and participation in Christ that bind believers together and carry real obligations.

"In the flesh and in the Lord" (v. 16). "In the flesh" refers to ordinary human life, including the household relationship between master and slave. "In the Lord" refers to their shared life in Christ. Onesimus is to be a brother to Philemon in both.

Related names. Onesimus is not the same man as Onesiphorus (2 Timothy 1:16). Epaphras, from Colossae (v. 23; Colossians 1:7; 4:12), should not be confused with Epaphroditus of Philippi (Philippians 2:25). Archippus (v. 2) and Aristarchus (v. 24) are also different men. Mark is John Mark, the cousin of Barnabas (Acts 12:12; Colossians 4:10).

Book Flow

Philemon 1:1–7: Paul's Greeting and Thanksgiving

Paul, "a prisoner of Christ Jesus," writes with Timothy to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, and the church in Philemon's house. He thanks God for Philemon's "love and faith" toward the Lord Jesus and all the saints, and prays that the sharing of his faith will become effective. The section closes with Paul's joy that "the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you" (v. 7), which prepares the ground for his request.

Philemon 1:8–16: Paul's Appeal for Onesimus

Paul could command, but "for love's sake" he appeals as an aged man and a prisoner. Only then does he name Onesimus, his son in the faith, once unprofitable but now profitable. Paul sends him back as "my own heart," though he wanted to keep him, because he wants Philemon's good deed to be voluntary. He suggests God may have allowed the separation so that Philemon might receive Onesimus forever, "no longer as a slave but more than a slave," as "a beloved brother" (v. 16). Verse 16 is the heart of the letter; it names the new relationship on which the whole request rests.

Philemon 1:17–22: Paul's Request to Receive Onesimus

Paul states his request plainly: "receive him as you would me" (v. 17). Writing in his own hand, he takes any debt onto himself, "put that on my account… I will repay" (vv. 18–19), while reminding Philemon of what he owes Paul. He asks Philemon to "refresh my heart in the Lord," expresses confidence that he will do even more than asked, and requests a guest room, hoping to be released through the church's prayers.

Philemon 1:23–25: Final Greetings and Benediction

Epaphras, Paul's "fellow prisoner," sends greetings, along with Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke. The letter ends as it began, with grace: "The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit."

Christ and Life

The fellowship that runs through the letter is more than friendship. Philemon's love flows from "every good thing which is in you in Christ Jesus" (v. 6). Elsewhere Scripture describes this fellowship as a share in the life of the Father and the Son (1 John 1:3) through the Holy Spirit (2 Corinthians 13:14). Because believers share Christ's life, they belong to one another. That is why Paul can ground his request in partnership (v. 17). Living in Christ means opening our homes and hearts to everyone He has received.

There is a beautiful picture of Christ in the way Paul stands with Onesimus. "Receive him as you would me" (v. 17). Onesimus comes before Philemon with Paul standing for him, asking to be received on the basis of Paul's relationship and standing rather than his own. In a far greater way, we are received by God because we are in Christ. God "made us accepted in the Beloved" (Ephesians 1:6). Our acceptance rests not on making ourselves worthy but on Jesus.

Paul then places himself between Onesimus and whatever he may owe: "If he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account" (v. 18), and he signs the pledge with his own hand (v. 19). If any wrong or debt stood between Onesimus and Philemon, Paul was willing to take responsibility for it himself. Jesus has done infinitely more for us. Our sin was truly ours, yet He "bore our sins in His own body on the tree" (1 Peter 2:24), and God "made Him who knew no sin to be sin for us, that we might become the righteousness of God in Him" (2 Corinthians 5:21). Paul's offer is a human act of love that illustrates something of Christ's work. Only Christ's sacrifice atones for sin, and it is complete.

Onesimus is not merely having a debt settled. He is coming back with a new standing, "no longer as a slave but more than a slave," as "a beloved brother" (v. 16). In its immediate meaning, this is what Paul asks of Philemon: a changed relationship within his own household and church. It also reflects what Christ has done for us. He does more than forgive our sins. He reconciles us to God, brings near those who were far off, and makes us "members of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:13, 19). Forgiveness clears the account; reconciliation gives us a place in the family.

Philemon must now live out the grace he has received. Whatever wrong or loss may have occurred between them, Onesimus has genuinely been changed by Christ. His name means "useful"; he had been "unprofitable," and Christ made him what his name promised (v. 11). "If anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17). Grace does not call wrong right or pretend the past never happened. It makes reconciliation possible and teaches believers to see one another in light of what Christ is doing in them rather than by their past or their station (2 Corinthians 5:16; Colossians 3:11). Those who have been received by God are called to "receive one another, just as Christ also received us" (Romans 15:7).

Reading Guide

One-Sentence Summary

Writing from prison, Paul sends Onesimus, a slave who has become a believer, back to his master Philemon with a personal appeal to receive him as a beloved brother, offering to pay whatever Onesimus may owe.

Read This Book Remembering...

  • This is a real letter about real people. Begin with what Paul actually asked Philemon to do. Paul's offer to pay is a picture of Christ's work, but the letter is not an allegory in which every detail stands for something else.

  • The letter leaves much unsaid. It does not tell us why Onesimus left, whether he owed anything, or how Philemon responded. Do not fill these gaps as though the text supplied them.

  • Paul's silence on slavery as an institution is not approval of it. Read the letter alongside Paul's teaching that in Christ there is neither slave nor free (Galatians 3:28; Colossians 3:11). Some hear in "even more than I say" (v. 21) a hint that Philemon should free Onesimus or send him back to serve Paul. The text does not specify, and Paul's explicit request is that Philemon receive him as a brother.

  • Paul's appeal is firm but not forced. His reminders (vv. 19, 21) come from a spiritual father, yet he leaves the decision with Philemon so that his goodness will be voluntary (v. 14).

  • Reconciliation here is not merely private. The letter was addressed to the church as well (v. 2), so the whole congregation would see how Philemon responded.

Helpful Notes

A Prison Epistle and a companion to Colossians. Philemon is one of Paul's four Prison Epistles, with Ephesians, Philippians, and Colossians. Colossians names Tychicus and Onesimus as the men bringing news to Colossae (Colossians 4:7–9), so Onesimus very likely returned carrying this letter along with Colossians.

Was Onesimus a runaway? The traditional view is that Onesimus fled Philemon, perhaps after taking money or property (v. 18), and later met Paul. Another view holds that he deliberately sought out Paul, a trusted friend of his master, to plead his case after some offense; Roman legal writers discussed such situations, and a slave who did this was not always treated as a fugitive. A third view suggests Philemon had sent him to Paul. The letter says only that Onesimus "departed for a while" (v. 15) and may owe something (v. 18).

Mark and Demas. Mark, whose earlier departure once divided Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:37–39), is now among Paul's "fellow laborers," and Paul later calls him "useful to me for ministry" (2 Timothy 4:11). Demas, also named here, later forsook Paul, "having loved this present world" (2 Timothy 4:10).

Onesimus in later history. Around AD 110, Ignatius of Antioch wrote to the church at Ephesus and mentioned its bishop, Onesimus. Some believe he was the same man, but the name was common and the identification cannot be confirmed.

A Reformation reading. Martin Luther observed that Paul does for Onesimus with Philemon what Christ does for us with the Father, and remarked that "we are all his Onesimi."
