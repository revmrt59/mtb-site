Book Questions

Does Paul approve of slavery? Why doesn’t he tell Philemon to free Onesimus? (Philemon 1:16)

Verse 16 names the new relationship. Onesimus is to be received “no longer as a slave but more than a slave,” as “a beloved brother” (Philemon 1:16). Verse 17 gives Philemon a concrete way to honor that relationship: “receive him as you would me.” Paul leaves Onesimus’s future legal status open.

That silence is not approval. Ancient slavery involved real coercion and a deep imbalance of power, and Onesimus was not simply an employee. Yet Paul asks Philemon to see the man he held as a slave as his brother “both in the flesh and in the Lord” (Philemon 1:16), and in Christ “there is neither slave nor free” (Galatians 3:28; compare Colossians 3:11). That a brother in Christ cannot finally be treated as property is a theological implication of the letter, not a stated command.

So Philemon is neither an endorsement of slavery nor an abolition manifesto. It is an appeal that sets a relationship governed by Christ above the one governed by ownership.

Read: Philemon 1:15–17, Galatians 3:28, Colossians 3:11 · Related: What is the “even more” Paul expects?

Was Onesimus a runaway slave? (Philemon 1:10–18)

MTB holds the traditional reading: Onesimus ran away from Philemon and came to faith through Paul while Paul was in prison. Paul calls him “my son Onesimus, whom I have begotten while in my chains” (Philemon 1:10). The letter gives no details of how they met, and Paul describes the absence gently: “he departed for a while” (Philemon 1:15). Philemon most likely lived in Colossae (Colossians 4:9).

Some interpreters think Onesimus was not a fugitive but went to Paul deliberately, seeking his help with his master. The letter does not settle the question.

Paul also writes conditionally about any wrong: “if he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account” (Philemon 1:18). The letter leaves open whether Onesimus stole anything or owed Philemon money.

Read: Philemon 1:10–18, Colossians 4:9 · Related: Was Onesimus’s departure part of God’s plan?

Passage Questions

Philemon 1:4–7 · Thanksgiving for Philemon’s Love and Faith

Is “the sharing of your faith” about evangelism? (v. 6)

Paul prays “that the sharing of your faith may become effective” (v. 6). The sharing is the common life believers have in Christ. Paul returns to that same idea when he asks Philemon to count him “a partner” (v. 17).

Paul writes personally to Philemon and includes the church in his house in the greeting (vv. 1–2). Receiving Onesimus as a brother would show what that common life looks like. It would make Philemon’s faith visible to the whole congregation.

Read: Philemon 1:1–7, Philemon 1:17

Philemon 1:8–11 · Paul Appeals for Love’s Sake on Behalf of Onesimus

How does Paul seek a willing response? (vv. 8–21)

Paul says he “might be very bold in Christ to command,” yet “for love’s sake I rather appeal” (vv. 8–9). He wants Philemon’s good deed to be “voluntary,” not “by compulsion” (v. 14). He keeps his apostolic authority and still writes with confidence in Philemon’s “obedience” (v. 21).

His words carry real moral weight. He reminds Philemon that “you owe me even your own self besides” (v. 19), most likely because Philemon came to faith through Paul’s ministry. That is honest pastoral appeal from a spiritual father, not manipulation. Paul neither gives up his authority nor uses it to force the outcome.

Read: Philemon 1:8–14, Philemon 1:19–21

Philemon 1:12–16 · No Longer as a Slave but a Beloved Brother

Was Onesimus’s departure part of God’s plan? (v. 15)

Paul writes, “For perhaps he departed for a while for this purpose, that you might receive him forever” (v. 15). He invites Philemon to see how the separation may have opened the way to receiving Onesimus as a beloved brother. The “perhaps” matters. Paul leaves room for God’s providence without claiming to know it.

Providence does not excuse wrong. Paul keeps responsibility in view: “if he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account” (v. 18). “Receive him forever” speaks of a lasting relationship, including in the Lord; it is not a text on which to build a doctrine of eternal security.

Read: Philemon 1:15–18 · Related: Was Onesimus a runaway slave?

Philemon 1:17–20 · Receive Him as You Would Me

Is Paul’s offer to pay a picture of what Christ has done? (vv. 17–19)

Yes, as a picture. Paul asks, “receive him as you would me” (v. 17), and offers, “if he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account” (v. 18). Believers are accepted through Christ’s standing (Ephesians 1:6), and Christ bore our sins (1 Peter 2:24; 2 Corinthians 5:21). Paul’s actions illustrate that welcome and that costly identification. Philemon, in turn, is to receive as he has been received (Romans 15:7).

The picture has limits. Paul’s offer is not an atoning act; only Christ’s sacrifice atones, and it is complete. Nor is the letter a full allegory in which Philemon stands for the Father, Paul for Christ, and Onesimus for the sinner, with every detail assigned a meaning. The picture comes from Paul’s own actions.

Read: Philemon 1:17–19, Ephesians 1:6, 1 Peter 2:24, 2 Corinthians 5:21, Romans 15:7

Philemon 1:21–22 · Confidence in Philemon and Hope of a Visit

What is the “even more” Paul expects? Did Philemon free Onesimus? (v. 21)

Paul writes, “knowing that you will do even more than I say” (v. 21). Some see a hint that Philemon would free Onesimus. Others think Paul hoped Philemon would send him back to serve with Paul, who had wished to keep him so “that on your behalf he might minister to me in my chains for the gospel” (v. 13). The text does not say.

The letter also does not record how Philemon responded, and MTB does not claim what he did.

Read: Philemon 1:13–14, Philemon 1:21 · Related: Does Paul approve of slavery?
