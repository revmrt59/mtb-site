BELOVED - (agapētós, Strong's G27)

Summary:

The word agapētos means "beloved," "dearly loved," or "cherished." It describes a person who is held in deep, genuine affection and valued highly by the speaker or by God. In Philemon 1:1, Paul uses this word to address Philemon as a deeply cherished brother in Christ. By opening his letter with this term, Paul affirms his personal affection for Philemon and establishes a warm, familial foundation of Christian love before making a sensitive appeal on behalf of the runaway servant Onesimus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greek world, words related to love carried various shades of meaning, ranging from friendship and familial affection to intense passion. The word agapētos belongs to the word group linked to Christian love (agapē). In general Greek literature, the word described a child who was uniquely cherished, such as an only child who brought special joy to parents.

In the New Testament, early Christian writers adopted this word to express the unique bond shared by members of the church. Because believers were brought into God's family through Christ, they viewed one another not merely as acquaintances or associates, but as dearly loved brothers and sisters. When Paul wrote to Philemon from prison, he used the standard letter-writing conventions of his day, but transformed them by filling his opening greeting with genuine spiritual affection.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word agapētos is an adjective that means "beloved," "dear," "dearly loved," or "highly esteemed." It identifies the person to whom it refers as an object of deep love and honor.

The word itself does not describe an abstract feeling. Instead, it describes a practical, recognized relationship of cherished value. Grammatically, in Philemon 1:1, the word is formatted as a masculine dative singular adjective, matching the name of Philemon. This grammatical form specifies Philemon himself as the individual directly receiving Paul's warm greeting and affection.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, agapētos is used in a few distinct ways:

First, it is used by God the Father to express His affection for His Son, Jesus Christ. At Jesus' baptism and transfiguration, the Father's voice from heaven declared Jesus to be His beloved Son.

Second, the apostles frequently used the word as a direct address to Christian congregations. Letters like 1 John, 1 Peter, and Romans address whole assemblies as "beloved," reminding believers of their shared standing as people loved by God.

Third, the word describes individual co-workers and personal friends in ministry. Paul frequently calls trusted companions—such as Tychicus, Luke, Epaphras, and Philemon—his beloved brothers. In these settings, the word highlights mutual trust, shared labor, and personal commitment within the kingdom of God.

4. Theological Implications

The use of agapētos between Christians points to a fundamental truth about the church: Christian fellowship is built on the love God has first shown to His people. Believers are able to hold one another in genuine affection because they share the same Heavenly Father and the same Savior.

Furthermore, calling a fellow believer "beloved" reflects the horizontal expression of vertical grace. When Paul addresses Philemon as beloved, he acknowledges that both he and Philemon exist within the sphere of God's redeeming mercy. This shared identity creates a strong moral obligation to act with love, forgiveness, and generosity toward others in the body of Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:1, Paul writes: "Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy our brother, To Philemon our beloved friend and fellow laborer." The NKJV translates agapētos here as "beloved."

This designation plays a crucial role in the argument of the letter. Paul was writing to Philemon to intervene for Onesimus, a runaway servant who had encountered Paul, converted to Christ, and was now returning to his master. Before Paul presents his difficult request—asking Philemon to forgive Onesimus and receive him back as a brother in Christ—he establishes the nature of his relationship with Philemon.

By calling Philemon "beloved," Paul does not use empty flattery. He genuinely honors Philemon as a cherished friend and co-laborer. This warm opening reminds Philemon of the deep spiritual bond they share. It sets a tone of mutual love and spiritual equality, preparing Philemon's heart to act with grace toward Onesimus.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 3:17 – God the Father declares Jesus to be His "beloved" Son at His baptism, establishing the primary divine pattern of holy affection.

  • Ephesians 6:21 – Paul refers to Tychicus as a "beloved" brother and faithful minister, illustrating how early Christians honored trusted partners in ministry.

  • Colossians 1:7 – Epaphras is described as a "dear" fellow servant, showing how the word highlights close fellowship among those working together for Christ.

  • 1 John 4:7 – John addresses his readers directly as "Beloved," grounding his command to love one another in the reality of God's love.

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a meaningful literary connection within the short letter to Philemon. In verse 1, Paul addresses Philemon as "beloved." Later in verse 16, Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus no longer as a slave, but as a "beloved brother."

By using the same word for both men, Paul subtlely shows that Christ reshapes human relationships. The same divine affection that connects Paul to Philemon must now extend to Onesimus. The greeting in verse 1 establishes a standard of love that eventually encompasses the entire household of faith.
