CHRIST - (Christós, Strong's G5547)

Summary:

The Greek word christos means "Anointed One" and is the New Testament equivalent of the Hebrew title Messiah. It describes a person specially chosen, set apart, and empowered by God for a holy office. Applied to Jesus of Nazareth, it identifies Him as God’s promised King and Savior. In Philemon 1:1, the title appears in the phrase "a prisoner of Christ Jesus," where it highlights Jesus as the true Lord over Paul’s life and circumstances, showing that Paul’s imprisonment was ultimately in service to his anointed King rather than a defeat by earthly authorities.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word christos comes from a Greek verb that means to smear or anoint with oil. In the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), christos was used to translate the Hebrew word Mashiach (Messiah). In ancient Israel, anointing with oil was the sacred ceremony used to consecrate kings, high priests, and occasionally prophets. The poured oil symbolized God's spirit setting a person apart for a specific divine responsibility.

Over centuries of prophecy, Israel looked forward to a ultimate Anointed One—a ruler from David’s line who would bring divine salvation, establish righteousness, and reign over an everlasting kingdom. By the time of the New Testament, "the Christ" was the recognized title for this promised Savior. When Jesus began His public ministry, the central question was whether He was indeed the Christ long awaited by God's people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, christos functions as both an official title ("Anointed One" or "Messiah") and a personal proper name when joined with the human name Jesus. The term designates divine authorization and empowerment by the Holy Spirit rather than a mere physical application of oil.

In early Christian writing, the title quickly combined with the personal name Jesus to form "Jesus Christ" or "Christ Jesus." This double designation identifies Jesus of Nazareth as the official Messiah sent by God. In Philemon 1:1, the title identifies Jesus Christ as the supreme, sovereign Master to whom Paul belongs, serving as the basis for Paul's authority and appeal.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, christos is used in several distinct ways:

  • As an official title ("the Christ"), highlighting His fulfillment of Old Testament messianic expectations.

  • As a proper name joined with Jesus ("Christ Jesus"), focusing on His identity as the risen Lord who reigns over His church.

  • As a shorthand reference to the Christian faith or life ("in Christ"), marking the spiritual union believers share with their Lord.

In the letters of Paul, "Christ Jesus" appears regularly at the start of greetings. In Philemon 1:1, the word appears in the genitive case ("of Christ Jesus"), indicating possession and relationship. Paul describes himself as a "prisoner of Christ Jesus." This expression demonstrates that Paul viewed his imprisonment in Rome as an assignment under Christ's direct authority rather than merely the result of Roman custody.

4. Theological Implications

The title christos carries profound theological significance:

  • Fulfillment of Prophecy: Identifying Jesus as the Christ affirms that God kept His ancient covenant promises made to Abraham, David, and the prophets.

  • Divine Authority: As the Anointed One, Jesus holds supreme office as Prophet, Priest, and King. His commands carry divine weight, and His kingdom transcends earthly empires.

  • Sovereign Control over Suffering: When Paul calls himself a prisoner of Christ Jesus, he anchors his present trials in Christ's lordship. Earthly imprisonment does not mean Christ has lost control; rather, Christ remains Lord over every circumstance, using even confinement for His gospel purposes.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:1, Paul introduces himself with an unusual phrase. Instead of using his customary opening title, "an apostle of Christ Jesus," Paul describes himself simply as "a prisoner of Christ Jesus."

This choice sets the tone for the whole letter. Paul is writing to Philemon, a wealthy Christian believer, regarding Onesimus, a runaway bondservant who became a believer through Paul's ministry in prison. By identifying as a captive belonging to Christ, Paul sets aside apostolical authority in favor of a humble, personal plea.

The phrase reminds Philemon that Christ Jesus is the supreme Lord over everyone in the church. Master and bondservant, free citizen and prisoner, all stand on equal footing before the anointed King. Paul's chains for Christ serve as a living example of self-sacrificing love, inviting Philemon to respond to Onesimus with the same Christlike grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:1 — "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham."

  • Acts 17:3 — "...explaining and demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead, and saying, 'This Jesus whom I preach to you is the Christ.'"

  • Ephesians 3:1 — "For this reason I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus for you Gentiles..."

  • 2 Timothy 1:9 — "...who has saved us and called us with a holy calling, not according to our works, but according to His own purpose and grace which was given to us in Christ Jesus before time began..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In another occurrence within Paul's letters, Ephesians 3:1 uses the exact same expression, "the prisoner of Christ Jesus." This repeated phrasing during Paul's Roman imprisonment highlights how deeply Paul held this perspective. He never viewed himself as a captive of Nero or the Roman legal system, but always as a bondservant belonging to Jesus Christ. This mindset reframes human suffering, transforming outward hardship into an opportunity to honor the anointed Lord.
