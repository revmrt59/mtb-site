JESUS - (Iēsoûs, Strong's G2424)

Summary:

The word Iēsous is a proper personal name derived from Hebrew, meaning "Yahweh saves" or "Yahweh is salvation." In the New Testament, it refers directly to Jesus of Nazareth, the Son of God and Savior of the world. In Philemon 1:1, Paul uses the name alongside "Christ" in his opening greeting, identifying himself as "a prisoner of Christ Jesus." This phrase shows that Paul views his physical imprisonment not as the doing of Rome, but as a captivity belonging to and endured for the sake of his Lord, Jesus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Letter to Philemon was written by the apostle Paul while he was imprisoned, likely in Rome. Unlike many of his other epistles addressed to entire congregations, this letter is directed primarily to an individual, Philemon, a Christian leader in Colosse, concerning his runaway bondservant Onesimus.

In the very first line of the letter, Paul introduces himself not as an apostle commanding authority, but as "a prisoner of Christ Jesus." By placing the personal name "Jesus" alongside the title "Christ," Paul establishes the personal and spiritual basis of his appeal. The personal name points directly to the earthly person and historical reality of the Lord in whose service Paul now bears chains.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Iēsous is a proper masculine noun. It is the Greek adaptation of the Hebrew name Yēšûaʿ (a shortened post-exilic form of Yěhôšûaʿ or Joshua).

Etymologically, the name means "Yahweh saves" or "Yahweh is salvation." In first-century Jewish culture, Iēsous was a common personal name borne by many individuals. In the New Testament, however, the name uniquely designates Jesus of Nazareth.

In Philemon 1:1, Iēsous functions as the personal name of the incarnate Lord, combined with the messianic title Christos ("Christ" or "Anointed One") to form the full title "Christ Jesus."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, Iēsous appears in several complementary ways:

  • Historical Name: In the Gospels, the name refers directly to the earthly life, humanity, ministry, death, and resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth.

  • Joined with Title: In the Epistles, the apostles frequently pair the personal name with the title "Christ" (either "Jesus Christ" or "Christ Jesus"). This combination asserts that the historical man Jesus is indeed the promised Old Testament Messiah.

  • Universal Authority: In the Acts of the Apostles and the Epistles, the name represents the power, authority, and salvation available exclusively through Him.

4. Theological Implications

The use of the name Iēsous anchors Christian faith in history. Salvation is not an abstract philosophy; it was accomplished by a real person who entered human history. The name explicitly points to God's saving initiative, recalling the divine instruction given before His birth that He should be named Jesus because He would save His people from their sins.

When Paul calls himself "a prisoner of Christ Jesus," he demonstrates how the Lord's personal lordship transforms a believer's perspective on earthly suffering. Paul does not describe himself as a victim of Nero or the Roman legal system. Instead, he views his imprisonment as an assignment within his relationship to Jesus.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:1, Paul opens his letter with the greeting: "Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy our brother..."

The grammatical form of the name identifies association and ownership. Paul belongs to Christ Jesus. His chains are borne on account of Jesus and under Jesus' sovereign authority.

This opening designation sets a distinct pastoral tone for the entire letter. Paul is preparing to ask Philemon to do something difficult: to forgive his runaway bondservant Onesimus and receive him back as a beloved brother. By emphasizing his own position as a prisoner of Jesus, Paul Models sacrificial love and humbles himself before making his warm appeal to Philemon.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:1 — "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham:"

  • Acts 17:3 — "demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead, and saying, 'This Jesus whom I preach to you is the Christ.'"

  • Romans 14:14 — "I know and am convinced by the Lord Jesus that there is nothing unclean of itself..."

  • 2 Corinthians 4:14 — "knowing that He who raised up the Lord Jesus will also raise us up with Jesus, and will present us with you."

  • Hebrews 6:20 — "where the forerunner has entered for us, even Jesus, having become High Priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Structural Contrast: In letters to churches where his authority was questioned (such as Galatia or Corinth), Paul often opens by asserting his title as an "apostle." In Philemon, he deliberately sets aside that title in favor of "prisoner of Christ Jesus," prioritizing brotherly fellowship and gentle entreaty over apostolic command.

  • Messiah and Savior: Reversing the customary order to read "Christ Jesus" (rather than "Jesus Christ") is common in Paul's writings, putting initial emphasis on the exalted office of the Messiah before naming the historical person who fulfills it.
