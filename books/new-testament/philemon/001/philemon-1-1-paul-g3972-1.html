PAUL - (Paûlos, Strong's G3972)

Summary:

The Greek proper name Paulos identifies the apostle Paul, born Saul of Tarsus, who was set apart by God to proclaim the gospel to the Gentile world. Derived from a Latin surname meaning "small" or "little," the name represents his Roman identity used throughout his missionary journeys. In Philemon 1:1, the name identifies the human author of the letter, who sets aside his formal title of apostle and presents himself instead as "a prisoner of Christ Jesus" to appeal in love to his friend Philemon.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Paulos refers to the apostle Paul, formerly known as Saul of Tarsus. Born a Jewish Pharisee in the Cilician city of Tarsus, he was also a Roman citizen by birth. As was common for Roman citizens in the Jewish diaspora, he carried both a Hebrew name (Saul) and a Latin name (Paulus).

In the narrative of Acts, Luke introduces the shift to his Roman name during Paul's first missionary journey. As Paul ministered across the Mediterranean world, his Roman name was natural and appropriate for his mission to Gentiles and Roman citizens. In Philemon 1:1, Paul writes from prison to Philemon, a Christian leader in Colosse, concerning a runaway slave named Onesimus.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Paulos is a proper noun adopted into Greek from the common Latin surname Paulus, which means "small" or "little." It functions in the New Testament as the proper name of the apostle.

In Roman naming conventions, Paulus was a recognized Roman cognomen (family or personal surname). While the root meaning of the Latin word is "small," Scripture never states that Paul adopted this name to symbolize personal humility or spiritual lowliness. Instead, the name simply reflects his legal Roman name alongside his traditional Hebrew name. In Philemon 1:1, the name identifies the apostle directly as the writer of this personal letter.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, the name Paulos appears in the historical narrative of Acts and at the beginning of thirteen New Testament epistles.

In Acts, Luke uses Paulos consistently after the missionary trip to Cyprus. In the epistles, Paul usually begins his greeting with his name followed by his divine commission, such as "Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ." However, in Philemon 1:1, he modifies his greeting in a distinct way. Rather than emphasizing his apostolic authority, he describes himself as "a prisoner of Christ Jesus."

4. Theological Implications

The presence of Paul's name at the start of this letter highlights God's sovereign grace in turning a former persecutor of the church into an ambassador for Christ. God prepared Paul through his unique background—trained in Jewish law, fluent in Greek, and holding Roman citizenship—to bridge diverse cultures with the gospel.

His self-identification in Philemon also demonstrates how christian authority functions. Although Paul possessed apostolic authority, he chose to appeal to Philemon on the basis of Christian love, brotherhood, and shared commitment to Jesus Christ. His name stands linked with Christ's imprisonment, proving that suffering for the gospel is an honor rather than a defeat.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:1, the apostle writes: "Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy our brother..." This greeting establishes a warm, intimate, and persuasive tone for the entire letter.

By calling himself "a prisoner of Christ Jesus," Paul frames his current physical imprisonment under Rome as a spiritual reality under the sovereignty of Christ. He is not writing from a place of worldly power, but from chains endured for the gospel. This humbled condition reinforces his request to Philemon. Paul is preparing to ask Philemon to forgive and receive Onesimus, a runaway slave who became a believer under Paul's prison ministry. Before asking Philemon to lay down his legal rights over a slave, Paul reminds Philemon that he himself is bound in chains for Christ's sake.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Grammatically, the form of Paulos in Philemon 1:1 functions as the nominative subject introducing the author of the letter.

Paul couples his name with Timothy ("and Timothy our brother"), demonstrating team ministry and pastoral care for the household church meeting in Philemon's home. Although Timothy did not co-author the letter, including his name shows solidarity and provides a second witness to the loving request Paul presents on behalf of Onesimus.
