APPEAL - (parakaléō, Strong's G3870)

Summary:

The Greek verb parakaleō fundamentally means to call someone alongside. Depending on the context, it can mean to entreat, urge, encourage, or comfort. In Philemon 1:10, the word describes an earnest personal entreaty. Paul chooses to appeal to Philemon on behalf of the runaway servant Onesimus, intentionally relying on love and persuasion rather than issuing an apostolic command.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word parakaleō is built from two roots: para, meaning "alongside," and kaleō, meaning "to call." Historically, the term carried the basic sense of calling someone to one's side to render assistance, give advice, offer encouragement, or present a formal request.

In the Greco-Roman world, authority figures routinely used official commands to enforce compliance. Paul had the authority to issue a direct order to Philemon regarding Onesimus. Instead, he steps away from blunt administrative command to make a personal request. By using parakaleō, Paul draws alongside Philemon as a brother in Christ, basing his request on mutual love and shared faith.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Depending on the situation and relationship between the speaker and the listener, parakaleō expresses several distinct actions:

  • To request or entreat earnestly (to plead, beg, or appeal).

  • To urge or exhort someone toward a necessary action or spiritual standard.

  • To speak words of strength and consolation to someone in distress (to comfort or encourage).

In Philemon 1:10, the word carries the sense of an earnest entreaty or appeal. Paul is asking Philemon to receive Onesimus with forgiveness and grace, presenting his desire as a request rather than a compulsory mandate.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, parakaleō serves several key functions:

  • Seeking help or mercy: People frequently approach Jesus in the Gospels to ask for healing or assistance.

  • Offering spiritual comfort: God and believers offer strength to those undergoing hardship or sorrow.

  • Pastoral exhortation: Apostles and church leaders urge believers to walk faithfully, endure trial, and maintain sound doctrine.

  • Personal entreaty: Believers appeal to one another on the basis of Christian fellowship, asking for unity or reconciliation.

In each setting, the word retains the posture of coming alongside a person to move them toward help, comfort, or right action.

4. Theological Implications

Paul's choice to appeal rather than command illustrates how authority operates within the Christian community. Gospel-centered leadership seeks voluntary obedience born of love, not forced compliance driven by authority alone.

Furthermore, the appeal demonstrates the transformative nature of Christian fellowship. Onesimus, formerly a runaway servant, is now Paul's spiritual son and Philemon's brother in Christ. By making this appeal, Paul acts as a mediator, stepping into the breach to facilitate reconciliation between two believers whose relationship has been radically altered by the gospel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the verses leading up to Philemon 1:10, Paul explicitly contrasts issuing a command with making an appeal. He acknowledges that he has full authority in Christ to order Philemon to do what is proper. Yet, "for love's sake," Paul sets aside command in favor of entreaty.

Paul speaks in the present tense as an apostle writing from prison ("I appeal"). He presents his request not from a position of earthly power, but from imprisonment, emphasizing his self-sacrificing love for Onesimus. By making an appeal, Paul leaves room for Philemon's good action to spring willingly from his own heart rather than from compulsion.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 14:22 — "exhorting" — Paul and Barnabas strengthened the disciples, "exhorting them to continue in the faith."

  • 1 Corinthians 1:10 — "plead" — Paul uses the word to appeal for church unity: "I plead with you, brethren, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that you all speak the same thing."

  • 2 Corinthians 7:6 — "comforted" — God's work of encouragement is highlighted: "God, who comforts the downcast, comforted us by the coming of Titus."

  • 1 Timothy 2:1 — "exhort" — Paul provides instruction on prayer: "I exhort first of all that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks be made for all men."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The verbal root of parakaleō is closely related to paraklētos, the word Jesus uses for the Holy Spirit as the "Helper" or "Comforter" who comes alongside believers. When Paul comes alongside Philemon with a spirit of gentle persuasion and appeal, his posture reflects the tender, encouraging work of the Holy Spirit within the church family.
