BEGOTTEN - (gennáō, Strong's G1080)

Summary:

The word refers to fathering a child, giving birth, or bringing someone or something into existence. In Philemon 1:10, Paul uses the word metaphorically to describe leading the runaway servant Onesimus to faith in Jesus Christ while imprisoned. By describing Onesimus as one he has begotten in his chains, Paul highlights his spiritual fatherhood and deep pastoral love for this new believer, urging Philemon to receive him as a beloved brother in Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek, the verb gennao primarily describes the biological act of fathering a child or bringing forth offspring. In ordinary conversation and family records, it established legal lineage, family descent, and fatherhood. Beyond literal birth, the ancient world also used the term metaphorically to describe producing an outcome, generating strife, or establishing a deep fatherlike relationship of mentorship and guidance.

In the New Testament setting, physical lineage was central to Jewish identity, as seen in the genealogical lists that trace God's promise through generations. However, Jesus and the apostles expanded this vocabulary to describe a far greater reality: the radical spiritual transformation of human hearts. When a person turns to Christ, a spiritual birth occurs that creates a new identity and a genuine family relationship within the church.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to fathering a child, bringing forth a baby, or giving birth. When used physically, it marks the beginning of human life and natural descent.

When used metaphorically of spiritual matters, it refers to bringing someone into spiritual life through the gospel or generating a spiritual outcome. In Philemon 1:10, Paul speaks in the first person, stating that he has spiritually fathered Onesimus. Paul does not mean he gave physical birth to Onesimus, but that God used Paul's preaching of the gospel to bring Onesimus into new spiritual life while Paul was imprisoned.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses this word across several clear settings:

  • Physical Lineage and Birth: The word frequently appears in genealogies to record family lines, tracing fathers and their children across generations. It also describes normal human birth and childhood.

  • Spiritual Rebirth by God: Jesus uses the word to explain that a person must be born again by the Spirit to enter the kingdom of God. In this sense, God is the ultimate source of spiritual life.

  • Spiritual Paternity in Ministry: Paul uses the term to describe his ministry relationship with converts. By bringing the gospel to nonbelievers and nurturing them, he viewed himself as their spiritual father.

  • Abstract or Figurative Generation: The word can also describe producing non-physical results, such as when foolish arguments cause or generate conflicts.

4. Theological Implications

Paul’s use of this word in Philemon reveals important truths about spiritual life and ministry. First, true spiritual conversion creates genuine familial bonds. Onesimus was not merely a recipient of religious instruction; he became Paul’s spiritual son. Gospel ministry does not merely inform people; it brings them into God's family.

Second, the word shows that God's power is not limited by human circumstances. Paul was in chains, held under Roman custody. Yet even in physical confinement, his gospel ministry was fruitful. Physical chains could not stop the Holy Spirit from bringing a dead heart to life.

Finally, spiritual birth redefines a person's worth and status. Onesimus was a runaway servant facing severe social and legal consequences. But through spiritual rebirth, he received a clean slate and a high calling as a child of God and a spiritual brother to Paul and Philemon.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:10, Paul writes a delicate and personal letter appealing to Philemon on behalf of Onesimus. Onesimus had fled from Philemon and somehow encountered Paul in Rome. While in prison, Paul led Onesimus to Christ.

Paul chooses his words carefully. Rather than demanding that Philemon forgive Onesimus, Paul grounds his request in Christian love. He calls Onesimus "my son," and immediately adds that he has "begotten" him "in my chains." Paul uses past-action language to describe what took place during his imprisonment.

This framing carries significant weight for Philemon. Paul reminds Philemon that Onesimus is no longer just a missing servant; he is Paul's own spiritual child, born during a time of intense hardship. By linking his own suffering ("my chains") with Onesimus's conversion, Paul creates a powerful emotional and spiritual argument. To reject Onesimus would be to reject the spiritual son Paul brought forth while bound in chains for the gospel.

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound wordplay embedded in the context of Philemon. The name Onesimus literally meant "useful" or "profitable" in the ancient Greco-Roman world, a common name given to servants. In verse 11, Paul notes that Onesimus was formerly useless to Philemon, but now has become useful to both Philemon and Paul.

The spiritual birth described by the word "begotten" in verse 10 is what caused this change. Apart from Christ, Onesimus was runaway and unprofitable. But once Paul begot him in the faith, his inner character was changed. Spiritual birth transformed him from a runaway servant into a reliable, valuable brother in Christ who lived up to his name.
