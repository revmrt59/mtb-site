BEGOTTEN - ( gennaō , Strong's G1080 )

Summary:

In Philemon 1:10, the word translated "begotten" (gennaō) refers to spiritual parentage rather than biological birth. Paul uses this intimate, familial term to describe his role in the conversion and spiritual birth of Onesimus, a runaway slave who met Christ through Paul's ministry in prison. This transformation turned a legal fugitive into a beloved spiritual son, demonstrating the profound power of the gospel to redefine human relationships.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • Gennaō historically refers to a father procreating or begetting a child, or a mother giving birth. It carries deep biological imagery of planting a seed that generates new, identical life. In Jewish and Hellenistic thought, teachers occasionally used the language of parenting to describe the relationship between a mentor and a disciple, implying that the teacher "fathered" the student's intellectual or moral life. Paul elevates this concept to a spiritual reality, describing the actual impartation of spiritual life through the proclamation of the gospel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Gennaō means to beget, father, generate, or bring forth.

  • When used of a father, it means to procreate or beget.

  • When used of a mother, it means to give birth to or bring forth.

  • In figurative contexts, it means to cause someone to be reborn spiritually, acting as the human instrument of their conversion and spiritual initiation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The New Testament uses gennaō in several distinct ways:

  • Physical birth: This is the most common literal usage, tracing genealogies (as in Matthew 1) or referring to physical childbearing.

  • The incarnation and divine sonship of Christ: It is used of the Messiah's relationship to the Father, highlighting His unique status.

  • Spiritual regeneration: It refers to the believer's new birth from God, signifying a complete inner transformation.

  • Apostolic spiritual parenting: It is used metaphorically of an apostle bringing a convert to faith, imparting the "seed" of the gospel which results in spiritual life.

4. Theological Implications

Spiritual life is not self-generated. Just as physical birth requires a parent, spiritual birth requires an external source—the Holy Spirit working through the seed of the Word of God. The gospel creates a deep, unbreakable bond of spiritual family. Those who lead others to Christ share a unique "parental" affection and responsibility for their spiritual growth, transcending social, cultural, and legal barriers. Human agency is honored in God's redemptive plan; while God is the ultimate source of regeneration, He uses human messengers to "beget" believers by preaching the truth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:10, Paul appeals on behalf of Onesimus, whom he calls "my son... whom I have begotten while in my chains." The phrase "in my chains" adds incredible emotional and theological weight. Despite physical restriction, the Word of God was not bound. Paul's captivity did not prevent him from being spiritually productive. Using familial language (son, begotten) is a strategic and pastoral appeal to Philemon. Under Roman law, Onesimus was a runaway slave subject to severe punishment. By declaring Onesimus his "begotten" son, Paul elevates Onesimus's status from a piece of property to Philemon's own spiritual brother, completely changing the dynamic of the request.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 3:3 - "Jesus answered and said to him, 'Most assuredly, I say to you, unless one is born again, he cannot see the kingdom of God.'"

  • 1 Corinthians 4:15 - "For though you might have ten thousand instructors in Christ, yet you do not have many fathers; for in Christ Jesus I have begotten you through the gospel."

  • Hebrews 1:5 - "For to which of the angels did He ever say: 'You are My Son, Today I have begotten You'?"

  • 1 John 2:29 - "If you know that He is righteous, you know that everyone who practices righteousness is born of Him."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The ancient world placed a high value on lineage and inheritance. A begotten child inherited the status, name, and wealth of the father. By calling Onesimus his begotten son, Paul metaphorically extends his own apostolic spiritual inheritance to a runaway slave. This text illustrates the ultimate equalizer of the gospel. In the Roman Empire, a master and a slave were on opposite ends of the social hierarchy. Through the spiritual begetting of Onesimus, both the slave and the master now share the same spiritual pedigree as brothers in Christ.
