PROFITABLE - ( euchrestos , Strong's G2173 )

Summary:

The Greek word euchrestos means "highly useful," "serviceable," or "very profitable." It combines the prefix eu- ("well" or "good") with chrestos ("useful" or "serviceable"), emphasizing someone or something that is exceptionally beneficial for a specific task, purpose, or relationship. In Philemon 1:11, Paul uses this word to construct a brilliant, grace-filled pun on the name of the runaway slave Onesimus (which literally means "profitable" or "useful"). Once "unprofitable" (achrestos) to Philemon as a fugitive, Onesimus has been transformed by the gospel of Jesus Christ into a brother who is now deeply "profitable" (euchrestos) to both Philemon and Paul. This word highlights how the grace of God redeems and restores broken lives, transforming human liabilities into valuable assets for the kingdom of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The compound structure of euchrestos reveals its rich visual background. The prefix eu- denotes goodness, ease, or abundance, while the root verb chraomai means "to use" or "to employ." Literally, the word describes something that is "easy to make use of" or "eminently usable."

In the Greco-Roman world, slaves were frequently given names that reflected their expected utility or economic value to their masters. "Onesimus" (derived from oninesemi, meaning "to profit" or "to benefit") was a common slave name, essentially translating to "Useful." When Onesimus fled from his master, Philemon, he made a mockery of his name, becoming functionally useless and financially damaging. Paul plays on this irony by contrasting Onesimus's name with his actual behavior, showing how Christ restored the reality behind his name.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Euchrestos is defined as pertaining to being very useful, highly beneficial, or easy to employ for a constructive purpose. It describes a high degree of utility and readiness for service. It stands in direct contrast to:

  • Achrestos (G890), which means "useless" or "unprofitable."

  • Asymphoros, which carries the sense of being disadvantageous or harmful.

The word goes beyond basic utility; it implies a quality of service that brings joy, relief, and genuine benefit to the one who employs or receives it.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term euchrestos appears only three times in the New Testament, each time carrying profound pastoral weight:

  • A Cleansed Vessel (2 Timothy 2:21): Here, the word describes a household vessel that has been cleansed and set apart. It is "useful (euchrestos) to the Master, prepared for every good work." The imagery is of a fine utensil, polished and kept in a prominent, clean place, ready for the master of the house to reach for and use at any moment.

  • A Restored Companion (2 Timothy 4:11): Paul requests that Timothy bring Mark to him in Rome, "for he is useful (euchrestos) to me for ministry." This usage mirrors the situation of Onesimus. John Mark had previously abandoned Paul on a missionary journey, becoming "useless" to Paul's work (Acts 15:37-38). Through restoration, Mark became highly useful once again.

4. Theological Implications

The transition from achrestos (useless) to euchrestos (profitable) serves as a beautiful theological metaphor for the doctrine of regeneration and sanctification:

  • The Transforming Power of the Gospel: Apart from Christ, human lives are spiritually unprofitable, failing to achieve the purpose for which they were created. Redemption changes a person's fundamental utility. The gospel does not merely make us "forgiven"; it makes us "useful" for the purposes of God.

  • Redemption of Identity: Onesimus’s conversion did not just change his behavior; it aligned his character with his identity. In Christ, we are enabled to become who God always intended us to be.

  • Reconciliation and Service: True spiritual fruitfulness is realized within the context of Christian community. Onesimus became profitable not in isolation, but in relationship to his spiritual father (Paul) and his Christian master (Philemon).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:11, Paul writes: "...who once was unprofitable to you, but now is profitable to you and to me."

  • Paul does not minimize Onesimus's past wrongdoing. He frankly admits that Onesimus was once "unprofitable" (achrestos). He may have stolen resources or simply deprived Philemon of valuable labor by running away.

  • The dramatic turn is marked by "but now." The moment Onesimus met Christ through Paul's prison ministry, his trajectory changed. He was no longer a liability.

  • Paul’s defense of Onesimus is deeply personal. He does not merely state that Onesimus is now useful in a general sense, but specifically "profitable to you and to me." The runaway slave had become an invaluable assistant to an apostle in chains, and he was returning to Philemon not as a rebellious servant, but as a beloved brother in Christ who would serve with a completely new heart.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Timothy 2:21 - "Therefore if anyone cleanses himself from the latter, he will be a vessel for honor, sanctified and useful to the Master, prepared for every good work."

  • 2 Timothy 4:11 - "Only Luke is with me. Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for ministry."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a fascinating historical and phonetic resonance to this wordplay. In Koine Greek, the word chrestos ("useful" or "good") was pronounced almost identically to Christos ("Christ") due to a linguistic phenomenon known as itacism. Secular writers of the era often confused the two, assuming that Christians (Christianoi) were actually called "useful ones" (Chrestianoi).

By declaring that Onesimus had changed from achrestos (without use) to euchrestos (very useful), Paul subtly links his usefulness directly to his union with Christos (Christ). To be in Christ is to be made truly useful to the world.
