UNPROFITABLE - ( achrēstos , Strong's G890 )

Summary:

The Greek word achrēstos describes someone or something that is useless, unprofitable, or of no service. In Philemon 1:11, Paul uses this word as a brilliant play on words with the name of the runaway slave, Onesimus, which means "profitable" or "useful." Before his conversion, Onesimus lived up to the term achrēstos—he was unprofitable to his master, Philemon, having likely stolen from him and fled. However, through his encounter with Christ and Paul, Onesimus underwent a radical transformation. This word highlights the power of the gospel to redeem what was once broken, useless, and unprofitable, making it genuinely valuable and fruitful for the kingdom of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • achrēstos is a compound word formed from the alpha privative (a-, meaning "not" or "without") and the noun chrēstos (meaning "useful," "good," or "serviceable," derived from chraomai, "to use"). Therefore, the word literally means "without use" or "unserviceable." In ancient Greco-Roman society, slaves were viewed primarily as economic assets or tools. A slave who fled, neglected his duties, or stole from his master was labeled achrēstos—a liability who failed to perform the function for which he was purchased. Paul's use of this term sets up an intentional, beautiful literary contrast. It plays on the name of the runaway slave himself, Onesimus (which literally means "profitable" or "useful," from onēsimos). Before Christ, Onesimus ("Mr. Useful") was actually achrēstos (useless).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In classical and New Testament Greek, achrēstos refers to that which is useless, unprofitable, worthless, or of no benefit. It describes a person, object, or action that fails to produce a desired or expected result, thereby becoming a burden rather than an asset. It stands in direct contrast to euchrēstos (very useful or profitable) and chrēsimos (useful). The term does not merely imply a passive lack of utility; in a master-slave context, it often carried the active connotation of being detrimental, harmful, or a source of loss.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word achrēstos appears only once in the Greek New Testament (a hapax legomenon), specifically in Philemon 1:11. However, its close relatives and the cultural concepts behind it paint a vivid picture. A related word, achreios (also translated as "unprofitable" or "worthless"), is used by Jesus in the Parable of the Talents to describe the lazy servant who hid his talent in the dirt (Matthew 25:30) and in Luke 17:10 to describe servants who have only done their basic duty. The word picture of achrēstos is that of a broken tool, a dry well, or a barren fruit tree—it occupies space and demands resources but yields absolutely nothing of value in return.

4. Theological Implications

The transition from achrēstos (unprofitable) to euchrēstos (profitable) lies at the very heart of the gospel's transforming power. Apart from Christ, human beings are spiritually unprofitable; we fail to fulfill our created purpose of glorifying God and serving His kingdom. We are liabilities, enslaved to sin and producing only fruit unto death. However, when the grace of God encounters a person, it does not merely improve them; it radically re-creates them. The gospel takes those who are useless to the Kingdom and makes them highly useful. This demonstrates that human worth and utility are not fixed states but are completely subject to the redeeming work of Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:11, Paul writes to Philemon concerning his runaway slave: "who once was unprofitable (achrēstos) to you, but now is profitable (euchrēstos) to you and to me." The immediate context is highly personal and diplomatic. Onesimus had run away, possibly after stealing from Philemon (v. 18). While in Rome, he crossed paths with the imprisoned apostle Paul, who led him to Christ. Paul does not minimize Onesimus's past misconduct; he freely admits that Onesimus was indeed "unprofitable." However, Paul uses this admission to magnify the change. By pairing achrēstos with euchrēstos ("profitable"), Paul highlights that the runaway slave is returning as a completely different man—no longer a worthless runaway, but a beloved brother and a valuable co-laborer in the gospel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because achrēstos is a hapax legomenon (occurring only once in the New Testament), there are no other direct New Testament verses containing this exact Greek word. However, closely related terms for "unprofitable" or "useless" illustrate the same theological concept:

  • Matthew 25:30 – "And cast the unprofitable (achreios) servant into the outer darkness."

  • Luke 17:10 – "So likewise you, when you have done all those things which you are commanded, say, 'We are unprofitable (achreios) servants. We have done what was our duty to do.'"

  • Titus 3:9 – "But avoid foolish disputes, genealogies, contentions, and strivings about the law; for they are unprofitable (anōpheles) and useless."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A subtle phonetic wordplay exists in early Christian literature that may also resonate here. In Greek, the word for "unprofitable" (achrēstos) sounds almost identical to achristos ("without Christ" or "not belonging to Christ"). Conversely, "profitable" (euchrēstos) sounds very similar to euchristos ("good in Christ"). While Paul is primarily playing on the name Onesimus, early readers would have easily heard this second layer of meaning: when Onesimus was achristos (without Christ), he was achrēstos (useless); but now that he is in Christ, he has become truly useful.
