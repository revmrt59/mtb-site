ONCE - (poté, Strong's G4218)

Summary:

The Greek particle pote indicates a past period of time, translated as "once," "formerly," or "at one time." It marks a time frame that has ended, often framing a direct contrast with the present. In Philemon 1:11, pote describes the time before Onesimus met Christ, acknowledging his past unfaithfulness while framing it as a closed chapter in his life.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek writing, pote functioned as an indefinite temporal particle. It was used when an author wanted to refer generally to an earlier time without specifying an exact calendar date. When used in narrative or letter writing, it established historical background by pointing to how things used to be.

In the New Testament, pote frequently serves a clear structural purpose: it introduces a former condition that stands in opposition to a current reality. Writers used it to draw a clear timeline between past behavior or status and present transformation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The particle pote refers to a past time or former state, translated by English terms such as "once," "formerly," "at one time," or "in times past." Depending on context, it can also point forward to an indefinite future time ("ever" or "at last"), but its primary function in narrative and letters is to look backward.

When pointing to the past, pote highlights that a previous condition is no longer active. It does not by itself describe the nature of that past condition; rather, it places that condition strictly in the past, establishing that a change has taken place.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, pote is an essential term in descriptions of spiritual conversion. Apostles regularly used it to remind believers of their former life outside of Christ. By placing "once" at the start of a description, the biblical writers established a firm boundary between who a person used to be and who they are now in the Lord.

This temporal particle frequently sets up the classic gospel structure of "once... but now." It appears in descriptions of former spiritual darkness, former rebellion, or former alienation. In every case, pote anchors the past behavior safely behind the believer, showing that past identity does not define current standing in Christ.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of pote lies in the historical reality of Christian redemption. Conversion is not merely a theoretical shift; it creates a genuine line in a person's history. By placing a person's former habits and spiritual deadness under the heading of "once," Scripture asserts that sin and spiritual failure belong to a former era.

At the same time, pote prevents believers from denying their past while removing the burden of guilt from it. Scripture does not pretend that a believer's past never happened. Instead, it acknowledges the reality of past failure ("once") to magnify the power of present grace ("but now").

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:11, Paul writes to Philemon about Onesimus, a runaway servant who had come to faith under Paul's ministry in prison. Paul writes that Onesimus "once was unprofitable to you, but now is profitable to you and to me."

Paul uses pote to frame Onesimus's past actions honestly. Before his conversion, Onesimus had lived up to the potential of an unfaithful servant, proving useless to his master. Paul does not gloss over this history or pretend that Philemon had suffered no loss. By using pote, Paul acknowledges the truth of the past while firmly locking it in the past.

The particle serves as the doorway to the contrast: Onesimus was once unprofitable, but that time is over. Through faith in Christ, his character and value have been transformed. Paul uses this word to invite Philemon to view Onesimus through the lens of redemption rather than resentment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 9:13 — "They brought him who formerly was blind to the Pharisees."

  • Romans 11:30 — "For as you were once disobedient to God, yet have now obtained mercy through their disobedience..."

  • Ephesians 2:11 — "Therefore remember that you, once Gentiles in the flesh... were at that time without Christ..."

  • Ephesians 5:8 — "For you were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord. Walk as children of light."

  • Titus 3:3 — "For we ourselves were also once foolish, disobedient, deceived, serving various lusts and pleasures..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul engages in a intentional play on words in Philemon 1:11. The name Onesimus literally means "useful" or "profitable." By stating that Onesimus was "once unprofitable," Paul playfully notes that Onesimus had failed to live up to his own name.

However, through the power of the gospel, the man whose conduct once contradicted his name had now become truly useful to both Paul and Philemon. The simple temporal marker pote thus bridges the gap between a broken past and a restored present, showing how divine grace aligns a believer's life with their true identity in Christ.
