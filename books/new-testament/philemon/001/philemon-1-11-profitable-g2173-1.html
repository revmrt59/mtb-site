PROFITABLE - (eúchrēstos, Strong's G2173)

Summary:

The word euchrestos describes someone or something that is highly useful, beneficial, or well-suited for service. In Philemon 1:11, Paul uses it to describe Onesimus, a runaway slave who became a Christian under Paul's ministry. Once unprofitable to his master Philemon, Onesimus was so transformed by Christ that he became genuinely useful both to Philemon and to Paul.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The letter to Philemon centers on a personal situation in the early church. Philemon was a Christian house-church leader in Colosse, and Onesimus was his slave who had run away. In ancient Roman society, a runaway slave was viewed as a financial loss and a societal burden, rendered utterly useless to his master.

While on the run, Onesimus came into contact with the apostle Paul, who was imprisoned. Through Paul’s ministry, Onesimus trusted in Christ. His conversion radically changed his life and character. Paul wrote this short letter to persuade Philemon to welcome Onesimus back—no longer merely as a runaway slave facing punishment, but as a beloved brother in Christ who had become genuinely beneficial to the gospel work.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word euchrestos is built from two basic concepts: "well" or "good," and "useful" or "serviceable." It refers to something that is easy to use, well-adapted for a specific task, or deeply helpful to another person.

When applied to a person, euchrestos means dependable, fit for practical service, and helpful in ministry. It stands in direct contrast to that which is unproductive, harmful, or useless.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In New Testament usage, euchrestos describes people who are fit for honorable service in God's household or helpful to those carrying out gospel ministry. It is not merely a statement about natural skill or talent, but about moral readiness and practical spiritual helpfulness.

In Philemon 1:11, Paul sets euchrestos directly against its opposite, achrestos ("unprofitable" or "useless"). By placing these two words side by side, Paul highlights the dramatic change that occurs when a person is saved and redirected toward God's purposes.

4. Theological Implications

The transformation of Onesimus illustrates the power of divine grace. Salvation does not merely change a person's vertical standing before God; it renovates their character and practical value in daily life and community.

Grace takes what was once broken, unproductive, or harmful and makes it useful for God's kingdom. A person's past failure or unprofitability does not have the final word; when Christ transforms a life, that person becomes fit for meaningful service in God's family.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:11, Paul writes with deep pastoral affection and clever literary warmth. The name Onesimus was a common slave name that literally meant "useful" or "profitable." Yet, before his conversion, Onesimus had failed to live up to his name—he had been achrestos ("unprofitable") to Philemon by running away.

Paul addresses this history directly, writing that Onesimus "who once was unprofitable to you, but now is profitable to you and to me." Through gospel conversion, Onesimus finally fulfilled the meaning of his name. He was no longer a liability; he was now euchrestos ("profitable") to Philemon as a Christian brother and to Paul as a valuable assistant in ministry.

Grammatically, the word appears in the accusative singular masculine form, functioning as an adjective that directly describes Onesimus in his new, redeemed condition.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Timothy 2:21 – "useful" — Paul writes that if a person cleanses himself from dishonorable things, he will be a vessel set apart, "useful to the Master, prepared for every good work."

  • 2 Timothy 4:11 – "useful" — Near the end of his life, Paul asks Timothy to bring Mark with him, noting, "for he is useful to me for ministry," highlighting Mark's restoration to fruitful service after an earlier failure.

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is worth noting the parallel between Onesimus in Philemon 1:11 and John Mark in 2 Timothy 4:11. In both cases, Paul describes a brother using the word euchrestos after a period of initial failure or departure. John Mark had previously deserted Paul on a missionary journey, yet later became deeply "useful" for ministry. Onesimus had run away from his master, yet became "profitable" after meeting Christ. This shows a consistent biblical pattern: gospel restoration equips believers for effective, real-world service.
