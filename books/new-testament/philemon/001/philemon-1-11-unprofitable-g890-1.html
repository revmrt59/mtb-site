UNPROFITABLE - (áchrēstos, Strong's G890)

Summary:

The word achrestos refers to something or someone that is useless, unserviceable, or without practical benefit. In Philemon 1:11, Paul uses achrestos to describe Onesimus in his former status as a runaway bondservant who had caused loss rather than providing faithful service to Philemon, setting up a sharp contrast with the transformation that made him truly valuable.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Apostle Paul wrote his letter to Philemon from prison, addressing a delicate personal matter involving a runaway bondservant named Onesimus. Onesimus had fled from his master Philemon and traveled to Rome, where he encountered Paul and came to faith in Jesus Christ.

In ancient Greek, the word achrestos was used to describe items, tools, or individuals that failed to yield expected value or service. An unserviceable tool, a bad crop, or an unhelpful servant could all be described with this term. Paul brings this word into his letter to describe what Onesimus was like before his conversion, acknowledging the real practical injury and lack of service Philemon had experienced.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word achrestos combines the negative prefix a- ("not" or "without") with a root derived from chrēstos, which means "useful," "good," or "serviceable."

The word refers to that which is useless, unprofitable, unserviceable, or of no benefit. When used of a person, it describes someone whose conduct or performance fails to fulfill an obligation or produce expected value. In this passage, it describes a person whose actions resulted in loss and unfaithfulness rather than helpful service.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The word appears as a masculine singular adjective in the accusative case, directly describing Onesimus in his former condition.

The nuance of the word in this passage depends heavily on a subtle literary wordplay. In the ancient Greco-Roman world, the name Onēsimos was a common name that literally meant "useful" or "profitable." When Onesimus ran away from Philemon, his actions contradicted his own name. Instead of being useful (Onēsimos), he had become useless (achrestos).

Paul does not use the word to suggest that Onesimus lacked inherent worth as a human being created in the image of God. Rather, the term highlights his practical failure and unfaithfulness toward his master prior to his conversion.

4. Theological Implications

The shift described in this passage from being unprofitable to becoming profitable highlights the practical effects of the gospel. Through faith in Christ, a person who was spiritually dead and practically unproductive receives new life and divine purpose.

Salvation does not merely wipe away guilt; it transforms character and conduct. Grace turns a former runaway into a trusted helper, demonstrating that God restores individuals so they can become fruitful in service to Him and to other believers.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:11, Paul places achrestos ("unprofitable") in explicit contrast with euchrestos ("very useful" or "profitable"). He structures the verse around a clear temporal shift: "who once was unprofitable to you, but now is profitable to you and to me."

Paul does not minimize or deny Onesimus's past wrong. By using achrestos, Paul openly acknowledges that Onesimus had been a source of loss and disappointment to Philemon. However, the contrast emphasizes that conversion changed Onesimus completely. The man who was once useless in Philemon's household had been remade by God's grace, making him valuable not only to Paul during his imprisonment but also to Philemon as a renewed servant and a brother in Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • This term is unique to this context and does not appear elsewhere in the biblical corpus.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's wordplay between unprofitable (achrestos) and profitable (euchrestos) rests on the root word chrēstos ("useful" or "good"). In ancient Greek pronunciation, chrēstos sounded very similar to Christos ("Christ"). While Paul's primary point is a play on the name Onesimus, early Christian readers recognized that living in real union with Christ (Christos) is what truly transforms an unprofitable life into one that is good and useful (chrēstos).
