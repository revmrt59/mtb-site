SENDING HIM BACK - ( anapestreo , Strong's G375 )

Summary:

The word anapestreo carries the core meaning of turning back or returning something to its place or owner. In Philemon 1:12, Paul uses this term to describe his deliberate and loving action of sending the runaway slave Onesimus back to his master Philemon, transforming a potentially punitive return into a profound act of Christian reconciliation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The term is built upon roots involving motion and turning, capturing the physical action of reversing direction or bringing someone back to where they previously belonged.

  • In the ancient Greco-Roman world, the return of a runaway slave was typically fraught with danger, as masters possessed absolute legal authority to punish or execute fugitive slaves.

  • Paul deliberately reframes this standard cultural practice by turning a legal obligation into an expression of deep spiritual fellowship and mutual Christian love.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Defined primarily as turning back, returning, or sending back someone or something to a previous location or person.

  • It signifies not just a movement in space, but a purposeful restoration or restitution.

  • The verb emphasizes the completed action of leading or sending an individual back to their proper authority or home.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The word pictures inherent in the term emphasize movement directed backward—reversing a journey.

  • For Onesimus, the physical journey away from Colosse in rebellion is now deliberately inverted by this word; he retraces his steps physically, but spiritually he returns as an entirely new creation.

  • The vocabulary underscores Paul's submission to earthly order while simultaneously elevating that order through the higher law of Christian brotherhood.

4. Theological Implications

  • This act serves as a vivid New Testament picture of reconciliation, echoing the broader gospel theme of restoration between alienated parties.

  • Just as Paul intercedes and sends Onesimus back, Christ reconciles believers to God, not counting their trespasses against them, yet placing them securely within their proper relationship to the Father.

  • It demonstrates how the gospel transforms social structures from within by re-envisioning human relationships through the lens of grace rather than coercion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • Paul describes Onesimus as his very heart (splanchnon), making the act of sending him back a personal sacrifice for the apostle.

  • The nuance is one of tender care; Paul is not merely offloading a legal liability or an inconvenient runaway, but entrusting a beloved spiritual son back to his master with a transformed status.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • The specific Greek verb anapestreo (G375) appears only here in the New Testament (Philemon 1:12), making it a unique lexical contribution by Paul to describe this specific act of restoration. (Note: While related root words involving strepho or anatrepho appear elsewhere, this exact compound form is a hapax legomenon in the New Testament corpus).

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Paul's choice of action highlights the practical ethics of the gospel: grace does not abolish earthly responsibilities (such as returning to one's master), but it radically transforms the spirit in which those responsibilities are met.

  • The verse showcases the delicate pastoral wisdom of Paul, who refuses to bypass Philemon's legal rights, yet gently maneuvers Philemon into a position where he must respond with Christlike grace.
