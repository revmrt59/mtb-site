HEART - ( splagchnon , Strong's G4698 )

Summary:

In Philemon 1:12, the word translated "heart" refers quite literally to the vital internal organs—specifically the bowels, stomach, or upper viscera—which ancient people viewed as the seat of deep human emotion, affection, and compassion. Paul uses this deeply visceral term to describe Onesimus as a part of his own very soul, urging Philemon to welcome his runaway slave back with the same profound, heartfelt affection that Paul feels for him.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • In ancient anatomical understanding, particularly in the Greco-Roman and Semitic worlds, the inner organs (such as the liver, heart, lungs, and bowels) were considered the physical headquarters of emotional life.

  • While modern Western culture locates emotion primarily in the abstract "heart" or the brain, ancient writers used the term for these visceral organs to describe the sudden, physical sensation of deep feeling—such as the churning of the stomach during grief, anxiety, or profound love.

  • The term carries a raw, earthy intimacy, moving far beyond polite surface affection to describe a bond rooted in the deepest level of human existence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Splagchnon refers fundamentally to the internal organs or viscera (the heart, lungs, liver).

  • Figuratively, it denotes the seat of the emotions, specifically tender affection, compassion, sympathy, and heartfelt mercy.

  • In Christian literature, it frequently represents the inward wellspring of Christian love and empathy produced by the indwelling Spirit.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The word is often translated in various English versions as "bowels," "affections," "heart," or "tender mercies."

  • Paul uses it to convey a love that is not merely dutiful or theoretical, but deeply felt and emotionally gripping.

  • By calling Onesimus his very splagchnon, Paul paints a picture of a man so intertwined with his spiritual son that sending Onesimus away is akin to tearing a vital organ from his own body.

4. Theological Implications

  • This term models the radical reordering of human relationships brought about by the Gospel, where master and slave become beloved brothers sharing one spiritual anatomy in Christ.

  • It reflects the divine compassion of God, whose mercies toward humanity spring from His own inner being.

  • Christian fellowship is portrayed not as an institutional association, but as a visceral, organic union where the pain or joy of one believer is physically and emotionally felt by another.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • Paul is writing a delicate letter of intercession to Philemon regarding Onesimus, a runaway slave who has been converted to Christ under Paul's ministry.

  • By sending Onesimus back "you, that is, my own heart," Paul disarms Philemon's potential anger and demands a reception marked by extraordinary grace.

  • The appeal relies entirely on relational solidarity: to reject or punish Onesimus harshly would be to inflict pain directly upon Paul's own inner self.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Corinthians 6:12 - "You are not restricted by us, but you are restricted in your own affections."

  • 2 Corinthians 7:15 - "And his affections are greater for you as he remembers the obedience of you all..."

  • Philippians 1:8 - "For God is my witness, how greatly I long for you all with the affection of Jesus Christ."

  • Colossians 3:12 - "Therefore, as the elect of God, holy and beloved, put on tender mercies, kindness, humility, meekness, longsuffering..."

  • Philemon 1:7 - "For we have great joy and consolation in your love, because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The related verb form (splagchnizomai) is frequently used in the Gospels to describe the deep, gut-level compassion Jesus felt when looking upon the crowds, the sick, and the grieving.

  • Paul's use of this word in Philemon elevates the restoration of a runaway slave from a legal or social transaction into an act of profound, heartfelt Christian love.
