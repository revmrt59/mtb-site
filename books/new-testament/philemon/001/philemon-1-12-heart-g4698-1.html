HEART - (splánchnon, Strong's G4698)

Summary:

The Greek word splagchnon originally referred to the internal organs of the body—the heart, lungs, liver, and intestines. In ancient Mediterranean culture, these deep inner organs were viewed as the center of intense emotion, particularly deep compassion, tender mercy, and personal affection. In Philemon 1:12, Paul uses this word metaphorically to describe Onesimus, calling him his "very heart." By using this tender expression, Paul tells Philemon that welcoming back his former runaway servant is equivalent to receiving Paul's own deepest personal love and inner life.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek, splagchnon referred to the vital inner organs located in the chest and abdomen. In both Greek culture and Hebrew thought, people did not associate deep emotion primarily with the brain or heart alone; they associated intense feelings with the lower internal organs. Modern English uses the word "heart" or phrases like "gut feeling" to convey deep emotional depth, but the ancient world used the internal organs to describe the site of strongest feeling.

In the New Testament period, this word moved from its basic physical meaning to describe deep compassion, warm affection, and tender mercy. The Epistle to Philemon was written while Paul was imprisoned. Onesimus, a slave belonging to a Christian leader named Philemon in Colosse, had run away and eventually met Paul. Under Paul's ministry, Onesimus became a follower of Jesus Christ and a trusted helper. Paul wrote this short, personal letter to send Onesimus back to Philemon, appealing to Philemon to forgive Onesimus and welcome him as a beloved brother in Christ.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word splagchnon literally means the internal organs or noble viscera of the body. In figurative usage, it carries several related meanings:

  • Deep compassion, mercy, or pity.

  • Warm affection, heartfelt love, or emotional attachment.

  • The seat or source of personal affection within a person.

In Philemon 1:12, Paul uses the word as a figure of speech for a person who is the object of intense love. By calling Onesimus "my very heart," Paul expresses that his love for Onesimus is so deep that Onesimus feels like part of Paul's own inner being.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, splagchnon expresses the deepest level of affection and mercy known in human and divine experience. It describes the tender mercy of God that brought salvation to the world, as well as the deep spiritual bond that unites believers in the church.

In the Epistle to Philemon, this word plays a prominent role in Paul's argument:

  • In Philemon 1:7, Paul commends Philemon because the "hearts" of the believers had been refreshed through his ministry.

  • In Philemon 1:12, Paul calls Onesimus his "very heart" as he asks Philemon to receive him.

  • In Philemon 1:20, Paul asks Philemon to refresh his "heart" in Christ by granting his request.

This repeated use links the spiritual health of the church with deep, felt affection and active reconciliation among believers.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of splagchnon in Philemon reveals how the gospel transforms personal relationships and social structures.

In ancient society, a runaway slave had no legal standing and faced severe punishment upon return. Yet through faith in Jesus Christ, Onesimus became Paul's spiritual son and was described as Paul's "very heart." This highlights the reality of adoption into God's family. In Christ, social divisions disappear, replaced by genuine brotherly affection and spiritual unity.

Furthermore, Paul's language demonstrates that Christian fellowship is not merely a legal duty or administrative arrangement. It is rooted in Christlike compassion that values every individual believer as a precious member of the body of Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:12, Paul writes: "whom I have sent back. You therefore receive him, that is, my very heart."

Paul does not demand that Philemon obey an apostolic order. Instead, he appeals to Philemon's love. By describing Onesimus as his "very heart," Paul transfers his own personal standing to Onesimus. For Philemon to reject Onesimus would be to inflict pain on Paul himself; to welcome Onesimus would be to welcome Paul.

Grammatically, the noun appears in the neuter plural accusative form (ta ema splagchna), functioning as the direct object in the sentence ("receive him, that is, my very heart"). The plural form was the standard way to express this deep emotional concept in ancient Greek. Paul uses it here to place the highest possible value on Onesimus, ensuring that Philemon sees his returning servant through the lens of divine grace and deep apostolic love.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structure of Paul's letter to Philemon centers on the idea of refreshing the inner spirit of believers. Philemon had established a reputation for refreshing the hearts (splagchna) of God's people in Colosse (verse 7). When Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus—Paul's "very heart" (verse 12)—he is asking Philemon to put that same ministering spirit into practice toward a former runaway.

Finally, when Paul asks Philemon in verse 20 to "refresh my heart in the Lord," he closes the circle of his appeal. By forgiving Onesimus, Philemon would bring comfort and refreshment to Paul's own inner spirit, proving that gospel grace produces real emotional warmth and practical reconciliation in the Christian community.
