OWN - (emós, Strong's G1699)

Summary:

Emos is a possessive pronoun and adjective meaning "my," "mine," or "my own." It specifies intense or distinct personal ownership, origin, or affection, distinguishing what belongs to the speaker from what belongs to another. In Philemon 1:12, Paul uses emos when referring to the runaway servant Onesimus as his "own heart," expressing a deep personal tie that unites Paul and Onesimus as one.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word emos functions as a possessive term of the first person singular in Greek. While general possessive senses can be expressed by simple pronouns, emos draws direct attention to the speaker's personal identity, possession, or unique relationship to the object described. It highlights a claim of special personal connection, as in "that which is specifically mine."

In the letter to Philemon, the apostle Paul writes from prison regarding Onesimus, a runaway bonded worker who had come to faith under Paul’s ministry. Paul sends Onesimus back to his original master, Philemon, who was also a believer. To ensure that Philemon receives Onesimus with Christian love rather than resentment or punishment, Paul describes Onesimus using emos.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Emos can refer to:

  • Something personally owned or held by the speaker ("my own property").

  • A personal quality, desire, or purpose originating from the speaker ("my own will," "my own judgment").

  • A person or object held in close, personal relationship or deep affection ("my own").

The word denotes more than mere legal possession. When applied to people or inner emotions, it highlights personal identification, commitment, and heartfelt attachment.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the New Testament, emos frequently distinguishes what belongs to Jesus or an apostle from what belongs to the world or other individuals.

For example, Jesus uses the term to differentiate His own doctrine, peace, joy, and judgment from human traditions or worldly standards. It underscores divine origin and personal authority. When used by Paul, the word often indicates the deep spiritual bond he shares with his converts or fellow workers, treating their spiritual well-being as tied to his own identity.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of emos in pastoral contexts highlights the transformative nature of Christian fellowship. In the gospel, believers are not isolated individuals; they are bound together in shared life.

When Paul calls another believer his "own," it shows how spiritual rebirth creates relationships as deep and meaningful as family ties. In the ancient Roman world, a slave was viewed legally as property without personal status. By placing the possessive emos alongside the word for inner affection, Paul reshapes social realities through gospel grace, showing that every Christian belongs to the household of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:12, Paul writes that he is sending Onesimus back to Philemon, adding the description: "that is, my own heart."

The word emos appears here in the neuter plural accusative form to match the underlying Greek term for inner deep affections or heart (splagchna). Paul does not treat Onesimus as a mere servant returning to fulfill an obligation. Instead, Paul describes Onesimus as an extension of his own physical body and deep personal feelings.

By telling Philemon that Onesimus is his "own heart," Paul makes a strong implicit appeal. To welcome Onesimus is to welcome Paul himself; to reject or mistreat Onesimus would be to inflict pain on Paul’s own heart. The word emos turns what could have been a simple legal return into a personal test of Christian love and fellowship.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 25:27 — "So you ought to have deposited my money with the bankers, and at my coming I would have received back my own with interest."

  • John 5:30 — "I can of Myself do nothing. As I hear, I judge; and My judgment is righteous, because I do not seek My own will but the will of the Father who sent Me."

  • John 14:27 — "Peace I leave with you, My peace I give to you; not as the world gives do I give to you."

  • 2 Corinthians 2:3 — "And I wrote this very thing to you, lest, when I came, I should have sorrow over those from whom I ought to have joy, having confidence in you all that my joy is the joy of you all."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The expression "my own heart" demonstrates Paul's personal approach to Christian leadership. Rather than relying on apostolic authority to order Philemon's compliance, Paul uses the personal force of emos to appeal directly to Philemon's conscience and love. This shows how deep personal commitment and affection serve as the primary motivation for Christian unity and restoration.
