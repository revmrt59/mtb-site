RECEIVE - (proslambánō, Strong's G4355)

Summary:

The word proslambano means to take someone or something to oneself, to welcome a person into one's company, or to accept someone into close fellowship. In Philemon 1:12, Paul commands Philemon to welcome his formerly runaway servant Onesimus back into his household, receiving him not as a disgraced offender or mere piece of property, but with personal warmth as Paul's very heart.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the Greco-Roman world, the relationship between a slave master and a runaway servant was governed by strict legal custom. A servant who departed without permission could face harsh punishment, imprisonment, or severe legal penalties upon return. When the apostle Paul wrote his letter to Philemon, he addressed a delicate and legally complex situation. Onesimus had fled from his master Philemon, but while away, he encountered Paul, heard the gospel, and became a follower of Jesus Christ.

Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon in Colosse, but not without an earnest written appeal. Rather than relying solely on legal right or social custom, Paul framed the request within the warmth of Christian fellowship. The word chosen for "receive" describes far more than opening a door or acknowledging someone's physical arrival; it describes welcoming a person warmly into one's home, circle, or heart.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word proslambano combines the basic idea of taking or receiving with an emphasis on taking someone to oneself. It can refer to taking physical items, taking a person aside, or receiving someone into fellowship and hospitality.

Depending on the context, the word can mean:

  • To take along or take beside oneself, such as taking a companion along on a journey.

  • To take food or nourishment into the body.

  • To take someone aside privately for conversation.

  • To accept or welcome someone into fellowship, household hospitality, or personal relationship.

In Philemon 1:12, the word carries the sense of personal acceptance and welcoming hospitality. Paul uses the command form to direct Philemon to take Onesimus back into his home and life with genuine acceptance.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, proslambano appears in several distinct settings that clarify its meaning:

First, it can describe taking someone aside for private speech. When Peter pulled Jesus away from the other disciples to rebuke Him concerning His coming suffering, this word describes that physical and social action of taking Him aside.

Second, it describes hospitality and welcome. When Paul was shipwrecked on the island of Malta, the islanders showed unusual kindness and welcomed the survivors around a warm fire. In that setting, the word highlights genuine, hospitable reception of strangers.

Third, the word carries significant spiritual weight in Paul's letters when describing how believers ought to treat one another. In Romans, Paul uses the word to urge believers to receive those who are weak in faith, and to welcome one another just as Christ received believers. In these contexts, the word goes beyond mere tolerance; it demands active, affectionate inclusion into the Christian community.

4. Theological Implications

Paul's request in Philemon 1:12 sits at the intersection of Christian reconciliation and the transforming power of the gospel. By asking Philemon to receive Onesimus, Paul calls for a complete restructuring of social relationships in light of Christ.

  • Grace Overcomes Barrier and Penalty: Under Roman law, Philemon had the right to penalize Onesimus. Paul's command to receive him calls Philemon to exercise the same grace God showed to him in Christ.

  • Reconciliation in the Household of Faith: Onesimus left as a servant, but he returned as a brother in Christ. Paul asks Philemon to welcome him as an extension of Paul's own life and ministry, showing that shared faith in Jesus forms a new, binding brotherhood.

  • The Mirror of Divine Acceptance: Just as Christ takes sinners to Himself and welcomes them into God's family, Christians are called to receive fellow believers with open arms, regardless of past wrongs or social barriers.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:12, Paul writes, "whom I have sent back. You therefore receive him, that is, my own heart." The grammatical structure expresses a direct, personal directive to Philemon, urging immediate personal compliance.

Paul reinforces this request with deep emotional language. He describes Onesimus as "my own heart," showing that any reception given to Onesimus is viewed by Paul as given directly to himself. By linking Onesimus's return to his own heart, Paul removes any possibility that Philemon could receive Onesimus coldly or grudgingly.

A few verses later, in verse 17, Paul uses the same word again to drive the point home: "receive him as you would me." Thus, the word in verse 12 lays the groundwork for complete reconciliation. Philemon is asked to extend full, restorative hospitality to a man who once caused him loss, treating him with the same warmth he would show to the apostle Paul himself.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 28:2 — "And the natives showed us unusual kindness; for they kindled a fire and welcomed us all, because of the rain that had begun and because of the cold."

  • Romans 14:1 — "Receive one who is weak in the faith, but not to disputes over doubtful things."

  • Romans 15:7 — "Therefore receive one another, just as Christ also received us, to the glory of God."

  • Philemon 1:17 — "If then you count me as a partner, receive him as you would me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The distinction between mere social tolerance and biblical reception is clearly illustrated by proslambano. In first-century society, a slave owner might allow a returned runaway back onto the property out of economic necessity while keeping him isolated or under heavy punishment. That would not satisfy Paul's directive.

By employing proslambano, Paul demands a deliberate act of the will that brings the individual into personal closeness and fellowship. The word emphasizes the initiative of the recipient to draw the other person near. For Philemon, obeying this call meant publicly restoring Onesimus, setting aside personal resentment, and demonstrating to his entire household that Christ brings former enemies into harmonious brotherhood.
