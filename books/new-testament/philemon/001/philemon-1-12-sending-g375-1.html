SENDING - (anapémpō, Strong's G375)

Summary:

Anapempo combines the prefix ana ("back" or "up") with pempo ("to send"), meaning to send back, send up to a higher authority, or return someone to their original location. In Philemon 1:12, Paul uses this word to describe returning the runaway servant Onesimus to his master Philemon. By sending him back, Paul demonstrates a commitment to godly integrity, real-world restitution, and gospel-centered reconciliation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Greek word anapempo is a compound verb. The root verb pempo means "to send," while the prefix ana adds the direction "up" or "back." In legal and civil contexts in the ancient world, the word often described sending a person back to an original jurisdiction, transferring an accused individual to a superior magistrate, or returning a servant to their owner.

When Paul writes to Philemon, he uses this term within a deeply personal letter. Onesimus, a bondservant who had fled from Philemon's household in Colosse, had encountered Paul while Paul was imprisoned and had become a believer in Jesus Christ. Although Onesimus had become immensely helpful to Paul during his imprisonment, Paul chooses to send him back to Philemon, showing that spiritual conversion leads to practical honesty and restored relationships.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Anapempo can refer to sending a person back to where they came from, sending someone up to a higher authority or legal tribunal, or returning an individual to their master or home.

In biblical usage, the word carries two primary nuances depending on the context:

1. Legal transfer: Sending an accused person to another authority or magistrate for trial or questioning.

2. Personal return: Sending someone back to the person or location to whom they have a duty or prior relationship.

In Philemon 1:12, the word means to send back or return. Paul is returning Onesimus to Philemon's household accompanied by this letter of appeal.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses anapempo in official, legal, and relational contexts.

In Luke's Gospel, the word describes legal proceedings during the trial of Jesus Christ. Pontius Pilate sent Jesus to Herod Antipas because Jesus fell under Herod's regional jurisdiction. After questioning Him, Herod sent Him back to Pilate. In those instances, the word highlights formal civil transfers between authorities who found no grounds for capital punishment.

In Paul's letter to Philemon, the word shifts from a courtroom transfer to a pastoral act of reconciliation. Paul does not treat Onesimus as a mere legal case or fugitive, but as a transformed brother in Christ. By sending him back, Paul respects social and legal realities while inviting Philemon to transform those relationships through Christian love.

4. Theological Implications

The use of anapempo in Philemon 1:12 highlights vital truths about salvation, repentance, and reconciliation:

First, spiritual regeneration does not erase earthly responsibilities. Becoming a believer changed Onesimus's heart and spiritual standing before God, but it did not eliminate his need to make things right with Philemon. True repentance leads believers to address past wrongs where possible.

Second, God's grace reshapes human relationships. Paul sends Onesimus back not as a slave to be punished, but as a beloved brother in the Lord. The gospel enters real human social structures and elevates them, uniting people across former social divides in Christian love.

Third, Paul models Christlike advocacy. In sending Onesimus back, Paul offers to personally cover any financial loss or debt Onesimus incurred, reflecting how Christ bore our debts to restore us to God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:12, Paul writes that he is sending Onesimus back. The verb form presents the action from the perspective of Philemon receiving the letter, as the return of Onesimus is taking place alongside the delivery of the message.

nutritional and pastoral weight is given to this act by the surrounding context. Paul calls Onesimus "my own heart." Sending Onesimus back was not a casual or cold obligation for Paul; it was a deeply costly act because Paul had grown spiritually attached to Onesimus as his son in the faith.

By sending Onesimus back with this letter, Paul sets up a powerful appeal. He sends back a man who was once unprofitable to Philemon, but who now returns as a faithful brother, profitable to both Paul and Philemon. The act of sending him back allows Philemon the opportunity to receive him willingly and graciously.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Roman law, runaway servants faced severe legal penalties, including branding, harsh imprisonment, or physical punishment. By using anapempo, Paul formally returns Onesimus, yet he places the entire situation under the spiritual banner of Christian forgiveness and brotherhood.

Additionally, Paul plays on Onesimus's name in the broader passage. The name Onesimus literally means "useful" or "profitable." Before his conversion, Onesimus had failed to live up to that name; but in sending him back, Paul reassures Philemon that Onesimus is returning as a truly useful brother in the Lord.
