GOSPEL - (euangélion, Strong's G2098)

Summary:

The word euangelion fundamentally means "good news" or "glad tidings." In the New Testament, it refers to the announcement of God's salvation through the life, death, resurrection, and lordship of Jesus Christ. In Philemon 1:13, Paul uses the word to explain the reason for his imprisonment, describing his bondage as "chains for the gospel." This highlights that Paul's suffering was a direct result of proclaiming Christ, while also showing that serving Paul in prison was a direct contribution to the gospel mission itself.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek society outside the Bible, the word euangelion originally referred to a reward given to a messenger who brought good news, or to the good news itself. It was often used to announce major public events, such as a military victory, the birth of a ruler, or the accession of an emperor.

In the biblical world, the word took on a much richer meaning. It became the supreme term for the joyful announcement that God's promised King had arrived to save His people. When Paul wrote his letter to Philemon, he was suffering in prison for preaching this message. In this short personal letter, Paul appeals to Philemon on behalf of Onesimus, a runaway slave who had come to faith under Paul's ministry. Paul explains that while he was in prison, Onesimus had become a faithful helper to him.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Euangelion means "good news," "glad tidings," or "a joyful message."

While the word could occasionally refer to any good news in everyday ancient life, the New Testament almost exclusively uses it for the specific message of Jesus Christ. It refers to:

  • The announcement of the arrival of God's kingdom.

  • The good news of salvation offered through Christ's death and resurrection.

  • The whole body of Christian truth regarding God's redemptive plan.

In Philemon 1:13, the word specifically identifies the Christian message for which Paul was imprisoned.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, euangelion carries a sense of joyful, triumphant proclamation. Jesus began His ministry by declaring the "gospel of the kingdom," calling people to repent and believe the good news.

After Jesus' resurrection, the apostles used the term to summarize the core truth about Christ: that He died for sins, was buried, rose again, and is Lord over all. Paul frequently speaks of serving the gospel, guarding the gospel, and suffering for the gospel. For Paul, the gospel is not an abstract philosophy; it is a living message empowered by God to transform lives and reunite broken relationships.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical idea of the gospel carries several key theological truths:

  • The gospel is an announcement of facts: It tells what God has accomplished in history through Jesus Christ, rather than offering human advice or moral self-improvement.

  • The gospel carries divine authority: Because it originates from God, it requires faith, allegiance, and obedience from all who hear it.

  • The gospel worthily demands sacrifice: Proclaiming the gospel can bring opposition, as seen in Paul's imprisonment. Suffering for the gospel does not mean the message has failed; rather, God often uses the trials of His messengers to advance His truth.

  • The gospel transforms human relationships: In the context of Philemon, the gospel breaks down cultural and social barriers, making a slave-owner and a runaway slave brothers in Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:13, Paul writes that he would have liked to keep Onesimus with him so that Onesimus could serve him in prison "in my chains for the gospel."

The grammatical form of the word connects Paul's physical chains directly to the gospel message. Paul was not in prison as a lawbreaker or criminal; his imprisonment was the direct consequence of preaching Christ.

By framing his imprisonment as "chains for the gospel," Paul introduces a subtle pastoral reminder. Serving Paul in prison was not merely doing a personal favor for an elderly friend; it was supporting the cause of the gospel itself. Onesimus's practical service in the prison cell was an active participation in Christ's ongoing mission.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Mark 1:15 — "and saying, 'The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand. Repent, and believe in the gospel.'"

  • Romans 1:9 — "For God is my witness, whom I serve with my spirit in the gospel of His Son..."

  • Galatians 1:7 — "...which is not another; but there are some who trouble you and want to pervert the gospel of Christ."

  • Philippians 1:12 — "But I want you to know, brethren, that the things which happened to me have actually turned out for the furtherance of the gospel,"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • In his prison letters, Paul repeatedly emphasizes that physical walls cannot stop the gospel message. Even while chained, Paul viewed his situation as an opportunity for the message to spread.

  • Practical assistance given to those who suffer for Christ is treated in Scripture as a direct partnership in the gospel. When Onesimus served Paul in prison, he was serving the minister of the gospel and thus contributing to the work of the gospel itself.

  • The letter to Philemon demonstrates that the gospel does not merely change a person's eternal destination; it fundamentally alters how believers view and treat one another in everyday life.
