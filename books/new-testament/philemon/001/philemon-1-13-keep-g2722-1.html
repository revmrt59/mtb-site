KEEP - (katéchō, Strong's G2722)

Summary:

The Greek verb katecho basic lexical meaning is to hold firm, retain, possess, or hold back. Depending on the context, it can describe holding fast to truth, detaining a person, or suppressing information. In Philemon 1:13, Paul uses the word to express his personal desire to retain Onesimus with him in Rome so that Onesimus could assist him during his imprisonment on Philemon's behalf.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Apostle Paul wrote his brief letter to Philemon while imprisoned for preaching the gospel. During his imprisonment, Paul met Onesimus, a runaway bondservant who belonged to Philemon, a Christian leader in Colosse. Through Paul's witness, Onesimus came to faith in Jesus Christ and began serving Paul faithfully during his incarceration.

In the culture of the ancient Roman Empire, holding or retaining another person's servant could easily trigger legal disputes over property rights. However, Paul frames the situation not through legal friction, but through the lens of gospel fellowship and mutual love. Before asking Philemon to receive Onesimus back as a beloved brother, Paul openly shares his personal preference: he deeply wished to keep Onesimus at his side to assist him in prison.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word katecho is a compound verb built from a directional prefix implying thoroughness combined with the standard verb for having or holding. It can refer to:

  • Holding fast or retaining something desirable.

  • Detaining or keeping a person from leaving.

  • Possessing property or holding a position.

  • Restraining or suppressing something.

In everyday ancient usage, the term was applied to holding on to physical property, retaining guests in a home, or detaining someone in custody. In Philemon 1:13, the word means to retain or keep a companion nearby rather than sending him away.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, katecho carries several distinct nuances based on what is being held or restrained:

  • Holding fast to spiritual truth: Believers are repeatedly exhorted to hold fast to the word of God, sound traditions, and their confession of faith without wavering.

  • Detaining or keeping a person: The word describes keeping someone present, as when a crowd attempted to keep Jesus from departing, or when Paul desired to keep Onesimus with him.

  • Suppressing or restraining: The word is used for people who suppress God's truth in unrighteousness, as well as for a force that restrains lawlessness.

  • Possessing temporal goods: It describes holding or possessing material items and earthly status.

4. Theological Implications

Paul's use of katecho reveals an essential Christian principle regarding freedom and love. Paul felt a strong practical need and personal affection that made him want to keep Onesimus. Onesimus was providing valuable assistance to an elderly apostle in chains.

However, Paul refused to act on his personal desire without Philemon's voluntary consent. Gospel ministry relies on willing partnership rather than forced obligation. By choosing not to keep Onesimus unilaterally, Paul demonstrated that love does not demand its own rights or shortcut Christian courtesy. True fellowship honors the conscience and voluntary generosity of fellow believers.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:13–14, Paul writes, "whom I wished to keep with me, that on your behalf he might minister to me in my chains for the gospel. But without your consent I wanted to do nothing."

The word "keep" highlights how valuable Onesimus had become to Paul's personal well-being and gospel work. Onesimus was serving Paul on Philemon's behalf, rendering the kind of care Philemon himself would have offered had he been present in Rome.

Yet, Paul deliberately refrained from keeping him. By placing his wish to retain Onesimus alongside his refusal to act without consent, Paul set up a powerful appeal. He showed Philemon how deeply he valued Onesimus, making his decision to send him back all the more meaningful. Paul sent Onesimus back so that any good service Philemon provided would come from a willing heart, not from compulsion.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 8:15 — "But the ones that fell on the good ground are those who, having heard the word with a noble and good heart, keep it and bear fruit with patience."

  • Romans 1:18 — "For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who suppress the truth in unrighteousness."

  • 1 Corinthians 11:2 — "Now I praise you, brethren, that you remember me in all things and keep the traditions just as I delivered them to you."

  • 1 Thessalonians 5:21 — "Test all things; hold fast what is good."

  • Hebrews 10:23 — "Let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering, for He who promised is faithful."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Philemon 1:13, katecho appears as an infinitive attached to Paul's expression of personal intention ("I wished to keep"). Grammatically, the form expresses the action Paul contemplated taking. This choice of words underlines that retaining Onesimus was a genuine, realistic possibility that Paul intentionally set aside out of respect for Philemon and devotion to gospel principles.
