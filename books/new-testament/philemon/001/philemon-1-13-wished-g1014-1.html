WISHED - (boúlomai, Strong's G1014)

Summary:

The word refers to wishing, desiring, intending, or purposing to do something.

In Philemon 1:13, the word expresses Paul's personal desire and inclination to retain Onesimus in Rome as a helper during his imprisonment, an inclination Paul willingly sets aside to respect Philemon's voluntary choice.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb boulomai describes conscious intention, deliberate choice, or personal wish. In ancient usage, it often implied a considered decision or desire rather than a mere emotional impulse. It carries the sense of having a mind or plan to pursue a specific outcome.

In the Greco-Roman world, the term was used in legal, political, and ordinary everyday contexts to express a person's deliberate resolve or official wish. In the New Testament, it is used both for human intentions and for the sovereign determination of God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Boulomai can mean to wish, desire, intend, plan, or purpose.

When applied to people, the word describes an inclination, goal, or preference. It indicates what someone wants to happen or what they plan to do.

When applied to God, the word describes His divine counsel, sovereign choice, or purposeful intent. God's boulomai reflects His sovereign will and unforced determination.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, boulomai spans a range of intentionality:

  • Human intention or preference: A person formulates a plan or desires a particular outcome, such as Joseph considering how to handle his marriage to Mary, or Paul planning a travel route.

  • Authoritative decision: A ruler or official resolves upon a course of action, such as Pilate deciding to satisfy a crowd.

  • Sovereign divine purpose: God acts according to His own absolute design, such as Christ choosing to reveal the Father, or the Holy Spirit distributing gifts according to His divine choice.

In pastoral and personal contexts, human desires designated by boulomai remain subject to providence, Christian wisdom, and love for others.

4. Theological Implications

The distinction between divine intention and human intention is significant when examining boulomai. God's sovereign will is always effective and aligned with His holy character. Human intention, even when expressed by an apostle, must submit to God's higher purposes and operate within the bounds of Christian love.

Paul's use of boulomai in his personal letters demonstrates that Christian leadership does not steamroll the free response of others. Even when a leader's desire is good and helpful, the gospel invites believers to act out of willing love rather than compulsion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:13, Paul writes from prison to Philemon regarding Onesimus, a slave who had fled from Philemon but became a Christian under Paul's ministry. While in Rome, Onesimus proved to be an invaluable assistant to Paul during his imprisonment.

Paul uses the word boulomai to express what he personally wished to do: "whom I wished to keep with me, that on your behalf he might minister to me in my chains for the gospel." The phrasing captures Paul's sincere desire and inclination to keep Onesimus by his side.

However, Paul immediately balances this wish in the very next verse by stating that he wanted to do nothing without Philemon's consent. Paul transparently discloses his initial desire (boulomai), yet refrains from turning that personal desire into a demand. By stating what he "wished," Paul lets Philemon know how valuable Onesimus had become, while granting Philemon full freedom to act out of love rather than constraint.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:19 – Joseph was "minded" to put Mary away secretly, expressing a considered personal plan before the Lord directed him otherwise.

  • Luke 10:22 – Jesus declares that no one knows the Father except the Son, and anyone to whom the Son "wills" to reveal Him.

  • 1 Corinthians 12:11 – The Holy Spirit distributes spiritual gifts to each individual believer as He "wills".

  • 2 Corinthians 1:15 – Paul uses the word to explain his travel plans, writing that he "intended" to visit the believers at Corinth first.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's transparency in Philemon 1:13 provides a model of ministry diplomacy and Christian humility. Rather than concealing his preference or quietly enforcing it, Paul openly tells Philemon what he wished to do. This honesty validates the usefulness of Onesimus while leaving room for Philemon to make a genuine, unforced decision. It shows how Christian relationships prioritize voluntary love over apostolic pressure.
