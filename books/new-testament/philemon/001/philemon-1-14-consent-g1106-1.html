CONSENT - (gnṓmē, Strong's G1106)

Summary:

The word gnōmē refers to a person's mind, opinion, counsel, judgment, or deliberate consent. It describes an active determination or thoughtful decision made by an individual or a group. In Philemon 1:14, the word refers to Philemon's personal consent and deliberate approval. Paul refuses to retain Onesimus to assist him in prison without Philemon's willing agreement, ensuring that Philemon's generosity springs from a free, willing heart rather than apostolic pressure.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The letter to Philemon is one of Paul's most personal letters. Writing from prison, Paul addresses Philemon, a Christian leader in Colosse. Philemon’s bondservant, Onesimus, had run away, but while away he met Paul, heard the gospel, and came to faith in Jesus Christ. Onesimus became immensely helpful to Paul during his imprisonment.

Under Roman law, Paul could have faced legal questions for harboring a runaway, and as an apostle, Paul had the spiritual authority to ask Philemon for a favor. However, Paul chose to send Onesimus back to Philemon with this letter. Paul desired to keep Onesimus by his side to assist him in his gospel ministry, yet he refused to take that step without Philemon's explicit consent (gnōmē). By doing so, Paul placed brotherly love and mutual respect above personal convenience or exercise of authority.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of gnōmē is mind, thought, opinion, or judgment. It connects closely with the mental process of considering a matter and arriving at a clear conclusion.

Depending on how it is used, the word can mean:

  • A personal opinion, judgment, or advice offered to others.

  • A deliberate decision, resolve, or plan of action.

  • A shared mind, intention, or unanimity among a group.

  • An official approval, agreement, or consent given to permit an action.

In Philemon 1:14, the word carries this final sense of personal consent or deliberate approval. It refers to Philemon's own decision-making authority regarding his servant and his property.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, gnōmē highlights the voluntary nature of human decision-making. When used in personal relationships, it represents a judgment or advice rather than an absolute command. For example, when Paul gives counsel on practical matters where he has no direct command from the Lord, he offers his gnōmē—his spiritual judgment and honest advice—leaving room for believers to act according to conscience.

When used of groups or rulers, the word describes a united purpose or common decision. In every case, gnōmē emphasizes an inner mental agreement rather than outward force. This makes it a fitting word in Philemon 1:14, where Paul contrasts a good deed done by outward compulsion with one done voluntarily from personal consent.

4. Theological Implications

The use of gnōmē in Philemon highlights a vital biblical principle regarding Christian service: true obedience and generosity must flow from a willing heart.

God values the motive behind an action as much as the action itself. While Paul possessed apostolic authority to order Philemon to do what was right, he knew that forced compliance would destroy the spiritual value of Philemon's gift. Grace operates through willingness, not coercion. By asking for Philemon's consent, Paul treats Philemon as a mature brother in Christ who must be allowed to exercise his own faith and love freely. This demonstrates that gospel ministry respects personal accountability and freedom of conscience within the family of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:14, the word gnōmē appears in the genitive case following a preposition that means "without." Paul writes, "But without your consent I wanted to do nothing."

The immediate context makes Paul's intention clear. In verses 8 and 9, Paul mentions that although he has full authority in Christ to command Philemon to do what is fitting, he prefers to appeal on the basis of love. In verse 13, Paul admits how useful Onesimus has become to him in prison. Humanly speaking, keeping Onesimus would have greatly eased Paul’s suffering.

Yet in verse 14, Paul sets a firm boundary: without Philemon's explicit consent, he will do nothing. Paul adds the reason for this decision: "that your good deed might not be by compulsion, as it were, but voluntary." If Paul had kept Onesimus without asking, Philemon’s implicit permission would have felt forced. By seeking Philemon's consent first, Paul elevates Philemon’s choice, allowing any kindness Philemon shows to be a genuine act of love and service to God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 20:3 — "And stayed three months. And when the Jews plotted against him as he was about to sail to Syria, he decided (gnōmē) to return through Macedonia."

  • 1 Corinthians 1:10 — "Now I plead with you, brethren, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that you all speak the same thing, and that there be no divisions among you, but that you be perfectly joined together in the same mind and in the same judgment (gnōmē)."

  • 1 Corinthians 7:25 — "Now concerning virgins: I have no commandment from the Lord; yet I give judgment (gnōmē) as one whom the Lord in His mercy has made trustworthy."

  • 2 Corinthians 8:10 — "And in this I give advice (gnōmē): It is to your advantage not only to be doing what you began and were desiring to do a year ago."

  • Revelation 17:17 — "For God has put it into their hearts to fulfill His purpose, to be of one mind (gnōmē), and to give their kingdom to the beast, until the words of God are fulfilled."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A helpful contrast exists between gnōmē (opinion, advice, consent) and epitagē (command, authoritative decree). In 1 Corinthians 7 and 2 Corinthians 8, Paul carefully distinguishes between a command from God and his own spiritual advice or judgment (gnōmē).

In Philemon, this distinction becomes the center of Paul's pastoral strategy. Paul intentionally steps back from issuing a command (epitagē) so that Philemon can act out of personal consent (gnōmē). This provides a wise model for pastoral leadership and Christian relationships. Leaders should refrain from using positional power to pressure believers into actions that ought to spring voluntarily from love for God and others.
