CONSENT - ( gnōmē , Strong's G1106 )

Summary:

The Greek noun gnōmē denotes an opinion, judgment, purpose, or consent, springing from the root verb ginōskō ("to know"). In Philemon 1:14, the word carries the nuance of voluntary consent or deliberate decision. Paul refuses to force Philemon’s hand regarding the runaway slave Onesimus, desiring instead that Philemon’s compliance and generosity flow from his own free will, judgment, and heartfelt conviction rather than apostolic compulsion.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun gnōmē derives from the root verb ginōskō, which means "to know" or "to perceive." In ancient Greek culture and classical literature, gnōmē originally referred to the faculty of knowing—the mind, intellect, or organ of judgment. Over time, it came to represent the product of that mental activity: an opinion, a maxim, a resolve, a judgment, or a counsel. In public life, it designated a formal proposal or decree. The underlying word picture is one of thoughtful, conscious determination. It is not an unthinking emotional reaction, but a decision born of understanding and deliberate alignment of the mind.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of gnōmē spans several closely related concepts:

  • An opinion, judgment, or advice offered by someone.

  • A purpose, mind, or intent to do something.

  • Consent, approval, or decision representing the agreement of one's mind to a course of action.

In Philemon 1:14, the word expresses "consent" or "voluntary decision." It refers to Philemon's own subjective judgment and willing concurrence in the matter of Onesimus.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the New Testament, gnōmē describes human decision-making and agreement. It highlights the transition from internal thought to external action. When people are of "one mind" (mia gnōmē), they have come to a unified resolve or purpose. In legal and personal relations, acting "without gnōmē" means acting without the knowledge, advice, or voluntary consent of the party involved. In Philemon 1:14, the word emphasizes that Paul will not bypass Philemon's agency. The Apostle honors Philemon’s intellect and free will, refusing to treat him as a passive instrument of apostolic authority.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, gnōmē underscores the biblical reality of human responsibility and the value of voluntary obedience in the sight of God. True Christian service, charity, and reconciliation cannot be coerced. Paul's refusal to act without Philemon's gnōmē reflects God’s own economy of grace. God desires a "willing mind" and cheerful givers, not grudging compliance under compulsion. This highlights the dignity that the gospel restores to human agency: Philemon is treated not as a master to be commanded, but as a beloved brother whose mature, Christian judgment must be allowed to bear fruit naturally.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:14, Paul is returning Onesimus, the runaway slave who had become a believer and a helper to Paul in prison. Paul would have preferred to keep Onesimus to assist him, but doing so without Philemon's knowledge and "consent" (gnōmē) would have amounted to forcing Philemon's hand. Paul contrasts "compulsion" (anangkē) with "voluntary" action (hekousion). By seeking Philemon’s gnōmē, Paul elevates their relationship from one of authority and duty to one of partnership and love. He wants Philemon to choose to do the right thing because he genuinely wills it in his own mind, not because he feels manipulated or forced.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Corinthians 1:10: "Now I plead with you, brethren... that you be perfectly joined together in the same mind and in the same judgment (gnōmē)."

  • 1 Corinthians 7:25: "Now concerning virgins: I have no commandment from the Lord; yet I give my judgment (gnōmē) as one whom the Lord in His mercy has made trustworthy."

  • 1 Corinthians 7:40: "But she is happier if she remains as she is, according to my judgment (gnōmē)..."

  • 2 Corinthians 8:10: "And in this I give my advice (gnōmē): It is to your advantage not only to be doing what you began and were desiring to do a year ago..."

  • Revelation 17:13: "These are of one mind (gnōmē), and they will give their power and authority to the beast."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In classical Greek, gnōmē was also used to describe a "maxim" or a "proverb"—a distilled piece of wisdom born of deep understanding. This highlights that when Paul asks for Philemon's gnōmē, he is not merely asking for permission; he is inviting Philemon to apply the wisdom and mind of Christ to a highly complex, socially volatile situation. It is an appeal to Philemon's spiritual maturity.
