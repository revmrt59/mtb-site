COMPULSION - ( anankē , Strong's G318 )

Summary:

In Philemon 1:14, the word anankē refers to external pressure, necessity, or obligation that forces someone to act against their free will. Paul uses it to contrast a forced, reluctant obedience with a voluntary, love-driven response. Rather than commanding Philemon to release or receive Onesimus as a brother, Paul appeals to his heart, desiring that his good deed flow naturally from Christian love rather than apostolic pressure or social obligation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In classical Greek and historical usage, anankē carried the sense of physical force, binding chains, or the inescapable necessity of fate. It spoke of pressure so intense that it left no room for choice, alternative action, or personal preference. It was frequently used to describe torture, imprisonment, or the unavoidable constraints of nature and life.

In the Septuagint (the Greek translation of the Old Testament), anankē often translates Hebrew terms for distress, straits, or calamity—moments where a person is squeezed, cornered, or hemmed in by hostile external circumstances. The underlying picture is that of being squeezed into a corner or tightly bound, where only one course of action remains open.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term anankē denotes necessity, compulsion, constraint, or distress. It refers to that which is forced or mandatory, whether by external circumstances, divine decree, legal obligation, or moral duty.

In ethical and interpersonal contexts, anankē stands in direct opposition to free-will choices. It represents an external force that compels action, leaving the actor with a sense of "I must" rather than "I desire to." When applied to actions of kindness or service, it describes begrudging compliance prompted by pressure rather than willing generosity.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

New Testament writers employ anankē in three primary ways:

  • Physical or circumstantial distress: Suffering, hardship, or calamity that presses down upon someone (e.g., Luke 21:23; 1 Corinthians 7:26; 2 Corinthians 6:4).

  • Logical or moral necessity: Something that is unavoidable, proper, or required by duty and the nature of things (e.g., Romans 13:5; Hebrews 9:16).

  • Compulsion or constraint: External pressure that forces an action, rendering it non-voluntary (e.g., 2 Corinthians 9:7; Philemon 1:14).

The word picture in Philemon 1:14 is the contrast between a hand forced open by leverage (compulsion) and a hand opened freely from the heart (voluntariness).

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of anankē in relation to Christian obedience highlights a profound theological truth: God values heart-level willingness over begrudging compliance. While God is sovereign and lays a holy necessity upon His servants for their callings (such as Paul’s "compulsion" to preach), He refuses to coerce human devotion.

True Christian service, giving, and reconciliation must not be motivated by external coercion, social pressure, or legalistic demands. When believers act out of anankē, the moral beauty of the deed is obscured. Grace transforms the heart so that obedience becomes an act of free worship rather than a duty performed under pressure.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:14, Paul is sending the runaway slave Onesimus back to his master, Philemon. Paul, writing from prison, has found Onesimus to be of great use and comfort. Legally and apostolically, Paul could have kept Onesimus with him or commanded Philemon to release him. However, Paul refuses to use his apostolic authority to force Philemon's hand.

Paul contrasts acting "by compulsion" (kata anankēn) with acting "voluntary" (kata hekousion). If Paul had kept Onesimus without Philemon's prior consent, Philemon's eventual agreement would have been forced by the circumstances; he would have felt socially and spiritually compelled to agree. Paul deliberately surrenders his leverage to ensure that Philemon’s forgiveness and reception of Onesimus as a brother flow from sincere Christian love rather than apostolic coercion.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Corinthians 9:7: "So let each one give as he purposes in his heart, not grudgingly or of necessity; for God loves a cheerful giver." (Here, anankē represents the external pressure or obligation to give).

  • 1 Corinthians 9:16: "For if I preach the gospel, I have nothing to boast of, for necessity is laid upon me; yes, woe is me if I do not preach the gospel!" (An internal, divine compulsion that drives Paul's calling).

  • Romans 13:5: "Therefore you must needs be subject, not only because of wrath but also for conscience’ sake." (A moral and civic necessity).

  • Hebrews 9:16: "For where there is a testament, there must of necessity be the death of the testator." (A logical, legal necessity).

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greco-Roman world, the relationship between a slave and a master was defined entirely by anankē—the absolute legal compulsion of the master over the slave. Ironically, Paul subverts this societal structure. Instead of treating Philemon with the same arbitrary compulsion that Roman law allowed masters to use on slaves, Paul treats Philemon with Christlike gentleness. By refusing to use anankē on Philemon, Paul models the very grace, freedom, and mutual respect he wants Philemon to extend to the returning Onesimus.
