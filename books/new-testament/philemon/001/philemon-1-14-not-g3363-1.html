NOT - (hína mḗ, Strong's G3363)

Summary:

The Greek phrase hina mē is a conjunction formed by combining a word for purpose with a word for negation. It means "so that not," "in order that not," or "lest." It introduces an intentional negative goal, pointing to an outcome that someone purposefully acts to prevent or avoid.

In Philemon 1:14, the word expresses Paul's deliberate goal of preventing Philemon's good deed from being done out of compulsion. Paul refused to decide the matter without Philemon's consent so that Philemon's kindness would be truly voluntary rather than forced.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The expression hina mē combines two common Greek words: hina (which introduces purpose, goal, or result) and mē (a particle used to negate possibilities, intentions, or conditions). When joined, they function as a single logical connector pointing to a specific result that a person seeks to avoid.

In ancient writing and biblical literature, actions are often explained by their intended goals. The phrase hina mē marks a deliberate choice designed to guard against an undesirable outcome.

In the letter to Philemon, the Apostle Paul writes from prison to a Christian brother named Philemon. Onesimus, a runaway slave belonging to Philemon, had encountered Paul in prison and become a believer. Paul found Onesimus to be deeply helpful in his ministry and wished to keep him nearby. However, Paul knew that keeping Onesimus without consulting Philemon would deprive Philemon of the opportunity to offer that service willingly. Paul therefore sends Onesimus back, explaining his choice with this word of negative purpose.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The phrase hina mē can mean "so that not," "in order that not," or "lest." It directly connects an action or decision to a result that the speaker intends to prevent.

When a speaker uses this term, they explain the reasoning behind their choices. They show that an action was taken—or avoided—precisely to keep a specific outcome from happening.

In Philemon 1:14, the word negates the possibility of Philemon's act being forced. It establishes Paul's intentional limit: Paul refrained from taking matters into his own hands so that Philemon's goodness would not appear to be extracted under pressure.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, hina mē appears in warnings, practical safeguards, and explanations of deliberate self-restraint. It frequently introduces a protective goal designed to guard believers, preserve faith, prevent misunderstanding, or avoid spiritual harm.

Jesus and the apostles used this phrase to explain why they spoke or acted in particular ways. In some contexts, it introduces a divine or pastoral warning, urging readers to take action so that they avoid negative spiritual outcomes such as falling into pride, suffering judgment, or bringing discredit on the gospel.

In every case, the word highlights intentionality. The outcome being avoided is not an accident; the actor takes deliberate steps to ensure that the undesirable result does not take place.

4. Theological Implications

The use of hina mē in Philemon 1:14 reflects a deep principle of Christian ethics: God values willing, heart-motivated service over coerced compliance.

Paul possessed apostolic authority and could have ordered Philemon to let Onesimus remain. Yet Paul understood that genuine Christian love and generosity lose their true spiritual character when forced. Coerced goodness is a contradiction in terms.

This aligns with how God interacts with His people. God seeks voluntary devotion. While His moral commands are binding, true Christian service must flow from a heart changed by grace rather than external pressure or manipulation. By exercising restraint, Paul gave Philemon room to act out of genuine love.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:14, Paul sets up a direct contrast between compulsion and voluntary action: "that your good deed might not be as it were by compulsion, but voluntary."

Paul made his decision with careful purpose. He admitted in verse 13 that he wished to keep Onesimus with him to serve him in prison on Philemon's behalf. However, Paul intentionally restricted his own desires.

The word "not" (hina mē) marks the exact boundary Paul drew. Paul refrained from making the choice for Philemon precisely to protect the integrity of Philemon's response. Had Paul kept Onesimus without consent, any benefit Paul received would have felt forced upon Philemon. By stepping back, Paul protected Philemon from feeling pressured and honored his dignity as a fellow brother in Christ.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greco-Roman world, leaders and masters routinely used top-down authority to demand obedience and compliance. Paul's approach in this letter offers a striking contrast to contemporary culture.

Paul put the spiritual condition of Philemon's heart above his own immediate comfort in prison. He was willing to forgo personal help so that Philemon could exercise Christian freedom and virtue.

This choice shows that true Christian leadership seeks to build up the spiritual agency of others rather than override their freedom for short-term gain.
