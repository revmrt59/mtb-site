DEPARTED - (chōrízō, Strong's G5563)

Summary:

The word chōrizō means to separate, divide, or place distance between people, places, or things. In Philemon 1:15, Paul uses this word to describe the departure of the runaway bondservant Onesimus from his master Philemon. By phrasing this event gently as a departure or separation, Paul reframes a painful earthly separation as a temporary moment that God used to bring about eternal spiritual brotherhood.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek, chōrizō was a common verb used whenever two entities were separated or moved apart. It described dividing land, separating groups of people, physically traveling away from a location, or ending a marriage relationship.

In the letter to Philemon, Paul addresses a delicate social and personal situation. Onesimus, a bondservant belonging to Philemon, had left his master and fled to Rome. While there, Onesimus met Paul, heard the gospel, and became a follower of Jesus Christ. Paul wrote this short letter to persuade Philemon to receive Onesimus back not as a rebellious servant deserving punishment, but as a beloved brother in the Lord.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chōrizō means to separate, sever, divide, or depart. Depending on the context, the word can refer to:

  • Physical movement away from a place or person (to leave or depart).

  • The division or separation of what was previously joined, such as a marriage bond.

  • Spiritual or personal distance created between two parties.

When used of physical separation, the verb simply points to the act of moving away or being removed from someone's presence.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, chōrizō carries several clear nuances:

  • Marriage and Union: Jesus uses the word when commanding that human authority must not break apart what God has united in marriage.

  • Physical Travel: Luke uses the word to describe Paul leaving one city to travel to another, such as departing from Athens to go to Corinth.

  • Spiritual Security: Paul uses the word in his letter to the Romans to declare that no created thing can separate believers from the love of God in Christ Jesus.

  • Providential Separation: In Philemon 1:15, the word describes an earthly separation that God transformed into a occasion for spiritual grace.

4. Theological Implications

The use of chōrizō in Philemon highlights God's sovereign oversight in human affairs. While human actions may involve wrongdoing, friction, or abandonment, God is able to work through painful separations to accomplish His good purposes.

Onesimus's departure was physically real and legally serious. Yet God used the distance created by that separation to bring Onesimus to Rome, where he heard the gospel through Paul. What began as a disruptive loss for Philemon became the means of Onesimus's salvation and Philemon's gain of an eternal brother. Scripture shows here that human separation never catches God off guard; He can use temporary departures to establish permanent spiritual bonds.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:15, Paul writes: "For perhaps he departed for a while for this purpose, that you might receive him forever."

Paul handles this situation with extraordinary pastoral gentleness:

  • Softened Language: Instead of using harsh terms like "ran away" or "stole from you," Paul uses the verb chōrizō ("departed" or "was separated"). This choice of words avoids unnecessary provocation while acknowledging the reality of what happened.

  • Passive Phrasing: The form of the verb presents the departure gently, focusing on the state of separation rather than dwelling on blame. This shifts Philemon's attention toward what God might be doing through the event.

  • Providential Contrast: Paul pairs "departed for a while" with "receive him forever." The short-term physical loss of a servant is set against the long-term spiritual gain of an eternal brother in Christ.

  • Pastoral Restraint: By introducing the sentence with "for perhaps," Paul does not claim to read God's hidden counsel with absolute certainty. Instead, he invites Philemon to look past his immediate hurt and recognize God's redeeming hand at work.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 19:6 — "So then, they are no longer two but one flesh. Therefore what God has joined together, let not man separate."

  • Acts 18:1 — "After these things Paul departed from Athens and went to Corinth."

  • Romans 8:35 — "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword?"

  • 1 Corinthians 7:10 — "Now to the married I command, yet not I but the Lord: A wife is not to depart from her husband."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding chōrizō in Philemon 1:15 provides a powerful lesson in pastoral reconciliation. Painful separations often produce bitterness and resentment. By describing the event with chōrizō, Paul teaches believers to look beyond immediate human offense. Without excusing wrong behavior, Paul guides Philemon to view life through the lens of divine providence, recognizing that temporary loss can lead to permanent, spiritual blessing.
