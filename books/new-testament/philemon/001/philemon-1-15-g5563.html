DEPARTED - (chōrizō, Strong's G5563)

Summary:

In Philemon 1:15, the Greek word chōrizō is used in the passive voice ("was separated") to describe Onesimus's flight from Philemon. Rather than using a harsh term for running away or theft, Paul uses a tactful passive verb that points to the hand of God. The word means to divide, separate, or put distance between. By framing Onesimus's departure as a temporary separation, Paul invites Philemon to view the painful event through the lens of divine providence—a brief, earthly separation designed by God to result in an eternal, spiritual reunion.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb chōrizō is derived from the adverb chōris, which means "apart from" or "without." This, in turn, comes from chōra, meaning a place, land, or open space. The underlying word picture is the creation of a physical space, gap, or chasm between two entities that were previously joined or situated together. In classical Greek, the word was used in everyday contexts for separating physical items, parting ways with a companion, or the legal separation of a husband and wife.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In its active form, chōrizō means to divide, separate, sever, or put asunder. In the passive voice (which is the form used in Philemon 1:15, echōristhē), it means to be separated, to depart, or to go away.

  • Physical Departure: Moving away from a geographical location or leaving a person's presence.

  • Marital Separation: The act of separating or divorcing.

  • Spiritual/Relational Separation: The state of being estranged or kept apart from someone.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the New Testament, chōrizō carries strong emotional and relational weight. It describes the tragic breaking of the marriage covenant (Matthew 19:6) and the impossible dividing of the believer from the love of Christ (Romans 8:35).

In Philemon 1:15, the grammar of the word provides a profound theological word picture. Paul writes that Onesimus "departed" (NKJV), but the Greek verb is in the passive voice (echōristhē), which literally translates to "he was separated." This is a classic example of the "divine passive" (passivum divinum)—a grammatical construction used to imply that God is the ultimate agent behind the action. By using the passive voice, Paul shifts the focus away from Onesimus’s active rebellion and onto God’s sovereign direction. Onesimus did not merely run away; in the grander scheme, he "was separated" for a season so that God could do a deeper work.

4. Theological Implications

The use of chōrizō in this passage highlights the doctrine of divine providence. It demonstrates how God overrides human sin and rebellion to accomplish His redemptive purposes.

  • Sovereignty over Sin: Onesimus’s flight was unlawful and sinful, yet God used that very departure to bring him to Rome, where he heard the gospel through Paul and was saved.

  • Temporal vs. Eternal: Paul contrasts the temporary separation (pros hōran, "for a while") with an eternal reunion (aiōnion, "forever"). Earthly relationships and separations are bound by time, but the spiritual bond of the gospel transcends time.

  • Redemption of Pain: The pain of betrayal and material loss experienced by Philemon is reframed as a necessary, temporary gap that God allowed in order to return Onesimus as more than a servant—an eternal brother in Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Paul's pastoral tact in Philemon is masterful. He intentionally avoids the harsh, legally charged language of the Roman legal system. He does not use terms like "fugitive" (fugitivus), "theft," or "rebellion." Instead, he gently writes that Onesimus "was separated" for a brief period.

Furthermore, Paul introduces this thought with the word "perhaps" (tacha). This shows pastoral humility; Paul does not claim to have absolute, prophetic insight into the secret counsels of God, but he invites Philemon to look past his immediate anger and consider what God might be doing in the situation. The separation was painful, but the reunion would be glorious.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 19:6: "So then, they are no longer two but one flesh. Therefore what God has joined together, let not man separate."

  • Romans 8:35: "Who shall separate us from the love of Christ? Shall tribulation, or distress, or persecution, or famine, or nakedness, or peril, or sword?"

  • Romans 8:39: "...nor height nor depth, nor any other created thing, shall be able to separate us from the love of God which is in Christ Jesus our Lord."

  • Acts 18:2: "And he found a certain Jew named Aquila... recently come from Italy with his wife Priscilla (because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome); and he came to them."

  • Hebrews 7:26: "For such a High Priest was fitting for us, who is holy, harmless, undefiled, separate from sinners, and has become higher than the heavens;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between chōrizō (to separate) in verse 15 and the verb apechō (to receive back fully/possess) at the end of the verse is stark. Philemon lost a servant for a brief space of time (chōrizō) so that he might possess him fully (apechō) for eternity. This word study offers a powerful template for pastoral counseling: when believers face painful departures, broken relationships, or unfair treatment, they are encouraged to look for the "divine passive"—trusting that God may be allowing a temporary separation to pave the way for an eternal, redemptive work.
