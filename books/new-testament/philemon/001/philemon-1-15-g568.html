RECEIVE - ( apechō , Strong's G568 )

Summary:

The Greek verb apechō (G568) in Philemon 1:15 is translated "receive" in the NKJV, carrying the specific nuance of receiving back in full or having an account fully settled. In the commercial world of the first century, the word served as a technical term used on receipts to indicate that payment had been made in full. In Philemon 1:15, Paul masterfully employs this term to transform Philemon's perspective on his runaway slave, Onesimus. Rather than viewing Onesimus's departure as an irreparable loss or a debt owed, Philemon is encouraged to see it as a temporary parting that resulted in an eternal gain. In Christ, Onesimus returns not merely as a recovered servant, but as a beloved brother, settling the relational ledger forever.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • apechō is a compound verb derived from the preposition apo ("away from" or "off") and the verb echō ("to have" or "to hold"). In its transitive sense, it literally means "to have from" or "to have back in full."

In the everyday life of the ancient Greco-Roman world, apechō was the standard legal and commercial term written on receipts. When a merchant or creditor received payment, they would sign the invoice with the word apechō, meaning "paid in full" or "I have received the payment and the account is closed." This business background provides a vivid backdrop for Paul's letter. Onesimus had run away, likely stealing from his master in the process. When Paul sends him back, he does not ask Philemon to simply tolerate him; he tells him to "receive" him back as a debt that has been fully settled and compensated by God’s grace.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb apechō possesses three distinct meanings in the New Testament:

  • Transitive (To receive in full / have back): To possess completely, to have received payment, or to get back what is due. This is the sense intended in Philemon 1:15, where Philemon gets Onesimus back completely and permanently.

  • Intransitive (To be distant / far off): To be physically separated by a distance.

  • Middle Voice (To abstain): To hold oneself back or keep away from something harmful or sinful.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Gospels, Jesus uses the commercial word picture of apechō to deliver a sobering warning about religious hypocrisy. In Matthew 6:2, 5, and 16, He declares that those who perform religious acts to be seen by men "have their reward" (apechousin). The word picture is that of a finalized business transaction: they have received their payment in full through human applause, and their account with God is closed; there is no outstanding reward left for them in heaven.

Conversely, Paul uses the word with joy in Philippians 4:18 to express his gratitude for the financial support sent by the Philippian church: "Indeed I have all (apechō panta) and abound." Paul uses the term as a formal acknowledgment of receipt, assuring them that their generous gift has fully met his needs and settled any outstanding lack.

4. Theological Implications

The use of apechō in Philemon illustrates the doctrine of divine providence and the completeness of Christian redemption:

  • Providential Compensation: God's sovereignty often uses temporary human losses to bring about permanent spiritual gains. Onesimus's flight was a sin and a material loss to Philemon, yet God redirected the event so that both men would be joined as brothers for eternity.

  • Full Restitution in Christ: In the ancient world, a runaway slave was a financial liability. By using apechō, Paul frames the gospel as the ultimate satisfier of debts. The relational and spiritual wealth Philemon gains in receiving a Christian brother far outweighs any earthly loss he suffered.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:15, Paul writes, "For perhaps he departed for a while for this purpose, that you might receive him (apechēs) forever." Paul masterfully balances several contrasts in this verse to soften Philemon’s heart:

  • Temporal vs. Eternal: Onesimus was gone "for an hour" (pros hōran), but Philemon will have him back "forever" (aiōnion).

  • Passive vs. Active: Onesimus "departed" (passive voice, implying God's hand was behind the departure), but Philemon is to "receive him back" (active voice, requiring a choice of the will).

By choosing apechō instead of a standard verb for welcoming (such as dechomai or proslambanō), Paul elevates the reunion. He signals to Philemon that this is not a temporary patching up of a broken legal contract. Rather, Philemon is receiving Onesimus back in a way that is complete, permanent, and spiritually satisfying. The account between master and slave is settled, replaced by the eternal bond of brotherhood.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 6:2 - "Therefore, when you do a charitable deed, do not sound a trumpet before you as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and in the streets, that they may have glory from men. Assuredly, I say to you, they have [apechousin] their reward."

  • Luke 6:24 - "But woe to you who are rich, for you have received [apechousin] your consolation."

  • Luke 15:20 - "And he arose and came to his father. But when he was still a great way off [apechontos], his father saw him and had compassion, and ran and fell on his neck and kissed him."

  • Philippians 4:18 - "Indeed I have [apechō] all and abound. I am full, having received from Epaphroditus the things sent from you..."

  • Mark 14:41 - "Then He came the third time and said to them, 'Are you still sleeping and resting? It is enough [apechei]! The hour has come; behold, the Son of Man is being betrayed into the hands of sinners.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In verse 18, Paul tells Philemon, "But if he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account." This offer of personal substitutionary payment works hand-in-hand with the term apechō in verse 15. Paul is willing to personally cover any material debt Onesimus owes, ensuring that Philemon can indeed sign the "receipt" of their relationship as "paid in full" without any lingering bitterness or financial grievance.
