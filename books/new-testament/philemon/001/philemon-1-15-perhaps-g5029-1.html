PERHAPS - (tácha, Strong's G5029)

Summary:

The Greek word tacha is an adverb that means "perhaps," "possibly," or "it may be." It is used to express a reasonable possibility or to frame a statement with delicate reserve rather than absolute certainty. In Philemon 1:15, Paul uses tacha as he invites Philemon to view the flight of his runaway servant, Onesimus, through the lens of God's overarching providence. Rather than boldly declaring God's hidden motives as a proven fact, Paul gently suggests that God may have allowed a temporary separation so that Philemon could receive Onesimus back forever as a brother in Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The adverb tacha belongs to a ancient word family associated with speed or swiftness. Over time, in standard Greek usage, it developed into a common word for expressing likelihood or possibility, translated into English as "perhaps," "perchance," or "it may be."

In the Letter to Philemon, Paul writes from prison on behalf of Onesimus, a bondservant who had fled from his master, Philemon. While away, Onesimus encountered Paul, heard the gospel, and came to faith in Jesus Christ. Paul sends Onesimus back to Philemon, not as a defiant runaway to be punished, but as a transformed believer. In Philemon 1:15, Paul reflects on the painful event of Onesimus's departure and offers a spiritual interpretation of what took place.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Tacha functions as an adverb of contingency or possibility. It means:

  • "perhaps" or "it may be"

  • "possibly" or "perchance"

The word indicates that a statement is presented as a plausible suggestion rather than a dogmatic fact. When a speaker uses tacha, they step back from claiming absolute knowledge, choosing instead to propose a thoughtful perspective. In biblical usage, the word does not imply doubt about God's power, but rather reflects human modesty when attempting to explain how God works behind the scenes of human history.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the New Testament, tacha is used sparingly to introduce a qualified statement. It allows a writer to express a realistic or gentle assertion without making an absolute claim.

When used in personal dialogue or argument, tacha softens the tone. Instead of demanding that the listener accept an interpretation as an unquestionable truth, it respectfully asks the listener to consider a possibility. It functions as an instrument of pastoral wisdom, encouraging spiritual reflection without overstepping the boundaries of divine revelation.

4. Theological Implications

Paul's use of tacha in Philemon 1:15 offers an important insight into the biblical doctrine of providence. God is entirely sovereign over human events, capable of directing even bad choices and wrongdoing toward His holy purposes. However, human beings—even inspired apostles—do not always know the full hidden counsel of God regarding specific circumstances.

By saying "perhaps," Paul demonstrates holy restraint. He does not excuse Onesimus's sin of running away, nor does he claim to have received a special revelation explaining why God permitted it. Instead, he highlights the glorious possibility that God overruled a wrongdoing to produce a greater, eternal good. Paul shows that believers can recognize and praise God's hand in past events without presumptuously claiming to fully speak for God's undisclosed intentions.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:15, Paul writes, "For perhaps he departed for a while for this purpose, that you might receive him forever."

Paul uses tacha to approach Philemon with tremendous tact. Philemon was the wronged party, and legally he had every right to be angry with his runaway servant. If Paul had forcefully commanded Philemon to accept his interpretation of events, it might have caused resentment. By framing his insight with tacha ("perhaps"), Paul invites Philemon to join him in discerning God's gracious activity.

The contrast in the verse is striking: Onesimus departed "for a while" (a brief temporal loss), but God's purpose allowed him to be restored "forever" (an eternal spiritual gain). The word tacha bridges these two realities. It urges Philemon to look past his immediate loss and consider whether God allowed a short separation so that master and servant might be reunited as eternal brothers in the family of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 5:7 — "For scarcely for a righteous man will one die; yet perhaps for a good man someone would even dare to die."

Here tacha introduces a rare human exception. While someone might possibly find the courage to die for an exceptionally good person, Christ demonstrated His unique love by dying for ungodly sinners.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's use of tacha serves as a wise model for Christian speech today. Believers often face painful or puzzling circumstances where God's ultimate purpose is not explicitly explained in Scripture.

While faith trusts that God causes all things to work together for good for those who love Him, humility refrains from declaring exact, secret reasons for every suffering or trial. Saying "perhaps God is using this for good" honors God's sovereignty while acknowledging human limitations. It transforms personal grievances into opportunities to witness God's redeeming grace.
