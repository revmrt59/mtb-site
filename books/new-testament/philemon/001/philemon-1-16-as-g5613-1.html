AS - (hōs, Strong's G5613)

Summary:

The Greek word hos is a conjunction and comparative particle used to draw comparisons, describe roles or capacities, indicate degree or time, or mark how something is viewed. In Philemon 1:16, Paul uses the word to redefine how Philemon should view his returning bondservant Onesimus. Onesimus is no longer to be received merely in the role or capacity of a slave, but as a beloved brother in Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Roman world, household servants held a low social position and were often treated purely as economic property. When Onesimus fled from his master Philemon, he faced severe legal penalties under Roman law. However, while away, Onesimus met the apostle Paul, heard the gospel, and put his faith in Jesus Christ.

Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon, carrying this personal letter. Paul did not write to challenge the Roman legal system directly through political action, but to appeal to Philemon’s Christian faith. By introducing the comparative word hos ("as"), Paul reoriented the entire relationship between master and servant around their shared life in Christ.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word hos functions as a comparative particle and conjunction. It serves several related functions in Greek writing:

  • Comparison or resemblance: meaning "as," "like," or "as it were."

  • Role or capacity: indicating how a person or thing is regarded or treated ("in the capacity of," "as").

  • Time or circumstance: meaning "when," "after," or "while."

  • Approximation: meaning "about" when placed before numbers or quantities.

In Philemon 1:16, the word marks a comparison of status and role. It describes the category or mindset through which Philemon should receive Onesimus.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, hos is a flexible connecting word that helps readers understand relationships, comparisons, and perspectives.

When used for comparison, it highlights a true reflection or appropriate standard of behavior. For example, believers are told to conduct themselves as wise people or to treat others as brothers. In other settings, the word introduces an approximate number or indicates the moment an event takes place.

In Paul's letters, the word often sets up a contrast between how the world views a person and how God views them. It helps show that spiritual reality transforms outer roles and earthly relationships.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of this comparative word in Philemon highlights the transforming power of the gospel in human relationships. In the kingdom of God, spiritual identity in Christ reshapes legal and social categories.

When Paul writes that Philemon should receive Onesimus "no longer as a slave," he does not deny the past, but he elevates the relationship to a higher standard. Believers belong to the body of Christ, where worldly distinctions no longer define a person's worth before God or within the church. The gospel calls Christians to look past outward human status and treat fellow believers according to their identity as children of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:16, Paul places hos in a sharp contrast: "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave—a beloved brother." The word "as" introduces the old framework that ruled Roman society—viewing a person strictly by economic or legal function.

Paul urges Philemon to change his perspective entirely. While Onesimus was returning to Philemon's house, Philemon was to receive him not merely in the character or capacity of a bondservant, but above that status—as a genuine Christian brother. Paul adds that Onesimus is a brother "both in the flesh and in the Lord," meaning this brotherhood is real both in daily earthly life and in their shared faith.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The word hos highlights how local Christian churches were called to live out a different social reality than the surrounding culture. While Roman law recognized strict boundaries between masters and slaves, Paul's use of hos asked Philemon to look at Onesimus through the lens of divine grace. This simple conjunction carried revolutionary weight, laying the moral groundwork for genuine Christian fellowship across social divides.
