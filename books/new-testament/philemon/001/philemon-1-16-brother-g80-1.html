BROTHER - (adelphós, Strong's G80)

Summary:

The Greek noun adelphos fundamentally means a physical brother or fellow sibling, but throughout the New Testament it frequently describes a fellow believer in Jesus Christ who shares in God's spiritual family. In Philemon 1:16, the word designates Onesimus, a runaway bondservant who embraced faith in Christ, instructing Philemon to receive him no longer as a slave, but as a beloved spiritual brother.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek usage, adelphos originally referred to a male sibling born of the same parents or mother. Over time, the term broadened in Greek culture to refer to close companions, fellow countrymen, or members of a shared society or religious group.

In the first-century Roman world, household legal structures maintained strict social distinctions between masters and bondservants. Slaves had no legal status as family members. By applying adelphos to a runaway slave returning to his master, Paul uses established family vocabulary to subvert ancient social expectations, establishing a new reality grounded in shared faith in Christ.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Adelphos is used in Scripture in several related ways:

  • A physical brother sharing the same parentage.

  • A fellow countryman or ethnic Israelite.

  • A fellow believer in Jesus Christ who belongs to the spiritual family of God.

In Philemon 1:16, adelphos carries this third sense of a fellow believer in Christ. Paul informs Philemon that Onesimus is no longer merely a bondservant, but a beloved brother whose primary identity is now defined by his connection to Jesus and his inclusion in God's household.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the New Testament, adelphos is one of the primary terms used to describe Christians and their relationship to one another. Jesus broadened the concept of family by identifying those who do the will of God as His spiritual brothers and sisters.

The early church quickly adopted adelphos as a standard designation for fellow disciples. The term emphasizes shared spiritual adoption, mutual affection, and equal standing before God. It highlights that the Christian community is not merely an organization, but a spiritual family bound together by God's grace.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of adelphos rests on the doctrine of divine adoption. God adopts believers as His children, making them heirs with Christ and brothers and sisters to one another.

This truth directly impacts social relationships. While Roman law viewed bondservants as property, Christian doctrine recognizes every believer as an equal member of God's family. Calling a slave a brother does not merely offer kind sentiment; it establishes that both master and servant stand as equals before the heavenly Father, accountable to the same Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:16, Paul addresses Philemon regarding Onesimus, a bondservant who had departed from Philemon, met Paul, and come to saving faith. Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave—a beloved brother."

The noun adelphos functions grammatically as an accusative singular masculine noun describing the new status in which Onesimus is to be received. Paul emphasizes that Onesimus is a brother "both in the flesh and in the Lord." "In the flesh" refers to his physical presence in Philemon's household and daily life, while "in the Lord" refers to their shared spiritual life in Christ. Paul does not merely request leniency for Onesimus; he asks Philemon to welcome him into a restored relationship characterized by family love rather than servitude.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 25:40 — "And the King will answer and say to them, 'Assuredly, I say to you, in as much as you did it to one of the least of these My brethren, you did it to Me.'"

  • Luke 15:27 — "And he said to him, 'Your brother has come, and because he has received him safe and sound, your father has killed the fatted calf.'"

  • Hebrews 2:11 — "For both He who sanctifies and those who are being sanctified are all of one, for which cause He is not ashamed to call them brethren."

  • 1 John 2:9 — "He who says he is in the light, and hates his brother, is in darkness until now."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s use of adelphos in Philemon demonstrates how the gospel reshapes daily life. Rather than forcing a legal resolution, Paul appeals to Philemon on the basis of love and spiritual family ties. The transformation of Onesimus from an unprofitable servant into a beloved brother illustrates the power of Christ to reconcile broken human relationships and create genuine brotherhood across deep social divides.
