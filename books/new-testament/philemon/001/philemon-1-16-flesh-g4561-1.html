FLESH - (sárx, Strong's G4561)

Summary:

The Greek noun sarx refers fundamentally to physical human flesh, the body, or earthly existence. Depending on its context, it can describe human physical tissue, human mortality, family lineage, everyday temporal life, or fallen human nature prone to sin. In Philemon 1:16, the word refers to everyday human, physical, and temporal life. Paul uses it to show that Onesimus is now a beloved brother to Philemon not only in spiritual faith, but also in their tangible, daily earthly relationship.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word sarx is common throughout ancient Greek. In basic everyday speech, it named the physical substance of human or animal bodies—the muscular tissue and skin that make up a living creature. Over time, the term expanded to describe the whole physical body and human nature as bound by earthly limitations.

In the New Testament, sarx carries several distinct shades of meaning. Biblical writers use it to describe physical bodies, mortal human life, natural family ties, and sometimes human nature as infected by sin. Understanding which sense is intended requires careful attention to the immediate passage, because sarx is not a sinful thing in itself. In Paul's letter to Philemon, the word appears in a personal, social setting involving a master, a runaway slave who has embraced Christ, and the practical restoration of their household relationship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word sarx can refer to:

  • The physical material of the human body.

  • Human mortality, weakness, or temporal existence.

  • Natural physical descent, biological family, or human social connections.

  • Human nature apart from God, inclined toward sin and self-reliance.

Here in Philemon 1:16, sarx means temporal, earthly human life and social existence. The word appears in the dative singular form within the phrase "in the flesh" (en sarxi). This grammatical phrase identifies the practical sphere of physical, temporal life in which Philemon and Onesimus live and work together in the same household.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

To read the New Testament accurately, one must distinguish between the different ways sarx is used:

  • Physical and Material Life: The word frequently denotes the bodily life of human beings, including Jesus Christ coming in physical flesh.

  • Human Kinship and Social Realm: The word can describe family lineage or natural human relationships in this present world.

  • Fallen Human Inclination: In many doctrinal passages (such as in Romans and Galatians), Paul uses sarx to contrast human nature corrupted by sin with the life of the Holy Spirit.

Confusing these senses creates serious misunderstandings. When Paul speaks negatively of "the flesh," he refers to sinful human self-reliance. But when he speaks of life "in the flesh" in a neutral or positive context, as in Philemon 1:16, he simply means concrete, physical human existence and everyday earthly relationships.

4. Theological Implications

The gospel does not abolish normal human life or dismiss physical realities as unimportant. When Onesimus became a Christian, he became Philemon's brother "in the Lord"—a spiritual reality rooted in their shared salvation. But Paul insists that this new spiritual reality directly transforms their physical, household reality "in the flesh."

Redemption does not float above practical life in a purely spiritual cloud. The grace of God enters daily human history, changing how believers treat one another in tangible, physical relationships. By describing Onesimus as a beloved brother "in the flesh," Paul teaches that spiritual brotherhood reshapes daily earthly conduct, social responsibilities, and practical affection between believers.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:16, Paul urges Philemon to receive his former runaway slave Onesimus "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave—a beloved brother, especially to me, but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord!"

Paul deliberately pairs two phrases: "in the flesh" and "in the Lord."

  • "In the Lord" highlights their shared spiritual union in Jesus Christ.

  • "In the flesh" highlights their visible, daily, household life together in this world.

Onesimus was returning to Philemon's household. Their reconciliation could not remain an abstract spiritual ideal. Paul shows that Philemon gained Onesimus back as a brother in two distinct yet overlapping spheres: as a spiritual equal in Christ, and as a deeply valued human being in everyday earthly life.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 16:17 — "Jesus answered and said to him, 'Blessed are you, Simon Bar-Jonah, for flesh and blood has not revealed this to you, but My Father who is in heaven.'"

  • John 3:6 — "That which is born of the flesh is flesh, and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit."

  • Romans 8:4 — "that the righteous requirement of the law might be fulfilled in us who do not walk according to the flesh but according to the Spirit."

  • Colossians 3:22 — "Bondservants, obey in all things your masters according to the flesh, not with eyeservice, as men-pleasers, but in sincerity of heart, fearing God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Comparing Philemon 1:16 with Colossians 3:22 highlights Paul's consistent framework. In Colossians, Paul refers to earthly masters as "masters according to the flesh," recognizing that temporal household authority belongs to this present earthly age.

In Philemon, Paul uses sarx to bridge the gap between social station and Christian affection. By calling Onesimus a brother "in the flesh," Paul does not erase the reality of daily household life, but elevates it. He prevents two potential errors: reducing Christianity to mere social restructuring, or spiritualizing faith so much that everyday physical relationships remain unchanged by love.
