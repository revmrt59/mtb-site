BROTHER - ( adelphos , Strong's G80 )

Summary:

The Greek word adelphos literally means "from the same womb," pointing to a biological sibling. In the New Testament, however, it is elevated to describe the profound, spiritual relationship between believers in Jesus Christ. In Philemon 1:16, Paul uses adelphos to radically redefine the relationship between Philemon (the master) and Onesimus (the runaway slave). By calling Onesimus a "beloved brother," Paul dismantles the rigid social hierarchy of the Roman Empire, placing both master and slave on equal footing as co-heirs of grace, united in the family of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The word adelphos is derived from the copulative prefix a- (signifying "unity" or "together") and delphys (meaning "womb"). Literally, it refers to those who share the same womb—biological brothers.

  • In the ancient Greco-Roman world, family ties were paramount, dictating inheritance, social status, and loyalty. To refer to someone outside of one's biological family as an adelphos was a powerful statement of intimacy and shared life.

  • By adopting this family-focused terminology, the early Christian community transformed a physical term into a spiritual reality. Believers are "from the same womb" in a spiritual sense, having been born again of the same Spirit, sharing God as their Father, and Jesus as their elder brother.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Adelphos refers to a male sibling who shares the same parentage.

  • In a broader sense, it denotes a fellow countryman, a member of the same nation or ethnic group.

  • In the New Testament, its most common figurative use is to designate a fellow believer, a member of the Christian community, or a companion in the faith.

  • In Philemon 1:16, the term is used in this spiritual, relational sense. Onesimus is no longer merely a legal possession or a servant; he has become a "brother" in Christ.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Jesus frequently used adelphos to redefine family boundaries, declaring that those who do the will of God are His brothers, sisters, and mother.

  • The New Testament writers use the plural adelphoi ("brethren" or "brothers and sisters") as the standard address for congregations, emphasizing the equality and mutual responsibility of the church family.

  • The word picture is one of shared inheritance. In Roman law, brothers (co-heirs) received equal shares of an estate. By calling fellow believers "brothers," the New Testament emphasizes that all Christians—regardless of earthly status—are joint-heirs with Christ.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Fatherhood of God: The term adelphos directly implies the fatherhood of God. We are brothers and sisters because we have been adopted by the same Father.

  • The Equality of Believers: In Christ, worldly distinctions of status, ethnicity, and gender are superseded by spiritual family ties. A slave and a master share the exact same spiritual standing.

  • The Covenant of Love: Brotherhood in Christ is not a passive status but an active calling to mutual love (philadelphia), bearing one another's burdens, and living in unity.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Philemon 1:16, Paul addresses a highly sensitive and legally volatile situation. Onesimus was a runaway slave who, under Roman law, could face severe punishment or death.

  • Paul does not explicitly command Philemon to legally manumit Onesimus, but he demands something far more revolutionary: Philemon must receive him "no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother."

  • By placing "slave" (doulos) and "brother" (adelphos) in direct contrast, Paul subverts the entire institution of slavery from the inside out. One cannot truly own, exploit, or abuse someone whom they recognize as a beloved brother in the Lord.

  • The phrase "both in the flesh and in the Lord" means that this brotherhood is not a mere theological abstraction. It must manifest in real-world, physical relationships ("in the flesh") as well as in their shared spiritual life ("in the Lord").

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 12:50 - "For whoever does the will of My Father in heaven is My brother and sister and mother."

  • Romans 8:29 - "For whom He foreknew, He also predestined to be conformed to the image of His Son, that He might be the firstborn among many brethren."

  • 1 John 3:14 - "We know that we have passed from death to life, because we love the brethren. He who does not love his brother abides in death."

  • Hebrews 2:11 - "For both He who sanctifies and those who are being sanctified are all of one, for which reason He is not ashamed to call them brethren."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The radical nature of the Christian adelphos relationship shocked the ancient world. The second-century critic Lucian of Samosata mocked Christians, writing, "Their first lawgiver persuaded them that they are all brothers of one another, once they have transgressed by denying the Greek gods."

  • What pagan critics mocked was the secret of the church's strength: a deep, familial bond that crossed deep-seated social, economic, and racial boundaries that no other ancient institution could bridge.
