LORD - (kýrios, Strong's G2962)

Summary:

The Greek word kyrios refers to an owner, master, ruler, or sovereign authority. In the New Testament, it is frequently used as a supreme title for God and for Jesus Christ as divine Lord. In Philemon 1:16, the word identifies Jesus as the ultimate Master over both Philemon and Onesimus, demonstrating that their common spiritual union under Christ transforms their earthly relationship into one of true brotherhood.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The letter to Philemon centers on Onesimus, an enslaved man who had fled from his Christian master, Philemon. After escaping, Onesimus encountered the apostle Paul, came to faith in Jesus Christ, and served Paul faithfully in prison. Paul then sent Onesimus back to Philemon, carrying this short epistle.

In ancient Roman society, a master held absolute legal authority over a household slave. By introducing the title kyrios in verse 16, Paul redefines the ground of their relationship. While Roman law recognized Philemon as master over Onesimus, Paul reminds Philemon that both men share a far higher allegiance to Jesus Christ, the true Lord.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word kyrios refers to someone holding ownership, dominion, or rightful authority. Depending on the context, it can mean:

  • An owner or master of property or servants.

  • A ruler, magistrate, or monarch exercising political supreme power.

  • A respectful title of address, similar to "sir" or "master."

  • The supreme divine Lord, translating the personal name of God in the Greek Old Testament and used throughout the New Testament for Jesus Christ.

In Philemon 1:16, kyrios refers specifically to Jesus Christ, the sovereign Ruler to whom every believer belongs.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, kyrios spans both everyday human settings and exalted theological statements. It describes earthly vineyard owners, household masters, and Roman officials. Yet its most significant use is christological. Early Christians confessed "Jesus is Lord" (kyrios), declaring that Jesus exercises divine authority over all creation and over the church.

When Paul uses the phrase "in the Lord" (en kyriō), he describes the realm of Christian existence. To be "in the Lord" means to live under Christ's direct authority and in spiritual union with Him.

4. Theological Implications

The lordship of Jesus Christ reframes every earthly structure of authority and status. When two people submit to Jesus as Lord, their relationship to each other is fundamentally redefined.

Christ's lordship does not erase every earthly distinction, but it places all human status under divine judgment and grace. Because Christ is Lord over both master and servant, earthly supremacy loses its absolute claim. In the family of faith, spiritual brotherhood under Christ supersedes social hierarchy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:16, Paul urges Philemon to receive Onesimus "no longer as a slave but more than a slave—a beloved brother, especially to me but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord."

The contrast between "in the flesh" and "in the Lord" is vital:

  • "In the flesh" refers to their earthly, temporal relationship within the physical household.

  • "In the Lord" refers to their shared spiritual life under the lordship of Jesus Christ.

Paul uses the dative form of kyrios following the preposition "in" to specify the shared spiritual realm where Philemon and Onesimus stand as equals. Because Onesimus now belongs to Christ, Philemon cannot view him merely as earthly property. Both men serve the same heavenly Master, making them genuine spiritual brothers.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 20:28 – "And Thomas answered and said to Him, 'My Lord and my God!'"

  • Ephesians 6:9 – "And you, masters, do the same things to them, giving up threatening, knowing that your own Master also is in heaven, and there is no partiality with Him."

  • Romans 14:6 – "He who observes the day, observes it to the Lord; and he who does not observe the day, to the Lord he does not observe it."

  • Philemon 1:5 – "hearing of your love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Ephesians 6:9, Paul uses kyrios twice in a single sentence to contrast earthly masters with their heavenly Master: human masters have a "Master" in heaven who shows no partiality. A similar dynamic occurs here in Philemon.

By grounding his appeal in the lordship of Jesus, Paul does not resort to legal demands or apostolic coercion. Instead, he appeals to the quiet, transformative power of Christ's supreme authority, which works within human hearts to disassemble pride and establish real fellowship among believers.
