MORE - (mâllon, Strong's G3123)

Summary:

Mallon is a Greek adverb that expresses comparison, degree, or preference. It can mean "more," "rather," "to a greater degree," or "all the more." In Philemon 1:16, Paul uses the word to describe how Philemon ought to receive his former runaway bondservant, Onesimus. While Onesimus had become a beloved brother to Paul during his imprisonment, Paul emphasizes that he should be "how much more" precious to Philemon, transforming a broken earthly relationship into a deep fellowship in Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The letter to Philemon is a deeply personal note written by Paul during his imprisonment. Philemon was a Christian leader in Colosse who hosted a local church in his home. Onesimus was Philemon's bondservant who had fled and eventually encountered Paul. Through Paul's ministry, Onesimus came to faith in Jesus Christ and proved to be a valuable helper to the apostle.

Roman law gave slaveholders absolute authority over runaway servants, including severe punishment. Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon, not as a fugitive escaping justice, but as a transformed disciple. In sending him home, Paul asks Philemon to reframe his relationship with Onesimus entirely—viewing him no longer primarily through the lens of earthly status, but through the eternal reality of redemption.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Mallon is an adverb that indicates comparison, contrast, or preference. Depending on how it is used in a sentence, the word can mean:

  • "More" or "to a greater extent," when comparing two degrees of an action or quality.

  • "Rather" or "instead," when choosing one alternative over another or correcting a statement.

  • "All the more," when intensifying an existing reality or duty.

When combined with phrases that emphasize extent (such as "how much"), mallon magnifies the comparison. In Philemon 1:16, the word is used in this comparative sense to show that while Paul has great affection for Onesimus, Philemon has reason to love and value him to an even greater degree.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the New Testament, mallon serves two principal functions in communication:

  • Comparative Intensity: It shows that one reality surpasses another in scope, value, or importance. It helps readers see that something is not merely true, but abundantly true.

  • Preferential Choice: It redirects attention from a lesser or mistaken option to a higher, better truth. When translated "rather," it urges believers to choose the superior path.

In the context of Christian fellowship, mallon often highlights how grace increases our affection, duty, and capacity to love. It shows that spiritual realities expand our earthly obligations rather than shrinking them.

4. Theological Implications

Paul's use of mallon in Philemon 1:16 points to the power of the gospel to transform human relationships. Onesimus was returning to the same household, but everything about his dynamic with Philemon had changed. He was no longer to be regarded merely as a servant, but as a beloved brother in the Lord.

The phrase "how much more" reflects the overflowing nature of God's grace. Redemption does not erase our earthly lives, but it elevates them. Because Philemon shared both an earthly connection ("in the flesh") and a spiritual union ("in the Lord") with Onesimus, his love for Onesimus was called to exceed even the deep bond Onesimus had formed with Paul.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:16, Paul writes that Onesimus is "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave—a beloved brother, especially to me, but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord."

The target word "more" emphasizes the depth of affection Philemon is urged to show. Onesimus had become "especially" dear to Paul during his imprisonment. Yet Paul reasons that Philemon has a double motivation to treasure him:

  • In the flesh: They share a history within the same household, now restored and healed.

  • In the Lord: They share the same Savior, making them spiritual equals and brothers in God's family.

Paul uses "more" to appeal to Philemon's heart rather than issuing a harsh command. He invites Philemon to step willingly into a higher level of love and brotherhood.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 10:20 — "Nevertheless do not rejoice in this, that the spirits are subject to you, but rather rejoice because your names are written in heaven."

  • Romans 5:15 — "For if by the one man’s offense many died, much more the grace of God and the gift by the grace of the one Man, Jesus Christ, abounded to many."

  • 2 Corinthians 12:9 — "And He said to me, 'My grace is sufficient for you...' Therefore most gladly I will rather boast in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's approach in this passage demonstrates pastoral wisdom. Instead of using his apostolic authority to dictate Philemon's actions, Paul relies on the gentle persuasion of gospel truth. By using comparative language ("how much more"), he gives Philemon space to act out of genuine love rather than mere compliance.

This highlights a key principle of Christian community: the gospel creates bonds that go beyond legal requirements or social expectations. Grace moves believers to treat one another with an abundance of kindness that far exceeds what common custom demands.
