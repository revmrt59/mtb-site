AS - (hōs, Strong's G5613)

Summary:

The Greek word hos is a conjunction and comparative particle used to express comparison, manner, role, degree, or time. Depending on the sentence, it can be translated into English as "as," "like," "how," "when," or "about." In Philemon 1:17, Paul uses the word to establish a standard of evaluation, calling on Philemon to regard Paul as a gospel partner and, on that basis, to receive the runaway servant Onesimus with the exact same welcome he would give to Paul himself.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In New Testament Greek, hos is one of the most frequently used connective words. It serves as a practical link between ideas, showing how one person, action, or state relates to another. It can draw a direct comparison ("like"), identify a capacity or function ("as"), describe the manner in which something happens ("how"), or mark a point in time ("when").

The Letter to Philemon provides a deeply personal setting for this word. Onesimus was a bondservant who had fled from his master, Philemon. After running away, Onesimus met the apostle Paul in prison and came to faith in Jesus Christ. Paul then sent Onesimus back to Philemon with this short letter, asking Philemon to forgive his former servant and to receive him as a beloved brother in the Lord. In verse 17, Paul uses hos to redefine how Philemon should view both Paul and Onesimus.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word hos primarily functions to connect two concepts by setting one as the standard, comparison, or description for the other.

The word can mean:

  • "As" or "in the capacity of," describing a person's role, standing, or treatment.

  • "Like" or "in the manner of," drawing a comparison or analogy between two things.

  • "When" or "as soon as," marking a temporal connection between actions.

  • "How," pointing out the degree, state, or manner of an action.

In Philemon 1:17, the word is used in the sense of role and treatment. It sets up a frame of reference: Philemon is asked to count Paul "as" a true partner in the faith and to treat Onesimus in that exact same way.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Because hos is a flexible conjunction, its exact nuance depends entirely on the surrounding sentence structure:

  • Comparative Function: When comparing two distinct items, hos shows resemblance or similarity. For example, a writer might state that something shone "like" the sun or that believers ought to live "as" wise people rather than foolish ones.

  • Role or Identity Function: When attached to a personal title or condition, hos indicates the capacity in which someone should be regarded or treated. It calls the listener to act toward a person according to a specific standing—such as treating someone "as" a brother or a church leader "as" a steward of God.

  • Temporal Function: When introducing a clause describing an action, hos often marks time, functioning like the English word "when" to show when an event occurred.

4. Theological Implications

Though hos is a small grammatical particle, Paul's use of it in Philemon 1:17 carries substantial theological weight. By asking Philemon to receive Onesimus "as" Paul himself, Paul models the core principle of gospel substitution and grace.

In the gospel, Jesus Christ takes believers into union with Himself, so that God receives sinners not according to their past debt or low status, but "as" He receives His own Son. Paul reflects this divine heart in human relationships. Onesimus had no legal or social claim to Philemon's favor. Yet Paul places his own standing between Philemon and Onesimus, asking Philemon to transfer the love and respect he feels for Paul directly to the former runaway servant. The small word "as" becomes the doorway to reconciliation, replacing earthly status with kingdom fellowship.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:17, Paul writes, "If then you count me as a partner, receive him as me." The word hos appears in the framing of this appeal.

Paul begins with a conditional statement that assumes a shared reality: Philemon does indeed consider Paul a true partner (koinōnos) in gospel ministry. By using hos to connect his partnership with Philemon to the reception of Onesimus, Paul bridges a vast social divide. In first-century Roman society, a master had absolute authority over a runaway bondservant. But Paul asks Philemon to set aside legal entitlement and cultural practice.

Instead of viewing Onesimus "as" a former slave who deserves punishment, Philemon is summoned to view him "as" Paul's own representative—and ultimately "as" a fellow heir of divine grace. The word "as" does not change the historical facts of Onesimus's past, but it radically alters the standard by which he is to be valued and welcomed.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s diplomatic sensitivity in Philemon is remarkable. Rather than issuing an apostolic decree, he relies on love and shared identity to motivate Philemon. Using hos allows Paul to invite Philemon into a voluntary act of spiritual maturity. It challenges Philemon to align his household conduct with the truth of the gospel he professes.

This passage demonstrates how Christian faith reshapes everyday relationships. A word as simple as "as" becomes a practical tool for Christian unity, calling believers to see and treat one another according to their shared standing in Jesus Christ rather than their social standing in the world.
