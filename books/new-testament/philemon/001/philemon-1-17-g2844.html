PARTNER - ( koinonos , Strong's G2844 )

Summary:

In Philemon 1:17, the word "partner" (koinonos) describes a deep, covenantal bond of shared life, faith, and mission. Far more than a casual associate or a modern business colleague, a koinonos is someone bound together with another in a joint venture where assets, liabilities, risks, and joys are fully shared. By appealing to Philemon as his "partner," Paul raises their relationship from a mere friendship to a sacred alliance in the Gospel. He uses this profound partnership to advocate for the runaway slave Onesimus, urging Philemon to receive him not as a fugitive, but as he would receive Paul himself.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun koinonos is derived from the root koinos, which means "common" or "shared." In the classical and Greco-Roman world, the word was heavily utilized in legal and commercial contexts. It referred to business partners, joint-venture associates, or co-owners of property who entered into a formal contract. These partners shared mutual ownership, collective risks, and common profits. A vivid example of this occurs in the New Testament when James, John, and Simon Peter are described as partners (koinonoi) in their commercial fishing enterprise.

Historically, the term also carried philosophical weight, describing those who shared a common bond of friendship, intellectual pursuit, or religious devotion. When brought into the Christian vocabulary, koinonos was elevated to describe a supernatural fellowship. Believers are not merely business associates; they are joint-participants in the life of Christ, sharing a common salvation, a common Holy Spirit, and a common eternal destiny.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The lexical definition of koinonos centers on the concept of sharing, participation, and partnership. It denotes:

  • A partner or associate in an office, enterprise, or shared life.

  • A companion or comrade who shares in common experiences, whether of suffering or of joy.

  • A partaker or sharer in something spiritual, such as the divine nature, the gospel, or the glory of God.

In the active sense, a koinonos is not a passive spectator but an active contributor who invests their own resources into the shared pool of the community’s mission.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The New Testament utilizes koinonos to paint a picture of deep solidarity. It is used in several distinct ways:

  • Commercial Partnership: Describing those who share physical labor and business assets (Luke 5:10).

  • Spiritual Companionship: Describing believers who stand together in times of persecution and distress, sharing each other's burdens (Hebrews 10:33).

  • Covenantal Partaking: Describing the deep spiritual communion of believers with God, whereby they become partakers of the divine nature (2 Peter 1:4).

  • Shared Sufferings and Comfort: Highlighting how believers participate together in the afflictions of Christ, knowing they will also share in His comfort (2 Corinthians 1:7).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, koinonos underscores the reality that the Christian life is fundamentally corporate, not individualistic. Our union with Jesus Christ automatically brings us into a union with all other believers. This partnership is grounded in the koinonia (fellowship) of the Holy Spirit.

Furthermore, this partnership has radical social implications. In Christ, the dividing walls of class, status, and ethnicity are dismantled. The master (Philemon) and the slave (Onesimus) are brought into the same spiritual partnership. Because all believers share equally in the grace of God, they must treat one another with the same grace, erasing worldly hierarchies within the household of faith.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:17, Paul writes, "If then you count me as a partner, receive him as you would me." Paul’s choice of the word koinonos here is masterfully persuasive and pastorally tender.

  • An Appeal to Covenant Bond: Paul does not command Philemon as an apostle; instead, he appeals to him as a "partner." He reminds Philemon of their shared spiritual bank account. Everything Paul has poured into Philemon’s life, and everything Philemon has contributed to the ministry, establishes a mutual obligation.

  • The Law of Substitution: Paul uses the language of commercial partnership to propose a spiritual transaction. In a legal partnership, the debts of one partner are assumed by the other. Paul essentially tells Philemon, "If we are true business partners in the Gospel, charge Onesimus's debt to my account, and credit my status to his."

  • Elevating the Slave: By asking Philemon to receive Onesimus "as you would me," Paul elevates the runaway slave into the very same circle of partnership. Onesimus is no longer property; he is now a co-worker and a partner in grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 5:10: "...and so also were James and John, the sons of Zebedee, who were partners with Simon."

  • 2 Corinthians 8:23: "If anyone inquires about Titus, he is my partner and fellow worker concerning you."

  • Hebrews 10:33: "...partly while you were made a gazingstock both by reproaches and tribulations, and partly while you became companions of those who were so treated."

  • 2 Peter 1:4: "...that through these you may be partakers of the divine nature, having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The relationship between koinonos (partner) and the related noun koinonia (fellowship) is vital for understanding Paul's theology of community. Fellowship is not just social chatter over a meal; it is the active expression of being partners. In Philemon 1:6, Paul prays that "the sharing [koinonia] of your faith may become effective." Then, in verse 17, he calls Philemon his "partner" (koinonos). The verbal link is intentional. Paul is telling Philemon that the only way for his koinonia (fellowship) to be genuine and effective is for him to act like a true koinonos (partner) by forgiving and embracing Onesimus.
