RECEIVE - ( proslambanō , Strong's G4355 )

Summary:

The Greek verb proslambanō means to take to oneself, welcome, or receive into one's home or circle of intimacy. In Philemon 1:17, Paul uses this powerful word to urge Philemon to welcome his runaway slave, Onesimus, not as a renegade servant returning to punishment, but with the same warmth, acceptance, and honor that Philemon would extend to Paul himself. This transformation of relationship highlights the profound, status-shattering unity of believers in Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The compound verb proslambanō consists of the prefix pros (meaning "toward" or "to") and the verb lambanō (meaning "to take" or "lay hold of"). Literally, it means "to take toward oneself." The historical word picture is one of reaching out to draw someone close, pulling them into one's personal sphere, home, or circle of companionship. Rather than a passive acceptance or mere toleration, the term carries an active, welcoming warmth. It represents a deliberate embrace that bridges a gap, bringing someone who was distant or estranged into close, intimate association.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In classical and New Testament Greek, proslambanō carries several closely related nuances:

  • To take along or take with oneself as a companion.

  • To take aside for private conversation or instruction.

  • To receive or welcome into one's home, society, or circle of friendship with hospitality and kindness.

  • To accept or receive into fellowship, especially in a spiritual sense, acknowledging a shared bond.

In the middle voice, which is used in Philemon 1:17, the reflexive force of the verb is highlighted: "take him to yourself" or "welcome him into your own fellowship."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the New Testament, proslambanō illustrates deep personal acceptance and rescue. In Acts 28:2, the Maltese natives "welcomed" Paul and his shipwrecked companions by building a fire and providing shelter. In Acts 18:26, Priscilla and Aquila "took aside" Apollos to explain the way of God more accurately. In a spiritual sense, the word describes God's gracious acceptance of believers. In Romans 14:3, Paul commands believers not to judge one another because God has received them. The ultimate standard of this welcoming action is found in Romans 15:7, where believers are exhorted to receive one another just as Christ received us. The word picture is always one of active, relational embrace that removes barriers and establishes mutual belonging.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of proslambanō in the New Testament is immense, particularly regarding the doctrine of reconciliation and the unity of the church.

  • Vertical and Horizontal Reconciliation: The way believers receive one another is a direct reflection of how Christ has received them. Because Christ took believers to Himself when they were spiritual aliens and enemies, believers are obligated to take others to themselves, regardless of past offenses or social distinctions.

  • The Gospel of Grace: The word underscores that Christian fellowship is not based on merit, status, or social standing, but on the grace of God. Welcoming an undeserving brother demonstrates the gospel to a watching world.

  • New Identity in Christ: In the ancient Greco-Roman world, the social chasm between a master and a runaway slave was absolute. By calling Philemon to "receive" Onesimus, Paul demonstrates that the gospel completely restructures human relationships, elevating a former slave to the status of a beloved brother in the Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:17, Paul writes, "If then you count me as a partner, receive him as you would me." The word proslambanō (here in the imperative mood, proslabou) carries several critical contextual nuances:

  • Total Substitution: Paul connects the reception of Onesimus directly to his own relationship with Philemon. He asks Philemon to transfer the love, honor, and hospitality due to an apostle and "partner" (koinōnos) to a runaway slave.

  • Active Reconciliation: Under Roman law, Philemon had the legal right to punish, torture, or even execute Onesimus. Paul's command to receive him is a radical subversion of Roman legal and cultural norms, demanding a welcoming embrace instead of retribution.

  • Voluntary Love: The imperative is a personal appeal grounded in Christian partnership. Paul does not command Philemon out of apostolic authority alone, but appeals to Philemon's heart, asking him to make a deliberate, loving choice to take Onesimus back into his home and heart.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 14:1 - "Receive one who is weak in the faith, but not to disputes over doubtful things."

  • Romans 14:3 - "Let not him who eats despise him who does not eat, and let not him who does not eat judge him who eats; for God has received him."

  • Romans 15:7 - "Therefore receive one another, just as Christ also received us, to the glory of God."

  • Acts 28:2 - "And the natives showed us unusual kindness; for they kindled a fire and made us all welcome, because of the rain that was falling and because of the cold."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The middle voice of proslambanō in Philemon 1:17 emphasizes that this is not a public duty performed out of mere obligation, but a deeply personal action. Philemon is to take Onesimus "to himself." This would mean bringing Onesimus back into his household not as a piece of property to be tolerated in the background, but as a brother at the family table. It represents the practical outworking of Christian koinōnia (fellowship/partnership)—a shared life in Christ that overcomes the deepest societal divisions of the ancient world.
