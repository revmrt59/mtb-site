RECEIVE - (proslambánō, Strong's G4355)

Summary:

The word proslambanō means to take to oneself, to welcome, or to accept someone into one's home, care, or personal fellowship. In Philemon 1:17, Paul uses a direct command urging Philemon to accept the returning runaway slave Onesimus not as a fugitive deserving punishment, but with the exact same warmth, acceptance, and love that Philemon would show to Paul himself.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word combines a standard verb for taking or receiving with a preposition meaning "toward." Together, the idea is taking a person or thing close to oneself, drawing someone into personal association, or welcoming someone into a household or circle of friends.

In the ancient Greco-Roman world, social hierarchies dictated how people were received. A master receiving a returning runaway slave had absolute legal authority to punish, re-enslave, or brand him. Social customs offered no warmth or equal standing to such a person. By instructing Philemon to receive Onesimus into full Christian fellowship, Paul subverted the harsh cultural expectations of his day and replaced them with the radical warmth of gospel reconciliation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Proslambanō can refer to taking physical food or nourishment, taking a person aside to speak with them, or receiving someone into one's personal care, home, or Christian fellowship. When used of people in a social or spiritual sense, it means to receive someone with active welcome, kindness, and personal acceptance.

In Philemon 1:17, the word means to receive someone into full personal fellowship and acceptance. Paul is not asking Philemon merely to tolerate Onesimus's presence or grant him minimal legal freedom. He is calling Philemon to open his heart and home to Onesimus, treating him as a beloved brother in the Lord.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Scripture, the word carries several distinct shades of meaning depending on who is acting:

  • Taking Someone Aside: It describes pulling a person aside for private conversation, such as when Peter took Jesus aside to rebuke Him, or when Aquila and Priscilla took Apollos aside to explain the way of God more accurately.

  • Taking Food: It refers to taking physical nourishment or partaking of a meal for strength and sustenance.

  • Showing Hospitality: It describes welcoming travelers or strangers into shelter and care, as when the inhabitants of Malta showed kindness to Paul and his shipwrecked companions.

  • Accepting Believers into Fellowship: In the epistles, it becomes a crucial term for Christian unity. Believers are commanded to receive those who are weak in faith without passing judgment, recognizing that God Himself has already received them.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical pattern of receiving others is grounded entirely in how God receives sinners. In Christ, God took those who were estranged and far off and brought them into His own family. Because believers have been fully accepted by God through grace, they are called to extend that same acceptance to one another.

Paul's appeal to Philemon reflects this gospel dynamic. By urging Philemon to accept Onesimus as he would accept Paul, the apostle provides a vivid earthly picture of spiritual imputation. Just as believers are received by God the Father because they are united to Christ, Onesimus is to be received by Philemon because he is united to Paul and to Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:17, Paul writes, "If then you count me as a partner, receive him as you would me." The grammar marks this statement as a direct imperative—a clear command given to Philemon.

Paul intentionally links his personal spiritual partnership with Philemon to Philemon's treatment of Onesimus. Paul does not use his apostolic authority to force compliance; instead, he leverages their shared bond in Christ. He asks Philemon to take the love, honor, and joy he would normally reserve for Paul and place it entirely onto Onesimus.

This single request completely dismantles the old dynamic between master and slave. It places Onesimus in the category of a honored partner and brother in the Lord.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 18:26 — "When Aquila and Priscilla heard him, they took him aside and explained to him the way of God more accurately."

  • Acts 28:2 — "And the natives showed us unusual kindness; for they kindled a fire and welcomed us all, because of the rain that was falling and because of the cold."

  • Romans 14:1 — "Receive one who is weak in the faith, but not to disputes over doubtful things."

  • Romans 15:7 — "Therefore receive one another, just as Christ also received us, to the glory of God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Earlier in the letter (Philemon 1:12), Paul uses this same word when sending Onesimus back, telling Philemon to receive him as Paul's "own heart." By repeating the concept in verse 17, Paul emphasizes that welcoming Onesimus is equivalent to welcoming Paul himself.

This deep personal identification shows that Christian fellowship is not abstract. Receiving a fellow believer in practical, warm hospitality is one of the clearest physical demonstrations of gospel unity in the local church.
