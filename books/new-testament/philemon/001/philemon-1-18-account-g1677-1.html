ACCOUNT - (ellogéō, Strong's G1677)

Summary:

The word ellogeō means to charge something to a record, enter an amount into a ledger, or count a financial obligation against someone. In Philemon 1:18, Paul uses this word to instruct Philemon to transfer whatever financial debt or wrong Onesimus owes directly onto Paul's personal account. By taking on this obligation, Paul removes the financial barrier between the restored slave and his master, providing a clear picture of grace and substitution.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The term ellogeō comes from ancient commercial and financial language. It combines the prefix for entering into a record with the word for reckoning, calculating, or accounting. In the Roman world, merchants and household managers kept detailed ledgers to record debts, credit, loans, and payments. When an entry was made using this terminology, it signaled an official record of financial liability.

In his letter to Philemon, Paul writes on behalf of Onesimus, a slave who had separated from Philemon's household and had recently become a Christian under Paul's ministry. Onesimus may have taken money or damaged Philemon's property when he left. Before sending Onesimus back to Philemon as a brother in Christ, Paul addresses this outstanding financial debt directly, using formal accounting language to resolve the matter completely.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Ellogeō means to charge to an account, to enter into a ledger, or to calculate a legal debt against a person. It refers to the deliberate transfer or recording of an obligation.

When applied to financial transactions, it means putting a monetary debt onto a specific line item in a record book. When applied in legal or moral settings, it means counting an offense or obligation against someone so that they are held legally responsible to pay or satisfy it. In this passage, the word refers to taking a debt owed by one person and placing it squarely on the record of another.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In New Testament usage, ellogeō is a specialized accounting term that appears sparingly. It consistently carries the sense of officially recording or reckoning an obligation.

Scripture uses this term in two distinct contexts:

  • Financial and Relational Obligations: As seen in Philemon 1:18, it describes a voluntary agreement to take on another person's financial liability.

  • Legal Accounting of Sin: In Romans 5:13, it describes how sin is reckoned or held to a person's account in relation to the written law.

In both contexts, the word emphasizes that an obligation is not ignored or swept away; rather, it is formally accounted for and assigned to a specific ledger.

4. Theological Implications

While ellogeō itself is a standard term from ancient commerce, its usage in Scripture illustrates the core gospel principle of substitution. Paul does not ask Philemon to pretend that Onesimus committed no wrong or that no financial loss occurred. Instead, Paul asks Philemon to record that loss directly to Paul's personal record.

This action reflects how divine grace operates. Justice requires that debt be recognized and addressed. In the gospel, Christ steps into the place of sinners, taking their moral debt onto His own record so that they can be reconciled to God. Paul's gesture toward Onesimus is a practical human expression of the substitutionary grace that believers receive from Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:18, Paul writes, "But if he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account." The word functions as a direct command to Philemon, instructing him to make a specific accounting transfer.

Paul anticipates that Philemon might feel justified in demanding financial restitution before receiving Onesimus back into his home. To ensure that money does not become an obstacle to reconciliation, Paul steps between the two men. By telling Philemon to place the charge onto Paul's ledger, Paul guarantees full repayment and removes any lingering resentment. The immediate context shows Paul backing this request with a handwritten pledge in the next verse, confirming that the transfer of liability is official and binding.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 5:13 — "For until the law sin was in the world, but sin is not imputed when there is no law."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul strengthens his request in verse 19 by adding a formal legal guarantee: "I, Paul, am writing with my own hand. I will repay." In the ancient Roman world, adding a personal signature or writing a document in one's own hand served as a legally binding promissory note. By pairing the accounting word in verse 18 with his signed pledge in verse 19, Paul completely clears Onesimus of debt and guarantees that Philemon will suffer no financial loss.
