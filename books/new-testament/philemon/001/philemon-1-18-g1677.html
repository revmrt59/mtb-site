PUT THAT ON MY ACCOUNT - ( ellogeō , Strong's G1677 )

Summary:

The verb ellogeō is a financial and accounting term that means to charge to someone's account or to impute. In the context of Philemon 1:18, Paul uses this commercial term to assume the legal and financial liabilities of the runaway slave Onesimus. By telling Philemon to charge any debt or wrong to his own account, Paul provides a vivid, real-world illustration of the theological concept of imputation—where one person's debts are transferred to another's ledger, and the debtor is set free.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word ellogeō is a compound verb derived from en ("in" or "upon") and logos (which, in a commercial context, refers to a "reckoning," "bill," or "ledger account"). Historically, it was used in business, banking, and accounting practices in the Greco-Roman world. It describes the formal act of entering a transaction into a ledger, specifically charging an expense, debt, or credit to a specific person's account. The word picture is that of a physical accounting book where a debt is written down next to someone's name, legally binding them to the balance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term means to charge to someone's account, to impute, or to reckon. It denotes the formal transfer of a financial obligation or moral culpability from one party's record to another. In accounting papyri of the New Testament era, it is used for recording payments or outstanding debts to an individual's ledger.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The New Testament uses ellogeō only twice, and both occurrences carry the weight of legal and financial ledger transfers. In secular Greek literature and papyri, it was the standard technical term for bookkeeping. The word picture is clean and unsentimental: it is not a vague plea for mercy or a request to overlook a debt, but a formal, legally binding instruction to transfer a debt from one ledger column to another. The debtor's column is cleared, and the guarantor's column is charged.

4. Theological Implications

This term lies at the heart of the doctrine of imputation, specifically the imputation of sin and righteousness. It illustrates how Christ deals with human debt. Just as Paul volunteered to have Onesimus's financial and moral debts charged to his personal ledger, Christ takes the spiritual debt of believers and places it on His own account, paying it in full on the cross. Conversely, His righteousness is credited to the believer's account. The term shows that salvation is not a mere bypass of justice, but a legal satisfaction of debt through substitution.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:18, Onesimus, a runaway slave, had likely stolen from his master, Philemon, or caused financial loss by his escape. Paul does not minimize Onesimus's wrongdoing. Instead, he addresses the legal and financial reality of the situation. By saying "put that on my account," Paul acts as a guarantor, legally binding himself to pay whatever Onesimus owes. In the next verse, Paul reinforces this with a formal promissory note: "I, Paul, am writing with my own hand. I will repay." This personal guarantee removes the financial barrier to reconciliation between the master and his restored brother in Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 5:13 – "(For until the law sin was in the world, but sin is not imputed when there is no law.)"

  • Philemon 1:18 – "But if he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between ellogeō (to charge to an account) and logizomai (to reckon or impute, used frequently in Romans 4) is subtle. While logizomai is a broader cognitive and accounting term meaning to count, calculate, or think, ellogeō is more specifically tied to the actual ledger entry—the formal, physical act of placing a charge against an account. Paul's use of this specific commercial term highlights the concrete, practical reality of Christian love and restitution.
