WRONGED - ( adikeō , Strong's G91 )

Summary:

The Greek verb adikeō means to act unjustly, commit a wrong, or cause damage to another. In Philemon 1:18, Paul uses it to address the financial or material damage Onesimus caused his master, Philemon, before fleeing. Paul’s appeal to "put that on my account" beautifully illustrates the theological concept of imputation, where the debt of the guilty party is transferred to a willing mediator, reflecting the heart of the gospel.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of adikeō is dikē, which represents custom, law, justice, or right order. The prefix a- is a negative particle (alpha privative) meaning "without" or "against." Historically, adikeō describes actions that disrupt or violate the established order of justice and equity in society. In classical Greece, it was used in legal and civic contexts to describe someone who violated a contract, stole property, or committed a crime against another citizen. The term carries the picture of tipping the scales of justice out of balance, creating a moral and legal debt that demands restitution.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb adikeō carries several closely related nuances depending on the context:

  • To act unjustly or wickedly; to do wrong in a moral sense.

  • To treat someone unjustly or unfairly.

  • To cause damage, harm, or injury, particularly of a material or financial nature.

  • In legal contexts, to commit a crime or stand as an offender.

In Philemon 1:18, the term refers to a concrete, historical action—specifically, the damage or loss Onesimus caused Philemon, whether through theft, property damage, or the loss of labor.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the New Testament, adikeō occurs 28 times. Its usage spans from moral wrongdoing to physical harm:

  • It is used of physical damage to the earth and environment, as seen in the apocalyptic visions of Revelation (e.g., Revelation 7:3, "Do not harm the earth").

  • It is used of ethical violations and social injustice, such as defrauding a brother in court (1 Corinthians 6:7-8).

  • It is used of legal guilt before civil authorities, as when Paul asserts before Festus that he has done no wrong to the Jews (Acts 25:10).

The underlying image is always the disruption of what is right, fair, and orderly, leaving a deficit or damage that must be repaired.

4. Theological Implications

The use of adikeō in Philemon 1:18 sets the stage for one of the most vivid illustrations of substitution and imputation in the New Testament. Onesimus was guilty of adikeō—he had violated justice and owed a debt. Paul, acting as a mediator, steps between the offended master and the guilty runaway slave. By stating, "put that on my account," Paul offers to take upon himself the consequences of Onesimus's wrongdoing. This directly mirrors the work of Christ, who took our moral and spiritual adikeō—our sins and offenses against God—and had them imputed to His account on the cross (2 Corinthians 5:21).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In this short, personal letter, Paul is writing to reconcile a runaway slave, Onesimus, who has recently become a Christian, to his Christian master, Philemon. The conditional clause "if he has wronged you" (ei de ti ēdikēsen se) uses a first-class conditional statement, which assumes the condition to be true for the sake of the argument. Paul is not doubting that Onesimus did wrong; rather, he is tactfully acknowledging the reality of the offense without being overly accusatory. Onesimus likely stole money or possessions to fund his escape to Rome. By naming this wrong and immediately offering to assume the debt, Paul removes the primary legal and financial barrier to reconciliation between the two brothers in Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 25:10: "So Paul said, 'I stand at Caesar’s judgment seat, where I ought to be judged. To the Jews I have done no wrong, as you very well know.'"

  • 1 Corinthians 6:7: "Now therefore, it is already an utter failure for you that you go to law against one another. Why do you not rather accept wrong? Why do you not rather let yourselves be cheated?"

  • Colossians 3:25: "But he who does wrong will be repaid for what he has done, and there is no partiality."

  • Revelation 22:11: "He who is unjust, let him be unjust still; he who is filthy, let him be filthy still; he who is righteous, let him be righteous still; he who is holy, let him be holy still."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Ancient Roman law regarding runaway slaves (fugitivi) was incredibly harsh. A master had the legal right to punish a returned runaway with branding, crucifixion, or hard labor. Furthermore, anyone who harbored a runaway was legally liable to the owner for any financial loss incurred during the slave's absence. By offering to repay Philemon, Paul is not only acting as a spiritual father but is also taking on the formal, legal role of a sponsor or guarantor (fidejussor) under Roman law, legally binding himself to cover Onesimus's liabilities.
