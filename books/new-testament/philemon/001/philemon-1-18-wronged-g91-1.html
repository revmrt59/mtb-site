WRONGED - (adikéō, Strong's G91)

Summary:

The word adikeō refers to acting unjustly, committing a wrong, causing harm, or inflicting financial or personal injury on another. In Philemon 1:18, Paul uses this word to acknowledge the real injury or loss that Onesimus may have caused his master Philemon before running away. Rather than downplaying the offense, Paul directly addresses the damage done and assumes financial responsibility for it so that reconciliation can take place.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The letter to Philemon presents a deeply personal pastoral situation. Onesimus, a bondservant belonging to Philemon, had left his master's household and eventually encountered the apostle Paul while Paul was imprisoned. Under Paul's ministry, Onesimus became a Christian. In ancient Roman society, a servant who departed without permission often caused serious economic harm to the household, sometimes taking money or goods to fund their journey.

In Philemon 1:18, Paul addresses the practical obstacle standing between Philemon and Onesimus: the real or potential financial damage caused by Onesimus' departure. By using adikeō, Paul acknowledges that Philemon suffered a genuine grievance, while simultaneously stepping in to settle the account himself.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word adikeō combines the negation prefix with the root for justice or standard rightness. It can refer to:

  • Committing an injustice or breaking a moral or civil law.

  • Doing harm, damage, or physical injury to someone.

  • Causing financial loss or taking dishonest advantage of another person.

  • Acting unfairly or dishonestly toward someone who deserves fair treatment.

In Philemon 1:18, the word refers specifically to harm or material loss inflicted on a master by a servant. It describes an action that disrupted justice and created a real debt between the two parties.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, adikeō carries various shades of meaning depending on whether the setting is legal, personal, or divine.

In legal settings, such as Paul’s defense before Roman authorities in Acts, the word describes breaking established laws or committing a crime worthy of judicial punishment. In personal ethics, the word describes unfair treatment among believers, such as taking a Christian brother to court or defrauding him of property.

In statements concerning God's judgment, adikeō refers to moral evil and lawlessness that God will ultimately judge. The word always retains the idea of violating what is right and producing damage or unfair treatment in the process.

4. Theological Implications

Paul’s handling of adikeō in Philemon offers a clear view of gospel reconciliation:

  • True reconciliation does not ignore real offenses: Paul does not pretend that Onesimus did nothing wrong. For forgiveness to be meaningful, the reality of the wrong (adikeō) must be acknowledged.

  • Substitutionary responsibility: Paul models Christ-like mediation. Just as Christ bore the penalty for human sin, Paul offers to have Onesimus’ debt placed on his own personal account.

  • Grace restores justice: Gospel grace does not destroy justice; it satisfies it. By offering to repay whatever was lost, Paul preserves Philemon's rights while paving the way for Onesimus to be received as a brother in Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:18, Paul writes, "But if he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account."

Paul uses a condition that frames the matter gently, yet he does not minimize the action. The verb states as a simple past fact that an offense may have occurred. Paul pairs the verb adikeō ("wronged") with a phrase addressing debt ("owes anything"), covering both personal misconduct and specific financial loss.

By inserting himself as the guarantor ("put that on my account"), Paul removes the financial barrier preventing Philemon from forgiving Onesimus. The emphasis falls on Paul's willingness to absorb the cost of another person's wrong.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 20:13 — "Friend, I am doing you no wrong. Did you not agree with me for a denarius?"

  • 1 Corinthians 6:7 — "Why do you not rather accept wrong? Why do you not rather let yourselves be cheated?"

  • 2 Corinthians 7:12 — "Therefore, although I wrote to you, I did not do it for the sake of the one who had done the wrong, nor for the sake of the one who suffered wrong..."

  • Colossians 3:25 — "But he who does wrong will be repaid for what he has done, and there is no partiality."

  • Revelation 22:11 — "He who is unjust, let him be unjust still..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In verse 19, Paul follows up his statement in verse 18 by adding, "I, Paul, am writing with my own hand. I will repay it." In ancient business contexts, handwriting a pledge served as a legal promissory note. By formalizing his offer to pay for the wrong (adikeō), Paul transformed a potential legal dispute into a demonstration of Christian love and partnership.
