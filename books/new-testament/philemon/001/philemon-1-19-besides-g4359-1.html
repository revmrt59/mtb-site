BESIDES - (prosopheílō, Strong's G4359)

Summary:

The Greek verb prosopheilō means "to owe in addition" or "to owe besides." It combines a preposition meaning "in addition" with a standard verb meaning "to owe" or "to be in debt." In Philemon 1:19, Paul uses this word to gently remind Philemon that while Paul is personally offering to pay any financial loss caused by the runaway servant Onesimus, Philemon himself owes Paul an even greater debt—his very spiritual life and salvation in Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The letter to Philemon is a personal appeal written by the apostle Paul while in prison. Onesimus, a bondservant belonging to Philemon, had fled from his master and eventually encountered Paul. Through Paul’s ministry, Onesimus came to faith in Jesus Christ.

Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon not as a mere fugitive, but as a beloved brother in the Lord. Knowing that Onesimus may have stolen money or caused financial loss when he left, Paul personally stepped in to cover the cost. Writing with his own hand to make the pledge legally binding, Paul tells Philemon to charge any outstanding debt to Paul's account. In verse 19, Paul uses prosopheilō to introduce a quiet but powerful reality: while Paul is willing to pay Onesimus's monetary debt, Philemon already owes Paul an additional debt that money cannot measure.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word prosopheilō is a compound verb formed from two Greek elements:

  • pros, a preposition meaning "to," "toward," or "in addition to"

  • opheilō, a common verb meaning "to owe money," "to be in debt," or "to have an obligation"

Together, the compound word means "to owe in addition" or "to be indebted besides." In standard usage, it referred to stacking an extra obligation onto an existing account or owing someone a further debt beyond what was currently being discussed.

In Philemon 1:19, the word describes spiritual indebtedness. Philemon is in debt to Paul for his spiritual rebirth, because Paul was the instrument God used to bring him to faith in Christ.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

This Greek word appears in a single biblical setting. Because of this, its specific nuance is shaped entirely by the surrounding dialogue in Paul's letter to Philemon.

Paul engages in a masterclass of gentle, gracious persuasion. He uses a well-known ancient rhetorical device called paralipsis—stating that he will not mention something while simultaneously mentioning it. Paul writes, "I will repay it—not to mention to you that you owe me even your own self besides." By inserting prosopheilō, Paul sets up a clear contrast between financial balance sheets and spiritual debts. Onesimus may owe Philemon money, but Philemon owes Paul his very self.

4. Theological Implications

This word brings several important biblical truths into focus:

  • Spiritual Debt vs. Financial Debt: Earthly debts can be measured in gold and silver, but the value of hearing the gospel and receiving eternal life is beyond physical calculation.

  • The Interconnectedness of Grace: Paul does not demand that Philemon discharge his debt by obeying a command. Instead, he highlights their shared gospel history to invite Philemon to act out of love.

  • Reflecting Christ’s Mediation: Paul’s willingness to cover Onesimus's financial obligation while pointing Philemon to his debt of grace mirrors the gospel itself. Believers who have been forgiven an unpayable spiritual debt by God are called to extend forgiveness and grace to others.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:17–19, Paul places himself directly between Philemon and Onesimus. He asks Philemon to receive Onesimus as he would receive Paul himself. If Onesimus wronged Philemon or owed him anything, Paul instructs Philemon to put that debt on Paul's personal account.

Paul then writes, "I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand. I will repay it." This statement functioned like a signed promissory note. But Paul immediately balances the equation by adding, "not to mention to you that you owe me even your own self besides."

The verb prosopheilō exposes the minor nature of Onesimus's financial debt when placed alongside the immense spiritual gift Philemon received through Paul's ministry. Paul is not leveraging this debt to bully Philemon, but to reframe the entire situation through the lens of gospel generosity. If Philemon remembers how much he has received, forgiving Onesimus becomes a natural response of grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • This term is unique to this context and does not appear elsewhere in the biblical corpus.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The verb in Philemon 1:19 addresses Philemon directly in the second person singular, emphasizing his individual, ongoing spiritual obligation to Paul.

Paul's use of commercial terms—such as "account," "repay," and "owe besides"—shows how everyday business language can be used to communicate deep spiritual truths. By placing physical debts next to spiritual debts, Paul helps believers see that earthly grievances fade when weighed against the gift of eternal life in Christ.
