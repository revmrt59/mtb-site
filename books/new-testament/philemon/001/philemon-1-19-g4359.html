OWE - ( prosopeilo , Strong's G4359 )

Summary:

In Philemon 1:19, the compound Greek verb prosopeilo carries the meaning of owing something "in addition" or "besides." Paul utilizes this accounting term with warm, pastoral irony to remind Philemon that while Paul is willing to personally cover any financial loss caused by the runaway slave Onesimus, Philemon actually owes Paul his very soul. Because Philemon came to saving faith in Jesus Christ through Paul's ministry, he is under a profound spiritual obligation that far outweighs any material debt Onesimus could ever accumulate.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word prosopeilo is a compound verb made of two parts:

  • The preposition pros, which means "to," "toward," or "in addition to."

  • The verb opheilo, which means "to owe" or "to be in debt."

In ancient Greek commercial and legal transactions, prosopeilo was used to record an additional or surplus debt on an account ledger. It pictured a situation where a person, while auditing a financial statement, realizes that the party claiming a debt actually owes a much larger, outstanding balance "on top of" the original amount. It is the picture of a double-indebtedness where the secondary debt completely dwarfs the primary claim.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term means to owe besides, to owe in addition, or to be further indebted. It refers to a lingering obligation that remains outstanding even after other accounts are settled. In the vocabulary of the Hellenistic world, it was strictly a bookkeeping term used to describe a balance due that exceeded previous calculations. In the New Testament, this word is a hapax legomenon, occurring only this one time in Philemon 1:19.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Paul constructs this entire section of Philemon using highly specific legal and financial terminology:

  • In verse 18, he uses elloga ("put that on my account") and opheilei ("owes").

  • In verse 19, he writes apotiso ("I will repay") and signs it like a formal promissory note ("I, Paul, am writing with my own hand").

  • He climaxes this ledger sheet metaphor with prosopeilo ("you owe me... besides").

The word picture is brilliant: Paul places Philemon's grievance against Onesimus on one side of the scale, balances it by offering his own resources to pay it off, and then drops Philemon's massive spiritual debt to Paul onto the other side of the scale. The "addition" (pros-) of Philemon's spiritual debt immediately bottoms out the scale, rendering Onesimus's financial debt utterly insignificant.

4. Theological Implications

This term highlights the vast difference between material and spiritual values in the economy of God's grace. Financial debts are finite, quantifiable, and can be repaid with silver or gold. Spiritual debts—such as the debt of hearing the Gospel, receiving eternal life, and being brought into the family of God—are infinite and can never be repaid in material terms.

Furthermore, it illustrates the profound bond of spiritual fatherhood. Those who bring the Gospel to others place those recipients in a unique debt of love and honor. While salvation is a free gift from God, believers owe a debt of gratitude, respect, and mutual ministry to the human instruments God used to bring them to saving faith.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:19, Paul is dealing with a delicate social and legal crisis. Under Roman law, Philemon had the legal right to severely punish or even execute Onesimus for running away and potentially stealing from him.

Paul does not demand that Philemon waive his rights; instead, he appeals to a higher law of love and spiritual reality. By using prosopeilo, Paul gently but firmly reminds Philemon of his own spiritual bankruptcy before he met Christ. Paul's logic is inescapable: "If you want to settle accounts on a strict, transactional basis, let us look at the master ledger. You owe me your very life. If I am willing to forgive your spiritual debt to me, you must be willing to forgive Onesimus's material debt to you." It is a pastoral appeal designed to melt Philemon's resentment through the recognition of the overwhelming grace he himself had received.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because prosopeilo is a hapax legomenon (occurring only once in the New Testament), there are no other biblical verses containing this exact compound verb. However, its primary root word opheilo (Strong's G3784), which means "to owe" or "to be indebted," is used in similar contexts of financial and moral obligation:

  • Romans 13:8: "Owe no one anything except to love one another, for he who loves another has fulfilled the law."

  • Matthew 18:28: "But that servant went out and found one of his fellow servants who owed him a hundred denarii; and he laid hands on him and took him by the throat, saying, 'Pay me what you owe!'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul employs a classical rhetorical device here called paraleipsis (or preterition), introduced by the phrase "not to say to you" (hina me lego soi). This is a literary technique where a writer states that they are not going to bring up a certain point, but by stating that they will not bring it up, they actually emphasize it perfectly. It allows Paul to exert maximum spiritual leverage on Philemon without sounding heavy-handed, dictatorial, or authoritarian. It preserves Philemon’s dignity while powerfully guiding his conscience toward mercy.
