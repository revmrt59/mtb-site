REPAY - ( apotinō , Strong's G661 )

Summary:

The Greek word apotinō refers to the act of making full restitution, paying back a debt, or compensating for damages. It was a technical, legal, and financial term widely used in ancient contracts and promissory notes to guarantee that any financial obligation or loss would be personally made good. In Philemon 1:19, Paul uses this strong legal term, coupled with his own physical signature, to promise Philemon that he will personally cover any financial loss or theft caused by the runaway slave Onesimus. This act of assuming another's debt serves as a beautiful, practical illustration of substitution and the gospel of grace.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word apotinō is a compound verb derived from apo, meaning "from" or "away" (often indicating completion or return), and tinō, meaning "to pay a price" or "to pay a penalty." Historically, in classical Greek and ancient papyri, apotinō carried a highly formal, legal weight. It was the standard term used in commercial contracts, treaties, and marriage dowry agreements to specify the legal liability of a party to compensate for damages, losses, or default. It evokes the image of a formal, legally binding promissory note that guarantees restitution.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb apotinō means to pay off, make compensation, or repay in full. It denotes a complete settlement of a financial or legal obligation. The word implies that a debt exists, and the person invoking apotinō is taking on the absolute responsibility to satisfy that debt so that the injured party is left without any loss.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Greco-Roman world, if a slave ran away, they often stole money or provisions to fund their escape, causing significant financial loss to their owner. The legal remedy was for the owner to seek full restitution (apotinō). By stepping into this legal framework, Paul uses a profound visual aid. He tells Philemon to charge Onesimus’s debt to his own account. To make this pledge legally binding, Paul writes the promise with his "own hand" (emē cheiri). This action mirrors the ancient practice of writing a personal promissory note (cheirographon), which carried the weight of a modern signed check or contract.

4. Theological Implications

While apotinō is a secular, financial term in this context, it carries profound theological resonance. It serves as a vivid picture of the doctrine of imputation and substitutionary atonement.

  • Imputation of Sin: Just as Paul tells Philemon to put Onesimus’s debt onto his account, our sins and spiritual debts were placed on Christ's account.

  • Substitutionary Payment: Paul’s promise, "I will repay," mirrors the work of Jesus Christ, who went to the cross to pay the debt of sin that we could never afford to pay.

  • The Ledger of Grace: Paul balances this legal transaction by reminding Philemon of his spiritual debt. While Paul is willing to legally resolve Onesimus's material debt, Philemon owes Paul his very soul because Paul brought him the gospel. This highlights how grace completely eclipses strict human bookkeeping.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:19, Paul is pleading for the restoration of Onesimus, who has become a brother in Christ. Paul does not minimize the wrong Onesimus committed; he acknowledges that a real debt may be owed. However, instead of demanding that Philemon simply "forget" the debt—which might violate Philemon's sense of justice—Paul offers to pay it himself. By using apotinō, Paul transforms a potentially tense legal and financial dispute into an opportunity to demonstrate gospel-centered love and reconciliation. He secures Onesimus's freedom and forgiveness by absorbing the cost of his transgression.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The verb apotinō is a hapax legomenon in the New Testament, appearing only in Philemon 1:19. However, the word appears frequently in the Septuagint (LXX), the Greek translation of the Old Testament, to describe the legal requirements for restitution under the Mosaic Law:

  • Exodus 21:34 - "the owner of the pit shall make it good [LXX: apotisei]; he shall give money to their owner, but the dead beast shall be his."

  • Exodus 22:1 - "If a man steals an ox or a sheep, and slaughters it or sells it, he shall restore [LXX: apotisei] five oxen for an ox and four sheep for a sheep."

  • Exodus 22:3 - "If the sun has risen on him, there shall be guilt for his bloodshed. He should make full restitution [LXX: apotisatō]; if he has nothing, then he shall be sold for his theft."

  • Exodus 22:14 - "And if a man borrows anything from his neighbor, and it is injured or dies, the owner of it not being with it, he shall surely make it good [LXX: apotinnuōn apotisei]."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The personal signature of Paul in Philemon 1:19 is a rare and deliberate act. Typically, Paul dictated his letters to an amanuensis (a secretary) and would only sign a brief greeting at the very end to prove the letter's authenticity (as seen in 2 Thessalonians 3:17). In this letter, however, Paul interrupts the flow of dictation, takes the pen, and personally writes the legal guarantee: "I, Paul, am writing with my own hand. I will repay." This personal touch added undeniable moral and legal gravity to his promise, making it impossible for Philemon to doubt Paul's commitment to resolve the matter.
