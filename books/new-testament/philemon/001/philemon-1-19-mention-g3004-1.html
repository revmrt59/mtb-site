MENTION - (légō, Strong's G3004)

Summary:

capabilities

The word lego is the standard Greek verb for speaking, saying, telling, or making mention of something. Depending on the context, it ranges from ordinary conversation to formal statements or subtle references. In Philemon 1:19, Paul uses the word in a rhetorical turn—stating that he will not "mention" something while actually raising it—to gently remind Philemon that he owes his very spiritual life to Paul while offering to pay Onesimus's debt out of his own pocket.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Letter to Philemon is a short, personal appeal written by the apostle Paul from prison. Onesimus, a slave who belonged to Philemon (a Christian leader in Colosse), had run away and eventually met Paul. Under Paul's ministry, Onesimus became a believer.

Paul sends Onesimus back to Philemon, not as a mere runaway slave returning to punishment, but as a beloved brother in Christ. Because Onesimus may have stolen from Philemon or caused financial loss by leaving, Paul takes personal responsibility for those debts. In verse 19, Paul writes a legal promise of repayment, using the word "mention" to bring up Philemon's own spiritual debt in a delicate, tactful manner.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lego is one of the most common verbs in the New Testament. It means "to say," "to speak," "to tell," "to declare," or "to mention." The word itself is simple and direct; it does not carry a complex or hidden theological meaning on its own. Its precise emphasis always comes from the context in which it is spoken.

When translated "mention" in Philemon 1:19, the word describes bringing a subject into a discussion or putting a thought into words. Paul uses the verb here within a phrase that expresses polite understatement, letting the idea be clearly understood without delivering a blunt command.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, lego serves many conversational and formal roles:

  • Direct Speech: It introduces ordinary statements and answers in daily dialogue.

  • Scriptural Authority: It frequently introduces Old Testament quotations, pointing to what God or a prophet "says."

  • Teaching and Preaching: Jesus and the apostles use it to introduce parables, moral instructions, and divine truths.

  • Rhetorical Framing: Writers use it to structure an argument, clarify an earlier statement, or emphasize a specific point.

In Philemon 1:19, the verb appears in a purpose clause expressing restraint ("so that I might not say"). Paul explicitly claims he will refrain from bringing something up, even as he states it directly in the same sentence.

4. Theological Implications

While the verb lego simply means to say or mention, Paul's use of it in this passage highlights vital biblical principles regarding grace, debt, and Christian relationships:

  • Substitutionary Love: Paul's offer to cover Onesimus's financial debt reflects the spirit of Christ, who bore the debt of sin for believers.

  • The Debt of Grace: By reminding Philemon that he owes his "own self" to Paul's gospel ministry, Paul frames all human relationships within the shadow of God's greater grace. Philemon received spiritual life through the gospel Paul preached; therefore, Philemon ought to show mercy to Onesimus.

  • Persuasion over Coercion: Paul had the apostolic authority to command Philemon, but he chose instead to appeal through love, using gentle tact rather than harsh demands.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:19, Paul writes, "I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand. I will repay—not to mention to you that you owe me even your own self besides."

The contrast in this verse is remarkable. Paul takes up the pen himself to write a binding pledge to repay whatever financial damage Onesimus caused. Immediately after making this commitment, Paul uses lego in a classic statement of polite understatement: "not to mention to you that you owe me even your own self besides."

By using this turn of phrase, Paul accomplishes two things at once:

1. He avoids making a legalistic demand on Philemon, keeping their partnership rooted in voluntary love.

2. He clearly reminds Philemon that any financial sum Onesimus owes is tiny compared to the spiritual debt Philemon owes Paul for bringing him the gospel.

The grammatical form of the verb sets up a conditional phrasing ("lest I say"), allowing Paul to raise the matter with warmth and light humor while leaving the moral weight of the decision in Philemon's hands.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's approach in Philemon offers a model for Christian communication in difficult circumstances. Rather than ignoring real debts or using power to crush another person, Paul combines personal sacrifice with clear truth-telling. His subtle use of language shows how deep theological truths—such as forgiveness, reconciliation, and spiritual debt—can be applied practically and graciously in everyday relationships.
