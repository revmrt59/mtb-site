NOT - (hína mḗ, Strong's G3363)

Summary:

The word hina mē is a Greek conjunction formed by combining two words to express negative purpose or intended prevention. It is commonly translated as "so that not," "in order that not," "lest," or "not to." It explains the reason for taking an action to prevent an undesirable outcome or introduces a polite conversational boundary.

In Philemon 1:19, hina mē is rendered as "not" within the expression "not to mention." Paul uses it to make a tactful, gentle statement to Philemon. While offering to pay any money owed by the runaway servant Onesimus, Paul uses this phrase to remind Philemon politely that Philemon owes his very spiritual life to Paul, encouraging him to act out of love rather than obligation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The term hina mē combines two basic Greek particles: hina, which points to a purpose or goal ("in order that"), and mē, which negates that outcome ("not"). Joined together, they form a standard conjunction used across the ancient Greek world to state a negative purpose or intended result.

In first-century letters and legal agreements, written promises of repayment were common. When Paul wrote his personal letter to Philemon, he was intervening on behalf of Onesimus, a bondservant who had fled from Philemon but subsequently came to faith in Christ through Paul's ministry. In writing to Philemon, Paul assumes financial liability for any loss caused by Onesimus, using the pen to make a formal pledge while appealing to Philemon's heart as a Christian brother.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Hina mē functions as a negative conjunction. It can mean:

  • "so that not"

  • "in order that not"

  • "lest"

  • "not to [do or say something]"

When used to state a purpose, the word identifies an action taken specifically to prevent an outcome. In conversational contexts, it can introduce a polite caveat or deliberate restraint, equivalent to saying "so that I need not say" or "not to mention."

In Philemon 1:19, it serves this polite conversational function, allowing the writer to bring up an important truth while explicitly stating a desire not to press the matter heavily.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, writers use hina mē when giving commands, stating warnings, or explaining the reasons behind an action. It often guards against spiritual dangers or unwanted results, such as falling into temptation, acting out of pride, or causing offense.

The phrase frequently highlights divine protection and human responsibility:

  • It marks God's goal to protect His people from spiritual ruin.

  • It sets boundaries to prevent human arrogance or boastfulness.

  • It frames polite, respectful communication between believers.

In Philemon 1:19, the term acts as a gracious transitional phrase. Rather than issuing an authoritative command, Paul frames his statement as something he ought not need to say out loud, trusting that Philemon will understand the point without harsh pressure.

4. Theological Implications

The use of hina mē in Philemon 1:19 reflects the spirit of gospel ministry and spiritual leadership. Paul demonstrates that true Christian leadership relies on love and willingness rather than legalistic force.

Although Paul possessed apostolic authority to command Philemon, he chose to appeal to him on the basis of Christian fellowship. By offering to pay Onesimus's debt, Paul reflects the self-sacrificing love of Christ. By using hina mē to gently mention Philemon's own spiritual debt, Paul highlights the reality that every believer has received unmerited grace from God through others, which should make them eager to grant grace to those who have wronged them.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:19, Paul writes, "I, Paul, am writing with my own hand. I will repay—not to mention to you that you owe me even your own self besides."

Paul takes up the pen himself to write a personal guarantee to cover whatever financial loss Philemon suffered. Immediately following this pledge, hina mē introduces a delicate balance:

  • On one hand, Paul promises to settle any physical or financial debt.

  • On the other hand, Paul gently reminds Philemon that he owes his spiritual conversion and eternal hope to Paul's ministry.

By using "not to mention" (hina mē), Paul brings Philemon's spiritual debt into view without making an overt demand. The phrase allows Paul to express deep affection and mutual obligation while giving Philemon the dignity of responding freely out of love.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 22:32 – Jesus prayed for Peter "that" his faith should "not" fail during his coming trial.

  • John 12:46 – Jesus came as light into the world so "that" whoever believes in Him should "not" abide in darkness.

  • Ephesians 2:9 – Salvation is granted by grace through faith and "not" of works, "lest" anyone should boast.

  • Hebrews 4:11 – Believers are exhorted to be diligent "lest" anyone fall into the same example of disobedience.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The expression in Philemon 1:19 provides a classic example of gracious persuasion in biblical literature. Rather than forcing compliance, the speaker mentions a truth while framing it politely, appealing to the listener's conscience and gratitude.

This passage illustrates how Christian relationships are transformed by the gospel. Financial matters and personal offenses are evaluated in light of the vast debt of grace that God has already forgiven in Christ.
