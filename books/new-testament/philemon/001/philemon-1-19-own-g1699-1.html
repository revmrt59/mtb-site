OWN - (emós, Strong's G1699)

Summary:

The word emos is a possessive term meaning "my," "mine," or "my own." It indicates that a person, object, statement, or action belongs directly to the speaker. In Philemon 1:19, Paul uses the word when he writes "with my own hand." By taking the pen to write this phrase, Paul transforms his appeal for Onesimus into a personally signed guarantee, showing his direct willingness to take personal responsibility for any debt owed to Philemon.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Paul wrote his short letter to Philemon while under Roman imprisonment, sending it back with Onesimus, a runaway servant who had come to faith in Christ through Paul's ministry. Under ancient Roman legal standards, a runaway servant faced severe consequences. If Onesimus had stolen property or caused financial loss upon departure, those debts remained legally binding under Roman customs.

Letters in the ancient Greco-Roman world were routinely dictated to a secretary. At the end of a document, the author would often take the pen to write a final greeting or signature, validating the letter's authenticity. In Philemon 1:19, Paul takes the pen directly into his hand to write a personal guarantee, making his pledge to cover Philemon's losses explicit and legally binding in the eyes of his reader.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word emos is a possessive pronoun and adjective meaning "my," "mine," or "belonging to me." It identifies something as the personal property, attribute, statement, or action of the speaker.

Unlike general possessive forms that simply state a relationship, emos often highlights the distinct personal connection between the speaker and the item mentioned. Depending on context, it can refer to personal possessions, personal words, personal motives, or direct personal involvement. In Philemon 1:19, it modifies the word "hand," underscoring that Paul himself physically wrote this section of the letter.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In New Testament usage, emos appears across the Gospels and Epistles to emphasize personal identity, origin, and ownership.

Jesus frequently uses the term to distinguish His words, works, and authority from human initiative or worldly standards. For instance, He speaks of "My teaching," "My peace," or "My own will." In those settings, the word highlights the precise source and unique character of what He gives or does.

When used by the apostles in the epistles, emos often underscores personal signature, deep affection, or apostolic commitment. In Paul's letters, writing "with my own hand" serves as a physical mark of authenticity and a sign of sincere pastoral care for the readers.

4. Theological Implications

While emos itself is a simple possessive word meaning "my own," its use in Philemon 1:19 highlights how gospel-driven love assumes tangible responsibility for others.

Paul does not merely offer verbal encouragement regarding the reconciliation of Philemon and Onesimus. By pledging his own signature and financial resources, Paul places himself between the offended party and the debtor. His action reflects the broader pattern of the gospel, where Christ takes personal responsibility for debts He did not owe so that believers might be reconciled to God and to one another.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:18–19, Paul addresses the practical financial obstacle standing in the way of reconciliation. He tells Philemon, "If he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account." Then, in verse 19, he adds, "I, Paul, am writing with my own hand. I will repay it."

The word emos matches the dative case, singular number, and feminine gender of the word for "hand." This personal emphasis establishes a formal commitment. Paul could have simply dictated a promise, but by physically writing this sentence with his own hand, he creates a personal obligation. It functions like a signed promissory note, removing any financial barrier that might hinder Philemon from receiving Onesimus as a brother in Christ.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient correspondence, taking the pen from a secretary to write closing remarks served two main purposes: authenticating the letter against forgery and expressing warm personal regard. By adding his own handwriting to a financial guarantee, Paul combines personal affection with serious commitment. He demonstrates that Christian fellowship involves concrete responsibility, rather than abstract advice or mere legal demand.
