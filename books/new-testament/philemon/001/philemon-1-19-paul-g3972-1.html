PAUL - (Paûlos, Strong's G3972)

Paulos ( G3972 )

Summary:

In biblical Greek, Paulos is a proper noun derived from a Latin surname meaning "small" or "little." It serves in the New Testament as the Roman name of the apostle, who was also known by his Hebrew name, Saul.

In Philemon 1:19, the name Paul appears within a written pledge. The apostle writes his own name with his own hand to give a binding guarantee that he will personally cover any debt owed by the runaway slave Onesimus to his master Philemon.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Paulos is the Greek form of the Latin surname Paulus. In the ancient Roman world, citizens held multiple names, including a family name or surname. As a Jew born in the Cilician city of Tarsus who inherited Roman citizenship, the apostle held both a Hebrew name (Saul) and a Roman name (Paul).

In the book of Acts, Luke uses the Hebrew name Saul while describing his early life and Jewish ministry in Jerusalem and Damascus. However, when his ministry expands across the broader Greco-Roman world, the text explicitly notes his Roman name, and Paul becomes his primary name throughout his letters and the remainder of Acts.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Paulos is a proper noun, specifically a Roman personal name.

  • Etymologically, the Latin name Paulus means "small," "little," or "slight."

  • It was a common Roman surname used across various social classes.

  • Scripture does not connect this meaning to Paul's physical stature or personal character. It was simply his given Roman name from birth or youth.

  • In Philemon 1:19, the name identifies the apostle himself as he writes a personal promissory note.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, Paulos is used to identify the apostle who carried the gospel to the Gentiles. In ancient letter writing, authors usually dictated their letters to a secretary, adding a short closing greeting in their own handwriting to verify authenticity.

When Paul includes his own name inside the body of a letter alongside a pledge written with his own hand, he elevates the letter from an informal letter of request to an official legal guarantee. In Roman law, a handwritten promise bearing the author's name functioned as a binding promissory note.

4. Theological Implications

Although the name Paulos itself carries no inherent theological definition, Paul's action in this passage provides a clear picture of gospel grace. By attaching his personal name to the debt of Onesimus, Paul acts as a guarantor and peacemaker.

He places himself between a offended master and a runaway servant, offering to pay a debt he did not cause so that reconciliation can occur. This offer mirrors the larger work of Jesus Christ, who took upon Himself the debt of human sin to reconcile believers to God. Paul's personal pledge demonstrates how gospel grace operates in human relationships.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:19, Paul writes: "I, Paul, am writing this with my own hand. I will repay it—not to mention to you that you owe me even your own self besides."

The insertion of his name at this specific moment is deliberate. Grammatically, the word appears in the nominative case as the subject of the sentence, emphasizing his personal commitment. By stating "I, Paul," he leaves no doubt about who assumes financial liability for Onesimus.

Paul balances this legal guarantee with a gentle pastoral reminder. While he offers to pay whatever money Onesimus owes, he reminds Philemon that Philemon owes his spiritual life to Paul's ministry. Paul uses his apostolic authority not to demand compliance, but to appeal to Philemon's heart through love and shared spiritual fellowship.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient society, formal debts were documented on written tablets or parchments known as bond notes. If someone wished to assume another person's debt, they would write out a personal obligation in their own handwriting.

By physically writing "I, Paul... I will repay it," the apostle gave Philemon an undeniable commitment. This physical act converted his appeal for mercy into an official act of substitution, ensuring that Onesimus could return to Philemon without fear of financial punishment.
