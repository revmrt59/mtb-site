WRITING - (gráphō, Strong's G1125)

Summary:

this word refers to tracing letters or recording words in writing to create a written message or document. In Philemon 1:19, Paul uses the word as he personally takes the stylus in his own hand to write a legally binding promissory note, pledging to pay whatever debt or financial damage Onesimus owed to Philemon.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greco-Roman world, letter writing was a standard form of long-distance communication. Most educated or prominent individuals hired professional scribes (amanuenses) to take dictation. The sender would normally dictate the text of the letter and then personally write a short closing greeting or sign their name at the bottom. This final handwritten note served as a seal of authenticity, confirming that the letter genuinely came from the sender.

In the New Testament world, written documents carried significant legal and social weight. Contracts, receipts, decrees, and personal promises became official once recorded in writing. When Paul wrote his letter to Philemon, he was imprisoned. Although he likely dictated most of the epistle, he paused at verse 19 to personally write out a specific sentence. By taking the pen himself, Paul transformed his letter into an official IOUs or promissory note on behalf of a runaway slave.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb graphō means to engrave, carve, trace characters, or record words on a writing surface. In general Greek usage, it covers any physical act of writing, whether drafting a personal letter, recording a civic decree, penning a legal contract, or composing literature.

In biblical usage, the word can refer to several distinct types of written material:

  • Recording divine revelation or quoting Old Testament Scriptures ("it is written").

  • Penning apostolic letters to churches or individuals to instruct and encourage them.

  • Writing formal legal notices, financial debts, or public inscriptions.

In Philemon 1:19, the word describes Paul physically penning the words himself: "I, Paul, am writing with my own hand. I will repay." Here it means making a written, legally accountable pledge to settle a debt.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, graphō appears in various settings that illustrate the power of the written word.

First, the word frequently introduces Old Testament quotations in the Gospels and Epistles. The phrase "it is written" points to the permanent authority and unchanging truth of God's Word. What God inspired in writing stands settled and binding.

Second, the word describes the ministry of the apostles as they instructed the early church. Written letters allowed apostles like Paul, Peter, and John to exercise authority, teach doctrine, and care for congregations across vast distances. Written instructions provided a standard that churches could read, re-read, and preserve.

Third, the word applies to formal records and debt notes. A written agreement held a debtor or guarantor accountable. In Philemon 1:19, Paul draws on this formal sense. By stating that he is personally writing the commitment, Paul assumes financial liability for Onesimus in a clear and undisputed way.

4. Theological Implications

Paul's personal act of writing in Philemon 1:19 reflects the heart of the gospel. Onesimus was a runaway slave who had likely caused financial loss or stolen from his master, Philemon. Under Roman law, Onesimus faced severe punishment, and he had no means to pay back what he owed.

Paul stepped between the offended master and the indebted slave. By writing "I will repay" with his own hand, Paul acted as a guarantor, transferring Onesimus's debt onto his own account. This practical action mirrors the work of Jesus Christ, who took upon Himself the debt of human sin. Where believers were hopelessly indebted and unable to satisfy divine justice, Christ placed Himself as our substitute, canceling our spiritual debt through His death on the cross.

Furthermore, Paul's gesture demonstrates that Christian love is concrete. It does not merely offer spiritual advice; it assumes practical responsibility to heal broken human relationships and bring reconciliation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The letter to Philemon is one of Paul's shortest and most personal epistles. Onesimus had met Paul while the apostle was in chains, had come to faith in Christ, and had become a valuable helper to Paul. However, Christian fellowship required resolving the unresolved conflict between Onesimus and his Christian master, Philemon. Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon, accompanied by this letter.

In verse 19, Paul's sentence structure changes to a direct personal pledge: "I, Paul, am writing with my own hand. I will repay." The verb is an aorist active indicative form, which presents Paul's action as a clear, decisive statement. Paul is not making a vague gesture of goodwill; he is executing a formal, binding promise right at that moment in the letter.

To make the pledge legally undisputed, Paul explicitly states that he is writing it "with my own hand." In ancient practice, a handwritten line from the author made the document legally enforceable. Paul removes any financial obstacle that might prevent Philemon from forgiving and receiving Onesimus as a brother in Christ. At the same time, Paul gently reminds Philemon of a deeper reality: "not to mention to you that you owe me even your own self besides." Philemon owed his spiritual salvation to Paul's gospel ministry, putting Paul's financial offer in a gracious light.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 19:19 – Pontius Pilate "wrote" a title and put it on the cross: "JESUS OF NAZARETH, THE KING OF THE JEWS," showing how a written statement served as an official public declaration.

  • 2 Corinthians 7:12 – Paul reflects on a previous letter and explains why he "wrote" to them, illustrating how written apostolic communication was used for pastoral care and spiritual restoration.

  • 1 John 2:12 – John tells his readers, "I write to you, little children, because your sins are forgiven you for His name’s sake," showing how written text conveys settled spiritual assurance.

  • Revelation 14:13 – A voice from heaven commands John, "Write: 'Blessed are the dead who die in the Lord from now on,'" demonstrating how written divine command preserves God's promises for future generations.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient commercial transactions, a handwritten bond of debt was called a cheirographon (literally "hand-writing"). When a person wrote out a debt obligation by hand, it stood as unassailable proof of what was owed.

In Philemon 1:19, Paul creates a handwritten obligation of his own free will, turning a potential legal liability for Onesimus into an act of sacrificial love. Rather than forcing Philemon to absorb the financial loss or leaving Onesimus in jeopardy, Paul personally absorbs the cost. This blend of legal precision and Christian grace highlights how biblical faith operates practically in everyday social and economic matters.
