CHURCH - (ekklēsía, Strong's G1577)

Summary:

In the Greco-Roman world, ekklēsia described an assembly of citizens gathered for public business. In the New Testament, believers used this word to describe the assembly of God's people gathered in Christ. In Philemon 1:2, the word refers specifically to the local congregation that met in Philemon's home. Paul addresses his letter not only to Philemon as an individual, but to the entire house church, placing Philemon's personal decision regarding Onesimus within the supportive witness and care of his local Christian community.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek society, ekklēsia was a common term for an assembly or public gathering. In Greek city-states, it referred to the official assembly of citizens summoned to conduct civic affairs. When Jewish scholars translated the Old Testament into Greek (the Septuagint), they used ekklēsia to describe the assembly of the congregation of Israel gathered before God in the wilderness.

By the time of the New Testament, early Christians adopted this word to describe their own gatherings. Rather than viewing themselves as an isolated philosophical club or a private religious society, believers saw themselves as the assembly of God's people called together by Jesus Christ. In cities across the Roman Empire, early Christians did not yet own dedicated church buildings. Instead, the local ekklēsia met in private homes, making the household a primary center for worship, fellowship, and mutual care.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

distributions of meaning for ekklēsia focus on an "assembly," "gathering," or "congregation." It refers fundamentally to a group of people gathered together rather than to a physical structure or building.

In New Testament usage, ekklēsia carries two primary senses depending on context:

  • The local assembly: A specific, visible group of believers meeting in a particular location (such as a city or a private household).

  • The universal church: The entire body of believers across space and time who belong to Jesus Christ.

In Philemon 1:2, the word refers directly to the local assembly meeting in Philemon's residence ("the church in your house"). It highlights a physical, relational community of believers who gather regularly in a specific domestic setting.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, ekklēsia is used flexibly to describe Christian gatherings at different scales. At times, it designates the whole global body of believers united under Christ as Head. At other times, it refers to all believers living within a specific city or region. Frequently in the New Testament letters, it refers to a small home assembly—the local group of believers meeting under a specific roof.

The word always emphasizes people rather than places. The early church owned no real estate and constructed no sacred buildings; the ekklēsia was the community itself. Whether meeting in a public hall, by a riverbank, or inside a private home, believers constituted the church whenever they gathered in the name of the Lord.

4. Theological Implications

The choice of ekklēsia carries practical theological significance for Christian community:

First, it shows that faith in Christ is inherently communal. A believer is called not into isolated individual spirituality, but into an assembled body of fellow disciples.

Second, the household setting in Philemon 1:2 reflects how the gospel transforms everyday spaces. A private residence became a holy place where the word was taught, prayers were offered, and believers served one another.

Third, addressing the ekklēsia in a letter concerning a private dynamic (a master and a returning bondservant) shows that personal relationships among Christians are matters of shared concern and support. Christian reconciliation is not merely a private agreement; it is nurtured and witnessed within the fellowship of the church.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:2, Paul addresses his letter to "Philemon our beloved friend and fellow laborer, to the beloved Apphia, Archippus our fellow soldier, and to the church in your house." Grammatically, the dative form marks the local church as a co-recipient of Paul's greeting alongside Philemon and his family.

This opening address shapes the entire letter. Although the epistle deals primarily with Philemon's personal relationship with his runaway servant Onesimus, Paul intentionally includes the whole local assembly meeting in Philemon's home.

By including "the church in your house," Paul places Philemon's decision within a supportive, grace-filled community. Philemon is being asked to receive Onesimus back no longer merely as a bondservant, but as a beloved brother in Christ. By addressing the church, Paul ensures that when Onesimus returns, he will be welcomed by the entire local body. The home church provides both the environment of grace that encourages Philemon to forgive and the community that will embrace Onesimus into fellowship.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greco-Roman world, households were structured strictly around legal authority and social rank. When a local ekklēsia met within a household, those traditional social barriers were reshaped by the gospel. In the house church, masters and servants sat together at the same table and called one another brothers and sisters. Paul's greeting to "the church in your house" reminded Philemon that inside his home, a new community met—one where gospel grace guided how members of the household treated one another.
