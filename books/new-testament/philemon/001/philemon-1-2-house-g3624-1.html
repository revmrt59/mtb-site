HOUSE - (oîkos, Strong's G3624)

Summary:

The Greek noun oikos refers to a dwelling place, physical house, family line, or household. In Philemon 1:2, it identifies the physical home of Philemon, which served as the regular gathering place for the local assembly of believers.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the first century, Christians did not possess dedicated church buildings or public religious facilities. Local congregations gathered in private residences owned by believers who had sufficient space to host them. The word oikos describes the physical building or home where family life occurred and where these early believers assembled.

In Philemon 1:2, Paul addresses his letter to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, and "the church in your house." Philemon opened his domestic residence for Christian worship, instruction, prayer, and fellowship. The physical home provided the sanctuary and meeting space for the local body of believers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Oikos has a few related meanings in Scripture:

  • A physical structure, dwelling, home, or house.

  • The occupants of a home, including a family, household, or domestic unit.

  • A broader family line, lineage, or house (such as the house of David).

  • A spiritual dwelling place, such as the temple or the church as God's household.

In Philemon 1:2, the word refers specifically to the physical residence owned or managed by Philemon, which functioned as the meeting place for the local assembly.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, oikos ranges from literal physical structures to spiritual descriptions of God's people. It describes personal homes, the temple in Jerusalem, and the overarching family of God.

Early Christian ministry operated both in public settings and in individual homes. Private residences were central to the life of the early church. When applied to local Christian gatherings, oikos emphasizes hospitality, close community life, and the practical integration of faith into daily domestic reality.

4. Theological Implications

The practice of hosting the church in a private home shows how early believers dedicated their material possessions and personal space to Christ. Private property became a tool for gospel ministry and community worship.

Furthermore, addressing the letter to the church meeting in Philemon's oikos indicates that private personal matters had churchwide significance. The restored relationship between Philemon and his runaway slave Onesimus was not merely a private transaction. It was a matter of shared interest and care for the entire household of faith meeting under that roof.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Paul sends his opening greetings not only to Philemon individually, but also to his family members and the local church gathering in his home. The noun is singular and masculine, acting as the location where the assembly meets.

By mentioning the church in Philemon's house, Paul places the upcoming appeal concerning Onesimus before the full local Christian community. The host's home was both a private residence and the public meeting ground of the church. Any step Philemon took to forgive and welcome Onesimus as a brother in Christ would be witnessed, supported, and shared by the church family meeting in his residence.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Mark 11:17 — "Then He taught, saying to them, 'Is it not written, "My house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations"?'"

  • Luke 19:9 — "And Jesus said to him, 'Today salvation has come to this house, because he also is a son of Abraham.'"

  • Acts 5:42 — "And daily in the temple, and in every house, they did not cease teaching and preaching Jesus as the Christ."

  • 1 Corinthians 16:19 — "The churches of Asia greet you. Aquila and Priscilla greet you heartily in the Lord, with the church that is in their house."

  • 1 Timothy 3:15 — "but if I am delayed, I write so that you may know how you ought to conduct yourself in the house of God, which is the church of the living God, the pillar and ground of the truth."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The mention of home gatherings in Paul's letters highlights the vital role of biblical hospitality in early Christianity. Opening a home to host the church required sacrifice, courage, and dedication, especially during periods when Christian worship could invite public scrutiny or opposition. Philemon's home provided a essential physical place for the gospel to be lived out in practical Christian community.
