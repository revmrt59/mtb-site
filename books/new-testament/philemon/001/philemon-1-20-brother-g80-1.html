BROTHER - (adelphós, Strong's G80)

Summary:

The Greek word adelphos literally refers to a brother born of the same parents. Across the New Testament, it expands beyond biological family to describe fellow Israelites and, most frequently, fellow believers in Jesus Christ. In Philemon 1:20, Paul uses the word to address Philemon directly as a spiritual brother. By calling him "brother," Paul grounds his request for the runaway slave Onesimus not in official apostolic authority, but in mutual Christian love and shared membership in the family of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Apostle Paul wrote his short letter to Philemon while imprisoned, likely in Rome. Philemon was a wealthy Christian leader who hosted a house church in Colosse. The letter centers on Onesimus, a slave belonging to Philemon who had escaped and later met Paul. Through Paul's ministry, Onesimus became a believer in Jesus Christ.

In the Greco-Roman world, runaway slaves faced severe legal penalties, including physical punishment or death. Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon, not as a mere fugitive returning to punishment, but with a personal letter asking Philemon to receive him as a beloved brother in Christ. In verse 20, Paul reaches the climax of his appeal, speaking directly to Philemon as his own brother in the faith.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Adelphos fundamentally means a male sibling, coming from roots that refer to sharing the same physical womb. Over time, the word developed several distinct levels of meaning:

  • Physical Sibling: A biological brother or half-brother born of the same parents or parent.

  • Fellow Countryman: A fellow member of the Jewish nation or ethnic family.

  • Spiritual Sibling: A fellow believer in Jesus Christ who shares the same heavenly Father through salvation and spiritual rebirth.

In the New Testament, early Christians regularly used adelphos to describe one another. The word expresses genuine relational closeness, spiritual equality, and mutual obligation within the church.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses adelphos in three main ways:

First, the Gospels frequently use the word for physical siblings, such as the brothers James and John, or Martha's brother Lazarus.

Second, in the Book of Acts, speakers use the term when addressing fellow Jews, appealing to their shared physical heritage and covenant history.

Third, throughout the New Testament letters, the word becomes the primary description for members of the church. Jesus Himself extended the definition of family when He declared that whoever does the will of His Father is His brother, sister, and mother. The apostles took up this language to teach believers that faith in Christ creates a genuine spiritual family that transcends earthly, national, and social boundaries.

4. Theological Implications

The widespread use of adelphos among Christians carries important biblical truths:

  • Adoption into God's Family: Believers are not merely religious companions; they are children of God through faith in Christ. Because God is their Father, believers are truly spiritual brothers and sisters to one another.

  • Brotherhood with Christ: Jesus is the Firstborn among many brethren. He was not ashamed to call redeemed believers His brothers, raising them into a position of secure spiritual inheritance.

  • Transformation of Social Relationships: In ancient society, strict boundaries separated slave from free, rich from poor, and Jew from Gentile. Calling every believer a brother flattened these worldly hierarchies. Philemon and his former slave Onesimus were now brothers under the same Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:20, Paul addresses Philemon directly, using the word in the vocative case, which is the grammatical form used for direct address. He writes, "Yes, brother, let me have joy from you in the Lord; refresh my heart in the Lord."

Paul intentionally chooses the title "brother" rather than using an official title such as "apostle." Earlier in the letter, Paul noted that he had full authority in Christ to command Philemon to do what was right. Instead, Paul appeals on the basis of love. By calling Philemon "brother," Paul reminds him of their shared identity and common bond in Christ.

This direct address sets up Paul's request for Philemon to welcome Onesimus back—not merely as a returning servant, but as a brother in the Lord. Paul asks Philemon to act out the reality of his faith by extending brotherhood to Onesimus just as Paul extended it to Philemon.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Corinthians 15:58 — "Therefore, my beloved brethren, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that your labor is not in vain in the Lord."

  • Hebrews 2:11 — "For both He who sanctifies and those who are being sanctified are all of one, for which cause He is not ashamed to call them brethren."

  • 1 John 2:9 — "He who says he is in the light, and hates his brother, is in darkness until now."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The language of brotherhood in Philemon offers a vivid look at how the early church disrupted Roman social norms without political rebellion. In Roman law, a master owned a slave as property. But in Paul's letter, Philemon is asked to receive Onesimus "no longer as a slave, but more than a slave—a beloved brother" (Philemon 1:16).

When Paul calls Philemon "brother" in verse 20, he reinforces the spiritual foundation for this request. Paul implies that if Philemon treats Paul as a brother, he must also treat Onesimus as a brother, because both share the same relationship with Christ.
