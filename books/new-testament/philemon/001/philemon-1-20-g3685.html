HAVE JOY - ( oninēmi , Strong's G3685 )

Summary:

The Greek verb oninēmi means to receive assistance, derive benefit, or find enjoyment and pleasure in someone or something. In the New Testament, it occurs only once, in Philemon 1:20, where Paul uses the optative form onaimen ("let me have joy" or "may I benefit") as a warm, personal appeal to Philemon. Paul is employing a playful, affectionate wordplay on the name of Philemon’s runaway slave, Onesimus, whose name means "profitable" or "useful." By asking Philemon to let him "have joy" from him, Paul subverts the typical slave-master dynamic, urging Philemon to act in Christian love so that Paul may receive spiritual refreshment and joy from their relationship in the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In classical Greek, oninēmi carried the primary sense of being useful, helping, or bringing profit, while in the middle voice, it meant to derive profit, advantage, or pleasure from someone. It was frequently used in familial contexts, such as parents expressing the hope of "deriving benefit" or "joy" from their children in their old age, or friends finding mutual satisfaction in their bond. In the Greek Old Testament (LXX), the root appears in contexts of receiving benefit or help. The word evokes a deep sense of mutual benefit and relational satisfaction within a family or a close-knit community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb oninēmi means to be of use, to benefit, or to help. In the middle voice, it means to derive benefit from, find enjoyment in, or have joy from. In this specific context, the term denotes experiencing satisfaction, receiving assistance, or enjoying the favor of someone. Rather than a mercenary or financial profit, the term in Philemon 1:20 denotes a spiritual and relational benefit—a deep, refreshing joy that one believer experiences through the obedience and love of another.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The single New Testament occurrence of oninēmi is highly stylistic and pastoral. By choosing onaimen ("let me have joy" or "may I benefit"), Paul creates an intentional, brilliant wordplay on the name Onesimos (Onesimus), which literally means "profitable," "useful," or "beneficial." Earlier in the letter, Paul notes that Onesimus was formerly "unprofitable" to Philemon but has now become "profitable" to both Philemon and Paul (Philemon 1:11). In verse 20, Paul turns the tables: he asks Philemon to become "Onesimus" (profitable) to him. The word picture is one of reciprocal grace—just as the slave Onesimus is now useful to both men, Philemon can now bring "usefulness" and "joy" to his father in the faith.

4. Theological Implications

This term underscores the interconnectedness and mutual dependence of the body of Christ. In the ancient Greco-Roman world, social relations were heavily transactional, built on patron-client systems and rigid hierarchies of utility. Paul redefines "benefit" (oninēmi) through a theological lens. True Christian profit is not material or social leverage; it is the spiritual refreshment that comes from mutual obedience, forgiveness, and love in Christ. The theological reality of being "in the Lord" transforms how believers relate, making their actions a source of deep spiritual joy and sustenance for one another.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:20, Paul writes, "Yes, brother, let me have joy from you in the Lord; refresh my heart in the Lord." The optative mood (onaimen) expresses a polite, yet earnest and affectionate wish. Paul is not commanding Philemon as an apostle; instead, he appeals to him as a "brother." The phrase "from you in the Lord" anchors this joy not in human pleasing, but in Christ. If Philemon forgives Onesimus and receives him back as a brother, he will be ministering directly to Paul’s soul. The context links this "joy/benefit" directly to the command to "refresh my heart," showing that Paul’s joy is bound up with the spiritual maturity and obedience of his converts.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because oninēmi is a New Testament hapax legomenon (occurring only in Philemon 1:20), there are no other direct New Testament cross-references for this specific verb. However, its usage in the Greek translation of the Old Testament (LXX) reflects the same semantic meaning:

  • Sirach 30:2 (LXX) – "He who disciplines his son will find benefit [or have joy] in him, and will boast of him among his acquaintances." (Uses the future middle form onēsetai).

7. Other Relevant Insights

Ignatius of Antioch, an early Church Father writing in the early second century, was deeply influenced by Paul's letter to Philemon. In his letters (such as To the Ephesians 2:2 and To the Romans 5:2), Ignatius explicitly mimics Paul’s wordplay on oninēmi. Ignatius writes to the Ephesians, "May I always have joy of you, if indeed I am worthy," using the exact same verb form (onaimēn) to express his desire for spiritual refreshment from his fellow believers. This demonstrates how deeply this Pauline concept of mutual, Christ-centered joy resonated within the early Christian community.
