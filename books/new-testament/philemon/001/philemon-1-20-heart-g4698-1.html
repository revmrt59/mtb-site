HEART - (splánchnon, Strong's G4698)

Summary:

The Greek word splanchnon originally referred to the internal physical organs—the heart, lungs, liver, and intestines. In ancient Greek thought, these inner organs were viewed as the seat of deep personal emotions, intense affection, and genuine compassion. In Philemon 1:20, Paul uses this word to ask Philemon to refresh his "heart" in the Lord by welcoming back and forgiving his runaway servant, Onesimus. By using this term, Paul expresses how deeply his own personal spirit and emotional well-being are wrapped up in Philemon's godly response to Onesimus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek literature, splanchnon referred literally to the inward parts of the body—the vital organs including the heart, lungs, liver, and intestines. While modern Western culture often speaks of the heart as the center of emotion and the brain as the center of thought, ancient Greco-Roman culture viewed the lower visceral organs as the site of deep emotional reactions. It represented the place where a person feels profound affection, deep pity, or intense grief—what people today might describe as a "gut feeling" or deep-seated compassion.

By the time of the New Testament, Greek writers regularly used the term metaphorically to describe strong feelings of love, mercy, tender affection, and compassionate care. Rather than referring to shallow sentiment, splanchnon points to a personal reaction felt in the very depth of one's inner being.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Literally, splanchnon refers to the internal physical organs of the body. Metaphorically, it refers to the deep seat of affection, compassion, pity, and tender care felt toward another person.

When used in reference to God or Christ, the word describes profound divine mercy and tender compassion toward needy humanity. When used between believers, it describes heartfelt affection, warmth, Christian love, and mutual care within the body of Christ. In passages like Philemon 1:20, the word functions as a bodily metaphor for the deep inner person, describing one's heart, inner self, or emotional core.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the New Testament, splanchnon appears in both a literal sense and a rich metaphorical sense. In its literal sense, it describes physical internal organs, such as in the narrative of Judas Iscariot's death in Acts. However, its primary usage across the epistles and Gospels is metaphorical, capturing deep spiritual and relational warmth.

The word is translated into English using terms like "heart," "affections," "tender mercy," or "compassion." It carries several distinct nuances in apostolic writing:

  • Deep parental or divine mercy that responds to human suffering.

  • Warm Christian affection that binds believers together in genuine fellowship.

  • The inward personal sanctuary of a believer where spiritual comfort, relief, and joy are experienced.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of splanchnon highlights how deeply Christian fellowship and gospel transformation affect a person's inner life. Christian love is not presented in Scripture as an abstract duty or formal compliance with rules. Instead, gospel grace transforms a person's inner feelings, creating genuine emotional care for others.

Furthermore, God's own mercy toward sinners is rooted in tender compassion. The gospel brings believers into a community where Christ's love flows through believers toward one another. When Christians show mercy, grant forgiveness, and restore broken relationships, they express the very character of God, whose tender mercy met them in their lost condition.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:20, Paul writes to Philemon, a wealthy Christian master, appealing to him regarding Onesimus, Philemon's runaway servant who had become a believer through Paul's ministry. Paul asks Philemon for a personal favor, saying, "Yes, brother, let me have joy from you in the Lord; refresh my heart in the Lord."

Throughout this short letter, Paul repeatedly uses splanchnon. In verse 7, Paul notes that the "hearts" of the saints had been refreshed by Philemon's love. In verse 12, Paul describes Onesimus as his own "heart," showing how deeply attached he had become to the young convert. Now in verse 20, Paul brings the letter to a personal climax by asking Philemon to "refresh my heart in the Lord."

Paul uses this vivid term to show that receiving Onesimus as a brother in Christ is not a cold administrative decision. It affects Paul directly at the deepest personal level. If Philemon forgives Onesimus and welcomes him, Paul himself will experience deep spiritual relief, joy, and comfort in his spirit. The phrase "in the Lord" anchors this request in Christ, indicating that true spiritual refreshment between believers is powered by their shared union with Jesus.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 1:78 — "Through the tender mercy of our God, with which the Dayspring from on high has visited us..." Here the word expresses the deep, compassionate mercy of God that prompted the sending of the Messiah to bring salvation to those in darkness.

  • Colossians 3:12 — "Therefore, as the elect of God, holy and beloved, put on tender mercies, kindness, humility, meekness, longsuffering..." Paul exhorts believers to clothe themselves with heartfelt compassion as part of their new identity in Christ.

  • Philemon 1:7 — "For we have great joy and consolation in your love, because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother." Paul commends Philemon for how his active love and generosity had previously comforted and encouraged fellow believers.

  • Philemon 1:12 — "I am sending him back. You therefore receive him, that is, my own heart..." Paul expresses his immense personal love for Onesimus, viewing the former servant as an extension of his own inner self.

  • 1 John 3:17 — "But whoever has this world’s goods, and sees his brother in need, and shuts up his heart from him, how does the love of God abide in him?" John uses the word to describe the closing off of natural Christian compassion when faced with a brother's practical need.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Philemon 1:20, the target word appears in the accusative plural, functioning as the direct object of the verb "refresh." The plural form was standard in Greek when using this visceral organ metaphor to represent the emotional core or inner life of a person.

Paul's choice to ask for his heart to be "refreshed" plays on a common ancient military and agricultural term meaning to give rest, relief, or revitalization after heavy labor or strain. Paul was in chains for the gospel, bearing heavy burdens for the churches. By granting forgiveness to Onesimus, Philemon would bring profound rest and renewal to Paul's inner spirit while he was in prison.
