JOY - (onínēmi, Strong's G3685)

Summary:

The Greek verb oninēmi means to receive benefit, profit, delight, or joy from someone or something. In biblical usage, it expresses a warm desire to derive spiritual comfort and encouragement from another person's godly actions. In Philemon 1:20, Paul uses this word to ask Philemon for a personal favor in the Lord—namely, that Philemon would bring joy and refreshment to Paul's heart by forgiving and welcoming his runaway slave, Onesimus, as a beloved brother in Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Paul wrote his short letter to Philemon while in prison. Philemon was a Christian leader in Colosse who hosted a church in his house. His bondservant, Onesimus, had run away and eventually met Paul. Under Paul's ministry, Onesimus trusted in Christ and became a helpful companion to the apostle.

Roman law permitted harsh punishment for runaway slaves, but Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon with this letter. Rather than issuing a command, Paul appeals to Philemon to receive Onesimus not as a fugitive, but as a brother in the Lord. Philemon 1:20 stands at the climax of Paul's appeal, where Paul asks Philemon to bring him personal joy and spiritual refreshment by granting this gracious request.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

exact action represented by oninēmi means to profit, gain benefit, derive pleasure, or find joy from a person or situation. In general Greek usage outside the Bible, the word often described receiving physical help, material advantage, or satisfaction from a relative or friend.

In this passage, where the word is translated "let me have joy," it carries the sense of receiving deep spiritual satisfaction and comfort. The specific grammatical form Paul uses expresses a polite wish or earnest request ("may I have benefit" or "let me have joy"). Rather than demanding compliance through his apostolic office, Paul asks Philemon to allow him to experience genuine spiritual delight through Philemon's willing obedience and love.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Because this specific word appears only once in the New Testament, its biblical nuance is drawn directly from its setting in Paul's letter to Philemon. Paul does not use the word for financial gain or selfish benefit. Instead, he speaks of a spiritual benefit that flows between believers who are united in Christ.

Paul qualifies the joy he seeks with the phrase "in the Lord." The benefit Paul desires is not material payment, but the spiritual joy of seeing the gospel produce reconciliation between a Christian master and his newly converted slave. The word highlights how Christian leaders find true joy when believers act in step with God's grace.

4. Theological Implications

Paul's request highlights how the gospel transforms personal relationships within the Christian community. In Roman society, a master had absolute authority over a slave. But Paul bases his appeal on a higher reality—their shared bond as brothers in Christ.

By asking Philemon to grant him joy in the Lord, Paul shows that acts of mercy within the church bring encouragement to the whole body of Christ. When one believer forgives and restores another, it comforts and strengthens others who witness it. Spiritual joy in the church grows as God's grace overcomes human divisions and restores broken relationships.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:20, Paul links "let me have joy from you in the Lord" with the parallel request, "refresh my heart in the Lord." This pairing shows that the joy Paul requests is directly connected to the refreshment of his inner spirit.

Paul addresses Philemon affectionately as "brother," placing their relationship on the ground of Christian family rather than hierarchy. By asking for joy from Philemon, Paul makes a personal appeal that leaves the choice in Philemon's hands, knowing that forced compliance yields no true spiritual fruit. If Philemon receives Onesimus warmly, Paul receives the ultimate spiritual benefit: witnessing the power of the gospel bring reconciliation between two brothers.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • This term is unique to this context and does not appear elsewhere in the biblical corpus.

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a meaningful connection between this word and the name of the slave at the center of the letter. The name Onesimus means "useful" or "profitable," coming from the same underlying language root as oninēmi. Earlier in the letter, Paul mentioned that Onesimus was once "unprofitable" to Philemon, but had now become "profitable" to both Philemon and Paul (verse 11).

When Paul writes in verse 20, "let me have joy [or benefit] from you," he makes a subtle play on words. It is as if Paul is saying, "Let me have 'Onesimus-like' benefit from you, brother!" This adds personal warmth to Paul's appeal, tying Philemon's choice to forgive directly to the transformed life of Onesimus.
