LORD - (kýrios, Strong's G2962)

Summary:

The Greek word kyrios refers to a person who possesses authority, ownership, or supreme rule, ranging from an earthly master or owner to the supreme Lord. In Philemon 1:20, Paul uses the word to designate Jesus Christ, appealing to Philemon to grant him joy and relief within the framework of their common commitment to Christ as Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek culture, kyrios was a standard word used for someone who held rightful authority over people or property. It described the head of a household, an owner of livestock or land, or a person holding legal control. When the Greek translation of the Old Testament was made, kyrios was chosen to translate the divine name of God, grounding the term in supreme divine majesty and covenant authority.

In the New Testament world, Roman authorities also used titles of lordship for rulers and emperors. Against this cultural backdrop, early Christians declared that Jesus of Nazareth is the ultimate kyrios. This was not merely a polite greeting, but a confession that Jesus possesses all power, authority, and ownership over heaven and earth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to:

  • An owner of property or animals.

  • A master who has authority over servants or slaves.

  • A ruler, leader, or person in high authority.

  • A respectful title of address, similar to "sir."

  • God the Father or the Lord Jesus Christ.

In Philemon 1:20, the word identifies Jesus Christ as the exalted Lord in whom believers live, serve, and relate to one another.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses kyrios across a wide spectrum of human and divine relationships. In everyday settings, it describes human masters who govern households or own property. When applied to God, it highlights His absolute sovereignty and authority over creation.

In the writings of Paul, the phrase "in the Lord" is especially common. It describes the realm of life created by belonging to Jesus. When Paul asks believers to do something "in the Lord," he grounds the request not in earthly pressure or social status, but in their shared allegiance to Christ.

4. Theological Implications

The lordship of Christ transforms all human power structures. In the ancient world, a master owned slaves and held total legal power over them. By identifying Jesus as the true Lord over both masters and servants, the gospel redefines how believers view authority and ownership.

Because Jesus is Lord, no human master possesses absolute authority over another person. Earthly authority is strictly accountable to the Lord in heaven. Furthermore, because all believers belong to the same Lord, their relationships with one another are built on love, mutual accountability, and brotherhood in Christ rather than worldly power dynamics.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the letter to Philemon, Paul is writing on behalf of Onesimus, a runaway servant who had become a Christian under Paul's ministry. Philemon was a wealthy Christian and an owner of servants—a human kyrios. Yet Paul gently reminds Philemon that both he and Onesimus serve a higher Lord.

In verse 20, Paul asks Philemon for a personal favor: "Yes, brother, let me have joy from you in the Lord; refresh my heart in the Lord." The target word occurs in the dative case as the object of a preposition ("in the Lord"), specifying the spiritual setting of Paul's request.

Paul does not command Philemon by apostolic authority, nor does he rely on mere personal friendship. Instead, he places his request "in the Lord." He asks Philemon to receive Onesimus back as a brother because both Paul and Philemon belong to Christ. Yielding to Paul's request brings spiritual joy and encouragement precisely because it reflects the character and command of the Lord they both serve.

  • John 20:28 — "And Thomas answered and said to Him, 'My Lord and my God!'"

  • 1 Peter 2:3 — "...if indeed you have tasted that the Lord is gracious."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a striking intentional focus on authority throughout the short letter to Philemon. Philemon held legal authority over Onesimus in human society. Yet Paul repeatedly highlights the true authority of Jesus as Lord. By framing the entire appeal around what is pleasing to the Lord, Paul transforms a potentially tense legal and social matter into an opportunity for gospel grace.
