REFRESH - (anapaúō, Strong's G373)

Summary:

The Greek verb anapauō means to give rest, relieve strain, or refresh someone who is tired or burdened. In Philemon 1:20, Paul uses this word to request that Philemon bring spiritual relief and joy to Paul's own heart by forgiving and welcoming Onesimus as a brother in Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In everyday Greek usage outside the New Testament, anapauō described bringing an activity or burden to a halt so that recovery could take place. It was used for resting weary travelers, giving tired laborers a break, or allowing farmland to rest between crop seasons. The word combines a basic verb meaning "to stop" or "to cease" with a prefix indicating a return to a previous state of peace or strength. In the New Testament, while the word can refer to physical rest, it frequently describes the spiritual and emotional refreshment that believers experience through God's grace and genuine Christian fellowship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb anapauō can mean to give rest, cause to cease from exertion, or afford relief to someone under pressure.

When used regarding physical labor or daily activity, it refers to taking rest or relaxing from work.

When used regarding the human spirit, mind, or heart, it means renewing someone's strength, easing emotional strain, or providing comforting relief.

In Philemon 1:20, the word means to bring heartfelt relief and spiritual comfort to Paul ("refresh my heart in the Lord").

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across its various uses in the New Testament, anapauō carries a warm, restorative nuance.

In the Gospels, Jesus uses the word when inviting the weary and heavy-laden to come to Him for spiritual rest (Matthew 11:28). It also describes disciples taking physical rest after exhausting ministry (Mark 6:31), or a wealthy man seeking selfish comfort (Luke 12:19).

In the Epistles, Paul frequently uses the term to describe how faithful believers revive the spirits of church leaders and fellow Christians through love, hospitality, and encouraging news (1 Corinthians 16:18; 2 Corinthians 7:13; Philemon 1:7).

In 1 Peter, the word describes how the Spirit of glory rests upon believers who suffer for Christ (1 Peter 4:14).

In Revelation, those who die in the Lord are said to rest from their earthly labors (Revelation 14:13).

4. Theological Implications

The use of anapauō in Philemon 1:20 demonstrates that believers serve as instruments through whom Christ ministers real refreshment to His people. Spiritual comfort is not merely an internal feeling generated in isolation; God often mediates relief through the loving actions and obedience of fellow Christians. When a believer extends forgiveness, reconciliation, and Christian care, Christ uses that action to refresh the hearts of others.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Paul writes to Philemon from prison on behalf of Onesimus, Philemon's runaway slave who encountered Paul and became a Christian. Paul sends Onesimus back, appealing to Philemon to receive him no longer as a slave, but as a beloved brother in the Lord.

Earlier in the letter, Paul commended Philemon because "the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother" (Philemon 1:7). Philemon had a proven reputation for reviving the weary spirits of God's people.

In verse 20, Paul turns that known quality into a personal appeal: "Yes, brother, let me have joy from you in the Lord; refresh my heart in the Lord." Paul asks Philemon to do for him in prison what Philemon had so faithfully done for others. By forgiving Onesimus and restoring the relationship, Philemon would ease Paul's deep burden and bring profound spiritual joy to his heart.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In verse 20, the word appears in a direct request form ("refresh my heart"), expressing Paul's expectation that Philemon will act in love. Although Paul had apostolic authority to command Philemon, he chose instead to appeal on the basis of love and brotherhood.

The object of refreshment here is Paul's "heart" (splagchna), a word referring to the deep inner seat of human emotion and affection. Paul is showing that Philemon's response will directly affect Paul's emotional and spiritual well-being.

Furthermore, Paul specifies that this refreshment must take place "in the Lord." The relief Paul seeks is not personal flattery or worldly favor, but a deep joy rooted in seeing Christ's gospel bring about genuine forgiveness and unity.
