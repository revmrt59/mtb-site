YES - (naí, Strong's G3483)

Summary:

The Greek word nai is an affirmative particle translated as "yes," "indeed," "truly," or "even so." It serves to confirm a statement, grant a request, express strong agreement, or introduce an earnest appeal. In Philemon 1:20, Paul uses nai ("Yes, brother, let me have joy from you in the Lord; refresh my heart in the Lord") to transition from his formal pledge regarding Onesimus to a warm, personal request, appealing directly to Philemon's heart as a fellow believer.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In everyday Greek speech and writing, nai was the standard word used to give a direct affirmative answer or to strengthen an agreement. While it often functions as a simple "yes," speakers and writers also placed it at the beginning of a sentence to introduce an earnest entreaty or to underscore mutual trust between friends.

In the Greco-Roman world, written communications between associates often relied on formal legal terms to secure agreements. In his letter to Philemon, Paul shifts away from legal terminology. After promising to personally pay any debt owed by the runaway servant Onesimus, Paul uses nai in verse 20 to change the tone from financial arrangement to warm spiritual fellowship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The particle nai expresses clear agreement or earnest affirmation. Depending on the context, the word can refer to:

  • A direct answer meaning "yes" or "certainly."

  • An emphatic assertion meaning "indeed," "truly," or "so it is."

  • A warm, persuasive introduction to a request, functioning like "yes, indeed" or "yes, brother."

Here in Philemon 1:20, the word functions as a warm particle of persuasion. Paul uses it to summarize his previous statements and appeal directly to Philemon's shared commitment in Christ.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, nai is used in several distinct settings:

  • Direct Answers: Used when answering a question plainly, such as confirming belief or assent.

  • Confessions of Faith: Used by individuals who wholeheartedly accept Jesus' teaching or identity, expressing deep agreement with His authority.

  • Truthfulness in Speech: Used to describe honest, consistent speech where a person's word can be trusted without oath-taking.

  • Pastoral Entreaty: Used to introduce a personal appeal based on shared faith, encouraging a willing and joyful response.

4. Theological Implications

While nai is simply an affirmative particle, its biblical usage highlights how Christian speech ought to be characterized by truthfulness, clarity, and harmony.

Jesus calls His disciples to let their "yes" (nai) be "yes," teaching that believers should speak with such integrity that extra oaths are unnecessary. In addition, when believers express assent to Christ's lordship, their affirmative response reflects genuine faith and submission.

In Paul's appeal to Philemon, the word demonstrates how Christian leadership operates. Rather than relying on apostolic command or external pressure, Paul uses a warm affirmation to invite Philemon into voluntary obedience rooted in Christian love.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the short letter to Philemon, Paul intercedes for Onesimus, a servant who had fled from Philemon but became a Christian under Paul's ministry. Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus no longer merely as a servant, but as a beloved brother in Christ.

In verse 19, Paul writes with his own hand that he will personal repay whatever Onesimus owes. Then, at the start of verse 20, Paul opens with "Yes" (nai): "Yes, brother, let me have joy from you in the Lord; refresh my heart in the Lord."

Opening the verse with nai creates an affectionate bridge. Paul moves from discussing financial obligations to reminding Philemon of their deep spiritual bond. By calling him "brother" immediately after saying "Yes," Paul expresses full confidence that Philemon will act out of Christian love rather than obligation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 5:37 – "But let your 'Yes' be 'Yes,' and your 'No,' 'No.' For whatever is more than these is from the evil one."

  • Matthew 15:27 – "And she said, 'Yes, Lord, yet even the little dogs eat the crumbs which fall from their masters' table.'"

  • John 11:27 – "She said to Him, 'Yes, Lord, I believe that You are the Christ, the Son of God, who is to come into the world.'"

  • 2 Corinthians 1:17 – "Therefore, when I was planning this, did I do it lightly? Or the things I plan, do I plan according to the flesh, that with me there should be Yes, Yes, and No, No?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Refreshment in Christ: In verse 20, Paul asks Philemon to "refresh my heart in the Lord." This builds directly on verse 7, where Paul commended Philemon because the hearts of the saints had been refreshed by him. Paul is asking Philemon to extend that same ministry of spiritual refreshment to Paul himself.

  • Voluntary Obedience: Throughout the letter, Paul intentionally refrains from commanding Philemon. His use of nai reinforces this gentle approach, encouraging Philemon to respond willingly from the heart.
