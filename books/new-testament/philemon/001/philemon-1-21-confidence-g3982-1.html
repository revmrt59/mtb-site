CONFIDENCE - (peíthō, Strong's G3982)

Summary:

Peithō is a Greek verb that means to persuade, convince, or win over. When used in active forms to describe a state of mind, it means to be persuaded, confident, or trusting in someone or something. In Philemon 1:21, Paul uses the word to express his settled confidence in Philemon's spiritual character and obedience. By assuring Philemon of this trust, Paul makes his request not through apostolic force, but through appeal to a brother whose heart he knows will respond willingly to the gospel.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Peithō was widely used across ancient speech, appearing in courtrooms, political speeches, and private correspondence. Its root idea focuses on persuasion—influencing someone's mind or heart through speech, evidence, or trust until they agree or rely on another person.

In ancient civic life, a speaker used arguments to persuade a council, or a leader attempted to win over a public assembly. In personal relationships, the word described the trust established between companions or the confidence one placed in a reliable friend.

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament and throughout the New Testament, peithō moves between active persuasion and the resulting condition of trust. It can describe attempting to convince an audience, yielding to good advice, trusting in God, or resting in a firm conviction about someone's character.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Peithō carries several related senses depending on who is acting and how the sentence is constructed:

  • To persuade, convince, win over, or satisfy another person.

  • To listen to, obey, or yield to advice or command.

  • To trust, rely on, or place confidence in God, a person, or a concept.

  • To stand convinced or hold a settled assurance regarding a matter.

In Philemon 1:21, the word describes Paul's inner persuasion regarding Philemon. Paul is not trying to talk himself into trusting Philemon, nor is he merely voicing a vague hope. He stands in a settled state of confidence regarding Philemon's willingness to obey Christ's law of love.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, peithō operates in three main settings:

First, it describes the work of persuasive speech. Gospel preachers use reasoning and appeal to persuade listeners to receive God's truth. Conversely, public figures or false teachers sometimes attempt to persuade crowds toward wrong action.

Second, it describes where a person places ultimate trust. Scripture warns against misplaced trust—such as trusting in personal righteousness, wealth, or human wisdom—while commending wholehearted trust in God.

Third, it describes personal confidence between believers or toward God's promises. In this setting, peithō represents an assurance rooted in proven character and divine grace. It is not blind optimism, but a quiet conviction based on observed spiritual fruit and shared faith.

4. Theological Implications

Paul’s use of peithō in Philemon highlights how leadership and fellowship function in the body of Christ. Although Paul possessed apostolic authority to issue direct commands, he chose to appeal on the basis of love. Expressing confidence in Philemon grounds the request in Christian trust rather than institutional coercion.

This reflects a fundamental principle of Christian life: genuine gospel obedience springs from a heart transformed by grace, not from external pressure. When Paul expresses confidence in Philemon's obedience, he honors the Holy Spirit's ongoing work in Philemon's life. Spiritual trust between believers builds an atmosphere where obedience flows willingly out of love for Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In his letter to Philemon, Paul addresses a sensitive situation: the return of Onesimus, a runaway servant who had come to faith under Paul's ministry. Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus back no longer merely as a bondservant, but as a beloved brother in Christ.

In verse 21, Paul writes: "Having confidence in your obedience, I write to you, knowing that you will do even more than I say."

The word translated "confidence" sets the tone for Paul's closing words. Paul communicates that his mind is already made up regarding Philemon's Christian character. He does not doubt Philemon's devotion to the Lord or his love for the saints.

By stating that he writes out of confidence rather than suspicion, Paul encourages Philemon to act freely out of genuine conviction. Furthermore, when Paul adds that Philemon "will do even more than I say," he demonstrates that true Christian trust looks for a generous, heart-driven response that goes beyond bare obligations.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 18:9 — "Also He spoke this parable to some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others:"

  • Romans 14:14 — "I know and am convinced by the Lord Jesus that there is nothing unclean of itself;"

  • 2 Corinthians 1:9 — "Yes, we had the sentence of death in ourselves, that we should not trust in ourselves but in God who raises the dead,"

  • Philippians 1:6 — "being confident of this very thing, that He who has begun a good work in you will complete it until the day of Jesus Christ;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s posture in Philemon offers a model for encouraging growth in others. Rather than managing behavior through heavy demands, Paul expresses trust in what God has done in Philemon's heart.

This trust is not a naive denial of human weakness; it is grounded in the real, working grace of God. When believers express genuine confidence in one another's commitment to Christ, it strengthens mutual bonds and inspires brothers and sisters to live out the gospel with joy and freedom.
