DO - (poiéō, Strong's G4160)

Summary:

The verb poieō means to do, perform, accomplish, make, or carry out an action. In Philemon 1:21, Paul uses this word to express complete confidence in Philemon's response, trusting that he will perform even more than what Paul requested regarding the forgiveness and welcome of his former runaway bondservant, Onesimus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The letter to Philemon is a deeply personal note written by the apostle Paul while imprisoned. Philemon was a Christian leader in Colosse whose bondservant, Onesimus, had fled, likely taking property or money in the process. While on the run, Onesimus encountered Paul, came to saving faith in Jesus Christ, and became a valuable helper to the apostle.

Paul writes to ask Philemon to receive Onesimus back—no longer merely as a bondservant, but as a beloved brother in Christ. Rather than issuing a heavy-handed demand, Paul appeals to Philemon on the basis of love and Christian partnership. Near the conclusion of his letter, Paul writes with firm trust that Philemon will act generously in response to this appeal.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb poieō refers to taking action, bringing something about, performing a task, or producing a specific result. Depending on how it is used, the word can mean:

  • To make, build, or bring something into existence.

  • To perform an duty, carry out a command, or fulfill a request.

  • To practice or live out a specific course of conduct.

The central emphasis of poieō is concrete action and practical execution rather than mere inner feeling or passive agreement.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, poieō appears in a wide variety of settings, ranging from everyday physical tasks to deep spiritual responsibilities. It describes God’s work in creation, Jesus’ miraculous works, and human obedience to God's commands.

Biblical writers frequently pair poieō with moral principles to show that true faith inevitably expresses itself through conduct. To "do" the truth or to "do" good means that a person's inner convictions actively shape their daily behavior and choices toward others.

4. Theological Implications

In Christian theology, genuine grace produces visible obedience. God does not merely reform our thinking; He transforms our hearts so that our outward actions reflect His character. Scripture consistently emphasizes that belief and behavior belong together.

When believers respond to God's mercy, their obedience is not motivated by legalistic compulsion. Instead, grace empowers believers to act out of love. In the church, practical acts of reconciliation, forgiveness, and restoration serve as clear evidence that the gospel has taken deep root in a person's life.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:21, Paul writes: "Having confidence in your obedience, I write to you, knowing that you will do even more than I say."

Paul could have used his apostolic authority to command Philemon to forgive Onesimus. Instead, he appeals to Philemon’s Christian heart, expressing confidence in what Philemon will do. By saying Philemon will perform "even more than I say," Paul points to a spirit of Christian love that goes far beyond basic legal requirements. The verb appears in a form that expresses future active action singular in number, directly addressing Philemon. Paul envisions Philemon taking positive, practical steps toward Onesimus—restoring him fully, treating him as an equal brother in Christ, and perhaps even granting him freedom.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:24 — "Then Joseph, being aroused from sleep, did as the angel of the Lord commanded him and took to him his wife."

  • Luke 3:11 — "He answered and said to them, 'He who has two tunics, let him give to him who has none; and he who has food, let him do likewise.'"

  • John 3:21 — "But he who does the truth comes to the light, that his deeds may be clearly seen, that they have been done in God."

  • James 4:17 — "Therefore, to him who knows to do good and does not do it, to him it is sin."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s trust in Philemon shows a key principle of gospel leadership: true obedience is best drawn out through encouragement and shared love rather than forced compliance. When Paul expresses confidence in what Philemon will do, he gives Philemon room to act freely out of genuine love. Gospel transformation turns moral obligation into joyful, generous action that exceeds basic expectations.
