OBEDIENCE - ( hupakoē , Strong's G5218 )

Summary:

The Greek noun hupakoē denotes a state of listening attentively, conforming to authority, and submitting to a command or request. Formed by combining the preposition hupo (under) and the verb akouō (to hear), it paints the picture of "hearing under" or placing oneself under what is heard. In Philemon 1:21, Paul expresses absolute confidence in Philemon's willing compliance, knowing that his brother in Christ will not only grant his request regarding the runaway slave Onesimus but will even go beyond what is asked. It describes an obedience born of mutual love and spiritual fellowship rather than legalistic compulsion.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • Hupakoē is a compound word derived from hupo, meaning "under," and akouō, meaning "to hear" or "to listen." The historical word picture is that of a gatekeeper who goes to the door to listen and answer when someone knocks. To "hear under" means to listen with a submissive posture, ready to act upon what is spoken. It stands in contrast to parakoē (disobedience), which means "hearing alongside" or letting a command slip by without paying attention. In ancient Greek literature, the term evolved from simply listening or answering a knock at the door to a deeper disposition of compliance, submission, and yielding to an authority figure, a parent, or a deity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Hupakoē carries the lexical meaning of obedience, compliance, or submission. It refers to a voluntary and active response to a command, instruction, or request. Unlike mere outward compliance driven by fear of punishment, the biblical usage of hupakoē emphasizes an internal alignment of the will with the speaker's desire. It is a submissive listening that manifests in corresponding action. In the New Testament, it is frequently used to describe a believer's response to the gospel, Christ's submission to the Father, and the relational obligations among believers within the household of faith.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the New Testament, hupakoē occurs 15 times, always translating to "obedience" or "obeying" in the NKJV. The word picture of "hearing under" highlights that true obedience is never blind or unthinking; it begins with an attentive ear.

  • Hearing with Intent: The ear must be tuned to the master's voice. Without hearing, there is no understanding; without understanding, there can be no genuine obedience.

  • Submitting the Will: Placing oneself "under" the authority of the speaker indicates a deliberate choice to surrender personal autonomy in favor of the speaker's instruction.

  • Active Alignment: The term is dynamic, never passive. It implies that the hearer's life and actions are brought into conformity with the standard or request presented.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, hupakoē is deeply connected to faith and discipleship.

  • The Obedience of Faith: Paul uses the phrase "the obedience of faith" (hupakoē pisteōs) in Romans 1:5 and 16:26 to show that saving faith is not merely intellectual assent but an active, submissive response to the gospel. True faith produces obedience, and true obedience is fueled by faith.

  • Christ as the Pattern: In Romans 5:19 and Hebrews 5:8, hupakoē describes Jesus' perfect submission to the Father. He learned obedience through suffering, setting the ultimate pattern for believers.

  • Sanctification and Holiness: Peter describes believers as "obedient children" (1 Peter 1:14) whose lives are marked by the sanctifying work of the Spirit unto obedience (1 Peter 1:2). Holy living is the natural byproduct of a heart that listens and submits to God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:21, Paul writes, "Having confidence in your obedience, I write to you, knowing that you will do even more than I say." The context is highly personal and sensitive. Paul is appealing to Philemon to receive his runaway slave, Onesimus, no longer as a slave but as a beloved brother in the Lord.

  • Paul intentionally avoids using his apostolic authority to command Philemon. Instead, he appeals on the basis of love (Philemon 1:8–9).

  • By using the word hupakoē, Paul is not demanding blind obedience to a legal decree but expressing confidence in Philemon's spiritual maturity. He trusts that Philemon's ear is tuned to the voice of Christ and the prompting of the Holy Spirit.

  • The "obedience" Paul expects is one of voluntary love and partnership. Because Philemon "hears under" the lordship of Christ, Paul knows he will graciously welcome Onesimus and even go beyond what is formally requested.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 1:5: "Through Him we have received grace and apostleship for obedience to the faith among all nations for His name,"

  • Romans 5:19: "For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so also by one Man's obedience many will be made righteous."

  • Romans 16:19: "For your obedience has become known to all. Therefore I am glad on your behalf..."

  • 2 Corinthians 10:5: "casting down arguments and every high thing that exalts itself against the knowledge of God, bringing every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ,"

  • 1 Peter 1:2: "elect according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, in sanctification of the Spirit, for obedience and sprinkling of the blood of Jesus Christ: Grace to you and peace be multiplied."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A related word is the verb hupakouō (to obey), which shares the same root etymology of "hearing under." It is worth noting how Christian obedience differs radically from the Greco-Roman cultural expectations of obedience. In the Roman household (paterfamilias), obedience was often extracted through absolute power, fear, and legal obligation. In contrast, Paul appeals to Philemon for hupakoē on the basis of Christian equality, mutual love, and shared life in Christ. This demonstrates that in the kingdom of God, true obedience is an act of free, loving worship rather than begrudging duty.
