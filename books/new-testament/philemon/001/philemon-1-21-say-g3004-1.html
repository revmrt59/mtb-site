SAY - (légō, Strong's G3004)

Summary:

Legō means to say, speak, tell, declare, or call. It is the standard Greek verb used to express spoken or written thoughts, ranging from everyday speech to formal declarations. In Philemon 1:21, Paul uses the word to describe his written requests regarding Onesimus. Paul expresses confidence that Philemon will obey his request and even go beyond what Paul explicitly says.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek, legō was the basic and most frequent verb used for communication. It covers all forms of speaking, including stating a fact, telling a story, giving an instruction, or introducing a quotation.

When Paul wrote his letter to Philemon, he was in prison for preaching the gospel. Onesimus, a bondservant who had run away from Philemon, met Paul, trusted in Christ, and served Paul faithfully. Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon with this short letter, asking Philemon to receive him no longer merely as a servant, but as a beloved brother in Christ. Near the end of the letter, Paul uses legō to summarize the requests he has put into words.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Legō means to say, speak, tell, or declare. Depending on the context, it can also mean to call, name, or address someone.

The word itself is a simple, direct term for communication. It does not carry special authority on its own. Instead, the weight of what is said depends entirely on who is speaking, what is being communicated, and the context of the statement. When God or an apostle speaks, the statement carries divine authority, but the verb legō simply serves as the vehicle for that message.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the New Testament, legō introduces spoken discourse, teaching, parables, and scriptural quotations. It is used when Jesus preaches to the crowds, when prophets deliver God's message, when opponents question Christ, and when apostles write to local churches.

In English translations, the verb appears as "say," "says," "said," "saying," "tell," or "called." The word can introduce direct dialogue or summarize a broader message. In letters like Philemon, the verb often refers to written communication, because ancient letters were written to be read aloud as the sender's spoken voice to the recipient.

4. Theological Implications

While legō is an ordinary verb of speech, Scripture shows that words carry real spiritual weight and responsibility. When an apostle speaks or writes under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, his words convey God's will and call believers to active obedience.

At the same time, Paul's approach in Philemon reveals how gospel leadership functions. Rather than commanding Philemon with legalistic demands, Paul appeals to brotherly love and Christian fellowship. He states his expectations clearly, yet leaves room for Philemon to act freely out of genuine love for Christ. Truth spoken in love seeks willing cooperation rather than forced compliance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:21, Paul writes: "Having confidence in your obedience, I write to you, knowing that you will do even more than I say."

Here, "what I say" refers to everything Paul requested in the letter:

  • Welcoming Onesimus back as a brother in Christ.

  • Receiving him as Philemon would receive Paul himself.

  • Charging any debt or wrongdoing by Onesimus to Paul's personal account.

Paul speaks in the present tense ("I say"), referring directly to the message he is writing in this letter. By stating that Philemon will do "even more than I say," Paul expresses complete trust in Philemon's spiritual maturity. Paul sets a baseline with his written request, but he anticipates that Christian love will prompt Philemon to exceed that request, whether by granting Onesimus full freedom or sending him back to assist Paul in ministry.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient letter writing, a written letter functioned as the author's personal presence and spoken voice. When Paul wrote "than I say," he viewed his letter as a living conversation with Philemon.

Furthermore, Paul’s gentle confidence sets an example for pastoral leadership. He states his heart clearly, but he gives room for the Holy Spirit to work in Philemon's heart, trusting that genuine love produces generosity that goes far beyond minimum requirements.
