WRITE - (gráphō, Strong's G1125)

Summary:

The verb grapho means to draw characters, record words, or compose a written document. Across the New Testament, it describes everything from writing personal letters to recording inspired Scripture. In Philemon 1:21, Paul uses the word to express his deep confidence in Philemon's spiritual maturity, explaining that he writes not to command compliance, but out of absolute trust that Philemon will exceed his request regarding Onesimus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greco-Roman world, written correspondence served as a vital bridge between people separated by distance. The verb grapho originally meant to engrave, scratch, or draw lines, but over time it became the standard word for writing with ink on papyrus, parchment, or wooden tablets.

When the apostles spread the gospel across the Mediterranean, writing became essential for guiding and nurturing young churches. Letters like Philemon were composed by an apostle—often dictated to an amanuensis—and delivered by hand to local believers. While Paul was imprisoned, writing allowed him to shepherd the church, resolve delicate personal disputes, and display the power of the gospel in practical life.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Grapho refers to the act of recording words in written form. Depending on who is writing and what is being written, the word can mean:

  • To write or record information on a scroll or document.

  • To draft a personal letter or formal message to an individual or group.

  • To cite or reference established Holy Scripture ("It is written").

In Philemon 1:21, the word means to compose a direct, personal letter to a friend and brother in Christ.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses grapho in a few distinct ways:

First, it marks Old Testament divine revelation. When New Testament writers quote ancient Scripture, they routinely say, "It is written," showing that written text carries divine authority.

Second, it refers to apostolic letters. Apostles used the word when giving instruction, offering comfort, or correcting errors in local churches.

Third, it appears in intimate, individual correspondence. In Philemon, Paul uses grapho within a deeply personal appeal. He is not setting down universal church doctrine in abstract terms; he is writing to a specific house-church leader about a real-world situation involving a runaway slave named Onesimus.

4. Theological Implications

The written word is God's chosen medium for preserving His truth. By inspiring written texts, God ensured that His message would remain clear, stable, and accessible across generations.

At the same time, Paul's use of grapho in Philemon highlights how Christian authority works. Paul possessed apostolic authority to issue directives, yet in this letter he appeals through personal affection and Christian fellowship. The written letter becomes an instrument of encouragement rather than heavy-handed coercion. It demonstrates how biblical truth addresses real human conflict, calling believers to extend mercy, forgiveness, and brotherly love in daily life.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:21, Paul writes: "Having confidence in your obedience, I write to you, knowing that you will do even more than I say." The word write appears near the conclusion of his appeal for Onesimus, a converted former slave who was returning to his master, Philemon.

Paul deliberately grounds his letter in trust. He states that he writes not because he doubts Philemon's heart, but because he is confident in Philemon's godly character. The verb is in the aorist tense, active voice, and indicative mood, simple grammatical features that state the straightforward fact of Paul sending this message. Paul puts his appeal in writing to set a gentle standard before Philemon, giving him the opportunity to act freely and generously out of love for Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Corinthians 4:14 — "I do not write these things to shame you, but as my beloved children I warn you."

  • 2 Corinthians 7:12 — "Therefore, although I wrote to you, I did not do it for the sake of the one who had done the wrong..."

  • 1 John 2:12 — "I write to you, little children, because your sins are forgiven you for His name’s sake."

  • Revelation 2:18 — "And to the angel of the church in Thyatira write..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Writing allowed Paul's ministry to break through physical barriers. Even though he was confined in chains, his written words traveled freely across the Roman world.

Furthermore, a written letter provided a lasting record. By committing his request to parchment, Paul gave Philemon time to read, reflect, and pray before taking action. It converted a potentially tense conflict into a shining example of gospel reconciliation, preserving it for the church throughout all ages.
