GRANTED - ( charizomai , Strong's G5483 )

Summary:

The word charizomai is deeply rooted in the concept of charis (grace) and means to bestow a favor, give graciously, or forgive. In Philemon 1:22, Paul uses this word to express his hope that he will be "granted" or graciously restored to the Colossian believers. Rather than viewing his potential release from prison as a stroke of good luck, a legal triumph, or a self-evident right, Paul recognizes it as a divine gift of grace given by God to the church in response to their prayers.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • Charizomai is derived from the noun charis, which means grace, favor, or goodwill.

  • In classical Greek, the word carried the idea of showing a favor, doing something agreeable to please someone, or presenting a gift freely without any expectation of payment or return.

  • The historical word picture is that of a benefactor extending benevolence out of goodwill rather than obligation. It contrasts sharply with a transaction based on debt, wages, or legal duty.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The verb primarily means to give freely, grant as a favor, or bestow graciously.

  • Because giving freely often involves overlooking faults, charizomai also developed the strong meaning of "to forgive" or "to cancel a debt" out of sheer grace.

  • In Philemon 1:22, the verb is used in the passive voice (charisthēsomai), meaning "I shall be graciously given" or "I shall be granted as a favor."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The New Testament writers employ charizomai in two main ways:

  • Gracious Giving: Bestowing gifts, life, or deliverance upon someone (e.g., Rom 8:32, Gal 3:18).

  • Gracious Forgiving: Forgiving an offender or canceling a moral or financial debt (e.g., Eph 4:32, Col 3:13).

  • The linguistic overlap between giving and forgiving in this single word is highly instructive. Both actions flow from the same source: a heart of grace. To forgive someone is to hand them the "gift" of an erased debt. To be granted to a community is to be given to them as a living token of God's favor.

4. Theological Implications

  • Sovereignty and Prayer: Paul links the divine act of granting (charizomai) with the church's intercession ("through your prayers"). This highlights the mystery of prayer: God sovereignly decrees to bestow His gracious gifts through the prayers of His people.

  • The Divine Passive: The passive form of the verb serves as a "divine passive." God is the ultimate Giver who will perform the granting. Paul does not credit the Roman justice system or his own persuasive defense; his freedom is entirely in God’s hands.

  • Ministry as a Gift: Paul does not say, "I trust I shall be granted my freedom." Instead, he says, "I shall be granted to you." An apostle’s life, freedom, and presence are not his own property; they are God's gracious gifts to the church.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Philemon 1:22, Paul is applying gentle, pastoral leverage. By asking Philemon to prepare a guest room, Paul expresses immense confidence that God will answer their prayers for his release.

  • This expectation of a visit would naturally encourage Philemon to act quickly and favorably regarding Onesimus. If Paul is soon to be "granted" to them as a guest, Philemon would want to have the matter of the runaway slave settled in a manner consistent with Christian love and forgiveness before Paul arrives.

  • The use of charizomai subtly reminds Philemon of the very atmosphere of grace that he is being asked to extend to Onesimus.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 7:42 - "And when they had nothing with which to repay, he freely forgave them both."

  • Romans 8:32 - "He who did not spare His own Son, but delivered Him up for us all, how shall He not with Him also freely give us all things?"

  • Ephesians 4:32 - "And be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, even as God in Christ forgave you."

  • Philippians 1:29 - "For to you it has been granted on behalf of Christ, not only to believe in Him, but also to suffer for His sake,"

  • Colossians 3:13 - "...bearing with one another, and forgiving one another, if anyone has a complaint against another; even as Christ forgave you, so you also must do."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The use of charizomai in Philemon 1:22 highlights the relational ecology of the early church. Paul's imprisonment was not his private trial, nor would his release be his private joy. The spiritual bond between the apostle and the house church at Colossae was so tight that his physical presence was viewed as a shared gift of grace.
