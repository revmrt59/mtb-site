GRANTED - (charízomai, Strong's G5483)

Summary:

, The word charizomai means to give freely, bestow favor, grant release, or forgive an debt or offense. It describes an action rooted in favor and grace rather than obligation. In Philemon 1:22, Paul uses the word to express his confident hope that God will answer the church's prayers by graciously granting his release from prison so he can visit them again.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word charizomai comes from the same word group as charis, which means grace or unmerited favor. In ancient Greek life, the word was used in civic, social, and personal settings when someone bestowed a generous gift, granted a special favor, showed mercy to a debtor, or released a prisoner as an act of goodwill.

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament and in early Christian writings, the word carried this underlying sense of unearned generosity. Whether describing a king granting a pardon or a benefactor giving a free gift, charizomai consistently highlights the open-handed goodness of the giver.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word charizomai can refer to several distinct actions, all linked by the idea of grace:

  • To give freely or bestow a gift without demanding payment or earning it.

  • To grant a favor, such as releasing a prisoner or sparing a life.

  • To forgive a debt, an injury, or a sin, wiping away what was owed.

In Philemon 1:22, the word means to be granted or given as a favor. Paul expresses his expectation that God will graciously permit his release from imprisonment so that he may be restored to his friends and fellow workers.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, charizomai appears in three main settings:

First, it describes God's generous giving to humanity. God freely gives salvation, spiritual blessings, and even His own Son to believers out of His abundant favor.

Second, it describes the act of forgiveness. Just as God graciously forgives believers their spiritual debts, Christians are called to forgive one another freely, without holding grudges or demanding payment.

Third, it refers to granting physical freedom, safety, or life as a favor. In court setting or captivity, a person might be "granted" to someone—either released safely into their fellowship or handed over to their custody. In Philemon 1:22, Paul uses this sense, seeing his hoped-for release from custody as a gracious answer to prayer.

4. Theological Implications

Paul’s use of charizomai in Philemon 1:22 highlights an important biblical truth: God works through the prayers of His people to accomplish His gracious purposes. Paul does not attribute his potential release merely to Roman legal decisions or political luck. Instead, he views his freedom as a gift granted directly by God in response to the church's persistent intercession.

This reveals how Christian prayer functions. God's answers to prayer are gifts of His favor, not wages earned by human effort. When God answers prayer, He does so out of His free grace. Additionally, by viewing his potential reunion with Philemon as something "granted," Paul frames even human relationships and physical presence as undeserved blessings from God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the closing lines of his letter to Philemon, Paul asks his friend to prepare a guest room for him. He writes, "I trust that through your prayers I shall be granted to you" (Philemon 1:22).

The verb form indicates future action receiving a favor ("I shall be granted"). God is the implied giver behind the action, while Philemon and his local church are the recipients who will enjoy the benefit of Paul's presence.

This request fits beautifully into the message of the whole letter. The main purpose of the letter was for Paul to ask Philemon to receive Onesimus—a runaway slave who had become a Christian—with forgiveness and grace. By concluding with a note about how Paul himself hopes to be "granted" to Philemon by God's grace, Paul gently reminds Philemon that everyone in the kingdom of God lives by grace. Just as Philemon hopes to receive Paul back as a gracious gift from God, he should graciously welcome Onesimus back as a brother in Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 27:24 — "Do not be afraid, Paul; you must be brought before Caesar; and indeed God has granted you all those who sail with you."

  • Romans 8:32 — "He who did not spare His own Son, but delivered Him up for us all, how shall He not with Him also freely give us all things?"

  • Philippians 1:29 — "For to you it has been granted on behalf of Christ, not only to believe in Him, but also to suffer for His sake."

  • Colossians 3:13 — "bearing with one another, and forgiving one another, if anyone has a complaint against another; even as Christ forgave you, so you also must do."

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is worth noting the warm relationship between Paul and the household of Philemon. Paul did not treat prayer as a empty religious formality. He genuinely believed that the prayers of believers in Colosse had real weight before God and could affect his earthly release.

At the same time, Paul remained humble. He did not demand his freedom as a right, nor did he boast about his own importance. Instead, he saw his entire life and ministry—including his freedom to travel—as something completely dependent on the free grace of God.
