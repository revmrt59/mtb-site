CHRIST - (Christós, Strong's G5547)

Summary:

The word Christos means "Anointed One" or "Messiah." It identifies Jesus as God's chosen King promised in the Old Testament. In Philemon 1:23, Paul uses the title as part of the personal name "Christ Jesus" to describe Epaphras as his fellow prisoner, showing that their imprisonment was suffered in union with and for the cause of King Jesus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient world, physical anointing with oil was used to set apart individuals for special sacred offices. In the Old Testament, God ordered oil to be poured over prophets, priests, and kings to mark their divine appointment and authorization to serve Him.

The Greek word Christos translates the Hebrew word Mashiach (Messiah). By the time of the New Testament, Jewish expectation centered heavily on "the Christ"—the coming Davidic King whom God would send to deliver His people, establish His kingdom, and fulfill His promises. When the New Testament writers apply Christos to Jesus, they affirm that Jesus of Nazareth is this long-awaited Savior and rightful King.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Christos refers literally to someone who has been anointed. It can mean "Anointed One" or function as a formal title, "the Messiah."

In the Gospels, the word frequently functions as a title denoting office ("Are you the Christ?"). In the New Testament epistles, including Paul's letter to Philemon, the word is often combined directly with the personal name Jesus ("Christ Jesus" or "Jesus Christ"). In these contexts, it operates both as a confession of faith—affirming Jesus as God's Anointed King—and as a proper name. In Philemon 1:23, the word forms part of the personal title "Christ Jesus," identifying the Lord whom Paul and Epaphras serve.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses Christos across several distinct settings:

  • Messianic Title: The Gospels and Acts frequently use the term with the definite article ("the Christ") to emphasize Jesus as the fulfillment of Israel's Old Testament scriptures and royal expectations.

  • Personal Identification: In the Epistles, Christos appears routinely alongside "Jesus" to designate the exalted Lord now seated at the right hand of God.

  • Spiritual Sphere: Paul frequently connects prepositions with Christos, such as "in Christ." This describes a vital spiritual union where the believer's entire life, status, and endurance are located within the lordship of Jesus.

4. Theological Implications

The name Christos carries the whole weight of biblical salvation history. To call Jesus "Christ" is to recognize that He fulfills God's eternal redemptive plan. He is the ultimate Prophet who speaks God's word, the great High Priest who offers Himself as the final sacrifice for sin, and the exalted King who rules over creation.

In Paul's writings, union with Christ redefines every human circumstance. Sufferings, losses, and even physical chains lose their secular character when connected to Him. Rather than viewing imprisonment merely as a punishment imposed by human rulers, believers recognize that hardship endured for the gospel is borne in association with King Jesus.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:23, Paul writes near the conclusion of his brief letter, sending greetings to Philemon from his close companions: "Epaphras, my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus, greets you."

Epaphras was a worker from Colosse who had labored faithfully in the ministry. By describing Epaphras as a "fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus," Paul highlights two critical truths about their shared situation:

  • The Cause of Confinement: Epaphras was not imprisoned for any criminal activity under Roman law, but because of his faithful devotion to Christ Jesus and His gospel.

  • The Sphere of Endurance: The dative singular form links their imprisonment directly to Christ Jesus. Their physical confinement took place within the realm of their union with Him. Roman guards held the keys to their cell, but Jesus held authority over their lives and ministry.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:1 — "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham."

  • John 11:27 — "She said to Him, 'Yes, Lord, I believe that You are the Christ, the Son of God, who is to come into the world.'"

  • Acts 17:3 — "...explaining and demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead, and saying, 'This Jesus whom I preach to you is the Christ.'"

  • Ephesians 3:1 — "For this reason I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus for you Gentiles—"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Ephesians 3:1, Paul describes himself as "the prisoner of Christ Jesus," and here in Philemon 1:23 he extends that same honorable title to Epaphras as a "fellow prisoner." This reveals a shared brotherhood in suffering among early Christian workers.

The target occurrence in Philemon 1:23 appears in the dative case, masculine gender, and singular number. Grammatically, this case identifies Christ Jesus as the person in whom, and for whose sake, this captivity occurred.
