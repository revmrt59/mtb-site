GREETS - (aspázomai, Strong's G782)

Summary:

Aspazomai means to greet, welcome, pay respects to, or express personal affection toward someone. In everyday life, it described welcoming a guest or saying farewell. In early Christian letters, it served as a meaningful expression of brotherly love, care, and unity across geographic distances. In Philemon 1:23, Paul conveys warm personal greetings from Epaphras, his fellow prisoner, showing Philemon that he is surrounded by faithful companions who share his faith and interest in his household.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek culture, aspazomai was used for personal greetings upon meeting someone, welcoming guests into a house, or saying goodbye at departure. It could involve verbal expressions of welcome, a handshake, or a warm physical embrace.

When the apostles wrote letters to churches and individuals across the Roman Empire, they used aspazomai to send personal greetings to specific people. In a world where long-distance communication was slow and difficult, sending greetings by name was a vital way to keep believers connected, express authentic Christian love, and build unity within the body of Christ.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means to greet, welcome, pay respects to, or express affection for someone. Depending on how it is used, the word can mean:

  • To welcome a visitor upon arrival with words or physical affection.

  • To say farewell to friends at departure.

  • To send warm regards or Christian blessings to someone through a letter or messenger.

The word itself refers to the act of greeting or welcoming. The spiritual depth and warmth of a greeting come from the shared faith and relationship between the people involved, rather than from the dictionary definition alone.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, aspazomai appears in a variety of personal and public settings:

  • Everyday Courtesy: Jesus taught His followers about basic hospitality and kindness, noting that even non-believers greet their own brothers.

  • Physical Meetings: The word describes scenes where people meet in person, such as Mary visiting Elizabeth and greeting her.

  • Letter Closings: The word appears frequently near the end of New Testament letters, where Paul and other writers list fellow workers by name and send their personal regards to the church receiving the epistle.

In the letters of the New Testament, sending a greeting was not an empty social formality. It was an active expression of Christian fellowship, identifying individuals as cherished partners in the work of the gospel.

4. Theological Implications

The frequent use of aspazomai in apostolic letters shows that the gospel creates a warm, personal community. Christian ministry is not merely about transmitting abstract doctrine; it is carried out through personal relationships bound together by Christ.

When believers send greetings across cities and regions, it demonstrates that local churches are part of a larger global family. Even when separated by imprisonment, travel, or distance, believers remain joined in prayer, mutual care, and shared purpose.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:23, Paul writes to Philemon, a house-church leader in Colosse, appealing on behalf of the runaway bondservant Onesimus. Before concluding the letter, Paul writes: "Epaphras, my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus, greets you."

Epaphras was a minister who had labored faithfully among the believers in Colosse. Philemon knew Epaphras well. By sending Epaphras's greetings from Rome, Paul brings a familiar and trusted voice into his appeal. Epaphras was sharing Paul's imprisonment for the sake of the gospel, and his personal greeting reminds Philemon that the appeal for Onesimus carries the backing of trusted Christian leaders who are suffering for Christ.

Paul uses the verb in the present tense, showing that Epaphras is actively thinking of Philemon and sending his love at the very moment of writing. This simple greeting shows that even while facing imprisonment, believers remained focused on encouragement and reconciliation within the church.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 5:47 — "And if you greet your brethren only, what do you do more than others?"

  • Luke 1:40 — "and entered the house of Zacharias and greeted Elizabeth."

  • Romans 16:3 — "Greet Priscilla and Aquila, my fellow workers in Christ Jesus,"

  • 1 Corinthians 16:20 — "All the brethren greet you. Greet one another with a holy kiss."

  • Colossians 4:12 — "Epaphras, who is one of you, a bondservant of Christ, greets you,"

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is worth noting that Epaphras is mentioned sending greetings in both Colossians 4:12 and Philemon 1:23. These two letters were sent at the same time by the same messenger, Tychicus (along with Onesimus).

In Colossians 4:12, Epaphras is described as praying fervently for the whole church. In Philemon 1:23, he sends a direct personal greeting to Philemon's home. This shows how personal care and focused prayer went hand in hand in the early church. Distance and physical hardship did not prevent believers from caring for one another.
