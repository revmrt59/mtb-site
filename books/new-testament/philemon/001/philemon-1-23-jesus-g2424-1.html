JESUS - (Iēsoûs, Strong's G2424)

Summary:

The name Jesus is the Greek form of the Hebrew name Joshua, which means "Yahweh is salvation" or "the LORD saves." In the New Testament, it is the personal name given to God's Son born of Mary, identifying Him as the promised Savior who delivers His people from sin. In Philemon 1:23, the name appears alongside Christ to identify the living Lord in whose service Epaphras was imprisoned, showing that Christian ministry and hardship are bound directly to the person and authority of Jesus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greek-speaking Jewish world, personal names carried deep historical and religious meaning. The Greek name Iēsous is the transliteration of the Hebrew name Yehoshua (or its shortened form Yeshua), rendered in English as Joshua. In the Old Testament, Joshua was the faithful successor to Moses who led the Israelites into the Promised Land. By the first century, the name was common among Jewish families, expressing hope in God's saving power.

When applied to the Messiah in the New Testament, the name takes on ultimate fulfillment. Before His birth, the angel instructed Joseph to name Mary's child Jesus because He would save His people from their sins. Throughout the New Testament, this personal name grounds Christian faith in a real historical person who lived, died, rose, and reigns as Messiah and Lord.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name Jesus is a proper personal noun adapted into Greek from Hebrew. Etymologically, it combines the divine name Yahweh with a Hebrew root meaning to save or deliver, yielding the meaning "Yahweh is salvation" or "the LORD saves."

In the New Testament, this name identifies the historical person Jesus of Nazareth, the incarnate Son of God. While the name originally belonged to several Old Testament figures, in early Christian usage it specifies the resurrected Savior. When combined with the title Christ (meaning "Anointed One"), as in Philemon 1:23, it forms a full confession that Jesus of Nazareth is God's appointed King. In this passage, the name identifies the Lord for whose sake Epaphras endured captivity.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, the name Jesus is used in several distinct contexts:

  • As a standalone personal name in the Gospels, emphasizing His true humanity, earthly ministry, and personal interactions with disciples and crowds.

  • In combination with His earthly origin, such as "Jesus of Nazareth," highlighting His historical entrance into human history.

  • Joined with His exalted title, as "Jesus Christ" or "Christ Jesus," emphasizing both His historical human identity and His official role as God's anointed Savior.

  • As a title of supreme authority, where believers preach, heal, gather, and pray in the name of Jesus.

In the New Testament Epistles, Paul frequently pairs "Christ" and "Jesus." Using "Christ Jesus" keeps the historical human identity of the Savior inseparable from His exalted position as the reigning King.

4. Theological Implications

The name Jesus anchors Christian theology in historical incarnation and divine redemption. Salvation is not a philosophical idea or an abstract moral system; it is bound to a specific person. The name reminds believers that God Himself intervened in human history to rescue sinners.

Furthermore, combining the title Christ with the name Jesus demonstrates that the Jesus of history fulfills all Old Testament promises concerning the Davidic King and Suffering Servant. To be "in Christ Jesus" means to be united to the living Savior. Suffering for His sake is not a tragic accident, but a meaningful participation in His kingdom work.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:23, Paul writes to his friend Philemon near the conclusion of his letter regarding the runaway servant Onesimus. He sends personal greetings from his companion: "Epaphras, my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus, greets you."

Paul uses "Christ Jesus" to define the setting and reason for Epaphras's confinement. Epaphras was not a common prisoner serving a sentence for a civil crime. His captivity was tied directly to his allegiance to Jesus as the Christ.

This short phrase highlights two practical truths in the passage:

First, Epaphras's devotion to Jesus resulted in real earthly hardship. Sharing the gospel brought him into conflict with worldly authorities.

Second, his imprisonment carried profound spiritual dignity. Epaphras was not merely Paul's cellmate; he was a "fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus." His physical bonds were redefined by his relationship to his Lord. Belonging to Jesus transformed a Roman cell into a place of gospel partnership.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:1 — "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham."

  • Acts 17:3 — "explaining and demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead, and saying, 'This Jesus whom I preach to you is the Christ.'"

  • Romans 14:14 — "I know and am convinced by the Lord Jesus that there is nothing unclean of itself; but to him who considers anything to be unclean, to him it is unclean."

  • Hebrews 6:20 — "where the forerunner has entered for us, even Jesus, having become High Priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In first-century Roman society, imprisonment brought social shame and dishonor. By framing Epaphras's situation as captivity "in Christ Jesus," Paul reframes how believers view hardship. What the Roman world viewed as disgrace, the church recognized as faithful service to the true King. This provided quiet encouragement to Philemon and the house church meeting in his home, reminding them that standing firm for Jesus is never in vain.
