PRISONER - (synaichmálōtos, Strong's G4869)

Summary:

The Greek word synaichmalotos means a fellow prisoner, co-captive, or companion in confinement. It combines the prefix for togetherness with a word describing a captive taken in war. In the New Testament, Paul uses this term to describe believers who shared his physical confinement or severe hardship for the sake of the gospel. In Philemon 1:23, Paul uses this noun to describe Epaphras, highlighting that Epaphras stood faithfully alongside Paul in his imprisonment for serving Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Greek word synaichmalotos is a compound noun formed from two basic parts: the preposition syn (meaning "with" or "together") and aichmalotos (meaning "captive" or "prisoner of war"). In ancient Greek military culture, aichmalotos originally designated someone captured at the point of a spear. When combined with syn, the compound term describes a fellow prisoner of war—someone sharing the same captivity and hardship under a common captor.

By the time of the New Testament, the term described anyone sharing imprisonment or physical custody with another person. Paul adopted this term during his own Roman imprisonments to describe close ministry partners who shared his chains, loss of freedom, and public reproach for the sake of the gospel of Jesus Christ.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means a fellow captive, co-prisoner, or companion in confinement. It refers to someone who undergoes imprisonment alongside another person.

When applied to Christian ministry, the word describes a companion who shares the physical or spiritual cost of serving Christ under persecution. The word does not merely state that two people happen to be in the same location; it emphasizes a shared bond born out of joint suffering and mutual devotion to the same cause.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the New Testament, synaichmalotos appears exclusively in Paul's writings during his house arrest or imprisonment. Paul uses simple terms like desmios when speaking of himself generally as a "prisoner." However, when he wants to recognize specific companions who shared his incarceration or severe restriction for the gospel, he chooses synaichmalotos.

The term carries a tone of deep affection and honor. To be named a fellow prisoner was to be recognized as a battle-tested comrade in gospel ministry. It transformed what the Roman world viewed as a badge of shame into a mark of shared Christian honor.

4. Theological Implications

The use of synaichmalotos demonstrates that gospel work often involves cost and persecution, but believers do not bear these trials alone. God provides faithful companions to share the burden of ministry suffering.

Furthermore, Paul attaches the phrase "in Christ Jesus" to this captivity. This indicates that their imprisonment was not the result of common crime, but of their shared union with Jesus Christ. True Christian fellowship extends beyond shared joy; it encompasses shared suffering for the truth of the gospel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:23, Paul is bringing his personal letter to Philemon to a close. Philemon was a believer in Colosse, and Epaphras was a minister from that same city who had likely founded or served the church there. Epaphras had traveled to visit Paul during Paul's imprisonment to bring news of the churches in the Lycus Valley and to assist the apostle.

By identifying Epaphras as his fellow "prisoner" in Christ Jesus, Paul gives a glowing commendation of Epaphras's character and sacrifice. Epaphras was not merely a visitor sitting at a safe distance; he willingly shared Paul's dangerous situation and bondage for the sake of Christ. This title authenticated Epaphras's testimony to Philemon and reminded the church that their local minister was paying a personal price for the gospel.

Grammatically, the word is a singular masculine noun functioning as a title attached to Epaphras. It directly identifies his current status and relationship alongside Paul.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 16:7 — Paul sends greetings to Andronicus and Junia, calling them his countrymen and fellow prisoners, honoring their shared suffering and early service in the gospel.

  • Colossians 4:10 — Paul includes greetings from Aristarchus, calling him his fellow prisoner, demonstrating how faithful ministry partners stayed by Paul's side during his imprisonment.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Distinct from general imprisonment words: While standard Greek terms for a prisoner simply indicate someone in chains or in custody, synaichmalotos explicitly emphasizes companionship in that bound state.

  • Connection between Colossians and Philemon: Epaphras is mentioned in both Colossians and Philemon, which were written and delivered together. In Colossians 1:7 he is called a "dear fellow servant," and in Philemon 1:23 he is honored as a fellow prisoner.
