MARK - (Márkos, Strong's G3138)

Summary:

The Greek name Markos is the New Testament transliteration of the common Roman name Marcus. In Scripture, it refers to John Mark, the cousin of Barnabas and the author of the Gospel of Mark. In Philemon 1:24, Paul includes Mark among his active "fellow laborers" who send greetings from prison, demonstrating Mark's complete restoration to Paul's confidence and his vital role in kingdom ministry.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Markos is the Greek form of the Latin name Marcus, one of the most common personal names in the Roman world. In the ancient Greco-Roman context, Jewish individuals living in the Roman Empire often bore both a Hebrew name and a Greek or Latin name. In the Book of Acts, this individual is introduced by both names: "John whose surname was Mark" (Acts 12:12).

Mark grew up in Jerusalem in a household of some prominence; his mother Mary owned a large house that served as a gathering place for the early Jerusalem church. He was also the cousin of Barnabas. Mark accompanied Paul and Barnabas on their first missionary journey, but he departed from them prematurely at Perga in Pamphylia. This early departure caused sharp disagreement between Paul and Barnabas prior to their second journey, leading them to split into two ministry teams. Over time, however, Mark matured in faith and ministry, resulting in full reconciliation with Paul.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Markos is a masculine proper noun. It enters the New Testament vocabulary as a direct transliteration of the Latin personal name Marcus.

In ancient Latin, the name Marcus was originally associated with Mars, the Roman deity of war, or derived from the Latin noun marcus, which refers to a hammer or iron tool. The exact linguistic root in ancient Latin remains uncertain, as Marcus functioned primarily as a standard praenomen (first name) throughout the Roman world.

In the New Testament, the name is used strictly as a proper noun to identify John Mark. There is no evidence that the biblical writers intended any symbolic or spiritual meaning from the etymology of the Latin name itself. Its identity in the New Testament is defined entirely by the historical individual who bore it: the companion of apostles, writer of the Second Gospel, and co-worker in the spread of the gospel.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, the name Mark appears in connection with three major leaders of the early church: Barnabas, Peter, and Paul.

In the Book of Acts, Mark is described in his early years of ministry. He leaves Jerusalem with Barnabas and Saul after they deliver relief aid (Acts 12:25) and assists them on their first missionary venture before turning back.

In the Epistles, Mark appears as a trusted and mature leader. Peter calls him "Mark my son" (1 Peter 5:13), reflecting a close pastoral and spiritual relationship in Rome. Paul mentions Mark in his prison letters as a valued companion. In Colossians 4:10, Paul instructs the church to welcome Mark if he comes to them, noting his family connection to Barnabas. By the time Paul writes his final letter from prison, Mark's standing is beyond question: Paul instructs Timothy to "get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for ministry" (2 Timothy 4:11). In Philemon 1:24, Mark stands alongside other key leaders as one of Paul's recognized "fellow laborers."

4. Theological Implications

The presence of Mark in Philemon 1:24 carries rich theological depth regarding grace, redemption, and Christian service.

First, Mark's journey illustrates the reality of restoration in Christian ministry. Early failure or faltering does not permanently disqualify a believer from being useful to God. Though Paul once hesitated to trust Mark due to his early withdrawal, divine grace transformed Mark into an indispensable partner in preaching the gospel.

Second, Mark’s inclusion highlights the collaborative nature of apostolic ministry. Paul did not serve in isolation; the kingdom of God advances through interconnected believers working together in shared commitment to Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the closing lines of the Epistle to Philemon, Paul extends personal greetings from his ministry companions in Rome (Philemon 1:23–24). Writing from prison, Paul lists Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke.

The presence of Mark in this verse is especially significant given the main theme of the letter to Philemon. Paul is writing to plead for Onesimus, a runaway servant who had converted to Christ under Paul’s ministry. Paul asks Philemon to forgive Onesimus and receive him no longer as a slave, but as a beloved brother in Christ.

By sending greetings from Mark—a man whom Paul himself had once rejected after a failure, but whom he later forgave, restored, and honored as a "fellow laborer"—Paul subtly reinforces his message. Philemon would know Mark's story. In listing Mark as a valued co-worker, Paul models the exact pattern of grace and reconciliation he asks Philemon to extend to Onesimus.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 12:25 — "And Barnabas and Saul returned from Jerusalem when they had fulfilled their ministry, and they also took with them John whose surname was Mark."

  • Colossians 4:10 — "Aristarchus my fellow prisoner greets you, with Mark the cousin of Barnabas (about whom you received instructions: if he comes to you, welcome him)."

  • 2 Timothy 4:11 — "Only Luke is with me. Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for ministry."

  • 1 Peter 5:13 — "She who is in Babylon, elect together with you, greets you; and so does Mark my son."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Early church history consistently connects this same Mark with the composition of the second Gospel. Writing in the second century, early Christian leaders recorded that Mark served as Peter's translator and written recorder, compiling Peter’s eyewitness accounts of Jesus' life and ministry into the Gospel of Mark.

In Philemon 1:24, Paul explicitly designates Mark as one of his "fellow laborers." This designation places Mark on equal footing with Paul's trusted associates, showing that he was actively engaged in the heavy work of church planting, pastoral oversight, and gospel proclamation.
