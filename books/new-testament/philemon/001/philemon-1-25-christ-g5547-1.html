CHRIST - (Christós, Strong's G5547)

Christos (Christos, Strong's G5547)

Summary:

Christos means "anointed one." In Greek, it translates the Hebrew title Mashiach (Messiah), referring to the royal and priestly Deliverer promised by God throughout the Old Testament. While the word began as a divine title for Jesus, the early church also used it alongside His personal name. In Philemon 1:25, Christos forms part of the full title "our Lord Jesus Christ," identifying Him as the divine source of grace that sustains Christian fellowship and empowers gospel reconciliation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Greek noun Christos comes from a verb meaning to smear or anoint with oil. In ancient Israel, kings, priests, and occasionally prophets were anointed with physical oil during their commissioning. This ritual was a visible sign that God had appointed, set apart, and empowered a person for a divine task.

When Jewish scholars translated the Old Testament Scriptures into Greek (producing the Septuagint), they selected Christos to translate the Hebrew word Mashiach. Over centuries of prophetic revelation, Israel came to look forward to one specific Anointed One—the Messiah—who would come from the line of David to establish God's kingdom, deliver His people, and fulfill His covenant promises. By the New Testament era, Christos pointed directly to this expected Redeemer.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Christos means "Anointed One," "Messiah," or "the Anointed."

In the New Testament, the term serves two distinct but connected roles:

  • As an official title: "the Christ" (the promised Messiah of Israel).

  • As a proper name or title phrase: "Jesus Christ" or "our Lord Jesus Christ."

The connection between Hebrew Mashiach and Greek Christos is well-attested across biblical and ancient Jewish literature. In Philemon 1:25, the noun appears in the genitive case, singular number, and masculine gender. It functions as part of a compound title that identifies Jesus as the divine Lord and Messiah who bestows grace upon His people.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, the use of Christos reflects the early church's central declaration: Jesus of Nazareth is God's long-promised Messiah.

In the Gospels, the term often retains its primary title function. People debate whether Jesus is "the Christ," and His disciples confess Him as such. In the Book of Acts and the Epistles, Christos is frequently joined directly with the personal name Jesus. The combined phrase "Lord Jesus Christ" brings together three distinct truths about Him:

  • "Lord" affirms His divine authority and supreme rule.

  • "Jesus" recalls His historical human life and His mission to save His people from sin.

  • "Christ" marks Him as the anointed fulfillment of Old Testament prophecy.

4. Theological Implications

The word Christos grounds the Christian faith in biblical history. Jesus did not arrive in a vacuum; He came as the ultimate fulfillment of God's redemptive plan revealed through the Old Testament Scriptures. His anointing was not with ceremonial oil, but with the Holy Spirit without measure.

In Paul's letters, Christos is central to the doctrine of salvation. Believers are said to be united with Christ, to have died with Christ, and to be raised with Him to new life. Furthermore, when Paul opens or closes his letters by wishing grace from "our Lord Jesus Christ," he places Christ alongside God the Father as the divine source of grace, mercy, and peace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Philemon 1:25 forms the closing benediction of Paul's short letter to Philemon. Throughout the letter, Paul addresses a delicate and challenging situation: he appeals to Philemon to forgive his runaway bondservant Onesimus, who has become a Christian, and to welcome him back as a beloved brother in the Lord.

Asking Philemon to lay aside cultural rights and potential resentment required extraordinary spiritual transformation. Paul concludes his letter by pointing Philemon to the true source of that spiritual power: "The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit." By using the full title "our Lord Jesus Christ," Paul reminds Philemon that Jesus is the anointed Lord who has extended grace to both slave and master alike. The grace needed to forgive and restore comes directly from Christ Himself.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:1 – "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham." Here Christ connects Jesus directly to the ancient covenant promises made to Abraham and David.

  • Acts 17:3 – "...explaining and demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead, and saying, 'This Jesus whom I preach to you is the Christ.'" Here Christ refers to the Old Testament Messiah whose identity was proven through His suffering, death, and resurrection.

  • Romans 6:8 – "Now if we died with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with Him." Here Christ refers to the Savior with whom believers are spiritually united in His death and resurrected life.

  • 1 Peter 4:14 – "If you are reproached for the name of Christ, blessed are you..." Here Christ represents the shared identity of believers, who receive divine honor when enduring hardship for their allegiance to Him.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Philemon 1:25, the word "your" in "your spirit" is plural in the original Greek text. While the letter contains personal requests directed to Philemon specifically, Paul intentionally addresses the entire household community mentioned at the beginning of the letter—including Apphia, Archippus, and the church that met in Philemon's house. The grace of Christ is meant to inhabit and strengthen the whole corporate fellowship as they walk together in gospel unity.
