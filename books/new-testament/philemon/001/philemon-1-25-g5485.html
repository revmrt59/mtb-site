GRACE - (charis, Strong's G5485)

Summary:

In the New Testament, charis represents the unmerited, active favor of God toward undeserving humanity. It is not merely a passive attitude of goodwill, but a dynamic, life-transforming force that initiates salvation, sustains believers in their daily walk, and empowers spiritual growth. In Philemon 1:25, Paul uses this word as a powerful closing benediction, praying that the active, personal presence of Jesus Christ's grace would rest upon the innermost being ("spirit") of Philemon and his household, enabling them to extend the very same grace to Onesimus.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Greek word charis is derived from the root chairo, which means to rejoice or be glad. In classical Greek, the word originally described something that causes joy, pleasure, delight, or physical beauty (such as elegance of form or speech).

Over time, charis evolved to denote a favor freely done by a benefactor for someone of lower status, which naturally called for a response of gratitude. In the Greco-Roman patron-client system, a patron bestowed charis (favor/benefits) on a client, who in turn owed the patron loyalty and public gratitude.

The writers of the New Testament, under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, took this common cultural concept and elevated it. In the Christian context, charis becomes the ultimate expression of God's free, unmerited, and costly favor toward rebellious humanity, completely detached from any human merit, works, or system of mutual obligation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In biblical usage, charis carries several distinct but closely related meanings:

  • Unmerited favor and goodwill: The merciful, loving disposition of God toward undeserving sinners, offering salvation and reconciliation (Romans 3:24, Ephesians 2:8).

  • Divine influence and enablement: The operational power of God working in the heart of a believer, giving them the desire and the ability to do His will (1 Corinthians 15:10, 2 Corinthians 12:9).

  • Gratitude or thanks: The response of praise and thankfulness for favors received (Luke 17:9, 1 Corinthians 15:57).

  • Graciousness of speech or manner: Spiritual beauty, kindness, and pleasantness in human interaction (Colossians 4:6).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the New Testament, charis is frequently contrasted with works (ergon) and debt (opheilema). In Romans 4:4, Paul notes that a worker’s wage is not counted as charis (a gift) but as a debt. Therefore, grace is depicted as a free, unconditional gift (dorea).

The word is also illustrated through vivid contrasts:

  • The Throne of Grace: In Hebrews 4:16, God's presence is described not as a terrifying throne of judgment, but as a "throne of grace" where believers can boldly find help in times of need.

  • A Reigning Power: In Romans 5:21, grace is personified as a reigning monarch, superseding the reign of sin and death.

  • A Teacher or Trainer: In Titus 2:11-12, saving grace is described as an instructor that "teaches us" to deny ungodliness and live soberly in the present age.

4. Theological Implications

Theology is fundamentally defined by grace. Salvation is initiated, sustained, and completed by grace. Because humans are dead in sin, they have no capacity to earn God's favor. Therefore, grace is entirely monergistic in salvation—God acts on behalf of the helpless.

Furthermore, grace is Christological. It is not an abstract substance or an impersonal force; it is deeply personal, embodied in the person and work of Jesus Christ. The "grace of our Lord Jesus Christ" refers to His self-emptying condescension—though He was rich, for our sakes He became poor, that we through His poverty might become rich (2 Corinthians 8:9).

Finally, grace is the source of all spiritual gifts (charismata), which are simply concrete expressions of God’s grace distributed to believers for the building up of the church (Romans 12:6, 1 Peter 4:10).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:25, Paul concludes his letter with the blessing: "The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Amen." Far from a routine sign-off, this benediction is the theological engine of the entire epistle.

Paul has spent the letter asking Philemon to do something culturally outrageous and incredibly difficult: to forgive his runaway slave, Onesimus, who had likely stolen from him, and to welcome him back not as a slave, but as a beloved brother in Christ. In the Roman Empire, runaway slaves were routinely tortured or executed to deter others. To forgive and elevate Onesimus required Philemon to act completely contrary to societal expectations, his legal rights, and his natural human pride.

Paul recognizes that Philemon cannot accomplish this supernatural feat of forgiveness in his own strength. He needs the charis of Christ.

  • Corporate Grace: The Greek pronoun for "your" (hymon) in verse 25 is plural. Paul is praying for Christ's grace to rest upon Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, and the entire house church. The community must collectively support this act of radical reconciliation.

  • The "Spirit": By praying that this grace be "with your spirit" (pneuma), Paul targets the innermost seat of human attitude, volition, and emotion. The transformation must happen from the inside out. Only the living power of Christ's grace can soften a betrayed master's heart to embrace a runaway slave as a brother.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 1:17: "For the law was given through Moses, but grace and truth came through Jesus Christ."

  • Romans 3:24: "being justified freely by His grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus,"

  • 2 Corinthians 12:9: "And He said to me, 'My grace is sufficient for you, for My strength is made perfect in weakness.'"

  • Ephesians 2:8: "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God,"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s typical epistolary greeting and closing—pairing "grace" (charis) and "peace" (eirene)—is a beautiful blending of Greek and Hebrew worlds. The traditional Greek greeting was chairein ("greetings"), which Paul transforms into the spiritually rich charis ("grace"). The Hebrew greeting was shalom ("peace"). By combining them, Paul shows that God's grace is the only source of true peace, both peace with God and peace with one another.
