GRACE - (cháris, Strong's G5485)

Summary:

The Greek word charis basic meaning refers to unmerited favor, goodwill, or a generous gift given without expectation of return. Depending on the context, it can also describe gracious speech, financial help, or an expression of gratitude. In Philemon 1:25, charis serves as the final benediction of the letter. Paul prays that the undeserved favor and spiritual power of the Lord Jesus Christ will rest upon the inner spirit of Philemon and his church community, providing them with the divine enablements required to practice costly Christian forgiveness and reconciliation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The letter to Philemon is a personal yet public appeal written by the apostle Paul while in prison. Philemon was a Christian master whose bondservant, Onesimus, had run away and likely stolen from him. After meeting Paul, Onesimus became a believer. Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon, asking Philemon to receive him no longer merely as a slave, but as a beloved brother in Christ.

Reconciling a master and a runaway slave required genuine spiritual transformation. It called for Philemon to forgo legal retaliation and financial restitution, choosing instead brotherly love. To close this delicate pastoral letter, Paul pronounces a final blessing of charis ("grace") in verse 25. By calling upon the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, Paul grounds the demanding duty of reconciliation in the free, undeserved favor and strengthening power that believers receive from Christ.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Charis carries a rich semantic range in the New Testament. The word can mean:

  • Undeserved favor or goodwill extended by a superior to an inferior.

  • Divine help and enabling power granted by God to believers for holy living and ministry.

  • A voluntary benefit, gift, or charitable contribution.

  • Graciousness, charm, or winsomeness in speech and conduct.

  • Expressions of gratitude or thanksgiving.

Here in Philemon 1:25, charis functions as the subject of a prayerful blessing. It refers to the merciful favor and active spiritual power flowing from Jesus Christ to sustain the believer's inner life.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, charis is used in several distinct ways:

  • Divine Salvation and Favor: Scripture frequently uses charis to describe God's free initiative in saving sinners apart from works or human merit.

  • Gracious Speech and Conduct: The word describes wholesome speech that builds up listeners or conduct that is pleasing and honorable before God.

  • Spiritual Enablement: It represents the active work of God in the human heart, empowering believers to endure trials, serve the church, and obey God's commands.

  • Expressions of Thanks: In several passages, charis carries the practical sense of giving thanks or expressing gratitude.

In Philemon 1:25, charis is coupled with the phrase "of our Lord Jesus Christ." This shows that grace is not an abstract force, but a personal blessing rooted in the person and work of Christ.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of charis at the conclusion of Philemon highlights a central truth of Christian ethics: believers cannot fulfill gospel demands in human strength alone. Forgiving someone who has wronged you requires a dynamic work of God within the heart.

Because believers have received unlimited, unmerited grace from Christ, they are enabled to extend grace to others. Christ's grace is both the pattern for human forgiveness and the power that accomplishes it. Without divine grace, Philemon would be driven by cultural expectations of penalty and pride; with grace, he is enabled to embrace his former runaway servant as a brother.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Philemon 1:25 reads: "The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Amen."

In this closing verse, charis functions as the summary blessing for the entire letter. Paul addresses "your spirit," where the word "your" in the original phrasing is plural. This indicates that while the letter focuses on Philemon, the blessing is pronounced upon his whole household and the local church that meets in his home.

Paul prays that Christ's grace will dwell in their innermost spirit—the center of thought, devotion, and decision. Paul leaves Philemon with a gentle reminder: the decision to forgive Onesimus must spring from the inward reception of Christ's grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 1:30 — "Then the angel said to her, 'Do not be afraid, Mary, for you have found favor with God.'"

  • Ephesians 2:5 — "...even when we were dead in trespasses, made us alive together with Christ (by grace you have been saved)..."

  • Ephesians 4:29 — "Let no corrupt word proceed out of your mouth, but what is good for necessary edification, that it may impart grace to the hearers."

  • 1 Peter 2:19 — "For this is commendable, if because of conscience toward God one endures grief, suffering wrongfully."

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is noteworthy that Paul opens and closes the letter to Philemon with grace. In verse 3, he writes, "Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ." In verse 25, he ends with "The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit."

This literary bookend places the sensitive issue of Onesimus entirely within the realm of grace. Everything Paul asks Philemon to do—accepting Onesimus, forgiving his debt, and treating him as an equal in Christ—is framed by the grace that began their Christian lives and the grace that continues to sustain them.
