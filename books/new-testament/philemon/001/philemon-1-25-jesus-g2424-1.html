JESUS - (Iēsoûs, Strong's G2424)

Summary:

The name Iēsous is a proper noun meaning "Yahweh is salvation" or "the LORD saves," serving as the Greek form of the Old Testament Hebrew name Joshua. Throughout the New Testament, it identifies Jesus of Nazareth, the incarnate Son of God and promised Messiah. In Philemon 1:25, the name forms part of the title "our Lord Jesus Christ," identifying the divine Savior whose grace empowers believers to forgive one another and walk in Christian unity.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Iēsous is the Greek adaptation of the Hebrew personal name Yehōšūaʿ (Joshua) or its shortened post-exilic form Yēšûaʿ. In first-century Jewish society, the name was relatively common, carrying deep historical associations with Joshua, the successor of Moses who led Israel into the Promised Land. In the New Testament, God directed that Mary's son be named Jesus because He would save His people from their sins.

Paul wrote his letter to Philemon while imprisoned, addressing a private yet corporately significant matter. Onesimus, a bondservant who had fled from his master Philemon, had encountered Paul and embraced faith in Christ. Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon with this short letter, urging Philemon to receive him no longer merely as a servant, but as a beloved brother in the Lord.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Iēsous is a proper noun used to identify a specific person. Etymologically, the name derives from Hebrew roots combining the divine name Yahweh with a verb meaning to save, deliver, or rescue. Thus, the established meaning of the name is "Yahweh is salvation" or "Yahweh saves."

In the Greco-Roman world, translating Hebrew names into Greek was standard practice for Jewish writers communicating in the common language of the Mediterranean basin. The meaning of the name is well-established across biblical and ancient Jewish literature. In Philemon 1:25, the name identifies the historical Person who lived, died, and rose again, combined here with the divine title "Lord" (Kyrios) and the messianic title "Christ" (Christos).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, Iēsous appears in several distinct combinations:

  • As a standalone personal name in the narrative Gospels (such as "Jesus said" or "Jesus went"), drawing direct attention to His earthly life, interactions, and ministry.

  • Combined with His title as "Jesus Christ," highlighting that the historical person Jesus is the anointed Messiah promised in Old Testament Scripture.

  • Combined as "Lord Jesus" or "our Lord Jesus Christ," emphasizing His present exalted authority as sovereign Ruler over the church and creation.

In the Epistles, Paul rarely uses the name "Jesus" in isolation. He routinely joins it with "Lord" and "Christ." This practice reminds readers that the historical man who walked the roads of Galilee is identical to the resurrected and reigning Lord of glory.

4. Theological Implications

The name Iēsous connects the New Covenant directly to God's ancient promise of salvation. By identifying Jesus as the one through whom God saves, the text grounds Christian faith in history rather than abstract philosophy.

In Philemon, this saving identity carries direct practical force. Philemon was called to grant costly forgiveness and welcome a former runaway. Human effort and social convention in the Roman empire militated against such mercy. However, because Philemon himself had received salvation through Jesus, the posture of his heart was meant to reflect the saving character of his Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Philemon 1:25 forms the closing benediction of the letter: "The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Amen."

The grammatical form of Iēsous here is masculine singular in the genitive case, showing that grace belongs to and flows from Jesus Christ. Grace is not an impersonal force or a vague religious feeling; it is the undeserved favor and spiritual strength that comes directly from the Lord Jesus.

By concluding his letter with this prayer, Paul places the resolution of the conflict between Philemon and Onesimus under the authority and grace of Jesus. Philemon could not fulfill Paul's request in his own natural strength. He needed the ongoing grace of the Lord Jesus Christ to transform his heart, overcome social pride, and extend genuine brotherhood to Onesimus.

  • Romans 14:14 — "I know and am convinced by the Lord Jesus that there is nothing unclean of itself..."

  • Philippians 1:8 — "For God is my witness, how greatly I long for you all with the affection of Jesus Christ."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The full title "our Lord Jesus Christ" balances His divine authority ("Lord"), His saving historical identity ("Jesus"), and His messianic office ("Christ").

  • In New Testament epistles, a closing benediction is not a mere polite closing formula. It serves as an active pastoral invocation, asking God to supply the spiritual strength necessary for the letter's instructions to be lived out.

  • While the English word "your" in verse 25 sounds singular, the context of the blessing addresses the entire local house church gathered with Philemon, showing that the grace of Jesus builds up the whole fellowship in shared love and reconciliation.
