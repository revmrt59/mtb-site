LORD - (kýrios, Strong's G2962)

Summary:

The Greek word kyrios refers to an owner, master, ruler, or person possessing supreme authority. In everyday ancient settings, it described a head of a household or a master of servants. In the New Testament, the word is frequently used as a supreme title for Jesus Christ, affirming His divine authority, headship over the church, and rightful ownership of believers. In Philemon 1:25, kyrios appears in Paul's closing blessing ("our Lord Jesus Christ"), anchoring the entire letter's appeal regarding master and servant under the higher authority of Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greek world, kyrios was a standard word used for anyone who held authority, ownership, or legal control over persons or property. It described household heads, estate owners, rulers, and civil authorities. When Greek translators produced the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), they used kyrios to translate the divine name of God (YHWH) as well as the title Adonai ("Lord").

Because of this Old Testament background, kyrios carried immense weight for early Jewish and Gentile believers. Calling someone kyrios could range from a polite address like "sir" to an absolute declaration of divine sovereignty. When applied to Jesus Christ, the title identified Him not merely as a respected teacher, but as the supreme Lord of all, sharing in the identity and authority of God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to a master, owner, ruler, or sovereign commander. Depending on who is being addressed and the context of the statement, the term carries several distinct senses:

  • An owner or master of property, domestic servants, or slaves.

  • A polite title of respect, similar to "sir" or "master."

  • A ruler, monarch, or official holding legitimate authority.

  • The divine name or title for God the Father.

  • The supreme title for Jesus Christ, expressing His divine identity, resurrection authority, and personal lordship over believers.

In Philemon 1:25, the word refers directly to Jesus Christ as the supreme Lord who possesses ultimate authority over Paul, Philemon, and Onesimus.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, kyrios is used in both earthly and spiritual contexts. In simple social interactions, people used the word to address leaders or property owners with basic respect. In domestic settings, it identified the master of a house or servants.

However, the dominant Christian usage of kyrios centers on Jesus Christ. By calling Jesus kyrios, early Christians declared that He had risen from the dead and received authority over all creation. Confessing Jesus as Lord was not a casual statement; it meant acknowledging His supreme right to direct a person's life, choices, and relationships.

4. Theological Implications

The title kyrios highlights the divine authority and ruler-ship of Jesus. Believers do not merely admire Jesus as a moral guide; they submit to Him as their sovereign Master.

Because Jesus is Lord, all human relationships and social structures are brought under His oversight. Masters and servants, leaders and followers, must yield their rights and conduct to His commands. Furthermore, because kyrios was the term chosen to represent the divine name of God in Greek, applying this title to Jesus places Him on the same level of worship, honor, and obedience as God the Father.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:25, Paul closes his letter with a benediction: "The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit." The word appears in the genitive case, singular number, and masculine gender, functioning as a descriptive title alongside "Jesus Christ."

This closing mention of Jesus as "Lord" is deeply significant for the message of the whole letter. Paul had spent the letter asking Philemon, a Christian slave owner, to receive back Onesimus, a runaway slave who had become a Christian, no longer merely as a slave, but as a beloved brother in Christ.

By referring to Jesus as "our Lord" at the conclusion, Paul reminds Philemon that neither of them is his own ultimate master. Philemon may be an earthly master over servants, but he has a higher Master in heaven. Onesimus may have been an earthly servant, but he now shares in the grace of the same Lord. The phrase "our Lord" unites Paul, Philemon, and Onesimus under one shared Master, making love, forgiveness, and brotherhood the necessary response to His grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 20:28 — "And Thomas answered and said to Him, 'My Lord and my God!'"

  • Ephesians 6:9 — "And you, masters, do the same things to them, giving up threatening, knowing that your own Master also is in heaven, and there is no partiality with Him."

  • Acts 21:13 — "Then Paul answered, 'What do you mean by weeping and breaking my heart? For I am ready not only to be bound, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus.'"

  • Philemon 1:5 — "hearing of your love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The title kyrios creates a vital connection between grace and authority. Notice that in Philemon 1:25, Paul speaks of "the grace of our Lord." His lordship is not a harsh tyranny, but the source of life-transforming grace.

When believers submit to Jesus as Lord, they receive His unmerited favor, which reshapes how they treat others. Authority in the kingdom of Christ operates through grace, prompting believers to show mercy, grant forgiveness, and yield their personal rights out of devotion to their heavenly Master.
