CHRIST - (Christós, Strong's G5547)

Summary:

The word Christos translates the Hebrew title Mashiach (Messiah) and literally means "Anointed One." In the New Testament, it functions both as the royal title for God's promised King and as part of the proper name of Jesus.

In Philemon 1:3, "Christ" identifies Jesus as the exalted Messiah who, alongside God the Father, is the divine source of grace and peace for believers and the church.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Greek word Christos comes from a verb meaning to smear or rub with oil. In ancient Israel, priests, prophets, and kings were anointed with oil to set them apart for holy service and to signal God's authorization for their role.

When Jewish scholars translated the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, they consistently used Christos to render the Hebrew word Mashiach (Messiah). By the time of the New Testament, God's people eagerly looked for the Messiah—the promised Son of David who would bring salvation, establish righteousness, and rule as King.

In the New Testament, Jesus of Nazareth is revealed as the fulfillment of these ancient promises. Early Jewish believers recognized Him as the Christ. As the good news spread throughout the Greco-Roman world, Christos came to be used both as a title ("Jesus the Christ") and as a proper name ("Jesus Christ" or "Christ Jesus").

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Christos is a Greek title and proper name that means "Anointed One."

The word identifies a person specifically designated and empowered by God for a divine mission. In Biblical usage, it refers exclusively to:

  • The expected Messianic Deliverer promised in the Old Testament.

  • Jesus of Nazareth, who fulfills that Messianic role through His life, death, resurrection, and ongoing rule.

While earthly kings were anointed with physical oil, Jesus was anointed by God with the Holy Spirit and power to save His people.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Gospels, Christos frequently appears with the definite article ("the Christ"), highlighting His title and office. In legal trials and public debates, the central question was whether Jesus was indeed "the Christ, the Son of God."

In the New Testament Epistles, particularly in Paul's writings, Christos appears in several distinct patterns:

  • As part of a full title: "the Lord Jesus Christ."

  • As a double name: "Jesus Christ" or "Christ Jesus."

  • Standalone: "Christ," emphasizing His ongoing, living identity as Lord of the church.

Paul frequently places believers "in Christ," describing the spiritual union that binds believers to Jesus and bestows on them all the blessings of salvation.

4. Theological Implications

The title Christ anchors Christian faith firmly in God's historical redemptive plan. Calling Jesus "Christ" affirms that He is not an isolated moral teacher, but the long-promised Savior foretold throughout Scripture.

Furthermore, when the New Testament joins "the Lord Jesus Christ" with "God our Father" as the source of divine blessing, it reveals the divine identity and authority of Jesus. Grace and peace do not originate from human effort or worldly circumstances, but come directly from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:3, Paul writes: "Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."

Here, the word Christ forms part of the full title "the Lord Jesus Christ." The word is in the genitive case, showing that grace and peace flow from Him as their source. By joining Jesus Christ to God the Father under a single preposition ("from"), Paul shows that divine favor and spiritual wholeness originate equally from the Father and the Son.

This greeting serves an important purpose in the letter. Paul is preparing to ask Philemon to forgive and receive his runaway slave, Onesimus, as a brother in Christ. By reminding Philemon of the grace and peace already granted to him by the Lord Jesus Christ, Paul establishes the spiritual foundation for reconciliation. Forgiveness among believers flows directly from the grace received from Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:1 — "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham."

  • John 11:27 — "She said to Him, 'Yes, Lord, I believe that You are the Christ, the Son of God, who is to come into the world.'"

  • Romans 6:8 — "Now if we died with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with Him,"

  • 1 Corinthians 1:3 — "Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."

  • 1 Peter 4:14 — "If you are reproached for the name of Christ, blessed are you, for the Spirit of glory and of God rests upon you."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The full designation "the Lord Jesus Christ" brings together three distinct aspects of His identity:

  • Lord (Kyrios): His supreme authority, sovereignty, and divine standing.

  • Jesus (Iesous): His earthly name, highlighting His real humanity and His mission as Savior.

  • Christ (Christos): His official Messianic title, highlighting His role as God's anointed King.

In Philemon 1:3, this full title emphasizes that the appeal Paul is about to make carries the weight and blessing of the exalted Savior Himself.
