FATHER - (patḗr, Strong's G3962)

Summary:

The word pater refers to a male parent, an ancestor, or God in His character as Creator, Provider, and spiritual Father to His people. In Philemon 1:3, the term identifies God as the common heavenly Father shared by Paul, Philemon, and the church, grounding the identity and mutual relationships of believers in the divine family.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greek world, pater referred primarily to a biological father, male progenitor, or head of a household who exercised authority over his family. In the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, the word described earthly fathers and patriarchs, but it was also used to describe God’s caring, disciplinary, and protective relationship toward the nation of Israel.

By the time of the New Testament, Jesus brought this divine title into central focus, teaching His followers to approach God as their Father. In the letters of the apostles, God as Father becomes the foundational title for the first person of the Trinity, highlighting spiritual adoption and the shared family bond enjoyed by all believers in Christ.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word pater can refer to:

  • A biological father or immediate male parent.

  • A forefather, ancestor, or patriarch of a family or nation.

  • An elder, leader, or spiritual mentor addressed with respect.

  • God the Father, the first person of the Trinity, as Creator and Redeemer.

In Philemon 1:3, the word refers directly to God in His role as the heavenly Father of believers.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the New Testament, pater is used in two primary settings: human families and divine identity.

In human relationships, it identifies immediate biological fathers, ancestral lineages (such as Abraham), and heads of domestic households.

In divine relationships, Jesus uses the word continually to describe His eternal, unique relationship with God. Jesus also extends this privilege to His followers, instructing them to address God as their heavenly Father. The apostles frequently open their letters with the greeting "God our Father," joining the Father and Jesus Christ together as the single source of grace, peace, and spiritual blessing.

4. Theological Implications

Calling God "Father" balances divine authority with intimate, tender care. God is not a distant ruler; He is a loving heavenly Father who knows the needs of His children, corrects them for their good, and protects them.

Furthermore, identifying God as Father establishes the corporate identity of the church. When believers share one Father, they become brothers and sisters to one another. This divine family structure redefines human relationships, replacing social division and hostility with mutual love, forgiveness, and equal dignity in Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:3, Paul writes, "Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ." Paul is preparing to make a delicate pastoral request of Philemon. Onesimus, a runaway bondservant who belonged to Philemon, had encountered Paul in prison, come to faith in Christ, and was now being sent back.

By referring to "God our Father," Paul establishes a shared identity before making his appeal. Philemon was a master and house-church host, but above all, he was a child of God. Onesimus, though previously a servant who fled, was now also a child of the same heavenly Father. By placing the greeting under the headship of "God our Father," Paul gently reminds Philemon that their shared family connection in God must govern how Philemon receives Onesimus. Grace and peace flow from the Father, calling Philemon to reflect that same grace and peace toward his newly converted brother.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Roman society during the first century, the male head of a house held immense legal authority over children and servants.

When Paul invokes "God our Father" in a letter to a house-church leader, he redefines authority. Earthly household roles still existed, but they were now governed by the higher authority of God's family. Philemon's primary identity was not master over a house, but a son under God the Father. This truth gently transformed legal rights into opportunities for gospel forgiveness and brotherly love.
