GRACE - (charis, Strong's G5485)

Summary:

In Philemon 1:3, charis (grace) is Paul's opening blessing of God's unmerited favor, transforming a standard Greco-Roman greeting into a deep spiritual benediction. In the context of Philemon, it is not merely a polite greeting but the foundational reality of the gospel that enables reconciliation, forgiveness, and restored relationships between a master and a runaway slave.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In classical Greek culture, charis carried the sense of beauty, attractiveness, favor, or a gift that brings joy. It was closely connected to the concept of reciprocity—giving a gift with the expectation of receiving favor in return. The greeting chairein ("greetings" or "rejoice") was the standard opening for Greco-Roman letters. The New Testament writers took this secular, culturally loaded term and infused it with profound theological weight. Instead of a superficial wish for wellness or a transactional favor, charis became the supreme word for God's free, unearned, and loving initiation toward undeserving humanity. Paul famously couples charis with the Hebrew concept of peace (shalom/eirene), transforming a mundane greeting into a powerful declaration of gospel reality.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Charis refers to:

  • Goodwill, favor, or lovingkindness shown by a superior to an inferior, entirely apart from any merit or claim on the part of the recipient.

  • The state of being in favor or receiving kind treatment.

  • A gift, benefit, or expression of generosity.

  • Gratitude or thanks offered in response to a benefit received.

In the New Testament epistles, it most frequently denotes the merciful, saving kindness of God in Christ, which regenerates, strengthens, and sustains believers.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Charis is used over 150 times in the New Testament, with the vast majority appearing in the Pauline epistles. It functions as a multi-dimensional word picture of God's heart:

  • A Fountain of Generosity: It represents a gift that flows downward from a sovereign Giver, requiring no payment and leaving the recipient in joyful, willing devotion.

  • Transforming Power: It is not just a passive attitude in God, but an active, divine influence on the human heart that restrains sin, inspires holiness, and equips for ministry.

  • Spiritual Atmosphere: It represents the new realm in which the believer stands, moving from the kingdom of law and condemnation into the security of divine acceptance.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, charis is the catalyst of the entire Christian life.

  • Source of Salvation: Salvation is entirely by grace through faith, apart from works, preventing human boasting.

  • Sovereign Initiation: God moves toward humanity first. Grace is given to those who deserve the opposite—wrath and judgment.

  • Equalizing Force: Grace levels all human distinctions. In the economy of grace, the social hierarchy of the Roman Empire (such as master and slave) is subordinated to a shared identity in Christ. Both Philemon (the master) and Onesimus (the slave) stand on the same ground of unmerited favor.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:3, Paul writes: "Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."

  • A Gospel Foundation for Reconciliation: Paul's use of charis is strategically placed. He is preparing to ask Philemon to do something culturally shocking: forgive and receive his runaway slave, Onesimus, as a beloved brother. Paul begins by reminding Philemon of the charis he himself has received from God.

  • Vertical to Horizontal: Because Philemon is a recipient of divine grace, he is expected to extend horizontal grace to Onesimus. The theological greeting acts as the ethical framework for the entire letter.

  • Divine Source: This grace flows from a dual source—"God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ"—highlighting the deity of Christ and the unified divine will to bestow favor on the church meeting in Philemon's house.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 1:14: "And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld His glory, the glory as of the only begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth."

  • Romans 3:24: "being justified freely by His grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus,"

  • Ephesians 2:8: "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God,"

  • Titus 2:11: "For the grace of God that brings salvation has appeared to all men,"

  • Hebrews 4:16: "Let us therefore come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Structural Symmetry: Paul begins almost all of his letters with a variation of "Grace and peace to you" and ends them with a benediction of grace. Grace is the bookend of the Christian experience; the Christian life begins, continues, and ends in the grace of God.

  • Cultural Subversion: In Roman society, the relationship between a patron and a client was governed by strict, transactional rules of leverage, debt, and obligation. By contrast, Paul introduces a community governed by charis—a free, generous, and non-exploitative love that reshapes all social dynamics.
