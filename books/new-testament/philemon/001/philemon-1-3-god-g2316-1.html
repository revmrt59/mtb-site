GOD - (theós, Strong's G2316)

Summary:

The word theos means "god" or "God." In the Greek New Testament, it refers primarily to the one true God, the creator and redeemer who reveals Himself throughout Holy Scripture. In Philemon 1:3, Paul uses theos to identify God as "our Father" and the joint source—along with the Lord Jesus Christ—of divine grace and peace. This opening blessing establishes that all believers belong to the same heavenly family, laying the foundation for Paul's request that Philemon forgive and welcome his runaway servant as a brother in Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the broader Greek-speaking world of the first century, people used the word theos for any deity, whether pagan idol, mythological god, or deified emperor. However, when Jewish and early Christian authors wrote in Greek, they reclaimed theos to designate the unique, self-existent Creator who made heaven and earth and entered into covenant with His people.

When Paul wrote his letter to Philemon from prison, he addressed a Christian leader, his family, and the church meeting in his house. By beginning his greeting with a blessing from theos, Paul immediately draws the readers' attention away from earthly hierarchies and redirects it toward the supreme Lord over all.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Theos refers to God as the ultimate divine Being. In biblical usage, it means:

  • The one true God, Creator of heaven and earth.

  • God the Father, distinguished in personal role within the Trinity.

  • The God who acts in history to redeem His people through Jesus Christ.

In Philemon 1:3, the word identifies God specifically as "our Father," emphasizing both His divine majesty and His loving relationship with believers.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, theos is the standard term for the living God. The writers do not treat God as a distant force, but as a living Person who speaks, saves, judges, and shows mercy.

The word frequently joins with title descriptions to clarify God's character and relationship to His people:

  • God as Father (patēr), highlighting His loving care and family identity with believers.

  • God as the source of salvation, power, and wisdom.

  • God in close connection with Jesus Christ, demonstrating that divine blessings flow directly through the Son.

In greetings throughout Paul's letters, theos routinely opens the epistle by grounding all ministry, hope, and instruction in God's authority.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of theos in Philemon 1:3 carries rich theological significance for Christian life and relationship:

  • Divine Origin of Grace and Peace: Grace (unmerited favor) and peace (reconciliation and spiritual wholeness) do not originate in human effort or social status; they come directly from God.

  • Shared Fatherhood: By identifying God as "our Father," Paul places himself, Philemon, and every Christian under the same fatherly authority. All believers are children of the same heavenly Father.

  • Unity of Father and Son: Paul links God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ as the shared source of grace and peace, affirming the divine authority of Jesus alongside the Father.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Philemon is a deeply personal letter about reconciliation, forgiveness, and Christian brotherhood. Onesimus, a bondservant belonging to Philemon, had run away and later came to faith in Christ through Paul's ministry. Paul is writing to ask Philemon to receive Onesimus back—no longer merely as a servant, but as a beloved brother.

The use of theos in verse 3 sets up this delicate pastoral appeal. Before Paul addresses the practical conflict between Philemon and Onesimus, he reminds Philemon that God is "our Father." This simple phrase establishes three key truths for the passage:

  • Philemon's authority as a master is subordinate to God's authority as Father.

  • Philemon and Onesimus now share the exact same heavenly Father.

  • The grace and peace Philemon has received from God must shape how he treats others.

By grounding his letter in theos, Paul demonstrates that earthly grievances must be evaluated in light of God's family grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 6:27 — "Do not labor for the food which perishes, but for the food which endures to everlasting life, which the Son of Man will give you, because God the Father has set His seal on Him."

  • 1 Corinthians 2:5 — "that your faith should not be in the wisdom of men but in the power of God."

  • 1 Timothy 2:5 — "For there is one God and one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus."

  • 1 Peter 1:2 — "elect according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, in sanctification of the Spirit, for obedience and sprinkling of the blood of Jesus Christ: Grace to you and peace be multiplied."

  • 1 John 4:7 — "Beloved, let us love one another, for love is from God; and everyone who loves is born of God and knows God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Grammatically, theos in Philemon 1:3 functions in the genitive case, marking God as the source from whom grace and peace proceed.

It is also significant that Paul does not offer a bare philosophical declaration about God. He immediately links theos to relational terms ("our Father") and apostolic blessings ("grace and peace"). This reminds the reader that biblical theology is never purely theoretical. Knowing who God is directly changes how Christians view one another and resolve personal conflicts within the household of faith.
