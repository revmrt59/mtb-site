GRACE - (cháris, Strong's G5485)

Summary:

The word refers to unmerited favor, goodwill, loving generosity, or divine assistance given freely to someone. In Philemon 1:3, Paul invokes God's undeserved favor upon Philemon and his household as the vital foundation for Christian fellowship and the forgiving spirit he will soon ask Philemon to display.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Paul wrote his letter to Philemon while imprisoned, addressing a personal and delicate situation. Philemon was a Christian leader in Colosse whose slave, Onesimus, had run away and later met Paul. After coming to faith in Jesus, Onesimus was being sent back to his master.

In ancient Greek letter-writing, standard openings usually featured a formal word for "greetings." Early Christian writers adapted this convention, replacing the standard greeting with charis, meaning "grace," and joining it with the Jewish blessing of "peace." In Philemon 1:3, Paul opens his letter by pronouncing this double blessing from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ upon Philemon, his family, and the church meeting in his home.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Charis fundamentally refers to favor, kindness, or generosity shown freely without obligation or expectation of return. It describes goodwill that originates in the heart of the giver rather than in the merit of the receiver.

In human relationships, the word can describe personal charm, winsomeness, acts of favor, or expressions of thanks. When used of God, it refers to His unmerited favor toward sinners, His generous granting of salvation, and His ongoing spiritual strength given to believers. In Philemon 1:3, the word is a feminine noun in the nominative case, functioning as the subject of Paul's prayerful blessing, identifying divine favor as the essential gift desired for the recipients.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, charis carries several distinct shades of meaning depending on the passage:

  • Free Salvation: It frequently describes God's saving kindness, by which He forgives sinners and grants them eternal life purely through Christ, completely apart from human merit.

  • Sustaining Power: It identifies the daily divine help and spiritual capacity God supplies to believers so they can endure trials and live faithfully.

  • Practical Generosity: It can refer to tangible acts of love or financial relief collected by the church for those in need.

  • Expressions of Gratitude: It is sometimes rendered as "thanks" or "thankfulness" in response to kindness received.

The common thread throughout all these uses is openhanded generosity that empowers and blesses the recipient.

4. Theological Implications

The apostolic greeting "Grace to you and peace" reflects a precise theological order: grace always precedes peace. Human beings cannot experience true peace with God or with one another until they have first received God's unmerited favor.

Furthermore, Paul links the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ as the equal source of this grace. Divine favor is not earned through human effort; it flows continually from the Godhead to the church. Because believers live under the umbrella of divine grace, their relationships with one another are meant to be shaped by that same generous spirit.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:3, Paul's choice to speak of grace is deeply relevant to the request he is about to make. Paul is writing to ask Philemon to forgive Onesimus—a runaway slave who had wronged him—and to receive him back not as property, but as a brother in Christ.

Human nature and Roman law demanded punishment and restitution. By opening the letter with a prayer for God's grace to rest upon Philemon, Paul reminds him of the abundant, undeserved mercy he himself has received from God. Philemon is asked to extend grace to Onesimus because he continually lives in the atmosphere of divine grace. The opening greeting sets the moral and spiritual foundation for the entire letter.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 1:30 – "Then the angel said to her, 'Do not be afraid, Mary, for you have found favor with God.'"

  • Romans 5:20 – "But where sin abounded, grace abounded much more."

  • 2 Corinthians 8:4 – "imploring us with much urgency that we would receive the gift and the fellowship of the ministering to the saints."

  • Ephesians 2:5 – "even when we were dead in trespasses, made us alive together with Christ (by grace you have been saved)."

7. Other Relevant Insights

By pairing "grace" (a transformed Greek greeting) with "peace" (the traditional Hebrew greeting), Paul unites believers from different cultural backgrounds into one body.

Additionally, invoking divine grace highlights that forgiving others requires more than human willpower. True Christian reconciliation depends on the ongoing work of God's grace in the heart, supplying the strength needed to let go of offenses and restore broken relationships.
