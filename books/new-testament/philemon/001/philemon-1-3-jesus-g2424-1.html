JESUS - (Iēsoûs, Strong's G2424)

Summary:

The Greek name Iēsous is the New Testament equivalent of the Hebrew name Joshua, which means "Yahweh saves" or "Yahweh is salvation." It functions as the human personal name of the incarnate Son of God, Jesus of Nazareth. In Philemon 1:3, the name appears within Paul's opening apostolic greeting: "Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ." Here, the target word identifies Jesus as the joint source alongside God the Father from whom divine grace and peace flow to the church, establishing His supreme divine authority over the reconciliation Paul will request in the letter.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Iēsous is the Hellenized form of the Late Biblical Hebrew name Yēšûaʿ (or the earlier Yĕhôšûaʿ), which translated into English as "Joshua." In the Greco-Roman world of the first century, Iēsous was a relatively common Jewish masculine name borne by several figures in history.

In the New Testament, however, the name carries distinct historical and redemptive significance because it was assigned directly by divine revelation prior to the Messiah's birth. The angel instructed Joseph to name Mary's son Jesus specifically because He would save His people from their sins. While the historical name itself was used by other individuals in antiquity, its theological weight in the Epistles rests entirely on the identity and work of Jesus of Nazareth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Iēsous is a proper noun that identifies the person of Jesus Christ. Etymologically, the underlying Hebrew name combines the divine name YHWH with a root meaning "to deliver" or "to save," yielding the meaning "Yahweh saves" or "Yahweh is salvation."

As a lexical term in the Greek New Testament, it serves as:

  • The proper personal name of Jesus of Nazareth during His earthly life and post-resurrection glory.

  • A formal component of His apostolic titles, frequently combined with Kurios ("Lord") and Christos ("Christ" or "Anointed One").

In Philemon 1:3, the name specifies the historical, resurrected person who reigns as Lord alongside God the Father.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, the usage of Iēsous shifts intentionally between narrative and epistolary settings:

  • In the Gospels: The name is used as Jesus' standard personal name, highlighting His genuine humanity, earthly historical ministry, and human interactions.

  • In the Epistles: Apostles rarely use the bare name Iēsous without an accompanying title unless they are explicitly highlighting His earthly humbleness or His historical suffering. More commonly, the Epistles combine Iēsous with titles of authority, such as "the Lord Jesus" or "Lord Jesus Christ."

In Paul's letters, combining "Lord" (Kurios), "Jesus" (Iēsous), and "Christ" (Christos) summarizes the whole truth about Him: He is divine Lord, human Savior, and promised Messiah.

4. Theological Implications

In the opening greetings of Paul's letters, the inclusion of Jesus alongside God the Father as the source of grace and peace carries deep theological truth. In Jewish thought, grace (charis) and peace (eirēnē) in their ultimate spiritual sense are gifts that belong to God alone. By naming the Lord Jesus Christ co-equally with God our Father as the giver of these divine blessings, Paul demonstrates the deity of Jesus.

Furthermore, because Iēsous signifies salvation, invoking His name reminds the reader that grace and peace are not abstract principles. They are gifts secured through the saving life, death, and resurrection of the historical person, Jesus.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:3, Paul writes: "Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ." This greeting serves a direct function in the letter to Philemon.

Paul is writing to ask Philemon, a wealthy Christian slave owner, to forgive and welcome back Onesimus, a runaway slave who had become a Christian. Before Paul makes his delicate request, he roots the entire household in the spiritual reality of grace and peace derived from the Lord Jesus Christ.

Because Philemon himself has received unmerited favor (grace) and reconciliation (peace) from the Lord Jesus, he is morally and spiritually prepared to grant grace and peace to Onesimus. The presence of Jesus' name at the start of the letter establishes the supreme authority under which all Christian relationships and forgiveness must operate.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The target occurrence in Philemon 1:3 carries genitive masculine singular grammar, which functions syntactically to show the origin or source of the greeting's blessings. Grace and peace are presented as coming directly from two named sources joined by a single preposition: "from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."

Grammatically, this tight coordination places Jesus on the exact same functional level as God the Father regarding the distribution of divine favor and spiritual wholeness to the church.
