LORD - (kýrios, Strong's G2962)

Summary:

The Greek word kurios means one who possesses supreme authority, ownership, or control. It can refer to a master of a household, an owner of property, an earthly ruler, or God Himself. When used of Jesus Christ, it identifies Him as the sovereign Lord and Master over all creation and the church. In Philemon 1:3, kurios identifies Jesus Christ alongside God the Father as the direct source of divine grace and peace for believers, establishing Christ's ultimate authority over all earthly masters and servants.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greek-speaking world, kurios described someone who held legal ownership, authority, or supreme power over people or property. It was used for heads of households, slave owners, civic leaders, and kings. When Jewish scholars translated the Old Testament into Greek (the Septuagint), they regularly used kurios to translate the divine name of God (YHWH). This established kurios as the standard term for the Lord God of Israel.

By the time the New Testament was written, the term carried both everyday secular meaning (referring to masters or bosses) and high theological weight (referring to God). When early Christians called Jesus kurios, they were using a title that expressed His absolute authority, divine lordship, and right to command human lives.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word kurios can refer to:

  • An owner or possessor of property.

  • A master or head of a household who exercises authority over servants or dependents.

  • An earthly ruler, king, or emperor.

  • A title of respect, similar to "sir" or "master."

  • God the Creator, the supreme ruler of the universe.

  • Jesus Christ, as the exalted divine ruler and owner of believers.

The word fundamentally signifies rightful control and authority. It identifies someone who has the final say and to whom obedience or honor is due.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, kurios operates across several levels of meaning:

  • Human authority: It describes earthly masters who own slaves or manage households.

  • Respectful address: It functions as a polite title given to someone of higher status.

  • Divine authority: It translates Old Testament passages about God the Father.

  • Christological title: It is repeatedly assigned to Jesus after His resurrection, proclaiming His exalted authority over heaven and earth.

When the New Testament joins "God our Father" and "the Lord Jesus Christ" together, it places Jesus on the same divine level of authority and honor as God the Father.

4. Theological Implications

The assignment of the title kurios to Jesus Christ is central to Christian faith. To confess Jesus as Lord means recognizing that He is not merely a teacher or prophet, but the sovereign ruler over all creation. Because He died and rose again, He holds absolute rights of ownership over those He redeemed.

Furthermore, when New Testament writers routinely name Jesus as the source of grace, peace, and salvation alongside God the Father, they affirm the full deity of Christ. Spiritual blessings flow equally from the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:3, Paul writes: "Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."

Grammatically, the word is in the genitive case ("of the Lord Jesus Christ"), working with the preposition "from" to show origin. Grace and peace originate from both God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ together.

This greeting carries profound contextual weight in the letter to Philemon. Philemon was a Christian who owned slaves, making him an earthly master (kurios) in his own household. His runaway slave, Onesimus, had become a Christian through Paul's ministry. By invoking "the Lord Jesus Christ" at the very beginning of the letter, Paul establishes a critical framework: Philemon may be an earthly master, but he himself answers to a higher Master—the Lord Jesus. Any decision Philemon makes regarding Onesimus must yield to the supreme lordship of Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 25:24 – "Then he who had received the one talent came and said, 'Lord, I knew you to be a hard man...'"

  • John 20:28 – "And Thomas answered and said to Him, 'My Lord and my God!'"

  • Ephesians 6:9 – "And you, masters, do the same things to them, giving up threatening, knowing that your own Master also is in heaven, and there is no partiality with Him."

  • Philemon 1:16 – "no longer as a slave but more than a slave—a beloved brother, especially to me but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Comparing Philemon 1:3 with Philemon 1:16 highlights Paul's purposeful use of this word. In verse 3, Jesus is identified as the supreme "Lord." Later, in verse 16, Paul urges Philemon to receive his former slave Onesimus as a brother "in the Lord." Because both the human master (Philemon) and the former slave (Onesimus) belong to the same heavenly Lord, their earthly relationship is transformed by their common submission to Jesus.
