PEACE - (eirḗnē, Strong's G1515)

Summary:

The word eirēnē refers to peace, order, harmony, and complete well-being. In ancient Greek culture, it described the absence of war or civil strife, while in biblical usage, it reflects the deep Hebrew concept of shalom, which includes spiritual wholeness and right relationship with God. In Philemon 1:3, Paul pronounces this peace as a divine blessing from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ. By speaking peace over Philemon and his household, Paul lays a foundation of divine harmony that sets the stage for personal reconciliation between Philemon and his runaway servant, Onesimus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In classical Greek, eirēnē primarily described a state of tranquility, order, or the cessation of war between nations. It stood in contrast to turmoil and conflict, representing a calm and stable society.

When the Hebrew Scriptures were translated into Greek, eirēnē was chosen to translate the Hebrew word shalom. This expanded the word's meaning beyond political quietness. In biblical thought, peace is not merely the absence of trouble, but the presence of total well-being, health, righteousness, and spiritual restoration.

In the New Testament, apostolic letters regularly begin with a greeting of peace. By pairing eirēnē with "grace," early Christian writers transformed a traditional greeting into a profound declaration of God's redemptive work through Jesus Christ.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word eirēnē can refer to:

  • Freedom from warfare, civil unrest, or external disturbance.

  • Harmony and restored relationship between estranged individuals or groups.

  • Inner calm and stillness of heart granted through trust in God.

  • The objective state of reconciliation between God and humanity established through Jesus Christ.

In Philemon 1:3, the word means the divine gift of spiritual harmony and wholeness. It is not an abstract human wish, but a reality given directly by God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ to the church gathering in Philemon's home.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses eirēnē across several distinct contexts:

  • Spiritual Reconciliation: The word describes the outcome of justification. Believers have peace with God because Christ satisfied the debt of sin.

  • Inner Quietness: The word describes a calm heart that relies on God during hardship, protecting the mind against worry.

  • Communal Harmony: The word refers to unity within the church. Believers are called to walk together in peace, overcoming personal disputes, social divisions, and grievances.

  • Apostolic Greetings: The word appears as an authoritative pronounceable blessing upon Christian congregations, wishing them the practical experience of God's favor and order.

Across these uses, peace is always rooted in God's character and action rather than human effort.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of eirēnē in apostolic greetings carries clear theological weight:

  • God is the Source: Peace flows directly from God the Father and Jesus Christ. True peace cannot be manufactured by human diplomacy or psychological discipline apart from God.

  • Grace Precedes Peace: In every Pauline greeting, grace always comes before peace. God's unmerited favor is the root, and spiritual peace is the fruit.

  • Divine Peace Ground Human Relationships: Because believers have received peace from God, they are called to reflect that peace in their horizontal relationships.

In the letter to Philemon, this theological truth carries immediate practical weight. Human conflict must yield to the peace that God has already established in Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Philemon is a short, personal letter written by Paul regarding Onesimus, a bondservant who had fled from his master Philemon. After running away, Onesimus met Paul, trusted in Christ, and served Paul in prison. Paul sent Onesimus back to Philemon, asking Philemon to receive him no longer merely as a slave, but as a beloved brother in the Lord.

Before making this delicate request, Paul opens his letter in verse 3 with the greeting: "Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."

This greeting is not filler. By pronouncing God's peace upon Philemon, his family, and the church meeting in his home, Paul establishes the atmosphere for the entire letter. Philemon had been legally and personally wronged by Onesimus. Human nature would demand retribution or resentment. But Paul reminds Philemon that he lives under the realm of divine grace and peace. Because Philemon has received peace from God, he is equipped to extend peace, forgiveness, and brotherhood to Onesimus.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 14:27 — "Peace I leave with you, My peace I give to you; not as the world gives do I give to you."

  • Romans 1:7 — "To all who are in Rome, beloved of God, called to be saints: Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."

  • Ephesians 4:3 — "endeavoring to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace."

  • Philippians 4:7 — "and the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus."

  • 2 Thessalonians 3:16 — "Now may the Lord of peace Himself give you peace always in every way."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The opening greeting pairs "grace" (a word familiar in Greek culture) with "peace" (a word central to Jewish life). Christ unites both traditions into a single blessing for the church.

  • In Philemon 1:3, "peace" functions grammatically as part of the main greeting delivered to the entire household. It applies equally to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, and the local assembly meeting in their house.

  • The source of peace is explicitly shared between "God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ," showing that Christ acts with equal divine authority alongside the Father in granting spiritual peace.
