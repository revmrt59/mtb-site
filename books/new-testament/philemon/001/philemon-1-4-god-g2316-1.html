GOD - (theós, Strong's G2316)

Summary:

The Greek word theos means a god, divine being, or the supreme God. In the New Testament, it refers to the one living and true God, the Creator and Redeemer of mankind. In Philemon 1:4, the word functions as the direct recipient of Paul's worship, as Paul expresses personal gratitude to "my God" for the faith and love shown by Philemon.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the Greco-Roman world of the first century, the word theos was widely used to describe the many deities of pagan pantheons. Gentile culture applied the title to mythical idols, localized spirits, and Roman emperors who claimed divine honors.

When the Hebrew Old Testament was translated into Greek (the Septuagint), Jewish translators chose theos to render the Hebrew names for God, particularly Elohim. New Testament writers continued this practice, reclaiming the word from pagan culture to refer exclusively to the sovereign Creator of heaven and earth. In Paul's opening greeting to Philemon, he uses this word within a personal expression of praise, directing his prayers to the true God who redeemed him.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of theos is deity or God. While ancient Greek literature used the word for any deity, the New Testament uses it almost always for the supreme, personal God revealed in Jewish and Christian Scripture.

In Philemon 1:4, the word appears in the dative singular masculine form as the personal object of Paul's thanksgiving ("I thank my God"). Paul does not treat God as a distant concept or philosophical abstraction, but as the living Person to whom gratitude and prayer are offered.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, theos carries several distinct nuances depending on context:

  • God the Father: Most often, theos refers specifically to God the Father, distinguished alongside Jesus Christ and the Holy Spirit.

  • The One True Deity: The term highlights God's absolute oneness, authority, and distinction from false idols.

  • Jesus Christ: In select key passages, the New Testament explicitly applies theos to Jesus Christ, affirming His full deity.

  • Personal Relationship: When combined with personal pronouns (such as "my God"), the word highlights an active, relational bond between the worshiper and the Lord.

In Philemon 1:4, Paul writes "my God" to highlight his personal devotion and intimate fellowship with the Lord who rules over his life and ministry.

4. Theological Implications

The use of theos in Philemon 1:4 highlights the personal nature of Christian prayer. Paul does not direct his gratitude to fate, chance, or human effort. He addresses the God who knows believers individually and works graciously in their lives.

Furthermore, expressing thanks to God for Philemon's spiritual health shows that all Christian virtues—such as faith, love, and hospitality—have their ultimate source in God. When believers love one another, God receives the praise because He produces that spiritual fruit within them.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The Letter to Philemon is a short, personal epistle written while Paul was imprisoned. Before presenting his delicate request regarding Onesimus, the runaway bondservant, Paul establishes a warm spiritual foundation grounded in shared faith.

When Paul states, "I thank my God, making mention of you always in my prayers," he sets the tone for the entire letter. By directing his gratitude to God for Philemon's life, Paul reminds Philemon of the grace they both received from the same Lord. This reminder of God's work in Philemon's heart prepares Philemon to respond to Paul's appeal with Christlike forgiveness and love.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Corinthians 14:18 — "I thank my God I speak with tongues more than you all;"

  • John 6:27 — "Do not labor for the food which perishes, but for the food which endures to everlasting life, which the Son of Man will give you, because God the Father has set His seal on Him."

  • 1 Timothy 2:5 — "For there is one God and one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus,"

  • Colossians 3:3 — "For you died, and your life is hidden with Christ in God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul regularly opens his letters by giving thanks to "my God" for the believers he serves. This pattern demonstrates that prayer and thanksgiving were central to apostolic leadership. Rather than taking credit for the spiritual growth of early churches, Paul consistently redirected all gratitude toward God, acknowledging Him as the author of every good work.
