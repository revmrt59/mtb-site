MAKING - (poiéō, Strong's G4160)

Summary:

The Greek verb poieō basic meaning is to do, make, bring about, perform, or accomplish. It is one of the most common action verbs in the New Testament, used for everything from physical creation and practical tasks to fulfilling moral duties and carrying out specific actions. When combined with a noun indicating memory or remembrance, the word forms an idiom meaning "to make mention" or "to bring to mind." In Philemon 1:4, Paul uses the word to describe his regular practice of bringing Philemon before God by name during his personal times of prayer.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb poieō is a foundational action word in Greek. It describes the act of bringing something into existence or carrying out a specific task, function, or duty. In ancient Greek literature, the word could refer to crafting an object, composing a poem, producing fruit, performing an action, or causing an outcome to occur.

In the New Testament, the word appears in a wide variety of everyday, legal, religious, and relational contexts. Beyond its literal meaning of making or doing something physical, Greek writers frequently paired poieō with abstract nouns to form standard phrases that express specific actions. In personal letters, pairing poieō with the word for remembrance created a polite and warm phrase that translated roughly to "making mention" or "bringing to mind." When Paul wrote his prison letter to Philemon, he drew on this well-established expression to report his regular prayer habits.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to making, forming, producing, doing, performing, or accomplishing something. Its exact nuance depends on the object or action connected to it.

When applied to physical items or creation, the word means to fashion, build, or bring into being. When applied to behaviors, duties, or moral choices, it means to perform, practice, or carry out an action. When paired with a noun expressing memory or speech, it functions as part of an idiom that means to mention someone, recall them to mind, or speak of them in a specific setting. In Philemon 1:4, the word carries this idiomatic sense: Paul describes himself as actively making mention of Philemon in his regular prayers.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses poieō in several distinct ways across different settings:

First, it describes God’s creative and redeeming work. God makes the heavens and earth, brings about salvation, and performs marvelous deeds.

Second, it describes human action and behavior. Jesus often used the word to distinguish between those who merely hear His words and those who do them. In moral contexts, doing good or doing evil highlights the outward execution of an internal belief or desire.

Third, it forms standard relational idioms in New Testament letters. When Paul tells a church or an individual that he is "making mention" of them in prayer, he uses poieō to describe the deliberate act of naming them and bringing their circumstances before God.

4. Theological Implications

The use of poieō in prayer reports shows that Christian intercession is an intentional, practical act. Biblical prayer involves more than vague, warm feelings toward brothers and sisters in Christ. It involves the concrete action of naming specific people and their needs before the Lord.

Paul’s letter to Philemon demonstrates how intercessory prayer strengthens Christian fellowship. Before Paul brings a challenging request to Philemon concerning his runaway bondservant Onesimus, Paul reminds Philemon that he actively brings him before God’s throne in thanksgiving and intercession. This establishes a foundation of shared faith, practical love, and mutual accountability in Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:4, Paul writes, "I thank my God, making mention of you always in my prayers." The word "making" describes the action that accompanies Paul's giving of thanks. As Paul prays, he actively names Philemon and calls to mind Philemon's faith and love toward the Lord Jesus and all the saints.

This opening thanksgiving sets an affectionate and encouraging tone for the letter. Paul was a prisoner, yet his personal burdens did not prevent him from actively remembering fellow believers. By letting Philemon know that he was regularly making mention of him in prayer, Paul affirmed Philemon's vital place in the ministry and prepared his heart to receive the difficult pastoral request that would follow.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding how poieō functions in ancient letter writing helps illuminate Paul's pastoral heart. In the ancient Greco-Roman world, standard letters often included a generic wish for the recipient's health or a brief mention to the gods. Paul transformed this conventional greeting into a deeply Christian act of intercession. Instead of offering a formal or empty courtesy, Paul filled the phrase "making mention" with genuine spiritual substance, pointing directly to his private intercession before the true God.
