ALL - (pâs, Strong's G3956)

Summary:

The word pas basic meaning is "all," "every," or "the whole." Depending on how it is used, it can refer to every individual item in a group, the entirety of a single object, or all categories of a given thing. In Philemon 1:5, the word describes the complete scope of Philemon's love for God's people. Paul highlights that Philemon does not restrict his love to a few favored companions, but extends it toward "all the saints." This expansive scope prepares the way for Paul's request that Philemon welcome the restored runaway slave, Onesimus, as a beloved brother in Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Apostle Paul wrote the letter to Philemon while imprisoned, likely in Rome. Philemon was a prosperous leader in the church at Colosse, a man known for his hospitality and leadership. Onesimus, a slave belonging to Philemon, had fled from his master and eventually encountered Paul. Through Paul's ministry, Onesimus came to faith in Jesus Christ.

Before asking Philemon to receive Onesimus back not merely as a servant but as a Christian brother, Paul begins his letter with a warm prayer of thanksgiving. He commends Philemon for the news he continually receives about Philemon's active faith and love. By noting that Philemon's love reaches "all the saints," Paul establishes the shared, inclusive nature of the Christian community before making a specific pastoral appeal.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Pas is a foundational Greek adjective that denotes totality or completeness. It answers questions about scope and quantity. In Scripture, the word can function in several distinct ways:

  • Individual sense ("every"): Distributing an action or description to every single member of a group considered individually.

  • Collective sense ("all"): Describing the entire group as a whole body or totality.

  • Comprehensive sense ("whole"): Referring to the entire structure or full extent of a singular thing.

In Philemon 1:5, the word appears in a plural form describing a group of people. Here it means "all" in the collective sense—encompassing every believer without exception or partiality.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, pas is used to define boundaries—or the lack of boundaries—in God's redemptive work and Christian living. It frequently highlights the universal reach of the gospel, the unconditional nature of divine truth, and the unified identity of the church.

When applied to people within the church, pas guards against favoritism. Human affection naturally gravitates toward friends, family, or those of similar social status. The addition of pas to descriptions of love or service emphasizes that Christian fellowship transcends social class, economic status, cultural background, and past history.

4. Theological Implications

The use of pas in relation to "the saints" points to important biblical truths about the church:

  • Undivided Fellowship: God does not divide His family into premier and second-class members. Because Christ gave Himself for the entire body, believers are called to love the entire body.

  • Love as the Mark of Faith: In Philemon 1:5, faith toward the Lord Jesus and love toward all the saints are closely connected. Genuine faith in Christ inevitably expresses itself in active love for all whom Christ has redeemed.

  • Social Reconciliation: In the ancient Roman world, deep divisions existed between free citizens and enslaved people. By affirming love for all the saints, Scripture establishes that spiritual brotherhood in Christ reshapes social relationships.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:5, Paul writes that he hears of Philemon's "love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints."

Grammatically, pas describes the plural object "saints." The word indicates that Philemon's reputation for generosity and care was not limited to a select circle of friends or house-church leaders in Colosse. His home and heart were open to any believer who came his way.

This observation is vital to Paul's argument. Onesimus was returning to Philemon as a newly baptized believer—a saint. If Philemon's love truly extended to all the saints, it must now extend to Onesimus as well. Paul gently uses Philemon's own established character to show that receiving Onesimus with forgiveness and love was the natural fulfillment of the faith Philemon already practiced.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s phrasing reveals pastoral wisdom. Rather than commanding Philemon directly from a position of apostolic authority, Paul praises the genuine work God has already done in Philemon's heart. By highlighting that Philemon already loves "all the saints," Paul places Onesimus under the umbrella of Philemon's existing love. The word pas serves as a quiet reminder that Christian love cannot draw arbitrary boundaries around who deserves kindness and brotherhood.
