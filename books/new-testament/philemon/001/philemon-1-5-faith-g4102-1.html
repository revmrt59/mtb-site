FAITH - (pístis, Strong's G4102)

Summary:

The Greek word pistis means trust, reliance, belief, or faithfulness. It describes an active, personal dependence on God and a reliable commitment in personal relationships. In Philemon 1:5, pistis refers to Philemon's personal trust in the Lord Jesus and his active fidelity toward fellow Christians. Paul highlights Philemon's genuine faith as the foundational root of his godly character, preparing Philemon to put that faith into action by forgiving and welcoming back his runaway servant, Onesimus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek culture, pistis carried meanings of trust, confidence, trustworthiness, and mutual reliability in social, civic, and commercial relationships. It described the bond of confidence between friends, citizens, or business partners. When early Jewish and Christian writers translated the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, pistis was chosen to translate the Hebrew concept of 'emunah, which highlights steadfast fidelity, stability, and enduring trust in God.

In the New Testament, pistis takes on a distinct character. Rather than describing a passive mental opinion, it describes a personal reliance on God and His promises. It involves the total heart response of a person receiving Jesus Christ as Lord and Savior. At the same time, pistis keeps its historical sense of faithfulness, showing that real trust in Christ naturally expresses itself through loyal love and integrity toward others.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Pistis can refer to:

  • Trust or reliance placed in a person or message, especially active faith in God or Jesus Christ.

  • Faithfulness, loyalty, or trustworthiness in personal conduct and relationships.

  • The content of what is believed, referring to the body of Christian truth or doctrine.

In Philemon 1:5, the word identifies Philemon's active personal trust in Christ and his enduring fidelity toward God's people. The noun is feminine in gender, singular in number, and functions as the direct object of Paul's hearing ("hearing of your love and faith"). This shows that Philemon's faith was not merely a private, invisible conviction, but an observable quality of his life that others noticed and talked about.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, pistis appears in several related ways. First, it describes saving faith—the personal trust by which a person relies on the sacrificial death and resurrection of Jesus Christ for forgiveness and eternal life. Second, it describes daily walking faith—the continued reliance on God's power and promises through trials and daily decisions. Third, it describes personal faithfulness—the reliable character produced by the Holy Spirit in a believer's conduct toward others.

These senses are closely joined together in Scripture. Real reliance on Christ produces active fidelity toward people. True biblical faith is never isolated from action; it trusts God's grace and responds by living faithfully within the church community.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, pistis shows that salvation and daily Christian living rest entirely on active trust in God rather than human achievement. Faith is the open hand that receives God's grace in Christ. Yet because saving faith unites a believer to Jesus, it transforms how that person relates to everyone else.

Philemon 1:5 demonstrates that true faith in Christ cannot remain hidden or inactive. It shapes how a believer views fellow Christians. Trust in Jesus produces love, mercy, and faithfulness toward the household of faith. This theological connection is essential in the letter to Philemon: a person who has received God's mercy through faith is called to extend that same mercy to others.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:5, Paul opens his letter with thanksgiving and prayer for Philemon. He mentions hearing about Philemon's "love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints." Paul pairs faith (pistis) directly with love (agape).

The structure of Paul's sentence connects Philemon's faith and love both to Jesus and to fellow believers. Philemon trusts in the Lord Jesus, and that personal faith produces active love and faithfulness toward all the saints. Paul praises Philemon for this established reputation of faith not merely to pay him a compliment, but to set up the main purpose of the letter.

Onesimus, Philemon's former slave, had run away, met Paul, and become a believer in Christ. Paul was sending Onesimus back with this letter, asking Philemon to receive him no longer as a slave, but as a beloved brother. By commending Philemon's pistis in verse 5, Paul lays the groundwork for his request in the rest of the letter. Paul essentially says: because your faith in Christ is real and known by all, put that faith into practice now by forgiving Onesimus and welcoming him as a brother.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 10:17 — "So then faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God."

  • Ephesians 3:17 — "that Christ may dwell in your hearts through faith; that you, being rooted and grounded in love..."

  • 2 Thessalonians 1:3 — "We are bound to thank God always for you, brethren, as it is fitting, because your faith grows exceedingly, and the love of every one of you all abounds toward each other..."

  • Hebrews 11:1 — "Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen."

  • James 2:22 — "Do you see that faith was working together with his works, and by works faith was made perfect?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In 2 Thessalonians 1:3, Paul uses a very similar combination of words, thanking God because the Thessalonians' "faith grows exceedingly" and their love abounds toward one another. This common pairing shows that Paul routinely evaluated the health of a local church or believer by looking for faith toward Christ working alongside love for fellow Christians.

In Philemon's case, his house was host to a local church meeting (Philemon 1:2). His active faith created a place of refuge, refreshment, and joy for believers in Colosse. Paul appeals to this proven track record of faith as the basis for asking Philemon to do something difficult: grant full forgiveness and reconciliation to someone who had wronged him.
