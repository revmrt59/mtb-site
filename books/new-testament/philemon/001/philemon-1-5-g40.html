SAINTS - ( hagios , Strong's G40 )

Summary:

The term "saints" in Philemon 1:5 translates the Greek word hagios, denoting those who are set apart, consecrated, and holy to God. Far from describing a special tier of exceptionally spiritual Christians, the New Testament uses this designation for all believers. In the immediate context of Philemon, the apostle Paul uses the word to remind Philemon of his holy identity and fellowship with the entire body of Christ, grounding their shared Christian love and practical ministry in the reality of their common consecration to God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • Rooted in the Old Testament concept of holiness (qodesh), which fundamentally means "separation" or "withdrawal" from common, profane use for exclusive dedication to God.

  • In ancient Greek culture, the cognate adjective described things or places dedicated to the gods, removed from ordinary secular traffic.

  • Applied to people in the biblical context, the term shifts from mere ritual cleanliness to a profound moral and spiritual positioning: individuals claimed by God, belonging entirely to Him, and stamped with His character.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Hagios functions primarily as an adjective meaning sacred, pure, morally blameless, or consecrated.

  • When used substantively as a plural noun ("saints" or "holy ones"), it designates the people of God.

  • It carries a dual dimension: positional holiness (set apart legally and spiritually by God's grace at conversion) and practical holiness (living a life that reflects that distinct status).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The New Testament uniformly democratizes the term; it is never reserved for post-mortem canonized individuals or an elite spiritual class.

  • Believers are pictured as temple vessels, ritually and spiritually washed, and dedicated exclusively to the Master’s use.

  • The plural form emphasizes corporate identity—saints do not exist in isolation but belong to a holy community.

4. Theological Implications

  • Holiness is initiated by God; people become "saints" not by achieving moral perfection through self-effort, but by being called and claimed by God through Christ.

  • The identity of being a saint precedes and empowers the behavior of living like one. Action flows directly from status.

  • Because all believers share this same holy standing before God, earthly distinctions of class, race, or status are leveled within the body of Christ, creating a deep spiritual solidarity.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Philemon 1:5, Paul hears of Philemon's "love and the faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints."

  • Here, hagios highlights the objects of Philemon's Christian affection and service: his fellow believers in the local church.

  • By calling them "saints," Paul subtly reinforces that the people Philemon is called to love—and toward whom he must now extend forgiveness and reconciliation regarding Onesimus—belong to God. Mistreating or ignoring a fellow saint is a failure in duty toward one consecrated to the Lord Himself.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 1:7 - "To all who are in Rome, beloved of God, called to be saints: Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."

  • 1 Corinthians 1:2 - "To the church of God which is at Corinth, to those who are sanctified in Christ Jesus, called to be saints, with all who in every place call on the name of Jesus Christ our Lord, both theirs and ours:"

  • Ephesians 1:1 - "Paul, an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God, To the saints who are in Ephesus, and faithful in Christ Jesus:"

  • Philippians 1:1 - "Paul and Timothy, bondservants of Jesus Christ, To all the saints in Christ Jesus who are in Philippi, with the bishops and deacons:"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The use of hagios as a noun in the plural emphasizes that holiness is communal; individual believers are incorporated into a holy nation and a royal priesthood.

  • The affectionate warmth with which Paul addresses the "saints" underscores that biblical holiness is marked by deep relational love, not cold asceticism or aloof self-righteousness.
