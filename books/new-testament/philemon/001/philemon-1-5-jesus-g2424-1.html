JESUS - (Iēsoûs, Strong's G2424)

Summary:

The name Iēsous is the Greek form of the Hebrew name Yēšûaʿ (Joshua), meaning "the LORD saves" or "Yahweh is salvation." It is the personal name given to the Son of God at His birth, identifying Him as the historical savior of His people. In Philemon 1:5, Paul uses this name alongside the title "Lord" to specify the personal object of Philemon's faith and devotion, grounding Philemon's Christian life and love for fellow believers in his direct relationship with Jesus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the first-century Greco-Roman world and among Jewish communities, personal names were chosen with great intentionality and carried deep historical and cultural meaning. Iēsous was a common male name among Jewish families during the Second Temple period, borne by several notable figures in Jewish history.

When Paul wrote his personal letter to Philemon, a leader in the Colossian church, he opened with a warm expression of thanksgiving. Paul commended Philemon for the spiritual character he displayed within the Christian community. In doing so, Paul specifically named the target of Philemon's trust: the Lord Jesus. Rather than referring to God in vague or abstract terms, Paul anchored Philemon's faith and ministry in the historical, risen Savior who had transformed Philemon's life.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Iēsous is a proper masculine personal name. It is the Greek transliteration of the Hebrew name Yēšûaʿ (a shortened form of Yehōšūaʿ, or Joshua). The established etymological meaning of the name is "Yahweh is salvation" or "the LORD saves."

In terms of origin, the name reflects a rich Old Testament heritage, drawing directly from the Hebrew language and Jewish naming traditions before being adapted into Hellenistic Greek. In the New Testament, this name identifies the historical person born in Bethlehem to Mary, who lived, died, rose again, and reigns as the incarnate Son of God.

Etymologically, the name highlights the divine mission of deliverance. However, when biblical authors use the name in personal greetings and letters, their focus is on identifying the specific person of Jesus Himself—the living Savior in whom believers place their trust—rather than continually making a wordplay on the name's linguistic origin. In Philemon 1:5, the name identifies the personal object of Philemon's faith.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, Iēsous appears in various settings. In the four Gospels, the name frequently stands on its own to describe the earthly ministry, teaching, miracles, and suffering of Jesus of Nazareth.

In the Epistles, the New Testament writers regularly combine the personal name Iēsous with divine titles. Most frequently, it appears as "Jesus Christ" or "the Lord Jesus." This combination serves an important function:

  • The title "Lord" (Kyrios) acknowledges His divine sovereignty, authority, and exaltation.

  • The personal name "Jesus" (Iēsous) retains the personal, historical reality of His incarnation and saving work on the cross.

By speaking of "the Lord Jesus," the apostles reminded early Christians that the supreme Ruler of the universe is the same person who took on human flesh, walked among humanity, and died for their sins.

4. Theological Implications

The use of the personal name Iēsous in apostolic writings reinforces several vital truths:

  • Faith is personal: Christian faith is not merely adherence to a moral system or philosophy. It is trust directed toward a real, historical person who lives today.

  • Salvation is anchored in history: By identifying Jesus by name, Scripture guards against mythical or purely speculative ideas about God. God revealed Himself decisively in a real human life.

  • Devotion shapes human relationships: In the New Testament, devotion to Jesus directly impacts how believers treat one another. Faith in the Savior naturally flows into practical love for the people He redeemed.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:5, Paul writes that he continually gives thanks to God, "hearing of your love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints."

Grammatically, "Jesus" functions as the direct object of Philemon's faith and love, paired directly with the title "Lord." Paul sets up a clear relationship between Philemon's spiritual vertical allegiance and his horizontal relationships:

1. Philemon exercises trust and devotion toward the Lord Jesus.

2. That same devotion generates active love toward "all the saints"—the fellow members of the body of Christ.

This contextual link is crucial for the rest of Paul's letter. Paul is preparing to ask Philemon to forgive and welcome back Onesimus, a runaway slave who had recently become a Christian. By highlighting Philemon's existing faith in the Lord Jesus, Paul reminds Philemon that his allegiance to Jesus must govern how he treats a reconciled brother in Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:1 — "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham."

  • Acts 17:3 — "explaining and demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead, and saying, 'This Jesus whom I preach to you is the Christ.'"

  • 2 Corinthians 4:14 — "knowing that He who raised up the Lord Jesus will also raise us up with Jesus, and will present us with you."

  • Hebrews 6:20 — "where the forerunner has entered for us, even Jesus, having become High Priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The combination "the Lord Jesus" in Philemon 1:5 brings together divine authority and human accessibility. As "Lord," Jesus possesses absolute authority over Philemon's household, decisions, and heart. As "Jesus," He is the compassionate Redeemer who gave Himself to save sinners, including Philemon and Onesimus.

This dual focus sets a gentle but firm foundation for Paul's request. Before Paul asks Philemon to show extraordinary mercy to Onesimus, Paul affirms that Philemon already possesses true faith in the Lord Jesus. The grace Philemon received from Jesus becomes the standard and motivation for the grace he is called to extend to others.
