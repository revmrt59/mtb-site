LORD - (kýrios, Strong's G2962)

Summary:

energy The Greek word kyrios means master, owner, ruler, or lord. It refers to someone who holds rightful authority, ownership, or headship over others. In ordinary ancient speech, it could describe a household master or serve as a polite term of respect. Throughout the New Testament, it is used as the supreme title for God and for Jesus Christ.

In Philemon 1:5, Paul refers to "the Lord Jesus." Here, the title kyrios identifies Jesus as the sovereign ruler and supreme Master of the believer's life. By placing Philemon's faith and love in direct relation to "the Lord Jesus," Paul establishes that Christ's authority is the foundation for how Philemon must live and how he must treat fellow believers.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greco-Roman world, a kyrios was a legal owner, master, or head of a house. A kyrios possessed recognized authority over property, household decisions, and servants. The word expressed valid ownership and headship within social and civic structures.

When Jewish scholars translated the Old Testament into Greek (the Septuagint), they regularly chose kyrios to translate both the Hebrew title Adonai ("Lord") and the holy personal name of God (YHWH). Because of this biblical history, the word carried immense divine weight for early Jewish and Christian readers. Calling someone kyrios in a religious setting was not just polite address; it signaled divine sovereignty, authority, and rightful worship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word kyrios fundamentally denotes one who possesses authority or control. Depending on who is being addressed, the word can mean:

  • An owner or master who holds legal authority over servants, land, or property.

  • A respectful title address, similar to "sir" or "master."

  • A ruler or sovereign leader possessing supreme administrative authority.

  • The divine Ruler of all, used specifically for God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.

In Philemon 1:5, the noun appears in the accusative masculine singular form as part of the phrase "the Lord Jesus." Grammatically, it serves as the object toward which Philemon's trust and allegiance are directed, identifying Jesus as his absolute Master.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, kyrios serves several distinct purposes:

  • Civil and Social Usage: The word describes earthly masters who own bondservants or managers who oversee estates.

  • Old Testament Citations: Writers use kyrios when quoting Old Testament Scriptures that refer to God Almighty.

  • The Exalted Title of Jesus: Following His resurrection and ascension, Jesus is confessed as kyrios over all creation.

To confess Jesus as kyrios means acknowledging that He holds complete rights of ownership over the believer. Salvation itself is bound up with confessing that Jesus is Lord, placing one's entire life under His command and care.

4. Theological Implications

The application of kyrios to Jesus Christ is one of the strongest affirmations of His deity in the New Testament. By receiving the title kyrios, Jesus is identified with the God of Israel who rules over heaven and earth.

Confessing Jesus as Lord reframes every earthly obligation. In the early church, declaring "Jesus is Lord" set Christians apart from a culture that demanded ultimate allegiance to political rulers. For the believer, Jesus is the final authority. Earthly positions, legal rights, and personal preferences must all bow to His sovereign will.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:5, Paul opens his letter by thanking God for Philemon, citing the "love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints."

Paul deliberately connects Philemon's inner faith in "the Lord Jesus" with his practical love for "all the saints." This connection is vital to the main message of the letter. Philemon was a wealthy man and an earthly master (kyrios in a human sense) who owned a runaway slave named Onesimus. But Paul reminds Philemon right at the start that Philemon himself has a Master in heaven: the Lord Jesus.

Because Jesus is Philemon's true Lord, Philemon cannot treat Onesimus purely according to Roman law or personal grievance. Philemon's earthly mastership is superseded by the lordship of Christ. True faith in the Lord Jesus must issue in love toward everyone who belongs to Him, including a returning servant who has now become a brother in Christ.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The title kyrios sets the standard for Christian ethics throughout the short letter to Philemon. Paul does not issue harsh apostolic orders to Philemon; instead, he appeals to his Christian character. Reminding Philemon that Jesus is "the Lord" establishes that true obedience flows from a heart submitted to Christ's authority. When believers remember that Jesus is their Lord, they view other people not through the lens of human status, but through their shared relationship to the Master who bought them.
