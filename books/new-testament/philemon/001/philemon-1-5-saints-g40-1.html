SAINTS - (hágios, Strong's G40)

Summary:

The Greek word hagios describes someone or something set apart from common use and dedicated entirely to God. When applied to people, it does not mean an elite group of exceptionally pious Christians, but rather identifies all believers who belong to Jesus Christ as God's set-apart people. In Philemon 1:5, the word refers to the Christian community whom Philemon actively loves, establishing the groundwork for Paul to request that Philemon welcome his runaway servant Onesimus as a fellow member of God's holy family.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The foundational idea of hagios is separation and dedication. In ancient Greek culture, the word was used for temples, sacrifices, or places set apart for religious devotion, separate from everyday secular affairs. In the Greek translation of the Old Testament, the term expressed God's absolute moral purity and unique glory, as well as the items, rituals, and people consecrated for His worship.

In the New Testament, the concept shifts from ceremonial objects and Jewish priestly rituals to describe all believers in Jesus. Through the death and resurrection of Christ, God claims a new people for Himself, removing them from the world's ownership and setting them apart as His personal possession.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can mean "holy," "sacred," or "set apart." When used as an adjective, it describes God, His Spirit, or things dedicated to Him. When used as a plural noun, as in Philemon 1:5, it refers directly to God's people—the "holy ones" or "saints."

The word identifies a believer's status before God rather than an earned spiritual title. Scripture does not limit the title to a small group of dead believers recognized for miraculous deeds or extraordinary moral achievements. Instead, every person reconciled to God through faith in Christ is called a "saint" because God has cleansed them and set them apart for His purposes.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, hagios carries a dual emphasis on position and character:

  • Positional Standing: Believers are saints because God called them and cleansed them by Christ's sacrifice. Their holy status rests entirely on what Christ has done for them.

  • Practical Conduct: Because believers possess a holy status, God commands them to live lives that mirror His holy character. Their everyday conduct is meant to reflect the family to which they now belong.

The word also emphasizes corporate unity. In almost every place where hagios identifies believers in the New Testament, it appears in the plural. It describes a community of people joined together in Christ rather than isolated individuals.

4. Theological Implications

Calling believers "saints" highlights several central biblical truths:

  • Salvation rests on God's initiative: Believers do not make themselves holy; God sets them apart by His grace.

  • Shared identity across social barriers: The title applies equally to every believer regardless of wealth, ethnicity, gender, or social class. Slaves, free citizens, Jews, and Gentiles all share the same identity as saints.

  • Essential connection between faith and community: Love for the Lord Jesus naturally manifests in love toward those who belong to Him. A disciple cannot claim true devotion to the Head while disregarding His body.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:5, Paul commends Philemon because he has heard of Philemon's "love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints." Paul deliberately ties Philemon’s devotion to the Lord with his love for fellow believers.

Philemon’s faith was not merely a private, personal belief; it expressed itself in tangible practical love toward the believers in his local house church and region. Crucially, Paul highlights that Philemon’s love extends to "all" the saints, leaving no room for selective affection or social prejudice.

This word choice lays the foundation for the main request of the letter. Onesimus, Philemon’s runaway servant, had recently come to faith in Christ through Paul's ministry. By reminding Philemon of his reputation for loving "all the saints," Paul gently prepares Philemon to receive Onesimus back not as a rebellious slave deserving punishment, but as a brother in Christ—a fellow saint.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 9:13 – "Lord, I have heard from many about this man, how much harm he has done to Your saints in Jerusalem."

  • 1 Corinthians 14:33 – "For God is not the author of confusion but of peace, as in all the churches of the saints."

  • Ephesians 4:12 – "...for the equipping of the saints for the work of ministry, for the edifying of the body of Christ."

  • Revelation 5:8 – "...golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the saints."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Philemon 1:5, the word functions as the object of Philemon’s active love. This shows that Christian love is not a vague feeling, but a deliberate action directed toward concrete individuals.

Furthermore, loving "all the saints" presents a ongoing spiritual challenge. Fellow believers can sometimes be difficult, flawed, or socially distant from us. Yet because God has designated every believer as a "saint," Christians are called to love one another based on who they are in Christ rather than personal preference or social convenience.
