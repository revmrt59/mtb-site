TOWARD - (eis, Strong's G1519)

Summary:

The Greek word eis is a preposition that indicates direction, goal, or movement toward a target. It is often translated as "into," "to," "toward," or "for." In Philemon 1:5, the word describes how Philemon's active Christian love and faith are directed outward toward all of God's holy people.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In everyday ancient Greek, prepositions were short words used to show how actions, objects, or people related to one another in space, time, or purpose. The preposition eis was one of the most common directional words in the language. It signaled movement into a place, direction toward a goal, or the orientation of an attitude toward a person.

When New Testament writers spoke about spiritual qualities like faith, love, hope, or service, they frequently used prepositions to show that these virtues were not static ideas. Prepositions like eis showed that a believer's faith and love had a clear destination and object.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Eis functions as a preposition indicating direction or target. Depending on the setting, it can be translated into English using several related terms:

  • Movement into a space or condition ("into")

  • Direction toward a person or place ("to" or "toward")

  • Intended outcome, goal, or purpose ("for" or "so that")

  • Relational orientation ("toward" or "in")

Here in Philemon 1:5, the word means "toward." It marks the recipients who receive the outward expression of Philemon's spiritual life.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, eis carries several practical nuances depending on how it connects words in a sentence:

  • Spatial direction: It describes physical movement into a home, town, or boat.

  • Direction of faith: It frequently marks Jesus as the object into whom a believer places trust.

  • Direction of personal care: It shows love, kindness, or generosity directed toward specific individuals or groups.

  • Purpose or result: It introduces the intended goal or outcome of an action.

The key nuance of eis is its focus on direction. Rather than describing an internal feeling that stays hidden, eis highlights where an action or attitude is aimed and where it lands.

4. Theological Implications

While eis is a simple preposition, its usage in Scripture highlights an important truth about Christian life: true spiritual virtues move outward toward specific targets. Faith does not exist in a vacuum; it reaches out toward Jesus Christ. Likewise, Christian love is not merely an internal emotion; it moves toward actual people.

It is important not to load full theological systems into a short preposition. The word eis itself does not prove doctrines like eternal security or sinless perfection. What it does establish is direction and target. Biblical faith and love are relational and targeted, moving out from the believer toward God and neighbor.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:5, Paul opens his letter by thanking God for Philemon. Paul mentions that he regularly hears about Philemon's love and faith, which are directed toward the Lord Jesus and "toward" (eis) all the saints.

This directional focus plays a critical role in the letter's main purpose. Paul is preparing to ask Philemon for a major act of mercy: to welcome back Onesimus, a runaway slave who had become a Christian under Paul's ministry. By highlighting that Philemon's love already extends "toward all the saints," Paul appeals to an established habit of heart and life. Onesimus is now one of those "saints" (God's set-apart people). Therefore, Philemon's love, which already reaches out toward the whole church, has a fresh opportunity to reach out toward Onesimus as well.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 11:25 — "He who believes in Me, though he may die, he shall live." Here the word is translated "in," identifying Jesus as the direct object of saving faith.

  • Romans 5:18 — "Through one man’s righteous act the free gift came to all men..." Here the word is translated "to," showing how the gift of God's grace reaches toward all people.

  • 1 Corinthians 5:5 — "Deliver such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh..." Here the word is translated "for," marking the intended goal or purpose of the disciplinary action.

  • 1 Timothy 3:7 — "Lest he fall into reproach and the snare of the devil." Here the word is translated "into," describing movement into a dangerous condition or trap.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding eis helps explain how Paul structures his encouragement in short personal letters. Before Paul asks Philemon to do something difficult, he reminds Philemon of how God has already worked in his life. Philemon was not a man whose faith was kept to himself. His life was characterized by love flowing outward toward the entire Christian community. When Paul asks him to forgive and receive Onesimus, he is not asking Philemon to start a new practice, but to direct his existing, active love toward one more brother in Christ.
