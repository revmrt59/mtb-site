CHRIST - (Christós, Strong's G5547)

Summary:

The Greek word christos means "Anointed One" and is the direct Greek translation of the Hebrew title Mashiach (Messiah). In the Old Testament, anointing with oil marked individuals—such as kings, priests, and prophets—who were set apart by God for holy service. In the New Testament, the term serves both as an official title for Jesus as God's promised Savior and as a proper name. In Philemon 1:6, the title refers to Jesus as the realm, source, and ultimate target of every spiritual blessing, reminding believers that their faith and good deeds find their true meaning in Him.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, anointing a person's head with oil was a sacred act. God instructed His people to anoint kings, high priests, and prophets to set them apart for special divine assignments. Over centuries of prophetic promise, God promised a supreme King—the Messiah—who would come to deliver His people, establish God's reign, and fulfill all righteousness.

When the Hebrew scriptures were translated into Greek before the time of Jesus, translators chose the Greek word christos to render the Hebrew mashiach. By the time the New Testament was written, christos was used by early Christians to identify Jesus of Nazareth as the long-awaited Messianic King. In the letters written by the apostles, the title frequently joined with the personal name Jesus, becoming "Christ Jesus" or "Jesus Christ."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Christos comes from a Greek verb that means "to smear with oil" or "to anoint." The word refers specifically to one who has been anointed for a special office or calling by God.

In New Testament usage, the word functions in two main ways:

  • As a formal title: "the Christ," highlighting Jesus as the promised King and Messiah of OT prophecy.

  • As a proper name: "Christ" or "Christ Jesus," identifying Jesus personally in His resurrected authority and divine Lordship.

In Philemon 1:6, the word is used as a proper name and title for Jesus. It identifies Him as the living Lord in whom believers are spiritually connected and toward whom all Christian growth and fellowship are directed.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, christos carries rich significance depending on how it appears in a sentence:

  • In the Gospels, people often debated whether Jesus was "the Christ"—the promised Son of David sent to redeem Israel.

  • In the book of Acts, the apostles preached that God had made Jesus both Lord and Christ, proving His identity through His resurrection.

  • In the Epistles, Paul and other writers frequently place believers "in Christ." This expression describes a real spiritual connection where a Christian belongs to Christ, shares in His life, and lives under His reign.

Scripture also speaks of doing things "for Christ" or "toward Christ." In these settings, the title reminds believers that Jesus is the chief goal and purpose of their daily actions.

4. Theological Implications

The title christos connects Jesus directly to the central promise of the whole Bible. God did not send an ordinary teacher or moral guide; He sent His chosen, anointed King to accomplish salvation.

Because Jesus is the Christ, every blessing and spiritual gift given to believers comes through Him. Disciples do not live by their own strength or cultural standards. Instead, they derive their identity, power, and purpose from their union with the Anointed King. When Christians interact with one another, they are called to recognize that every member of the church belongs to the same Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:6, the apostle Paul writes a deeply personal prayer for his friend Philemon: "that the sharing of your faith may become effective by the acknowledgment of every good thing which is in you in Christ Jesus."

Paul is writing to Philemon regarding Onesimus, a runaway slave who had recently come to faith in Jesus through Paul's ministry. Under Roman law, Philemon had legal rights to punish or demand payment from a returned slave. But Paul prays that Philemon will recognize every good gift that God has put inside him through his union with Christ Jesus.

The name "Christ" in this verse serves a crucial purpose. It shifts Philemon's eyes away from cultural expectations, legal rights, and personal offense, redirecting them to Jesus, the Anointed King. Because Philemon is "in Christ," his active fellowship and generosity must reflect the nature of the King he serves. Forgiveness, reconciliation, and brotherly love are the natural fruit of acknowledging the good things present in those who belong to Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 17:3 — "explaining and demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead..."

  • Romans 6:8 — "Now if we died with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with Him..."

  • 2 Corinthians 5:16 — "Therefore, from now on, we regard no one according to the flesh. Even though we have known Christ according to the flesh, yet now we know Him thus no longer."

  • Philippians 3:7 — "But what things were gain to me, these I have counted loss for Christ."

  • 1 Peter 4:14 — "If you are reproached for the name of Christ, blessed are you, for the Spirit of glory and of God rests upon you."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the short letter to Philemon, social barriers are overturned by the gospel. In ancient Roman society, a slave master and a runaway slave existed in entirely different social classes with no common ground.

By grounding his appeal in "Christ Jesus," Paul places both Philemon and Onesimus on equal spiritual footing. Both men were redeemed by the same Anointed King, both were filled with spiritual gifts by the same Spirit, and both were called to glorify the same Lord. The title "Christ" transforms ordinary human relationships by bringing them under the supreme authority and grace of Jesus.
