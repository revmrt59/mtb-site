SHARING - ( koinōnia , Strong's G2842 )

Summary:

In Philemon 1:6, the word "sharing" (koinōnia) refers to a mutual participation, fellowship, or common partnership in the Christian faith. Rather than representing a passive belief or a simple distribution of resources, it represents a deep, active partnership that unites believers and manifests itself in tangible, practical expressions of love, reconciliation, and generosity. In this specific context, Paul prays that Philemon’s active partnership in the faith will become powerfully effective as he recognizes and expresses every good thing that is ours in Christ Jesus, particularly as it relates to welcoming back his runaway slave, Onesimus, as a brother.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun koinōnia derives from the root koinos, which means "common" or "shared." In the ancient Greco-Roman world, the word family was used in commercial, marital, and religious contexts to describe a binding partnership or a joint venture. Business partners who pooled their resources and shared both risks and profits were said to have koinōnia (as James, John, and Peter did in their fishing business in Luke 5:10). In marriage, it described the shared life and destiny of a husband and wife. Thus, the word picture behind "sharing" is not merely a polite conversation or a casual acquaintance; it is the deep, costly, and legally binding reality of a joint-stock venture where everything is held in common and the partners are mutually committed to a singular purpose.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun koinōnia denotes the state of sharing in common, fellowship, close association, or active participation.

  • Active participation or sharing: The act of taking part in something with others, such as a shared endeavor, faith, or experience.

  • Fellowship or close relationship: A deep, mutual bond of association characterized by unity, harmony, and shared values, particularly among believers in Christ.

  • Generous contribution: The outward expression of fellowship through financial or material assistance given to those in need (often translated as "contribution" or "distribution").

In Philemon 1:6, the term highlights the relational and active partnership of faith that Philemon shares with Paul and the broader body of Christ, which must now express itself in how he treats Onesimus.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the New Testament, koinōnia is used to describe both vertical and horizontal relationships. Vertically, it describes the believer’s fellowship with the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit (1 John 1:3; 1 Corinthians 1:9; 2 Corinthians 13:14). Horizontally, it describes the mutual bond among believers.

  • The Shared Table: The early church expressed koinōnia by breaking bread together, sharing meals, and holding all possessions in common (Acts 2:42-44).

  • The Partnership in the Gospel: Paul uses the word to describe the financial and spiritual partnership he shared with the Philippian church in advancing the gospel (Philippians 1:5).

  • The Shared Suffering: Believers are invited into the koinōnia of Christ’s sufferings, indicating a deep, experiential participation in His life and death (Philippians 3:10).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, koinōnia is not a humanly engineered community or a mere social club; it is a supernatural reality created by the Holy Spirit.

  • Trinitarian Foundation: Christian fellowship is patterned after and sourced in the eternal, loving communion within the Trinity. Believers are drawn into this divine fellowship through Christ.

  • Corporate Identity: The New Testament knows nothing of solitary Christianity. To have faith in Christ is to be brought into an inseparable, mutual partnership with all other believers.

  • Incarnational and Practical: True koinōnia must always move from an abstract theological concept to a visible, concrete reality. It demands the sharing of resources, the bearing of one another's burdens, and the extending of forgiveness and reconciliation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:6, Paul prays "that the sharing of your faith may become effective." The phrase "sharing of your faith" (hē koinōnia tēs pisteōs sou) can be understood in a few ways, but the context of Philemon's relationship with Paul and his runaway slave, Onesimus, guides the interpretation.

  • Active Partnership: Paul is not just referring to Philemon telling others about his faith, but rather the active, lived-out expression of his faith in partnership with other believers.

  • Testing of Fellowship: The effectiveness (energēs) of Philemon's koinōnia is about to be put to the ultimate test. Onesimus, Philemon's runaway slave who likely defrauded him, has become a Christian. He is returning not as a slave, but as a "beloved brother."

  • Effective through Acknowledgment: For Philemon's partnership to be effective, he must "acknowledge" (fully recognize and act upon) every good thing that is in him through Christ. This includes forgiveness, grace, and treating a former slave as an equal partner in the gospel. If Philemon refuses to welcome Onesimus, his koinōnia is proven to be ineffective and hollow.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 2:42: "And they continued steadfastly in the apostles' doctrine and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and in prayers."

  • 1 Corinthians 1:9: "God is faithful, by whom you were called into the fellowship of His Son, Jesus Christ our Lord."

  • 2 Corinthians 13:14: "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Spirit be with you all. Amen."

  • Philippians 1:5: "for your fellowship in the gospel from the first day until now,"

  • Hebrews 13:16: "But do not forget to do good and to share, for with such sacrifices God is well pleased."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Power of Energēs: The word "effective" in Philemon 1:6 is energēs, from which we get "energy" or "energetic." When koinōnia is active, it is not static; it releases a supernatural energy and power within the community that serves as a powerful witness to the outside world.

  • Social Transformation: In the first-century Roman Empire, the social chasm between a master and a slave was absolute. By appealing to koinōnia, Paul quietly but radically undermines the entire institution of Roman slavery. In the koinōnia of Christ, the master and the slave share the same Lord, the same table, and the same inheritance.
