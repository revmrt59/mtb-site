THING - (agathós, Strong's G18)

Summary:

Agathos describes that which is inherently good, beneficial, useful, or upright. In moral and spiritual contexts, it refers to high moral character, generous acts, and divine blessings that produce spiritual profit. In Philemon 1:6, the word appears in a neuter form translated "good thing," referring to every spiritual blessing, grace, and Christlike virtue that God has placed within believers. Paul highlights these "good things" so that Philemon will recognize the rich grace he has received in Christ, moving his heart to show forgiveness and warm fellowship toward his runaway bondservant, Onesimus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the Greek world, agathos was widely used to describe items, actions, or persons that possessed practical value or noble quality. It could refer to fertile soil, useful tools, honorable character, or acts of civic generosity. While another common word for good (kalos) often emphasized visible beauty, outward charm, or moral excellence, agathos focused especially on internal goodness, practical benefit, and useful outcome.

When the New Testament authors used agathos, they elevated its meaning to reflect God's divine standard. God is the ultimate source of all true goodness, and human actions are good only when they flow from God's character and grace. In the early church, where believers from diverse backgrounds lived together in tight-knit communities, recognizing and practicing true goodness was essential for demonstrating the reality of the gospel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Agathos means good, beneficial, upright, noble, or morally excellent. Depending on how it functions in a sentence, the word can describe:

  • Moral goodness or integrity in a person's character.

  • Actions that bring practical help, blessing, or spiritual encouragement to others.

  • Things that are advantageous, profitable, or of high quality.

  • Divine gifts and spiritual blessings provided by God.

In Philemon 1:6, the word is a neuter singular form functioning as a noun, rendered "good thing." Joined with the word for "every," it encompasses all the spiritual gifts, moral graces, and righteous qualities that believers possess through their union with Jesus Christ.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses agathos in several related ways across different settings:

  • Divine Goodness: Jesus declared that God alone is good in an absolute, ultimate sense (Mark 10:18). Human goodness is derived from God's nature.

  • Moral Fruitfulness: A good heart or a good tree naturally produces good fruit (Matthew 7:18; Luke 8:15). Practical righteousness flows from an inner transformation made by God.

  • Generous Actions and Works: Believers are called to work with their hands to do what is good and to perform good works that serve others (Ephesians 4:28; Colossians 1:10).

  • Providential Care: God works all things together for good for those who love Him (Romans 8:28), showing that divine providence brings ultimate benefit out of every circumstance.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical idea of agathos reveals that true goodness is anchored in God Himself. Human beings cannot produce genuine spiritual goodness apart from divine grace. Through redemption in Christ, God cleanses the heart and implants spiritual life, enabling believers to display real goodness in their daily conduct.

Furthermore, spiritual blessings are not intended to remain hidden or self-contained. The good things God places inside a believer are meant to be understood, appreciated, and expressed outward in acts of love, forgiveness, and active fellowship. Spiritual growth involves growing in the knowledge and practical application of what God has already provided in Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:6, Paul is writing a personal prayer for his friend Philemon, a house-church leader in Colosse. Philemon’s slave, Onesimus, had stolen from him and fled to Rome, where he met Paul and came to faith in Christ. Paul is now sending Onesimus back, asking Philemon to receive him not as a slave, but as a beloved Christian brother.

Before making his direct request, Paul prays that Philemon's sharing of faith may become effective through the full acknowledgment or recognition of "every good thing" that is in believers in Christ Jesus. The grammatical construction presents this "good thing" as a rich category of grace—the forgiveness, mercy, love, and spiritual wealth that belong to every follower of Jesus.

Paul’s strategy is pastorally wise. He does not begin with an intense command; instead, he reminds Philemon of the immense spiritual goodness God has poured into his life. When Philemon recognizes how much divine goodness lives in him through Christ, extending forgiveness and fellowship to Onesimus becomes the natural, joyful expression of that inner reality.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 7:18 – "A good tree cannot bear bad fruit, nor can a bad tree bear good fruit."

  • Luke 8:15 – "But the ones that fell on the good ground are those who, having heard the word with a noble and good heart, keep it and bear fruit with patience."

  • Romans 8:28 – "And we know that all things work together for good to those who love God, to those who are the called according to His purpose."

  • Ephesians 4:28 – "Let him who stole steal no longer, but rather let him labor, working with his hands what is good, that he may have something to give him who has need."

  • Colossians 1:10 – "that you may walk worthy of the Lord, fully pleasing Him, being fruitful in every good work and increasing in the knowledge of God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding agathos in Philemon 1:6 highlights the connection between spiritual awareness and active Christian behavior. Paul links the effective sharing of faith with the "acknowledgment" (deep personal knowledge and appreciation) of God's gifts. Coldness or hesitation to forgive often stems from forgetting how much grace one has received. By bringing Philemon’s focus back to every "good thing" placed within him by Christ, Paul sets a foundation for reconciliation, showing that biblical goodness is a living reality meant to transform human relationships.
