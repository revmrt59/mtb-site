BROTHER - (adelphós, Strong's G80)

Summary:

The word adelphos fundamentally means a brother, referring literally to a male born from the same parents. Throughout the New Testament, its meaning extends to include fellow countrymen and, most significantly, fellow believers in Jesus Christ who share God as their spiritual Father. In Philemon 1:7, Paul addresses Philemon directly as "brother," affirming their shared bond in God's family and acknowledging how Philemon's love has refreshed the hearts of God's people.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek culture, adelphos referred to a biological brother. The word combined elements signifying "from the same womb," emphasizing the physical origin shared by children of the same mother or parents. Over time, the term broadened in Greek life to refer to close relatives, members of a society, or trusted companions.

When the Jewish Scriptures were translated into Greek, adelphos was used to translate the common Hebrew word for brother. In addition to blood relatives, Israelites used the term for fellow members of the covenant nation of Israel. By the time of the New Testament, Jesus and the apostles took this familiar word and applied it directly to the Christian community. Because believers are adopted into God's family through Christ, they relate to one another as true spiritual brothers and sisters.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word adelphos can refer to:

  • A physical brother sharing the same mother or parents.

  • A fellow countryman or member of the same nation.

  • A fellow believer in Jesus Christ, united through faith in God.

The word itself identifies a familial relationship. When applied to Christians, it does not lose its basic meaning of family connection; rather, it defines the church as a spiritual family whose members share the same Father and the same spiritual life.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The New Testament uses adelphos across several distinct settings:

  • Physical Family: It describes biological brothers, such as James and John or the physical brothers of Jesus (Matthew 1:2; Mark 12:19).

  • National Identity: It refers to fellow Israelites who share common Abrahamic descent (Acts 7:23).

  • Spiritual Fellowship: It serves as the standard name for fellow believers within the church (1 Thessalonians 2:9; 1 Corinthians 15:58).

  • Christ's Relationship to Believers: In Hebrews 2:11, Jesus is presented as calling those He redeems His "brethren," highlighting the reality of spiritual adoption.

In early church life, calling a fellow Christian a "brother" was not a formal title. It was a warm recognition that faith in Christ created a real, permanent relationship deeper than earthly ties.

4. Theological Implications

The extended use of adelphos for believers rests on the doctrine of divine adoption. God does not merely save individuals in isolation; He brings them into a family. Because believers share the same Father, they share a common identity and a mutual obligation to love and support one another.

This spiritual brotherhood dramatically affected social relationships in the first century. In Roman society, strict boundaries separated masters from slaves, rich from poor, and Jews from Gentiles. The church brought people from all these groups into one household. To call another Christian "brother" meant recognizing that earthly distinctions do not create different classes of citizens in the kingdom of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:7, Paul writes: "For we have great joy and consolation in your love, because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother."

Paul uses a direct, personal address ("brother"), expressed in a masculine singular vocative form, to speak to Philemon directly from the heart. Before Paul makes his primary request—asking Philemon to forgive and welcome back his runaway slave, Onesimus—he establishes the relational setting of his letter.

Paul commends Philemon because his active love has brought comfort and refreshment to God's people. By addressing Philemon as "brother," Paul places himself and Philemon on equal ground within God's family. Paul does not issue an apostolic order; instead, he appeals to a beloved fellow Christian as a brother. This direct address serves as a gentle reminder that Philemon's identity as a brother in Christ shapes how he should treat others in the church family.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 25:40 — "And the King will answer and say to them, 'Assuredly, I say to you, inasmuch as you did it to one of the least of these My brethren, you did it to Me.'"

  • Luke 15:27 — "And he said to him, 'Your brother has come, and because he has received him safe and sound, your father has killed the fatted calf.'"

  • Acts 7:23 — "Now when he was forty years old, it came into his heart to visit his brethren, the children of Israel."

  • 1 Corinthians 15:58 — "Therefore, my beloved brethren, be steadfast, immovable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, knowing that your labor is not in vain in the Lord."

  • 1 John 2:9 — "He who says he is in the light, and hates his brother, is in darkness until now."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's use of "brother" in Philemon demonstrates how spiritual language was meant to transform daily behavior. Philemon was a wealthy house-church host, yet Paul addresses him not by his social status or wealth, but by his spiritual family identity.

This term sets up the entire argument of the short letter. Later in the passage, Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus no longer as a mere bondservant, but as a beloved brother in the Lord. By first affirming Philemon as his own brother in verse 7, Paul invites Philemon to extend that same brotherly welcome to a slave who has now become a follower of Christ.
