HEARTS - ( splanchnon , Strong's G4698 )

Summary:

The Greek word splanchnon refers literally to the internal organs—the heart, lungs, and liver. In the ancient world, these "inward parts" were viewed as the seat of the deepest human emotions, much like the modern English use of "heart" or "gut." In Philemon 1:7, Paul praises Philemon because the "hearts" (the deep emotional and spiritual centers) of the believers have been refreshed through his practical expressions of love.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • splanchnon literally refers to the physical viscera, the internal organs of the human body, such as the heart, liver, lungs, and kidneys. In ancient Greek literature and culture, these internal organs were considered the seat of intense human emotions, passions, and affections. While modern Western culture associates deep emotion primarily with the heart and intuition with the "gut," the ancient world viewed the lower and upper viscera as the origin of deep-seated feelings like pity, anger, love, and anxiety. This is similar to the physical sensation of having "butterflies in the stomach" or feeling a "pit in the stomach" when experiencing intense emotional states.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In classical and Hellenistic Greek, the plural form splanchna describes the physical entrails, particularly those sacrificed to deities. Metaphorically, however, the term transitions to denote the seat of the emotions, specifically close affection, deep compassion, tender mercy, or the psychological core of a person. In the New Testament, it represents the innermost self, the seat of feeling, and the center of personal affection. Depending on the context, English translations render the word as "hearts," "affections," "tender mercies," or "compassion."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The verb form of this word, splanchnizomai, means "to be moved with compassion." It is used frequently in the Gospels to describe the visceral reaction of Jesus when He saw the hurting, helpless crowds or individuals in desperate need. His compassion was not a mere intellectual acknowledgment of suffering but a deep, physical ache that compelled Him to heal, feed, and teach.

  • The noun form conveys a love that is profound, vulnerable, and deeply felt. Paul uses the term to describe his intense longing and spiritual affection for the Philippian believers, stating that he longs for them "with the affection of Jesus Christ." This indicates that Christian love is not a cold duty but a warm, visceral connection modeled on and empowered by Christ Himself.

4. Theological Implications

  • Genuine Christian love is deeply experiential and felt at the core of one's being. It is not characterized by cold duty or intellectual assent but by a profound emotional investment in the well-being of others.

  • The indwelling of Christ transforms the believer's emotional capacity. The deep compassion (splanchnon) that a Christian experiences for others is a participation in the very heart of Jesus Christ.

  • Within the body of Christ, believers share an organic, mutual connection. The spiritual and emotional health of one member affects the internal state of the entire community, demanding active care and relief.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Philemon 1:7, Paul commends Philemon because the "hearts" of the saints have been "refreshed" (anapauo) through him. Philemon’s love and generosity did not merely provide surface-level relief; they penetrated to the very core of his fellow believers' souls, bringing deep peace, comfort, and emotional renewal.

  • This commendation is a strategic pastoral move. Paul is preparing Philemon to receive his runaway slave, Onesimus, who has become a Christian.

  • Later in the letter, Paul refers to Onesimus as "my own heart" (splanchna, v. 12) and appeals to Philemon to "refresh my heart (splanchna) in the Lord" (v. 20) by forgiving Onesimus. By repeating this specific word, Paul connects Philemon's established reputation for restoring the deep emotional cores of the saints to the specific, difficult obedience of restoring both Onesimus and Paul himself.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Philippians 1:8 - "For God is my witness, how greatly I long for you all with the affection of Jesus Christ."

  • Colossians 3:12 - "Therefore, as the elect of God, holy and beloved, put on tender mercies, kindness, humility, meekness, longsuffering;"

  • Philemon 1:12 - "You therefore receive him, that is, my own heart."

  • Philemon 1:20 - "Yes, brother, let me have joy from you in the Lord; refresh my heart in the Lord."

  • 1 John 3:17 - "But whoever has this world's goods, and sees his brother in need, and shuts up his heart of compassion from him, how does the love of God abide in him?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • In the King James Version, splanchnon was often translated literally as "bowels" (e.g., "bowels of the saints are refreshed"). While this translation was accurate to the physical idiom of the seventeenth century, the modern shift to "hearts" or "affections" captures the true metaphorical intent for contemporary readers.

  • The physical reality of the word reminds teachers and preachers that biblical love is meant to be felt. It is an emotional reality that registers physically in the human body, motivating believers to tangible actions of mercy.
