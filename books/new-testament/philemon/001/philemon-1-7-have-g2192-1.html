HAVE - (échō, Strong's G2192)

Summary:

The Greek verb echō broadly means to have, hold, possess, or experience. In Philemon 1:7, the word describes Paul and his companions experiencing deep joy and encouragement because of Philemon’s sacrificial love for God's people.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Apostle Paul wrote the Letter to Philemon while imprisoned, sending it to a key leader in the church at Colosse. The immediate purpose of the letter was to intercede for Onesimus, a runaway servant who had come to faith under Paul’s ministry. Before making his specific request, Paul opens with a personal thanksgiving for Philemon’s life and ministry.

In verse 7, Paul uses the verb echō to express how Philemon's Christian character affected Paul and his fellow workers. Even while confined in chains, Paul was filled with gladness because he received reports of Philemon's ongoing faithfulness to the church.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb echō is a foundational action word in the New Testament. It can describe several types of possession or experience:

  • Physical possession or holding (owning property, carrying an object, or holding a position).

  • An internal or emotional state (experiencing peace, joy, fear, or anxiety).

  • A relational condition or spiritual status (having a mediator, having faith, or having eternal life).

In Philemon 1:7, the verb describes experiencing an internal state. Paul does not use the word to talk about physical property, but about possessing a deep sense of joy and comfort provided by the faithful service of a fellow believer.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, echō adapts to the nouns it governs. When paired with physical items, it refers to practical ownership. When paired with abstract or spiritual concepts, it refers to personal experience and spiritual reality.

For example, when used with terms like "faith," "peace," or "joy," the verb indicates that a person actively experiences or enjoys that spiritual reality. It is not merely potential or theoretical; it marks a present reality in the believer's life. In Philemon 1:7, pairing echō with "joy" and "consolation" highlights the personal emotional relief and spiritual gladness that Paul received through Philemon's love.

4. Theological Implications

Paul’s statement demonstrates that the body of Christ functions interdependently. The spiritual maturity and practical love of one Christian directly bring joy and comfort to others.

Spiritual comfort and joy are gifts from God, but God frequently channels these blessings through the actions of His people. When Philemon refreshed the hearts of the believers, he became the instrument through which God provided encouragement to Paul in prison. This shows that Christian love is active and practical, producing real, experienced encouragement within the wider church family.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:7, Paul writes, "For we have great joy and consolation in your love." The plural form ("we have") indicates that Paul is speaking on behalf of himself and his ministry companions. Philemon’s reputation for love was not a private matter; it brought shared gladness to those laboring for the gospel.

This verse serves a strategic function in the letter. By recognizing the joy Philemon has already brought to the church ("because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you"), Paul establishes a warm, affirming atmosphere. Before asking Philemon to make a costly sacrifice by forgiving and receiving Onesimus as a brother, Paul acknowledges the genuine love Philemon has already proven.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 6:40 — "...everyone who sees the Son and believes in Him may have everlasting life..."

  • Here the word describes possessing eternal life as a present spiritual reality through faith in Jesus Christ.

  • John 15:13 — "Greater love has no one than this, than to lay down one’s life for his friends."

  • Here the word denotes possessing or demonstrating the highest standard of sacrificial love.

  • 1 Corinthians 7:28 — "...such will have trouble in the flesh..."

  • Here the word describes experiencing earthly hardship or pressure within human circumstances.

  • 2 Corinthians 3:4 — "And we have such trust through Christ toward God."

  • Here the word refers to possessing deep spiritual confidence toward God through the work of Christ.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s use of echō in verse 7 leads directly into his appeal in verse 8 ("Therefore, though I might be very bold in Christ to command you..."). Paul bases his pastoral request not on legal force, but on the proven character of Philemon. The joy Paul already experienced from Philemon's love gave him full confidence to ask Philemon to extend that same love to Onesimus.
