SAINTS - (hágios, Strong's G40)

Summary:

The Greek word hagios means set apart, sacred, or holy. When used as a noun in the plural to describe believers, it refers to "holy ones" or "saints"—those set apart by God for Himself through faith in Jesus Christ. In Philemon 1:7, Paul uses the term for the members of the Christian community whose hearts were deeply encouraged and refreshed by Philemon's practical love and ministry.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The underlying concept of hagios carries the basic meaning of being set apart from common or ordinary use and devoted to God. In the Old Testament background, God brought the people of Israel into covenant with Himself and called them to be a holy nation because He is holy.

In the New Testament, this idea expands to include all who belong to Jesus Christ. Early Christians did not adopt the title "saints" as a self-made badge of superior morality. Instead, it was given to them by God to define their new standing. Every person who trusts in Christ is set apart by God, redeemed from sin, and called to live as part of God's holy family. Paul writes to Philemon, a house-church leader in Colosse, praising him for how his faithful service has strengthened this community of believers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word hagios can refer to anything dedicated to God and kept separate from ordinary use:

  • As an adjective, it means "holy," "sacred," or "consecrated."

  • As a noun in the plural form, it refers to "holy ones" or "saints."

The New Testament does not restrict the word to a small group of exceptionally pious Christians or individuals recognized after death. Instead, it describes all living believers in Christ. Believers are called saints not because they are inherently perfect, but because God has cleansed them and set them apart for His divine purpose.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses hagios across a wide range of contexts. It describes God Himself, His Holy Spirit, His holy scriptures, His holy angels, and His holy commandments. When applied to God's people, the word highlights identity first, followed by lifestyle.

In the letters of the New Testament, "saints" is the primary title used for members of local churches. Paul routinely addresses entire congregations—such as those in Rome, Corinth, Ephesus, and Philippi—as "saints." This usage shows that holiness in the New Testament is both a present gift from God and a practical standard for daily life. Believers are set apart positionally when they are saved, and they are called to live set-apart lives practically as they follow Christ.

4. Theological Implications

Calling believers "saints" grounds Christian identity entirely in divine grace. Holiness is not something people produce on their own; it is received through union with Jesus Christ. God takes people out of darkness, forgives their sins, and sets them apart as His personal possession.

This title also highlights the communal nature of the Christian faith. The term appears almost always in the plural when referring to God's people. Scripture presents no concept of an isolated Christian walking alone. Saints are meant to live in local fellowship, carrying one another's burdens, encouraging one another, and laboring together for the gospel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:7, Paul writes, "For we have great joy and consolation in your love, because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother."

Paul highlights how Philemon's faith took concrete form in practical love toward the local body of believers. The word "hearts" refers to the inner person, including the emotions, spirit, and mind. Philemon's generous hospitality, encouragement, and material support provided actual relief and renewal to the believers in his region.

Paul's use of "saints" here reminds Philemon that those he served were precious to God. Because Philemon cared for God's set-apart people, he brought great joy and comfort to Paul. This observation sets up Paul's upcoming appeal for Onesimus, showing that Philemon already had a proven track record of refreshing fellow believers.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 9:13 – "Lord, I have heard from many about this man, how much harm he has done to Your saints in Jerusalem."

  • 1 Corinthians 14:33 – "For God is not the author of confusion but of peace, as in all the churches of the saints."

  • Ephesians 4:12 – "for the equipping of the saints for the work of ministry, for the edifying of the body of Christ."

  • Revelation 5:8 – "golden bowls full of incense, which are the prayers of the saints."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The word "refreshed" in Philemon 1:7 was often used in ancient Greek to describe resting after hard labor or giving physical relief to tired travelers. Combining this word with "saints" paints a vivid picture of church life: local believers faced hostility, hardship, and exhaustion, but entering Philemon's home brought them genuine spiritual and physical rest.

By calling Philemon "brother" immediately after mentioning the "saints," Paul links family affection with holy duty. Refreshing fellow believers is not merely a social courtesy; it is how members of God's holy family care for one another.
