BOLD - (parrhēsía, Strong's G3954)

Summary:

The Greek word parrhēsia denotes freedom of speech, unreserved openness, confidence, and courage. In ancient Greek society, it referred to the political right of citizens to speak freely in public assemblies. In the New Testament, it shifts to describe plain speech without figures, public openness without secrecy, bold gospel witness before authorities, and confident access to God in prayer. In Philemon 1:8, the word describes the apostolic confidence and spiritual authority Paul possesses in Christ to direct Philemon, an authority he graciously refrains from enforcing so that Philemon may act willingly out of love.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In classical Greek civic life, parrhēsia was a key ideal of democratic society. It named the citizen's cherished privilege to speak openly, frankly, and without fear of punishment in the public assembly. Someone with parrhēsia was not bound by political intimidation or social fear; they possessed the liberty to speak the complete truth publicly.

In the biblical setting, this political freedom became a spiritual quality. The early church lived in a environment where speaking openly about Jesus Christ carried significant social and legal risks. Rather than retreating into secret practices or soft language, the apostles prayed for parrhēsia—the holy boldness to preach the gospel clearly, openly, and fearlessly. In Paul's letters, the term also highlights the unreserved honesty he maintained with the churches and the complete spiritual confidence believers enjoy through faith in Jesus Christ.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word parrhēsia combines basic elements meaning "all" and "speech." It carries the root sense of saying everything freely, holding nothing back, and speaking without concealment or fear.

The word is used in several distinct ways in Scripture:

  • Plainness of speech: Speaking directly, clearly, and without veiled or figurative language.

  • Public openness: Doing or saying something openly in full public view rather than secretly.

  • Courageous confidence: Speaking or acting with fearless assurance before opposition or official authority.

  • Spiritual assurance: Approaching God with complete confidence and freedom, without guilt or dread.

In Philemon 1:8, the word functions as a noun referring to apostolic confidence and freedom of action. Paul points to the full spiritual liberty he has in Christ to give Philemon a direct command concerning what is right.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, parrhēsia highlights open truth and spiritual courage in contrast to secrecy, fear, or hesitation.

In the Gospels, particularly in John, Jesus uses parrhēsia when speaking "plainly" to His disciples rather than using figures of speech. It also describes Jesus moving or teaching "openly" in public despite the plots of political and religious leaders against Him. Truth in the ministry of Jesus was not a hidden secret for a select few; it was proclaimed openly to the world.

In the book of Acts, parrhēsia becomes a defining mark of the early church's proclamation. When facing threats from authorities, the believers did not pray for protection from persecution; they prayed for "boldness" to continue speaking God's word. The religious leaders were astonished by the unreserved confidence of ordinary men like Peter and John.

In the Epistles, Paul uses the word to describe the transparency of his ministry ("speech") and the confident standing believers have before God ("boldness"). Instead of approaching God with fear or shame, Christians possess complete freedom of access through Jesus Christ.

4. Theological Implications

Paul's use of parrhēsia in Philemon 1:8 reveals important truths about spiritual authority and Christian leadership. First, spiritual authority in the church is real and grounded in Jesus Christ. As an apostle, Paul possessed genuine authority (parrhēsia) to direct church members toward righteous action and covenant obligations. Church leadership is not merely human advice; it carries spiritual weight when aligned with Christ's truth.

Second, gospel authority operates through love rather than raw coercion. Although Paul had full confidence and authority to command Philemon, he intentionally chose to appeal on the basis of love instead. The existence of authority does not mean it must always be enforced by decree. True gospel ministry aims for willing, heart-level transformation rather than mechanical compliance forced by official title.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The Epistle to Philemon centers on Onesimus, a runaway servant who had met Paul while the apostle was imprisoned. Under Paul's ministry, Onesimus became a Christian believer. Paul was sending Onesimus back to his master, Philemon, who was also a Christian leader in whose house a local church met.

In verse 8, Paul writes: "Therefore, though I might be very bold in Christ to command you what is fitting..." The word "bold" translates the noun parrhēsia, which describes the absolute freedom and apostolic authority Paul possessed. Paul had every right in Christ to issue a direct order telling Philemon how to treat Onesimus—specifically, to receive him back with forgiveness as a brother in the Lord.

Yet in verse 9, Paul immediately turns his argument: "yet for love's sake I rather appeal to you." This contrast is central to the passage. Paul establishes that his restraint is deliberate. He possesses the authority (parrhēsia), but he voluntarily lays aside his right to command. By doing so, Paul invites Philemon to act out of genuine Christian love and personal commitment rather than forced duty.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 7:26 – "But look! He speaks boldly, and they say nothing to Him."

  • Acts 4:13 – "Now when they saw the boldness of Peter and John, and perceived that they were uneducated and untrained men, they marveled."

  • Ephesians 3:12 – "...in whom we have boldness and access with confidence through faith in Him."

  • Philippians 1:20 – "...according to my earnest expectation and hope that in nothing I shall be ashamed, but with all boldness, as always, so now also Christ will be magnified in my body, whether by life or by death."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The target occurrence in Philemon 1:8 is a singular feminine noun in the accusative case, serving as the direct object of Paul's expression regarding the freedom he possesses ("having much boldness in Christ").

This verse presents a profound model of pastoral care. By explicitly mentioning his apostolic confidence (parrhēsia) before declining to use it as a weapon of compulsion, Paul honors Philemon's spiritual maturity. He treats Philemon as a partner in Christ whose faith and love will naturally produce the right action when given the opportunity to respond freely.
