CHRIST - (Christós, Strong's G5547)

Summary:

The Greek word Christos means "Anointed One" and is the New Testament equivalent of the Hebrew title Messiah. It describes Jesus as God's chosen King, Priest, and Savior sent to fulfill divine prophecy. In Philemon 1:8, Paul speaks of his boldness "in Christ," showing that true spiritual authority comes directly from union with Jesus, even as that authority must be exercised with love and pastoral wisdom.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Christos comes from a Greek verb meaning to anoint or smear with oil. In the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, Christos was used to translate the Hebrew word Mashiach (Messiah). In ancient Israel, kings, priests, and occasionally prophets were anointed with oil to signify that God had set them apart for a specific sacred office. By the time of the New Testament, Christos was recognized both as the title of the promised Savior-King and as a proper name for Jesus of Nazareth, who fulfilled those Old Testament expectations.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word Christos means "Anointed One" or "Messiah." It refers to an individual specifically consecrated and empowered by God to accomplish His divine purpose. In secular ancient Greek, the term referred broadly to anything applied as a salve or ointment. In biblical usage, however, the word refers to divine appointment and consecration. In the New Testament, Christos appears both as a formal title ("Jesus the Christ") and as a proper name ("Jesus Christ" or simply "Christ"). In Philemon 1:8, the word identifies the exalted Lord in whom believers share a common life and authority.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the New Testament, Christos carries significant depth. In the Gospels, the central question often concerns whether Jesus is "the Christ"—the long-awaited Son of David who comes to save His people. In the Book of Acts, the apostles preach continuously to show from the Scriptures that Jesus is indeed the Christ. In the New Testament epistles, Paul frequently pairs the term with prepositions, creating expressions like "in Christ." This phrase highlights the spiritual connection between Jesus and those who trust in Him, indicating that a believer's standing, moral direction, and spiritual authority flow directly from this union.

4. Theological Implications

The name Christos anchors the identity of Jesus firmly in Old Testament promise. It demonstrates that Jesus is the true divine King who reigns over God's kingdom, the ultimate High Priest who offered Himself as a sacrifice for sin, and the final Prophet who declares God's truth perfectly. Furthermore, Paul's frequent reference to being "in Christ" points to the doctrine of union with Christ. Believers do not function on their own strength, righteousness, or status. Instead, their lives and ministry operate within the authority and character of Jesus Himself.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:8, Paul addresses his friend Philemon concerning Onesimus, a runaway slave who had recently become a Christian. Paul notes that he has great boldness "in Christ" to command Philemon to do what is proper. The phrase "in Christ" specifies the source of Paul's apostolic authority. The word appears in a dative grammatical form following a preposition, marking the sphere in which Paul's boldness exists. Because Paul is united to Christ and serves as His apostle, he possesses genuine authority to direct fellow believers. However, Paul immediately follows this statement in verse 9 by explaining that he prefers to appeal to Philemon out of love rather than rely on command. Paul's authority comes from Christ, but the spirit of Christ leads him to seek voluntary obedience grounded in genuine love rather than forced compliance.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's phrase "in Christ" serves as a foundational concept across his letters. It reminds readers that Christian authority and leadership are never meant to be autocratic or coercive. When Paul references his boldness "in Christ" in Philemon 1:8, he establishes that spiritual authority must always reflect the character of Christ Himself. Because Christ leads through sacrificial love and humility, those who exercise authority in His name must aim to restore relationships, encourage willing obedience, and promote genuine Christian fellowship.
