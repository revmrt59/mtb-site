COMMAND - (epitássō, Strong's G2004)

Summary:

The Greek verb epitassō means to give an official order, issue an authoritative directive, or command with binding authority. In ancient usage, it described the orders issued by rulers, commanders, or masters who possessed recognized positional authority over others. In Philemon 1:8, the word refers to Paul's apostolic right to issue a binding directive to Philemon regarding the runaway slave Onesimus. Instead of asserting his authority through an official command, Paul voluntarily waives this right, choosing instead to appeal to Philemon on the basis of Christian love.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greco-Roman world, social structures depended heavily on clear lines of authority. Rulers issued decrees, military officers gave orders to soldiers, and masters commanded slaves. An official order from a superior was expected to be obeyed without debate.

When the early church was established, the apostles carried genuine spiritual authority granted by Christ to guide congregations, teach doctrine, and direct Christian conduct. Writing from prison, the apostle Paul addresses Philemon, a wealthy leader of a house church in Colossae. Philemon’s slave, Onesimus, had run away, encountered Paul in prison, and become a believer. According to Roman law, Philemon had absolute authority over Onesimus. However, according to gospel fellowship, Onesimus was now Philemon’s brother in Christ. Paul writes to resolve this situation, possessing full apostolic authority to step in, yet choosing a softer approach.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb epitassō is built from two basic elements: a preposition meaning "upon" or "over" (epi) and a verb meaning "to set in order" or "to arrange" (tassō). Together, the word means to lay an order upon someone, to give a direct command, or to mandate an action with official authority.

When a person with recognized standing uses this word, it expresses an expectation of compliance. It does not merely offer advice or express a desire; it sets down an authoritative requirement. In Philemon 1:8, the word means to issue a binding command based on apostolic authority.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Elsewhere in Scripture, epitassō describes actions where authority is clear and absolute. In the Gospels, the word frequently highlights the supreme authority of Jesus Christ. Jesus commands unclean spirits, winds, and waves, and they obey Him immediately. His speech carries complete, effective power.

The word is also used for official human decrees, such as a ruler giving orders to an executioner or a religious leader directing an officer. In every case, the word emphasizes the positional right of the speaker to demand compliance from the hearer. It highlights the direct obligation placed upon the person receiving the order.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of epitassō in Philemon 1:8 highlights a vital truth about spiritual authority in the kingdom of God. Paul genuinely possessed apostolic authority; he was not exaggerating when he claimed he had the right in Christ to command Philemon. True spiritual leadership is backed by God's truth and carries real authority.

However, Paul's refusal to use this authority reveals how the gospel transforms the exercise of power. While legal standard and formal authority can force outward compliance, the gospel seeks voluntary obedience born of love. God desires heartfelt sacrifice rather than forced obligation. By stepping back from issuing a command, Paul models the character of Christ, who led through love and humble service rather than harsh dominance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:8, Paul writes, "Therefore, though I might be very bold in Christ to command you what is fitting, yet for love's sake I rather appeal to you." The verb appears as an infinitive describing the action Paul refrains from taking.

Paul acknowledges that he has the boldness and the spiritual right in Christ to direct Philemon's actions. What Paul would have commanded was "fitting"—meaning it was morally right, proper, and aligned with Christian duty. Yet Paul intentionally sets aside his power to command.

By replacing an authoritative command with a personal appeal, Paul places the decision entirely in Philemon's hands. He gives Philemon the freedom to welcome Onesimus back voluntarily. This approach transforms a potential act of mere duty into an act of genuine love and spiritual maturity.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The striking contrast in Philemon 1:8–9 is between epitassō (to command) and parakaleō (to appeal or exhort). While epitassō operates from a position of power above the recipient, parakaleō operates alongside the recipient, coming as a brother to encourage right action.

Paul shows that while authority is real and sometimes necessary, leading through love builds true Christian unity and allows believers to grow in genuine virtue.
