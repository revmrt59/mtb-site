FITTING - (anḗkō, Strong's G433)

Summary:

The Greek word anēkō refers to conduct or duty that is proper, suitable, or appropriate for a given situation or relationship. In the New Testament, it describes behavior that naturally aligns with a believer's identity in Jesus Christ. In Philemon 1:8, Paul uses the word to describe the righteous, forgiving action that Philemon owes toward his converted bondservant Onesimus, while choosing to appeal to Philemon in love rather than imposing a strict command.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word anēkō originally meant "to reach to," "to relate to," or "to belong to." In Greek moral discussion, it gradually developed into an ethical term for actions that are suitable, proper, or required by duty. It highlighted behavior that naturally belonged to a specific position, role, or relationship.

When early Christian writers adopted this word, they transformed its standard of measurement. What is proper or suitable is no longer determined merely by social expectation or civic custom. Instead, Christian duty is defined by life in Christ and the demands of gospel grace. In Paul's letter to Philemon, the setting is deeply personal: Onesimus, a runaway servant, had met Paul in prison and embraced faith in Jesus. Paul sends him back to Philemon and uses anēkō to frame the moral obligation Philemon has toward this newly transformed brother.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Anēkō means to be proper, suitable, standard, or appropriate. When used in describing conduct, it points to what ought to be done in light of one's standing and relationships.

In the New Testament, the term appears primarily in the sense of moral propriety—doing what is morally right and spiritual fit for a Christian. It does not refer to optional social etiquette. Rather, it identifies conduct that naturally accords with a holy calling and gospel truth.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses anēkō to mark out clean boundaries between conduct that honors God and conduct that dishonors Him. It consistently addresses how believers ought to treat one another and how they ought to speak and live in everyday life.

Rather than providing a legalistic checklist, the term points believers toward actions that fit their redeemed status. Behavior that is "not fitting" stands in contrast to a life shaped by thanksgiving, purity, and mutual love. Conversely, that which is "fitting" reflects the spirit of the gospel worked out in concrete relationships.

4. Theological Implications

The word anēkō carries rich theological truths regarding Christian conduct and obedience:

  • Gospel propriety flows from redeemed identity. Christian obligations are not arbitrary rules imposed from outside; they are actions that naturally fit who a person has become in Christ.

  • Freedom and love elevate duty. While a believer has clear moral obligations ("what is fitting"), God desires obedience to spring from voluntary love rather than external pressure.

  • Faith reshapes social relationships. In Christ, old social divisions and grievances yield to a higher standard of brotherhood, forgiveness, and mercy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:8, Paul writes, "Therefore, though I might be very bold in Christ to command you what is fitting, yet for love's sake I rather appeal to you..."

Paul explicitly recognizes that he possesses apostolic authority to order Philemon to do "what is fitting"—specifically, to forgive Onesimus, welcome him back, and receive him as a brother in the Lord. Forgiving and welcoming Onesimus was the proper, Christlike action required in this circumstance.

However, Paul intentionally chooses not to exercise his right to command. He understands that genuine Christian virtue flourishes best when performed willingly. By bringing up "what is fitting," Paul reminds Philemon of his true moral obligation before God, but by choosing to "appeal," Paul gives Philemon the opportunity to perform this duty out of authentic love. The word helps establish the baseline of Christian duty while highlighting the glory of voluntary grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ephesians 5:4 – "...neither filthiness, nor foolish talking, nor coarse jesting, which are not fitting, but rather giving of thanks."

  • Colossians 3:18 – "Wives, submit to your own husbands, as is fitting in the Lord."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Paul's letters, what is "fitting" is frequently joined to the phrase "in the Lord." This shows that Christian obligation is never isolated from a believer's union with Jesus. What makes an action proper is not human traditions or personal convenience, but how well it reflects the character and lordship of Christ.
