BOLD - ( parrēsia , Strong's G3954 )

Summary:

The Greek noun parrēsia combines the words for "all" (pas) and "speech" (rhēsis), originally denoting the democratic right of a free citizen in ancient Greece to speak openly, candidly, and without fear of reprisal. In the New Testament, it transcends political speech to describe a divinely empowered boldness, confidence, and freedom of speech before God and humans. In Philemon 1:8, Paul uses it to describe his apostolic authority and freedom in Christ to command Philemon, though he immediately relinquishes this "boldness" in favor of an appeal based on love.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The term parrēsia is a compound of pas ("all") and rhēsis ("speaking" or "speech"), literally meaning "speaking everything" or "saying it all."

  • In classical Greek democracy, particularly in Athens, parrēsia was a highly valued civic privilege. It represented the right of a free citizen to stand before the public assembly and speak their mind frankly, with complete candor, without fear of censorship or state punishment.

  • It stood in stark contrast to the guarded, fearful, and self-censoring speech of slaves or subjects living under a tyrant.

  • Over time, the word expanded from the political sphere to personal relationships, signifying open, honest, and unreserved communication between close friends, free from flattery, deception, or hidden agendas.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Parrēsia refers to a state of boldness, courage, or fearlessness in speaking, especially in the face of opposition or danger.

  • It denotes plainness, openness, or directness of speech that leaves no room for ambiguity, obscurity, or misunderstanding.

  • It signifies the spiritual freedom, assurance, and confidence that a believer has in standing before authority figures, whether human rulers or God Himself.

  • Within the New Testament, this boldness is not self-reliance or natural bravado; it is a spiritual freedom and holy confidence grounded in the redemptive work of Christ and empowered by the Holy Spirit.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The New Testament employs parrēsia in several distinct ways:

  • Plainness of speech: Jesus speaking openly and directly to His disciples rather than using figures of speech or parables (John 11:14; 16:29).

  • Public openness: Jesus or the apostles teaching in public view, hiding nothing, and holding nothing back from the authorities (John 7:4; 18:20).

  • Apostolic courage: The fearless proclamation of the gospel under the threat of persecution or death (Acts 4:13, 31).

  • Believer's access to God: The joyful, unhindered assurance with which Christians approach the throne of grace in prayer (Hebrews 4:16; 10:19).

  • The word picture is that of a person standing upright, chin up, speaking with clear, unhurried, and unreserved words because they are completely secure in their standing and have nothing to hide.

4. Theological Implications

  • Parrēsia highlights the radical transformation of the believer's relationship with God under the New Covenant. Under the Old Covenant, approaching the holy presence of God was restricted and marked by fear. Now, because of Christ's sacrifice, believers possess absolute freedom of access and speech before God (Hebrews 10:19).

  • It is a direct fruit of the Holy Spirit's presence. When the early church was threatened by authorities, they prayed for parrēsia, and the Holy Spirit filled them, enabling them to speak the word of God with boldness (Acts 4:31).

  • It represents the standard for true gospel ministry. True Christian ministry does not employ deceptive speech, flattery, or hidden agendas; it operates with open, transparent, and bold proclamation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:8, Paul writes, "Therefore, though I might be very bold in Christ to command you what is fitting..."

  • Here, parrēsia (translated as "very bold" when combined with the Greek verb echōn, "having [much] boldness") refers to Paul’s apostolic authority. As an apostle of Jesus Christ, Paul had the divine right and spiritual freedom to speak plainly and issue a direct command to Philemon regarding his runaway slave, Onesimus.

  • The source of this boldness is explicitly "in Christ." It is not based on Paul’s personal charisma, social status, or natural assertiveness, but on his authorized position as Christ's representative.

  • However, Paul immediately contrasts this bold authority with the way of love in verse 9: "yet for love's sake I rather appeal to you." By mentioning his parrēsia only to lay it aside, Paul demonstrates pastoral wisdom. He desires Philemon's obedience to flow from a willing, loving heart rather than from apostolic compulsion. Laying aside his rightful "boldness" makes his appeal far more powerful and persuasive.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 4:13: "Now when they saw the boldness of Peter and John, and perceived that they were uneducated and untrained men, they marveled."

  • Ephesians 3:12: "in whom we have boldness and access with confidence through faith in Him."

  • Hebrews 4:16: "Let us therefore come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy and find grace to help in time of need."

  • Hebrews 10:19: "Therefore, brethren, having boldness to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus..."

  • 1 John 4:17: "Love has been perfected among us in this: that we may have boldness in the day of judgment; because as He is, so are we in this world."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The transition of parrēsia from the classical political realm to the spiritual realm is profound. In ancient Greece, parrēsia was a right reserved strictly for free, male citizens of high status. In Christ, this ultimate freedom of speech and access is granted to all believers—regardless of social standing, gender, or past. A runaway slave like Onesimus and a wealthy master like Philemon now share the exact same parrēsia before the King of Kings.

  • Paul’s refusal to demand compliance using his parrēsia serves as a powerful model for Christian leadership. True spiritual authority often accomplishes its greatest work not by asserting its rights, but by laying them down to appeal to the hearts of others.
