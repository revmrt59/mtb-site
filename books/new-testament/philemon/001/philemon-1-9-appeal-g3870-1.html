APPEAL - (parakaléō, Strong's G3870)

Summary:

The Greek word parakaleō means to call to one's side, entreat, encourage, exhort, or comfort. Depending on the context, it can range from a desperate plea for help to a tender word of encouragement or an earnest moral request. In Philemon 1:9, the word means to make a warm, heart-felt personal request, where Paul intentionally appeals to Philemon on the basis of Christian love rather than issuing an apostolic command.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Apostle Paul wrote the short letter to Philemon while under imprisonment for the gospel. Onesimus, a bondservant belonging to Philemon, had run away and eventually met Paul. Under Paul's ministry, Onesimus came to faith in Jesus Christ. Paul was now sending Onesimus back to Philemon, asking Philemon to receive him not merely as a slave, but as a beloved brother in the Lord.

In the Greco-Roman world, a master had total legal authority over a returned runaway slave, including the right to inflict severe punishment. Paul possessed apostolic authority in the church to command Philemon to do what was right. However, Paul chose a different path. Instead of issuing an official decree, he made a personal entreaty. The word Paul selected for this entreaty is parakaleō, translated here as "appeal."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb parakaleō is built from two basic parts: a prefix meaning "alongside" and a root verb meaning "to call." At its fundamental level, the word means to call someone to one's side. From this basic meaning, several practical senses developed in ancient Greek usage:

  • To request or entreat earnestly: asking someone to grant a favor or act on a request.

  • To exhort or urge: calling someone to adopt a right course of action or lifestyle.

  • To encourage or comfort: coming alongside a person in distress to strengthen their spirit.

In Philemon 1:9, the word refers to an earnest personal appeal. Paul speaks in the first person ("I appeal"), presenting his request to Philemon as a brother in Christ asking another brother to act out of love.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, parakaleō carries different shades of meaning based on who is speaking and what the situation requires:

  • Desperate pleas for help: In the Gospels, people often call upon Jesus to heal their sick family members or deliver them from distress.

  • Exhortation and teaching: In the book of Acts and the Epistles, church leaders use the word when urging believers to remain faithful to God, live holy lives, and preserve Christian unity.

  • Comfort and encouragement: The word is frequently used when God or fellow believers bring spiritual comfort and strength to those undergoing trial, sorrow, or persecution.

The common thread across all these usages is a personal, relational connection. The speaker does not remain distant; rather, the speaker comes alongside the hearer to entreat, strengthen, or guide.

4. Theological Implications

Paul’s choice to appeal rather than command reflects an important theological truth about Christian obedience. Gospel-driven obedience flows from a heart transformed by grace, not from external force or legal obligation.

Paul explicitly explains his motive in the context: "yet for love's sake I rather appeal to you." By grounding his request in love, Paul demonstrates how Christlike leadership operates. Christ does not treat believers as unwilling subjects forced into compliance, but as friends and brothers invited to walk in love. When leaders appeal on the basis of gospel grace, they honor the work of the Holy Spirit inside the believer's heart.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The immediate context of Philemon 1:8–9 highlights a striking contrast. In verse 8, Paul acknowledges that he has "great boldness in Christ to command you what is fitting." As an apostle, Paul had the moral and spiritual authority to order Philemon to welcome Onesimus back.

Yet in verse 9, Paul sets aside command in favor of appeal: "yet for love's sake I rather appeal to you—being such a one as Paul, the aged, and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ." Paul reinforces this entreaty by mentioning his advanced age and his chains for the sake of the gospel. He does not use his suffering to guilt Philemon, but to emphasize the sincerity and weight of his love.

By appealing rather than commanding, Paul gives Philemon the opportunity to perform a good deed voluntarily. As Paul notes later in verse 14, he wanted Philemon's generosity to be "not as it were of necessity, but voluntary." The word parakaleō captures this delicate balance: it is strong enough to convey deep urgency, yet gentle enough to respect Philemon's freedom to act out of love.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Corinthians 1:10 — "Now I plead with you, brethren, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that you all speak the same thing..." Here the same Greek word is translated "plead," as Paul appeals to the believers to end divisions and walk in unity.

  • 2 Corinthians 10:1 — "Now I, Paul, myself am pleading with you by the meekness and gentleness of Christ..." Paul uses the same verb ("pleading") to entreat the church using the gentle spirit of Jesus.

  • 1 Thessalonians 3:2 — "...and sent Timothy, our brother and minister of God, and our fellow laborer in the gospel of Christ, to establish you and encourage you concerning your faith..." In this passage, the same word is translated "encourage," showing its power to build up believers during trials.

  • 1 Timothy 2:1 — "Therefore I exhort first of all that supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks be made for all men..." Here the word is rendered "exhort," introducing a pastoral charge regarding prayer.

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is worth noting that the related Greek noun paraklētos is the title Jesus uses for the Holy Spirit in the Gospel of John, often translated as "Comforter," "Helper," or "Advocate." When Paul comes alongside Philemon to appeal for mercy and reconciliation, his approach reflects the comforting and encouraging work of the Spirit Himself.

By framing his request as an appeal, Paul lifts the issue of Onesimus out of the realm of human legal rights and places it squarely in the realm of Christian brotherhood and gospel grace.
