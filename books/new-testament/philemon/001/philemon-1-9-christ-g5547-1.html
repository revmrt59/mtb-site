CHRIST - (Christós, Strong's G5547)

Summary:

The word Christos means "Anointed One" or "Messiah," identifying Jesus as God's promised King and Savior. In Philemon 1:9, the word identifies the divine Lord whom Paul serves and to whom he belongs while in physical chains. By calling himself a "prisoner of Jesus Christ," Paul shows that his captivity is subject to Christ's royal authority, framing his gentle appeal for Onesimus around shared loyalty to their heavenly King.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Greek word Christos comes from a verb meaning "to anoint." In the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, the Septuagint, Christos was chosen to translate the Hebrew word Mashiach (Messiah). In Israel's history, prophets, priests, and kings were anointed with oil to signify that God had set them apart for a specific duty. Over time, God promised a supreme Anointed One who would come to deliver His people, establish righteousness, and rule as King forever.

By the time of the New Testament, the term designated this expected Savior. While the Gospel accounts often show people asking whether Jesus was "the Christ," the New Testament letters frequently combine the title directly with His name, using "Jesus Christ" or "Christ Jesus." In Paul's letters, Christos functions both as a title of royal authority and as a personal name for the risen Savior.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Christos means "Anointed One" or "Messiah." It designates Jesus as the true King, Priest, and Prophet chosen by God to accomplish salvation.

In Philemon 1:9, the word refers directly to Jesus as the exalted Lord. The grammatical form used here indicates relationship and belonging. Paul describes himself as a "prisoner of Jesus Christ," meaning a prisoner who belongs to Christ and suffers on His behalf. Rather than viewing himself as a captive of the Roman government, Paul views his life and circumstances as entirely held within the authority of Jesus the Messiah.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, Christos appears in several distinct settings:

  • As the promised Messiah: In the Gospels and early preaching in Acts, the focus is often on proving that Jesus is the Christ foretold by Old Testament scripture.

  • As the exalted Lord: In the Epistles, Christos highlights the authority of Jesus after His resurrection and ascension.

  • As the believer's dynamic union: Paul frequently speaks of believers being "in Christ," indicating a shared spiritual life with the Savior.

When Paul calls himself a prisoner "of Christ," he uses the title to redefine his earthly trouble. Earthly authorities physically guarded Paul, but Paul understood that his true master was Christ.

4. Theological Implications

The title Christos carries strong biblical depth. Calling Jesus "Christ" affirms that He fulfills God's long-standing covenant promises to Israel and the nations. It proclaims that Jesus holds ultimate authority over human history, rulers, and spiritual powers.

Paul's use of the title in connection with his imprisonment reveals how faith reinterprets suffering. Human courts condemned Paul, but because he was serving the true Anointed King, his chains were a mark of service to Christ rather than personal disgrace. His allegiance to Christ took priority over physical freedom or social status.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In his letter to Philemon, Paul asks a Christian slave owner to forgive and receive back Onesimus, a runaway slave who had become a Christian under Paul's ministry. Paul had full apostolic authority to order Philemon, but in verse 9 he intentionally chooses a different approach: "yet for love’s sake I rather appeal to you—being such a one as Paul, the aged, and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ."

By referring to himself as a "prisoner of Jesus Christ," Paul makes a quiet yet powerful point:

  • He demonstrates his own sacrifice for the gospel. If Paul can endure chains for the sake of Christ, Philemon can set aside personal pride and financial loss to forgive Onesimus.

  • He reminds Philemon of their common master. Both Paul and Philemon serve the same King, Jesus Christ.

  • He shifts the foundation of his request from legal command to Christian love and shared submission to Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:1 — "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham."

  • Acts 17:3 — "...explaining and demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead, and saying, 'This Jesus whom I preach to you is the Christ.'"

  • Ephesians 3:1 — "For this reason I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus for you Gentiles..."

  • 1 Peter 4:14 — "If you are reproached for the name of Christ, blessed are you, for the Spirit of glory and of God rests upon you."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Roman society, imprisonment brought public shame and stripped a person of dignity. By linking his chains directly to the name of Christos, Paul completely transforms the meaning of his confinement. He does not ask Philemon for pity; he highlights his imprisonment as a badge of allegiance to Jesus.

This perspective directly supports Paul's main request in the letter. Just as Christ's lordship transformed Paul's captivity from a mark of shame into a position of spiritual honor, Christ's lordship also transformed Onesimus from a worthless runaway slave into a beloved brother in the Lord.
