FOR - (diá, Strong's G1223)

Summary:

The Greek preposition dia fundamentally expresses agency, instrumentality, or cause, translating into English as "through," "by means of," "because of," or "for the sake of." In Philemon 1:9, the word is translated "for" (in the expression "for love's sake"), where it marks the underlying reason for Paul's request. Instead of commanding Philemon on the basis of apostolic authority, Paul grounds his appeal in love, demonstrating that gospel leadership seeks willing obedience rooted in affection rather than forced compliance.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In New Testament Greek, prepositions function as connective links, showing how concepts relate to one another in time, space, cause, or action. The preposition dia is one of the most common relational words in the New Testament. It connects an action to its intermediate channel or its motivating cause.

When the apostle Paul wrote his brief letter to Philemon, he was addressing a delicate situation. Onesimus, a bondservant who had separated from Philemon, had encountered Paul in prison, come to faith in Christ, and rendered valuable service to the apostle. Paul was sending Onesimus back, but he wanted Philemon to receive him not merely as a servant, but as a beloved brother in the Lord. To accomplish this, Paul deliberately chose words that set aside legalistic demands in favor of Christian fellowship. The choice of dia in verse 9 sets the tone for Paul's entire request.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The preposition dia indicates a connection between two things, specifying either the channel through which an outcome is accomplished or the reason why an action takes place.

Depending on its grammatical setting in a sentence, the word functions in two primary ways:

  • Agency or Means: When showing agency or intermediate means, the word means "through" or "by means of." It identifies the instrument or mediator through whom something is done (for example, God speaking "through" a prophet, or salvation coming "through" Jesus Christ).

  • Reason or Motive: When indicating cause or motive, the word means "on account of," "because of," or "for the sake of." It identifies the underlying reason or purpose behind an action.

In Philemon 1:9, the word functions in this second sense. Translated as "for" in the phrase "for love's sake," dia identifies love as the supreme motive driving Paul's appeal.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, dia serves as a vital grammatical bridge in two distinct ways:

First, it often marks Jesus Christ or divine grace as the intermediate channel through which God acts. God creates, saves, reconciles, and blesses "through" Christ. In these contexts, the word emphasizes that human beings do not access God's blessings directly on their own merit, but through the mediator God has provided.

Second, the word frequently explains human or divine motivation. When Scripture describes actions done "because of" faith, "on account of" the gospel, or "for the sake of" love, dia points the reader to the internal driver behind the external conduct. In Philemon 1:9, Paul uses the word in this motivational sense. He does not base his request on his official rank or Philemon's duty; he bases it on love.

4. Theological Implications

While dia itself is simply a functional connector, its specific placement in Philemon 1:9 highlights an important principle of Christian leadership and ministry.

Paul possessed genuine apostolic authority. In verse 8, he notes that he had full freedom in Christ to command Philemon to do what was right. Yet in verse 9, he deliberately pivots away from authority and chooses the path of entreaty "for love's sake." This reveals that in the kingdom of God, the manner of an appeal is deeply connected to the gospel itself. Force or coercion might produce external compliance, but love produces genuine transformation of the heart.

By appealing through the motive of love, Paul honors Philemon's spiritual maturity and invites him to act out of shared Christian affection. True Christian obedience flows from internal conviction and love for God and others, rather than pressure from human leadership.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:8-9, Paul constructs a clear contrast between two ways of exercising leadership:

  • Command based on authority (verse 8)

  • Appeal based on love (verse 9)

Paul explicitly states that although he has the right to command what is fitting, he chooses not to do so. Instead, using dia ("for love's sake"), he frames his request around the mutual love that binds believers together in Christ.

Paul reinforces this gentle approach by identifying himself as "Paul, the aged, and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ." Rather than issuing orders from a position of power, he appeals as an elderly servant suffering in chains for the gospel. The preposition "for" acts as the hinge of this transition, turning the conversation away from obligation and toward voluntary, love-driven restoration.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 5:17 — "For if by the one man’s offense death reigned through the one, much more those who receive abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness will reign in life through One, Jesus Christ."

  • Galatians 1:15 — "But when it pleased God, who separated me from my mother’s womb and called me through His grace,"

  • 1 Timothy 5:23 — "No longer drink only water, but use a little wine for your stomach’s sake and your frequent infirmities."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's choice to base his request on love rather than command illustrates profound pastoral wisdom. When leaders rely solely on position or command, they risk creating resentment or outward conformity without heart engagement. By grounding his request in love, Paul gives Philemon room to act freely, turning a potential obligation into an opportunity for spiritual growth.

Furthermore, this appeal reflects the character of God's grace. God does not treat believers as forced laborers, but as beloved children. In the same way, Paul invites Philemon to welcome Onesimus back not out of sheer duty, but out of the genuine love that characterises the body of Christ.
