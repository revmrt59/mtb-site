APPEAL - ( parakaleo , Strong's G3870 )

Summary:

In Philemon 1:9, the word appeal represents the Greek verb parakaleo, which carries the core meaning of calling someone to one's side to encourage, exhort, or make an earnest request. Rather than asserting his apostolic authority to command obedience, Paul uses this relational term to entreat Philemon on the basis of Christian love. This shift from authoritative command to loving appeal highlights the power of voluntary, grace-driven transformation within the body of Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word parakaleo is a compound verb formed from para (beside) and kaleo (to call). Historically, it meant to call someone to one's side. In classical Greek, this concept was applied in legal contexts to describe an advocate, helper, or legal assistant summoned to aid a person in court. It was also used in military settings, where commanders would "call alongside" their troops to exhort, encourage, and strengthen their resolve before battle. This history embeds a rich dual sense of tender comfort and strong, motivating exhortation within the word.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of parakaleo includes several distinct nuances depending on the context:

  • To invite, call for, or summon.

  • To appeal to, urge, or exhort.

  • To implore, beseech, or entreat.

  • To encourage, comfort, or console.

In Philemon 1:9, the word functions in the sense of making an earnest, respectful appeal or entreaty. It denotes a request that respects the personal agency and freedom of the hearer rather than demanding compliance.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the New Testament, parakaleo is used to describe the gentle yet urgent nature of Christian persuasion. It stands in stark contrast to authoritarian commands. A key word picture is that of a friend or brother walking alongside another, placing a hand on their shoulder, and speaking words of encouragement or earnest request. The related noun parakletos (Advocate or Helper) is used by Jesus to describe the Holy Spirit in John's Gospel, emphasizing a supportive, encouraging presence.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, parakaleo illustrates how God relates to believers and how believers should relate to one another. God does not merely coerce obedience through raw power; instead, He frequently appeals to His people on the basis of His mercy and love. When Paul appeals to Philemon, he models this divine approach. True Christian obedience and transformation flow from a willing heart motivated by love, not from external pressure, legalistic demand, or raw coercion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:8–9, Paul contrasts two approaches to ministry leadership: commanding (epitasso) and appealing (parakaleo). Paul explicitly states that although he has "boldness in Christ" to command Philemon to do what is fitting, he chooses "for love's sake" to appeal to him instead. He strengthens this appeal by identifying himself as "Paul, the aged, and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ." By setting aside his apostolic authority and highlighting his vulnerability, Paul disarms any potential defensiveness, moving the issue from a matter of legal duty to one of mutual love and voluntary grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Philemon 1:10 - "I appeal to you for my son Onesimus, whom I have begotten while in my chains,"

  • Romans 12:1 - "I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that you present your bodies a living sacrifice..."

  • Ephesians 4:1 - "I, therefore, the prisoner of the Lord, beseech you to walk worthy of the calling with which you were called,"

  • 2 Corinthians 5:20 - "Now then, we are ambassadors for Christ, as though God were pleading through us: we implore you on Christ's behalf, be reconciled to God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The choice of parakaleo in Philemon underscores the horizontal equality inherent in Christian fellowship (koinonia). While Paul holds a higher office as an apostle, his use of this term levels the ground, addressing Philemon as a partner and beloved brother. It serves as a model for pastoral leadership, demonstrating that relational appeals rooted in love and shared identity in Christ are often far more powerful than formal directives.
