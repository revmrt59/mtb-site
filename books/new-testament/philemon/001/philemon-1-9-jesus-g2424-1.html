JESUS - (Iēsoûs, Strong's G2424)

Summary:

The name Iēsous is the Greek form of the Hebrew name Joshua (Yeshua), which means "Yahweh saves" or "Yahweh is salvation." In the New Testament, it is the personal name given to God's incarnate Son, who saves His people from their sins. In Philemon 1:9, Paul refers to himself as "a prisoner of Jesus Christ." By using this personal name, Paul identifies his bodily imprisonment not as a source of shame or political defeat, but as a commitment endured directly in the service of his living Lord and Master.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Jewish world, Iēsous was a widely used personal name. It was the Greek adaptation of the Hebrew name Yehoshua or Yeshua (Joshua), a name rich in Israelite history. The original Joshua was Moses' successor who led the nation into the Promised Land.

By the time of the New Testament, many Jewish men bore this name. However, when the angel commanded Joseph and Mary to name their child Jesus, the ordinary personal name took on unique historical purpose. As the Gospel writers record, He was given this name because He would save His people from their sins. In the Greco-Roman world, the Greek form Iēsous became the universal title of identification for Jesus of Nazareth. In Paul's letters, the personal name "Jesus" is routinely combined with "Christ" (the Greek translation of Messiah) to express both His human identity and His exalted divine office.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Iēsous is a proper personal noun. Its established etymological meaning is derived from Hebrew roots signifying "Yahweh saves" or "Yahweh is salvation." The historical and linguistic background of the name is well attested across ancient Jewish and early Christian literature.

In Philemon 1:9, the name identifies the historical person of Jesus Christ—the crucified, risen, and ascended Son of God.

While the background meaning of the name ("Yahweh saves") reflects the overarching mission of Christ, Paul is not using the name in this verse to make a wordplay on salvation. Instead, he uses "Jesus" alongside "Christ" as a formal title for the Lord whom he worships and serves. The name designates the personal Master on whose behalf Paul is currently held in chains.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, the name Iēsous carries distinct nuances depending on the literature:

  • Gospels: "Jesus" serves as the primary personal name used during His earthly ministry. It appears when He teaches His disciples (Matthew 11:25; Matthew 17:25), prays or journeys with them (Matthew 26:36), performs healings and miracles (Luke 5:12; John 9:11), and stands trial before Roman authorities (John 19:5).

  • Acts of the Apostles: The name connects the earthly Jesus with the ascended Lord who continues His work through the church (Acts 1:1). It forms the centerpiece of apostolic preaching, declaring that the crucified Jesus is indeed the promised Christ (Acts 17:3).

  • Pauline Letters: Paul frequently combines "Jesus" with "Christ" or "Lord" (Romans 14:14; 2 Corinthians 4:14; Philippians 1:8; 2 Thessalonians 2:1). This combined usage ensures that early Christian belief never separates the exalted Lord of glory from the real human being who lived, suffered, and died in history.

4. Theological Implications

The ongoing use of the personal name "Jesus" alongside "Christ" grounds the Christian faith firmly in human history. Salvation is not based on abstract ideals or distant philosophy, but on the concrete life, death, and resurrection of a real person.

Furthermore, naming Jesus as Lord claims absolute priority over all other human authorities. When Paul describes his circumstances in Philemon 1:9, he places his suffering directly under the authority of Jesus. Earthly rulers may physically hold Paul captive, but spiritually and legally, Paul belongs to Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:9, Paul writes to Philemon to request mercy and brotherhood for Onesimus, a runaway slave who had become a Christian under Paul's ministry. Paul intentionally avoids using his apostolic authority to demand compliance. Instead, he grounds his appeal in brotherly love.

To strengthen his gentle request, Paul describes himself as "Paul, the aged, and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ." The word "Jesus" appears in the genitive case (masculine singular), which establishes a relationship of possession and cause. Paul does not consider himself a prisoner of Rome or Nero; he is a prisoner belonging to and serving Jesus Christ.

By linking his current bondage directly to the personal name of his Lord, Paul offers Philemon a compelling model of sacrificial service. Paul's physical chains are proof of his commitment to Jesus. This reminder invites Philemon to respond to Paul's request with a similar spirit of cross-shaped love toward Onesimus.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:1 – "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham."

  • Acts 17:3 – "...explaining and demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead, and saying, 'This Jesus whom I preach to you is the Christ.'"

  • 2 Corinthians 4:14 – "...knowing that He who raised up the Lord Jesus will also raise us up with Jesus, and will present us with you."

  • Hebrews 6:20 – "...where the forerunner has entered for us, even Jesus, having become High Priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Paul's prison letters, describing himself as a captive of Christ reinterprets human hardship through divine sovereignty. Rome believed it held power over Paul's physical freedom. However, Paul's language shows that his suffering was bound up in the purpose of Jesus.

This perspective provided Paul with deep pastoral authority. He did not ask Philemon to do anything harder than what Paul himself was doing. Just as Paul was yielding his bodily freedom for the sake of Jesus Christ, he asked Philemon to yield his social rights and welcome Onesimus as a brother in the Lord.
