PAUL - (Paûlos, Strong's G3972)

Summary:

The Greek proper name Paulos comes from the Latin surname Paulus, which means "small" or "little." In the New Testament, it functions as the personal name of the apostle Paul, the former Pharisee Saul of Tarsus who became the apostle to the Gentiles. In Philemon 1:9, Paul places his own name directly into his request to Philemon ("being such a one as Paul, the aged, and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ"), using his personal identity, age, and imprisonment to appeal to his friend in love rather than commanding him by apostolic authority.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Paulos is a Greco-Roman name adopted from Latin family names. In the Roman world, individuals often carried multiple names, including a family name (cognomen). Paul was a Jewish man born in Tarsus who also possessed Roman citizenship. Consequently, he had both a Jewish name, Saul (Saulos), and a Roman name, Paul (Paulos).

In the book of Acts, the writer Luke uses the name Saul throughout Paul’s early life and persecution of the church. However, when Paul begins his first missionary journey among Gentile populations in the Roman empire, Luke notes that Saul was "also called Paul." From that point forward in the New Testament narrative, he is referred to exclusively as Paul. The name reflects his background as a Roman citizen and his specific calling to bring the gospel to the Gentile world.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Paulos is a masculine proper noun. It originates as a Latin personal surname meaning "small," "little," or "few."

In biblical usage, Paulos identifies two individuals:

  • Sergius Paulus, the Roman proconsul of Cyprus.

  • Paul the apostle, formerly known as Saul of Tarsus.

While the etymological origin of Paulos means "small" or "little," Scripture itself never draws a symbolic connection between this definition and Paul’s personal character, physical stature, or spiritual humility. It was simply his established Roman personal name. In Philemon 1:9, the name functions directly to identify the apostle himself as he writes a warm, personal appeal to his fellow believer.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, the name Paulos appears primarily in the book of Acts and as the opening sender in the Pauline epistles.

In the book of Acts, the name marks a major transition point in missionary focus. As the message of Jesus spreads beyond Jerusalem into Greco-Roman cities, Paul’s Roman name becomes his primary identifier in biblical history.

In most of Paul's letters, his name appears in the opening verse to establish his authority as an apostle called by God. However, Philemon is a deeply personal letter. Instead of emphasizing his formal apostolic rank at the beginning of his request, Paul drops his official title in verse 9 and simply introduces his own name: "being such a one as Paul." Here, his name carries the weight of a long friendship, shared faith, and mutual love rather than an official command.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of Paul’s personal name in Philemon 1:9 highlights how God works through personal relationships within the Christian community. Gospel ministry is not conducted solely through institutional commands or structural authority. It relies heavily on genuine love, personal accountability, and shared sacrifice.

By using his own name in connection with his physical weakness ("the aged") and his suffering ("a prisoner of Jesus Christ"), Paul shows that Christian leadership operates through humility and self-giving love. God used a real historical person—with a specific name, Roman citizenship, and personal history—to demonstrate how Christ's grace transforms human identity and motives.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:8–9, Paul addresses Philemon regarding Onesimus, a runaway bondservant who had come to faith under Paul's ministry in prison. Paul explicitly states in verse 8 that he has full authority in Christ to command Philemon to do what is right. Yet in verse 9, he intentionally sets aside authoritative command in favor of an appeal based on love.

The insertion of his name in verse 9 anchors this appeal in personal relationship. The phrase "being such a one as Paul" invites Philemon to look at who is asking:

  • He is Paul, Philemon’s spiritual father and trusted partner in the gospel.

  • He is "the aged," an elder brother who has spent his years laboring for the church.

  • He is "a prisoner of Jesus Christ," currently wearing chains for the sake of the very gospel they both serve.

Grammatically, the name stands in the nominative case, identifying Paul as the one speaking and making the heartfelt appeal. By naming himself in this vulnerable context, Paul makes it morally and relationally easy for Philemon to respond with forgiveness and love toward Onesimus.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 13:7 — "who was with the proconsul, Sergius Paulus, an intelligent man. This man called for Barnabas and Saul and sought to hear the word of God."

  • Acts 16:28 — "But Paul called with a loud voice, saying, 'Do yourself no harm, for we are all here!'"

  • Acts 23:11 — "But the following night the Lord stood by him and said, 'Be of good cheer, Paul; for as you have testified for Me in Jerusalem, so you must also bear witness at Rome.'"

  • 1 Corinthians 1:12 — "Now I say this, that each of you says, 'I am of Paul,' or 'I am of Apollos,' or 'I am of Cephas,' or 'I am of Christ.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A widespread misunderstanding is that God changed Saul's name to Paul at his conversion, similar to God changing Abram's name to Abraham or Jacob's name to Israel. Scripture nowhere states that God gave Saul a new name. As a Jewish man with Roman citizenship living in a multicultural empire, he possessed both names from birth. He used his Hebrew name (Saul) in Jewish contexts and his Roman name (Paulos) when ministering in the Greek and Roman world.

In Philemon 1:9, Paul’s self-description pairs his personal name with two striking qualifications: "the aged" and "a prisoner." Rather than appealing to his strength or rank, Paul appeals from a position of weakness and hardship, illustrating that true Christian persuasion relies on sacrifice and genuine love.
