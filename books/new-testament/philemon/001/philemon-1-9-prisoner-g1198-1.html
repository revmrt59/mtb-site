PRISONER - (désmios, Strong's G1198)

Summary:

The word refers to a captive or someone held in legal custody. In Philemon 1:9, Paul uses the word to identify himself as a prisoner for the sake of Jesus Christ. Rather than commanding Philemon with apostolic authority, Paul frames his appeal as an elderly, bound servant of Christ, inviting Philemon to act out of love rather than obligation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Roman world, prisoners were kept in public jails, military barracks, or under house arrest while awaiting trial or sentencing. Confinement was physically restrictive, and prisoners depended heavily on friends, family, or personal associates for food, clothing, and basic needs.

When Paul wrote his letter to Philemon, he was in physical custody under Roman authority. However, Paul did not view his imprisonment as mere bad luck or political misfortune. By calling himself a prisoner of Jesus Christ, he anchored his physical confinement directly in his service to the Lord.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word desmios refers to a person who is bound, held in chains, or kept in legal custody—a prisoner or captive.

In general biblical usage, the word describes:

  • A person physically detained by legal or military authority.

  • An individual undergoing custody under civil or imperial officials.

When Paul applies this word to himself, he describes his literal legal condition while pointing to the true reason for his confinement: his obedience to Jesus Christ.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In historical narratives such as the Gospels and Acts, the word describes individuals kept under guard by legal authorities. It is used for criminal or political prisoners held during Roman trials, as well as for Paul during his custody before Roman commanders and governors.

In Paul's letters, the word takes on a distinct perspective. Paul repeatedly refers to himself as a prisoner, but he modifies the term by linking it to Jesus Christ. He calls himself "a prisoner of Jesus Christ" or "a prisoner in the Lord." By doing so, Paul emphasizes that Roman authorities are not his ultimate masters. Imperial power may control his physical movements, but Jesus Christ remains sovereign over his life and ministry.

4. Theological Implications

Paul’s use of this word highlights several clear biblical truths:

  • Physical restrictions do not limit the spread of the Gospel. Suffering and confinement cannot defeat God's work.

  • God rules over human circumstances. Even while held in Roman custody, Paul views his life as under the sovereign care and purpose of Christ.

  • Christian leadership operates through service and humble endurance. Paul's influence is demonstrated not through earthly power, but through faithful sacrifice for Christ and His church.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philemon 1:8–9, Paul addresses a delicate situation regarding Onesimus, a runaway servant who became a believer through Paul's ministry. As an apostle, Paul possessed the spiritual authority to command Philemon to do what was right. Instead, he chooses to appeal to him "for love's sake."

To set the tone for this request, Paul presents himself as "Paul, the aged, and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ." Here, the word identifies Paul's current condition and reinforces his gentle posture. He does not enforce his rank; instead, he reminds Philemon of his advanced age and his chains for the Gospel. This approach appeals to Philemon's heart, encouraging him to act freely out of love and Christian brotherhood.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ephesians 3:1 — Paul identifies himself as "the prisoner of Christ Jesus" for the sake of the Gentiles, explaining that his imprisonment serves God's broader purpose.

  • 2 Timothy 1:8 — Paul urges Timothy not to be ashamed of the testimony of the Lord or of Paul "His prisoner," framing physical bonds as a witness of faithful service.

  • Acts 23:18 — A Roman officer reports that "Paul the prisoner" requested an interview, showing the standard historical description of Paul's custody.

  • Philemon 1:1 — Paul opens his letter by calling himself "a prisoner of Christ Jesus," establishing humility and devotion from the very beginning.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Philemon 1:9, the nominative singular form functions as a personal descriptive title alongside "Paul, the aged." This grammatical connection links Paul's advanced age and his physical captivity together into a single compelling description of his identity.

In Roman society, being a prisoner brought social disgrace and dishonor. By adopting this title freely, Paul transforms what the world viewed as shame into a mark of faithful devotion to Christ.
