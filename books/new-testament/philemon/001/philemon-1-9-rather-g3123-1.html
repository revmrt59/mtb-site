RATHER - (mâllon, Strong's G3123)

Summary:

The word mallon is an adverb used to express comparison or preference, meaning "more," "all the more," or "rather." In Philemon 1:9, Paul uses it to signal an intentional choice to set aside his apostolic authority in favor of an appeal rooted in Christian love. Instead of commanding Philemon to do his duty, Paul prefers to request his action, demonstrating that genuine Christian obedience flowers best through love rather than pressure.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Paul wrote his letter to Philemon from prison, addressing a delicate situation concerning Onesimus, a bondservant who had separated from Philemon and subsequently met Paul. Under Paul's ministry, Onesimus became a Christian. Paul was sending Onesimus back to Philemon, but he needed to address how Philemon would receive him.

As an apostle, Paul possessed the authority in Christ to issue direct orders to believers under his care. He could have legally and biblically commanded Philemon to act rightly. However, Paul deliberately shifted his approach. The adverb mallon marks this transition in Philemon 1:9, setting aside official decree to ground the request in brotherly love and voluntary devotion.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Mallon functions grammatically as an adverb of comparison or preference. It expresses two primary senses depending on context:

  • Comparative degree: meaning "more," "all the more," or "to a greater extent."

  • Preference or contrast: meaning "rather," "instead," or "in preference to."

In Philemon 1:9, the word indicates preference between two choices. Paul contrasts issuing an authoritative command with making a loving request. By using mallon, Paul expresses that he considers an appeal based on love to be the far better path.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, mallon frequently contrasts two options or highlights a superior alternative. When used in comparative statements, it emphasizes that one reality greatly exceeds another—such as God's care for His children exceeding His care for the rest of creation.

When used in moral or relational instruction, mallon redirects the reader from a natural or lesser course of action toward a higher spiritual priority. It does not merely distinguish between right and wrong; it often points to what is spiritually preferable, more excellent, and most consistent with the gospel.

4. Theological Implications

Paul's use of mallon illustrates how gospel grace reshapes leadership and fellowship within the Christian community. While legitimate spiritual authority exists, the gospel does not rely on heavy-handed force to produce righteousness. God desires heart-level obedience born of love rather than external conformity produced by compulsion.

By choosing to appeal rather than command, Paul mirrors the way Christ leads His church. God's grace transforms human motivations so that believers choose to serve one another freely out of love for Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In verse 8, Paul explicitly mentions his right to command: "though I might be very bold in Christ to command you what is fitting." Yet in verse 9, he writes, "yet for love’s sake I rather appeal to you." The word mallon ("rather") forms the hinge between these two verses.

Paul reinforces this appeal by describing himself as "Paul, the aged, and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ." He intentionally sets aside the title of commander and takes on the posture of an aged sufferer for the gospel. Later in verse 14, Paul explains his reasoning: he wanted Philemon's good deed to be done voluntarily, not out of compulsion. The word mallon shows that love and willingness are superior to formal obligation in accomplishing the work of Christ.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's choice of mallon aligns with his broader ministry philosophy seen across his letters. He consistently refused to lord his authority over the faith of believers, preferring to act as a fellow worker for their joy. By giving Philemon the freedom to act from love, Paul gave him the opportunity to grow in maturity and reflect Christ's character voluntarily.
