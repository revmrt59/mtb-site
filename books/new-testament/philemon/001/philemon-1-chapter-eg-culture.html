EG Culture - Philemon 1

HONOR || Through His Eyes

Culture Statement

Value Statement

Relationships are protected when we ascribe heaven’s value to the people around us. Like Jesus, we choose to interact with people, not as they deserve, but according to their God given value.

Living It in Philemon 1

Under Roman law Onesimus was Philemon's property, and Paul admits that he had once been "unprofitable" (v. 11). Yet Paul introduces him by what God has made him: "my son" (v. 10), "my own heart" (v. 12), and a brother to be received "no longer as a slave but more than a slave" (v. 16). Paul does not deny Onesimus's legal position or pretend his past never happened. He asks Philemon to treat him according to the value Christ has placed on him. Paul honors Philemon as well. He thanks God for his love, calls him a brother, and asks rather than commands (vv. 4–9). Honor runs both ways in this letter, toward the man with power and toward the man without it.

Honor is easy toward people who rank above us or have treated us well. Philemon 1 tests it toward someone with less standing and a past that may be complicated. Paul wrote, "Therefore, from now on, we regard no one according to the flesh" (2 Corinthians 5:16). Believers are called to see people that way: the employee who reports to us, the relative who once let us down, the person whose background makes others uneasy. Honor does not ignore wisdom or erase accountability. It refuses to let a person's position or record decide how we treat someone Christ has received.

FELLOWSHIP || Assembly Required

Culture Statement

Value Statement

We are united by our pursuit of the Lord. We come together to discover His heart and partner with His will. Breaking bread is optional, but strongly encouraged.

Living It in Philemon 1

Paul addresses this letter to "the church in your house" (v. 2). Believers gathered in Philemon's home, and the hearts of the saints had been refreshed there (v. 7). Fellowship in this letter is more than attending a meeting. Paul prays that "the sharing of your faith" will become effective (v. 6), and he appeals to Philemon as a "partner" (v. 17). Both words describe a common life in Christ that joins believers to one another and carries real obligations. When the Lord adds someone to His people (Acts 2:47), those who gather must make room for that person. Paul expected Onesimus to be received as a beloved brother "both in the flesh and in the Lord" (v. 16).

The letter also shows fellowship carrying people through a hard moment. Paul writes with Timothy (v. 1), names Apphia and Archippus (v. 2), and closes with greetings from five coworkers (vv. 23–24). Colossians, very likely sent at the same time, says that Tychicus was coming with Onesimus and calls Onesimus "a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you" (Colossians 4:7–9). Onesimus did not make his return alone. Tychicus traveled with him, and Paul's letters set the meeting within a wider fellowship of believers. Believers today need the same kind of fellowship: people who pursue the Lord together, show up when a hard step must be taken, and help one another do His will.

We are a House of Prayer

Culture Statement

Value Statement

Jesus identified His Father’s house as a House of Prayer. We assemble to seek the heart of the Father and partner with Him in faith, inquiry, intercession, supplication, petition, lament, and thanksgiving. We contend for mercy, righteousness, healing, and justice, believing that God transforms lives, cities, and nations through a praying Church.

Living It in Philemon 1

Prayer surrounds Paul's request. He thanks God for Philemon in his prayers (v. 4) and intercedes for the sharing of his faith (v. 6). Near the end he asks for a guest room, "for I trust that through your prayers I shall be granted to you" (v. 22). The "your" is plural. Paul is counting on the whole church that meets in Philemon's house. He believed that a small congregation gathered in a private home could seek God about a decision that rested with the authorities who held him, and that God would hear. He also said only, "I trust," leaving the answer with God.

Thanksgiving, intercession, and petition all appear in this short letter, and each reaches beyond Paul himself. He prays for Philemon's faith to work outward in love, and he asks the church to pray for his release. Believers can pray the same way. Ask God to make the faith of others effective, not only to make their lives easier. Bring reconciliation needs to the Lord, including the ones that seem stuck. When you face a closed door, invite others to pray with you. A praying church does not leave its members to carry their burdens alone. It brings them to the Father together and expects Him to act.

Forgiveness

Culture Statement

Value Statement

Jesus forgave so we forgive. The moment we stop forgiving, we stop reflecting the heart of the Father. Refusing to forgive is spiritual death for you and the people you lead.

Living It in Philemon 1

The word "forgive" does not appear in Philemon, and the letter never says that Onesimus wronged Philemon. Paul writes "if" (v. 18). Yet the appeal recognizes that something might stand between the two men, and Paul asks Philemon not to let it. He asks him to receive Onesimus as he would receive Paul (v. 17), offers to cover whatever wrong or debt there may be (vv. 18–19), and reminds Philemon of what he himself has received: "you owe me even your own self besides" (v. 19). Philemon had been given new life through the gospel. Any release he offers rests on that gift.

Colossians, very likely read in the same church, states the principle plainly: "even as Christ forgave you, so you also must do" (Colossians 3:13). Philemon's response would not be private. The letter was addressed to the church in his house (v. 2), and the believers there would see how he received Onesimus. Forgiveness affects more than the two people involved. When those who host, serve, or lead in God's house hold on to offenses, the people around them learn to do the same. When they release offenses and receive people back, they teach the whole church what Christ's forgiveness looks like. Forgiveness does not always restore trust at once, and it never requires anyone to place himself or herself in danger. It also does not cancel every legitimate obligation. Paul did not tell Philemon to waive any debt; he offered to pay it himself (v. 19). What forgiveness releases is the offense. Those whom Christ has forgiven refuse to hold resentment against a brother or sister, even while a real obligation is being settled.

Communication

Culture Statement

Value Statement

We “real talk” because we desire real relationships built on trust. We have 100% of our conversations, choosing to be authentic, honest, and open with one another. Awkward today, stronger tomorrow.

Living It in Philemon 1

Paul does not handle this situation indirectly. He tells Philemon that he could command him and explains why he chooses to appeal instead (vv. 8–9). He names Onesimus (v. 10). He states his own wish to keep him and why he will not (vv. 13–14). He says plainly what he wants: "receive him as you would me" (v. 17). He even raises the possibility of wrong or debt rather than leaving it unspoken (v. 18). Paul openly addresses what needs attention, yet he uses discretion: he does not assume what has not been established or expose more than the situation requires. Nothing is said harshly. Paul has the whole conversation, with honesty and warmth together.

Onesimus faced a hard conversation of his own. Paul sent him back to the household he had left (vv. 12, 15), and that meeting would likely not have been easy for either man. Paul's letter prepared the way, but it did not replace the face-to-face meeting. Many believers avoid conversations like this. They talk about a strained relationship with others but not with the person involved, or they let silence harden into distance. Paul taught the wider church the same principle: "putting away lying, 'Let each one of you speak truth with his neighbor,' for we are members of one another" (Ephesians 4:25). The first conversation may be awkward, but honest words spoken in love are how trust is rebuilt.
