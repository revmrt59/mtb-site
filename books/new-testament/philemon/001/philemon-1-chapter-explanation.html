NKJV

Philemon 1:1 Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy our brother, To Philemon our beloved friend and fellow laborer,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:1 This letter is from Paul, a prisoner for preaching the Good News about Christ Jesus, and from our brother Timothy. I am writing to Philemon, our beloved co-worker, (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul introduces himself by his chains rather than his apostolic title, as “a prisoner of Christ Jesus,” writing with “Timothy our brother” to “Philemon our beloved friend and fellow laborer.” From the first line, Paul’s imprisonment and their shared work in the gospel set the tone for a letter that will appeal rather than command.

Verse Explanation

Paul introduces himself by his chains rather than his apostolic title, as “a prisoner of Christ Jesus,” writing with “Timothy our brother” to “Philemon our beloved friend and fellow laborer.” From the first line, Paul’s imprisonment and their shared work in the gospel set the tone for a letter that will appeal rather than command.

"Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus,"

Paul wrote this letter from prison. He was under guard, most likely in Rome, and the letter was carried to Colossae along with the letter to the Colossians (Colossians 4:7–9). In most of his letters Paul introduces himself as an apostle, but here he calls himself a prisoner. That is the only title he gives himself as he begins a personal request to a friend.

He says he is a prisoner "of Christ Jesus," not of Rome. The Roman authorities held the keys, but Paul understood that he was in chains because he belonged to Christ and preached Him, and that his imprisonment was in Christ's hands. He describes himself the same way to the Ephesians: "I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus for you Gentiles" (Ephesians 3:1). To the Philippians he wrote that it had become evident to the palace guard "that my chains are in Christ" (Philippians 1:13). His circumstances had not stopped his ministry. It was during this imprisonment that Onesimus, the man this letter is about, came to faith through Paul (v. 10).

"and Timothy our brother,"

Timothy was Paul's younger coworker from Lystra, the son of a Jewish mother and a Greek father, who had traveled with Paul for years (Acts 16:1–3). He is named alongside Paul at the opening of several letters, including Colossians and Philippians, and here he joins Paul in greeting Philemon.

"Brother" means a fellow believer. Those who trust in Christ belong to one family, with God as their Father, and the early Christians used family language for one another as a matter of course. Paul says "our brother," not just "my brother." Timothy was a brother to Philemon as well, part of the same family of faith, even if they had never met.

"To Philemon our beloved friend and fellow laborer,"

Philemon was a believer who lived in Colossae, a town in the Roman province of Asia, in what is now western Turkey. Onesimus is called "one of you" when Paul writes to the Colossians (Colossians 4:9), and Archippus, who is greeted in the next verse, is mentioned there too (Colossians 4:17). Paul had not visited Colossae himself (Colossians 2:1), so he and Philemon had probably come to know each other elsewhere. Many think they met in Ephesus, where Paul taught for two years "so that all who dwelt in Asia heard the word of the Lord Jesus" (Acts 19:10).

The Greek behind "beloved friend" is simply "the beloved," a word built on the New Testament's main word for love. Philemon was dear to Paul, and that warmth runs through the whole letter. "Fellow laborer" means coworker, someone who works alongside another in the same task. Paul uses this word for close partners such as Timothy (Romans 16:21) and Titus (2 Corinthians 8:23). By calling Philemon his fellow laborer, Paul counts him a partner in the work of the gospel, serving Christ at Colossae as Paul served Him in prison.

Study Notes

Paul does not say where he was imprisoned, and three places have been proposed. The traditional view is Rome. Acts says Paul spent two years there under guard in his own rented house, free to receive visitors and preach (Acts 28:16, 30–31).

That setting fits well. Aristarchus, named in verse 24, sailed with Paul to Rome (Acts 27:2), and Luke's "we" in that account shows that he did too, and a huge city was a natural place for a runaway to disappear.

Some prefer Caesarea, where Paul was also held for two years (Acts 24:27).

Others suggest Ephesus. Acts records no imprisonment there, but before any Roman imprisonment Paul could already speak of being "in prisons more frequently" (2 Corinthians 11:23). Ephesus lay only about a hundred miles from Colossae, compared with well over a thousand to Rome. Supporters of this view point to the request for a guest room (v. 22), which seems more natural from nearby. Still, Paul could make travel plans from Rome as well, and travel between Rome and the province of Asia was common.

Rome is the traditional and most widely accepted location, placing the letter around AD 60–62. The letter itself does not identify the place of imprisonment.

"to the beloved Apphia,"

Apphia was closely connected with Philemon's household and the church that met there, though Scripture does not say how she was related to Philemon. Paul calls her "beloved," a warm word for someone dear to him and to the family of believers. Later in the letter he uses that same word for Onesimus, the returning slave (1:16).

"Archippus our fellow soldier,"

Archippus also appears in Paul's letter to the Colossians, where Paul sends him a personal charge: "Take heed to the ministry which you have received in the Lord, that you may fulfill it" (Colossians 4:17). He was evidently serving the church in some role of ministry. Scripture does not say how he was related to Philemon or Apphia.

Paul calls him a "fellow soldier." Paul used the same title for Epaphroditus, whom he called his "fellow worker, and fellow soldier" (Philippians 2:25). Paul often pictured the Christian life as a soldier's service, telling Timothy to "endure hardship as a good soldier of Jesus Christ" (2 Timothy 2:3). Soldiers serve under one commander, share hardship, and stand together in the fight. Writing from prison, Paul knew that the gospel work cost something, and he honors Archippus as one who stood with him in it.

"and to the church in your house:"

In the New Testament, a "church" is a gathering of believers, not a building. Christians had no church buildings yet, so they met in private homes. Paul mentions "the church that is in their house" of Priscilla and Aquila (Romans 16:5) and the church in the house of Nymphas (Colossians 4:15). Philemon opened his home to the believers at Colossae, which suggests he had a house large enough to host them and the means to support that kind of hospitality.

The word "your" here is singular and points to Philemon. That makes it significant that Paul includes the whole church in the greeting. This letter makes a deeply personal request of one man about his own household, yet it would be read aloud to the believers who gathered in his home. The matter of Onesimus was not simply private business between a master and his slave. It concerned the whole fellowship, because Onesimus was coming back not only to Philemon's house but to the church that met there. These believers would hear Paul's appeal, they would see how Philemon responded, and they would be the family into which Onesimus was welcomed as a brother.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Paul greets them all with “Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.” Everyone in that house church shares one Father and one Lord, and the grace that has made them a family is the ground on which Paul will soon ask Philemon to welcome a new brother.

"Grace to you and peace"

Ordinary Greek letters of the time opened with a simple word of greeting, much like saying "hello." Paul instead uses the word "grace," which sounds similar in Greek, and joins it with "peace," the familiar greeting of the Jewish people (shalom). He opens nearly all his letters this way, but these are more than polite words. Grace is God's undeserved kindness, His favor given to people who have not earned it and could never earn it. It is the reason anyone is saved at all: "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God" (Ephesians 2:8).

Peace is what grace produces. The Hebrew idea behind it is more than the absence of conflict. It means wholeness and well-being, a life set right. Above all, it means being at peace with God: "having been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1). Grace always comes first, and peace follows from it.

The word "you" is plural, so the blessing is spoken over Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, and the whole church that met in Philemon's house (1:2). Everyone listening as the letter was read had received God's undeserved kindness. That matters for what follows, because Paul will ask Philemon to extend that same kind of kindness to Onesimus, who had run away and was returning as a brother in Christ.

"from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."

"Our Father" binds Paul, Timothy, Philemon, and the church together as one family under God, which is why Paul can call fellow believers brothers and sisters (1:1–2). Paul also names the Father and Jesus Christ together, with a single "from," as the one source of grace and peace. Only God can give these gifts, and Paul places Jesus right alongside the Father as their giver.

Calling Jesus "Lord" carries special weight in a letter about a master and his slave. "Lord" means master, the one with rightful authority. In the letter to the Colossians, sent to the same city around the same time and carried in part by Onesimus himself (Colossians 4:9), Paul tells Christian masters to treat their bondservants justly, "knowing that you also have a Master in heaven" (Colossians 4:1). Philemon and Onesimus stood under the same Lord, and both depended on the same grace.

Philemon 1:4 I always thank my God when I pray for you, Philemon, (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul tells Philemon that he thanks “my God” for him, “making mention of you always in my prayers.” Before he says a word about Onesimus, Paul lets Philemon know that he has been carried before God with gratitude.

Verse Explanation

Paul tells Philemon that he thanks “my God” for him, “making mention of you always in my prayers.” Before he says a word about Onesimus, Paul lets Philemon know that he has been carried before God with gratitude.

"I thank my God,"

Paul's thanks go to God, not first to Philemon. The love and faith Paul is about to praise in him (v. 5) are real, but Paul sees them as God's work in Philemon's life, so he thanks the One who produced them. Calling Him "my God" is personal. Paul is not speaking of a distant deity but of the God he knows and serves, the same God he shares with Philemon as a fellow believer. Paul often opened his letters this way. He told the Philippians, "I thank my God upon every remembrance of you" (Philippians 1:3).

From here through verse 21, the "you" Paul addresses is singular in Greek. Having greeted the whole church in Philemon's house, Paul now turns to speak to Philemon himself, though the church still hears what he writes.

"making mention of you always in my prayers,"

To "make mention" of someone in prayer means to name that person before God. Paul used the same kind of language with the believers in Rome, telling them that "without ceasing I make mention of you always in my prayers" (Romans 1:9). "Always" does not mean Paul prayed every waking moment. It means that whenever he prayed, Philemon was included. His friend had a regular place in his prayers, and each time Paul brought him before God, he gave thanks for him.

This was the prayer of a man in chains for Christ. Paul could not travel to Colossae or visit Philemon, but he could still bring him before God, and he did so faithfully. Paul goes on to describe what he asked God to do in Philemon's life (v. 6). The prayer would also go the other way. Near the end of the letter, Paul tells Philemon that he hopes "through your prayers" to be released and come to them (v. 22).

Main Idea

Paul’s thanks rise from what he keeps hearing about Philemon: “your love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints.” Philemon’s faith in the Lord has shown itself in love for God’s people, and that love will soon be asked to make room for one more saint.

Verse Explanation

Paul’s thanks rise from what he keeps hearing about Philemon: “your love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints.” Philemon’s faith in the Lord has shown itself in love for God’s people, and that love will soon be asked to make room for one more saint.

"hearing of your love and faith"

This is why Paul thanks God whenever he prays for Philemon (v. 4): he keeps hearing good reports about him. Paul was in prison and could not visit Colossae, so the news came to him through others. Epaphras, a believer from Colossae, was with Paul at this time (v. 23), and he had already told Paul about the Colossian believers' "love in the Spirit" (Colossians 1:7–8). He may well be one source of what Paul heard about Philemon, though Paul does not say. Philemon's love and faith were evident enough that people far away talked about them.

Paul names love first. Faith is the deeper root, but love is what this letter is about. Paul will soon say that Philemon's love has refreshed the hearts of God's people (v. 7), and he will make his request on the basis of love (v. 9).

"which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints,"

Philemon's faith is directed toward the Lord Jesus, and his love reaches out to God's people. Paul joins the two because they belong together. Trusting Christ shows itself in loving those who belong to Him. Paul elsewhere describes true faith as "faith working through love" (Galatians 5:6), and John puts the two side by side as God's command: "that we should believe on the name of His Son Jesus Christ and love one another" (1 John 3:23). Paul praises the Colossian church in nearly the same words: "your faith in Christ Jesus and of your love for all the saints" (Colossians 1:4).

"Saints" translates a Greek word meaning "holy ones," those set apart for God. In the New Testament it is not a title for an especially devout few. It describes every believer, because everyone who belongs to Christ has been set apart by God as His own. Paul calls whole churches "saints," including congregations with serious problems (1 Corinthians 1:2). So "all the saints" means all of God's people, ordinary believers included.

The word "all" matters in this letter. Philemon's love was not limited to friends or to people of his own social standing. That is the kind of love Paul is about to call on, because Onesimus, Philemon's runaway slave, has now become a believer and is one of the saints too. Paul's request will ask Philemon to show the same love he already shows to God's people to the one person he might be least inclined to welcome.

Study Notes

The Greek word order here is unusual. A close rendering of the Greek is "your love and the faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints," so both qualities seem attached to both objects. "Faith toward all the saints" sounds strange, so most interpreters read the sentence crosswise: love toward the saints, faith toward the Lord. Ancient writers often used this kind of crisscross pattern, called a chiasm. Paul's change of preposition, one word before "the Lord Jesus" and a different one before "all the saints," may support that reading.

The parallel in Colossians, likely written about the same time, puts the pairs in the plain order: "your faith in Christ Jesus and of your love for all the saints" (Colossians 1:4).

A second view takes "faith" here in its other common sense of faithfulness or loyalty, which a believer can show toward fellow Christians as well as toward Christ.

Philemon 1:6
Philemon 1:6
NKJV

Philemon 1:6 that the sharing of your faith may become effective by the acknowledgment of every good thing which is in you in Christ Jesus.(NKJV)

Philemon 1:6 And I am praying that you will put into action the generosity that comes from your faith as you understand and experience all the good things we have in Christ. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul prays “that the sharing of your faith may become effective,” meaning that the fellowship Philemon’s faith has created with other believers would become active and fruitful as he recognizes “every good thing” that is theirs “in Christ Jesus.” In Greek, the word translated “sharing” belongs to the same family as the word translated “partner” in verse 17, so this prayer already leans toward the request Paul is about to make.

Verse Explanation

Paul prays “that the sharing of your faith may become effective,” meaning that the fellowship Philemon’s faith has created with other believers would become active and fruitful as he recognizes “every good thing” that is theirs “in Christ Jesus.” In Greek, the word translated “sharing” belongs to the same family as the word translated “partner” in verse 17, so this prayer already leans toward the request Paul is about to make.

"that the sharing of your faith"

Here Paul tells Philemon what he prays for whenever he mentions him in prayer (v. 4). The word translated "sharing" is the Greek koinonia, often rendered "fellowship." It means having something in common with others and taking part in it together, like partners in a shared work. Paul used the same word when he thanked the Philippians "for your fellowship in the gospel from the first day until now" (Philippians 1:5). So Paul is not mainly talking about Philemon telling others about Jesus, as "sharing your faith" usually means today. He means the partnership Philemon has with other believers because they hold the same faith. Philemon had already shown this partnership through his love for all of God's people and his generosity toward them (vv. 5, 7). Later Paul builds on this bond when he asks Philemon to count him as a "partner" (v. 17).

"may become effective"

The Greek word for "effective" means active or at work, and it gives us our word "energy." Paul uses it elsewhere for "a great and effective door" of opportunity (1 Corinthians 16:9), and Hebrews uses it to describe the word of God as "living and powerful" (Hebrews 4:12). Paul prays that the faith Philemon shares with other believers will not sit still but will go to work in practical ways. Faith that belongs to Christ is meant to act, the kind of "faith working through love" (Galatians 5:6). Paul knows that Philemon will soon face a real test of this, because the letter will ask him to welcome back Onesimus.

"by the acknowledgment of every good thing which is in you"

The word "acknowledgment" means more than knowing facts. It describes a full, personal understanding that grows deeper over time. Paul prayed something similar for the believers at Colossae, Philemon's own city: that they would "be filled with the knowledge of His will" so they would be "fruitful in every good work and increasing in the knowledge of God" (Colossians 1:9–10). The "good thing" here is every blessing God has given His people. Paul says elsewhere that God "has blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ" (Ephesians 1:3). As Philemon understands more fully how much God has given him, that understanding will spill over into how he treats others. A man who knows how much grace he has received will be ready to show grace.

Paul uses the same word "good" again a few verses later, when he hopes Philemon's "good deed" will be done willingly rather than by compulsion (v. 14). The good Philemon has received in Christ is meant to become the good he does.

"in Christ Jesus."

To be "in Christ" means to belong to Him and to be joined to Him by faith. It describes a believer's new identity: "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17). Every good thing a Christian has, including forgiveness, new life, and a place in God's family, comes through this union with Christ. It is also what binds believers to one another. Philemon's partnership with other Christians rests on the fact that they all belong to the same Lord. That same bond now includes Onesimus, who has also come to belong to Christ.

Study Notes

"The sharing of your faith" has three main readings. Some take it as evangelism, Philemon passing his faith on to others, though Paul nowhere else uses koinonia in that sense. Others take it as the fellowship or partnership believers have through their common faith. A third view sees the generous sharing that faith produces, since Paul uses the same word for a financial gift: "to make a certain contribution for the poor among the saints" (Romans 15:26). The second and third readings overlap, and both fit Philemon's known kindness (v. 7) and the request Paul is about to make.

"Acknowledgment" (epignosis) is Paul's usual word for full, personal knowledge. It does not mean other people giving Philemon credit for the good in him.

There is also a textual difference. The Greek text behind the NKJV reads "every good thing which is in you." Many manuscripts, including the early Codex Alexandrinus and Codex Ephraemi, read "in us," and most modern translations follow them, though the oldest, Codex Sinaiticus, reads "in you." The two Greek words differ by a single letter and came to be pronounced almost alike, which made copying slips easy. "In us" would make the good things blessings all believers share, while "in you" focuses on Philemon. The overall sense is not greatly affected.

Verse Explanation

Philemon’s love has brought Paul “great joy and consolation,” because “the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother.” Philemon is known for giving rest and relief to God’s people, and in verse 20 Paul will ask him to do the same once more.

"For we have great joy and consolation in your love,"

"For" ties this verse back to Paul's thanksgiving. He has been thanking God in his prayers because he hears of Philemon's "love and faith" toward the Lord Jesus and toward all God's people (1:4–5). Now he tells Philemon what that news has done for him personally. The word translated "consolation" means comfort or encouragement, the kind that strengthens someone who is going through hardship. Paul was writing as a prisoner for Christ (1:1, 9), and reports of how Philemon loved other believers lifted his spirits in his chains. Paul did not need to be in Colossae to be helped by Philemon's love. Simply hearing about it brought him joy.

"because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you,"

This is why Philemon's love meant so much to Paul. It was not a feeling he kept to himself but something that had done real good for fellow believers. The Greek word for "hearts" literally refers to a person's inner organs. In the ancient world people spoke of these inward parts as the place where deep feelings are felt, much as we speak of the heart today. The word points to a person's inner self and deepest affections. Paul uses it twice more in this short letter. He calls Onesimus "my own heart" (1:12), and he asks Philemon to "refresh my heart in the Lord" (1:20).

The verb "refreshed" means to give rest or relief, like a weary traveler stopping to recover his strength. Jesus used the same verb when He promised, "Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). Philemon had given that kind of rest to God's people, renewing them in their inner lives. Paul does not say exactly how he did it. Since the church met in his home (1:2), many interpreters think his hospitality and generosity toward the believers there were part of it. Whatever form it took, his love had touched people deeply and left them stronger.

"brother."

Paul ends the sentence by calling Philemon "brother," a fellow believer in God's family. The word comes last in the Greek sentence, which gives it extra warmth. Paul speaks to Philemon as a friend he loves, not as an apostle issuing orders. The man who had brought rest to so many of God's people is about to hear Paul's request, and Paul makes it as one brother speaking to another.

Verse Explanation

With “Therefore,” Paul turns from thanksgiving to his appeal. He states plainly that he could be “very bold in Christ to command you what is fitting,” for his authority in Christ is real and what he is about to ask is the right thing to do.

"Therefore,"

This word connects Paul's request to what he has just said about Philemon. Paul has thanked God for Philemon's love and faith toward the Lord Jesus and toward all God's people, and he has told him that "the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother" (v. 7). Philemon had already shown what kind of man he was. Because of that, Paul now turns to the matter that is the reason for the letter.

"though I might be very bold in Christ"

The Greek word behind "bold" describes freedom to speak plainly and openly, without hesitation. Paul is saying he has every right to be direct with Philemon. That right comes "in Christ." It does not rest on Paul's age, his friendship with Philemon, or his force of personality, but on his relationship to Christ and the calling Christ gave him. Paul was an apostle, sent out personally by the risen Lord, "not from men nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father" (Galatians 1:1). As an apostle, he had authority to give churches binding instructions. He did this elsewhere, writing, "We command you, brethren, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (2 Thessalonians 3:6).

That makes the opening of this letter stand out. In the letter to the Colossians, which was carried to the same city at about the same time (Colossians 4:7–9), Paul introduces himself as "an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God" (Colossians 1:1). Here he never uses that title. He calls himself "a prisoner of Christ Jesus" (v. 1). Paul does not deny his authority now. He names it plainly, but only so Philemon will understand that he is choosing not to use it.

"to command you what is fitting,"

"Command" means to give an order as someone with the right to be obeyed. "What is fitting" describes what is proper and right, what ought to be done. Paul uses the same expression when he speaks of conduct that is "fitting in the Lord" (Colossians 3:18). The request he is about to make is not an optional favor or a matter of personal taste. It is what a believer should do, and Paul could have required it.

The sentence does not end here, though. Paul has the authority to command, but he sets it aside and appeals to Philemon instead. He wanted Philemon to do what was right because he chose to, not because he was ordered to. That gives Philemon room to show the same love that had already refreshed so many of God's people.

"yet for love's sake I rather appeal to you—"

In the previous verse Paul said he could be "very bold in Christ" to command Philemon to do what is right (1:8). As an apostle he had that authority, but here he sets it aside. The word "rather" shows his choice: instead of giving an order, he makes a request. The Greek word translated "appeal" means to urge, plead with, or encourage. It is how a friend speaks to a friend, not how a commander speaks to a soldier.

Paul makes his request "for love's sake." Love is the ground he stands on. He has already thanked God for Philemon's "love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints" (1:5), and he has said that he took great joy and comfort in that love, "because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother" (1:7). Philemon was already known for showing love to God's people. Paul now appeals to that same love. He is not asking Philemon to become a different person. He is asking him to keep being the man he has already shown himself to be.

The way Paul asks also fits what he is asking for. A loving response cannot really be forced. If Paul ordered Philemon to show kindness, the kindness would come from obedience to Paul rather than from Philemon's own heart. By choosing to ask in love, Paul treats Philemon as a brother and leaves room for him to respond freely.

"being such a one as Paul, the aged,"

Paul describes himself as an old man. His exact age is not given, but decades earlier, when Stephen was stoned, he was called "a young man named Saul" (Acts 7:58). Since then he had spent years traveling, preaching, and suffering for the gospel. He had been beaten, shipwrecked, and imprisoned many times (2 Corinthians 11:23–27). In the ancient world, as in many cultures today, an older man's request carried weight and called for respect. Paul does not mention his age to win pity. He is asking Philemon to consider who is making this request: an old friend who has given his life to serving Christ.

"and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ—"

Paul is not only old. He is "now also" in chains. He opened the letter by calling himself "a prisoner of Christ Jesus" (1:1), and he repeats it here. Roman guards held him, but he did not see himself as Rome's prisoner. He was there because of his service to Christ, and he understood his chains as part of belonging to his Lord. He used the same description when urging the Ephesians: "I, therefore, the prisoner of the Lord, beseech you to walk worthy of the calling with which you were called" (Ephesians 4:1).

This detail adds tenderness to the appeal. Paul is not writing from comfort or safety, asking Philemon to make a sacrifice he himself has never made. He writes as someone who has already given up his freedom for Christ. An old man in chains, choosing to ask rather than command, makes his request in a way that speaks to Philemon's heart rather than pressuring him.

Study Notes

A small spelling difference stands behind a real translation question. The Greek manuscripts read presbytes, "old man," which the NKJV renders "the aged." A very similar word, presbeutes, means "ambassador." Spelling was not fully standardized at the time, so some scholars, notably J. B. Lightfoot in the nineteenth century, argued that Paul meant "ambassador." The Revised Standard Version adopted that reading, and several modern versions mention it in a footnote. Support for it comes from Ephesians, written at about the same time, where Paul uses the related verb: "I am an ambassador in chains" (Ephesians 6:20). A few ancient texts also use the "old man" spelling for envoys.

The NKJV's rendering, which most translations keep, fits the verse better. Paul has just set aside his right to command (v. 8), and claiming an ambassador's rank would bring that authority right back in. An old man in chains is someone appealing, not someone giving orders. "Aged" need not mean very old by modern standards. Ancient writers could use the word for a man in his fifties, which would suit Paul's likely age at this time.

NLT

Philemon 1:18 If he has wronged you in any way or owes you anything, charge it to me. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul adds, “But if he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account.” Without naming any wrong or debt, Paul offers to have whatever may stand against Onesimus charged to himself, a clear picture of Christ, who bore our sin so that we could be reconciled to God, though only Christ’s sacrifice actually atones for sin.

Verse Explanation

Paul adds, “But if he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account.” Without naming any wrong or debt, Paul offers to have whatever may stand against Onesimus charged to himself, a clear picture of Christ, who bore our sin so that we could be reconciled to God, though only Christ’s sacrifice actually atones for sin.

"But if he has wronged you or owes anything,"

Paul speaks of two possibilities: Onesimus may have wronged Philemon or may owe him something. The first concerns harm or injustice, and the second concerns an obligation to repay. The verse leaves both possibilities open.

The word “if” matters. Paul leaves the facts of any claim open and offers to bear the cost himself. His offer is part of a personal appeal that the congregation will hear.

"put that on my account."

This is the language of business records. The Greek verb means to charge something to someone's account, the way a merchant writes a debt under a customer's name. Paul asks Philemon to charge him for whatever Onesimus may owe. He makes the promise in his own hand in the next verse. Paul offers to take away any such obstacle so that nothing would stand in the way of Philemon receiving Onesimus as a brother.

Paul’s offer gives a picture of Christ’s work. We stood before God guilty, with a debt of sin we could never repay. Christ bore our sins and brought us to God. “For Christ also suffered once for sins, the just for the unjust, that He might bring us to God” (1 Peter 3:18). Paul offers to meet whatever claim Philemon may have. Christ actually bore our sins and paid the cost with His life.

He then adds a pointed reminder that “you owe me even your own self besides,” most likely because Philemon came to faith through Paul’s ministry, so any debt Onesimus may owe is small beside what Philemon has received.

"I, Paul, am writing with my own hand."

Paul usually dictated his letters to a helper who wrote them down. One of them, Tertius, even added his own greeting in Romans (Romans 16:22). Paul would often take the pen at the end to add a greeting in his own handwriting as a mark that the letter was genuinely his (2 Thessalonians 3:17). Here his handwriting does more than confirm who wrote the letter. In the ancient world, a statement of debt written in a person's own hand was a binding promise to pay, much like a signed IOU today. By writing these words himself, Paul turns the offer he just made, "put that on my account" (v. 18), into a commitment Philemon could hold him to.

"I will repay—"

The Greek word for "repay" was used for paying damages or making good on a loss. Paul does not simply ask Philemon to overlook a possible loss. Paul offers to cover whatever loss Philemon may have suffered so that nothing stands in the way of Philemon receiving him.

Paul’s handwritten pledge illustrates Christ’s costly work of reconciliation. Paul stood ready to cover any wrong or debt so that Onesimus could be welcomed. Christ gave His own life to bring guilty people to God (1 Peter 3:18).

"not to mention to you that you owe me even your own self besides."

Paul says he will "not mention" something, and then mentions it, a gentle way of reminding Philemon of something they both knew. Most likely Philemon came to faith through Paul's ministry, perhaps during Paul's long stay in Ephesus, when "all who dwelt in Asia heard the word of the Lord Jesus" (Acts 19:10). Paul had never visited Colossae himself (Colossians 2:1), so Philemon probably met him elsewhere. If Philemon heard the gospel from Paul, then he owed Paul far more than money. He owed him the message through which he received eternal life.

Paul places any amount Onesimus may owe beside what Philemon has received through Paul’s ministry. His written promise remains in force. The reminder gives Philemon a reason to act generously. Jesus’ parable of the unforgiving servant makes a related point about extending the mercy one has received (Matthew 18:21–35).

Verse Explanation

Calling him “brother” again, Paul asks, “let me have joy from you in the Lord; refresh my heart in the Lord.” Philemon has refreshed the hearts of the saints (v. 7), and Paul now asks him to refresh his own heart in the same way, by welcoming the one Paul has called “my own heart.”

"Yes, brother,"

"Yes" carries Paul's appeal to its final, warm request. He has just offered to repay whatever Onesimus owes and gently reminded Philemon that he owes Paul "even your own self besides" (1:19). Now he speaks as family. "Brother" marks Philemon as a fellow believer, and it is the same word Paul used when he praised the way Philemon had refreshed God's people (1:7). Paul is not pulling rank. He is speaking to a brother he loves about a brother they now share.

"let me have joy from you in the Lord;"

The Greek verb behind "let me have joy" means to receive benefit or delight from someone. It comes from the same root as the name Onesimus, which means "useful" or "profitable." Paul seems to be gently playing on the name again. He is asking Philemon to let him enjoy the benefit of this request: in receiving the useful one, Philemon will be useful to Paul. After mentioning the debt Philemon owes him, Paul asks for repayment in only one form, the joy of seeing Onesimus welcomed.

"In the Lord" shows what kind of joy Paul wants. He is not asking a personal favor for his own comfort. He is asking for the joy that comes when believers who belong to Christ treat one another as Christ would have them do.

"refresh my heart in the Lord."

This request repeats the words Paul used to praise Philemon earlier: "the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother" (1:7). The word "refresh" means to give rest or relief, like a weary traveler finally able to stop and recover. Philemon was known for bringing that kind of relief to other believers. Now Paul, sitting in chains for Christ, asks Philemon to do for him what he has already done for so many others.

The word "heart" here refers to the inner self, the place of deepest affection, and Paul has already used it for Onesimus. When he sent him back, he called him "my own heart" (1:12). Since Onesimus is so closely bound to Paul's affections, Philemon cannot refresh Paul's heart while turning Onesimus away. The way to give Paul rest is to receive Onesimus kindly. Philemon's welcome to his returning slave would reach all the way to a prison cell and lift the spirit of the apostle who loved them both.

Verse Explanation

Paul writes “having confidence in your obedience,” a word that shows his appeal still carries apostolic weight, yet he expects more than bare compliance: Philemon “will do even more than I say.” Paul may be hinting that Onesimus could be freed or sent back to help him (compare v. 13), but the letter leaves that “more” unnamed.

"Having confidence in your obedience, I write to you,"

Paul has spent this letter appealing rather than commanding. He said he could have been "very bold in Christ to command you what is fitting, yet for love's sake I rather appeal to you" (vv. 8–9), and he refused to keep Onesimus without Philemon's consent, so that his good deed would not be "by compulsion, as it were, but voluntary" (v. 14). Now, near the end, Paul uses the word "obedience." He is not quietly taking back his gentle tone. The obedience he has in mind is a willing response to what is right in the Lord. Paul had set aside his authority as an apostle, but what he asked still carried real moral weight, and he trusted Philemon to recognize it and act on it.

Paul's confidence was not flattery. He had heard of Philemon's "love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints" (v. 5), and he knew Philemon had refreshed the hearts of God's people (v. 7). A man with that kind of record could be trusted to do what love required. By putting his confidence in writing, Paul also let Philemon know what he expected. The whole church meeting in his house would hear the letter, so his response would be seen by fellow believers who knew what Paul had asked.

"knowing that you will do even more than I say."

Paul has asked Philemon to receive Onesimus as he would receive Paul himself (v. 17) and to charge any wrong Onesimus may have done to Paul's account (v. 18). Now Paul says he expects Philemon to go beyond even that. He does not explain what "more" means. Many interpreters think Paul was hoping Philemon would set Onesimus free, especially since Paul had already asked him to receive Onesimus "no longer as a slave but more than a slave—a beloved brother" (v. 16). Others suggest Paul hoped Philemon would send Onesimus back to help him, since Paul had wanted to keep him for service during his imprisonment (v. 13). Either is possible, but Paul does not say which he meant.

By leaving the "more" unnamed, Paul stays consistent with the rest of the letter. He has already given up commanding, so he does not now spell out a further demand. He leaves room for Philemon to decide for himself how far his love will go. Paul's confidence rests on the kind of man Philemon already was: someone who would not settle for the bare minimum when a brother in Christ was involved.

"But, meanwhile, also prepare a guest room for me,"

Paul has finished his appeal for Onesimus, and now he adds a personal request. "Meanwhile" means at the same time: while Philemon acts on what Paul has asked, he should also get a room ready for Paul. The Greek word for "guest room" means lodging or hospitality, a place to stay as a guest. Early Christians were expected to welcome traveling believers into their homes (Romans 12:13), and Philemon already opened his house to the church and had "refreshed" the hearts of God's people (v. 7). Paul could ask this of him as easily as one friend asks another.

The request also carries a gentle weight. If Paul came to visit, he would see for himself how Philemon had welcomed Onesimus. He does not say this as a threat. He has already said he is confident Philemon will do even more than he asks (v. 21). But the prospect of seeing Paul face to face would give Philemon every reason to follow through gladly.

"for I trust that through your prayers I shall be granted to you."

"I trust" translates a Greek word meaning "I hope." Paul expected to be released from his chains for Christ, but he did not claim to know it for certain. He spoke the same way in Philippians: "I trust in the Lord that I myself shall also come shortly" (Philippians 2:24). Scripture does not tell us whether Paul ever made this visit to Philemon.

In Greek, both "your" and "you" are plural here, even though the command to prepare a room is spoken to Philemon alone. Paul is counting on the prayers of the whole church in Philemon's house. Early in the letter he told Philemon that he remembered him in his own prayers (v. 4). Now he asks the church to pray for him. Paul believed God works through His people's prayers. He wrote to the Philippians that his situation would "turn out for my deliverance through your prayer and the supply of the Spirit of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:19).

The word "granted" means given freely as a gift, and it comes from the same root as "grace," God's undeserved kindness. If Paul was released, it would not be because Rome owed him freedom. It would be God's gift, given to the church in answer to their prayers. The believers would receive Paul back as a gift from God, much as Philemon was being asked to receive Onesimus back.

Verse Explanation

Epaphras, whom Paul calls “my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus,” sends his greetings to Philemon. Colossians 4:12 calls Epaphras “one of you,” so he most likely came from Philemon’s own community, and Paul honors him as one who shares his captivity for Christ’s sake.

"Epaphras,"

Epaphras was a Colossian, "one of you," as Paul told that church (Colossians 4:12). He seems to have been the one who first brought the gospel to Colossae, since Paul reminded the believers there that they had heard the truth "as you also learned from Epaphras, our dear fellow servant, who is a faithful minister of Christ on your behalf" (Colossians 1:7). He also cared for the believers in the nearby towns of Laodicea and Hierapolis (Colossians 4:13). Even while away from home, he kept "laboring fervently for you in prayers, that you may stand perfect and complete in all the will of God" (Colossians 4:12). Philemon and the church in his house would have known Epaphras well, and some of them likely first heard about Jesus from him.

"my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus,"

The Greek word for "fellow prisoner" literally describes a fellow captive, someone taken prisoner in war. Paul uses the same word for Aristarchus (Colossians 4:10) and for Andronicus and Junia (Romans 16:7). Scripture does not say whether Epaphras had been arrested himself or had chosen to share Paul's confinement so he could stay with him. Either way, he was suffering alongside Paul for the same reason. Paul calls himself "a prisoner of Christ Jesus" (v. 1), and Epaphras shared those chains "in Christ Jesus," because he belonged to Christ and served Him. The picture of a fellow captive also fits the soldier language Paul used for Archippus, "our fellow soldier" (v. 2). These men were in the same fight, and partnership in the gospel cost them more than effort. It cost them their freedom.

"greets you,"

The "you" here is singular in Greek, so Epaphras sends his greeting to Philemon personally. Many interpreters see more than courtesy in it. Epaphras was a respected teacher from Philemon's own church, and his greeting comes at the end of a letter asking Philemon to welcome back Onesimus, his runaway slave, as a brother. When Philemon heard the letter read, he would know that Epaphras stood with Paul, and that this man he trusted shared Paul's love for the new believer now returning home.

"as do Mark,"

These men join Epaphras in sending greetings to Philemon (v. 23). The same group sends greetings at the end of Colossians (Colossians 4:10–14), the letter that went to the same town with Onesimus on the same journey (Colossians 4:9).

Mark is John Mark, "the cousin of Barnabas" (Colossians 4:10). Years earlier he had abandoned Paul and Barnabas partway through their first missionary journey (Acts 13:13). When Barnabas later wanted to take him along again, Paul refused, and the disagreement was so sharp that the two men parted ways (Acts 15:37–39). Now Mark is back at Paul's side in prison, and Paul had already told the Colossians to welcome him if he came (Colossians 4:10). Near the end of his life Paul wrote, "Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for ministry" (2 Timothy 4:11). The Greek word for "useful" there is the same word Paul uses for Onesimus, who "once was unprofitable to you, but now is profitable to you and to me" (v. 11). Philemon was being asked to welcome back the man who had run away from his household, and Mark himself showed that such a person could become a valued servant of Christ.

"Aristarchus,"

Aristarchus came from Thessalonica in Macedonia (Acts 20:4; 27:2). He was with Paul in Ephesus when a mob dragged him into the theater during the riot over the goddess Diana (Acts 19:29), and he sailed with Paul on the dangerous voyage to Rome (Acts 27:2). In Colossians Paul calls him "my fellow prisoner" (Colossians 4:10). He had stayed with Paul through mobs, shipwreck, and chains.

"Demas,"

Demas is also named among those greeting the Colossians (Colossians 4:14), and here he is counted as one of Paul's fellow laborers. Sadly, his story did not end well. Paul later wrote, "Demas has forsaken me, having loved this present world, and has departed for Thessalonica" (2 Timothy 4:10). Mark had once turned back and later proved faithful. Demas served faithfully for a time and later turned away.

"Luke,"

Paul calls him "the beloved physician" (Colossians 4:14). Luke wrote the Gospel that bears his name and the book of Acts, and in Acts he often says "we" when describing Paul's travels, showing that he was there himself, including the voyage to Rome (Acts 27:1). He stayed with Paul to the end. In his final imprisonment Paul wrote, "Only Luke is with me" (2 Timothy 4:11).

"my fellow laborers."

Paul does not describe these men as his assistants or followers but as workers alongside him in the gospel. He used the same title for Philemon in the opening of the letter (v. 1). Philemon was being asked to act as a member of this same team, and Onesimus, now a believer, was joining that company as well.

Verse Explanation

Paul ends with a blessing: “The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Amen.” In Greek this “your” is plural, so the grace that opened the letter (v. 3) now rests on the whole church, and that same grace makes possible the welcome Paul has asked for.

"The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ"

Paul opened the letter with "Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ" (v. 3), and now he closes it the same way. Grace is God's undeserved kindness, His favor given to people who have not earned it and could never repay it. Everything Paul asked of Philemon lies between these two mentions of grace. Paul asked him to forgive, to welcome back the man who had run away, and to treat that man as a beloved brother. That request makes sense only among people who have already received grace themselves. Philemon had been forgiven a debt he could never pay, and Christ bore the cost of his sin so that he could be reconciled to God. Now Paul prays for that same grace to stay with him as he decides how to receive Onesimus.

The word "our" matters here. Paul, Timothy, Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, the church in Philemon's house, and now Onesimus all share one Lord. Philemon was Onesimus's earthly master, but both of them answered to the same Master. In the letter to the Colossians, likely written about the same time, Paul told masters to treat their bondservants justly, "knowing that you also have a Master in heaven" (Colossians 4:1). The grace Philemon depends on comes from the Lord who rules over him as well as over Onesimus.

"be with your spirit."

In Greek, "your" is plural here. Paul has addressed Philemon personally through most of the letter, but this closing blessing includes the whole church that would hear it read. The "spirit" is a person's inner life, the part of a person where God meets, strengthens, and changes them. Paul prays that Christ's grace will be at work deep inside each of them, not only governing their outward behavior. Paul ends his letter to the Galatians with the same blessing: "Brethren, the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Amen" (Galatians 6:18).

"Amen."

"Amen" is a Hebrew word meaning "so be it" or "truly." It confirms the prayer as something Paul wholeheartedly wants and trusts God to do. When the letter was read aloud in Philemon's house, the believers gathered there could add their own "Amen" and join Paul in asking for that grace.
