Chapter Study

Philemon 1

Verse-by-Verse
Philemon 1:1

NKJV

Philemon 1:1 Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, and Timothy our brother, To Philemon our beloved friend and fellow laborer,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:1 This letter is from Paul, a prisoner for preaching the Good News about Christ Jesus, and from our brother Timothy. I am writing to Philemon, our beloved co-worker, (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul identifies himself as a prisoner of Christ Jesus rather than by his apostolic title, and he greets Philemon as a beloved friend and fellow laborer. The opening names the relationships on which Paul's appeal will rest.

Verse Explanation

“Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus,”

In most of his letters Paul introduces himself as an apostle. Philemon is the only letter in which he opens by calling himself a prisoner. He was in custody as he wrote (vv. 9, 10, 13), yet he does not call himself Rome's prisoner. He is the prisoner “of Christ Jesus.” He belongs to Christ, and his chains are the result of serving Christ and the gospel (v. 13).

The choice suits the letter. Paul is about to ask Philemon to set aside rights the law gave him over Onesimus. He does not open by asserting his rank. He comes as a man who has already given up his own freedom for Christ's sake, and he will mention his chains again when he makes his appeal (v. 9).

“and Timothy our brother,”

Timothy was Paul's longtime coworker and is named in the opening of several of his letters (2 Corinthians 1:1; Philippians 1:1; Colossians 1:1). “Our brother” introduces the family language that runs through the letter. Paul will call Philemon “brother” (vv. 7, 20) and ask him to receive Onesimus as “a beloved brother” (v. 16).

“To Philemon our beloved friend and fellow laborer,”

“Beloved” describes Philemon as someone dearly loved. Paul will give the same word to Onesimus in verse 16, so master and slave are both called “beloved” in the same letter.

“Fellow laborer” means coworker, a word Paul uses for those who share in the work of the gospel (Romans 16:3; Philippians 4:3). The letter does not say that Philemon preached or traveled. What it does describe is a home opened to the church (v. 2) and a love that refreshed the saints (v. 7). Paul counts that service as labor alongside his own. The partnership he names here prepares for his request in verse 17: “If then you count me as a partner, receive him.”

Study Notes

Paul alone writes. Timothy shares the greeting, but from verse 4 onward the letter speaks with Paul's “I,” and Paul names himself again in verses 9 and 19. The appeal is Paul's.

“Prisoner.” The Greek word (desmios) means “one who is bound.” Paul refers to his imprisonment throughout the letter (vv. 1, 9, 10, 13) and calls Epaphras his “fellow prisoner” (v. 23). If he wrote from Rome, as most interpreters hold, he lived under guard in rented quarters, bound with a chain yet free to receive visitors (Acts 28:16, 20, 30-31). Those conditions explain how Onesimus could come to him and serve him (v. 13). Ephesus and Caesarea have also been proposed as the place of writing.

“Beloved friend.” The Greek reads simply “the beloved.” The NKJV supplies “friend” for natural English.

Spirit & Life

Paul was in Roman custody, yet he calls himself “a prisoner of Christ Jesus.” He does not deny the chain. He names the One he belongs to, and that changes what the confinement means. A prisoner of Rome is a man whose life has been interrupted. A prisoner of Christ is still in his Master's service. Rome held the keys, but Christ remained Lord of Paul's life, and Paul could serve Him in chains as truly as on the road. Paul uses the same title when he urges the Ephesians to walk worthy of their calling (Ephesians 4:1). His chains did not end his ministry. Christ worked through him in them. Believers face their own seasons of confinement, such as illness, caregiving, limited means, or closed doors. Not every restriction comes with an explanation, but none of them changes whose we are. Those who belong to Christ remain His, and He can still work through them where they are.

Paul then calls Philemon a “fellow laborer,” though nothing suggests Philemon preached or traveled. His labor was a home opened to God's people and a love that refreshed them. Paul counts that as work alongside his own. “We are God's fellow workers” (1 Corinthians 3:9). Callings differ, and each one “will receive his own reward according to his own labor” (1 Corinthians 3:8). Yet God values faithful service whether many see it or almost no one does. The believer who opens a home and the apostle who plants churches serve the same Lord in the same field.

Philemon 1:2

NKJV

Philemon 1:2 to the beloved Apphia, Archippus our fellow soldier, and to the church in your house:(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:2 and to our sister Apphia, and to our fellow soldier Archippus, and to the church that meets in your house. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul also addresses Apphia, Archippus, and the church that meets in Philemon's house. The request is personal, but the whole congregation will hear it.

Verse Explanation

“to the beloved Apphia,”

Apphia was a woman closely connected with the household. Many interpreters take her to be Philemon's wife. That is possible, but the letter does not say so. Paul greets her by name as a beloved believer. The matter of Onesimus concerned the whole household, and Paul includes her rather than treating it as Philemon's business alone.

“Archippus our fellow soldier,”

Archippus appears again in Colossians, where Paul tells the church, “say to Archippus, ‘Take heed to the ministry which you have received in the Lord, that you may fulfill it’” (Colossians 4:17). He evidently held a ministry among these believers. “Fellow soldier” pictures believers who endure hardship and struggle together for the gospel. Some suggest Archippus was a member of Philemon's family, perhaps his son, but the letter does not say.

“and to the church in your house:”

The “your” is singular and most likely refers to Philemon, the first person addressed. Early Christians commonly met in homes (Romans 16:5; 1 Corinthians 16:19; Colossians 4:15). There is no clear evidence of buildings set apart for Christian worship until long after the New Testament period.

By addressing the church, Paul ensures that the congregation will hear his request. The body of the letter speaks to Philemon personally, but the request is made in the presence of the believers who worship in his house. Onesimus would be returning to that same household, and how he was received would be seen by the whole church. Paul's request concerns their life together, not only a private arrangement between two men.

Study Notes

“Beloved” or “sister.” The NKJV's “the beloved Apphia” follows the Greek text behind it. The earliest manuscripts generally read “the sister,” and most modern translations follow them (NLT “our sister Apphia”). Either reading presents Apphia as a fellow believer held in affection.

Apphia's place in the household. Apphia is a native Phrygian name found in inscriptions from the region around Colossae, which fits the letter's likely setting. If she was Philemon's wife, she may have overseen the household slaves day to day, as the mistress of a household often did. Onesimus's return would then affect her as directly as anyone.

Whose house? Because Archippus is named just before “your house,” a minority of interpreters have argued that the house, and Onesimus, belonged to Archippus. Most take “your” to refer to Philemon, the chief addressee, whose love and hospitality the following verses describe (vv. 5-7).

“Fellow soldier.” The Greek word (systratiōtēs) occurs elsewhere in the New Testament only of Epaphroditus (Philippians 2:25). Archippus should not be confused with Aristarchus (v. 24).

Spirit & Life

The church that met in Philemon's house did not gather in a building set apart from ordinary life. It met in a home, where people ate, worked, rested, and served. The same rooms that heard prayers and Scripture on the day the church gathered saw the routines of the household the rest of the week. That is a picture of what God wants. He desires to dwell with His people, and His presence is not meant to stay in the hour of worship. The Lord who was honored when the saints gathered in Philemon's house was also Lord of every room in it and every relationship under its roof. Whatever is true of Christ in our gatherings is meant to become true in our homes.

Paul also names each person: Apphia, Archippus, and the church. He does not write as though the matter belonged to Philemon alone. God's family is made of people with names, and what happens in one household touches everyone who shares its life. When God works to restore a relationship, He has every person it affects in view.

Philemon 1:3

NKJV

Philemon 1:3 Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:3 May God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ give you grace and peace. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul speaks grace and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ over the whole congregation. Grace opens the letter before any difficulty is named.

Verse Explanation

“Grace to you and peace”

This is Paul's usual greeting, but it is not an empty formula. “Grace” is God's free and undeserved favor, the kindness He shows to those who have not earned it. “Peace” is the wholeness and right relationship that grace brings, first with God and then with one another.

The “you” is plural. Paul speaks grace and peace over the whole congregation in Philemon's house. Both words suit what follows. The letter will ask for a relationship in this household to be restored, and that kind of peace begins with grace received from God.

“from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.”

Grace and peace come from the Father and from the Lord Jesus Christ together. “Our Father” also places everyone in the house church in one family under one Father. That family now includes Onesimus, whom Paul will call “a beloved brother” (v. 16).

Grace opens the letter here and closes it in verse 25. Everything Paul asks for in between depends on it.

Study Notes

One source of grace. A single Greek preposition (“from”) governs both “God our Father” and “the Lord Jesus Christ,” so Paul names the Father and the Son together as the source of grace and peace. The construction does not by itself prove Christ's deity. It is, however, fully consistent with the New Testament's teaching that Jesus is God (John 1:1), in whom “dwells all the fullness of the Godhead bodily” (Colossians 2:9).

Greek and Hebrew greetings. Ordinary Greek letters opened with chairein, “greetings.” Paul uses the related word charis, “grace,” and joins it with “peace,” the familiar Hebrew blessing shalom. He takes the customary greeting and fills it with the gospel.

Spirit & Life

Paul has a delicate request to make, yet before he mentions it he speaks grace and peace over the whole household. The order matters. Grace is not something Philemon will earn by responding well. It is where everything begins. Before God asks anything of His people, He gives. And the grace He gives does more than forgive. “The grace of God that brings salvation has appeared to all men, teaching us that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly in the present age” (Titus 2:11-12). Grace teaches and enables the obedience God asks for. What Paul will ask of Philemon can be met only by drawing on the grace spoken over him at the start.

Peace between people is not first something we produce. It is something we receive together from God before we can extend it to one another. Christ “Himself is our peace,” who made two into one and broke down the wall that divided them (Ephesians 2:14). Grace does not make reconciliation careless. Restoring a broken relationship may still require honest words, accountability, restitution, and time, and Paul himself will deal openly with any wrong or debt (v. 18). But grace changes the ground on which those things are done. People who stand together under the same grace can tell the truth without seeking to crush and can set things right without seeking revenge. And “God our Father” reminds the household who they are. Before they are host, guest, master, or slave, they are children of one Father. Every other relationship under the roof answers to that one.

Philemon 1:4

NKJV

Philemon 1:4 I thank my God, making mention of you always in my prayers,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:4 I always thank my God when I pray for you, Philemon, (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul thanks God for Philemon and remembers him constantly in prayer. The appeal that follows comes from a man who has been bringing Philemon before God.

Verse Explanation

“I thank my God,”

Paul now writes in his own voice. As in most of his letters, the greeting is followed by thanksgiving (Romans 1:8; Philippians 1:3; Colossians 1:3). He thanks God, not Philemon, for what he is about to describe. Philemon's love and faith are the work of God's grace in him, so the thanks go to the Giver. “My God” is personal. Paul speaks of the God he knows and serves.

“making mention of you always in my prayers,”

Paul brings Philemon before God by name. “Always” expresses persistent, regular remembrance: Philemon has a settled place in Paul's prayers, though Paul is not claiming to have named him in every individual prayer. The appeal Paul will make in verse 10 does not come from a stranger. It comes from someone who has been thanking God for Philemon and praying for him. Verse 6 gives the content of that prayer.

Study Notes

Singular “you.” From here through verse 21, “you” and “your” are singular in Greek, addressed to Philemon himself, with one exception. In verse 6, “your faith” is singular, but “in you” is plural in the Greek text behind the NKJV (see the note on verse 6). The plural returns in verse 22 (“your prayers”) and verse 25 (“your spirit”). The church listens in, but the request is put to one man, who must answer it himself.

A thanksgiving that introduces the letter. Paul's thanksgivings often anticipate the themes of the letters they open. Here the love (vv. 5, 7), the fellowship (v. 6), the “good” (v. 6), and the refreshing of hearts (v. 7) all return in the appeal (vv. 9, 14, 17, 20).

Spirit & Life

Paul thanks God for Philemon before he asks Philemon for anything. Thanksgiving changes how we see people. When we regularly thank God for someone, we begin to see that person as God's work rather than as a means to our own ends. The good we notice in them becomes a reason to praise God, not a resource to use.

Paul also brings Philemon before God by name. A difficult request lands differently when it comes from someone who has been praying for us, and it is carried differently in the heart of the one who has been praying. Before Jesus told Peter he would fail, He had already prayed for him: “I have prayed for you, that your faith should not fail” (Luke 22:32). The person we may one day need to correct, confront, or ask something hard of should first be someone we have carried before God.

Philemon 1:5

NKJV

Philemon 1:5 hearing of your love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:5 because I keep hearing about your faith in the Lord Jesus and your love for all of God’s people. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul has heard of Philemon's love and faith toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints. This known love for all the saints becomes the ground of the appeal that follows.

Verse Explanation

“hearing of your love and faith”

Paul's thanksgiving rests on reports about Philemon. If Philemon lived in Colossae, as seems likely, the reports probably came through Epaphras, who had told Paul of the Colossian believers' love (Colossians 1:7-8; 4:12).

Paul names love before faith. When he describes the Colossian and Ephesian believers, he names faith first (Colossians 1:4; Ephesians 1:15). Putting love first fits a letter whose appeal will be made “for love's sake” (v. 9).

“which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints,”

The wording is arranged crosswise: love, faith, the Lord Jesus, all the saints. The most natural reading pairs the inner terms and the outer terms. Faith is directed toward the Lord Jesus, and love reaches out to all the saints. Paul's similar statements in Colossians 1:4 and Ephesians 1:15 make this pairing explicit. Still, the two cannot be neatly separated. Faith in Christ works through love (Galatians 5:6), and love for the saints flows from faith in their Lord.

“The saints” are all believers, people set apart to God through Christ. Philemon's love reached “all” of them. That word matters as the letter unfolds, because Onesimus is now one of the saints too.

Study Notes

Two prepositions. Paul uses different words for “toward”: pros with the Lord Jesus and eis with the saints. Some see a distinction between faith directed to Christ and love reaching out to people. The difference should not be pressed, since both words can mean “toward.”

“Faith” or “faithfulness.” The Greek word pistis can also mean faithfulness. Some interpreters read the phrase as Philemon's love and faithfulness toward both Christ and the saints, which removes the crosswise pairing. The parallel statements in Colossians 1:4 and Ephesians 1:15 favor the crosswise reading.

Spirit & Life

Paul heard of Philemon's love before he mentions his faith. Love is what others see. Faith lives in the heart, but love shows itself at the table, in an open door, and in how a person treats others. The wording of the verse places faith in the Lord Jesus at the center, with love for the saints around it. The root is hidden; the fruit is visible. People usually hear of our love before they learn of our faith, and genuine love leads them back to the Lord who is its source. “By this all will know that you are My disciples, if you have love for one another” (John 13:35).

Philemon's love reached “all the saints.” Love for all the saints cannot choose which saints count. God decides who belongs to His family, and when He brings someone in, our love is called to make room. In Philemon's own household, the word “all” is about to be tested.

Philemon 1:6

NKJV

Philemon 1:6 that the sharing of your faith may become effective by the acknowledgment of every good thing which is in you in Christ Jesus.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:6 And I am praying that you will put into action the generosity that comes from your faith as you understand and experience all the good things we have in Christ. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul prays that Philemon's participation in the shared life of faith will become effective through a fuller recognition of every good thing God has given His people in Christ Jesus.

Verse Explanation

Verse 6 gives the content of the prayer Paul mentioned in verse 4. It is one of the most difficult verses in Paul's letters to translate, but its main direction is clear: Paul prays that Philemon's fellowship with other believers will become active and fruitful.

“that the sharing of your faith”

“Sharing” translates koinonia, a word for fellowship, participation, and partnership: having something in common with others. “The sharing of your faith” most likely refers to Philemon's participation with other believers in the common life that faith brings. That fellowship was more than a feeling. Paul often uses this word for practical sharing, including gifts to believers in need (Romans 15:26; 2 Corinthians 9:13), and verse 7 shows that Philemon's fellowship had already refreshed the saints.

In modern English, “sharing your faith” usually means telling others about Christ. That is not the main sense here. Paul is speaking of the fellowship believers have with one another in Christ. The same word family appears in verse 17: “If then you count me as a partner, receive him.” The fellowship Paul prays for here is the fellowship he will appeal to there.

“may become effective”

“Effective” means active, working, producing results. Paul prays that Philemon's fellowship will not remain a reputation or a good intention but will show itself in action. The letter will soon name a specific way it can.

“by the acknowledgment of every good thing which is in you in Christ Jesus.”

“Acknowledgment” translates a word for full knowledge, a knowing that comes through recognition and experience, not just information. “Every good thing” refers to the blessings God has given His people in Christ. Paul links the effectiveness of Philemon's fellowship with this fuller recognition, though the Greek is compressed and the exact connection is debated. The NKJV's “by” presents the recognition as the means through which fellowship becomes effective. Others read the phrase as the result, a fellowship that becomes effective in deepening this knowledge. Either way, a growing grasp of God's goodness in Christ and fellowship that shows itself in action belong together.

The word “good” returns in verse 14, where Paul wants Philemon's “good deed” to be voluntary. The good God has placed in Philemon in Christ is meant to become the good he freely does.

Study Notes

Interpreting “the sharing of your faith.” Three main readings have been proposed. (1) The fellowship with other believers that faith produces, expressed in practical love. This fits Paul's use of koinonia and the link to “partner” in verse 17. (2) Generosity specifically, the material sharing that springs from faith; the NLT renders the phrase in terms of generosity. This is close to the first reading with a narrower focus. (3) Communicating the faith to others. This matches the modern English phrase but not Paul's usual use of koinonia, which describes participation and sharing rather than proclamation. The first reading best explains both the verse and the letter.

“In you” or “in us.” The NKJV's “in you” follows the Greek text behind it, where “you” is plural, unlike the singular “your faith” earlier in the verse. Many manuscripts read “in us,” also plural, followed by most modern translations. Neither reading refers to Philemon alone. Either way, the phrase refers to the good God gives believers in Christ.

“In Christ Jesus.” The Greek preposition here (eis) normally means “into” or “toward.” Several translations render it “for the sake of Christ.” On this reading, the good believers possess is directed toward Christ and His glory.

“Effective” and “acknowledgment.” “Effective” (energēs) describes “a great and effective door” (1 Corinthians 16:9) and God's Word as “living and powerful” (Hebrews 4:12). “Acknowledgment” (epignōsis) is the word Paul uses in Colossians, sent with this letter, when he prays that the believers will be “filled with the knowledge of His will” (Colossians 1:9).

Spirit & Life

Paul prays for “the acknowledgment of every good thing” that God's people have in Christ Jesus. Whatever the precise grammar of the verse, the phrase points to a truth found throughout Scripture: many believers live far below what God has given them. The gifts are not missing; they simply have not been recognized. In the same way, Paul prays elsewhere that the eyes of believers' understanding will be enlightened, so that they may know “the riches of the glory of His inheritance in the saints” (Ephesians 1:18).

That recognition is not meant to stay private. Knowing God's goodness and sharing it belong together. A believer who grasps how much he has received in Christ finds it natural to open his hand to others. In the sharing, he comes to know the Giver more deeply. Faith becomes effective, working and fruitful, when what God has placed in us flows out to His people.

Philemon 1:7

NKJV

Philemon 1:7 For we have great joy and consolation in your love, because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:7 Your love has given me much joy and comfort, my brother, for your kindness has often refreshed the hearts of God’s people. (NLT)

Main Idea

Philemon's love has brought Paul great joy and encouragement, because through him the hearts of the saints have been refreshed. Paul will soon ask Philemon to refresh his own heart in the same way (v. 20).

Verse Explanation

“For we have great joy and consolation in your love,”

“For” explains why Paul gives thanks. News of Philemon's love has brought him great joy and “consolation,” which means encouragement or comfort. Reports of that love reached Paul in his imprisonment and encouraged him there. Love shown in one place strengthened a believer in another.

“because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you,”

“Hearts” translates a Greek word for the inner organs, which were regarded as the seat of deep feeling and affection. Paul uses this word three times in the letter. Here the saints' hearts have been refreshed; in verse 12 Paul calls Onesimus “my own heart”; and in verse 20 he asks Philemon, “refresh my heart in the Lord.”

“Refreshed” means given rest or relief. Jesus used the same verb when He said, “Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest” (Matthew 11:28). Paul does not say exactly how Philemon refreshed the saints. It may have included hospitality to the church in his house (v. 2) and to traveling believers, but the text leaves the details open. The result is clear: believers found rest and relief through him.

“brother.”

Paul ends with a single word of affection, and in Greek it stands last in the sentence for emphasis. He will use it again when he presses his request, “Yes, brother” (v. 20), and he will ask Philemon to see Onesimus the same way (v. 16).

Study Notes

“We have” or “I had.” The NKJV's “we have” (plural, present tense) follows the Greek text behind it. Other manuscripts read “I had” (singular, past tense), pointing to the joy Paul felt when he first heard of Philemon's love.

“Hearts.” The Greek word (splanchna) literally means the inward parts, which the KJV renders “bowels.” A related verb describes Jesus being “moved with compassion” for the crowds (Matthew 9:36; 14:14).

“Refreshed.” Paul uses the same verb (anapauō) for believers who “refreshed my spirit” (1 Corinthians 16:18) and for Titus, whose spirit “has been refreshed by you all” (2 Corinthians 7:13).

Spirit & Life

Jesus promised rest to the weary: “Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest” (Matthew 11:28). In Philemon's house, that rest reached tired believers through a human host. Christ is the source of the rest, but He often gives it through people who make room for His people. A meal, a welcome, an attentive ear, or an open home can become the place where the Lord refreshes someone's heart. Those who refresh others are not replacing Christ. They are serving as the hands through which His rest arrives.

The refreshment did not stop with those who received it. News of Philemon's love brought joy and comfort to a prisoner. God carries faithful kindness further than the one who offers it can see, and He uses it to strengthen people we may never meet. No act of love done for Christ's people is small in His hands.

Philemon 1:8-16 - Paul's Appeal for Onesimus

Philemon 1:8

NKJV

Philemon 1:8 Therefore, though I might be very bold in Christ to command you what is fitting,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:8 That is why I am boldly asking a favor of you. I could demand it in the name of Christ because it is the right thing for you to do. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul has genuine authority in Christ to command Philemon to do what is fitting. By stating it plainly, he makes clear that the appeal to follow is a choice, not a lack of authority.

Verse Explanation

“Therefore,”

“Therefore” ties the request to the thanksgiving in verses 4-7. Because Philemon's love and fellowship are real, Paul has confidence to bring his request. Paul's praise in those verses was sincere, and it also prepares Philemon for what Paul is about to ask. The love Paul has thanked God for is the ground on which his request now stands.

“though I might be very bold in Christ”

“Be very bold” refers to confident, open speech, the freedom to say plainly what someone ought to do. Paul's authority rests “in Christ,” not in himself. He speaks as an apostle and as the man through whom Philemon came to faith (v. 19). He does not pretend to lack that authority, and later he writes with confidence in Philemon's “obedience” (v. 21).

“to command you what is fitting,”

“Command” means to give an order. “What is fitting” means what is proper or required, the conduct that suits a believer. The phrase quietly tells Philemon that receiving Onesimus well is not an optional favor. It is the right thing for someone in Christ to do. Paul could have made it an order, but as verse 9 shows, he chooses not to.

Study Notes

“Bold.” The Greek word (parrēsia) describes freedom and confidence of speech. It is used of the apostles' boldness before the Jewish council (Acts 4:13; compare 4:29).

“Fitting.” The same verb appears in Colossians, sent with this letter, for conduct that is “fitting in the Lord” (Colossians 3:18). Compare Ephesians 5:4. In each case it describes behavior that properly belongs to those who know Christ.

Spirit & Life

Paul knows he has authority. He does not pretend otherwise, and he does not apologize for it. But he describes it carefully: he could be bold “in Christ,” and he could command only “what is fitting.” His authority comes from Christ and is limited to what Christ wants. It is a trust, not a possession. Spiritual authority that forgets this turns into control. Authority that remembers it can be exercised gently, because the leader is not defending his own position but serving his Lord's will. Jesus said that the rulers of the nations lord it over others, “Yet it shall not be so among you” (Mark 10:42-43).

Paul does not give up his apostolic authority. He still holds it, and later he writes with confidence in Philemon's obedience (v. 21). What he sets aside is the choice to command. The strongest leaders in God's kingdom are often those secure enough not to use all the authority they have. Paul does not need to prove anything. His security rests in Christ, not in being obeyed, and so he is free to ask.

Philemon 1:9

NKJV

Philemon 1:9 yet for love's sake I rather appeal to you"”being such a one as Paul, the aged, and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ"”(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:9 But because of our love, I prefer simply to ask you. Consider this as a request from me — Paul, an old man and now also a prisoner for the sake of Christ Jesus. (NLT)

Main Idea

Rather than command, Paul appeals to Philemon for love's sake, speaking as an aged man and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ.

Verse Explanation

“yet for love's sake I rather appeal to you”

“Appeal” means to urge, plead, or call on someone. It is stronger than a casual request, yet it leaves the decision with the one being asked. “Rather” shows that Paul prefers this way. “For love's sake” names the basis of the appeal: love, the very quality Paul has praised in Philemon (vv. 5, 7). Paul wants Philemon to act from love, so he appeals to love instead of issuing a command.

“being such a one as Paul, the aged,”

“The aged” means an old man. Paul does not present himself here as an official exercising authority. He speaks as an older man worn by years of labor and suffering for Christ (2 Corinthians 11:23-28).

“and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ”

Paul repeats the description from verse 1. “Now also” adds his imprisonment to his age. Neither one is a claim to power. Both describe what Paul has given up for Christ, and a request from such a man carries a weight that a command would not.

Study Notes

“Aged” or “ambassador.” The Greek manuscripts read presbytēs, “an old man” (compare Luke 1:18; Titus 2:2). Some translations, such as the RSV, render the word “ambassador,” as though it were the closely related presbeutēs. The two forms were sometimes interchanged in spelling, and Paul calls himself “an ambassador in chains” in Ephesians 6:20. The NKJV's “the aged” follows the manuscripts and fits the context. An appeal to official rank would sit awkwardly beside Paul's decision not to command.

Paul's age. Luke first introduces Paul as “a young man” at the stoning of Stephen (Acts 7:58). Many years of travel, hardship, and imprisonment lay between that day and this letter. His exact age is unknown.

“For love's sake.” The phrase may refer to Philemon's love, to Paul's love, or to love as the principle governing the whole request. The last is most likely, though all three are related.

“Appeal.” The Greek verb (parakaleō) is the word Paul uses when he describes the gospel itself: “as though God were pleading through us: we implore you on Christ's behalf, be reconciled to God” (2 Corinthians 5:20).

Spirit & Life

Paul uses the same verb for his appeal that he uses for the gospel itself: “as though God were pleading through us: we implore you on Christ's behalf, be reconciled to God” (2 Corinthians 5:20). The Lord of heaven has every right to command, and at times He does. Yet in the gospel He also pleads. “All day long I have stretched out My hands,” He says of a disobedient people (Romans 10:21). Paul's choice to appeal “for love's sake” reflects the heart of the God he serves. Love asks because it wants more than outward compliance. It wants the heart.

Paul also calls himself “the aged.” Luke first introduces him as the young man at whose feet the witnesses laid their clothes when Stephen was stoned (Acts 7:58), and soon afterward he was dragging believers off to prison (Acts 8:3). Now the man who once bound others is himself in chains, and he spends his strength pleading for a slave to be welcomed as a brother. Grace does its work across a lifetime. A life given to Christ should grow gentler, not harder, as the years pass.

Philemon 1:10

NKJV

Philemon 1:10 I appeal to you for my son Onesimus, whom I have begotten while in my chains,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:10 I appeal to you to show kindness to my child, Onesimus. I became his father in the faith while here in prison. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul names the person his appeal concerns: Onesimus, whom he calls his son because Onesimus came to faith through Paul while Paul was in chains.

Verse Explanation

“I appeal to you for my son Onesimus,”

Paul repeats “I appeal” and now names its subject. He appeals “for” Onesimus, on his behalf. Before Philemon hears anything about the past, he hears that Onesimus is Paul's son. “Son” here means spiritual child. Paul uses the same family language for those he led to faith and cared for as a father (1 Corinthians 4:14-15; Galatians 4:19).

“whom I have begotten while in my chains,”

“Begotten” means fathered. Paul uses the word for bringing someone to new life in Christ through the gospel: “in Christ Jesus I have begotten you through the gospel” (1 Corinthians 4:15). Onesimus came to faith through Paul's ministry while Paul was in prison. Paul's chains had not stopped his work. They became the setting in which Onesimus was born into God's family.

The letter does not explain how Onesimus came to be with Paul. It says only that he “departed for a while” (v. 15).

Study Notes

Greek word order. In Greek the sentence runs, “I appeal to you for my child, whom I begot in my chains, Onesimus.” The name comes last. Philemon hears who Onesimus now is before he hears the name.

How Onesimus came to Paul. Three main explanations have been proposed. (1) The traditional view holds that Onesimus fled from Philemon, possibly after some wrong or loss (v. 18), and later met Paul. It has long support in the church and fits Paul's mention of a separation (v. 15) and a possible wrong or debt (v. 18), but the letter never calls him a runaway. (2) Another view holds that Onesimus deliberately sought out Paul, a trusted friend of his master, to plead his case after some offense. Roman legal writers discussed such situations, and a slave who did this was not always treated as a fugitive. This explains how Onesimus found Paul and fits Paul's role as mediator. (3) A third view suggests that Philemon or the church sent Onesimus to serve Paul. This fits “on your behalf” (v. 13) but sits less easily with Paul's language of separation and possible wrong (vv. 15, 18). The first two views fit the letter better than the third. Between them, the evidence is not decisive, and the letter's silence should be respected.

Spirit & Life

Paul speaks of Onesimus first by his relationship in Christ: “my son.” Philemon hears that before he hears anything about the past. When we must speak about someone whose history is known, the order of our words matters. Leading with who a person now is in Christ does not deny the past. It states what is now most true of that person. God speaks of His people this way. After listing what some of the Corinthians had been, Paul writes, “And such were some of you. But you were washed, but you were sanctified, but you were justified” (1 Corinthians 6:11). God calls His people by what His grace has made them.

Paul's language also shows what spiritual fatherhood involves. He did not lead Onesimus to Christ and leave him there. He took responsibility for him, valued his service, and now stands up for him before the man to whom he is returning. “Though you might have ten thousand instructors in Christ, yet you do not have many fathers” (1 Corinthians 4:15). New believers need more than instruction. They need someone who will vouch for them, speak for them, and walk with them through what lies ahead.

Philemon 1:11

NKJV

Philemon 1:11 who once was unprofitable to you, but now is profitable to you and to me.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:11 Onesimus hasn’t been of much use to you in the past, but now he is very useful to both of us. (NLT)

Main Idea

Onesimus, whose name means “useful,” was once unprofitable to Philemon but is now profitable to both Philemon and Paul. His new life in Christ has made him what his name promised.

Verse Explanation

“who once was unprofitable to you,”

Onesimus was a common name for slaves, and it means “useful” or “profitable.” Paul plays on it: the man named “Useful” had been useless to Philemon. Paul does not say how. It may concern Onesimus's past service, his departure, or the wrong or debt Paul mentions as a possibility in verse 18. Paul speaks honestly about the past without describing it.

“but now is profitable to you and to me.”

“Now” marks the change that came when Onesimus came to faith (v. 10). He has already proved useful to Paul by serving him in prison (v. 13). He will be useful to Philemon as well, returning as a changed man and as a brother in Christ (v. 16). Paul names Philemon first, assuring him that Onesimus's return will be for his good. In Christ, Onesimus has become what his name promised.

Study Notes

The wordplay. “Unprofitable” (achrēstos) and “profitable” (euchrēstos) both come from a root meaning “useful.” The name Onesimus comes from a different root with the same meaning, so the play is on the sense of the words rather than their sound. Achrēstos occurs only here in the New Testament. Paul later uses euchrēstos for the believer who is “useful for the Master” (2 Timothy 2:21) and for Mark, who is “useful to me for ministry” (2 Timothy 4:11).

Spirit & Life

Paul does not erase “once.” The gospel never pretends a person had no past. It says “once,” and then it says “now.” Paul tells his own story the same way: “although I was formerly a blasphemer, a persecutor, and an insolent man; but I obtained mercy” (1 Timothy 1:13). The honest “once” makes the “now” a testimony to grace rather than a cover story. No one is beyond the reach of God's “now.”

The change in Onesimus also appears in a particular place. He is now profitable “to you and to me.” Paul does not say what made him unprofitable before, and the reader need not supply the details. What Paul points to is the evidence of new life: Onesimus has become useful in service, and his usefulness reaches others. Genuine transformation does not stay hidden. Over time it shows itself in changed conduct and fruitful service, often first among the people who know us best. Christ does not merely forgive. He makes a life fruitful for others.

Philemon 1:12

NKJV

Philemon 1:12 I am sending him back. You therefore receive him, that is, my own heart,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:12 I am sending him back to you, and with him comes my own heart. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul sends Onesimus back to Philemon, asking him to receive the man Paul regards as his own heart. The expression reveals Paul's deep affection for Onesimus and how personally invested he is in Philemon's response.

Verse Explanation

“I am sending him back.”

Paul returns Onesimus to Philemon, who held legal rights over him. Onesimus goes back to a master with power over him. Colossians 4:7-9 names him alongside Tychicus, who carried the letter to the Colossians, which strongly suggests the two traveled together, though the details of the journey are not described. Paul does not keep him or hide him. He sends him back so that the matter can be settled openly and rightly between the two men.

“You therefore receive him,”

The NKJV includes a direct command here: Philemon is to receive Onesimus. “Receive” means to welcome someone to oneself. It is the same verb Paul uses in verse 17, where he states the full request.

“that is, my own heart,”

“Heart” is the word used in verse 7 for the inner person, the seat of deep affection. Paul calls Onesimus his own inner self. Sending him away is like sending part of himself. Whatever welcome Philemon gives Onesimus will touch Paul personally, which prepares for verse 17.

Study Notes

“Receive him” in verse 12. The Greek text behind the NKJV includes “You therefore receive him.” Many early manuscripts lack these words, and most modern translations follow them. In those manuscripts the sentence that begins in verse 12 is not completed until “receive him” in verse 17, so verse 17 contains the letter's first direct command.

“I am sending back.” The Greek verb is in the past tense, literally “I sent.” Letter writers often used the past tense for an action that would be past when the letter was read. This suggests that Onesimus traveled with the letter. The verb (anapempō) can also mean referring a case to the proper authority, as when Pilate sent Jesus to Herod (Luke 23:7). Some see that sense here: Paul refers Onesimus's case to Philemon for his decision.

Spirit & Life

Paul calls Onesimus “my own heart.” He identifies so closely with this new believer that whatever happens to Onesimus happens to him. Paul learned this from Christ. On the road to Damascus, the risen Lord asked him, “Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting Me?” (Acts 9:4). Saul thought he was attacking believers. Jesus said he was attacking Him. Christ so joins Himself to His people that what is done to them is done to Him (Matthew 25:40). The man who once heard those words now asks that a new member of Christ's body be received as his own heart. How we treat the least-regarded believer is how we treat the Lord who calls that believer His own.

“I am sending him back” shows that the gospel can reach into difficult relationships rather than simply leaving them behind. Paul judged that this return was right, and he did not send Onesimus back unprepared. Onesimus went with Paul's letter, Paul's appeal, and Paul's pledge. Reconciliation in Scripture is never careless. It honors truth, justice, wisdom, and safety. Forgiveness never requires anyone to return to an abusive or dangerous situation. Yet where reconciliation is possible and wise, grace gives believers the courage to face what needs to be made right instead of avoiding it, and Christ is able to make new what once seemed beyond repair.

Philemon 1:13

NKJV

Philemon 1:13 whom I wished to keep with me, that on your behalf he might minister to me in my chains for the gospel.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:13 I wanted to keep him here with me while I am in these chains for preaching the Good News, and he would have helped me on your behalf. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul wanted to keep Onesimus with him to serve him, on Philemon's behalf, during his imprisonment for the gospel. Sending him back costs Paul a helper he valued.

Verse Explanation

“whom I wished to keep with me,”

Paul is honest about his own desire. He wanted Onesimus to stay. Sending him back was not easy for Paul, and it cost him real help in his imprisonment.

“that on your behalf he might minister to me”

“Minister” means to serve. The word is used both for practical service and for Christian ministry. Onesimus had been serving Paul's needs, and Paul would have liked that service to continue. “On your behalf” is a gracious touch. Paul treats Onesimus's service as though it were Philemon's own, assuming that Philemon would gladly have helped him if he could. Onesimus could have served in his place.

“in my chains for the gospel.”

Paul's imprisonment is for the gospel. Serving him was serving the cause of the gospel, and Philemon, as Paul's fellow laborer (v. 1), had a share in it.

Study Notes

“I wished.” The verb (boulomai) is in the imperfect tense, “I was wishing.” It describes a desire Paul felt but did not act on. Verse 14 states the decision that overruled it.

Serving on behalf of others. Epaphroditus served Paul as the Philippian church's representative, supplying what they could not give in person (Philippians 2:25, 30). Paul pictures Onesimus serving him in a similar way on Philemon's behalf.

Spirit & Life

Paul wanted Onesimus to stay, “that on your behalf he might minister to me.” The word for “minister” is the word used throughout the New Testament for Christian service. The one who had been “unprofitable” (v. 11) now serves an apostle in chains for the gospel. Whatever his work had been before, it has become ministry. In Colossians, sent at the same time, Paul tells slaves, “whatever you do, do it heartily, as to the Lord and not to men ... for you serve the Lord Christ” (Colossians 3:23-24). In Christ, ordinary work done for others becomes service offered to Him.

“On your behalf” also shows the generosity of love. Paul assumes the best of Philemon, that he would gladly have helped Paul if he could, and he credits Philemon with the service Onesimus gave. Love “believes all things, hopes all things” (1 Corinthians 13:7). Assuming the best of a brother is not naïve. It is often the way love draws out the best in him.

Philemon 1:14

NKJV

Philemon 1:14 But without your consent I wanted to do nothing, that your good deed might not be by compulsion, as it were, but voluntary.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:14 But I didn’t want to do anything without your consent. I wanted you to help because you were willing, not because you were forced. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul would do nothing without Philemon's consent, because he wants Philemon's good deed to be freely chosen rather than compelled.

Verse Explanation

“But without your consent I wanted to do nothing,”

“Consent” means Philemon's judgment and approval. Paul's desire pointed one way (v. 13), but his decision went another. He would not keep Onesimus without Philemon's agreement.

“that your good deed might not be by compulsion, as it were,”

If Paul had kept Onesimus and informed Philemon afterward, Philemon would have had little real choice. His agreement would have been forced by circumstances. “As it were” shows that Paul wanted to avoid even the appearance of pressure. In context, the “good deed” most directly concerns the help Philemon might give Paul through Onesimus's service (v. 13). The same principle applies to Philemon's whole response to the letter. “Good” is the word Paul used in verse 6.

“but voluntary.”

“Voluntary” means freely willed, given from one's own choice. Paul does not remove all moral weight from his appeal. He will remind Philemon of what he owes (v. 19) and express confidence in his obedience (v. 21). But the response Paul seeks is willing obedience that comes from love, not reluctant compliance.

Study Notes

“Wished” and “wanted.” Verse 13 uses boulomai in the imperfect tense, describing a desire Paul had. Verse 14 changes to thelō in a different tense. The tense alone does not prove a single settled decision, but the context makes the contrast clear. Paul wanted to keep Onesimus, yet he resolved not to act without Philemon's consent. He let the right decision overrule a strong and legitimate desire.

Compulsion and willingness. “By compulsion” and “voluntary” set the same contrast found in 1 Peter 5:2, “not by compulsion but willingly,” and in 2 Corinthians 9:7, where God loves the giver who gives “not grudgingly or of necessity.” The word for “voluntary” (hekousios) appears only here in the New Testament.

Roman law. Under Roman law, a person who kept another's slave without permission could be held liable to the owner for lost labor. Paul's restraint was legally proper, but the reason he gives is not legal. He wants Philemon's goodness to be free.

Spirit & Life

Paul had a real need and a genuine desire. He wanted to keep Onesimus. Yet he decided to do nothing without Philemon's consent. Much of the life of faith is lived in the space between what we want and what we have decided before God is right. Paul's desire was not sinful. It simply was not the deciding voice. Mature faith lets the right decision overrule even a good desire.

Paul's reason is that he wants Philemon's goodness to be willing, and in this he reflects what God Himself desires. “Your people shall be volunteers in the day of Your power” (Psalm 110:3). God seeks more than outward conformity. He works within His people so that they come to want what He wants: “it is God who works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure” (Philippians 2:13). The obedience He delights in comes from hearts He has changed. Those who lead or influence others can learn from this. Pressure may secure the right action for a time, but leaders who want lasting fruit will seek willing participation and pray for the inward change only God can give.

Philemon 1:15

NKJV

Philemon 1:15 For perhaps he departed for a while for this purpose, that you might receive him forever,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:15 It seems you lost Onesimus for a little while so that you could have him back forever. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul suggests that Onesimus's separation from Philemon for a while may have served a greater purpose: that Philemon might receive him back forever. Paul's “perhaps” keeps this view of God's hand in events tentative.

Verse Explanation

“For perhaps”

“For” gives a further reason for sending Onesimus back. “Perhaps” is important. Paul sees a possible purpose in what happened, but he does not claim to know God's purposes with certainty.

“he departed for a while”

“Departed” translates a passive verb, “he was separated.” Paul does not use a harsher word. The passive form by itself does not prove that God brought about the separation. Paul's suggestion of a purpose comes from the verse as a whole, especially “perhaps” and “for this purpose.” He allows that God may have been at work, yet he never calls any wrong good, and he does not make God the author of wrongdoing. God can bring good out of a situation without excusing whatever wrong may have been part of it. “For a while” means a short time.

“for this purpose, that you might receive him forever,”

“Receive” here translates a different verb from verses 12 and 17. It means to have back in full. Paul sets two time spans side by side: Philemon was without Onesimus “for a while,” but he may now have him “forever.” The relationship between them has changed. As brothers in Christ (v. 16), their bond will last beyond this life.

Study Notes

“Was separated.” The passive voice (echōristhē) leaves room for God's hand behind events, but it does not establish it. A passive verb can also simply avoid naming who acted. Paul's tentative suggestion of a providential purpose rests on the verse's language of purpose. Compare Joseph's words to his brothers: “you meant evil against me; but God meant it for good” (Genesis 50:20). Paul's “perhaps” is more reserved than Joseph's confident statement, and the comparison should not be pressed. See the note on verse 10 for the views on how Onesimus came to Paul.

“For a while.” The Greek is literally “for an hour” (pros hōran), an idiom for a short time (compare 2 Corinthians 7:8).

“Receive.” The verb (apechō) was used on receipts to mean “paid in full” (compare “they have their reward,” Matthew 6:2, and “I have all,” Philippians 4:18). Philemon would have Onesimus back fully, in a fuller relationship than before.

“Forever.” The word (aiōnion) is the usual New Testament word for “eternal.” Here it describes the lasting relationship Philemon and Onesimus now share in the Lord. It should not be used to build a doctrine of salvation that the verse does not address.

Spirit & Life

Paul says “perhaps.” He sees a possible purpose in what happened, but he will not claim to know God's mind with certainty. That humility protects believers in two directions. We can trust that God is able to work good through painful separations (Romans 8:28) without pretending to know exactly what He was doing in every detail. And we never treat a good outcome as proof that whatever wrong may have been part of the story was acceptable. God can redeem a story without approving every chapter of it.

The verse also sets a short time beside a long one: “for a while” and “forever.” Philemon was without Onesimus for a season, and he may now have him back as a brother whose bond with him will outlast this life. Paul is not promising that every painful separation will end in a happier reunion on earth. Some do not. But when God redeems a situation, He does not merely put things back as they were. Philemon would not receive the same relationship he had before. He would receive a brother. God's redemptive work often produces something new, and what He makes new in Christ lasts forever.

Philemon 1:16

NKJV

Philemon 1:16 no longer as a slave but more than a slave"”a beloved brother, especially to me but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:16 He is no longer like a slave to you. He is more than a slave, for he is a beloved brother, especially to me. Now he will mean much more to you, both as a man and as a brother in the Lord. (NLT)

Main Idea

Philemon is to have Onesimus back no longer as a slave but as more than a slave, a beloved brother. This new relationship, reaching into everyday household life as well as their shared life in the Lord, is the heart of the letter and the basis of Paul's request.

Verse Explanation

This verse is the heart of the letter. It names the new relationship on which Paul's whole request rests.

“no longer as a slave but more than a slave”

The word “as” is important. Paul does not say that Onesimus is no longer a slave in law. He says Philemon is not to have him back as merely a slave. Under Roman law a slave was property. Paul tells Philemon that Onesimus is more than that, and “no longer” shows that the change is real and lasting.

Paul does not command Philemon to free Onesimus. Neither should his silence be read as approval of slavery. What Paul asks is that Philemon receive Onesimus as a brother. That request carries implications Paul does not spell out. A brother in Christ cannot finally be treated as property.

“a beloved brother,”

Onesimus now carries the same titles as Philemon. Paul called Philemon “beloved” (v. 1) and “brother” (v. 7). Now he calls Onesimus both. Master and slave share one Father (v. 3) and belong to one family. Paul teaches the same truth in Colossians, sent with this letter: in the new man “there is neither ... slave nor free, but Christ is all and in all” (Colossians 3:11).

“especially to me but how much more to you,”

Onesimus is especially dear to Paul, who is his spiritual father (v. 10). Yet he should be dearer still to Philemon. Philemon will share daily life with him, and the two of them now belong to the same Lord.

“both in the flesh and in the Lord.”

“In the flesh” refers to ordinary human life, including the household and the working relationship between master and slave. “In the Lord” refers to their shared life in Christ. Onesimus is to be Philemon's brother in both. Their brotherhood is not limited to the church gathering in Philemon's house. It reaches into the daily life of the household.

Study Notes

Roman slavery. Roman law treated a slave as property under the owner's power, with little protection from abuse. The Roman writer Varro classed slaves among the equipment of a farm as tools that can speak. Against that background, Paul's call to receive Onesimus as “a beloved brother” was a striking claim about his dignity before God.

Paul and slavery. The New Testament gives instructions to believing slaves and masters (1 Corinthians 7:21; Ephesians 6:5-9; Colossians 3:22-4:1) rather than issuing a direct command to abolish slavery. Colossians tells masters to give their slaves “what is just and fair, knowing that you also have a Master in heaven” (Colossians 4:1). Whether Paul expected Philemon to free Onesimus is debated, especially in light of “even more than I say” (v. 21). The letter is not an abolition manifesto, but the relationship it describes works against the institution itself.

“Brother.” Paul uses “brother” four times in this short letter: of Timothy (v. 1), of Philemon (vv. 7, 20), and of Onesimus (v. 16).

Spirit & Life

Paul's words echo what God has done for every believer: “Therefore you are no longer a slave but a son, and if a son, then an heir of God through Christ” (Galatians 4:7). Paul does not say Onesimus has ceased to be a slave in law. But he asks Philemon to receive him “no longer as a slave” and as “a beloved brother.” In doing so, he asks Philemon to treat Onesimus the way God has treated Philemon. God did not bring us into His house as servants on probation. He made us sons and daughters, and He calls us to see one another the same way.

“Both in the flesh and in the Lord” carries brotherhood into the place where it is hardest. In the household, Philemon was master and Onesimus was slave, and every daily task reminded them of that difference. Paul does not deny the difference, but he will not let it have the last word. The relationship Christ creates must be honored in the very setting where the world's ranking is strongest. James warns believers against honoring the rich and dishonoring the poor in the assembly (James 2:1-4). Paul carries the same truth into the home and the workplace. Believers who differ in income, position, or status are brothers and sisters first, and that bond is meant to change how they treat one another every day.

Philemon 1:17-22 - Paul's Request to Receive Onesimus

Philemon 1:17

NKJV

Philemon 1:17 If then you count me as a partner, receive him as you would me.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:17 So if you consider me your partner, welcome him as you would welcome me. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul states his request plainly: if Philemon counts Paul as a partner, he should receive Onesimus as he would receive Paul himself.

Verse Explanation

“If then you count me as a partner,”

“Then” draws together everything Paul has said in verses 10-16 and moves to the request itself. The “if” does not express doubt. Paul expects Philemon to say yes, and he builds his request on that shared conviction.

“Partner” comes from the same word family as “sharing” in verse 6 (koinonia). Partners hold something in common. What concerns one concerns the other. Paul and Philemon share a common life in Christ and a common stake in the gospel. If that partnership is real, it includes the people Paul has made his own.

“receive him as you would me.”

This is the request the whole letter has been moving toward. “Receive” means to welcome someone to oneself, to take a person into one's home and fellowship. It is the same verb Paul used in verse 12.

Paul does not ask Philemon only to refrain from punishment or to tolerate Onesimus at a distance. He asks for the welcome Philemon would give Paul himself, the welcome given to a beloved friend and honored guest. Paul's own standing with Philemon becomes the measure of how Onesimus is to be received.

Study Notes

“If.” The Greek construction (ei with the indicative) presents the condition as real for the sake of the argument. Here the context makes clear that Paul fully expects Philemon to count him a partner.

“Partner.” The Greek word (koinōnos) can describe business partners, as when James and John were “partners with Simon” in fishing (Luke 5:10). Paul also uses it of Titus, “my partner and fellow worker” (2 Corinthians 8:23). The commercial overtone fits the language of accounts and repayment in verses 18-19.

“Receive.” The verb (proslambanō) means to take someone to oneself. Paul uses it in “Receive one who is weak in the faith” (Romans 14:1) and in “receive one another, just as Christ also received us” (Romans 15:7). Luke uses it of the islanders on Malta who welcomed the shipwrecked travelers (Acts 28:2).

Spirit & Life

Onesimus comes to Philemon on Paul's standing, not his own: “Receive him as you would me.” The welcome Philemon would give Paul becomes the measure of the welcome Onesimus receives. Paul's request gives a picture of how believers are received by God. We do not come on the strength of our own record. We come in Christ, and the Father receives us with the love He has for His Son. Jesus prayed that the world would know the Father has loved them “as You have loved Me” (John 17:23). The believer is not tolerated at the edge of God's house. He is welcomed as the Son is welcomed.

Jesus taught a related pattern of receiving: “He who receives you receives Me, and he who receives Me receives Him who sent Me” (Matthew 10:40). Those who come to us in Christ's name come bearing His name, and how we receive them is bound up with how we receive Him. Partnership in Christ is not only a shared work. It is a shared family, and it includes the people He sends to our door.

Philemon 1:18

NKJV

Philemon 1:18 But if he has wronged you or owes anything, put that on my account.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:18 If he has wronged you in any way or owes you anything, charge it to me. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul removes a possible obstacle to reconciliation. If Onesimus has wronged Philemon or owes him anything, Philemon is to charge it to Paul's account.

Verse Explanation

“But if he has wronged you”

“Wronged” means to treat someone unjustly or cause them harm. The sentence is conditional. Paul does not say what Onesimus did, and the letter gives no details. Paul neither denies the possibility nor describes it. He does not name or rehearse any wrong in front of the church, yet he does not pretend that nothing could stand between the two men.

“or owes anything,”

“Owes” refers to a debt, something Philemon could rightly claim. The letter does not say what that might be. Whatever wrong or debt may exist, Paul brings it into the open so it can be dealt with.

“put that on my account.”

“Put that on my account” is the language of bookkeeping: charge it to me. Paul takes responsibility for whatever Onesimus may owe. He does not ask Philemon to pretend there was no loss. He offers to bear it himself, so that no unpaid claim can stand in the way of Philemon receiving Onesimus as he would receive Paul (v. 17).

Study Notes

The form of the condition. As in verse 17, the construction presents the condition as real for the sake of the argument. Many interpreters conclude from this verse that Onesimus had caused Philemon some actual loss. Proposals include money or goods taken when he left, or labor lost during his absence. The grammar alone does not prove that a wrong occurred, and the letter does not identify one.

“Put that on my account.” The verb (ellogaō) was a business term in the papyri for charging an amount to someone's account. In the New Testament it appears only here and in Romans 5:13, where Paul writes that “sin is not imputed when there is no law.”

Spirit & Life

Paul says “if.” He does not establish that Onesimus owes anything. He offers to take responsibility for whatever may be owed. With us there is no “if.” “All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God” (Romans 3:23), and our debt before God is certain. Yet Paul's offer points toward something far greater. Isaiah saw that “the LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all” (Isaiah 53:6). Paul offered to cover a possible debt between two men. Christ took on Himself the real debt of sinners before a holy God, and only His sacrifice could pay it.

Paul's wording also shows how love handles another person's past. He does not name any wrong in front of the church, and he does not pretend nothing could be owed. “He who covers a transgression seeks love, but he who repeats a matter separates friends” (Proverbs 17:9). Love does not needlessly expose a brother's possible failures to others, yet it deals honestly with whatever must be made right. Paul is willing to cover by paying, not by pretending.

Philemon 1:19

NKJV

Philemon 1:19 I, Paul, am writing with my own hand. I will repay"”not to mention to you that you owe me even your own self besides.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:19 I, PAUL, WRITE THIS WITH MY OWN HAND: I WILL REPAY IT. AND I WON’T MENTION THAT YOU OWE ME YOUR VERY SOUL! (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul guarantees his pledge in his own handwriting, “I will repay,” while reminding Philemon that he owes Paul his very self.

Verse Explanation

“I, Paul, am writing with my own hand.”

Paul normally dictated his letters to a scribe and added a closing line in his own handwriting (1 Corinthians 16:21; Galatians 6:11; Colossians 4:18; 2 Thessalonians 3:17). Here his handwriting serves a further purpose. Paul personally guarantees his promise in writing. A signed note promising payment is a helpful comparison for the seriousness of the commitment, though Paul is not drawing up a formal legal document. Philemon would hold Paul's promise in Paul's own hand.

“I will repay”

This is a firm commitment. Paul binds himself to cover whatever wrong or debt verse 18 has in view. The offer is sincere. Though he is in prison, Paul takes personal responsibility for anything Onesimus may owe.

“not to mention to you that you owe me even your own self besides.”

“Not to mention” is a way of saying something while declining to press it. Paul reminds Philemon that he owes Paul “even your own self.” This implies that Philemon came to faith through Paul or through his ministry. How and where that happened, the letter does not say. Whatever Onesimus may owe Philemon, Philemon owes Paul far more: his new life in Christ.

Paul does not withdraw his pledge, and he does not demand that Philemon cancel the debt. He sets the two debts side by side and lets Philemon draw the conclusion. This is real moral pressure, but it comes from a spiritual father who has just bound himself to pay, not from someone seeking advantage.

Study Notes

Paul's handwriting. Interpreters differ over whether Paul wrote only this pledge in his own hand or the whole letter. Either way, Paul's handwriting personally reinforces his promise to repay. “Am writing” is in the past tense in Greek, “I wrote,” reflecting the reader's point of view, as in verse 12.

“Repay” and “owe besides.” The verb for “repay” (apotinō) appears only here in the New Testament. It was used for paying compensation or damages. “Owe besides” (prosopheilō), meaning “owe in addition,” also appears only here.

Did Paul expect to pay? Some think Paul hoped Philemon would cancel the account rather than collect it. Others take the pledge as fully meant. The text presents it as a genuine promise.

Where Philemon came to faith. Paul apparently had not visited Colossae (Colossians 2:1). Many suggest Philemon met Paul during Paul's long ministry in Ephesus, when the word spread throughout the province of Asia (Acts 19:10). This is a reasonable inference, not a stated fact.

Spirit & Life

Paul wrote his pledge, “I will repay,” in his own hand. The same journey carried Paul's letter to the believers at Colossae, where Philemon most likely lived. In that letter Paul wrote that God has “wiped out the handwriting of requirements that was against us,” and has “taken it out of the way, having nailed it to the cross” (Colossians 2:14). The wording of the two passages differs, and Paul does not draw the comparison himself, yet reading them side by side is instructive. Paul's note was a personal promise to cover whatever a new believer might owe another man. The “handwriting” in Colossians was the record that stood against us before God. Paul could offer to pay a possible debt between two men. Only Christ could remove the record against us, and He did it at the cross. Those who know their own debt has been nailed to the cross are the ones most free to take responsibility for someone else's.

Then Paul reminds Philemon, “you owe me even your own self.” Behind that reminder stands a greater one. Every believer owes Christ not a sum but a self. “You are not your own ... For you were bought at a price” (1 Corinthians 6:19-20). This is not a burden but a release. A person who knows he belongs entirely to Christ can hold loosely what others owe him.

Philemon 1:20

NKJV

Philemon 1:20 Yes, brother, let me have joy from you in the Lord; refresh my heart in the Lord.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:20 Yes, my brother, please do me this favor for the Lord’s sake. Give me this encouragement in Christ. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul asks Philemon to give him joy and to refresh his heart in the Lord. The man who has refreshed the saints can now refresh Paul by receiving Onesimus.

Verse Explanation

“Yes, brother,”

“Yes” gathers up the whole appeal and presses it warmly. “Brother” returns from verse 7. Paul speaks as one member of the family to another.

“let me have joy from you in the Lord;”

“Have joy” translates a verb meaning to benefit or gain from someone. It is related to the name Onesimus, so Paul plays on the name a second time. Having sent Philemon the “useful” one, Paul now asks for something useful from Philemon. What Paul seeks is not money or service. He seeks the benefit of seeing Philemon respond in a way that honors Christ, “in the Lord.”

“refresh my heart in the Lord.”

Paul repeats the two words of verse 7, “refresh” and “heart.” Philemon has refreshed the hearts of the saints. Now Paul asks the same for himself. Because Paul has called Onesimus “my own heart” (v. 12), receiving Onesimus is how Philemon can refresh Paul's heart.

Study Notes

“Have joy.” The verb (onaimēn, from oninēmi, “to benefit”) occurs only here in the New Testament. It shares its root with the name Onesimus, which makes this a gentler second play on the name (compare v. 11).

“In the Lord” or “in Christ.” The NKJV reads “in the Lord” twice, following the Greek text behind it. Many manuscripts read “in Christ” in the second clause (“refresh my heart in Christ”).

Spirit & Life

Paul plays on Onesimus's name one more time: “let me have joy from you,” or in effect, “let me have benefit from you.” Having sent Philemon a man who became useful, Paul asks Philemon to be useful to him in return. But the benefit Paul seeks is not money or help. It is the joy of seeing a brother act like Christ. John wrote, “I have no greater joy than to hear that my children walk in truth” (3 John 4). The deepest joy of spiritual fathers and mothers is not what they receive from those they serve but what they see Christ do in them.

Paul also admits his own need: “refresh my heart.” The apostle who has strengthened churches in many provinces asks a friend to refresh him. Leaders are not exempt from weariness, and they are not meant to carry their burdens without the refreshment God gives through His people. It takes humility to say, “My heart needs refreshing,” and grace to let someone else be God's answer.

Philemon 1:21

NKJV

Philemon 1:21 Having confidence in your obedience, I write to you, knowing that you will do even more than I say.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:21 I am confident as I write this letter that you will do what I ask and even more! (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul writes with confidence in Philemon's obedience, sure that he will do even more than Paul asks. Paul leaves that “more” unspecified.

Verse Explanation

“Having confidence in your obedience,”

Paul has appealed rather than commanded (vv. 8-9), but he has not given up his authority. “Obedience” shows that he expects Philemon to recognize Paul's request as the right response for a believer, the “fitting” thing of verse 8. Paul's confidence is also an encouragement. He writes as someone who trusts Philemon to do what is right.

“I write to you,”

Paul refers to this letter itself. He wrote it because he is confident of Philemon's response.

“knowing that you will do even more than I say.”

Paul's confidence goes beyond his request. He expects Philemon's response to exceed what Paul has asked. He does not say what that “more” might be. He leaves it to Philemon's love to discover.

Study Notes

“Obedience.” The Greek word (hypakoē) is the ordinary word for obedience. Here Paul's tone moves from request toward authority, though he frames it as confidence rather than command.

“Even more than I say.” Three main interpretations have been proposed. (1) Philemon would free Onesimus. This fits the direction of “more than a slave” (v. 16), though Paul never asks for it. (2) Philemon would send Onesimus back to serve Paul. This fits Paul's stated wish in verse 13. (3) Philemon would welcome Onesimus with a generosity beyond the minimum Paul has requested. This fits the letter's emphasis on willing goodness (v. 14). The text does not specify, and no one option should be presented as Paul's meaning. Philemon's response is not recorded.

Spirit & Life

Paul expects Philemon to do “even more” than he asks, but he does not say what. He leaves room for love to find its own answer. Obedience that comes from love does not ask how little it must do to satisfy a request. It asks how much it may give. Jesus described this kind of response: “whoever compels you to go one mile, go with him two” (Matthew 5:41). The first mile was compelled by another. The second was the mile Jesus commanded His disciples to add, a mile no one could force from them. Love that has been touched by grace does not stop at the minimum someone else can require.

Paul's confidence is also a gift to Philemon. He writes as one who believes Philemon will rise to what is right, and that kind of trust often draws a person toward it. Elsewhere Paul shows where such confidence rests: “being confident of this very thing, that He who has begun a good work in you will complete it” (Philippians 1:6). Our confidence in other believers is safest when it rests on what God is doing in them.

Philemon 1:22

NKJV

Philemon 1:22 But, meanwhile, also prepare a guest room for me, for I trust that through your prayers I shall be granted to you.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:22 One more thing — please prepare a guest room for me, for I am hoping that God will answer your prayers and let me return to you soon. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul asks Philemon to prepare a guest room, trusting that through the church's prayers he will be released and given back to them. His hope of coming adds a personal visit to the appeal.

Verse Explanation

“But, meanwhile, also prepare a guest room for me,”

“Meanwhile” means while Philemon is acting on the letter and while Paul awaits the outcome of his imprisonment. Paul asks for lodging because he hopes to be released and to visit. The request is practical and warm, and it also means Paul hopes to see Philemon in person. Philemon's response to Onesimus will not remain a matter of letters alone.

“for I trust that through your prayers”

“Your” is plural. Paul looks to the prayers of the congregation and relies on them, confident that their prayers matter. “I trust” expresses hope and confidence, not certainty. Paul leaves the outcome with God.

“I shall be granted to you.”

“Granted” means given freely as a gift. If Paul is released, it will be God's gracious answer to the church's prayers, a gift given to them. “To you” is again plural. Paul's freedom would be God's gift to the whole congregation.

Study Notes

“Guest room.” The Greek word (xenia) means lodging or hospitality. Its only other New Testament use is Acts 28:23, for Paul's lodging in Rome, where many came to hear him.

“Granted.” The verb (charizomai) is related to charis, “grace.” It means to give freely as a favor.

Paul's hope of release. In Philippians, Paul similarly expresses hope of release and of coming to visit (Philippians 1:25-26; 2:24). If both letters come from the same imprisonment, the hopes match. Whether Paul ever reached Philemon's home is unknown. Those who place the letter in Ephesus point to the shorter distance to Colossae as a reason a visit seemed realistic. If Paul wrote from Rome, the journey would have been much longer.

Spirit & Life

Paul is still in chains, yet he asks for a room to be prepared. When Jerusalem was under siege, the LORD told Jeremiah to buy a field, promising that “houses and fields and vineyards shall be possessed again in this land” (Jeremiah 32:15). Jeremiah acted on an explicit word from God. Paul has no such promise of release. He has a hope grounded in the church's prayers, and he says only, “I trust.” Yet both men let their expectation of God take practical form. Faith can make room for what it is asking God to do while leaving the outcome in His hands. Preparing a guest room did not presume on God's will. It expressed confidence that God hears prayer, together with readiness to accept whatever answer He gives.

Paul links his hope to “your prayers.” The prayers of a congregation meeting in a home could matter in the outcome of an apostle's imprisonment. When Peter was in prison, “constant prayer was offered to God for him by the church” (Acts 12:5). Yet Paul says he will be “granted” to them, a word rooted in grace. Prayer does not earn the answer. It asks the God who gives freely, and when He answers, the answer is still a gift.

Philemon 1:23-25 - Final Greetings and Benediction

Philemon 1:23

NKJV

Philemon 1:23 Epaphras, my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus, greets you,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:23 Epaphras, my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus, sends you his greetings. (NLT)

Main Idea

Epaphras, Paul's fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus, sends greetings to Philemon.

Verse Explanation

“Epaphras,”

Epaphras was from Colossae. Paul calls him “one of you” when writing to the Colossians (Colossians 4:12). The Colossian believers had learned the gospel from him (Colossians 1:7), and Paul describes him as “always laboring fervently for you in prayers” (Colossians 4:12). If Philemon lived in Colossae, as seems likely, Epaphras was probably well known to him and to the church in his house.

“my fellow prisoner in Christ Jesus,”

“Fellow prisoner” means one who shares captivity with another. Whether Epaphras was under arrest is uncertain. The title may mean that he was literally imprisoned with Paul, or it may describe a broader share in Paul's confinement and hardships for Christ. “In Christ Jesus” places his situation, like Paul's, within his service to Christ (v. 1).

“greets you,”

The “you” is singular. Epaphras sends his greeting to Philemon personally.

Study Notes

“Fellow prisoner.” The Greek word (synaichmalōtos) literally means a fellow prisoner of war. Paul uses it elsewhere of Andronicus and Junia (Romans 16:7) and, in Colossians, of Aristarchus (Colossians 4:10), while there he calls Epaphras a “fellow servant” (Colossians 1:7). Because the title moves between the two men, some take it figuratively, as a soldier's term for those who share Christ's service and its hardships. Others think Epaphras and Aristarchus took turns staying with Paul in his confinement. The text does not settle the question.

Epaphras and Epaphroditus. The name Epaphras may be a shortened form of Epaphroditus, and some interpreters have suggested the two were the same man. Most distinguish them, and they should not be confused. Epaphras was from Colossae (Colossians 4:12). Epaphroditus came from Philippi and served Paul on that church's behalf (Philippians 2:25; 4:18).

Spirit & Life

Epaphras sends a greeting, and Colossians tells us how he spent his time away from home. He was “always laboring fervently for you in prayers, that you may stand perfect and complete in all the will of God” (Colossians 4:12). The word Paul uses describes a struggle, the kind of effort an athlete puts into a contest. Epaphras could not be with the believers he had served, but he could still labor for them. Distance and hardship limited where he could go. They did not limit his intercession.

Prayer is real work, and some of the most important work in God's kingdom is done where no one sees it. Many believers came to faith or grew strong because someone kept praying for them, often from far away and often without their knowledge. Epaphras's brief greeting is a reminder that those who have served us in Christ may still be laboring for us before God, and that we can do the same for those we have served.

Philemon 1:24

NKJV

Philemon 1:24 as do Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke, my fellow laborers.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:24 So do Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke, my co-workers. (NLT)

Main Idea

Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, and Luke, Paul's fellow laborers, also send greetings. Paul's personal appeal is set within the wider fellowship of believers serving with him.

Verse Explanation

“as do Mark,”

Mark is John Mark, the cousin of Barnabas (Acts 12:12; Colossians 4:10). Years earlier he left Paul and Barnabas partway through their first missionary journey (Acts 13:13). Paul then refused to take him on the next journey, and the disagreement became so sharp that Paul and Barnabas parted ways (Acts 15:37-39). Now Mark stands among Paul's “fellow laborers.” Paul later asks for him because “he is useful to me for ministry” (2 Timothy 4:11). Scripture records the earlier break and Mark's later usefulness to Paul, though it does not describe how the relationship was restored. His name in a letter asking Philemon to receive Onesimus is a fitting reminder that a man once set aside can again be counted useful in the work of Christ.

“Aristarchus,”

Aristarchus was a Macedonian from Thessalonica (Acts 20:4). He was seized during the riot at Ephesus (Acts 19:29) and traveled with Paul on the voyage to Rome (Acts 27:2). In Colossians, Paul calls him “my fellow prisoner” (Colossians 4:10). He should not be confused with Archippus (v. 2).

“Demas,”

Demas is named here and in Colossians 4:14 as one of Paul's companions. Later Paul would write, “Demas has forsaken me, having loved this present world” (2 Timothy 4:10). At the time of this letter, he is counted among Paul's fellow laborers.

“Luke,”

Luke is “the beloved physician” (Colossians 4:14), traditionally recognized as the author of the Gospel of Luke and Acts. He traveled with Paul for long periods and remained with him during his later imprisonment: “Only Luke is with me” (2 Timothy 4:11).

“my fellow laborers.”

“Fellow laborers” is the same word Paul used for Philemon in verse 1. Philemon is counted among the same circle of coworkers. Paul's request is personal, but it is made within a wider fellowship of believers serving Christ together.

Study Notes

The matching lists. Colossians 4:10-14 sends greetings from the same five men, along with Jesus called Justus, who may have been unknown to Philemon. The overlap is one of the strongest indications that the two letters were written and sent together. Colossians 4:10-11 also indicates that Aristarchus and Mark were Jewish believers, which suggests that Epaphras, Demas, and Luke were Gentiles.

“Useful.” The word Paul later uses of Mark in 2 Timothy 4:11 (euchrēstos) is the same word he uses of Onesimus in verse 11, “profitable.” Paul did not write 2 Timothy until later, so the connection was not visible to Philemon, but it links the two men for later readers.

Spirit & Life

Three of the names in this verse appear again in a single passage written during Paul's later imprisonment: “Demas has forsaken me, having loved this present world ... Only Luke is with me. Get Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for ministry” (2 Timothy 4:10-11). Here all three stand side by side as Paul's fellow laborers. Later, one has left, one has stayed, and one who had once turned back has become useful again. A place among the faithful today does not guarantee tomorrow. Faithfulness is not a past achievement but a daily dependence on the grace that holds us. “Let him who thinks he stands take heed lest he fall” (1 Corinthians 10:12).

The same names also give hope. Mark had once left the work, and Paul had refused to take him again. Scripture does not tell us how Mark was restored, but it does record that when Paul would not take him, Barnabas did (Acts 15:39). Barnabas was the man the apostles had named “Son of Encouragement” (Acts 4:36). Failure did not have to be the last word in Mark's story. God is able to restore those who have turned back, and He often works through people who refuse to give up on them.

Philemon 1:25

NKJV

Philemon 1:25 The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Amen.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:25 May the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul closes by blessing the whole congregation with the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ. The grace that opened the letter also closes it.

Verse Explanation

“The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ”

Paul ends as he began. The letter opened with “grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ” (v. 3), and it closes with “the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ.” Grace frames everything in between. Every request Paul has made depends on it: receiving Onesimus as a brother, extending grace despite any possible wrong or debt, and refreshing Paul's heart in the Lord.

“be with your spirit.”

“Your” is plural, as in verses 3 and 22. Paul's final blessing rests on the whole congregation. “Spirit” refers to the inner person, the deepest part of a person, where God's grace does its work. Paul prays that the grace of Christ will be present and active in them.

“Amen.”

“Amen” means “so be it.” It affirms the blessing and closes the letter.

Study Notes

The same blessing elsewhere. Paul closes Galatians with the same words: “the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit” (Galatians 6:18). Compare 2 Timothy 4:22.

“Your spirit.” In Greek, “your” is plural while “spirit” is singular. The expression refers to the inner life of each person addressed and places the whole congregation under the same blessing.

“Amen.” Some early manuscripts do not include “Amen” at the end of the letter. The NKJV includes it, following the Greek text behind it.

Spirit & Life

Paul's last words are not a final instruction but a blessing: “The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit.” He has asked Philemon to receive Onesimus, offered to take responsibility for any possible wrong or debt, asked Philemon to refresh his heart, and expressed confidence that he will do even more. None of it can be accomplished by effort alone. Whatever resistance a person feels, whether resentment, hesitation, or wounded pride, lives in the inner person, and that is where grace must work. Paul prays elsewhere that believers will be “strengthened with might through His Spirit in the inner man” (Ephesians 3:16). Grace does not guarantee that every relationship will be restored, and it does not respond in our place. But it gives believers an inward strength to forgive, to love, and to pursue reconciliation that willpower alone cannot supply, and it calls them to act on what it gives.

The “your” is plural, and the blessing falls on the whole house church alike. Onesimus, now a brother in Christ, belongs among the believers Paul is blessing and shares the same grace as Philemon. One was master and the other slave, yet before Christ they stand on equal footing. Both receive the same grace from the same Lord, and grace does not come to anyone by rank.
