NLT

Philemon 1:1 This letter is from Paul, a prisoner for preaching the Good News about Christ Jesus, and from our brother Timothy. I am writing to Philemon, our beloved co-worker, (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul opens his letter by calling himself a prisoner of Christ Jesus rather than a prisoner of Rome, showing he sees his chains as part of serving Christ. He writes with Timothy to Philemon, a dear friend and partner in gospel work.

Verse Explanation

Paul opens his letter by calling himself a prisoner of Christ Jesus rather than a prisoner of Rome, showing he sees his chains as part of serving Christ. He writes with Timothy to Philemon, a dear friend and partner in gospel work.

"Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus,"

Paul wrote this letter from prison. He was under guard, most likely in Rome, and the letter was carried to Colossae along with the letter to the Colossians (Colossians 4:7–9). In most of his letters Paul introduces himself as an apostle, but here he calls himself a prisoner. That is the only title he gives himself as he begins a personal request to a friend.

He says he is a prisoner "of Christ Jesus," not of Rome. The Roman authorities held the keys, but Paul understood that he was in chains because he belonged to Christ and preached Him, and that his imprisonment was in Christ's hands. He describes himself the same way to the Ephesians: "I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus for you Gentiles" (Ephesians 3:1). To the Philippians he wrote that it had become evident to the palace guard "that my chains are in Christ" (Philippians 1:13). His circumstances had not stopped his ministry. It was during this imprisonment that Onesimus, the man this letter is about, came to faith through Paul (v. 10).

"and Timothy our brother,"

Timothy was Paul's younger coworker from Lystra, the son of a Jewish mother and a Greek father, who had traveled with Paul for years (Acts 16:1–3). He is named alongside Paul at the opening of several letters, including Colossians and Philippians, and here he joins Paul in greeting Philemon.

"Brother" means a fellow believer. Those who trust in Christ belong to one family, with God as their Father, and the early Christians used family language for one another as a matter of course. Paul says "our brother," not just "my brother." Timothy was a brother to Philemon as well, part of the same family of faith, even if they had never met.

"To Philemon our beloved friend and fellow laborer,"

Philemon was a believer who lived in Colossae, a town in the Roman province of Asia, in what is now western Turkey. Onesimus is called "one of you" when Paul writes to the Colossians (Colossians 4:9), and Archippus, who is greeted in the next verse, is mentioned there too (Colossians 4:17). Paul had not visited Colossae himself (Colossians 2:1), so he and Philemon had probably come to know each other elsewhere. Many think they met in Ephesus, where Paul taught for two years "so that all who dwelt in Asia heard the word of the Lord Jesus" (Acts 19:10).

The Greek behind "beloved friend" is simply "the beloved," a word built on the New Testament's main word for love. Philemon was dear to Paul, and that warmth runs through the whole letter. "Fellow laborer" means coworker, someone who works alongside another in the same task. Paul uses this word for close partners such as Timothy (Romans 16:21) and Titus (2 Corinthians 8:23). By calling Philemon his fellow laborer, Paul counts him a partner in the work of the gospel, serving Christ at Colossae as Paul served Him in prison.

Study Notes

Paul does not say where he was imprisoned, and three places have been proposed. The traditional view is Rome. Acts says Paul spent two years there under guard in his own rented house, free to receive visitors and preach (Acts 28:16, 30–31). That setting fits well. Luke and Aristarchus, both named in verse 24, sailed with Paul to Rome (Acts 27:2), and a huge city was a natural place for a runaway to disappear. Some prefer Caesarea, where Paul was also held for two years (Acts 24:27).

Others suggest Ephesus. Acts records no imprisonment there, but before any Roman imprisonment Paul could already speak of being "in prisons more frequently" (2 Corinthians 11:23). Ephesus lay only about a hundred miles from Colossae, compared with well over a thousand to Rome. Supporters of this view point to the request for a guest room (v. 22), which seems more natural from nearby. Still, Paul could make travel plans from Rome as well, and travel between Rome and the province of Asia was common.

Rome remains the most widely held view, which would place the letter around AD 60–62. The question cannot be settled with certainty, and nothing in the letter's message depends on it.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Paul also greets Apphia, Archippus, whom he calls a fellow soldier in the faith, and the church that meets in Philemon's home. Including the whole house church means this very personal request will be heard by the entire community of believers.

"to the beloved Apphia,"

Apphia was a woman closely connected to Philemon's household. Scripture does not say how, but many interpreters believe she was Philemon's wife, since she is named right beside him at the head of the home. If so, the letter concerned her directly. In a household of that time, the wife often oversaw the servants and the daily running of the home, so how Onesimus would be received was her concern too. Paul calls her "beloved," a warm word for someone dear to him and to the family of believers. Later in the letter he uses that same word for Onesimus, the returning slave (1:16).

"Archippus our fellow soldier,"

Archippus also appears in Paul's letter to the Colossians, where Paul sends him a personal charge: "Take heed to the ministry which you have received in the Lord, that you may fulfill it" (Colossians 4:17). He was evidently serving the church in some role of ministry. Some have suggested he was Philemon and Apphia's son, but Scripture does not say.

Paul calls him a "fellow soldier." Paul used the same title for Epaphroditus, whom he called his "fellow worker, and fellow soldier" (Philippians 2:25). Paul often pictured the Christian life as a soldier's service, telling Timothy to "endure hardship as a good soldier of Jesus Christ" (2 Timothy 2:3). Soldiers serve under one commander, share hardship, and stand together in the fight. Writing from prison, Paul knew that the gospel work cost something, and he honors Archippus as one who stood with him in it.

"and to the church in your house:"

In the New Testament, a "church" is a gathering of believers, not a building. Christians had no church buildings yet, so they met in private homes. Paul mentions "the church that is in their house" of Priscilla and Aquila (Romans 16:5) and the church in the house of Nymphas (Colossians 4:15). Philemon opened his home to the believers at Colossae, which suggests he had a house large enough to host them and the means to support that kind of hospitality.

The word "your" here is singular and points to Philemon. That makes it significant that Paul includes the whole church in the greeting. This letter makes a deeply personal request of one man about his own household, yet it would be read aloud to the believers who gathered in his home. The matter of Onesimus was not simply private business between a master and his slave. It concerned the whole fellowship, because Onesimus was coming back not only to Philemon's house but to the church that met there. These believers would hear Paul's appeal, they would see how Philemon responded, and they would be the family into which Onesimus was welcomed as a brother.

Philemon 1:3 Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:3 May God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ give you grace and peace. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul's greeting asks that God's undeserved kindness (grace) and His peace rest on the readers. Both gifts come from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ together, a reminder that this letter's hard request is rooted in what God has already given.

Verse Explanation

Paul's greeting asks that God's undeserved kindness (grace) and His peace rest on the readers. Both gifts come from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ together, a reminder that this letter's hard request is rooted in what God has already given.

"Grace to you and peace"

Ordinary Greek letters of the time opened with a simple word of greeting, much like saying "hello." Paul instead uses the word "grace," which sounds similar in Greek, and joins it with "peace," the familiar greeting of the Jewish people (shalom). He opens nearly all his letters this way, but these are more than polite words. Grace is God's undeserved kindness, His favor given to people who have not earned it and could never earn it. It is the reason anyone is saved at all: "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God" (Ephesians 2:8).

Peace is what grace produces. The Hebrew idea behind it is more than the absence of conflict. It means wholeness and well-being, a life set right. Above all, it means being at peace with God: "having been justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1). Grace always comes first, and peace follows from it.

The word "you" is plural, so the blessing is spoken over Philemon, Apphia, Archippus, and the whole church that met in Philemon's house (1:2). Everyone listening as the letter was read had received God's undeserved kindness. That matters for what follows, because Paul will ask Philemon to extend that same kind of kindness to Onesimus, his runaway slave, who had wronged him and deserved nothing from him.

"from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ."

"Our Father" binds Paul, Timothy, Philemon, and the church together as one family under God, which is why Paul can call fellow believers brothers and sisters (1:1–2). Paul also names the Father and Jesus Christ together, with a single "from," as the one source of grace and peace. Only God can give these gifts, and Paul places Jesus right alongside the Father as their giver.

Calling Jesus "Lord" carries special weight in a letter about a master and his slave. "Lord" means master, the one with rightful authority. In the letter to the Colossians, sent to the same city around the same time and carried in part by Onesimus himself (Colossians 4:9), Paul tells Christian masters to treat their servants justly, "knowing that you also have a Master in heaven" (Colossians 4:1). Philemon and Onesimus stood under the same Lord, and both depended on the same grace.

Philemon 1:4 I thank my God, making mention of you always in my prayers,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:4 I always thank my God when I pray for you, Philemon, (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul thanks God every time he mentions Philemon in his prayers. Beginning with genuine gratitude shows that Paul's upcoming appeal flows from real affection, not criticism.

Verse Explanation

Paul thanks God every time he mentions Philemon in his prayers. Beginning with genuine gratitude shows that Paul's upcoming appeal flows from real affection, not criticism.

"I thank my God,"

Paul's thanks go to God, not first to Philemon. The love and faith Paul is about to praise in him (v. 5) are real, but Paul sees them as God's work in Philemon's life, so he thanks the One who produced them. Calling Him "my God" is personal. Paul is not speaking of a distant deity but of the God he knows and serves, the same God he shares with Philemon as a fellow believer. Paul often opened his letters this way. He told the Philippians, "I thank my God upon every remembrance of you" (Philippians 1:3).

From here through verse 21, the "you" Paul addresses is singular in Greek. Having greeted the whole church in Philemon's house, Paul now turns to speak to Philemon himself, though the church still hears what he writes.

"making mention of you always in my prayers,"

To "make mention" of someone in prayer means to name that person before God. Paul used the same kind of language with the believers in Rome, telling them that "without ceasing I make mention of you always in my prayers" (Romans 1:9). "Always" does not mean Paul prayed every waking moment. It means that whenever he prayed, Philemon was included. His friend had a regular place in his prayers, and each time Paul brought him before God, he gave thanks for him.

This was the prayer of a man in chains for Christ. Paul could not travel to Colossae or visit Philemon, but he could still bring him before God, and he did so faithfully. Paul goes on to describe what he asked God to do in Philemon's life (v. 6). The prayer would also go the other way. Near the end of the letter, Paul tells Philemon that he hopes "through your prayers" to be released and come to them (v. 22).

NKJV

Philemon 1:5 hearing of your love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:5 because I keep hearing about your faith in the Lord Jesus and your love for all of God’s people. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul has heard about Philemon's love and faith directed toward the Lord Jesus and toward all of God's people. Faith in Christ and love for fellow believers naturally go together, and Philemon has shown both.

Verse Explanation

Paul has heard about Philemon's love and faith directed toward the Lord Jesus and toward all of God's people. Faith in Christ and love for fellow believers naturally go together, and Philemon has shown both.

"hearing of your love and faith"

This is why Paul thanks God whenever he prays for Philemon (v. 4): he keeps hearing good reports about him. Paul was in prison and could not visit Colossae, so the news came to him through others. Epaphras, a believer from Colossae, was with Paul at this time (v. 23), and he had already told Paul about the Colossian believers' "love in the Spirit" (Colossians 1:7–8). He may well be one source of what Paul heard about Philemon, though Paul does not say. Philemon's love and faith were evident enough that people far away talked about them.

Paul names love first. Faith is the deeper root, but love is what this letter is about. Paul will soon say that Philemon's love has refreshed the hearts of God's people (v. 7), and he will make his request on the basis of love (v. 9).

"which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints,"

Philemon's faith is directed toward the Lord Jesus, and his love reaches out to God's people. Paul joins the two because they belong together. Trusting Christ shows itself in loving those who belong to Him. Paul elsewhere describes true faith as "faith working through love" (Galatians 5:6), and John puts the two side by side as God's command: "that we should believe on the name of His Son Jesus Christ and love one another" (1 John 3:23). Paul praises the Colossian church in nearly the same words: "your faith in Christ Jesus and... your love for all the saints" (Colossians 1:4).

"Saints" translates a Greek word meaning "holy ones," those set apart for God. In the New Testament it is not a title for an especially devout few. It describes every believer, because everyone who belongs to Christ has been set apart by God as His own. Paul calls whole churches "saints," including congregations with serious problems (1 Corinthians 1:2). So "all the saints" means all of God's people, ordinary believers included.

The word "all" matters in this letter. Philemon's love was not limited to friends or to people of his own social standing. That is the kind of love Paul is about to call on, because Onesimus, Philemon's runaway slave, has now become a believer and is one of the saints too. Paul's request will ask Philemon to show the same love he already shows to God's people to the one person he might be least inclined to welcome.

Study Notes

The Greek word order here is unusual. Paul literally writes "your love and the faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints," so both qualities seem attached to both objects. "Faith toward all the saints" sounds strange, so most interpreters read the sentence crosswise: love toward the saints, faith toward the Lord. Ancient writers often used this kind of crisscross pattern, called a chiasm. Paul's change of preposition, one word before "the Lord Jesus" and a different one before "all the saints," may support that reading.

The parallel in Colossians, written at the same time, puts the pairs in the plain order: "your faith in Christ Jesus and of your love for all the saints" (Colossians 1:4). A second view takes "faith" here in its other common sense of faithfulness or loyalty, which a believer can show toward fellow Christians as well as toward Christ. Either reading arrives at the same point: Philemon's relationship with the Lord and his treatment of God's people go together.

Philemon 1:6
NKJV

Philemon 1:6 that the sharing of your faith may become effective by the acknowledgment of every good thing which is in you in Christ Jesus.(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:6 And I am praying that you will put into action the generosity that comes from your faith as you understand and experience all the good things we have in Christ. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul prays that Philemon's partnership in the faith will become active and effective as he grows in understanding all the good things that belong to believers in Christ. Knowing what Christ has done for us is meant to produce good actions toward others.

Verse Explanation

Paul prays that Philemon's partnership in the faith will become active and effective as he grows in understanding all the good things that belong to believers in Christ. Knowing what Christ has done for us is meant to produce good actions toward others.

"that the sharing of your faith"

Here Paul tells Philemon what he prays for whenever he mentions him in prayer (v. 4). The word translated "sharing" is the Greek koinonia, often rendered "fellowship." It means having something in common with others and taking part in it together, like partners in a shared work. Paul used the same word when he thanked the Philippians "for your fellowship in the gospel from the first day until now" (Philippians 1:5). So Paul is not mainly talking about Philemon telling others about Jesus, as "sharing your faith" usually means today. He means the partnership Philemon has with other believers because they hold the same faith. Philemon had already shown this partnership through his love for all of God's people and his generosity toward them (vv. 5, 7). Later Paul builds on this bond when he asks Philemon to count him as a "partner" (v. 17).

"may become effective"

The Greek word for "effective" means active or at work, and it gives us our word "energy." Paul uses it elsewhere for "a great and effective door" of opportunity (1 Corinthians 16:9), and Hebrews uses it to describe the word of God as "living and powerful" (Hebrews 4:12). Paul prays that the faith Philemon shares with other believers will not sit still but will go to work in practical ways. Faith that belongs to Christ is meant to act, the kind of "faith working through love" (Galatians 5:6). Paul knows that Philemon will soon face a real test of this, because the letter will ask him to welcome back Onesimus.

"by the acknowledgment of every good thing which is in you"

The word "acknowledgment" means more than knowing facts. It describes a full, personal understanding that grows deeper over time. Paul prayed something similar for the believers at Colossae, Philemon's own city: that they would "be filled with the knowledge of His will" so they would be "fruitful in every good work and increasing in the knowledge of God" (Colossians 1:9–10). The "good thing" here is every blessing God has given His people. Paul says elsewhere that God "has blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ" (Ephesians 1:3). As Philemon understands more fully how much God has given him, that understanding will spill over into how he treats others. A man who knows how much grace he has received will be ready to show grace.

Paul uses the same word "good" again a few verses later, when he hopes Philemon's "good deed" will be done willingly rather than by compulsion (v. 14). The good Philemon has received in Christ is meant to become the good he does.

"in Christ Jesus."

To be "in Christ" means to belong to Him and to be joined to Him by faith. It describes a believer's new identity: "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation" (2 Corinthians 5:17). Every good thing a Christian has, including forgiveness, new life, and a place in God's family, comes through this union with Christ. It is also what binds believers to one another. Philemon's partnership with other Christians rests on the fact that they all belong to the same Lord. That same bond now includes Onesimus, who has also come to belong to Christ.

Study Notes

This verse is widely regarded as the hardest sentence in the letter to translate, because nearly every phrase can be taken more than one way. "The sharing of your faith" has three main readings. Some take it as evangelism, Philemon passing his faith on to others, though Paul nowhere else uses koinonia in that sense. Others take it as the fellowship or partnership believers have through their common faith. A third view sees the generous sharing that faith produces, since Paul uses the same word for a financial gift: "to make a certain contribution for the poor among the saints" (Romans 15:26). The second and third readings overlap, and both fit Philemon's known kindness (v. 7) and the request Paul is about to make. "Acknowledgment" (epignosis) is Paul's usual word for full, personal knowledge. It does not mean other people giving Philemon credit for the good in him.

There is also a textual difference. The Greek text behind the NKJV reads "every good thing which is in you." Many manuscripts, including several of the oldest, read "in us," and most modern translations follow them. The two Greek words differ by a single letter and came to be pronounced almost alike, which made copying slips easy. "In us" would make the good things blessings all believers share, while "in you" focuses on Philemon. The overall sense is not greatly affected.

Main Idea

Philemon's love has brought Paul great joy and encouragement, because Philemon has refreshed the hearts of God's people. Paul will soon ask Philemon to refresh his own heart in the same way (v. 20).

Verse Explanation

Philemon's love has brought Paul great joy and encouragement, because Philemon has refreshed the hearts of God's people. Paul will soon ask Philemon to refresh his own heart in the same way (v. 20).

"For we have great joy and consolation in your love,"

"For" ties this verse back to Paul's thanksgiving. He has been thanking God in his prayers because he hears of Philemon's "love and faith" toward the Lord Jesus and toward all God's people (1:4–5). Now he tells Philemon what that news has done for him personally. The word translated "consolation" means comfort or encouragement, the kind that strengthens someone who is going through hardship. Paul was writing as a prisoner for Christ (1:1, 9), and reports of how Philemon loved other believers lifted his spirits in his chains. Paul did not need to be in Colossae to be helped by Philemon's love. Simply hearing about it brought him joy.

"because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you,"

This is why Philemon's love meant so much to Paul. It was not a feeling he kept to himself but something that had done real good for fellow believers. The Greek word for "hearts" literally refers to a person's inner organs. In the ancient world people spoke of these inward parts as the place where deep feelings are felt, much as we speak of the heart today. The word points to a person's inner self and deepest affections. Paul uses it twice more in this short letter. He calls Onesimus "my own heart" (1:12), and he asks Philemon to "refresh my heart in the Lord" (1:20).

The verb "refreshed" means to give rest or relief, like a weary traveler stopping to recover his strength. Jesus used the same verb when He promised, "Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). Philemon had given that kind of rest to God's people, renewing them in their inner lives. Paul does not say exactly how he did it. Since the church met in his home (1:2), many interpreters think his hospitality and generosity toward the believers there were part of it. Whatever form it took, his love had touched people deeply and left them stronger.

"brother."

Paul ends the sentence by calling Philemon "brother," a fellow believer in God's family. The word comes last in the Greek sentence, which gives it extra warmth. Paul speaks to Philemon as a friend he loves, not as an apostle issuing orders. The man who had brought rest to so many of God's people is about to hear Paul's request, and Paul makes it as one brother speaking to another.

Philemon 1:8 Therefore, though I might be very bold in Christ to command you what is fitting,(NKJV)

NLT

Philemon 1:8 That is why I am boldly asking a favor of you. I could demand it in the name of Christ because it is the right thing for you to do. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because of Philemon's proven love, Paul explains that he could use his authority as an apostle to simply order Philemon to do the right thing. He mentions this authority only to make clear that he is choosing not to use it.

Verse Explanation

Because of Philemon's proven love, Paul explains that he could use his authority as an apostle to simply order Philemon to do the right thing. He mentions this authority only to make clear that he is choosing not to use it.

"Therefore,"

This word connects Paul's request to what he has just said about Philemon. Paul has thanked God for Philemon's love and faith toward the Lord Jesus and toward all God's people, and he has told him that "the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother" (v. 7). Philemon had already shown what kind of man he was. Because of that, Paul now turns to the matter that is the reason for the letter.

"though I might be very bold in Christ"

The Greek word behind "bold" describes freedom to speak plainly and openly, without hesitation. Paul is saying he has every right to be direct with Philemon. That right comes "in Christ." It does not rest on Paul's age, his friendship with Philemon, or his force of personality, but on his relationship to Christ and the calling Christ gave him. Paul was an apostle, sent out personally by the risen Lord, "not from men nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father" (Galatians 1:1). As an apostle, he had authority to give churches binding instructions. He did this elsewhere, writing, "We command you, brethren, in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (2 Thessalonians 3:6).

That makes the opening of this letter stand out. In the letter to the Colossians, which was carried to the same city at about the same time (Colossians 4:7–9), Paul introduces himself as "an apostle of Jesus Christ by the will of God" (Colossians 1:1). Here he never uses that title. He calls himself "a prisoner of Christ Jesus" (v. 1). Paul does not deny his authority now. He names it plainly, but only so Philemon will understand that he is choosing not to use it.

"to command you what is fitting,"

"Command" means to give an order as someone with the right to be obeyed. "What is fitting" describes what is proper and right, what ought to be done. Paul uses the same expression when he speaks of conduct that is "fitting in the Lord" (Colossians 3:18). The request he is about to make is not an optional favor or a matter of personal taste. It is what a believer should do, and Paul could have required it.

The sentence does not end here, though. Paul has the authority to command, but he sets it aside and appeals to Philemon instead. He wanted Philemon to do what was right because he chose to, not because he was ordered to. That gives Philemon room to show the same love that had already refreshed so many of God's people.

Philemon 1:9 But because of our love, I prefer simply to ask you. Consider this as a request from me — Paul, an old man and now also a prisoner for the sake of Christ Jesus. (NLT)

Main Idea

Instead of commanding, Paul chooses to make a request based on love. He reminds Philemon that he is an old man and a prisoner for Jesus, appealing to Philemon's compassion rather than pressuring him.

Verse Explanation

Instead of commanding, Paul chooses to make a request based on love. He reminds Philemon that he is an old man and a prisoner for Jesus, appealing to Philemon's compassion rather than pressuring him.

"yet for love's sake I rather appeal to you—"

In the previous verse Paul said he could be "very bold in Christ" to command Philemon to do what is right (1:8). As an apostle he had that authority, but here he sets it aside. The word "rather" shows his choice: instead of giving an order, he makes a request. The Greek word translated "appeal" means to urge, plead with, or encourage. It is how a friend speaks to a friend, not how a commander speaks to a soldier.

Paul makes his request "for love's sake." Love is the ground he stands on. He has already thanked God for Philemon's "love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints" (1:5), and he has said that he took great joy and comfort in that love, "because the hearts of the saints have been refreshed by you, brother" (1:7). Philemon was already known for showing love to God's people. Paul now appeals to that same love. He is not asking Philemon to become a different person. He is asking him to keep being the man he has already shown himself to be.

The way Paul asks also fits what he is asking for. A loving response cannot really be forced. If Paul ordered Philemon to show kindness, the kindness would come from obedience to Paul rather than from Philemon's own heart. By choosing to ask in love, Paul treats Philemon as a brother and leaves room for him to respond freely.

"being such a one as Paul, the aged,"

Paul describes himself as an old man. His exact age is not given, but decades earlier, when Stephen was stoned, he was called "a young man named Saul" (Acts 7:58). Since then he had spent years traveling, preaching, and suffering for the gospel. He had been beaten, shipwrecked, and imprisoned many times (2 Corinthians 11:23–27). In the ancient world, as in many cultures today, an older man's request carried weight and called for respect. Paul does not mention his age to win pity. He is asking Philemon to consider who is making this request: an old friend who has given his life to serving Christ.

"and now also a prisoner of Jesus Christ—"

Paul is not only old. He is "now also" in chains. He opened the letter by calling himself "a prisoner of Christ Jesus" (1:1), and he repeats it here. Roman guards held him, but he did not see himself as Rome's prisoner. He was there because of his service to Christ, and he understood his chains as part of belonging to his Lord. He used the same description when urging the Ephesians: "I, therefore, the prisoner of the Lord, beseech you to walk worthy of the calling with which you were called" (Ephesians 4:1).

This detail adds tenderness to the appeal. Paul is not writing from comfort or safety, asking Philemon to make a sacrifice he himself has never made. He writes as someone who has already given up his freedom for Christ. An old man in chains, choosing to ask rather than command, makes his request in a way that speaks to Philemon's heart rather than pressuring him.

Study Notes

A small spelling difference stands behind a real translation question. The Greek manuscripts read presbytes, "old man," which the NKJV renders "the aged." A very similar word, presbeutes, means "ambassador." Spelling was not fully standardized at the time, so some scholars, notably J. B. Lightfoot in the nineteenth century, argued that Paul meant "ambassador." The Revised Standard Version adopted that reading, and several modern versions mention it in a footnote. Support for it comes from Ephesians, written at about the same time, where Paul uses the related verb: "I am an ambassador in chains" (Ephesians 6:20). A few ancient texts also use the "old man" spelling for envoys.

The NKJV's rendering, which most translations keep, fits the verse better. Paul has just set aside his right to command (v. 8), and claiming an ambassador's rank would bring that authority right back in. An old man in chains is someone appealing, not someone giving orders. "Aged" need not mean very old by modern standards. Ancient writers could use the word for a man in his fifties, which would suit Paul's likely age at this time.

Study Notes

Scripture never actually calls Onesimus a runaway, but the traditional view rests on good evidence. He "departed" (v. 15), he may have "wronged" Philemon or owed him something (v. 18), and he needed an advocate before he could be received again. The main alternative draws on Roman law. A legal opinion of the jurist Proculus, preserved in a later collection of Roman law (Justinian's Digest), held that a slave who went to a friend of his master to plead for help in a dispute was not legally a fugitive. On this view, Onesimus had fallen out with Philemon and deliberately sought out Paul, his master's respected friend, to speak for him, and came to faith while he was there.

A letter from Pliny the Younger, a Roman official writing a few decades later, shows that such appeals happened. A freedman, a former slave, who had angered his former master Sabinianus came to Pliny in tears, and Pliny wrote asking Sabinianus to forgive him. The comparison is striking. Pliny appeals to the man's repentance and to Sabinianus's gentleness, and he mentions that he scolded the man sharply. He never suggests their relationship has changed. Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus as a brother and offers to pay his debt himself.

The mediator view is possible, but the traditional understanding remains the more natural reading. The substance of Paul's appeal is the same under either.

The brotherhood Paul enjoyed for a short time in prison, Philemon could enjoy every day.

"both in the flesh and in the Lord."

"In the flesh" refers to Philemon's earthly connection with Onesimus: their life together in the same household, with its daily work and ordinary dealings as master and servant. "In the Lord" refers to their new spiritual bond as two men who both belong to Christ. Paul's tie to Onesimus was only spiritual, but Philemon's was both. This means their brotherhood in Christ could not stay limited to Sunday worship. It had to shape how Philemon spoke to Onesimus, how he treated him, and how he directed his work. Paul told Christian masters to give their servants "what is just and fair, knowing that you also have a Master in heaven" (Colossians 4:1). For Philemon, receiving Onesimus as a beloved brother would mean welcoming him back into his home on new terms, in everyday life as well as in the Lord.

Study Notes

Many wonder why Paul asks Philemon to treat Onesimus as a brother but never simply tells him to set Onesimus free. Some background helps. Slavery in the Roman world was not based on race. People became slaves through war, debt, birth, or kidnapping, and freeing slaves was common, especially in cities. It was still a system in which a human being was legal property and could be beaten or sold. Paul wrote as a prisoner to small Christian communities with no power over Roman law. The New Testament never calls believers to overthrow slavery by force, but neither does it treat slavery as good. Paul told believing slaves, "if you can be made free, rather use it" (1 Corinthians 7:21), and he listed "kidnappers," the men who supplied the slave trade, among the lawless (1 Timothy 1:10).

What Paul does here works from the inside. He asks Philemon to receive his slave as a "beloved brother," both "in the flesh and in the Lord," and to welcome him "as you would me" (v. 17). That is very hard to reconcile with continuing to treat Onesimus as property. Whether Paul's "even more than I say" (v. 21) meant freedom is not stated. What is stated is a brotherhood that many later Christians recognized as incompatible with one believer owning another.

Not every detail of the story matches. Paul offered to repay a financial loss, while Christ bore the full penalty of sin before a holy God. But the heart of it is the same: the one who did no wrong takes the guilty one's debt on himself so that the guilty one can be welcomed rather than rejected.

Paul usually dictated his letters to a helper who wrote them down. One of them, Tertius, even added his own greeting in Romans (Romans 16:22). Here his handwriting does more than confirm who wrote the letter. In the ancient world, a statement of debt written in a person's own hand was a binding promise to pay, much like a signed IOU today. By writing these words himself, Paul turns the offer he just made, "put that on my account" (v. 18), into a commitment Philemon could hold him to.

"I will repay—"

The Greek word for "repay" was used for paying damages or making good on a loss. Paul does not simply ask Philemon to overlook whatever Onesimus took or cost him. He promises to cover it himself, even though he is a prisoner with no obvious means. Whatever Onesimus owed his master, Paul places that debt on himself so that nothing stands in the way of Philemon receiving him.

This gives a clear picture of what Christ has done for sinners. Like Onesimus, we were enslaved and in the wrong, unable to make things right. Christ took our debt on Himself so that we could be welcomed by God. Paul wrote to the same church at Colossae that God had "wiped out the handwriting of requirements that was against us," taking it out of the way and "having nailed it to the cross" (Colossians 2:14). Paul's handwritten pledge was a small, human offer to pay a sum of money. Christ paid with His own life: "For Christ also suffered once for sins, the just for the unjust, that He might bring us to God" (1 Peter 3:18). Not every detail of Paul's situation lines up with the gospel, but the heart of it does: one person takes on another's debt so that the guilty can be received.

"not to mention to you that you owe me even your own self besides."

Paul says he will "not mention" something, and then mentions it, a gentle way of reminding Philemon of something they both knew. Most likely Philemon came to faith through Paul's ministry, perhaps during Paul's long stay in Ephesus, when "all who dwelt in Asia heard the word of the Lord Jesus" (Acts 19:10). Paul had never visited Colossae himself (Colossians 2:1), so Philemon probably met him elsewhere. If Philemon heard the gospel from Paul, then he owed Paul far more than money. He owed him the message through which he received eternal life.

That puts the two debts side by side. Onesimus owed Philemon something that could be counted and repaid. Philemon owed Paul something no one could ever repay. Paul does not cancel his promise, and he does not use Philemon's debt as a weapon to force his hand. He simply lets Philemon see the situation clearly. Jesus made the same point in His parable about a servant who was forgiven an enormous debt but refused to forgive a small one: "Should you not also have had compassion on your fellow servant, just as I had pity on you?" (Matthew 18:33). A man who has been given so much is well placed to forgive the far smaller wrong done to him.

He leaves room for Philemon to decide for himself how far his love will go. Paul's confidence rests on the kind of man Philemon already was: someone who would not settle for the bare minimum when a brother in Christ was involved.

"my fellow laborers."

Paul does not describe these men as his assistants or followers but as workers alongside him in the gospel. He used the same title for Philemon in the opening of the letter (v. 1). Paul prays that Christ's grace will be at work deep inside each of them, not just shaping their outward behavior.
