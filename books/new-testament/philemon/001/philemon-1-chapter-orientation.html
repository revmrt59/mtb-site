Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 1
Main Idea:

Paul appeals to Philemon, for love’s sake, to receive Onesimus no longer as a slave but as a beloved brother, as he would receive Paul, and to charge any wrong or debt to Paul’s account.

Setting:

Paul writes from prison, probably in Rome around AD 60–62, to Philemon, whose house church was most likely at Colossae (Colossians 4:9). Onesimus, Philemon’s runaway slave, has come to faith through Paul and is being sent home.

Chapter Outline:

  • Philemon 1:1–3 — Greeting to Philemon and the Church in His House

  • Philemon 1:4–7 — Thanksgiving for Philemon’s Love and Faith

  • Philemon 1:8–11 — Paul Appeals for Love’s Sake on Behalf of Onesimus

  • Philemon 1:12–16 — No Longer as a Slave but a Beloved Brother

  • Philemon 1:17–20 — Receive Him as You Would Me

  • Philemon 1:21–22 — Confidence in Philemon and Hope of a Visit

  • Philemon 1:23–25 — Greetings and Grace

Key Verse:

“no longer as a slave but more than a slave—a beloved brother, especially to me but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord.” (Philemon 1:16, NKJV)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Paul begins by thanking God for Philemon’s “love and faith which you have toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the saints” (1:5). Philemon’s home was a place where “the hearts of the saints have been refreshed” (1:7). The rest of the letter asks whether that love will reach one more person. Onesimus, the slave who had run away from Philemon, has come to faith and now belongs among the saints Philemon loves.

Paul describes Onesimus almost entirely by his new relationships. He is “my son Onesimus, whom I have begotten while in my chains” (1:10), “my own heart” (1:12), and now “a beloved brother” (1:16). His name means useful, and Paul plays on it: once “unprofitable,” he is now “profitable to you and to me” (1:11). Paul speaks gently of the flight, saying only that Onesimus “departed for a while” (1:15), and he mentions any wrong or debt only conditionally (1:18).

Paul could command, yet he appeals, “that your good deed might not be by compulsion, as it were, but voluntary” (1:14). And he does more than ask: “Receive him as you would me” (1:17); “put that on my account” (1:18). Here the letter gives a picture of the gospel. Believers are accepted through Christ’s standing (Ephesians 1:6), He bore their sins (1 Peter 2:24), they are received as members of God’s household (Ephesians 2:19), and those who have been welcomed this way are called to welcome others (Romans 15:7). Paul’s offer could not atone for anything, because only Christ’s sacrifice atones for sin, but Paul’s willingness to bear the cost points to what Christ has done.

Chapter Sections

Philemon 1:1–3 — Greeting to Philemon and the Church in His House

Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus, writes with Timothy to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus and the church that meets in Philemon’s house. Though the letter is personal, the whole congregation hears it, and grace and peace frame what follows.

Philemon 1:4–7 — Thanksgiving for Philemon’s Love and Faith

Paul thanks God for Philemon’s love and faith and prays that the fellowship he shares in Christ will work itself out fully. Philemon is known for refreshing God’s people, and Paul’s request will ask him to do it again.

Philemon 1:8–11 — Paul Appeals for Love’s Sake on Behalf of Onesimus

Though he could command, Paul chooses to appeal for love’s sake as an aged prisoner of Jesus Christ. Only then does he name Onesimus, his son in the faith, whose life now matches his name.

Philemon 1:12–16 — No Longer as a Slave but a Beloved Brother

Paul sends Onesimus back as his own heart, though he would have liked to keep him, so that Philemon’s kindness will be freely given. He suggests that God may have been at work in the separation, so that Philemon would have Onesimus back forever as a beloved brother.

Philemon 1:17–20 — Receive Him as You Would Me

Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus as he would receive Paul himself and, writing with his own hand, promises to repay whatever wrong or debt there may be. Paul reminds Philemon that Philemon owes him his very self, most likely because Philemon came to faith through Paul, and asks Philemon to refresh Paul’s heart in the Lord by welcoming Onesimus.

Philemon 1:21–22 — Confidence in Philemon and Hope of a Visit

Paul writes with confidence that Philemon will obey and will do even more than he asks, though he does not say what that more would be. He also asks for a guest room, trusting that their prayers will lead to his release.

Philemon 1:23–25 — Greetings and Grace

Paul passes on greetings from Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas and Luke. The letter that began with grace ends with it, as Paul commends them to the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ.

Why This Chapter Matters

Philemon shows what life in Christ does to human relationships. A master and a slave now stand as brothers “both in the flesh and in the Lord” (1:16). Paul does not command Onesimus’s freedom, but he plants a truth that cannot leave the master–slave relationship unchanged: in Christ a person cannot finally be treated as property.

The letter also shows that reconciliation costs someone something. Paul offers to pay so that the way between two brothers can be opened, giving a clear picture of what Christ did for sinners. And it shows how love works among God’s people: Paul seeks a willing response, because goodness that is forced is not the goodness he wants.

Key Themes to Watch For

Love as the ground of the appeal. Philemon’s known love becomes the basis of Paul’s request (1:5, 7, 9, 16).

A new identity in Christ. Onesimus becomes Paul’s son, profitable, and a beloved brother (1:10–11, 16).

Costly identification. Paul calls Onesimus his own heart and offers to take on whatever wrong or debt there may be (1:12, 17–19).

Voluntary goodness. Paul could command but appeals, wanting a free response (1:8–9, 14, 21).

Fellowship and partnership. The “sharing” of faith (1:6) and the word “partner” (1:17) express the same idea of a common life in Christ.

Key Words & Concepts

Onesimus. A Greek name meaning useful. Paul plays on that meaning when he says Onesimus was once “unprofitable” but is now “profitable” (1:10–11).

Heart. Greek splanchna, the inner self and deep affection. Paul calls Onesimus “my own heart” (1:12) and asks Philemon to “refresh my heart” (1:20).

Sharing and partner. Greek koinonia and a related word, a shared life in Christ that carries obligations to one another (1:6, 17).

Slave. Onesimus was legally Philemon’s property. Roman slavery involved real coercion and inequality, which gives Paul’s words “no longer as a slave” their force (1:16).

“Put that on my account.” Business language for charging a debt to someone else. Paul states it conditionally, “if he has wronged you or owes anything” (1:18).

The church in your house. Early believers met in homes, and a congregation gathered in Philemon’s house. Because the letter is addressed to that church as well as to Philemon, the whole fellowship would see how he received Onesimus (1:2).
