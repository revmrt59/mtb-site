ANY - (eí tis, Strong's G1536)

Summary:

The word ei tis combines a conditional word ("if") with an indefinite pronoun ("any," "anyone," or "anything"). It is used in Scripture to set up a condition, test a claim, or make a logical appeal based on a shared assumption. In Philippians 2:1, the word introduces Paul's passionate appeal for unity, asking the Philippians to consider the reality of their shared life in Christ—if they have experienced any encouragement, comfort, or fellowship in the Spirit, they ought to live together in humble harmony.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The expression ei tis combines two basic grammatical building blocks: the conditional particle ei (meaning "if") and the indefinite pronoun tis (meaning "any," "anyone," or "anything"). Together, they function as a single unit to pose a conditional scenario.

In ancient Greek writing and speech, this expression served a variety of communicative purposes. Depending on the setting, an author might use it to set an open condition, present a requirement for discipleship or leadership, or issue a persuasive rhetorical challenge. When used rhetorically, the speaker does not express doubt about whether the condition is true. Instead, the speaker invites the hearers to agree with a recognized reality and then act upon the logical consequences of that reality.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means "if any," "if anyone," or "if anything."

When a speaker uses this word, it introduces a premise that leads to a specific conclusion or command. The word can function in three main ways:

  • Setting a prerequisite: Framing a baseline condition that must be met for an action to follow (for example, "if anyone desires to follow Me").

  • Testing a claim: Challenging self-examination by testing whether a quality is genuinely present (for example, "if anyone considers himself religious").

  • Making a rhetorical appeal: Pointing to a truth that the audience already knows and experiences, using it as the ground for an urgent request (for example, "if there is any comfort of love").

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, this phrase appears in several distinct settings. Jesus uses it when laying out the demands of discipleship, presenting the choice to follow Him as an open invitation requiring wholehearted surrender. In the pastoral letters, Paul uses it to outline necessary qualifications for church overseers, specifying that if a man meets the basic standard of being blameless, he may be considered for leadership.

Elsewhere, apostles use the phrase to provoke self-reflection. James uses it to warn readers against empty religion, pointing out that if anyone claims to be religious while leaving his tongue unchecked, his religion is useless. Paul also uses the term to direct the minds of believers toward wholesome topics, urging them to focus on whatever is good if there is any excellence or anything praiseworthy.

4. Theological Implications

Scripture frequently grounds moral and relational obligations in the reality of what God has already done. Divine commands are not issued in a vacuum; they flow from spiritual truths already granted to God's people.

By framing appeals through conditional phrasing, biblical writers call believers to draw upon their lived experience of divine grace. Unity, humility, and love within the church are not arbitrary rules imposed from the outside. They are the natural fruit of the gospel. When believers reflect on whether they have received any mercy or spiritual fellowship from God, the obvious answer pushes them to extend that same grace to one another.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:1, Paul piles up four conditional statements back to back, each using this target expression: "if there is any consolation in Christ, if any comfort of love, if any fellowship of the Spirit, if any affection and mercy..."

Paul is not questioning whether the Philippian believers have experienced Christ's comfort or the Spirit's fellowship. He knows they have. By repeating "if any," he uses a persuasive rhetorical style that essentially means, "Since you have experienced all these rich blessings in Christ—and you know you have—then complete my joy by living in harmony."

The word serves as a mirror held up to the church. If the believers have received even the smallest measure of Christ's encouragement and mercy, that reality leaves no room for rivalry, conceit, or selfish ambition. The target word grounds Paul's call for selflessness in Philippians 2:2–4 directly in the reality of God's grace.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding how conditional phrasing functions in biblical appeals protects readers from misinterpreting Paul's tone. A casual reader might assume Paul felt uncertain about the spiritual state of the Philippians. However, comparing this passage to similar structures in Paul's writings reveals that he regularly uses this construction as a gentle, compelling argument.

Furthermore, this passage demonstrates a foundational principle of Christian ethics: gospel indicative precedes gospel imperative. Paul does not demand unity before pointing to Christ. He first reminds the church of the consolation, love, and fellowship they possess in Christ, using those realities as the absolute foundation for how they ought to treat one another.
