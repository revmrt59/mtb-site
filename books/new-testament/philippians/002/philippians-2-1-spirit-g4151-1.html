SPIRIT - (pneûma, Strong's G4151)

Summary:

The word pneuma broadly refers to wind, breath, a spirit, or the Holy Spirit. In Philippians 2:1, it identifies the Holy Spirit as the source and bond of Christian fellowship. Paul points to the shared life that believers possess through the Spirit as a compelling ground for humility and unity within the church.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greek world, pneuma carried the primary sense of air in motion, such as a breeze, wind, or the breath that sustains biological life. Over time, it was also used to describe immaterial existence, spiritual beings, or the human inner self.

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament and throughout the New Testament, the term received a distinct theological focus. While it can still denote breath, wind, or the human spirit, its most vital biblical usage refers to the Holy Spirit, the third Person of the Godhead. Rather than describing an impersonal force or natural phenomenon, the New Testament frequently presents pneuma as the active, personal presence of God indwelling believers and empowering the church.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word pneuma can refer to several distinct realities depending on the context:

  • Breath or physical air moving in and out of the lungs.

  • Wind or blowing air.

  • The human spirit, representing the non-material dimension of human nature, including consciousness, thoughts, and spiritual awareness.

  • An invisible spiritual entity, whether righteous (such as angels) or evil (such as unclean spirits).

  • The Holy Spirit, God's personal Spirit who convicts, regenerates, sanctifies, and unites God's people.

Here in Philippians 2:1, the word refers directly to the Holy Spirit.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, pneuma is used to highlight the contrast between the material and immaterial, as well as between human weakness and divine power. When describing humans, it indicates the inner seat of spiritual life, prayer, and responsiveness toward God. When describing demonic forces, it marks invisible spiritual opposition.

When used of the Holy Spirit, pneuma stresses divine agency. The Spirit regenerates sinners, imparts spiritual gifts, produces holy fruit, and guides believers into truth. The Spirit is also the unifying element among God's people. Church unity is not merely a social agreement; it is produced and sustained by the common indwelling of the same Holy Spirit in every true believer.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical presentation of pneuma as the Holy Spirit underscores several fundamental truths:

  • Personality and Deity: The Holy Spirit acts with intelligence, will, and emotion, working in harmony with the Father and the Son in the work of redemption.

  • Spiritual Regeneration: New birth and transformation are the sovereign work of the Spirit, who brings dead hearts to life.

  • Covenant Indwelling: Under the New Covenant, the Spirit abides within every believer, making the church collectively and Christians individually the temple of God.

  • Essential Unity: Because every believer receives the same Holy Spirit, the church possesses an underlying spiritual unity that Christians are called to guard and express in practical relationships.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:1, Paul builds an argument using four conditional statements ("if there is any..."). These are not expressions of doubt, but rhetorical appeals grounded in realities the Philippians have experienced. Paul lists four spiritual realities: encouragement in Christ, comfort of love, fellowship of the Spirit, and affection and mercy.

In this verse, pneuma appears in the genitive singular neuter form, linked directly with "fellowship" (koinōnia). This expression carries a rich double emphasis:

  • It refers to the partnership and common life created by the Holy Spirit among believers.

  • It points to the shared participation that all believers have in the Holy Spirit Himself.

Paul's argument in the opening verses of Philippians 2 moves directly into a call for selfless humility and unity ("fulfill my joy by being like-minded," Phil. 2:2). By reminding the church of their shared "fellowship of the Spirit," Paul shows that divisions, personal ambition, and pride directly contradict the very Spirit they share. The Holy Spirit is the common bond that makes true harmony in the church possible.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 4:1 — "Then Jesus, being filled with the Holy Spirit, returned from the Jordan and was led by the Spirit into the wilderness..." Here the word emphasizes the Spirit guiding and empowering the Son of Man for His earthly ministry.

  • Acts 2:38 — "Then Peter said to them, 'Repent, and let every one of you be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins; and you shall receive the gift of the Holy Spirit.'" Here the word describes the divine gift promised to all who turn to Christ in faith under the New Covenant.

  • Romans 8:11 — "But if the Spirit of Him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, He who raised Christ from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through His Spirit who dwells in you." Here the word highlights the Spirit as the agent of resurrection life and divine power dwelling within believers.

  • Ephesians 4:3 — "...endeavoring to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace." Here the word points to the Holy Spirit as the source of church unity, which Christians are commanded to preserve actively.

  • 1 John 3:24 — "Now he who keeps His commandments abides in Him, and He in him. And by this we know that He abides in us, by the Spirit whom He has given us." Here the word indicates the Holy Spirit as the internal witness assuring believers of their union with God.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The mention of the Spirit in Philippians 2:1 serves as the structural foundation for the famous passage that follows in Philippians 2:5–11, which describes the humiliation and exaltation of Jesus Christ. Before calling believers to adopt the mindset of Christ—who emptied Himself and took the form of a servant—Paul grounds the request in spiritual realities already granted to them.

The fellowship produced by the Holy Spirit provides both the motivation and the spiritual capacity for Christians to lay aside selfishness, value others above themselves, and walk together in genuine humility.
