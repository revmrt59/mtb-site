CONFESS - (exomologéō, Strong's G1843)

Summary:

The word exomologeō means to declare openly, acknowledge fully, give thanks, or confess. In Philippians 2:11, Paul uses this word to describe the universal public acknowledgment that Jesus Christ is Lord, bringing ultimate glory to God the Father.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Greek word exomologeō combines a basic root meaning "to say the same thing" or "to agree" with a prefix that stresses public expression or complete declaration. Rather than keeping a truth private or hidden, the word implies speaking out openly before others.

In the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, this term frequently rendered Hebrew words for praising God and giving Him thanks. It was used when individuals or congregations publicly declared God's greatness, works, or righteousness. It was also used when people openly acknowledged their sins before God. In both settings—praise and admission of sin—the core idea is bringing what is true into open, verbal declaration.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word exomologeō is used in a few closely related ways depending on the subject and context:

  • To declare openly or acknowledge publicly: making a clear declaration of truth.

  • To give thanks or offer praise: publicly declaring God's goodness, wisdom, and character.

  • To admit or confess wrong: openly acknowledging sin or fault before God or others.

  • To agree or promise: declaring consent or agreement publicly.

In Philippians 2:11, the word means to acknowledge or declare publicly. The surrounding context specifies the content of this declaration: stating that "Jesus Christ is Lord."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, exomologeō appears in two main settings: acknowledging sin or truth before others, and giving praise to God.

When used for acknowledging sin, as seen in the Gospels and Acts, the emphasis falls on bringing personal wrongdoing into the open through honest admission. People came to John the Baptist or gathered in the early church openly stating their deeds without concealment.

When used for praise, as seen in Jesus' prayers, the word carries the sense of joyful, public thanksgiving. Jesus thanks the Father for revealing divine truth to the humble.

In prophetic and eschatological contexts, such as Romans and Philippians, the word describes a solemn, universal acknowledgment of God's authority. Here, it is not merely a private feeling, but an audible, public declaration of reality.

4. Theological Implications

Philippians 2:9–11 presents the supreme exaltation of Jesus Christ following His voluntary humiliation and death on the cross. God raised Him high and bestowed on Him the name above every name.

The universal confession of Jesus as Lord marks the grand fulfillment of Christ's exaltation. Paul draws directly on the language of Isaiah 45:23, where God declares that every knee will bow and every tongue will take an oath of allegiance to Him alone. By applying this Old Testament declaration to Jesus, Paul highlights the divine identity and sovereign authority of Christ.

This universal confession shows that every created being—whether in heaven, on earth, or under the earth—will ultimately acknowledge the reality of Christ's reign. This declaration of Christ's lordship does not compete with the glory of God the Father; rather, acknowledging the Son brings supreme glory to the Father who exalted Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:11, the verb is formed as an aorist middle subjunctive in the third person singular, functioning within the purpose clause introduced by Paul ("that every tongue should confess"). The aorist form presents this ultimate cosmic declaration as a single, complete event in the unfolding purpose of God.

The immediate passage sets up a cosmic scope: "those in heaven, and those on earth, and those under the earth" will bow, and "every tongue" will confess. The word exomologeō highlights the vocal, outward nature of this tribute. Every creature will audibly acknowledge the true identity of Jesus: "Jesus Christ is Lord."

Paul places this vision within a pastoral exhortation to the church at Philippi. By showing that Jesus will receive the ultimate confession of all creation, Paul encourages believers to submit willingly to His lordship now, walking together in humility, selflessness, and unity.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 11:25 — "At that time Jesus answered and said, 'I thank You, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that You have hidden these things from the wise and prudent and have revealed them to babes.'" Here the word is rendered "thank," expressing public praise and thanksgiving from Jesus to the Father.

  • Acts 19:18 — "And many who had believed came confessing and declaring their deeds." In this context, the word describes believers in Ephesus publicly acknowledging their former ungodly practices.

  • Romans 14:11 — "For it is written: 'As I live, says the LORD, Every knee shall bow to Me, And every tongue shall confess to God.'" Paul quotes Isaiah to show that every person will ultimately acknowledge God's sovereign judgment.

  • Romans 15:9 — "and that the Gentiles might glorify God for His mercy, as it is written: 'For this reason I will confess to You among the Gentiles, And sing to Your name.'" Here the term speaks of declaring God's praise among the nations.

  • Revelation 3:5 — "He who overcomes shall be clothed in white garments, and I will not blot out his name from the Book of Life; but I will confess his name before My Father and before His angels." Jesus promises to publicly acknowledge those who remain faithful to Him.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The compound structure of exomologeō stresses an outward declaration. True biblical confession is not kept hidden in the heart; it is spoken out openly.

In Philippians 2:11, the content of the confession is the short declaration: "Jesus Christ is Lord" (Kyrios Iēsous Christos). In the Roman world, where individuals were expected to proclaim Caesar as lord, stating that Jesus is Lord was a profound declaration that supreme authority over creation belongs to Jesus Christ alone.
