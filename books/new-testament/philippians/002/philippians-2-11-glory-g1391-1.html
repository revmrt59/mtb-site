GLORY - (dóxa, Strong's G1391)

Summary:

The word doxa fundamentally refers to praise, honor, splendor, brightness, or majestic grandeur. In ancient Greek outside the Bible, the word originally referred to an opinion or reputation. In biblical usage, it expanded to describe the visible brilliance of God's presence and the supreme honor due to His name. In Philippians 2:11, the word identifies the ultimate target and purpose of Christ's exaltation: every tongue confessing that Jesus Christ is Lord brings honor and praise to God the Father.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek usage outside the Bible, doxa was closely tied to thought and expectation. It commonly referred to an opinion, an estimation, or public reputation—what people thought about a person.

When Jewish scholars translated the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, they chose doxa to translate the important Old Testament word kabod. The Hebrew term carried the idea of weight, heavy importance, visible splendor, and divine grandeur. Through this biblical connection, doxa shifted away from mere human opinion and took on a far richer meaning: the heavy, magnificent brilliance of God's revealed presence, holiness, and supreme worth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to several related concepts depending on how it is used:

  • Praise, honor, and recognition given to someone worthy of high respect.

  • Radiant brightness, light, or visible heavenly splendor.

  • Majestic majesty, power, or supreme dignity belonging to God or divine beings.

When used in relation to God, doxa describes both His inherent grandeur and the active worship and honor rendered to Him by creation. In Philippians 2:11, it means the honor, praise, and recognition directed to God the Father as the final goal of Christ's lordship.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, doxa appears in three main settings:

  • The manifest presence of God: It describes the brilliant glory that accompanied God's divine appearance, such as the light surrounding angels or the radiant brilliance of the exalted Christ.

  • Honor rendered to God: It frequently expresses the praise, adoration, and acclaim offered to God in response to His works of creation and salvation.

  • The future state of believers: It describes the glorious, transformed reality that believers will share in the resurrection, reflected in physical and spiritual perfection.

The word regularly connects divine action with divine worship. Whatever God accomplishes in history ultimately redounds to His praise.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of doxa in Christ's exaltation passage highlights the perfect unity within the Godhead. The crowning of Jesus as Lord over all creation does not compete with or diminish the honor of God the Father. Instead, honoring the Son is the precise way the Father receives glory.

Furthermore, the passage links ultimate glory to self-giving humility. Christ did not seize power through self-exaltation; He emptied Himself, took the form of a servant, and was obedient to the point of death on a cross. God's exaltation of Christ reveals that true heavenly glory stands in sharp contrast to earthly pride.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Philippians 2:5–11 forms a magnificent description of Christ's journey from eternal equality with God, down into humble human suffering, and up into supreme exaltation. After describing how God gave Jesus the name above every name so that every knee should bow, Paul concludes with the purpose of this universal homage: "and that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father."

The word doxa functions here as the final target of the entire passage. Grammatically, the word is a feminine noun in the accusative singular case, serving as the object of a preposition indicating purpose. This construction shows that the universal confession of Jesus' lordship is not an end in itself; its ultimate direction and result is the praise and honor of God the Father. The exaltation of the Son fulfills, rather than rivals, the glorification of the Father.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In contrast to human renown, which fades quickly, divine doxa is enduring and indestructible. Human glory is compared in Scripture to the fading flower of the field (as noted in 1 Peter 1:24), but God's glory remains eternal.

Understanding doxa as both divine splendor and target praise explains why all Christian worship centers on Christ. When believers honor Christ, they fulfill the very design of God's redemptive plan, bringing the ultimate praise to the Father who sent Him.
