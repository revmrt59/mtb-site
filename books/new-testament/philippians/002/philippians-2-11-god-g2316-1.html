GOD - (theós, Strong's G2316)

Summary:

The Greek word theos refers to God, the supreme Deity and Creator of all things. In the New Testament, it most frequently identifies the one true God revealed to Israel, distinct from false idols. In Philippians 2:11, theos identifies God the Father, showing that the ultimate purpose of Christ’s exaltation and universal lordship is to bring everlasting glory to God the Father.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greco-Roman culture, the term theos was widely used to refer to any divine being, pagan god, or personified deity. However, when Greek-speaking Jewish scholars translated the Hebrew Scriptures, they used theos to translate the divine names and titles of the one true God, such as Elohim.

By the time of the New Testament, Christian writers used theos within a strict biblical worldview. While surrounding polytheistic culture acknowledged many gods, Christians used theos to designate the self-existent God who created the heavens and the earth, redeemed His people, and revealed Himself supremely through His Son, Jesus Christ.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Theos refers to God as the supreme divine Being. It denotes the absolute ruler of the universe who possesses authority, holiness, and power over creation.

Depending on the passage, the word is used in several distinct ways in Scripture:

  • It refers most commonly to God the Father, the personal source and initiator of creation and redemption.

  • It refers directly to Jesus Christ in passages where His full deity is affirmed alongside the Father.

  • It refers to the divine nature or deity in contrast to human or created beings.

  • On rare occasions, it describes false gods or pagan idols worshipped by non-believers.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the New Testament, theos grounds every aspect of theology, worship, and salvation. God is not presented as an abstract concept, but as the living, active God who speaks, saves, judges, and makes covenants.

When New Testament writers place theos alongside titles like "Lord" (kyrios) or "Father" (patēr), they often distinguish the persons within the Godhead while maintaining the unity of the one true God. In Trinitarian contexts, theos frequently designates God the Father, while kyrios often designates Jesus Christ. Far from creating a conflict of allegiance, New Testament writers emphasize that honoring Jesus as Lord directly serves to honor God the Father.

4. Theological Implications

The use of theos in relation to Jesus Christ carries deep theological importance. In Philippians 2:5–11, Paul describes Jesus taking on human nature and humbling Himself to the point of death on a cross. Because of Christ’s complete obedience, God highly exalted Him and gave Him the name above every name.

The worship given to Jesus does not diminish or divide the glory of God. Instead, acknowledging Jesus as Lord fulfills the sovereign will of God the Father. True worship directed to the Son redounds directly to the glory of the Father, establishing that the exaltation of Christ is the central vehicle through which God displays His majesty to all creation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:11, Paul concludes a magnificent description of Christ’s humiliation and exaltation. The passage declares that "every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father."

Grammatically, the word "God" in this verse appears as a genitive singular masculine noun modifying "Father." This grammatical relationship links God directly with the title of Father (theou patros).

The context highlights a vital truth: confessing Jesus as Lord does not compete with the worship owed to God. When every knee bows and every tongue confesses Jesus as supreme Lord, this universal acclamation does not rob God of honor. On the contrary, acknowledging the Son as Lord is the very mechanism by which God the Father receives ultimate, cosmic glory.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:23 — "They shall call His name Immanuel, which is translated, 'God with us.'"

  • John 6:27 — "Do not labor for the food which perishes, but for the food which endures to everlasting life, which the Son of Man will give you, because God the Father has set His seal on Him."

  • Romans 10:9 — "If you confess with your mouth the Lord Jesus and believe in your heart that God has raised Him from the dead, you will be saved."

  • Ephesians 2:16 — "And that He might reconcile them both to God in one body through the cross, thereby putting to death the enmity."

  • 1 Peter 1:2 — "Elect according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, in sanctification of the Spirit, for obedience and sprinkling of the blood of Jesus Christ."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s phrasing in Philippians 2:10–11 directly reflects the language of Isaiah 45:23, where God declares, "To Me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall take an oath." In the Old Testament, this exclusive worship belongs solely to the God of Israel.

By applying this exact prophetic language to Jesus Christ, Paul makes an unmistakable claim regarding Christ's divine dignity. Yet, Paul immediately adds that this worship occurs "to the glory of God the Father." This demonstrates a harmonious divine order: honoring the Son fulfills the ultimate purpose of the Father, bringing the whole created universe into joyful submission before God.
