JESUS - (Iēsoûs, Strong's G2424)

Summary:

Iēsous is the Greek form of the Hebrew name Joshua, meaning "Yahweh is salvation" or "the Lord saves." In the New Testament, it is the personal name given to the Son of God at His incarnation, identifying Him as a real historical man born in Nazareth. In Philippians 2:11, Paul brings this personal earthly name into the grand climax of Christ's exaltation, declaring that every tongue will confess that "Jesus Christ is Lord." By using this specific name, the passage highlights that the very same Jesus who humbled Himself to die on the cross is the One whom God has exalted as Sovereign over all creation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Iēsous is the Greek transliteration of the Hebrew name Yehoshua (or its shortened form Yeshua), which translates to "Joshua" in English. In ancient Israel, the name was relatively common and carried rich Old Testament history. It literally means "Yahweh is salvation" or "the Lord saves." Just as Joshua led Israel out of the wilderness into the Promised Land, the name pointed forward to a deliverer who would bring God's people into true and lasting salvation.

When the angel instructed Joseph and Mary to name the child born in Bethlehem, the name was not chosen by human preference. It was assigned by divine command because it stated His exact mission: "you shall call His name JESUS, for He will save His people from their sins" (Matthew 1:21). In the surrounding Greco-Roman world, Greek speakers used Iēsous to render this Hebrew name, making it the primary title by which the historical Jesus was known during His earthly life and ministry.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name refers directly to the personal, human identity of Jesus of Nazareth. It designates the individual who took on human flesh, lived among humanity, suffered under Pontius Pilate, died on a Roman cross, and rose physically from the dead.

While titles such as "Christ" (meaning "Anointed One" or "Messiah") and "Lord" identify His divine office and supreme authority, Iēsous is His proper personal name. It identifies the incarnate Son in His humanity. The name serves as a constant reminder that salvation is not an abstract philosophical idea, but a concrete rescue accomplished by a real human being who is also fully God.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, Iēsous appears in contexts emphasizing both His humble earthly ministry and His exalted post-resurrection glory.

During the Gospel narratives, the simple name "Jesus" is used routinely to describe His everyday interactions, His teaching, His miracles, and His suffering. He walked dusty roads, grew weary, grieved, and surrendered to execution as a real man.

In the Acts of the Apostles and the Epistles, the name is frequently combined with His titles, producing formulations like "Jesus Christ" or "the Lord Jesus Christ." This combination anchors Christian faith in history. Apostles preached that the exalted King ruling in heaven is not a distant deity, but the exact same historical Jesus who was crucified in Jerusalem.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of the name Iēsous centers on the reality of the Incarnation and the scope of divine salvation.

First, the name grounds God's redemptive work in genuine human history. God did not send a phantom or a spiritual concept to redeem humanity; He sent a real human Son who bore a common personal name.

Second, the name fulfills the promise embedded within its meaning: "the Lord saves." Redemption is entirely the work of God. Human effort cannot remove sin or reconcile humanity to God; salvation comes exclusively through the Person and work of Jesus.

Finally, placing this human personal name alongside the title "Lord" (Kyrios) asserts the full deity of Christ. In the Greek Old Testament, Kyrios was used to translate the sacred personal name of God (Yahweh). To confess that "Jesus is Lord" is to affirm that the historical man from Nazareth possesses the full identity, glory, and authority of God Almighty.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:5–11, often called the "Christ Hymn," Paul sets forth the supreme model of humility and self-giving love. He traces Christ's voluntary journey from His pre-existent equality with God down to the absolute depth of humiliation—taking the form of a bondservant, being made in human likeness, and submitting to an agonizing death on a cross.

Because of this obedience, God highly exalted Him and bestowed on Him "the name which is above every name" (Philippians 2:9). The culmination of this divine exaltation appears in verses 10–11: at the name of Jesus every knee must bow, and every tongue must confess that "Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father."

The grammatical form of the name here serves as a nominative singular subject identifying the person being confessed. Paul specifically includes the personal earthly name "Jesus" in this supreme declaration. The contrast is intentional and dramatic. The One who receives universal worship from every creature in heaven, on earth, and under the earth is the very One who was mocked, beaten, and crucified under the personal name Jesus. Exaltation does not erase His earthly humbleness; rather, it crowns the obedient servant who bore that earthly name with divine sovereign honor.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the early church, declaring "Jesus Christ is Lord" carried immense weight. In the Roman Empire, citizens were regularly expected to pledge loyalty to the emperor by asserting that Caesar was lord. For early Christians, confessing that "Jesus is Lord" meant acknowledging a higher allegiance. It asserted that ultimate sovereignty belonged not to imperial Rome, but to a Jewish man who had been crucified under Roman authority and raised by God.

Furthermore, Paul emphasizes that this confession ultimately redounds "to the glory of God the Father." Exalting Jesus as Lord does not compete with the glory of the Father; rather, honoring the Son fulfills the ultimate purpose and desire of the Father.
