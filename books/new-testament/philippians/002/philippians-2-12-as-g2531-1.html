AS - (kathṓs, Strong's G2531)

Summary:

The Greek conjunction kathōs means "as," "just as," or "in accordance with." It introduces a comparison, standard, or pattern, showing how one statement corresponds to another. In Philippians 2:12, Paul uses this word to connect the Philippian believers' consistent past pattern of obedience with their present responsibility to continue growing in faith, even while Paul is absent from them.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word kathōs is an everyday conjunction in New Testament Greek formed by combining two smaller words: kata (meaning "according to") and hōs (meaning "as" or "like"). Together, they express agreement, proportion, or correspondence between two actions or ideas.

In common speech and letter-writing, the word served to point back to an established baseline or model. Whether pointing to an event in Scripture, a command previously given, or a habits-of-life standard already demonstrated, kathōs signals to the reader that what is being said now fits naturally with what came before.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The conjunction kathōs functions as a comparative word. It translates into English as "as," "just as," "even as," or "in the manner that."

Depending on the context, the word carries a few natural shades of meaning:

  • Direct comparison: Showing that one action is modeled on another ("do this just as that was done").

  • Historical correspondence: Showing that a present event matches what was written or done earlier.

  • Cause or grounding: Indicating that an action is appropriate because of an existing reality ("in view of the fact that").

In every case, the word itself is simply a grammatical bridge linking two concepts together in agreement or proportion.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, writers use kathōs to align human action with divine precedent or established historical truth. It frequently links the life of the believer directly to the character or actions of God and Jesus Christ.

For example, believers are told to forgive as God in Christ forgave them, or to walk as Jesus walked. In these settings, kathōs does not merely suggest a vague similarity; it sets up a clear moral standard. The prior work of God becomes the pattern and motivation for Christian conduct.

In other settings, kathōs introduces historical fulfillment, drawing a straight line between Old Testament promises and New Testament events.

4. Theological Implications

While kathōs is a small structural word, its biblical usage highlights an important principle of Christian discipleship: gospel living is always responsive. True biblical duty is never invented in a vacuum; it is patterned after God's prior initiative and established truth.

When Scripture commands believers to love, serve, forgive, or obey, it routinely attaches kathōs to remind them of the standard already set by Christ. The believer's daily walk is anchored in what God has already revealed and accomplished. Growth in grace is therefore an ongoing alignment with an established heavenly pattern.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:12, Paul writes, "Therefore, my beloved, as you have always obeyed, not as in my presence only, but now much more in my absence, work out your own salvation with fear and trembling."

Here, kathōs ("as") anchors Paul's pastoral instruction in thePhilippians' proven history. Paul is urging them to continue a lifestyle they have already practiced. He commends their past obedience while establishing that their commitment must not depend on his physical presence.

This connection is vital because it immediately follows the profound description of Jesus' humble obedience in Philippians 2:5–11. Just as Christ obeyed the Father, and just as the Philippians obeyed while Paul was with them, they must now press forward in steady, personal devotion to God. The word "as" highlights continuity: true obedience is grounded in a lasting commitment to God, regardless of who is watching.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 11:1 — "...Lord, teach us to pray, as John also taught his disciples."

  • John 15:9 — "As the Father loved Me, I also have loved you; abide in My love."

  • Ephesians 4:4 — "There is one body and one Spirit, just as you were called in one hope of your calling..."

  • 1 John 3:3 — "And everyone who has this hope in Him purifies himself, just as He is pure."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Small conjunctions like kathōs keep reading centered on the author's logical flow. Without noticing these connecting words, readers risk treating biblical commands as isolated rules rather than part of a unified life of faith.

In Philippians 2:12, kathōs guards against two spiritual misunderstandings:

  • It prevents complacency: past obedience is acknowledged, but it serves as the baseline for ongoing maturity rather than an excuse to stop growing.

  • It prevents hypocrisy: obedience that only occurs when an authority figure is nearby fails to match the steady standard that kathōs establishes.
