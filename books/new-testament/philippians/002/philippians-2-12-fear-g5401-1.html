FEAR - (phóbos, Strong's G5401)

Summary:

The Greek word phobos basic meaning is terror, dread, or alarm, but it also describes a deep reverence, holy awe, and profound respect toward God or authority. In Philippians 2:12, Paul uses phobos in the phrase "fear and trembling" to describe the humble, reverent attitude believers should have as they actively live out the salvation God has given them.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek literature, phobos originally described panic, flight, or overwhelming terror caused by danger. Over time, its meaning broadened to include not only fright or alarm, but also the deep respect, awe, and reverence given to a sovereign ruler, a judge, or God Himself.

When the New Testament authors wrote in Greek, they used phobos across this full range of meaning. In some contexts, it describes ordinary human fright or alarm. In spiritual contexts, it describes either a enslaving dread of judgment or a godly, reverent awe that leads to careful obedience. In Philippians 2:12, Paul writes from prison to encourage a faithful congregation. He urges them to maintain their spiritual diligence regardless of whether he is physically present with them, approaching their daily walk with holy reverence before God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word phobos can refer to:

  • Severe fright, alarm, or terror in the face of immediate danger or supernatural power.

  • Anxiety, distress, or dread regarding future hardship or punishment.

  • Deep reverence, pious awe, and high respect directed toward God or rightful human authorities.

In Philippians 2:12, phobos carries the sense of holy reverence and humble awe. Paired with "trembling," it does not mean panic or paralyzed dread. Instead, it describes a sober awareness of standing before a holy God who is actively working within His people.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, phobos appears in several distinct settings:

First, it describes physical or emotional terror. When people witness miracles, angel visits, or life-threatening storms, phobos captures their sudden shock and fright.

Second, it describes a bondage to dread. Before coming to faith, people often live under the oppressive dread of death, divine condemnation, or human enemies.

Third, it describes Christian reverence and holy honor. Believers walk in the "fear of the Lord," showing deep respect for God's holy character and sovereignty. Scripture also uses the term for the respect and honor due to human leaders and authorities out of devotion to Christ.

4. Theological Implications

In Christian theology, phobos helps define the relationship between divine grace and human responsibility. Salvation is entirely a gift of God's grace through faith, not something earned by human works. Yet receiving salvation changes how a believer lives.

Paul instructs believers to "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling." The word phobos reminds us that living for God is a sacred matter. It guards against spiritual carelessness, spiritual pride, and presumption. True assurance of salvation produces holy awe, not casual indifference.

Furthermore, Paul immediately grounds this holy reverence in God's sovereign work: "for it is God who works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure" (Philippians 2:13). Knowing that the almighty Creator is personally at work within the believer creates a deep sense of reverence (phobos) and humble dependence.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:12, Paul addresses a church he deeply loves. He commends them because they have always obeyed, not only in his presence but even more in his absence. He commands them to "work out" (katergazomai) their salvation with "fear and trembling."

The expression "work out" does not mean "work for" or earn salvation. Salvation is already possessed as a gift; Paul tells them to bring that salvation to its practical fulfillment in daily life through unity, humility, and obedience.

The word phobos here is grammatically in the genitive case, singular number, and masculine gender. It forms part of the prepositional phrase "with fear and trembling," which describes the inner spirit and attitude with which this living out of salvation must occur. The pairing of "fear and trembling" emphasizes humble self-distrust, awareness of human weakness, and complete reverence before God. Rather than relying on Paul's physical presence or their own human strength, the Philippians are to live in constant, reverent awareness of God's holy presence.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 2:43 — "Then fear came upon every soul, and many wonders and signs were done through the apostles."

  • Romans 8:15 — "For you did not receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption by whom we cry, 'Abba, Father.'"

  • 2 Corinthians 7:15 — "And his affection for you is far greater as he remembers the obedience of you all, how with fear and trembling you received him."

  • 1 John 4:18 — "There is no fear in love; but perfect love casts out fear, because fear involves torment."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul repeatedly links "fear" (phobos) with "trembling" (tromos) when describing serious spiritual responsibility and humble respect. In 2 Corinthians 7:15, he commends the Corinthians for receiving Titus with "fear and trembling," showing eager responsiveness and deep respect. In Ephesians 6:5, he commands bondservants to obey earthly masters with "fear and trembling," doing so as unto Christ.

In Philippians 2:12, this dynamic balances assurance with humility. Gospel assurance removes the tormenting dread of condemnation, but it replaces it with a beautiful, holy awe. The believer does not tremble because God is fickle, but because God is holy, majestic, and closely involved in every detail of life.
