OUT - (katergázomai, Strong's G2716)

Summary:

The Greek verb katergazomai means to accomplish, produce, bring about, or carry out to completion. It describes taking something and working it through until it reaches its intended result. In Philippians 2:12, the word is translated within the phrase "work out." Paul commands the Philippians to put their salvation into active, continuous practice, bringing into daily living the full spiritual fruit of the redemption God has granted them.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb katergazomai is a compound word formed from the preposition kata (which adds force or intensity) and the verb ergazomai (meaning to work or labor). Combined, the term carries the sense of working something through to its final output or bringing a task to full accomplishment. It is not merely about starting an activity, but about achieving a result through sustained labor.

Paul wrote his letter to the Philippians while imprisoned, likely in Rome. Despite his confinement, his primary concern was the spiritual stability, humility, and unity of the church at Philippi. In chapter 2, Paul first sets forth the supreme example of Jesus Christ, who humbled Himself to the point of death on a cross and was subsequently exalted by God. Immediately following this grand picture of Christ’s self-giving obedience, Paul transitions to practical instruction, urging the believers to express that same obedient spirit in their ongoing church life.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word katergazomai can refer to producing an effect, bringing about a condition, or executing an action completely. Depending on who is acting and what is being done, it can mean to perform, achieve, cause, or work out.

When applied to moral or spiritual growth, the verb highlights the process of translating an internal principle into tangible reality. It does not mean inventing something out of nothing, nor does it mean earning a favor that has not yet been granted. Instead, it means carrying out to completion what is already present in seed form or basic endowment. In Philippians 2:12, the word describes the active duty of believers to demonstrate, exercise, and complete the outer expression of their faith.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, katergazomai appears in contexts where one cause produces a distinct effect. For example, trials and afflictions are said to produce spiritual character, such as perseverance and eternal glory. In other passages, the word describes the practical execution of actions, whether good or evil, highlighting the real-world output of human choices.

The nuance of the word lies in its emphasis on results. It focuses on the thoroughness of the work. Where simple working might describe effort in progress, katergazomai emphasizes working through to the realized end. It points to faith active in life, showing that true spiritual life is not passive observation but active yield.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of katergazomai in Philippians 2:12 touches on the relationship between divine grace and human responsibility. Paul tells believers to "work out" their salvation, not to "work for" it. Salvation is given entirely by God's grace through faith, as taught throughout Paul's writings. Human effort can never purchase redemption or satisfy the righteous demands of God's law.

However, once salvation is received, it must be lived out. The theological emphasis here is sanctification—the ongoing growth in holiness that follows justification. The believer's responsibility to work out salvation is immediately grounded in verse 13: "for it is God who works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure." God provides the inner desire and the power to obey; believers respond by actively translating that divine energy into righteous living, unity, and love. Divine initiative and human action work together without making salvation a reward earned by human merit.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:12, Paul commands the believers: "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling." The grammatical form of the verb features a present tense, middle voice, imperative mood, and plural number.

The present tense indicates an ongoing, daily pursuit rather than a single completed act. The imperative mood renders this as an direct apostolic command, requiring active compliance. The plural number demonstrates that Paul is addressing the entire Christian community together, stressing that the local church as a body must collectively manifest the life of Christ.

The phrase "with fear and trembling" establishes the solemn atmosphere of this work. It does not mean terror, but rather profound reverence and awe before God, recognized as the holy Creator working within them. Because God Himself is active within them (v. 13), believers must approach their daily obedience with sacred care, realizing the high dignity and seriousness of reflecting His character.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 5:3 — "And not only that, but we also glory in tribulations, knowing that tribulation produces perseverance"

  • 2 Corinthians 4:17 — "For our light affliction, which is but for a moment, is working for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory"

  • 2 Corinthians 7:10 — "For godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, not to be regretted; but the sorrow of the world produces death."

  • James 1:3 — "knowing that the testing of your faith produces patience."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A helpful contrast exists in Philippians 2:12–13 between the words used for working. In verse 12, believers are told to work out (katergazomai) their salvation. In verse 13, God is said to work in (energeō, from which we get the word energy) both the desire and the ability to obey.

This contrast shows the divine source and human channel of spiritual growth. God works in the hidden places of the human heart, transforming the will and giving spiritual strength. The believer then works out that hidden transformational work into visible conduct, godly choices, and harmonious fellowship. The outer work of verse 12 is impossible without the inner work of verse 13, yet the inner work of verse 13 naturally issues in the outer fruit of verse 12.
