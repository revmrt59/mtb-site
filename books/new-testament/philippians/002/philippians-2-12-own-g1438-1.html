OWN - (heautoû, Strong's G1438)

Summary:

The word refers back to the subject of a sentence or clause, functioning as a reflexive pronoun meaning "himself," "herself," "itself," or in the plural, "themselves," "yourselves," or "ourselves." When modifying a noun, it emphasizes direct personal ownership or responsibility, often translated as "own." In Philippians 2:12, the word highlights personal responsibility in the Christian life. Paul calls believers to actively live out and bring to completion the salvation God has given them, emphasizing that each Christian must personally take responsibility for their spiritual walk rather than depending passively on leaders or mentors.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word heautou is a common Greek reflexive pronoun. Reflexive pronouns direct the action of a verb or the focus of a statement back to the subject. In the plural forms, it can refer to "ourselves," "yourselves," or "themselves," depending on the speaker and context.

When Paul wrote his letter to the Philippians, he was writing as a prisoner, separated from a church he deeply loved. The Philippian believers had consistently supported Paul and shown faithful obedience while he was present with them. However, with Paul far away in custody, the church needed to maintain its spiritual vitality and continue growing in maturity. In Philippians 2:12, Paul addresses this reality directly, encouraging them to continue obeying God not only when he was present, but even more now in his absence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word heautou indicates that an action or quality belongs directly to the subject being mentioned. Depending on the sentence structure, it can be translated as "himself," "herself," "itself," "yourselves," "ourselves," or "themselves." When placed in a possessive position before a noun, English translations frequently render it as "own" to stress distinct personal association.

In Philippians 2:12, the word appears in a genitive, plural, masculine form modifying the word "salvation." In this grammatical position, it functions possessively, rendered as "your own salvation." It identifies the believers themselves as the ones who must actively carry out and apply the salvation they have received.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, heautou serves several clear communicative purposes depending on its setting:

  • Personal action directed toward oneself: It marks an action that a person performs on or for themselves, such as Jesus humbling "Himself" (Philippians 2:7).

  • Personal ownership and responsibility: It marks property, duties, or obligations that belong specifically to the subject, such as working quietly to eat one's "own" bread (2 Thessalonians 3:12).

  • Internal reflection or reliance: It describes an inner attitude or focus within a person or group, such as deciding not to trust in "ourselves" (2 Corinthians 1:9).

In Philippians 2:12, the word brings personal obligation into sharp focus. Salvation is not a vague collective idea where individual responsibility disappears. While believers belong to one body, each disciple is called to personally engage in spiritual growth and obedience.

4. Theological Implications

Paul’s instruction to "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling" carries profound theological significance. The phrase "work out" does not mean "work for" or "earn." Salvation is entirely a gift of divine grace, granted through faith in Jesus Christ, not produced by human effort.

The word "own" guards against spiritual passivity and false reliance. Believers cannot live on borrowed faith or depend entirely on the spiritual vigor of spiritual leaders, parents, or pastors. While leaders provide guidance, each Christian has a direct relationship with God and a personal responsibility to live out the implications of their faith.

Importantly, Paul immediately follows verse 12 with verse 13: "for it is God who works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure." The personal call to work out "your own salvation" does not contradict divine sovereignty; rather, human responsibility rests on divine initiative. Believers work out what God has already worked in.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The context of Philippians 2:12 begins with the transition word "therefore." This connects Paul's exhortation directly to the magnificent description of Jesus Christ in Philippians 2:5–11. There, Paul describes how Christ humbled Himself, took the form of a servant, became obedient unto death, and was exalted by God to the highest place.

Because Jesus demonstrated ultimate obedience, believers are called to imitate His obedience. Paul notes that the Philippians had always obeyed when he was present. Now, in his absence, they must exercise spiritual initiative. The word "own" underscores this shift: Paul's absence must not lead to spiritual decline. Instead, it should compel each believer to take full ownership of their personal walk with God, cultivating reverence and healthy holy fear before the Lord.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Greek grammar, heautou provides a stronger level of personal emphasis than standard possessive pronouns. By selecting this reflexive form, Paul intentionally places responsibility square on the shoulders of the readers.

This emphasis prevents a church from becoming dependent on a single personality or leader. When a strong leader departs, a healthy church continues to thrive because individual members have learned to take responsibility for their own spiritual lives before God.
