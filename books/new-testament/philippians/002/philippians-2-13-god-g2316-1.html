GOD - (theós, Strong's G2316)

Summary:

The word refers broadly to a deity, but in the New Testament it almost always refers to the one true God, the Creator and Ruler of all things. In Philippians 2:13, the word identifies God as the personal, active source behind the believer's spiritual growth. By naming God as the active subject, Paul assures believers that the internal desire and power to obey come directly from God Himself.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greek world, theos was used to describe any of the many gods of pagan mythology. When the Jewish translators of the Old Testament translated Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, they adopted theos to translate the divine titles Elohim and YHWH. Through this usage, the term became firmly tied to the monotheistic revelation of Scripture.

In the New Testament, theos designates the living, personal, supreme God who created the universe, entered into covenant with Israel, and fully revealed Himself through Jesus Christ. In Philippians 2:13, Paul writes to a young church learning how to live faithfully amid surrounding pagan culture. He encourages them to pursue holiness, grounding their human effort in the primary, continuous action of God working inside them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to any object of worship, but in the New Testament it almost universally refers to God the Father, the supreme ruler of heaven and earth.

When Scripture uses theos for the true God, it describes a personal Being who acts with purpose, authority, and love. The word highlights:

  • God as the supreme Creator and Sovereign over all creation.

  • God as the ultimate source of life, salvation, and spiritual power.

  • God as the holy Father who guides and transforms His children.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, theos appears in various grammatical roles to describe God's identity, attributes, and actions. It frequently appears with the definite article ("the God"), pointing specifically to the one true God of Israel, distinguished from false idols.

Scripture uses the word to emphasize God as the initiator of redemption. God plans salvation, sends His Son, justifies sinners, and gives the Holy Spirit to believers. When used as the subject of a sentence, the word calls direct attention to God's initiative in human history and individual lives.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical revelation of God as theos sets Christian belief apart from both pagan polytheism and abstract philosophy. God is not an impersonal cosmic force or an distant deity who leaves creation to run on its own. He is the active Lord who personally engages with His creation.

In salvation and sanctification, God is the primary mover. Human beings respond to God, but God initiates and sustains the relationship. The use of theos reminds believers that true spiritual life depends on God's initiative rather than human wisdom, strength, or religious merit.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:12–13, Paul presents two sides of Christian living: responsibility and divine empowerment. Verse 12 commands believers to "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling." Verse 13 provides the grounding reason: "for it is God who works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure."

The presence of the word "God" at the beginning of verse 13 gives key weight to the sentence. Grammatically, the word is in the nominative singular masculine form, identifying God as the explicit subject carrying out the work.

Paul does not say that believers generate their own desire and strength for holy living. Instead, God acts directly within the believer to produce both the internal desire ("to will") and the effective action ("to do"). Human exertion in verse 12 is real and necessary, but verse 13 shows that human effort is the fruit of God's prior and ongoing work inside the Christian.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 10:9 — "...that if you confess with your mouth the Lord Jesus and believe in your heart that God has raised Him from the dead, you will be saved."

  • 2 Corinthians 9:7 — "So let each one give as he purposes in his heart, not grudgingly or of necessity; for God loves a cheerful giver."

  • Ephesians 2:16 — "...and that He might reconcile them both to God in one body through the cross, thereby putting to death the enmity."

  • 1 John 4:7 — "Beloved, let us love one another, for love is of God; and everyone who loves is born of God and knows God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The balance Paul establishes in Philippians 2:12–13 protects believers from two dangerous extremes: legalism and passivity.

If believers forget verse 13, they may slide into legalism or self-reliance, believing their growth depends entirely on human willpower. If believers forget verse 12, they may slide into spiritual apathy, assuming they have no active responsibility. Naming God as the source of internal motivation and action gives believers deep humility and strong confidence. They work actively because God is actively working in them.
