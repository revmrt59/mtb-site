PLEASURE - (eudokía, Strong's G2107)

Summary:

The word eudokia describes a state of delight, standard of satisfaction, good pleasure, or favorable purpose. When used of God, it speaks of His sovereign, gracious, and deliberate goodwill—what gives Him delight to decree and perform. In Philippians 2:13, the word designates the supreme standard and goal of the believer's spiritual growth: God energizes both the willing and the working in believers to accomplish what brings Him genuine delight and aligns with His good purpose.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek usage, eudokia expresses genuine delight, favorable disposition, approval, or desired purpose. It stems from ideas of thinking well of something or finding satisfaction in a particular outcome. While ancient writers sometimes used it for human approval or personal satisfaction, the New Testament frequently presents the word in relation to God's gracious sovereign will.

In the New Testament, eudokia often highlights God's uncoerced, benevolent favor. It is not a passive wish or a mere emotional reaction, but a purposeful, active disposition. God acts not out of external compulsion or obligation, but out of His own inner delight and gracious purpose.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word eudokia can refer to favor, goodwill, delight, pleasing purpose, or personal desire.

When God is the subject, the word means His gracious will, His good pleasure, or that which brings Him satisfaction and delight. It identifies what accords with His holy character and sovereign counsel.

When a person is the subject, the word describes a sincere desire, earnest goodwill, or generous intent toward others.

In Philippians 2:13, the noun is in the genitive, singular, feminine form, functioning in a descriptive sense that identifies the objective and standard of God's work within the believer: God actively works in His people so that their desires and actions fulfill His own good pleasure.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses eudokia to highlight the gracious origin and joyful outcome of God's plans.

In gospel passages, God expresses His delight in His Son and His good pleasure to grant the kingdom to His flock. When Jesus praises the Father for hiding truth from the proud and revealing it to babes, He affirms that this was well-pleasing in God's sight. In Paul's letters, the word often appears when describing the plan of redemption. God predestined believers for adoption according to the good pleasure of His will and made known the mystery of His will according to His good pleasure.

The word also describes human goodwill. Paul writes of his heart's desire for Israel's salvation, using the word to express a deep, compassionate longing. He also notes in Philippians that some preach Christ out of goodwill rather than rivalry.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical usage of eudokia underscores the gracious foundation of God's redemptive work. God does not save or transform His people reluctantly. Salvation and spiritual growth flow directly from His generous goodwill and sovereign delight.

This truth provides security for believers. Because God's actions toward His children arise from His own good pleasure, His work is stable and sure. His grace is rooted in His own character rather than human merit or performance.

Furthermore, eudokia clarifies the goal of Christian living. Christian obedience is not designed to earn God's affection, but to fulfill what brings Him pleasure. Sanctification is the process by which a believer's life is progressively aligned with God's holy standard, producing fruit that pleases Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:12–13, Paul instructs the Philippian believers to work out their own salvation with fear and trembling. To prevent this command from leading to self-reliance or legalistic anxiety, Paul immediately grounds human responsibility in divine activity: "for it is God who works in you both to will and to do for His good pleasure."

The word translated "pleasure" in verse 13 provides both the divine motive and the ultimate benchmark for Christian living. God Himself provides the internal motivation ("to will") and the functional power ("to do") within the believer. The ultimate aim of this inward energizing is God's own good pleasure.

This structure preserves a vital balance. Believers are genuinely active in working out their salvation in daily practice, yet their action is enabled entirely by God working within them. The goal is not self-fulfillment or human praise, but the satisfaction of God's holy and loving purpose.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 2:14 — "Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, goodwill toward men!" Here the angels announce that God's favor and gracious goodwill are being extended to humanity through the birth of Jesus Christ.

  • Romans 10:1 — "Brethren, my heart’s desire and prayer to God for Israel is that they may be saved." Paul uses the word to express his deep, sincere longing and earnest goodwill for the salvation of his fellow Israelites.

  • Ephesians 1:5 — "having predestined us to adoption as sons by Jesus Christ to Himself, according to the good pleasure of His will." This passage emphasizes that God chose to adopt believers purely out of His own gracious choice and sovereign delight.

  • Ephesians 1:9 — "having made known to us the mystery of His will, according to His good pleasure which He purposed in Himself." God's revelation of His redemption plan stems from His own free and joyful decision.

  • 2 Thessalonians 1:11 — "Therefore we also pray always for you that our God would count you worthy of this calling, and fulfill all the good pleasure of His goodness and the work of faith with power." Paul prays that God will powerfully complete every good resolve and holy desire produced by His grace in the believers' lives.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding eudokia protects against two common spiritual errors: legalism and passive fatalism.

Against legalism, eudokia shows that God's work in believers originates in His benevolent grace, not earned merit. Believers do not work to compel God to favor them; rather, God's favor empowers their obedience.

Against passive fatalism, eudokia shows that God works within believers specifically so that they will actively desire ("will") and carry out ("do") what pleases Him. Divine sovereignty does not eliminate human responsibility; it creates and sustains it. Believers can pursue holiness with confidence, knowing that God delights to work through their active obedience.
