ALL - (pâs, Strong's G3956)

Summary:

The word pas refers to totality, completeness, or the whole of a given category, meaning "all," "every," or "everything." When used in the neuter plural form as it is here, it functions substantively to mean "all things" or "everything." In Philippians 2:14, the word extends the command to every action, task, and duty within the Christian community, leaving no room for exceptions where complaining or disputing might be excused.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek, pas is the standard word used to express totality, inclusion, and lack of exception. It can describe every individual item in a group or the full total of a collective whole. When placed in the neuter plural form, the word functions like a noun meaning "all things" or "everything."

In the New Testament, writers use pas in a wide variety of settings. It can describe every member of a group, every instance of an event, or the complete sum of actions in daily life. Its exact scope is always defined by the surrounding sentence and the subject being addressed.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of pas is "all," "every," or "the whole." Depending on its grammatical form and context, the word can refer to:

  • The entire total of a group without excluding any part ("all").

  • Every individual instance of something ("every").

  • Everything as a comprehensive whole ("all things").

In Philippians 2:14, the word appears in the accusative case, plural number, and neuter gender. This grammatical form functions as "all things" or "everything," serving as the direct object of the command to "do."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

biblical authors use pas to set absolute boundaries or to emphasize comprehensive scope. Depending on how it is used, the word carries a few distinct nuances in Scripture:

When used with a singular noun, it often highlights every individual instance, such as "every tree" or "every person." When used with a plural noun, it refers to the entirety of a group. When used as a neuter plural on its own, as in Philippians 2:14, it encompasses all matters, duties, and situations within the topic under discussion.

The word itself does not define what those "things" are. Instead, the context specifies the boundaries. In ethical commands, pas emphasizes that the instruction applies across the board, leaving no room for partial compliance or selective obedience.

4. Theological Implications

The use of totalizing language like pas in biblical commands reflects how God calls His people to wholehearted devotion. Christian obedience is not divided into private spiritual duties and public practical tasks; every area of life falls under the lordship of Christ.

In Philippians 2, Paul points to Christ's self-emptying humility as the pattern for community life. Christ's complete obedience forms the grounding for how believers relate to one another. When Scripture commands believers to act without complaining or arguing in "all things," it highlights that Christian unity and humility must govern every interaction, task, and shared responsibility in the church.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:14, Paul writes: "Do all things without complaining and disputing." The placement of "all things" at the front of the command gives it strong emphasis in the sentence.

The immediate context is Paul's call for unity, humility, and mutual service in the church family (Philippians 2:1–4, 12–13). Believers are called to work out their salvation because God is actively working in them. Immediately following this, Paul issues the directive in verse 14.

By specifying "all things," Paul prevents believers from limiting this standard to pleasant tasks or spiritual gatherings. The command covers ordinary chores, administrative work, leadership decisions, and difficult interpersonal dealings. Grumbling and second-guessing often surface during tedious or challenging duties. The word "all" ensures that no task or situation is exempted from the call to maintain a spirit of peaceful, humble cooperation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 17:21 — "that they all may be one, as You, Father, are in Me, and I in You..."

  • 2 Corinthians 7:15 — "And his affection for you is far greater as he remembers the obedience of you all, how with fear and trembling you received him."

  • Colossians 1:4 — "since we heard of your faith in Christ Jesus and of your love for all the saints..."

  • Hebrews 13:18 — "Pray for us; for we are confident that we have a good conscience, in all things wishing to conduct ourselves honorably."

  • 1 John 3:20 — "For if our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and knows all things."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase "complaining and disputing" in Philippians 2:14 echoes the wilderness wanderings of Israel, where grumbling against leadership and God disrupted the community and expressed unbelief. By using pas here, Paul establishes a contrast between the historical pattern of Israel's murmuring and the new life required of God's people in Christ.

In verse 15, Paul immediately explains the goal of doing "all things" without complaining: so that believers "may become blameless and harmless, children of God without fault in the midst of a crooked and perverse generation, among whom you shine as lights in the world." Comprehensive harmony in every church task makes the gospel visible to a watching world.
