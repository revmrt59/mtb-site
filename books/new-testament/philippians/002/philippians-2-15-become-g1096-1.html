BECOME - (gínomai, Strong's G1096)

Summary:

Ginomai means to come into being, to happen, to take place, or to enter into a new state or condition. In Philippians 2:15, Paul uses this word to describe the growing reality of a believer's character. By turning away from grumbling and arguing, Christians enter into and visibly display a life that is blameless, pure, and without fault in a dark world.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Greek verb ginomai is one of the most common action words in the New Testament. While some verbs describe remaining in a fixed, unchanged state, ginomai emphasizes transition, movement, and arrival into a new condition. It describes things coming to pass, events happening, or people taking on a new quality or status.

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament and throughout the New Testament, ginomai appears in a wide variety of settings. It describes the creation of the world, the occurrence of historical events, and the personal transformation of individuals. When applied to Christian living, it points to the practical unfolding of a person's faith in daily life.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Ginomai can refer to several forms of coming into existence or changing states:

  • To happen, occur, or come to pass (describing events or circumstances).

  • To become or turn into something (describing a change in state, status, or character).

  • To be created or brought into existence.

  • To arrive at or appear in a particular place or condition.

In Philippians 2:15, the word means to become or prove to be. It marks the dynamic process where believers move into a state of spiritual integrity—becoming blameless and harmless as they yield to God's work within them.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses ginomai in both physical and spiritual realms. It often marks fulfillment, such as when an event takes place according to God's plan. It also marks personal change, such as when someone becomes a disciple, becomes weak for the sake of others, or becomes an example to fellow believers.

The word itself does not specify whether a change happens in an instant or over a long period. The surrounding sentence determines how the transition occurs. When used in direct exhortations, ginomai calls believers to align their behavior with their true standing in Christ, moving from one way of living into a life that clearly reflects God's character.

4. Theological Implications

In the broader context of Philippians 2, Paul reminds believers that God is actively working in them "both to will and to do for His good pleasure" (Philippians 2:13). The call to "become blameless and harmless" in verse 15 is not a demand to earn salvation by human effort. Rather, it is the visible result of God's grace taking root in a believer's daily habits and choices.

Transformation in the Christian life is real and observable. God's saving work causes a genuine change in how Christians treat one another and how they respond to difficult circumstances. As believers put off selfish ambition and grumbling, they step into the holy character God has prepared for them.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:14–15, Paul connects daily conduct directly to Christian witness: "Do all things without complaining and disputing, that you may become blameless and harmless, children of God without fault."

Internal friction, grumbling, and petty arguments damage the unity of the church and ruin its testimony. Paul shows that refraining from these negative attitudes is the key to becoming mature and unblemished witnesses. The grammatical form of ginomai here uses the aorist tense, middle voice, subjunctive mood, and plural number. The subjunctive mood presents this transformation as the intended goal of obeying Paul's command, while the plural form addresses the entire church body together. Becoming blameless is both a corporate and individual goal that the church pursues as a community.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Corinthians 14:20 — "Brethren, do not be children in understanding; however, in malice be babes, but in understanding be mature."

  • Galatians 4:16 — "Have I therefore become your enemy because I tell you the truth?"

  • 1 Thessalonians 3:4 — "For, in fact, we told you before when we were with you that we would suffer tribulation, just as it happened, and you know."

  • Hebrews 10:33 — "partly while you were made a spectacle both by reproaches and tribulations, and partly while you became companions of those who were so treated."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The goal of becoming "blameless and harmless" sets up a striking contrast with the surrounding culture. Paul describes the world as a "crooked and perverse generation." In such an environment, a local church that lives in peace without complaining or fighting stands out like a bright light in total darkness. The transition described by ginomai leads directly to public witness: as believers become pure in character, they "shine as lights in the world."
