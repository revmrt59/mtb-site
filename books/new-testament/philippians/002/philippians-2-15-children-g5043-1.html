CHILDREN - (téknon, Strong's G5043)

Summary:

The word teknon refers to a child, offspring, or descendant. It can describe a young child, a physical descendant, a spiritual student or child in the faith, or people characterized by a specific moral or spiritual quality. In Philippians 2:15, Paul uses the word to describe believers as "children of God" who are called to live pure and blameless lives as lights in a dark world.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Paul wrote his letter to the Philippians while imprisoned, encouraging a church facing outer opposition and inner personal disagreements. In chapter 2, Paul calls the church to humble unity and selflessness, pointing to Jesus Christ as the ultimate model of humble obedience.

Following Christ's example, believers are called to work out their salvation with fear and trembling, knowing that God is actively working in them. In Philippians 2:14–15, Paul urges them to do all things without grumbling or disputing. Living in this Christlike way allows them to stand out as "children of God" in a world filled with moral darkness and rebellion.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word teknon literally means a child or offspring. It is connected to the action of giving birth, emphasizing the real relationship between a parent and a child.

The word is used in several ways in Scripture:

  • A physical child, son, or daughter in a human family.

  • A physical descendant line across generations.

  • A spiritual child or disciple who has been guided into faith and spiritual growth by a mentor.

  • A person marked by a specific spiritual nature, character, or destiny.

When used of believers in relation to God, the word describes those who have been born into God's family, receiving spiritual life from Him and sharing His family name.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the New Testament, teknon highlights intimate relationship, birth origin, and shared nature. While other words for "son" sometimes emphasize legal standing or official inheritance rights, teknon focuses on the close, personal connection between parent and child and the character shared between them.

Scripture often speaks of children reflecting the qualities of their parents. Children naturally resemble those who raised them. Because of this, the term is frequently used spiritually. Believers are called children of God because they share His spiritual life and are expected to reflect His character in their daily conduct. Conversely, Scripture describes unrepentant sinners as "children of wrath" or "children of disobedience" because their lives reflect rebellion against God.

4. Theological Implications

The title "children of God" grounds Christian living in spiritual identity. Believers do not obey God in order to become His children; rather, they live holy lives because God has already made them His children. Identity comes first, and practical conduct follows.

This relationship is established through spiritual rebirth and adoption. God transforms believers, giving them a new nature. Because God is light and holy, His children are called to live as light in a dark world. Belonging to God's family brings both high privilege and clear responsibility. Believers carry their Father's reputation in the world, showing what God is like through their love, purity, and unity.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:15, Paul connects the believers' identity as children of God to their daily behavior. He instructs them to avoid complaining and arguing so that they "may become blameless and harmless, children of God without fault in the midst of a crooked and perverse generation."

The phrase "crooked and perverse generation" recalls Old Testament warnings where Israel was rebuked for acting corruptly and failing to reflect God's character. Paul applies this contrast to the Philippian church. Surrounded by a surrounding pagan culture that is morally twisted, Christians are to show a completely different way of living.

Grammatically, the word appears here as a nominative plural neuter noun, describing the believers together as God's children. Their blameless conduct proves that they belong to God. By refusing to grumble and fight, they shine like stars in the night sky, offering the word of life to those around them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 11:52 — Jesus died not only for the nation of Israel, but to gather together as one the "children" of God who were scattered across the world.

  • Romans 8:21 — Paul explains that creation itself will one day be set free from decay into the glorious freedom of the "children" of God.

  • 1 Corinthians 4:17 — Paul refers to Timothy as his beloved and faithful "son" in the Lord, showing how the word describes a deep spiritual partnership in ministry.

  • Ephesians 2:3 — Paul reminds believers of their former life apart from Christ, when they were by nature "children" of wrath.

  • 2 Timothy 2:1 — Paul addresses Timothy affectionately as his "son," urging him to remain strong in the grace found in Christ Jesus.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greco-Roman world of Philippi, citizenship and family lineage carried great social honor. People derived their social standing from their earthly fathers and Roman status.

Paul reframes the believers' understanding of honor. Their primary identity does not depend on Roman citizenship or earthly family roots. Their highest identity is that they are children of the living God. This spiritual reality gave early Christians courage to remain faithful, clean, and distinct even when facing social pressure or persecution.
