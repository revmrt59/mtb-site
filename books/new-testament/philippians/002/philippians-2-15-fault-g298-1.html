FAULT - (amṓmētos, Strong's G298)

Summary:

The Greek word amometos means blameless, unblemished, or free from moral fault and reproach. It describes a life of clear integrity that gives critics no valid ground for accusation. In Philippians 2:15, Paul uses this word to show how believers are to live as God's children amidst a dark and morally twisted culture. By living without complaining or arguing, believers maintain a testimony that is free from genuine fault, allowing the light of the gospel to shine brightly through their character.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word amometos is built from a root meaning "blemish," "blame," or "disgrace," combined with a prefix that negatives the root. Put together, the term describes someone or something that is without blemish, beyond reproach, or free from moral defect.

In ancient culture, the underlying idea was often connected to things set apart for inspection, such as sacrificial animals that had to be physically unblemished and free from defect. When used to describe personal character, the word shifted from physical inspection to moral evaluation. It describes a life that can stand up to public scrutiny because it is free from legitimate scandal or moral hypocrisy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word refers to being blameless, faultless, or irreproachable. It describes a person whose conduct is so transparently righteous that no real charge of wrongdoing can be brought against them.

Here the word does not mean sinless perfection in an absolute sense, for Scripture makes clear that all believers still struggle against sin. Instead, it describes a life characterized by integrity, honesty, and consistency. When a person lives amometos, any accusations made against them turn out to be false because their actual behavior is above reproach.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In New Testament writing, the word focuses on the public integrity of believers. It highlights how Christians ought to appear before both God and a watching world.

In its grammatical form in Philippians 2:15, the word functions as a nominative neuter plural adjective modifying the description of believers as God's blameless children. It sets up a vivid contrast between the pure, unblemished character of God's family and the corrupt standards of the surrounding world.

4. Theological Implications

The calling to live without fault rooted in the nature of God Himself. God is pure and holy, and His children are called to reflect His character in their daily lives. Living a blameless life is not a human effort to earn salvation; rather, it is the visible fruit of God working within the believer to transform their desires and actions.

A life free from fault protects the reputation of the gospel. When believers fall into public hypocrisy, moral failure, or constant strife, the name of God is dishonored among non-believers. Conversely, when Christians maintain moral integrity and genuine love, their lives confirm the truth and power of the message they proclaim.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:14-15, Paul places this word in a very practical setting. He instructs the Philippian church to "do all things without complaining and disputing." Immediately following this command, he explains the purpose: "that you may become blameless and harmless, children of God without fault."

Paul directly links being "without fault" to how believers handle internal church relationships and daily attitudes. Grumbling, petty arguing, and division create a bad testimony that blemishes the church. When believers abandon complaining and division, their moral witness becomes clean and unblemished.

This pure testimony is vital because believers live "in the midst of a crooked and perverse generation." Surrounded by a culture marked by moral confusion and self-interest, Christians are called to stand out. By living lives free from fault, they "shine as lights in the world," offering a clear contrast to the darkness around them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Peter 3:14 — "Therefore, beloved, looking forward to these things, be diligent to be found by Him in peace, without spot and blameless..."

In this passage, the word is translated "blameless." Peter uses it to encourage believers who are waiting for the return of Jesus Christ. Expecting the Lord's return should motivate believers to live pure, peaceful lives that are free from moral fault when He appears.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The emphasis on being blameless provides a helpful check on how we measure spiritual health. Often people measure spirituality by active involvement or outward dynamic gifts. However, Paul focuses on moral integrity, harmony, and blameless conduct.

Living without fault does not mean a Christian will never make mistakes or need to seek forgiveness. Rather, a blameless person handles failure with humility, confessing sin quickly and making restitution where needed. Integrity consists not in pretending to be perfect, but in living an honest life where no lingering charge of hidden wickedness or hypocrisy can stand.
