WORLD - (kósmos, Strong's G2889)

Summary:

The word kosmos refers fundamentally to order, an arranged system, the physical creation, the human race, or the earthly sphere of human life. In biblical writing, it often describes the human world as a place separated from God's holiness or organized in opposition to Him. In Philippians 2:15, the word refers to the present earthly realm of human society—a spiritually dark environment in which believers are called to stand out as blameless children of God and shine like bright stars.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In early Greek usage, kosmos originally denoted order, arrangement, or dynamic harmony. It described something properly set in order, such as an organized army, an orderly government, or an arrangement of ornaments. Because the visible universe exhibits remarkable order and beauty, the word naturally came to mean the created universe or the world.

In the New Testament, kosmos expands to describe the entire sphere of human existence. While God created the physical universe in an orderly and good condition, human fallenness introduced moral disorder. As a result, biblical writers frequently use kosmos to refer to human society on earth, especially human culture operating apart from God's truth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word kosmos can refer to:

  • The created physical universe or the earth itself.

  • The human race as the object of divine love and redemption.

  • The earthly sphere of human life, activities, and earthly possessions.

  • The fallen system of human society alienated from God and influenced by evil.

Here in Philippians 2:15, kosmos refers to the earthly realm of human society. It identifies the immediate moral and spiritual environment in which Christians live, work, and bear witness to Jesus Christ.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses kosmos in several related ways depending on the passage:

  • The Created Order: It denotes the physical earth and universe framed by God at creation.

  • Humanity: It refers to all people inhabiting the earth, as when God loves the world and sends His Son to offer salvation.

  • The Earthly Environment: It describes the physical domain where human history unfolds, contrasting earthly life with heavenly reality.

  • The Fallen Moral Order: It marks human culture and its values when operating in rebellion against God's law.

The context determines which shade of meaning is primary. The word itself does not automatically mean "evil"; rather, its moral character depends on whether it describes God's good creation, mankind receiving mercy, or human society standing in resistance to divine truth.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical view of the world holds two key truths together. First, the physical creation and humanity are good by virtue of creation and are the objects of God's redemptive care. God has not abandoned the world; He sent His Son to save people out of it and reconcile them to Himself.

Second, human society in its fallen state is morally broken and spiritually blind. The world values self-assertion, pride, and division, which stand opposed to the character of Christ. Believers reside within the world as physical inhabitants, but their spiritual allegiance belongs to the kingdom of God. This dual reality creates the framework for Christian mission: believers do not retreat from the world in total isolation, nor do they conform to its sinful patterns. Instead, they live in the world as visible representations of God's grace and truth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:15, Paul writes that believers are to be "blameless and harmless, children of God without fault in the midst of a crooked and perverse generation, among whom you shine as lights in the world."

The grammatical form of kosmos in this verse is dative, singular, and masculine. This dative form functions as a location marker, placing the Christian's daily life and witness directly inside this present earthly domain.

Paul contrasts two vivid realities in this passage:

  • The moral condition of the surrounding culture: "a crooked and perverse generation."

  • The spiritual location and purpose of believers: "lights in the world."

The word "lights" describes heavenly bodies or luminaries, such as stars shining in a night sky. The darker the sky, the clearer the light of the stars appears. By using kosmos, Paul reminds the Philippians that their surrounding culture—with all its hostility and moral corruption—is the exact backdrop against which God's grace in them is meant to be seen. Instead of complaining or arguing about the world's darkness (Philippians 2:14), believers are positioned in the world to hold forth the word of life.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ephesians 1:4 — "just as He chose us in Him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and without blame before Him in love;"

  • 1 Corinthians 1:21 — "For since, in the wisdom of God, the world through wisdom did not know God, it pleased God through the foolishness of the message preached to save those who believe."

  • John 17:16 — "They are not of the world, just as I am not of the world."

  • James 1:27 — "Pure and undefiled religion before God and the Father is this: to visit orphans and widows in their trouble, and to keep oneself unspotted from the world."

  • 1 John 2:17 — "And the world is passing away, and the lust of it; but he who does the will of God abides forever."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's phrase "crooked and perverse generation" in Philippians 2:15 echoes the language of Deuteronomy 32:5, where Moses described Israel's unfaithfulness in the wilderness. In Deuteronomy, God's covenant children had acted corruptly and grown crooked.

In Philippians, Paul flips this background on its head. True children of God, redeemed through Christ, are called to be the exact opposite of that wilderness generation. Rather than grumbling and disputing, believers maintain unity and moral purity. When they do so, their conduct marks them out in dramatic contrast to the surrounding culture. The world provides the dark backdrop, but the gospel gives believers the spiritual light to shine brightly within it.
