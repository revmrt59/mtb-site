CHRIST - (Christós, Strong's G5547)

Summary:

The word Christos means "Anointed One" or "Messiah," designating Jesus as God's chosen King and Savior. In Philippians 2:16, the word appears in the phrase "the day of Christ," identifying Jesus as the supreme Lord whose future return will bring final evaluation, vindication, and joy for faithful Christian labor.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Greek world, the verb from which Christos derives meant to smear or anoint with oil. When Jewish translators rendered the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, they used Christos to translate the Hebrew word Mashiach ("Messiah"). In Old Testament history, kings, priests, and prophets were anointed with oil as an outward sign that God had set them apart and empowered them for sacred leadership.

By the time of the New Testament, Christos had become the primary title for the long-awaited Savior and King whom God promised to send to deliver His people. In the letters of the apostle Paul, Christos functions both as a official royal title ("the Anointed Messiah") and as a proper name for Jesus of Nazareth, reflecting the early church's firm conviction that Jesus fulfills every Old Testament messianic prophecy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means "Anointed One" or "Messiah." It identifies a person designated, authorized, and empowered by God to carry out His divine mission.

When applied to Jesus, the title signifies that He is God's appointed King, the priest who offers final reconciliation, and the prophet who reveals God's ultimate truth. In Philippians 2:16, the word is expressed in the genitive case ("of Christ"), indicating possession and relationship. Here it describes a specific future time—"the day of Christ"—belonging to Jesus, when He will return in glory to evaluate the lives and service of His followers.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, Christos carries complementary shades of meaning:

  • In the Gospels, the term often highlights Jesus as the fulfillment of Israel's messianic hope, the promised Son of David expected to bring salvation.

  • In the Book of Acts, the apostles preach that the crucified and resurrected Jesus is indeed the Christ, proving His identity through Scripture and His resurrection.

  • In the Epistles, Christos is frequently joined with the personal name Jesus ("Jesus Christ" or "Christ Jesus") or stands alone to emphasize His sovereign Lordship over the church and creation.

In Paul's writings, the phrase "the day of Christ" builds upon the Old Testament concept of "the day of the Lord." By identifying this final day of judgment and vindication as "the day of Christ," Paul affirms that Jesus exercises full divine authority on the final day of history.

4. Theological Implications

The title Christos grounds the Christian faith in God's historical promises. Calling Jesus the "Christ" means He is not merely a wise teacher or moral example, but God's designated King who possesses absolute authority over heaven and earth.

The phrase "the day of Christ" carries significant theological weight for the Christian life. It reminds believers that history is moving toward a definite conclusion when Jesus will return. On that day, human accomplishments will be evaluated by divine standards. For leaders and gospel workers, the day of Christ provides a solemn standard of accountability as well as a powerful source of hope. Faithful labor for the gospel is never wasted because Jesus Himself will recognize and reward it when He comes.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:16, Paul urges the believers in Philippi to hold fast to the "word of life" amid a corrupt culture. He provides a deeply personal motivation for their obedience: "so that I may rejoice in the day of Christ, that I have not run in vain or labored in vain."

Grammatically, the target word is a masculine singular noun in the genitive case, linking "the day" directly to Jesus as its owner and center. The focus is not simply on an abstract end-time event, but on the personal return of Jesus Christ.

Paul views his own apostolic ministry through the lens of this coming day. If the Philippians remain steadfast in their faith, Paul will stand on the day of Christ with cause for holy celebration, knowing that his strenuous exertion—described using metaphors of athletic running and hard manual labor—produced lasting spiritual fruit. The presence of Christos reminds the Philippian believers that their ultimate loyalty belongs to the heavenly King, whose future judgment matters far more than present hardships or earthly rulers.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 11:27 – Martha expresses her faith in Jesus, saying, "Yes, Lord, I believe that You are the Christ, the Son of God, who is to come into the world."

  • Acts 17:3 – Paul reasons in the synagogue, "demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead."

  • Romans 6:8 – Paul highlights the believer's union with Jesus, writing, "Now if we died with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with Him."

  • Philippians 3:8 – Paul expresses his deep commitment to Jesus, writing, "Yet indeed I also count all things loss for the excellence of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Roman city of Philippi, residents were expected to pledge ultimate allegiance to the Roman Emperor. By repeatedly calling Jesus Christos (the Anointed King) and speaking of "the day of Christ," Paul presents a direct contrast to imperial claims.

Furthermore, Paul's reference to running and laboring "in vain" recalls Old Testament themes where faithful servants wondered if their work for God had accomplished anything lasting. By pointing to "the day of Christ," Paul assures ministers and believers alike that faithful service grounded in the word of life will receive eternal validation from the Anointed King Himself.
