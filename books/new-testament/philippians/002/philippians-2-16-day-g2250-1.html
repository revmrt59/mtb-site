DAY - (hēméra, Strong's G2250)

Summary:

The Greek noun hemera fundamentally refers to the period between sunrise and sunset, a twenty-four-hour day, or a specific period or point in time. In the New Testament, it is often used prophetically to point toward an appointed time of divine evaluation and fulfillment. In Philippians 2:16, Paul speaks of "the day of Christ," referring to the future return and judgment seat of Jesus Christ, when the faithful endurance and gospel ministry of believers will be evaluated and rewarded.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

peak

The word hemera is the standard Greek word for daylight or a calendar day. Throughout biblical history, time was understood through daily cycles marked by God’s ongoing providential care. In the Greek translation of the Old Testament, hemera translated the common Hebrew word for day (yom), frequently appearing in expressions describing historical periods, appointed festivals, or the ultimate "Day of the LORD"—the time when God steps into human history to judge evil and bring final salvation to His people.

In the New Testament, the early church inherited this rich orientation toward time. The apostles understood human history as moving deliberately toward a definite, divine conclusion. When the New Testament writers speak of a specific "day" associated with Jesus Christ, they draw upon this long heritage, transforming the Old Testament expectation of God's final reckoning into the eager expectation of Christ's second coming.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Hemera can refer to:

  • The light hours of a single day as opposed to night.

  • A full twenty-four-hour period.

  • A general timeframe, era, or period in history.

  • An appointed or fixed time for judgment, evaluation, or vindication.

In Philippians 2:16, hemera carries this final sense of a designated point in time. By combining it with "Christ," Paul refers specifically to the future return of Jesus Christ—the moment when the Lord will review the labors of His servants and grant eternal rewards.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, hemera serves both temporal and theological functions. In narrative descriptions, it simply marks the passage of routine time, such as daily activities or specific historical dates. However, when paired with divine titles or prophetic contexts, the word gains solemn theological weight.

Scripture speaks of "the last days," "the day of judgment," and "the day of redemption." In these contexts, hemera does not merely measure hours on a clock; it marks the decisive boundary between the present age and the age to come. It signifies the climax of divine redemption, where hidden works are brought to light, believers are fully glorified, and God's righteous rule is made visible to all.

4. Theological Implications

The expression "the day of Christ" carries profound implications for Christian living and pastoral leadership. First, it reminds believers that history is not aimless or circular. God has set a definitive day on His calendar when Jesus Christ will return in glory.

Second, "the day of Christ" establishes the true standard for spiritual success. Pastoral ministry and Christian service are not judged primarily by immediate earthly outcomes, popular applause, or temporary visible results. Instead, their true value will be revealed when Christ evaluates every heart and labor. For Paul, the prospect of this coming day provided deep motivation to endure hardship and present the believers mature in faith.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:14–16, Paul urges the believers in Philippi to live without grumbling or disputing, shining as blameless lights in a dark world while holding fast to the "word of life." He then links their faithful perseverance directly to his own joy on "the day of Christ."

The accusative, singular, feminine form of hemera functions here within a prepositional phrase indicating time and direction. Paul looks forward toward that future horizon. If the Philippians remain steadfast in the Gospel, Paul will have cause to rejoice on that final day, knowing that his intense personal ministry among them was not wasted effort or spent in vain. The term highlighted here roots apostolic hope not in current circumstances—such as Paul's imprisonment—but in the future, guaranteed reality of Christ's return and final approval.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Peter 3:10 – "But the day of the Lord will come as a thief in the night..." Here the word points to the sovereignly appointed time of divine judgment and ultimate cosmic renewal.

  • Acts 2:15 – "For these are not drunk, as you suppose, since it is only the third hour of the day." In this historical setting, the word simply marks the time of morning when Peter addresses the crowd at Pentecost.

  • 1 Timothy 5:5 – "Now she who is really a widow, and left alone, trusts in God and continues in supplications and prayers night and day." The word forms part of an idiom describing unceasing, faithful prayer offered continuously throughout daily life.

  • Hebrews 10:11 – "And every priest stands daily ministering and offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins..." The rendering highlights the routine, repeated nature of the Old Covenant rituals in contrast to Christ’s once-for-all sacrifice.

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is worth observing that Paul frequently uses expressions like "the day of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:6) or "the day of Christ" (Philippians 1:10; 2:16) in his letter to the Philippians. While Old Testament prophets spoke constantly of "the Day of the LORD," Paul seamlessly applies this divine judgment day to Jesus Christ Himself. This demonstrates the early church's clear conviction regarding the deity of Jesus: the one coming to judge and reward the world is none other than the Lord Jesus Christ.

Furthermore, Paul's focus on this future day frames Christian endurance with eternal hope. Rather than viewing present sufferings or ministerial labors through the lens of immediate hardship, the apostle measures present sacrifice against the glorious outcome of Christ's final appraisal.
