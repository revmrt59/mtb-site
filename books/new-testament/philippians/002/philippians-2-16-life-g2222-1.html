LIFE - (zōḗ, Strong's G2222)

Summary:

The Greek word zōē refers to life in its fullest, most active sense, particularly the divine, uncreated, and eternal life that originates in God. Unlike terms that emphasize mere biological existence or daily earthly routines, zōē routinely describes the spiritual vitality imparted to believers through Jesus Christ. In Philippians 2:16, zōē describes the character of the gospel message as "the word of life," identifying the gospel as the living proclamation that conveys God's salvation and transforms believers to shine as lights in a dark world.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek usage, the term zōē designated the fundamental principle of life, vitality, and conscious existence. It was often distinguished from bios, which typically referred to physical life, the duration of earthly existence, or the material means of livelihood.

In the New Testament, zōē receives its deepest meaning from divine revelation. Rather than focusing merely on physical breath or biological functioning, biblical writers use zōē primarily to describe the high, supernatural life of God Himself. In Philippians 2:16, the apostle Paul writes to the church at Philippi while imprisoned, urging them to maintain unified, faultless Christian conduct. He encourages them to hold fast to "the word of life" so that their dynamic witness will demonstrate that his apostolic labors were not in vain.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word zōē can refer to physical life, but most frequently in the New Testament it refers to spiritual, eternal, and divine life. When used of God, it denotes His self-existent, uncreated vitality. When used of believers, it refers to the spiritual quickening received through faith in Jesus Christ, which overcomes spiritual death and culminates in the future resurrection.

In Philippians 2:16, the word functions within the key phrase "the word of life." Here the word means that the gospel message possesses inherent spiritual vitality and actively imparts divine life to those who receive it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

across Scripture, zōē carries rich nuances depending on its grammatical and canonical context:

  • God's Uncreated Life: The word describes God as the original source and possessor of true life.

  • Present Spiritual Vitality: It describes the new birth and dynamic spiritual quickening experienced by believers in Christ today.

  • Eternal Future Existence: It designates the everlasting fellowship with God promised to believers beyond physical death and in the final resurrection.

  • Quality of the Gospel: When modifying terms like "word," it emphasizes that the message of salvation is not a dead philosophical system, but a vital, life-giving truth from God.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of zōē underscores that salvation is far more than a legal pardon or moral reform; it is the impartation of new divine life to those who are spiritually dead. Human beings apart from Christ lack this divine vitality.

Because true life resides in God, eternal life is received as a gift through Jesus Christ. The designation of the gospel as "the word of life" reminds the church that the gospel carries the power of the Holy Spirit to transform human hearts, sustain perseverance, and produce visible holiness in the believer's walk.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:14–16, Paul instructs believers to do all things without complaining and disputing, so that they may prove themselves blameless and harmless children of God. He pictures the surrounding culture as a "crooked and perverse generation," in the midst of which Christians are called to shine as lights.

Immediately after this call to clear testimony, Paul speaks of "holding fast the word of life." The noun zōē appears in the genitive singular feminine form, directly describing the nature of the "word" (logos). The message Paul preached is defined by the life of God. This life-giving word empowers believers to stand out against the darkness of the world.

Paul connects their faithful adherence to this word with his ultimate joy: "so that I may rejoice in the day of Christ that I have not run in vain or labored in vain." If the Philippians maintain their firm grip on the word of life—and continue to hold it forth to others—their lives demonstrate the real power of Paul's gospel ministry before the judgment seat of Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 11:25 – Jesus declared Himself to be the resurrection and the "life," showing that divine vitality is centered in His person.

  • Romans 8:2 – Paul explains that the law of the Spirit of "life" in Christ Jesus sets believers free from the law of sin and death.

  • Galatians 6:8 – Paul writes that those who sow to the Spirit will reap everlasting "life" from the Spirit.

  • 1 John 5:20 – John closes his epistle by affirming that Jesus Christ is the true God and eternal "life."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase "holding fast the word of life" in Philippians 2:16 can carry a twofold sense in Greek commentary: holding fast to the message for one's own perseverance, and holding forth the message as a torch held out to guide others. Both nuances fit the broader passage. Believers preserve the life-giving gospel in their hearts while publicly displaying its power through transformed lives, providing a living contrast to the darkness of the world around them.
