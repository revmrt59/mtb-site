GLAD - (chaírō, Strong's G5463)

Summary:

The verb chairō means to rejoice, to be glad, or to experience delight and joy. It can describe general human happiness or spiritual gladness in response to God's work. In Philippians 2:17, Paul uses this word to express his personal gladness while facing imprisonment and the possibility of martyrdom. He views his life as a sacrificial drink offering poured out for the faith of the Philippian believers, and far from despairing, he actively rejoices in their spiritual progress and invites them to share in his joy.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek, chairō was the standard word used to express gladness, happiness, and delight. It was a word of common daily life, used in formal greetings much like saying "hello" or "be well." It described the gladness felt upon receiving good news, welcoming a friend, or seeing a difficult situation turn out well.

In the New Testament, chairō carries this ordinary human sense of gladness, but it also takes on deep spiritual significance. Rather than depending on favorable circumstances, Christian gladness is rooted in who God is and what He is accomplishing. In the letter to the Philippians, Paul writes while imprisoned and awaiting trial. Throughout the epistle, chairō marks an unyielding joy that earthly hardship cannot destroy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chairō means to rejoice, to be glad, or to feel delight. Depending on how it is used, the word can refer to:

  • Expressing joy over positive news or events.

  • Experiencing inward gladness and spiritual satisfaction.

  • Extending a standard greeting or welcome.

In Philippians 2:17, the word means to be glad or to rejoice. Paul uses it in the first person ("I am glad") to describe his personal state of mind. He is not merely instructing others to feel happy; he is describing his own lived experience of joy in the middle of suffering.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, chairō appears in a variety of settings:

  • Everyday Gladness and Greetings: The word is used for normal human happiness, such as the disciples' joy upon seeing the resurrected Lord, or the happiness felt when something lost is restored. It is also used as a common greeting, translated as "Greetings" or "Hail."

  • Joy in Gospel Growth: Believers express chairō when they see the gospel advancing, people coming to faith, or churches walking in truth and unity.

  • Joy in Suffering: One of the most distinct uses of chairō occurs when believers experience real gladness while enduring trials or persecution for Christ's sake.

When people rejoice in Scripture, their gladness is an intentional response of the heart to God's faithfulness and work in the world.

4. Theological Implications

The gladness expressed by chairō differs from ordinary worldly happiness. Worldly happiness relies heavily on agreeable circumstances, comfort, and physical safety. When those external conditions disappear, happiness usually vanishes with them.

Spiritual gladness is grounded in the unchanging character and promises of God. Because God remains faithful regardless of circumstances, a Christian can experience genuine gladness even in painful situations. Gladness is not the absence of sorrow or hardship; rather, it is the settled confidence that God is working out His purpose for His glory and the good of His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:17, Paul presents a picture of self-sacrificing service. He compares his life and potential execution to a "drink offering"—a sacrificial liquid poured out alongside the primary sacrifice offered by the Philippians, which is their faith and service to God.

Humanly speaking, facing prison and potential death would bring sorrow and fear. Yet Paul states, "I am glad." His choice of chairō reveals that his primary focus is not his physical preservation or earthly comfort. Instead, he sees his life as a sacrificial offering that serves the spiritual growth of the Philippian believers. Because their faith is genuine and maturing, Paul finds rich reason to be glad.

The verb shows that Paul himself is performing the action of rejoicing in the present moment. His gladness is not a distant future hope contingent on getting released from prison; it is his current reality in chains. He immediately pairs this gladness with a call for mutual celebration, asking the Philippians to share in the exact same joy.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 2:10 — "When they saw the star, they rejoiced with exceedingly great joy."

  • Acts 11:23 — "When he came and had seen the grace of God, he was glad and encouraged them all that with purpose of heart they should continue with the Lord."

  • Romans 12:15 — "Rejoice with those who rejoice, and weep with those who weep."

  • Philippians 4:4 — "Rejoice in the Lord always. Again I will say, rejoice!"

  • Colossians 1:24 — "I now rejoice in my sufferings for you, and fill up in my flesh what is lacking in the afflictions of Christ, for the sake of His body, which is the church."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Philippians 2:17–18, Paul pairs chairō ("I am glad") with a compound form, synchairō ("and rejoice with you all"). The prefix syn- means "together with." By joining these terms, Paul highlights that biblical joy is meant to be shared within the community of faith.

Paul's gladness is not an isolated, private feeling. He rejoices in what God is accomplishing in the Philippian church, and he invites them to join him in that gladness. Shared participation in Christ's work creates a bond of mutual joy that hardship cannot break.
