SACRIFICE - (thysía, Strong's G2378)

Summary:

The Greek word thysia refers to an offering presented to God, traditionally involving an animal sacrifice placed on an altar. In the New Testament, it expands beyond ritual offerings to describe both the supreme, once-for-all sacrifice of Jesus Christ and the devoted lives, service, and gifts of believers. In Philippians 2:17, Paul uses thysia to describe the Philippians' faith and service as an acceptable worship offering to God, upon which Paul views his own life and labors as a supporting drink offering.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Mediterranean world, sacrifice was the central act of religious worship. Both in Israel's temple system and in surrounding cultures, bringing a sacrifice meant approaching an altar to offer something precious to God or a deity. In Israel's worship, God established sacrifices to make atonement for sin, express thanksgiving, and reaffirm covenant fellowship.

When early Christian writers used thysia, their readers immediately understood the picture of an altar, a dedicated gift, and a sacred act of worship. However, the gospel transformed this word. Instead of bringing repeated animal offerings to an altar, believers saw Jesus Christ as the true and final sacrifice, while their own lives became living offerings rendered to God through faith.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word thysia primarily refers to a sacrifice or offering presented to God.

The word can mean:

  • A ritual victim or physical animal sacrifice offered on an altar.

  • The act of offering a sacrifice to God.

  • Figuratively, a spiritual offering presented by a worshipper, such as praise, financial gifts, or a life dedicated to God's service.

In every case, the word carries the core idea of something dedicated or yielded up to God as an act of worship.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, thysia develops three primary lines of meaning:

First, it refers to Old Covenant animal sacrifices. The New Testament acknowledges these historical sacrifices commanded under the Law, while explaining that they were temporary copies pointing forward to a greater reality.

Second, it refers to the supreme sacrifice of Jesus Christ. Jesus offered Himself once for all to pay for sin, replacing the old sacrificial system with a perfect, permanent redemption.

Third, it refers to the spiritual sacrifices offered by Christians. Because believers are united to Christ, their wholehearted faith, acts of love, financial generosity, and verbal praise are described as acceptable sacrifices to God.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of thysia reinforces vital gospel truths:

  • The Sufficiency of Christ: Animal sacrifices could never permanently remove sin. Jesus' self-offering on the cross fully satisfied God's justice, making further sin offerings unnecessary.

  • The Priesthood of Believers: Because Christ opened the way into God's presence, all Christians serve as a royal priesthood. Worshippers no longer bring slain animals; instead, they offer their daily lives, service, and resources to God.

  • Grace Before Sacrifice: Spiritual sacrifices do not earn salvation or turn away divine wrath. God accepts the believer's worship because the believer already stands accepted in Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:17, Paul writes from prison, facing the real possibility of execution. He tells the Philippians: "Yes, and if I am being poured out as a drink offering on the sacrifice and service of your faith, I am glad and rejoice with you all."

Paul draws upon ancient sacrificial imagery. In Old Testament worship, a priest would lay the main offering (a burnt offering or grain offering) on the altar, and then pour a wine offering (a drink offering) alongside or over it.

Paul pictures the Philippians' faith and gospel service as the primary sacrifice (thysia) laid on God's altar. Paul does not highlight his own apostolic labor as the main event; instead, he views the believers' steadfast faith as the central sacrifice pleasing to God. He views his potential death or continued imprisonment merely as the drink offering poured out beside their sacrifice.

Grammatically, the word appears in the dative singular feminine form, marking the sacrifice upon which Paul's life is poured out. This picture transforms suffering and martyrdom into a joyful act of shared worship. Paul rejoices because their faith stands as a genuine offering to God, making any personal sacrifice on his part worthwhile.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 9:13 — "But go and learn what this means: 'I desire mercy and not sacrifice.' For I did not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance."

  • Romans 12:1 — "I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that you present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God, which is your reasonable service."

  • Ephesians 5:2 — "And walk in love, as Christ also has loved us and given Himself for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweet-smelling aroma."

  • Philippians 4:18 — "Indeed I have all and abound. I am full, having received from Epaphroditus the things sent from you, a sweet-smelling aroma, an acceptable sacrifice, well-pleasing to God."

  • Hebrews 9:26 — "He then would have had to suffer often since the foundation of the world; but now, once at the end of the ages, He has appeared to put away sin by the sacrifice of Himself."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding thysia in Philippians 2:17 gives believers a elevated view of ordinary Christian faithfulness. It is easy to view missionary work, church leadership, or martyrdom as the primary work of God, while viewing the quiet endurance of church members as secondary.

Paul flips this perspective. He calls the Philippians' everyday faith and gospel partnership the main sacrifice on the altar. Pastoral labor and personal hardship exist to cultivate and support that faith. When Christians trust God, endure trials, and serve one another, God receives their life as a sweet-smelling sacrifice.
