GLAD - (chaírō, Strong's G5463)

Summary:

The Greek verb chairō means to rejoice, to be glad, or to take delight in something. In everyday Greek, it was also used as a friendly greeting, but in the New Testament it frequently describes an inner state or open expression of Christian joy. In Philippians 2:18, Paul uses this word as a command to the church, urging the believers to share in his holy delight over the gospel, even while he faces prison and possible execution.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb chairō was part of everyday conversation in the ancient Greek-speaking world. People used it when meeting friends, writing letters, or celebrating good news. At its basic level, it describes the emotional experience and outward expression of gladness, pleasure, or cheer.

In the Greco-Roman world, happiness was usually tied to favorable events, health, prosperity, or high social standing. However, New Testament writers gave chairō a deeper spiritual setting. For believers in Jesus, gladness is not dictated by pleasant circumstances. Instead, it flows from God's salvation, the truth of the gospel, and the hope of Christ's return.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Chairō means to be glad, to rejoice, to take delight, or to be full of cheer. Depending on the sentence, it can describe a personal feeling of gladness, a public expression of joy, or a warm greeting.

The word can refer to:

  • The gladness felt upon receiving good news or seeing a loved one.

  • The spiritual joy experienced when recognizing God's work in the world.

  • A deliberate, ongoing attitude of praise and contentment in spite of hardship.

Unlike modern ideas of cheerfulness that depend on everything going smoothly, chairō refers to a deep gladness grounded in something firm and lasting.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, chairō appears in a wide range of situations. Sometimes it carries a simple social meaning, such as a spoken greeting ("Hail" or "Greetings"). In other places, it describes the gladness of people who encounter Jesus, witness a miracle, or see the church growing.

When chairō is used in command form, as it is in Philippians 2:18, it moves beyond a passive emotion. It becomes a personal choice and a spiritual duty. Scripture instructs believers to actively cultivate gladness because of who God is and what He has done. This gladness can exist alongside deep sorrow or severe trials, because its source is the unshakeable grace of God.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical call to be glad points to several important truths about the Christian life:

  • Godly joy is rooted in God's faithful character rather than personal comfort.

  • Suffering for Christ does not destroy spiritual gladness; instead, sharing in Christ's service brings deep, spiritual satisfaction.

  • Joy is meant to be shared within the community of believers. Christian gladness is relational, bringing believers together in unified praise.

When believers choose gladness in the middle of trial, they demonstrate to the world that Christ is more valuable than physical security or earthly ease.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:17–18, Paul writes from a Roman prison, contemplating the real possibility of execution. He uses the image of an Old Testament worship service, describing his own life as a drink offering being poured out upon the sacrificial service of the Philippians' faith. Humanly speaking, this was a moment for grief. Yet Paul declares that he is glad and rejoices with them all.

In verse 18, Paul turns to the congregation with a clear instruction: "For the same reason you also be glad and rejoice with me." The word translated "be glad" is a present active imperative verb in the plural form. Paul is addressing the whole church, calling on every member to adopt an ongoing stance of shared delight.

The phrase "for the same reason" anchors their gladness. The Philippians are not to mourn Paul's sacrifices as a tragic loss, but to view them as a meaningful offering to God. Because God is producing lasting spiritual fruit through their faith and Paul's ministry, both Paul and the Philippians have every reason to be genuinely glad together.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 19:6 — "So he made haste and came down, and received Him joyfully."

  • Here the word describes Zacchaeus's energetic gladness as he welcomed Jesus into his house after a life-changing encounter.

  • John 20:20 — "Then the disciples were glad when they saw the Lord."

  • The word captures the overwhelming joy of the disciples when the risen Savior appeared to them on Easter evening.

  • Acts 11:23 — "When he came and had seen the grace of God, he was glad, and encouraged them all that with purpose of heart they should continue with the Lord."

  • Barnabas experienced spiritual gladness when he saw Gentiles coming to faith and God's grace at work in Antioch.

  • Romans 12:15 — "Rejoice with those who rejoice, and weep with those who weep."

  • Paul uses the word to instruct believers to enter sympathetically into the gladness of their brothers and sisters in Christ.

  • Philippians 4:4 — "Rejoice in the Lord always. Again I will say, rejoice!"

  • Paul repeats this command to emphasize that continuous gladness is to mark the life of every believer whose trust is in the Lord.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The grammatical form in Philippians 2:18 gives useful insight into Paul's pastoral heart. By using a present active imperative verb in the plural, Paul gives a direct command to the entire congregation for continuous action. He does not treat gladness as an optional feeling reserved for a few optimistic personalities. Instead, he addresses the whole church community, expecting every believer to actively participate in this joy.

This reframes how believers view Christian service and sacrifice. Painful circumstances may be unavoidable, but they do not hold power over Christian gladness. When God is honored and the gospel moves forward, believers are commanded to rejoice together in the work He is accomplishing.
