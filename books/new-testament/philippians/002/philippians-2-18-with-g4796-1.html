WITH - (synchaírō, Strong's G4796)

Summary:

In Philippians 2:18, Paul commands the believers in Philippi to share in his gladness by using the verb sunchairō ("rejoice with me"). Facing imprisonment and potential martyrdom, Paul views his life as a sacrificial offering poured out for their growth in faith. Rather than letting his suffering produce despair or sorrow, Paul calls the church to enter directly into his spiritual joy and celebrate what God is accomplishing through his ministry.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word sunchairō is formed by combining the prefix sun (meaning "together with") and the verb chairō (meaning "to rejoice" or "to be glad"). Together, the compound word means to share in someone's joy, to celebrate alongside another person, or to express gladness in light of a common blessing.

In the ancient Greco-Roman world and throughout the New Testament, joy was rarely treated as a private or isolated feeling. When a person experienced deliverance, a blessing, or good news, it was natural to invite friends, family, and neighbors to share in the delight. Sunchairō highlights this social and communal dimension of gladness, bringing people together around a shared cause for praise.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Sunchairō means to rejoice together with someone, to join another person in celebration, or to congratulate a friend.

When a person is named as the object of the verb (such as "rejoice with me"), the word describes entering into that specific individual's gladness. When truth or a shared circumstance is the focus, it describes taking delight in a shared reality. In Philippians 2:18, the word functions as a direct command to the congregation, calling the church to actively participate in the Apostle Paul's joy.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses sunchairō to describe shared delight in various contexts:

In the Gospel of Luke, the word appears in settings of communal celebration. When Elizabeth gave birth to John the Baptist, her neighbors and relatives gathered because they heard of God's great mercy, sharing her gladness. Similarly, in Jesus' parables of the lost sheep and lost coin, the owner calls friends and neighbors together to celebrate the recovery of what was lost.

In Paul's letters, the word reflects the unity of believers within the church. When one member of the body of Christ is honored, the other members share in that joy rather than yielding to jealousy. In Philippians 2:17–18, Paul uses the word twice in close succession: first expressing his own shared joy with the church, and then calling the church to share in his joy.

4. Theological Implications

The call to share in apostolic joy highlights key truths about the Christian life:

First, Christian joy is rooted in the gospel rather than comfortable circumstances. Paul wrote to the Philippians while under house arrest in Rome, facing a real possibility of execution. Yet he framed his suffering as a drink offering poured out on the sacrificial service of their faith. The ability to rejoice in such circumstances demonstrates that true joy depends on the advancement of God's kingdom, not personal ease.

Second, shared joy displays the reality of Christian fellowship. Believers are not isolated individuals walking separate paths; they are members of one body. When God works through one servant, the entire congregation has reason to celebrate. Joining in another believer's joy guards the heart against envy, bitterness, and self-centered grief.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:17–18, Paul uses sacrificial language drawn from Old Testament worship traditions. He describes the Philippians' faith and service as a sacrifice offered to God, and he presents his own life as a drink offering poured out alongside it.

Instead of viewing his potential martyrdom as a tragic defeat, Paul declares in verse 17 that he is glad and rejoices with them all. Then, in verse 18, he addresses the church with a parallel command: "For the same reason you also be glad and rejoice with me."

The verb is a direct command in the active voice, directed to the whole congregation. Paul instructs the Philippians not to sink into sorrow or anxiety on his account. Because his suffering serves their spiritual progress and honors Christ, the church is called to adopt Paul's perspective and share in his gladness.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 1:58 – When Elizabeth gave birth to her son, her neighbors and relatives heard how the Lord had shown great mercy to her, and they "rejoiced" with her.

  • Luke 15:6 – Upon finding his lost sheep, the shepherd calls his friends and neighbors together, saying, "Rejoice" with me.

  • 1 Corinthians 12:26 – In describing the mutual care within the church, Paul explains that if one member is honored, all the members rejoice "with" it.

  • Philippians 2:17 – Immediately before the target verse, Paul writes that even if he is poured out as a drink offering, he is glad and rejoices "with" all the Philippians.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Philippians 2:17–18, Paul pairs two verbs for joy: simple gladness (chairō) and shared joy (sunchairō). This deliberate repetition creates a strong emphasis on mutual celebration. Paul rejoices with them, and he commands them to rejoice with him.

This double invitation shows that biblical joy is an active choice grounded in faith. By calling the Philippians to share his gladness, Paul invites them to look past his chains and focus on the enduring victory of Jesus Christ.
