ENCOURAGED - (eupsychéō, Strong's G2174)

Summary:

The word eupsycheō means to be glad in spirit, to be of good cheer, or to be genuinely heartened and refreshed in soul. It combines the ideas of wellness and the inner life, pointing to an inner state of comfort and gladness. In Philippians 2:19, Paul uses the word to express his desire to be filled with good cheer upon receiving a report about the spiritual health and well-being of the believers in Philippi.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word eupsycheō combines two common Greek roots: eu, meaning well or good, and psychē, referring to the soul, mind, or inner life. Taken together, the term describes a person who is well in soul, cheered in heart, or restored to good spirits.

In the Greek-speaking world of the first century, related forms of this word were often used in warm personal messages, grave inscriptions, and farewell letters to wish someone peace of mind, comfort, or good cheer. When early readers heard this word, they understood it as a heartfelt expression of inner gladness and relief from worry.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means to be of good cheer, to take courage, or to be heartened in spirit. It refers not merely to outward happiness or surface emotion, but to an inner state of gladness and reassurance in the soul.

When a person is the subject of this verb, it describes their transition from anxiety or concern into a condition of quiet confidence, joy, and peace of mind. Here in Paul's letter, it describes a personal sense of spiritual relief and gladness that comes from hearing good news about loved ones in Christ.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Philippians 2:19, the verb appears in the present tense, active voice, subjunctive mood, and singular number. The subjunctive mood expresses Paul's intention and hope ("that I also may be encouraged"), which rests upon his prospective plan to send Timothy to Philippi.

Although the grammatical form is active, the verb functions with an intransitive or reflexive sense, meaning to be glad or to become heartened within oneself. The choice of this specific word highlights the depth of Paul's emotional bond with the church. His personal cheer is directly tied to their spiritual state; he expects his own heart to be genuinely heartened when Timothy returns with news of their steadfastness.

4. Theological Implications

While eupsycheō carries a straightforward lexical meaning of being cheerful or heartened, its use by the apostle Paul highlights important spiritual truths about Christian community and pastoral care:

  • Mutual Bond in Christ: Believers are connected in such a way that the spiritual health of one directly affects the comfort and joy of another. Paul's own inner peace during imprisonment was tied to how well the Philippians were walking with God.

  • Joy Transcending Circumstances: Paul was writing from confinement, yet he looked forward to being filled with good cheer. Real encouragement in the Christian life does not depend on comfortable earthly circumstances, but on seeing the grace of God active in the lives of others.

  • Christ-Centered Unselfishness: Paul's desire for good cheer was not self-centered. He sought encouragement by focusing outward—on the spiritual welfare of the flock entrusted to his care.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2, Paul presents supreme examples of sacrificial service: the humility of Jesus Christ (vv. 5–11), the poured-out ministry of Paul himself (vv. 17–18), the faithful service of Timothy (vv. 19–24), and the selfless devotion of Epaphroditus (vv. 25–30).

Within this setting, verse 19 reveals the pastoral heart behind Paul's plan. He hopes in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy shortly, not merely as an administrative task, but so that Paul's own spirit might be refreshed when he learns how the Philippians are doing. He does not send Timothy only to minister to them; he also anticipates that Timothy's return report will bring great joy and encouragement to his own soul. This reveals the reciprocal warmth of genuine gospel partnership.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • This term is unique to this context and does not appear elsewhere in the biblical corpus.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding the components of eupsycheō adds warmth to Paul's statement:

  • The component eu stresses goodness, health, and well-being.

  • The component psychē locates this well-being in the seat of human consciousness, emotion, and life.

Thus, Paul is not talking about superficial entertainment or a temporary distraction from his imprisonment. He is speaking of deep, soul-level refreshment. Hearing of the Philippians' faith and unity would bring true, healthy gladness to his heart in the midst of his chains.
