JESUS - (Iēsoûs, Strong's G2424)

Summary:

The Greek name Iēsous is the transliteration of the Hebrew name Joshua or Jeshua, which means "Yahweh is salvation" or "the LORD saves." It is the personal name assigned to the incarnate Son of God at His birth. In Philippians 2:19, Paul uses the personal name "Jesus" together with the title "Lord" to express that his expectation and plans to send Timothy are grounded in the authority, guidance, and sovereign care of the living Savior.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Iēsous entered the Greek language as the translation of the Hebrew name Yĕhôšûaʿ or Yēšûaʿ (Joshua). In the Greek translation of the Old Testament, Iēsous was used for Joshua, the successor of Moses who led the Israelites into the Promised Land. By the first century, the name was common among Jewish families.

However, the name gained unique divine significance when the angel of the Lord instructed Joseph to name Mary's child "Jesus," explaining that He would save His people from their sins. Throughout the New Testament, the name designates the historical person who walked the earth, died on the cross, rose from the dead, and ascended to heaven. In the New Testament epistles, the writers frequently combine this personal name with titles such as "Lord" or "Christ" to declare both His true humanity and His exalted divine authority.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name means "Yahweh is salvation" or "the LORD saves." It functions primarily as a proper personal name identifying Jesus of Nazareth, the incarnate Son of God.

In its grammatical form in Philippians 2:19, the noun is dative singular masculine. Following the preposition "in," it identifies the person in whom Paul places his confidence and under whose sovereign oversight he orders his ministry decisions.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Gospel narratives, the simple name "Jesus" occurs frequently as the narrative designation for the Savior, emphasizing His earthly life, interactions, and historical presence among humanity.

In the Book of Acts and the Epistles, the name appears both individually and in paired titles such as "Lord Jesus" or "Jesus Christ." When paired with "Lord" (Kyrios), the focus combines His historical reality and human identity with His supreme divine authority over creation and the church.

When Paul writes of doing something "in the Lord Jesus," he indicates that his thoughts, plans, and actions take place within the sphere of Christ's authority and living presence.

4. Theological Implications

The personal name "Jesus" anchors the Christian faith in historical reality. Salvation is not based on abstract principles, but on a real person who entered human history, took on human flesh, lived a sinless life, died a substitutionary death, and rose bodily from the grave.

Joining the name "Jesus" with the title "Lord" affirms both the full deity and true humanity of Christ. The same Jesus who experienced earthly suffering and death is now exalted over all things. For ministry and Christian living, subjecting plans to "the Lord Jesus" acknowledges that every human intention remains subject to His divine providence and sovereign ruling power.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:19, Paul writes while under Roman imprisonment: "But I trust in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy to you shortly..." This verse follows Paul's description of Christ's self-emptying, earthly humiliation, and subsequent exaltation by God (Philippians 2:5–11).

In the immediate context, Paul presents Timothy and Epaphroditus as practical examples of selfless service that reflect the character of Christ. Although Paul is confined, he does not view his future or ministry logistics as governed by Roman guards or human circumstance. Instead, his trust to send Timothy rests "in the Lord Jesus."

The dative singular masculine form of the name works together with the preposition "in" to specify the personal object of Paul's trust. The personal name "Jesus" brings to mind the compassionate, historical Savior who cares for His church, while the title "Lord" highlights His absolute authority over Paul's travel plans and future circumstances.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The historical parallel between Joshua in the Old Testament and Jesus in the New Testament illustrates God's unfolding redemptive plan. Where Joshua led the people of Israel into an earthly inheritance, Jesus leads His people into eternal salvation and rest.

Paul's frequent use of the title "Lord Jesus" in his letters serves as a concise confession of faith. Confessing Jesus as Lord acknowledges both His historical identity as the crucified Savior and His present glory as the exalted Ruler of all creation.
