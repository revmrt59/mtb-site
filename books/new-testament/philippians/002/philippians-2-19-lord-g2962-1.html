LORD - (kýrios, Strong's G2962)

Summary:

The word kurios refers to a master, owner, ruler, or person possessing authority and supreme control. In ancient Greek, it described individuals who held authority over households, lands, or subjects. In the Greek Old Testament and throughout the New Testament, it is used for God the Father as sovereign Lord and for Jesus Christ as divine Ruler.

In Philippians 2:19, Paul writes of his hope to send Timothy "in the Lord Jesus." Here kurios identifies Jesus as the supreme Lord over Paul's plans, imprisonment, and ministry. Paul places his personal expectations and future administrative decisions under the direct authority and guidance of the risen Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek society, kurios was an everyday title for someone who held legitimate power, ownership, or authority. It described a master of a house, an owner of property, or a ruler with legal rights over subordinates.

When Jewish scholars translated the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, they frequently chose kurios to translate the personal name of God, Yahweh. As a result, kurios carried deep rich associations of God's supreme power, holiness, and rule over the universe.

By the time the New Testament was written, early Christians applied this same title to Jesus Christ. Calling Jesus kurios was a clear confession that Jesus shares the divine authority, honor, and sovereign rule of God. In Philippians 2:19, Paul writes while imprisoned in Rome, using this title to frame his reliance on Jesus for every practical decision.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to:

  • A master or head of a household who has authority over servant staff or affairs.

  • An owner of land, livestock, or property.

  • A human ruler, magistrate, or superior deserving respect.

  • God the Father as the supreme Lord and creator of all things.

  • Jesus Christ as the exalted Son of God who rules over His church and creation.

When applied to Jesus, the word indicates His divine standing and right to command obedience, loyalty, and faith.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses kurios in several distinct contexts:

In everyday social settings, the word was sometimes used as a respectful form of address, similar to "sir" or "master." People addressed Jesus this way during His earthly ministry before fully understanding His divine identity.

In legal or domestic settings, the word designated an owner or master who held rights over property or servants.

In theological settings, the word represents sovereign divine authority. When the New Testament authors call Jesus kurios, they join His human name ("Jesus") with His supreme title ("Lord"). This combination affirms that the historical Jesus of Nazareth is the sovereign Ruler of heaven and earth.

4. Theological Implications

The title kurios grounds the Christian confession that Jesus is supreme over every power. Earlier in this letter, Paul explains that God highly exalted Jesus and gave Him the name above every name, so that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord (Philippians 2:9–11).

Because Jesus is Lord, every area of a believer's life falls under His authority. Ministry plans, personal travels, suffering, and administrative decisions are not governed by human wisdom or secular authority alone. Recognizing Christ as Lord means acknowledging that He directs the circumstances of His people and the progress of the gospel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:19, the noun appears in a masculine singular dative form within the phrase "in the Lord Jesus." The dative form shows the sphere or personal relationship in which Paul places his hope.

Paul was a prisoner in Rome, facing physical confinement and unpredictable political powers. Humanly speaking, his ability to send Timothy to Philippi depended on Roman guards, trial schedules, and logistics. Yet Paul does not say he hopes in Roman permission or favorable circumstances. Instead, he places his hope "in the Lord Jesus."

By combining "Lord" with "Jesus," Paul shows that his confidence rests entirely in the exalted Ruler who oversees his life and ministry. Paul's expectation to send Timothy is not a casual human plan; it is an intention submitted to the sovereign direction of Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 20:28 — Thomas answered and said to Him, "My Lord and my God!"

  • Acts 21:13 — "For I am ready not only to be bound, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus."

  • Romans 14:6 — "He who observes the day, observes it to the Lord; and he who does not observe the day, to the Lord he does not observe it."

  • Ephesians 6:9 — "And you, masters, do the same things to them, giving up threatening, knowing that your own Master also is in heaven..."

  • Philemon 1:16 — "...no longer as a slave but more than a slave—a beloved brother, especially to me but how much more to you, both in the flesh and in the Lord."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Philippians 2, Paul provides two living examples of Christlike service: Timothy and Epaphroditus. Right after describing how Jesus humbled Himself and was exalted as Lord, Paul turns to these practical plans.

Using kurios in verse 19 connects high doctrine to daily leadership choices. For Paul, Jesus being Lord was not just a doctrine to confess in worship; it was the reality that governed how he planned travels, cared for churches, and deployed ministry partners like Timothy.
