SEND - (pémpō, Strong's G3992)

Summary:

The verb pempō means to dispatch, send, or direct someone or something from one place to another to accomplish a specific objective. It carries the idea of an authoritative sender commissioning a delegate, messenger, or gift. In Philippians 2:19, Paul uses the word to express his hope, under the sovereign direction of the Lord Jesus, to dispatch Timothy to the Philippian church. This mission was intended to bring encouraging news of their spiritual condition back to Paul during his imprisonment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient world, communication across long distances depended heavily on personal messengers and envoys. The verb pempō was commonly used in ordinary life, politics, and military affairs for dispatching a trusted representative, delivering official correspondence, or carrying out a specific assignment on behalf of a sender.

In the early church, distance and imprisonment posed significant challenges to pastoral care. Apostles and local churches could not simply send instant messages. Instead, maintaining spiritual fellowship, offering encouragement, and delivering apostolic instruction required physically sending faithful ministers. When an apostle dispatched a co-worker, that person traveled not merely on a personal errand, but as a recognized representative carrying pastoral authority, concern, and tidings of peace.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of pempō is to send, dispatch, or cause to go. The word can describe several types of sending depending on who initiates the action and what purpose is attached to it:

  • When a person sends a messenger or friend, it refers to dispatching someone to deliver news, provide assistance, or check on another's welfare.

  • When a church or authority sends delegates, it refers to commissioning trusted representatives to carry out a shared decision or mission.

  • When God is the sender, it refers to divine mission—such as God sending His Son into the world, sending the Holy Spirit to indwell believers, or sending messengers to accomplish His sovereign purpose.

In every case, the word highlights the relationship between the sender, the one being sent, and the goal of the journey.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, pempō plays a central role in describing the mission of God and the movement of gospel workers.

The word frequently appears in the Gospel of John to describe Jesus as the One sent by the Father. In those contexts, the term highlights Jesus' authority and obedience: He speaks the Father's words and accomplishes the Father's work because the Father sent Him. Similarly, Jesus promises to send the Holy Spirit to His disciples to comfort, teach, and empower them.

In the Book of Acts and the Epistles, the word describes the strategic movement of leaders and delegates within the early church. Paul frequently used the verb when describing his plans to dispatch leaders like Timothy, Tychicus, or Epaphroditus to care for local congregations. The one sent acted as a direct link between the apostle and the church, carrying mutual encouragement, instruction, and love across geographic boundaries.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of pempō reveals important truths about God and Christian ministry:

  • God is a Sending God: Redemption originates in the initiative of God. The Father sends the Son into the world to save sinners, and the Father and Son send the Holy Spirit to sustain the church. Mission is not a human invention; it flows directly from God's character and purpose.

  • Ministry Operates Through Delegated Care: Pastoral care in the New Testament is deeply relational and personal. Paul did not view church oversight as an abstract duty. He dispatched proven co-workers to shepherd, instruct, and report on the spiritual health of God's people.

  • Human Plans are Subject to Christ's Lordship: When Paul plans to send Timothy, he does not speak with self-reliant certainty. He grounds his intention "in the Lord Jesus." Even practical ministry logistics and travel plans belong under the authority and timing of Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:19, Paul writes: "But I trust in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy shortly to you, that I also may be encouraged when I know your state."

This verse marks a transition in the letter. After setting forth the supreme model of humility in Christ (Philippians 2:1-11) and calling believers to live out their salvation blamelessly (Philippians 2:12-18), Paul introduces two real-life examples of Christlike selflessness: Timothy (verses 19-24) and Epaphroditus (verses 25-30).

The grammatical form of the verb here functions as an active infinitive ("to send"). It expresses Paul's purposeful intention to dispatch Timothy. Paul presents this desire as a single, definite action he hopes to accomplish soon, provided the Lord permits it.

The context reveals Paul's deeply pastoral heart. Though Paul is imprisoned and facing uncertain outcomes, his concern remains focused on the Philippian believers. He wants to send Timothy not merely to give them information, but to receive news about their spiritual condition so that his own heart might be refreshed. Timothy is uniquely qualified for this assignment because, as Paul notes in the following verses, he genuinely cares for their welfare and seeks the things of Jesus Christ rather than his own interests. Dispatched by Paul, Timothy serves as a living illustration of the selfless, sacrificial ministry described throughout the chapter.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 15:26 — "But when the Helper comes, whom I shall send to you from the Father, the Spirit of truth who proceeds from the Father, He will testify of Me."

  • Acts 15:22 — "Then it pleased the apostles and elders, with the whole church, to send chosen men of their own company to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas..."

  • Romans 8:3 — "For what the law could not do in that it was weak through the flesh, God did by sending His own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, on account of sin: He condemned sin in the flesh..."

  • Ephesians 6:22 — "...whom I have sent to you for this very purpose, that you may know our affairs, and that he may comfort your hearts."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Structural Balance in Philippians 2: Notice how Paul pairs the sending of Timothy with the sending of Epaphroditus later in the chapter. Epaphroditus was the messenger sent by the Philippians to care for Paul; Timothy is the messenger Paul hopes to send back to care for the Philippians. This reciprocal sending demonstrates the deep unity, mutual care, and practical fellowship shared among early Christian communities.

  • Trusting Providence: Paul's phrase "I trust in the Lord Jesus" shows that apostolic decisions were made in conscious dependence on Christ. Paul longed to send Timothy "shortly," yet he submitted his scheduling to the wisdom of the Lord. True Christian planning holds personal desires in submission to God's sovereign hand.
