SHORTLY - (tachéōs, Strong's G5030)

Summary:

The Greek word tacheōs is an adverb that means "quickly," "soon," "speedily," or "without delay." It describes either the swift manner in which an action happens or the brief interval of time before it takes place. In Philippians 2:19, Paul uses the word to express his expectation to send Timothy to Philippi in the near future, subject to the Lord's will, so that he may receive encouraging news about the church's spiritual condition.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek, tacheōs served as a standard adverb to describe rapid movement, quick response, or an event occurring after a short time. When writers wanted to indicate that something would happen soon or that an action should be executed without delay, this word was a primary term.

In the New Testament, tacheōs appears in historical narratives and apostolic letters. In narratives, it often describes physical actions carried out promptly or suddenly. In the letters of Paul, it frequently indicates an intended timeframe for travel, ministerial visits, or an urgent response to a pastoral situation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word tacheōs functions as an adverb that modifies a verb by indicating speed or time. It carries two basic related senses:

First, it can refer to time, meaning "soon," "shortly," or "before long." In this sense, the emphasis is on a brief period elapsing before an expected event occurs.

Second, it can refer to manner, meaning "quickly," "hastily," or "without delay." In this sense, the emphasis is on the speed or suddenness with which an action is performed.

In Philippians 2:19, the word describes time. Paul is not stating that Timothy will travel at physical top speed, but rather that he hopes to dispatch Timothy to Philippi in the near future.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the New Testament, tacheōs adapts to the context in which it is used:

  • In narrative settings, it indicates immediate physical movement, such as going out into the streets promptly or leaving a house quickly.

  • In pastoral and congregational settings, Paul uses it when describing travel plans or urgent church matters. He uses it to express his hope of visiting congregations soon.

  • In warning contexts, it describes how rapidly believers can stray into spiritual error or become unsettled by false teaching.

  • In leadership instructions, it warns against acting hastily when appointing individuals to ministry leadership.

4. Theological Implications

While tacheōs is an ordinary adverb of time or manner, its placement alongside Paul's spiritual expressions reveals how biblical writers aligned practical plans with divine providence. Paul writes, "I trust in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy shortly."

Paul does not treat "shortly" as an absolute human guarantee. The fulfillment of his desire to send Timothy soon remains subject to the authority and timing of Jesus Christ. Human plans are held under God's sovereign supervision. Furthermore, Paul's desire to act quickly stems from genuine pastoral concern; he seeks timely news regarding the spiritual health of the Philippian believers.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:19, Paul writes while under house arrest in Rome. Despite his own uncertain circumstances, his concern remains focused on the welfare of the church at Philippi.

The adverb "shortly" highlights Paul's eagerness to maintain personal care for the church. Because Paul cannot travel immediately himself, he plans to send Timothy as his personal representative. Timothy shared Paul's genuine concern for their condition. By expressing his hope to send Timothy "shortly," Paul reassures the Philippians that their spiritual growth and well-being remain a high priority for him.

Paul carefully balances his language. He couples his desire for speed ("shortly") with his submission to Christ ("I trust in the Lord Jesus"). Paul's sense of urgency is real, yet it is governed by submission to the Lord's direction.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Grammatically, tacheōs functions strictly as an adverb modifying the verb "to send" in Philippians 2:19. Recognizing it as a simple adverb of time helps prevent over-interpreting the timeframe. The word communicates Paul's immediate intention and warm affection, while recognizing that prison constraints and divine timing determine the exact outcome.
