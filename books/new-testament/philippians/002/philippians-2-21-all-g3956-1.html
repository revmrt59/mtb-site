ALL - (pâs, Strong's G3956)

Summary:

The Greek word pas basic meaning is "all," "every," or "the whole." When used as a masculine plural noun, it means "everyone" or "all people."

In Philippians 2:21, Paul uses "all" to describe the general group of people around him who prioritize their own personal interests over the ministry of Jesus Christ. By contrasting this widespread self-interest with Timothy's faithful concern, Paul emphasizes how rare and valuable selfless devotion is in the service of the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word pas is one of the most common terms in the Greek language for describing totality, completeness, or an entire group. Depending on how it is used in a sentence, it can refer to every individual item in a list without exception, or it can describe a whole group collectively within a specific setting. In everyday conversation, ancient speakers used the word both in an absolute sense ("everyone on earth") and in a limited, contextual sense ("everyone in the room").

In the letter to the Philippians, Paul writes from imprisonment to update the church on his circumstances and plans. He intends to send Timothy to Philippi to encourage them, praising Timothy as a brother who shares his heart and sincerely cares for their spiritual health. In Philippians 2:21, Paul contrasts Timothy's exceptional character with the general disposition of others available to him at that time.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word pas refers to the whole of something or every member of a set. Depending on its context and grammatical function, the word can mean:

  • "Every" when attached directly to a singular noun.

  • "All" or "the whole" when describing an entire entity or collective group.

  • "Everyone" or "all people" when functioning on its own as a plural noun.

In Philippians 2:21, the authoritative grammatical form is masculine, plural, and nominative. It functions as the subject of the sentence, translated as "all" or "everyone."

The word itself simply indicates totality or the entire group in view. It does not possess an inherently negative or positive moral meaning. Any sense of self-interest or neglect in Philippians 2:21 comes from what Paul says about the group in the rest of the sentence, not from the dictionary definition of pas.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout Scripture, pas is used across several distinct settings:

  • Absolute totality: Referring to realities that apply to every human being or all creation, such as God's knowledge of all things or the offer of salvation to all people.

  • Contextual totality: Describing all individuals within a specific place, group, or situation, rather than every single person on earth.

  • Inclusive unity: Describing all believers gathered together as one spiritual body.

When writers use pas in a generalized statement, the surrounding passage clarifies whether the totality is absolute or relative to a specific circumstance. In Philippians 2:21, Paul uses the word in a contextual sense to describe the general pool of companions and potential helpers around him at that moment.

4. Theological Implications

While pas itself is simply the standard word for "all," its use in Philippians 2:21 highlights important biblical truths regarding human nature and Christian service:

  • The inclination toward self-interest: Human nature naturally turns inward, seeking personal comfort, safety, and recognition. Paul's statement reflects how easily self-centeredness can dominate human motivation, even among those close to ministry.

  • The contrast with the mind of Christ: Earlier in Philippians 2, Paul points to Jesus, who emptied Himself and took the form of a servant. The self-giving mindset of Christ stands in sharp contrast to the prevailing human tendency to pursue one's own agenda.

  • The value of proven devotion: By pointing out that "all seek their own," Paul underscores how rare Timothy's selfless character was. True gospel devotion is a work of divine grace that enables a believer to put Christ's interests and the needs of others ahead of personal preference.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:21, Paul writes: "For all seek their own, not the things which are of Jesus Christ."

The word "all" functions as the subject of the sentence in the nominative plural, identifying the group Paul was observing or evaluating. Paul is not making a sweeping claim that literally every human being without exception was failing, since Paul himself and Timothy were clear exceptions. Rather, "all" describes the prevailing norm among the available personnel around Paul—the broad majority of people was focused on personal affairs rather than Christ's work.

This generalized statement creates a striking contrast with verse 20, where Paul writes of Timothy: "I have no one like-minded, who will sincerely care for your state." The word "all" heightens the distinction between ordinary self-concern and Timothy's selfless commitment. It demonstrates to the Philippians why Timothy was uniquely suited for this mission and why faithful servants of Christ deserve special appreciation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 13:17 — "And when He said these things, all His adversaries were put to shame..."

In this passage, "all" describes the entire group of opponents present who were silenced by Jesus' teaching.

  • John 17:21 — "...that they all may be one, as You, Father, are in Me, and I in You..."

In His high priestly prayer, Jesus prays for the unity of "all" who believe in Him across every generation.

  • 1 Corinthians 12:29 — "Are all apostles? Are all prophets? Are all teachers? Are all workers of miracles?"

Paul uses "all" in a series of questions to show that not every believer receives the exact same role in the church.

  • Colossians 1:4 — "...since we heard of your faith in Christ Jesus and of your love for all the saints..."

Paul commends the Colossian church for showing genuine Christian love toward "all" fellow believers without partiality.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Distinguishing word definition from contextual description: It is essential not to import ideas of selfishness or sin into the definition of pas. The word pas simply means "all." The negative action in Philippians 2:21 comes from the phrase "seek their own," which describes what the group was doing, not what the word "all" means.

  • Realistic expectations in ministry: Paul's honest assessment provides practical wisdom for the church. Even during the apostolic era, dedicated workers who put Jesus Christ first were rare. Understanding this reality helps believers value and support faithful leaders while remaining vigilant against personal self-interest.
