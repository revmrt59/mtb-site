JESUS - (Iēsoûs, Strong's G2424)

Summary:

The proper name Iēsous is the Greek form of the Hebrew name Yēšûaʿ or Yĕhōšûaʿ (Joshua), meaning "the LORD is salvation" or "the LORD saves." In the New Testament, it identifies the historical person of Nazareth, who is God incarnate and the promised Messiah. In Philippians 2:21, the noun appears in the genitive masculine singular form, indicating possession and belonging. Paul uses the name to contrast those who pursue their own self-centered interests with faithful servants like Timothy, who seek the things that belong to Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Iēsous is the Greek adaptation of the Old Testament Hebrew name Yēšûaʿ (or Yĕhōšûaʿ), translated into English as Joshua or Jeshua. The name combines the divine covenant name of God with a root meaning "to save" or "salvation." Its basic meaning is "the LORD is salvation."

While Iēsous was a common personal name among Jewish families in the ancient Greco-Roman world, it took on unique redemptive weight when assigned by divine instruction prior to the birth of Christ. The angel instructed Joseph to name Mary's child Jesus because He would save His people from their sins. Throughout the New Testament, the name designates the real, earthly person who was born in Bethlehem, raised in Nazareth, crucified under Pontius Pilate, and raised from the dead.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word functions as a proper noun referring specifically to Jesus of Nazareth, the Son of God. It identifies the individual human identity of the Savior.

In biblical usage, the name often stands alone or combines with functional titles:

  • As a personal name identifying Jesus in His earthly life and historical ministry.

  • In combination with Christos ("Christ" or "Anointed One"), identifying Him as the promised messianic King.

  • In combination with Kyrios ("Lord"), emphasizing His divine sovereignty and exalted authority.

In Philippians 2:21, the noun is masculine singular and stands in the genitive case. The genitive case indicates ownership or association, rendered in English as "the things of Jesus Christ" or "the things which belong to Jesus Christ."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, the name Iēsous appears in a wide range of contexts. In the Gospels, it primarily highlights His earthly life, His personal interactions, His teaching, and His obedience to the Father's will up to the cross.

In the Book of Acts and the Epistles, the Apostles frequently pair Iēsous with titles like "Lord" and "Christ." This pairing connects the historical person who walked the earth with the exalted Lord now seated at God's right hand.

When Paul uses phrases like "of Jesus Christ," he often points to ownership, divine authority, and gospel mission. The believers, the ministry, and the work of the church do not belong to human leaders; they belong directly to Jesus.

4. Theological Implications

The name Jesus anchors Christian faith in historical reality. Salvation is not based on abstract philosophy or general moral principles, but on the specific historical person and work of Jesus Christ. His name serves as a constant reminder that God entered human history to accomplish salvation.

The name also reveals God's character as Savior. By giving His Son a name that means "the LORD is salvation," God declared that deliverance from sin comes entirely through His divine initiative and grace.

Furthermore, attributing ownership to Jesus—referring to "the things of Jesus Christ"—highlights His lordship over the church. Ministry priorities, pastoral care, and gospel work are not matters of personal ambition or human opinion. They belong to Jesus, who bought the church with His own blood and directs its mission.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:19–24, Paul writes about his hope to send Timothy to the church at Philippi. Paul praises Timothy's character, noting that Timothy shared Paul's genuine concern for the believers' spiritual welfare.

In verse 21, Paul offers a striking contrast: "For all seek their own, not the things which are of Jesus Christ." Paul laments that many workers focus on their own comfort, reputation, or personal goals rather than advancing the cause of the Lord.

The genitive form of Iēsous highlights ownership. The "things" in view are the interests, gospel work, and spiritual well-being of Christ's people. Self-seeking workers treat ministry as a means to promote themselves, whereas faithful workers like Timothy focus entirely on what belongs to Jesus and advances His kingdom.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The mention of Jesus in Philippians 2:21 connects directly with the famous passage earlier in the chapter. In Philippians 2:9–10, Paul states that God highly exalted Jesus and gave Him the name above every name, "that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow."

The one whose name receives supreme honor from God is the same one whose interests ought to govern the lives of His servants. Timothy's willingness to put Christ's interests ahead of his own reflects the selfless mindset of Jesus Himself, who emptied Himself and took the form of a servant (Philippians 2:7).
