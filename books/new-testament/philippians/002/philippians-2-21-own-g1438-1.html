OWN - (heautoû, Strong's G1438)

Summary:

The Greek word heautou is a reflexive pronoun used when an action or concern directs back to the subject of the sentence. Depending on the context, it translates into English as "himself," "themselves," "yourselves," "ourselves," or "their own." In Philippians 2:21, Paul uses the word to describe leaders who prioritize their own private interests and personal comfort over the cause of Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Paul wrote his letter to the Philippians from prison, addressing a church he loved deeply. Throughout the epistle, Paul places strong emphasis on gospel unity, humble service, and single-minded focus on Christ. In chapter 2, Paul presents the supreme model of selflessness in Jesus Christ, who humbled Himself for the redemption of sinners.

Immediately following this doctrinal peak, Paul discusses practical leadership within the church. He plans to send Timothy and Epaphroditus to Philippi as living examples of Christlike devotion. When commending Timothy, Paul laments that few others share Timothy's genuine concern for the believers. In this narrative setting, the reflexive pronoun heautou marks the sharp contrast between those who pursue their own private interests and those who selflessly serve the body of Christ.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word heautou is the standard reflexive pronoun in New Testament Greek. It returns the action, focus, or ownership back to the subject of the clause.

Depending on the context and grammatical structure, the word refers to:

  • oneself, himself, or herself

  • themselves, yourselves, or ourselves

  • one's own property, concerns, or private agenda

In Philippians 2:21, the word appears in a genitive, plural, masculine form. Combined with the verb for seeking, it refers to individuals pursuing their personal affairs, comfort, and private advantage rather than the interests of Jesus Christ.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, heautou performs several basic structural and thematic roles.

First, it describes simple reflexive action, where a subject acts directly upon or speaks regarding himself. For example, a person might testify about himself or seek his own honor.

Second, it marks personal possession or private ownership, pointing to what belongs to a specific person or group, such as returning to one's own home or eating one's own food.

Third, in moral and spiritual contexts, the word emphasizes the tension between self-interest and Kingdom loyalty. Scripture uses this pronoun both to expose human self-centeredness and to highlight Christlike sacrifice. Notably in Philippians 2, the word appears in two opposing settings: Christ emptied "Himself" for the sake of others (Philippians 2:7), whereas self-seeking workers focus exclusively on "their own" affairs (Philippians 2:21).

4. Theological Implications

The use of heautou in Philippians 2:21 highlights the pervasive human inclination toward self-preservation and personal comfort. Fallen human nature naturally turns inward, organizing life around personal advantage, safety, and recognition. Even within Christian ministry, individuals can easily drift into serving personal interests while maintaining outward religious activity.

The gospel reorients the human heart away from self-centeredness and toward God and others. Discipleship requires denying oneself, taking up the cross, and following Christ. Timothy stands as an example of this work of grace. Rather than seeking his own security, Timothy adopted Paul's pastoral concern for the church. The contrast in verse 21 shows that while self-seeking is the natural tendency of fallen humanity, self-giving service is the fruit of spiritual transformation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:19–24, Paul explains his plan to send Timothy to Philippi. Paul praises Timothy as a rare and trustworthy co-worker who shares his heart: "I have no one like-minded, who will sincerely care for your state" (Philippians 2:20).

Paul then explains why Timothy is so unique in verse 21: "For all seek their own, not the things which are of Jesus Christ." The word "own" exposes an inward-focused agenda. Those Paul contrasts with Timothy were consumed with personal convenience, protection, or status rather than advancing the gospel message.

This statement connects directly to Paul's earlier exhortation in the chapter: "Let each of you look out not only for his own interests, but also for the interests of others" (Philippians 2:4). Christ embodied this principle perfectly when He "made Himself of no reputation" (Philippians 2:7). Timothy lived out this Christlike mind, whereas others fell short by prioritizing "their own" concerns above Christ's Kingdom.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 7:18 — "He who speaks from himself seeks his own glory; but He who seeks the glory of the One who sent Him is true, and no unrighteousness is in Him."

  • John 20:10 — "Then the disciples went away again to their own homes."

  • Philippians 2:7 — "but made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant, and coming in the likeness of men."

  • 2 Thessalonians 3:12 — "Now those who are such we command and exhort through our Lord Jesus Christ that they work in quietness and eat their own bread."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s contrast between seeking one’s "own" things and seeking "the things which are of Jesus Christ" offers a practical diagnostic for ministry motives. Paul is not claiming that every other Christian in Rome had abandoned the faith. Rather, he is noting how rare it was to find leaders whose personal desires were completely aligned with Christ's mission.

Timothy stood out because he did not treat ministry as a means of self-advancement. Where others calculated the personal cost, Timothy served with sacrificial commitment. This passage challenges leaders and churches to evaluate their underlying motives, ensuring that ministry labor is driven by genuine love for Christ and His people rather than personal ambition or comfort.
