SEEK - (zētéō, Strong's G2212)

Summary:

directional target: The Greek verb zēteō means to search for, look for, strive after, or aim to obtain something. In Philippians 2:21, Paul uses the word to describe a widespread tendency among people to actively pursue their own personal interests rather than the priorities and glory of Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek usage, zēteō described ordinary daily searching, such as looking for a lost item, searching for a person, or investigating a matter to gain knowledge. It expressed conscious effort and focused attention directed toward an outcome. In biblical text, the word spans from simple physical searches to the deep motives and goals that drive human behavior.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to looking for something that is out of sight, trying to find a specific person, or inquiring about a question. When applied to goals and human desires, it means to strive after, pursue, or direct effort toward gaining something. In Philippians 2:21, the verb describes the persistent effort and priority people place on their daily pursuits.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, zēteō carries different nuances depending on the context and the object being pursued:

  • Physical search: Looking for a missing person, as when Mary and Joseph searched for Jesus in Jerusalem (Luke 2:49).

  • Intentional effort: Trying to accomplish a specific goal, such as leaders seeking to arrest Jesus (Luke 19:47) or crowd members seeking to find Him (Mark 3:32).

  • Spiritual focus: Directing life priorities toward God's kingdom and spiritual realities rather than physical anxiety (Luke 12:29).

  • Self-centered ambition: Aiming for personal honor, safety, or convenience rather than God's will.

4. Theological Implications

What a person seeks reveals what that person values most. Human hearts are never idle; everyone is actively pursuing something—whether personal comfort, human approval, material security, or God's kingdom. Scripture makes clear that pursuing self-interest as one's highest priority competes directly with faithful discipleship. Discipleship calls believers to align their desires with Christ's self-giving love rather than their own self-preservation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2, Paul commends Timothy as a faithful servant who genuinely cares for the spiritual well-being of the Philippians. In stark contrast to Timothy's selfless devotion, Paul laments that "all seek their own, not the things which are of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 2:21). The present active indicative form presents this seeking as an ongoing, active pattern of behavior. Paul is not asserting that every believer has entirely abandoned the faith, but he highlights how rare it is to find someone whose daily priorities are fully surrendered to Christ. While human nature defaults toward seeking personal ease and advantage, genuine gospel service requires setting aside self-seeking ambitions to serve others.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 11:9 — "So I say to you, ask, and it will be given to you; seek, and you will find; knock, and it will be opened to you."

  • Luke 12:29 — "And do not seek what you should eat or what you should drink, nor have an anxious mind."

  • John 5:30 — "I can of Myself do nothing. As I hear, I judge; and My judgment is righteous, because I do not seek My own will but the will of the Father who sent Me."

  • Romans 10:3 — "For they being ignorant of God’s righteousness, and seeking to establish their own righteousness, have not submitted to the righteousness of God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Philippians 2:5–11, Paul presents the ultimate example of selflessness: Jesus Christ, who humbled Himself and took the form of a servant. Paul's observation in verse 21 that people "seek their own" stands right alongside this portrait of Christ. The contrast highlights the spiritual standard set for the church. Timothy stands out precisely because his life reflects Christ's self-giving pattern rather than the world's self-seeking norm.
