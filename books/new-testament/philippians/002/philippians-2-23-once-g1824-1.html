ONCE - (exautēs, Strong's G1824)

Summary:

The word exautēs is an adverb that means "immediately," "at once," or "without delay." It describes an action taking place right away, leaving no room for hesitation or unnecessary waiting.

In Philippians 2:23, Paul uses this word to express his eager intent to send Timothy to the church at Philippi as soon as the outcome of his imprisonment becomes clear. By indicating that he will send Timothy at once, Paul shows his deep pastoral care and desire to relieve the Philippians' anxiety about his condition without delay.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Roman world, news and personal messages traveled slowly across distances, relying entirely on physical letters carried by trusted messengers. When churches faced trials or worried about their leaders, waiting for news created significant emotional strain.

Paul wrote his letter to the Philippian church while imprisoned, likely in Rome. The believers in Philippi had sent support through Epaphroditus, but they were deeply concerned for Paul's life and legal standing. To address their worries, Paul planned to send Timothy, his faithful companion. The adverb exautēs highlights Paul's intention to dispatch Timothy without any delay the moment his legal situation is resolved.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word exautēs functions as an adverb of time. It comes from a combination of the preposition meaning "from" and a demonstrative pronoun, literally pointing to "from this very moment."

In general usage, the word can mean:

  • At once

  • Immediately

  • Forthwith

  • Without delay

In Philippians 2:23, the word describes an action that will happen immediately after a specific condition is met. Paul does not mean he is sending Timothy that very second, but that the moment he learns the outcome of his trial, he will send Timothy right away.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, this adverb marks swift action driven by urgency, duty, or strong desire.

It appears in contexts where delay is unacceptable:

  • In sudden requests where someone demands immediate action.

  • In prompt responses to divine instruction, such as sending for God's messenger.

  • In swift official actions taken by military authorities to stop violence or protect a prisoner.

In all these cases, the word highlights an immediate transition from decision to action. It shows that once the way is clear, there is no hesitation.

4. Theological Implications

While exautēs is a practical temporal word, its use in Paul's letter reveals important spiritual principles about Christian care and leadership:

  • Pastoral Love Acts Promptly: Paul did not treat the Philippians' emotional peace as an afterthought. He planned to send Timothy at once because he cared about their comfort.

  • Stewardship of Time: When God opens a door or clarifies a situation, believers are called to act decisively rather than procrastinate.

  • Unselfish Focus: Even while in prison, Paul's attention was fixed on comforting others. His urgency was not for his own benefit, but for the spiritual encouragement of the church.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2, Paul presents Christ as the ultimate model of humble selflessness (verses 5–11). He then points to Timothy and Epaphroditus as practical examples of that same Christlike spirit (verses 19–30).

When Paul writes that he hopes to send Timothy "at once, as soon as I see how it goes with me," he connects his plans with his deep affection for the church. Paul wants the Philippians to know that he will not keep them in suspense. The moment his court outcome is known, Timothy will be on his way. This shows that Paul's future plans were organized entirely around serving the church and reassuring them of God's faithful work.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The same word appears in several other passages, illustrating its sense of urgency and immediate action:

  • Mark 6:25 – Salome demands that the king give her "at once" the head of John the Baptist, showing her urgent demand for immediate evil action.

  • Acts 10:33 – Cornelius tells Peter that he sent for him "immediately," reflecting his eagerness to hear God's message without delay.

  • Acts 21:32 – The Roman commander took soldiers and ran down "immediately" to stop a riot, showing prompt military action in a crisis.

  • Acts 23:30 – The commander sent Paul under guard to the governor "immediately" after discovering a plot against Paul's life.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Grammatically, exautēs modifies the verb "send," shaping how Paul's promise should be understood. It does not describe an idle hope, but a firm commitment to act quickly.

This passage demonstrates how sovereign trust and energetic planning work together in ministry. Paul trusted God completely with the outcome of his trial, yet he also made careful, prompt administrative plans to care for the believers. Faith in God's providence does not produce passivity; it moves leaders to act decisively for the good of God's people.
