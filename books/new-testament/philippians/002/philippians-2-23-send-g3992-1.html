SEND - (pémpō, Strong's G3992)

Summary:

The Greek verb pempo means to send, dispatch, or transmit someone or something from one location to another, usually with a defined mission or task. In Philippians 2:23, Paul uses this word to express his deliberate intention to dispatch Timothy as his personal representative to the church at Philippi as soon as the final decision in his legal trial becomes clear.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Graeco-Roman world, distance and communication posed significant practical challenges. Without modern technology, messages, news, financial support, and pastoral guidance had to be carried in person by reliable couriers. The verb pempo was the standard everyday word for dispatching a messenger, sending an envoy, transmitting a gift, or issuing an order for someone to travel.

When Paul wrote his letter to the Philippian church, he was under house arrest awaiting a decision on his legal case before Roman authorities. His physical confinement prevented him from visiting the churches he loved. To bridge this distance, Paul relied on trusted co-laborers who could travel on his behalf. Sending a representative was not merely a matter of delivering a written document; the messenger carried the author's personal voice, updates, pastoral care, and spiritual authority to the receiving congregation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word pempo refers broadly to the action of sending or dispatching. It describes moving an agent or object from one sender to a specific destination.

Depending on the context, the word can mean:

  • To dispatch a person as a messenger, representative, or delegate.

  • To transmit an object, such as a letter, financial gift, or relief effort.

  • To send out an agent with a specific task, authority, or purpose.

When God is the subject, the word describes divine initiative—such as God sending His Son into the world or sending the Holy Spirit to guide His people. When human leaders are the subject, it describes practical delegation and mission work within the church.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, pempo appears in a wide variety of settings. In the Gospels, particularly in the Gospel of John, it serves as a central word for Jesus' mission, frequently describing God the Father as "the One who sent Me." It also describes Jesus sending the Holy Spirit to believers after His resurrection.

In the book of Acts and the Epistles, pempo frequently describes church leadership and apostolic ministry. Leaders send trusted companions to check on local assemblies, deliver letters, carry financial aid, or resolve disputes.

In Philippians 2:23, the grammatical form is an active infinitive ("to send"), expressing Paul's purposeful intent. It captures a specific act of sending that Paul hopes to execute in the near future. Paul does not use the term loosely; sending Timothy is a considered, intentional choice aimed at serving the Philippian believers.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical pattern of sending highlights the inherently outbound nature of gospel ministry. God Himself is a sending God who dispatches His Son and His Spirit for the salvation and comfort of humanity. In turn, those who follow Christ participate in this sending dynamic by dispatching leaders and ministers to build up the body of believers.

Sending a representative requires deep Christian trust and character. Paul could not simply send anyone; he selected Timothy because Timothy shared his pastoral heart and held genuine concern for the Philippians' spiritual health. Biblical sending is grounded in shared truth, proven character, and sacrificial love.

Additionally, Paul's plan to send Timothy reflects a high view of divine providence. Paul formed clear pastoral plans, yet he submitted those plans to the actual outcome of his trial. Christian delegation balances faithful forward planning with humble submission to God's unfolding timing.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:19–30, Paul addresses his immediate and future communication plans with the Philippian church. He balances two distinct sendings:

  • He is sending Epaphroditus immediately (v. 25) because Epaphroditus had been gravely ill, and news of his illness had created anxiety among the Philippians.

  • He hopes to send Timothy shortly thereafter (v. 23), as soon as he sees how his own legal situation turns out.

Paul's use of pempo in verse 23 demonstrates extraordinary unselfishness. Even while facing trial and potential execution in prison, Paul's primary concern was not keeping his most valuable companions nearby for his own personal comfort. Instead, he was eager to send Timothy away to strengthen and encourage the Philippians. Because Timothy was like a spiritual son to Paul, sending Timothy was equivalent to sending Paul's own heart.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 15:26 – "But when the Helper comes, whom I shall send to you from the Father, the Spirit of truth who proceeds from the Father, He will testify of Me."

  • Acts 15:22 – "Then it pleased the apostles and elders, with the whole church, to send chosen men of their own company to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas..."

  • Romans 8:3 – "For what the law could not do in that it was weak through the flesh, God did by sending His own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, on account of sin..."

  • Ephesians 6:22 – "...whom I have sent to you for this very purpose, that you may know our affairs, and that he may comfort your hearts."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A meaningful connection appears when comparing Philippians 2:23 with Philippians 4:16. In Philippians 4:16, the same verb pempo describes how the Philippian church repeatedly "sent aid" to support Paul in his physical necessities.

This creates a touching administrative and spiritual exchange in the letter. The church at Philippi sent material support across the sea to sustain Paul in his imprisonment, and Paul, in turn, planned to send his dearest companions—Epaphroditus and Timothy—to supply the Philippians with spiritual encouragement and pastoral care.
