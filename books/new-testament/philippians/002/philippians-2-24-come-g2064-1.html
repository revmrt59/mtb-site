COME - (érchomai, Strong's G2064)

Summary:

The Greek verb erchomai refers broadly to movement from one place to another, commonly translated as "to come" or "to go." In Philippians 2:24, Paul uses the future form of this verb to convey his personal expectation of traveling to visit the believers in Philippi. Coupled with his trust in the Lord, the word expresses Paul's hope of arriving in person after sending Timothy, showing his pastoral care for the church while placing all his future plans under Christ's sovereign authority.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek, erchomai served as the standard, everyday verb to describe physical travel, movement, departure, or arrival. It was a versatile word used across all levels of speech and writing whenever someone moved from one point to another or when an event was approaching.

When Paul wrote his letter to the Philippians, he was under Roman imprisonment. Despite his physical confinement, he maintained active leadership and fellowship with the churches he had planted. In Philippians 2, Paul outlines his immediate plans for ministry care: sending Epaphroditus back to them and sending Timothy shortly. Alongside these arrangements, Paul uses erchomai to express his hope and confidence that he himself will soon be released and travel to Philippi.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The fundamental meaning of erchomai is to come or to go. Depending on the direction of movement and the surrounding sentence, it can refer to arriving at a destination, departing from a location, or approaching a person.

When applied to individuals, the word indicates literal spatial movement. When applied to time or events, it refers to an upcoming arrival or approaching period. In Philippians 2:24, the verb points specifically to Paul's physical journey to Philippi and his expected arrival among the believers there.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, erchomai appears in a wide range of contexts. It describes Jesus traveling from town to town, people coming to Jesus for healing or teaching, the arrival of promised spiritual realities, and the future return of Christ.

In Philippians 2:24, the verb is in the future tense, middle voice, indicative mood, and singular number. The future tense expresses an action that is anticipated in the time ahead. The indicative mood frames the trip as a real expectation rather than a mere wish, while the middle voice describes the subject undertaking the movement. Paul uses this form to state his expectation that he will personally make the journey to see them.

4. Theological Implications

Although erchomai is a standard word for travel, Paul's use of it in this verse reflects how believers ought to view future planning. Paul does not present his travel as an absolute promise guaranteed by his own willpower. Instead, he frames his future arrival with the words, "I trust in the Lord."

This highlights a key biblical principle regarding human scheduling and ministry plans. A Christian's desire to visit, serve, or labor in a particular place must always be submitted to God's providence. Paul deeply desires to go to Philippi to encourage the church, yet he recognizes that his release from prison and safe travel depend entirely on the Lord's timing and decision.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:19–30, Paul presents a three-part plan for the church's pastoral care. First, he plans to send Timothy to learn how they are doing (Philippians 2:19). Second, he sends Epaphroditus immediately to relieve their anxiety over his health (Philippians 2:25). Third, he expresses his trust that he himself will "come" shortly (Philippians 2:24).

The use of "come" demonstrates Paul's deep personal affection for the congregation. He is not satisfied merely sending letters or trusted co-workers; he wants to be with them in person. By stating his confidence that he will come soon, Paul bridges the gap between his present imprisonment and his hoped-for release. His aim in coming is not personal comfort, but the mutual joy and spiritual strengthening of the Philippian believers.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's expectation to come to Philippi connects directly to his earlier reflection in chapter 1. There, while contemplating the possibility of martyrdom, Paul expressed his conviction that remaining in the flesh was more necessary for their progress and joy in the faith (Philippians 1:24–25). In chapter 2, that internal conviction turns into practical expectation: Paul trusts that the Lord will allow him to visit them again in person.
