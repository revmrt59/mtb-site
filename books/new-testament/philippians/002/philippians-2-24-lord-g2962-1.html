LORD - (kýrios, Strong's G2962)

Summary:

The word refers to one who possesses authority, ownership, or sovereign rule over others. In ancient Greek settings, it described a master, ruler, or owner of property. In the New Testament, it is used for human masters, God the Father, and Jesus Christ as the supreme Savior and Lord. In Philippians 2:24, Paul uses the phrase "in the Lord" to show that his hope and confidence regarding his future travel plans are submitted entirely to the authority and sovereign direction of Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The term kyrios carries the fundamental idea of authority, power, and legal ownership. In ancient Greek society, a kyrios was a head of a household, a master of servants, or a magistrate with authority to rule within a specific domain.

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament, kyrios was used to translate both the Hebrew word Adonai ("Lord") and the divine personal name of God (YHWH). Because of this usage, the word became strongly connected with divine sovereignty, holy majesty, and absolute authority. In the New Testament, early Christians applied this title directly to Jesus Christ, affirming that He shares divine authority with the Father and possesses supreme lordship over creation and the church.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word refers to a ruler, master, or person holding authority over others. Its basic meanings include:

  • A master or owner of property and servants in human relationships.

  • A title of respectful address ("sir" or "lord").

  • The sovereign ruler of all things, referring to God or Jesus Christ.

In Philippians 2:24, the grammatical form is dative singular masculine, appearing in the phrase "in the Lord." Here, it identifies Jesus Christ as the authority within whose sphere Paul lives, plans, and ministers.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The New Testament uses kyrios across various contexts:

  • Human social settings, referring to household masters or property owners.

  • Old Testament quotations, where it stands for God the Lord.

  • Descriptions of Jesus Christ, emphasizing His divine deity, His resurrection victory, and His sovereign right to command His disciples.

Paul frequently writes the phrase "in the Lord" (en kyriō). For Paul, this expression describes a living relationship with Jesus Christ. Believers love, serve, make plans, and labor "in the Lord," meaning their entire existence is sustained by Christ and governed by His authority.

4. Theological Implications

Confessing Jesus as kyrios is a cornerstone of Christian faith. Only a few verses earlier in Philippians 2:9–11, Paul declares that God highly exalted Jesus and gave Him the name above every name, so that every tongue will confess that Jesus Christ is Lord.

In verse 24, Paul demonstrates what this doctrine looks like in practice. Calling Jesus "Lord" means recognizing His authority over daily life, including personal hopes and future schedules. Paul does not treat his travel plans as matter of pure self-determination or human chance; his confidence rests in the living Christ who rules over history, circumstances, and time.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:19–30, Paul discusses his practical plans to send Timothy and Epaphroditus to Philippi, while also sharing his personal desire to visit them soon. In verse 24, he states that he trusts "in the Lord" that he himself will come shortly.

This statement comes right after Paul's great description of Christ's self-humiliation and eventual exaltation to universal lordship (Philippians 2:5–11). Having stated that every knee will bow to Jesus as Lord, Paul immediately reflects that reality in his own leadership. He longs to see the Philippian believers, but he places his prospective visit under Christ's sovereign choice. His confidence is not based on his own freedom or physical ability, but on the Lord who directs his steps.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 20:28 — "And Thomas answered and said to Him, 'My Lord and my God!'"

  • Acts 21:13 — "Then Paul answered, 'What do you mean by weeping and breaking my heart? For I am ready not only to be bound, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus.'"

  • Romans 14:6 — "He who observes the day, observes it to the Lord; and he who does not observe the day, to the Lord he does not observe it."

  • Ephesians 6:9 — "And you, masters, do the same things to them, giving up threatening, knowing that your own Master also is in heaven, and there is no partiality with Him."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The dative form used in Philippians 2:24 functions with the preposition "in" to show sphere and location. Paul does not merely hope to travel under divine permission in a general sense; he lives his life inside the ruling lordship of Christ.

This reveals how practically early believers held the lordship of Jesus. Lordship was not simply a statement made in formal worship; it was the framework through which Paul made decisions, bore imprisonment, and anticipated the future.
