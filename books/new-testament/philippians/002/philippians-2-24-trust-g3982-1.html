TRUST - (peíthō, Strong's G3982)

Summary:

The Greek word peitho fundamentally means to persuade, convince, or induce confidence. When used in a settled state, it refers to having confidence, firm persuasion, or reliance upon someone or something. In Philippians 2:24, Paul uses this word to express his deep conviction and confidence that, under the Lord's sovereign control, he will soon be released from imprisonment and able to visit the Philippian believers in person.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek usage, peitho was widely used in speechmaking, legal settings, and daily conversation. It described the act of persuading someone by reason, evidence, or appeal, as well as the resulting state of being convinced. When a person was persuaded, they moved from uncertainty to conviction, placing their trust in the reliability of a person, message, or promise.

In the New Testament, peitho carries a range of related meanings, from seeking to convince others to holding a quiet, unwavering confidence. In Philippians 2, Paul writes while under Roman imprisonment. Though his physical circumstances and human future were uncertain, his confidence remained steady because it was anchored "in the Lord." The historical setting highlights how real spiritual confidence operates even when personal liberty is restricted.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

spaces

The word peitho can refer to persuading or convincing someone, yielding to persuasion, or possessing firm confidence and trust.

When describing an action directed toward others, it means to win over, persuade, or seek agreement. When describing an internal state of mind, it means to be fully convinced, confident, or assured of a reality or outcome.

In Philippians 2:24, the word means settled confidence or firm assurance. Paul is expressing his conviction that he will be able to visit the Philippian church. Crucially, Paul does not treat his future as a matter of self-determination; his confidence is explicitly qualified by being "in the Lord."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across its various appearances in the New Testament, peitho displays several key nuances:

  • Persuasion and Argument: It describes presenting arguments or evidence to persuade listeners to accept the gospel or a course of action.

  • Obedience or Compliance: In several contexts, being persuaded by an authority leads to following or obeying that authority.

  • Settled Assurance: It frequently denotes a quiet, firm confidence that rests on trustworthy character or divine promises.

In Philippians 2:24, the verb form is perfect active indicative singular. The perfect tense brings into view a settled state resulting from prior consideration. Paul is not expressing a passing wish or mere optimism; he describes an established conviction that remains firm as he considers his circumstances under God's sovereign oversight.

4. Theological Implications

Paul's use of peitho in Philippians 2:24 offers several clear insights into Christian confidence:

  • Confidence rests in God, not in human circumstances. Paul was in prison, facing trial before Roman authorities. Humanly speaking, his travel plans were out of his control. Yet his confidence was grounded "in the Lord," recognizing that God exercises supreme control over rulers, trials, and personal schedules.

  • Divine sovereignty directs human plans. Paul desired to see the Philippians, but he subjected his plans to God's wise ordering. True spiritual confidence submits personal desires to the wisdom and timing of God.

  • Settled assurance sustains hope in trials. Paul's conviction was formed by his ongoing experience of God's faithfulness. When believers trust God in difficult times, that trust builds upon God's proven character and revealed word.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Philippians 2 provides profound instruction on humility, service, and Christlike character. After describing how Jesus humbled Himself to the point of death on a cross (Philippians 2:5-11), Paul highlights practical examples of sacrificial service: Timothy and Epaphroditus.

Paul explains his plan to send Timothy shortly to care for the Philippian church. Immediately after commending Timothy's proven character, Paul adds his own expectation in verse 24: "but I trust in the Lord that I myself shall also come shortly."

The word "trust" here links Paul's warm pastoral affection with his complete reliance on divine providence. Paul loved the Philippian church and longed to minister to them face-to-face. However, he did not treat his future release as a self-made promise or an absolute guarantee. Instead, he expressed a settled, prayerful expectation ("I trust") that was explicitly grounded "in the Lord." This balance shows that strong Christian hope operates alongside humble submission to God's sovereign will.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 18:9 — "trusted"

  • Jesus spoke a parable to certain people who "trusted in themselves that they were righteous, and despised others." Here the word describes a misplaced, self-righteous confidence that stands in direct opposition to reliance on God.

  • 2 Corinthians 1:9 — "trust"

  • Paul explains that he and his companions experienced severe affliction so "that we should not trust in ourselves but in God who raises the dead." Suffering served to strip away self-reliance and redirect trust entirely to God's resurrecting power.

  • Philippians 1:6 — "confident"

  • Earlier in the same letter, Paul writes of being "confident of this very thing, that He who has begun a good work in you will perform it until the day of Jesus Christ." Here the word expresses firm assurance in God's faithful completion of His saving work.

  • Romans 14:14 — "convinced"

  • Paul writes, "I know and am convinced by the Lord Jesus that there is nothing unclean of itself." In this passage, the word refers to a settled conviction formed through the teaching and authority of Christ.

  • Acts 26:26 — "convinced"

  • Speaking before King Agrippa, Paul asserts that the king knows the facts of the gospel, "for I am convinced that none of these things escapes his attention." Here the word refers to being fully persuaded regarding public events.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Object of Trust Determines Its Value: Scripture shows that confidence itself is only as reliable as its object. Trusting in self-righteousness or human strength leads to spiritual ruin, whereas placing trust in the Lord produces stability and peace.

  • The Nature of Settled Conviction: The perfect tense in Philippians 2:24 highlights a state of confidence that has taken root and remains steady. Paul is not wavering in uncertainty; he rests in an established assurance regarding God's care over his life.

  • Practical Balance in Christian Planning: Paul models how believers ought to plan for the future. He forms clear goals and expresses deep desires, but he frames every plan with submission to God's sovereign will.
