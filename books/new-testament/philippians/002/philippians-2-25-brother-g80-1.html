BROTHER - (adelphós, Strong's G80)

Summary:

The word adelphos fundamentally refers to a biological brother born of the same parents, but Scripture frequently uses it to describe a fellow believer in Jesus Christ who shares in the spiritual family of God. In Philippians 2:25, Paul calls Epaphroditus "my brother," establishing their shared identity in Christ before highlighting their shared gospel labor and trials. Through this word, Paul affirms that Christian partnership is built on genuine spiritual kinship and love, not merely functional task completion.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek culture, adelphos derived from terms meaning "from the same womb," referring strictly to biological siblings. Over time, the word broadened in ordinary Greek speech and Jewish tradition to describe close relatives, members of a localized community, or fellow countrymen bound by shared heritage.

When the apostles wrote the New Testament, they adopted this familiar household word to describe a profound reality: those who place faith in Jesus Christ are adopted by God into a real, spiritual family. The early Christian community used adelphos to express the unbreakable bond that unites believers across ethnic, cultural, and social divisions.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Adelphos can refer to:

  • A physical male sibling who shares the same parents or parent.

  • A fellow countryman or member of the same nation.

  • A spiritual brother—a fellow believer in Christ who shares the same divine Father.

In Philippians 2:25, Paul uses the word in this third sense. By describing Epaphroditus as a brother, Paul acknowledges that their deepest connection is their shared life in Christ.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses adelphos across several distinct settings:

  • Physical Family: It describes literal brothers, such as Andrew and Peter, or Martha and Mary's brother Lazarus.

  • National Kinship: In Jewish historical contexts, it refers to fellow Israelites bound by covenant history.

  • Spiritual Household: Throughout the New Testament epistles, the plural form ("brethren") serves as a frequent address for entire congregations, while the singular ("brother") identifies individual Christian believers.

The word emphasizes shared life rather than organizational structure. A believer does not become a brother by joining a human program, but by being born again into God's family.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of adelphos highlights the reality of adoption. Believers do not merely act like a family; they are constituted as a spiritual family by the Holy Spirit.

Because God is the Father of all who trust in Christ, believers share a common spiritual inheritance and identity. Jesus Himself is not ashamed to call believers His brethren. This reality alters how Christians relate to one another: authority and leadership in the church operate within the context of family care rather than commercial hierarchy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:25, Paul writes to the church at Philippi regarding Epaphroditus, whom the Philippians had sent to care for Paul during his imprisonment. Epaphroditus had become gravely ill while serving Paul, and now Paul is sending him back to Philippi.

Paul describes Epaphroditus using three connected titles: "my brother, fellow worker, and fellow soldier." Notice the order in which Paul arranges these descriptions:

  • Brother: Identity and relationship in God's family.

  • Fellow worker: Shared labor in ministry and gospel proclamation.

  • Fellow soldier: Shared endurance through spiritual conflict and suffering.

Before Paul speaks of what Epaphroditus does for the gospel, he affirms who Epaphroditus is in Christ. Epaphroditus was sent by the Philippians to perform a task, but Paul does not view him as an assistant or subordinate. He views him as an equal family member in Christ.

Grammatically, the noun appears in the accusative singular masculine form, serving as a direct descriptive title for Epaphroditus as Paul presents him back to his home church.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 15:27 — "And he said to him, 'Your brother has come...'"

Here the word refers to a biological sibling in Jesus' parable of the prodigal son, illustrating earthly family ties.

  • 2 Corinthians 12:18 — "I urged Titus, and sent our brother with him."

Paul uses the word for a trusted Christian co-worker sent to serve a local congregation.

  • Philippians 1:12 — "But I want you to know, brethren, that the things which happened to me have actually turned out for the furtherance of the gospel;"

Paul addresses the entire Philippian church as his spiritual family, reassuring them while in prison.

  • Hebrews 2:11 — "For both He who sanctifies and those who are being sanctified are all of one, for which cause He is not ashamed to call them brethren,"

This passage highlights Christ's incarnation and union with His redeemed people, calling them His own brothers.

  • 1 John 2:9 — "He who says he is in the light, and hates his brother, is in darkness until now."

John uses the word to show that love for fellow believers is an essential marker of genuine spiritual life.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s warm commendation of Epaphroditus as a "brother" protects Epaphroditus from any potential dishonor. The Philippians had sent Epaphroditus on a mission, and he was returning home after an illness prevented him from staying longer. By honoring him as a brother, fellow worker, and fellow soldier, Paul instructs the church to receive Epaphroditus with joy and high regard. True Christian fellowship values believers for their shared life in Christ, not merely for their practical achievements.
