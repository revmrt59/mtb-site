SEND - (pémpō, Strong's G3992)

Summary:

The Greek verb pempo means to send, dispatch, or commission a person to travel somewhere to perform a specific task or deliver a message. In Philippians 2:25, Paul uses this word to express his decision to send Epaphroditus back to the church in Philippi, ensuring that the believers receive their beloved co-worker back safely while receiving encouragement in the gospel.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Roman world, maintaining personal connection across long distances required sending trusted messengers. Letters, financial relief, news, and pastoral encouragement could only travel as fast as a messenger could walk or sail. Sending someone on a long journey was a significant undertaking that required trust, expense, and commitment from both the sender and the messenger.

In the early church, sending leaders and co-workers served as a primary means of maintaining unity among local congregations. When the apostle Paul was imprisoned, local churches sent trusted delegates to assist him with practical gifts and personal care. In return, Paul dispatched trusted co-laborers back to those churches to deliver letters, provide news, and shepherd the believers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Pempo means to send, dispatch, or cause someone to go from one place to another for a clear purpose. The word focuses on the act of dispatching an agent or representative.

Unlike simple physical movement, pempo typically involves authorization and purpose. The person who is sent acts on behalf of the one who sent them. Depending on the context, the word can refer to:

  • Dispatching a messenger or representative to convey news or deliver a gift.

  • Commissioning a worker or minister to accomplish a spiritual mission.

  • God sending His Son or the Holy Spirit into the world to fulfill His redemptive purpose.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses pempo across many contexts, ranging from simple everyday sending to profound theological truths. In the Gospels, Jesus repeatedly speaks of God as "the Father who sent Me." In those contexts, pempo highlights Jesus' divine mission and His perfect alignment with the Father's mandate.

In the Book of Acts and the Epistles, pempo frequently describes apostolic ministry and administrative decisions within the early church. Apostles and churches sent trusted leaders to deliver letters, settle conflicts, strengthen new believers, and carry relief funds to saints in need.

In Philippians 2:25, the verb appears as an aorist active infinitive. The active voice shows that Paul is carrying out the action of dispatching Epaphroditus, while the infinitive mood completes the thought following his statement, "I considered it necessary." The aorist form presents the act of sending as a complete event within Paul's plan.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of sending lies at the heart of Christian mission. Salvation history unfolds as a story of divine sending: God sent His Son to save sinners, the Father and Son sent the Holy Spirit to empower the church, and Christ sends His followers into the world to proclaim the gospel.

In Paul's letters, human sending reflects this divine pattern of sacrificial love. Paul was in prison and could have used Epaphroditus' help for his own comfort and physical needs. Yet, Paul chose to send Epaphroditus back to Philippi because he cared deeply about the church's emotional and spiritual well-being. Sending Epaphroditus was an act of pastoral unselfishness, demonstrating that gospel leadership prioritizes the good of others over personal convenience.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Philippians 2 contains rich teaching on Christlike humility and practical selflessness. After setting forth Jesus as the supreme model of humble service (Philippians 2:5–11), Paul highlights two living examples of self-sacrificing service: Timothy and Epaphroditus.

Epaphroditus had been sent by the Philippian church to bring financial relief to Paul. While serving Paul in Rome, Epaphroditus became dangerously ill and nearly died. When Epaphroditus recovered, he was distressed because the believers in Philippi had heard he was sick and were worrying about him.

Understanding the anxiety felt by both Epaphroditus and the Philippian church, Paul wrote: "Yet I considered it necessary to send to you Epaphroditus, my brother, fellow worker, and fellow soldier, but your messenger and the one who ministered to my need."

Paul bestows high honor on Epaphroditus before sending him back. By using pempo, Paul makes it clear that Epaphroditus was not abandoning his assignment or returning home in failure. Rather, Paul was officially sending him back with his full apostolic approval and deep gratitude.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient letter writing, authors often used what is known as an epistolary perspective when describing their actions. From Paul's perspective as he wrote, sending Epaphroditus was a future plan. However, from the perspective of the Philippians reading the letter, Epaphroditus would already be standing right in front of them, having delivered the very letter they were reading.

By sending Epaphroditus with this letter, Paul provided a living illustration of the self-emptying love described earlier in the chapter. Epaphroditus risked his life for the work of Christ, and Paul unselfishly gave up his helper for the joy and peace of the Philippian believers.
