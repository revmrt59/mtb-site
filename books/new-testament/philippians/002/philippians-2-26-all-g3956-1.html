ALL - (pâs, Strong's G3956)

pas ( pas , Strong’s G3956 )

Summary:

The word pas refers to total scope, entire quantity, or complete inclusion without exception. In Philippians 2:26, "all" highlights Epaphroditus’ comprehensive affection for the entire church family in Philippi, showing that his longing embraced every member of the congregation without partiality.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word pas is the foundational Greek term used throughout the New Testament to express completeness, totality, and whole scope. It can function as an adjective, a pronoun, or a determiner depending on its position and grammatical relationship to surrounding words.

In ancient Greek literature and everyday speech, pas was used whenever a speaker wanted to emphasize that no individual or part was omitted from a statement. Whether describing an entire group of people, a whole collection of items, or the complete sum of a matter, the word sets a boundary of total inclusion.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of pas is "all," "every," "whole," or "everyone."

  • When paired with a singular noun, it often means "every" individual item or "the whole" entity.

  • When paired with a plural noun or pronoun, it means "all" members of the designated group.

  • When used by itself as a noun, it means "everyone" (masculine plural) or "everything" (neuter plural).

In Philippians 2:26, the word appears in the accusative plural masculine form. Grammatically, it serves as the direct object of Epaphroditus’ longing, modifying the pronoun for the Philippian believers ("you all"). It indicates that his pastoral care and deep affection were directed toward the entire congregation.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, pas is used to establish boundaries of totality. It distinguishes between partial involvement and complete involvement.

When applied to groups of believers, pas prevents favoritism or selective fellowship. It stresses that gospel community encompasses every believer regardless of background, social standing, or internal differences. The word does not merely mean "a large number"; it explicitly signifies the whole body without excluding anyone.

4. Theological Implications

The prominent use of pas in describing Christian relationships reflects the unified nature of the church. Because Christ died for the whole body and calls all believers into one family, pastoral care and brotherly affection must be equally whole.

Epaphroditus’ desire for "all" the believers in Philippi demonstrates how the gospel reshapes personal relationships. True Christian love does not divide a congregation into factions or limit its warmth to a favored few. It reflects God’s broad care by reaching out to the entire local assembly.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:25–30, Paul explains why he is sending Epaphroditus back to Philippi. Epaphroditus was the messenger sent by the Philippians to deliver financial aid to Paul in prison. While with Paul, Epaphroditus became dangerously ill, coming near death.

In verse 26, Paul notes that Epaphroditus "was longing for you all, and was distressed because you had heard that he was sick." The choice of "all" is significant in the broader context of the letter. Philippi was a faithful church, but it faced real threats of personal friction and division (as seen in Paul’s later appeal to Euodia and Syntyche in Philippians 4:2).

By explicitly stating that Epaphroditus longed for all of them, Paul shows that Epaphroditus held no partisan spirit. Furthermore, Epaphroditus’ distress was not over his own suffering, but over the fact that the Philippians were worried about him. His affection was selfless, personal, and extended to every believer in the city.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 14:20 — "So they all ate and were filled, and they took up twelve baskets full of the fragments that remained."

  • John 17:21 — "...that they all may be one, as You, Father, are in Me, and I in You..."

  • 2 Corinthians 7:15 — "And his affection is greater for you as he remembers the obedience of you all, how with fear and trembling you received him."

  • Colossians 1:4 — "...since we heard of your faith in Christ Jesus and of your love for all the saints;"

  • 1 John 3:20 — "For if our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart, and knows all things."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Throughout the letter to the Philippians, Paul repeatedly uses forms of the word pas when referring to the church. In Philippians 1:4, Paul prays for "you all" with joy; in Philippians 1:7, he speaks of having "you all" in his heart; in Philippians 1:8, he longs for "you all" with the affection of Jesus Christ.

When Paul describes Epaphroditus as longing for "you all" in Philippians 2:26, he shows that Epaphroditus shared the very same pastoral heart that Paul possessed. Both men modeled the selfless mindset of Christ described earlier in Philippians 2:3–5, putting the emotional and spiritual well-being of the whole church ahead of their own comfort.
