SICK - (asthenéō, Strong's G770)

Summary:

Astheneō means to be weak, powerless, or physically ill. In the Gospels and Acts, it frequently describes physical sickness, diseases, and bodily infirmities, while in Paul's epistles it can also describe physical weakness or weakness in faith. In Philippians 2:26, the word describes the grave physical illness that Epaphroditus suffered while serving Paul in Rome. Its use here highlights Epaphroditus's selfless heart, as his primary distress was not his own suffering, but the anxiety his illness caused for his home church.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Greek word astheneō comes from a root that means lacking strength or power. In everyday ancient Greek, it was used to describe physical illness, bodily exhaustion, or general frailty. When a person fell ill, they were recognized as having lost their physical strength and capacity for normal labor.

In the New Testament setting, physical sickness was a daily reality with direct social and personal consequences. Without modern medicine, serious illness often rendered a person helpless and dependent on others. In Philippians 2:26, Paul uses astheneō to describe his brother and fellow worker Epaphroditus, who had traveled from Philippi to Rome to deliver financial aid and minister to Paul during his imprisonment. During this mission, Epaphroditus contracted a severe illness that brought him to the brink of death.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Astheneō refers to a state of weakness or illness. Depending on the context in which it is used, the word can refer to:

  • Physical sickness or disease that incapacitates the body.

  • General bodily weakness, frailty, or physical limitation.

  • Spiritual or personal vulnerability, such as being weak in faith or lacking moral strength.

When used of physical health, astheneō signifies an illness that reduces a person's strength and leaves them physically impaired. In Philippians 2:26, the word refers specifically to a severe physical illness that threatened Epaphroditus's life.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, astheneō carries two primary shades of meaning: physical illness and functional or spiritual weakness.

In gospel narratives, astheneō consistently describes physical illness, bodily ailments, and conditions requiring healing. Jesus commanded His disciples to heal those who were sick, and large crowds brought their sick family members to Him. In these settings, the word emphasizes physical helplessness and the need for divine mercy and healing power.

In apostolic writings, astheneō can refer either to bodily weakness and physical sickness or to moral and spiritual weakness. For example, it describes those who are scrupulous or weak in faith, as well as the inherent weakness of human flesh. In Philippians 2:26, the focus is entirely physical: Epaphroditus experienced a genuine, life-threatening bodily sickness.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of astheneō in Philippians 2:26 carries several clear theological lessons:

  • Faithful ministry does not render believers immune to physical illness or bodily frailty. Epaphroditus was engaged in sacrificial Christian service, yet he experienced a severe, near-fatal illness.

  • God's mercy is central in recovery. Paul specifically records that God had mercy on Epaphroditus and restored his health, showing that recovery from sickness is an act of divine grace.

  • Christian love turns attention outward rather than inward. The gospel transforms human priorities so that believers care deeply for the emotional and spiritual peace of others, even when undergoing intense physical suffering.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:26, Paul writes that Epaphroditus "was distressed because you had heard that he was sick." The verb is an aorist active indicative form, presenting the illness as a completed event in past time during Epaphroditus's stay in Rome.

The surrounding passage adds vital depth to this statement:

  • Epaphroditus was sent by the Philippian church to bring aid to Paul in prison. While carrying out this mission, he fell critically ill ("sick almost to death," v. 27).

  • What grieved Epaphroditus was not merely his physical pain, but the knowledge that news of his illness had reached Philippi, causing worry among his brothers and sisters in Christ.

  • Instead of being consumed with self-pity during his sickness, Epaphroditus was burdened for the emotional well-being of his home congregation.

  • Paul highlights Epaphroditus as a living example of the Christlike mindset described earlier in Philippians 2, where believers are exhorted to look out not only for their own interests, but also for the interests of others.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 25:36 — "I was naked and you clothed Me; I was sick and you visited Me; I was in prison and you came to Me."

  • John 11:3 — "Therefore the sisters sent to Him, saying, 'Lord, behold, he whom You love is sick.'"

  • Acts 20:35 — "I have shown you in every way, by laboring like this, that you must support the weak. And remember the words of the Lord Jesus, how He said, 'It is more blessed to give than to receive.'"

  • Romans 14:1 — "Receive one who is weak in the faith, but not to disputes over doubtful things."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The contrast between Epaphroditus's physical weakness and his spiritual strength is striking. Though his body was weakened by illness, his pastoral concern and love for the church remained robust and active.

  • Paul did not perform an instantaneous miraculous healing on Epaphroditus, nor did he guarantee immunity from suffering. Instead, Paul recognized Epaphroditus's eventual recovery as a direct act of God's mercy, designed to spare Paul additional sorrow upon sorrow.

  • Epaphroditus's sickness serves as a practical illustration of Christian partnership. The Philippians sent help to Paul, but in doing so, their messenger suffered. Paul then lovingly returned him to Philippi to relieve their worry and complete the circle of mutual care.
