ONLY - (mónon, Strong's G3440)

Summary:

The word monon functions as an adverb meaning "only," "alone," "simply," or "merely." It is used to limit a statement to a single item or focus, or to set up an expansion when used in the phrase "not only... but also." In Philippians 2:27, Paul uses the word to explain that God's mercy in Epaphroditus's physical recovery was not restricted to Epaphroditus alone. By stating that God had mercy "not on him only, but on me also," Paul reveals that God spared Epaphroditus from death and simultaneously spared Paul from overwhelming grief.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Epaphroditus was a member of the church in Philippi who traveled to minister to Paul during Paul's imprisonment. While serving alongside Paul, Epaphroditus fell severely ill and nearly died. The Philippians heard of his sickness and became deeply concerned, while Epaphroditus himself felt distressed because his home church was anxious for him.

In Philippians 2:27, Paul addresses this intense situation. He confirms that Epaphroditus was indeed sick to the point of death, but he testifies to God's gracious intervention. Paul introduces the word monon within a classic expansion pattern: "not on him only, but on me also." This common particle of limitation allowed Greek writers to establish a baseline focus before broadening the scope to include another important subject.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word monon is an adverb that means "only," "merely," "alone," or "simply." It specifies boundary or limitation in a sentence.

When used in a simple statement, the word excludes other options or limits an action to a single requirement or person. When combined with a negative particle in the phrase "not only... but also," the word temporarily limits a concept only to break through that boundary and show that a reality extends further than the initial subject. In this context, it marks the transition from a narrower benefit to a wider one.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In New Testament usage, monon regularly serves both restrictive and contrastive functions.

When functioning restrictively, it calls for exclusive focus. For example, it can single out faith alone apart from fear, or direct attention to one specific obligation.

When functioning contrastively in "not only" constructions, it prevents readers from drawing too narrow a conclusion. Writers use it to show that an action, truth, or experience applies beyond its immediate target. In apostolic letters, this phrase often shifts the reader's sight from an individual instance to a corporate truth, or from a physical reality to a spiritual one.

4. Theological Implications

Paul's use of monon in Philippians 2:27 highlights how God's mercy works within the fellowship of believers. Epaphroditus's physical recovery was direct divine mercy toward Epaphroditus himself. Yet Paul insists that this act of healing cannot be limited to the one who was sick.

This reveals that God's caring providences are rarely isolated events. God considers the emotional and spiritual burdens of His children together. Sparing the life of Epaphroditus was equally an act of divine compassion toward Paul, shielding the apostle from layered heartbreak while in chains. God mercifully weighs the limits of human sorrow and provides relief that protects the shepherd as well as the servant.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2, Paul presents Christ as the supreme model of humble service and then highlights Timothy and Epaphroditus as living examples of Christlike care. Epaphroditus risked his life to supply what was lacking in the Philippians' service to Paul. His near-fatal illness created a crisis of grief for Paul and concern for the Philippian church.

When Paul writes that God had mercy "not on him only, but on me also, lest I should have sorrow upon sorrow," he connects Epaphroditus's restoration directly to his own emotional state. Paul was already carrying the burden of imprisonment and opposition. The death of his beloved fellow worker would have added a devastating weight to his existing trials. By using monon to broaden the scope of God's mercy, Paul teaches the Philippians that God orchestrated Epaphroditus's recovery with pastoral tenderness toward Paul's personal grief.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 3:29 — "Or is He the God of the Jews only? Is He not also the God of the Gentiles?"

  • 2 Corinthians 7:7 — "and not only by his coming, but also by the consolation with which he was comforted in you..."

  • James 1:22 — "But be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase "not only... but also" is one of Paul's favorite rhetorical patterns for showing spiritual connection. It reminds believers that no Christian lives or suffers in isolation.

When God shows kindness to one member of the body, that kindness overflows to bless others who love them. Paul saw God's hand not just in large miraculous decrees, but in the tender preservation of a friend's life that saved his own heart from being overwhelmed by grief.
