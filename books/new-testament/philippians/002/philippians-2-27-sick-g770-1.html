SICK - (asthenéō, Strong's G770)

Summary:

The word astheneō means to be weak, sick, frail, or without strength. It describes physical illness, bodily weakness, or a lack of spiritual and moral strength depending on how it is used. In Philippians 2:27, the word describes the severe, near-fatal physical illness suffered by Epaphroditus while he was serving Paul in ministry.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb astheneō comes from a root that literally means "without strength." In ancient Greek life, physical strength was prized for labor, military service, and everyday survival. To become astheneō was to be stripped of normal physical vigor, leaving a person vulnerable, incapacitated, or confined to bed.

When Epaphroditus traveled from Philippi to Rome, he carried financial aid to support Paul during his imprisonment. Traveling long distances in the ancient Roman world involved severe physical demands, exposure to harsh elements, and high risk of infectious disease. While completing this sacrificial mission, Epaphroditus became dangerously ill. Paul uses astheneō to describe the serious bodily affliction that overtook this faithful servant.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of astheneō is to lack strength or to be rendered powerless. Across the New Testament, the word carries two primary shades of meaning depending on the subject and context:

  • Physical sickness or infirmity: The word frequently refers to bodily disease, severe illness, or physical incapacitation that leaves a person helpless.

  • Spiritual, moral, or doctrinal weakness: The word can also describe someone whose faith, conscience, or understanding is immature or vulnerable to temptation and doubt.

In Philippians 2:27, the meaning is strictly physical. Epaphroditus suffered from a life-threatening bodily illness that brought him to the very brink of death.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Gospels and the Book of Acts, astheneō almost always refers to literal physical illness. It describes the crowds of suffering people who came to Jesus seeking healing, as well as specific individuals who were bedridden or dying. In these historical accounts, the word highlights human helplessness in the face of disease and God's power to restore health.

In Paul's letters, astheneō expands to include spiritual and moral conditions. Paul uses it to address believers who are weak in faith or conscience regarding disputed matters like dietary laws. He also uses it to describe the natural weakness of human flesh in contrast to the power of the Holy Spirit. However, when Paul turns to personal news about his ministry companions, as in Philippians, the word returns to its primary physical sense of bodily sickness.

4. Theological Implications

The illness of Epaphroditus provides an important theological perspective on suffering in the Christian life. Epaphroditus was a dedicated Christian minister doing the work of the Lord, yet he was not exempt from severe sickness. Physical weakness and bodily illness are common human experiences in a fallen world, even for those actively engaged in faithful gospel service.

Furthermore, Paul attributes Epaphroditus' survival to divine mercy. God restored Epaphroditus not only out of compassion for the sick man, but also out of mercy to Paul, sparing the apostle from overwhelming grief. This demonstrates that healing is an act of God's sovereign mercy and personal care for His servants, rather than an automatic mechanical right.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:25–30, Paul introduces Epaphroditus as his "brother, fellow worker, and fellow soldier." The Philippian church had dispatched Epaphroditus to care for Paul's practical needs in prison. Paul reports that Epaphroditus "was sick almost unto death."

Grammatically, the verb form used here is an aorist active indicative verb in the singular number. This form simply states the historical reality of Epaphroditus' past illness as a completed fact. Paul does not downplay the gravity of the sickness; he openly acknowledges that Epaphroditus reached the edge of death.

The emotional weight of the passage comes from the deep mutual love between Epaphroditus, Paul, and the Philippian congregation. Epaphroditus was deeply distressed not merely because he had been sick, but because the Philippians had heard about his condition and were worried. His physical weakness revealed his selfless heart: even in critical illness, his concern was focused on the peace of his home church rather than his own discomfort.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 10:8 – "Heal the sick, cleanse the lepers, raise the dead, cast out demons."

  • John 11:3 – "Therefore the sisters sent to Him, saying, 'Lord, behold, he whom You love is sick.'"

  • Acts 9:37 – "But it happened in those days that she became sick and died."

  • Acts 20:35 – "I have shown you in every way, by laboring like this, that you must support the weak."

  • Romans 14:1 – "Receive one who is weak in the faith, but not to disputes over doubtful things."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Epaphroditus' story shows a striking contrast between bodily weakness and spiritual boldness. Physically, his body was so weak (astheneō) that he nearly died. Yet spiritually, he displayed incredible bravery by risking his life for the work of Christ. Physical frailty does not lessen the spiritual value of a believer's service.

Additionally, Paul's open admission of sorrow shows his deep pastoral tenderness. Paul did not treat human affection or grief as a sign of spiritual weakness. He freely confessed that losing Epaphroditus would have caused him profound sorrow, making God's merciful healing of his friend a comfort to both of them.
