AGAIN - (pálin, Strong's G3825)

Summary:

The Greek word palin is an adverb that indicates repetition or return, translated as "again," "once more," or "back." It marks an action taking place a second time or returning to a previous state or location. In Philippians 2:28, Paul uses the word to describe the Philippian church seeing Epaphroditus once more after his recovery from a life-threatening illness. His return transforms their deep worry into renewed joy and eases Paul's own sorrow.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The adverb palin is a foundational word in New Testament Greek used to express repetition, sequence, or return. It answers the question of whether an event, movement, or statement is happening again or returning to a previous condition.

In the letter to the Philippians, Paul writes from imprisonment. The church at Philippi had sent Epaphroditus to care for Paul's physical needs and deliver a gift. While serving Paul, Epaphroditus became gravely ill and nearly died. The Philippians heard about his illness and were deeply distressed. God miraculously restored Epaphroditus to health, and Paul chose to send him back to Philippi. In this warm, personal letter, Paul explains his decision so the church will receive their messenger back with great joy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Palin functions as an adverb of time, direction, or sequence. Depending on how it is used in a sentence, the word can mean:

  • Repeating an action or event ("again," "once more").

  • Returning to a former place or state ("back," "in return").

  • Adding a related point or contrasting thought in discourse ("furthermore," "on the other hand").

In Philippians 2:28, the word describes a physical reunion: "that when you see him again, you may rejoice." It points back to their former fellowship before Epaphroditus left on his mission, now restored after severe trial.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, palin serves several practical functions in narrative and teaching:

  • Movement and Location: It describes Jesus or the apostles returning to a town or region they had previously visited.

  • Repeated Actions: It marks repeated words, prayers, or events, such as Jesus praying a second time in Gethsemane or groaning in spirit at the tomb of Lazarus.

  • Logical Addition: It connects Old Testament quotations or logical steps in an argument, meaning "furthermore" or "again."

  • Spiritual Contrast: It describes returning to a former spiritual state, such as falling back into fear or bondage.

Because palin is an adaptable adverb, its pastoral weight comes from the specific action or situation being repeated.

4. Theological Implications

While palin is a simple grammatical connector, its use in Philippians 2:28 highlights God's merciful providence in Christian fellowship. Epaphroditus was near death, but God spared his life. His return to Philippi is not merely a routine trip home; it is a visible display of God's mercy.

Seeing Epaphroditus again demonstrates how God restores what was threatened by grief and death. The repetition of their fellowship becomes a occasion for praise, turning corporate anxiety into shared thanksgiving. It shows that God cares about the emotional burdens of His people and uses restored relationships to strengthen their faith.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:25–30, Paul provides an updates on Epaphroditus. He calls him a brother, fellow worker, and fellow soldier. Epaphroditus was distressed because the Philippians had heard he was sick. Paul acknowledges that Epaphroditus was indeed sick to the point of death, but God had mercy on him, and on Paul as well, to prevent Paul from having "sorrow upon sorrow."

Because of this, Paul sends Epaphroditus back with urgency ("the more eagerly"). The word "again" in verse 28 stands at the center of a dramatic emotional shift. The Philippians had last known Epaphroditus as a sick man in grave danger. Seeing him again alive, well, and present in their assembly replaces their anxiety with joy. Furthermore, seeing their joy allows Paul himself to be "less sorrowful," even while remaining in chains.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 4:7 — "Jesus said to him, 'It is written again, "You shall not tempt the LORD your God."'"

  • John 11:38 — "Then Jesus, again groaning in Himself, came to the tomb. It was a cave, and a stone lay against it."

  • Acts 10:15 — "And a voice spoke to him again a second time, 'What God has cleansed you must not call common.'"

  • Romans 8:15 — "For you did not receive the spirit of bondage again to fear, but you received the Spirit of adoption by whom we cry, 'Abba, Father.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s use of "again" in this passage reflects deep pastoral selflessness. Though Paul was in prison and could have benefited greatly from Epaphroditus's ongoing help, he prioritized the spiritual health and joy of the Philippian church. Sending Epaphroditus back meant Paul forfeited a valuable personal helper, but he chose their comfort over his own convenience. The sight of Epaphroditus back among them was worth the personal sacrifice.
