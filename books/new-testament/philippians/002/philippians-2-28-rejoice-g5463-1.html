REJOICE - (chaírō, Strong's G5463)

Summary:

The verb chairo means to be glad, to delight, or to experience joy. In Philippians 2:28, the word describes the gladness and relief the Philippian believers would experience upon seeing Epaphroditus restored to health and present among them again. Paul sends Epaphroditus back to Philippi so that their anxiety over his illness might be turned to joy, showing how pastoral care and mutual affection promote gladness within the church.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Paul wrote his letter to the Philippians while imprisoned, likely in Rome. The church at Philippi had sent Epaphroditus to deliver a financial gift and to minister to Paul's personal needs. While carrying out this mission, Epaphroditus fell dangerously ill and nearly died. When news of his illness reached the Philippian church, the believers were deeply concerned, which in turn caused Epaphroditus distress over their anxiety for him.

After God mercifully restored Epaphroditus to health, Paul decided to send him back to Philippi without delay. The word chosen to describe their expected reaction—rejoice—captures the glad relief of a Christian community receiving back a faithful brother whose life was graciously spared by God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb chairo means to be glad, to rejoice, or to feel delight. It describes a state of joy prompted by good news, a welcome arrival, or a favorable outcome. In ancient Greek, the word was also commonly used as a standard form of greeting, such as "greetings" or "hail."

In biblical usage, chairo ranges from personal happiness over positive circumstances to deep spiritual gladness rooted in God's saving work and providential care. It refers not merely to an external display of cheer, but to genuine gladness of heart.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, chairo is used to express various forms of gladness. It describes the joy of the Wise Men when they saw the star leading to Christ, the delight of the crowds as Jesus performed miracles, and the gladness of the disciples upon seeing the resurrected Lord.

In Philippians 2:28, the grammatical form expresses a purposeful goal: Paul sends Epaphroditus so that the Philippian believers may rejoice when they see him again. Grammatically, the verb occurs in the aorist tense, passive voice, and subjunctive mood with a plural subject. This structure presents the desired outcome for the whole congregation upon Epaphroditus's arrival, framing their shared gladness as the ultimate objective of his return.

4. Theological Implications

Joy in the New Testament is intimately tied to God's providence, mercy, and gospel grace. In Philippians, joy is a central theme, yet it is never depicted as blind optimism or the total absence of sorrow. Epaphroditus had been on the brink of death, and Paul was facing imprisonment.

The gladness described here arises directly from God's mercy. God spared Epaphroditus, thereby sparing Paul from overwhelming grief and giving the Philippians cause for praise. True Christian joy recognizes God's active hand in preserving His servants and sustaining His people through trials.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:25–30, Paul presents Epaphroditus as a model of sacrificial Christian service. Epaphroditus risked his own life for the service of Christ and the care of Paul. Although Paul deeply appreciated his companionship, he selflessly prioritized the needs of the Philippian church over his own immediate comfort.

Paul explains his decision in verse 28: "that when you see him again you may rejoice, and I may be less sorrowful." The return of Epaphroditus brings a double benefit. First, it fills the Philippians with gladness, replacing their anxiety with relief. Second, it lightens Paul's own sorrow, because knowing his beloved congregation is comforted brings peace to his own heart even while in custody.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 2:10 — "When they saw the star, they rejoiced with exceedingly great joy."

  • Luke 13:17 — "And when He said these things, all His adversaries were put to shame; and all the people rejoiced for all the glorious things that were done by Him."

  • John 20:20 — "When He had said this, He showed them His hands and His side. Then the disciples were glad when they saw the Lord."

  • Philippians 4:4 — "Rejoice in the Lord always. Again I will say, rejoice!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Christian joy and earthly sorrow often coexist in the present age. Paul states that seeing the Philippians rejoice will make him "less sorrowful," demonstrating that joy does not instantly eliminate difficult circumstances but provides deep comfort within them.

  • The passage reveals the deep emotional bond within the early church. Epaphroditus was distressed because the Philippians were worried about him, and Paul was eager to send him back to restore their peace. The word rejoice marks the joyful resolution of this mutual care.
