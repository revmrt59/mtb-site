SENT - (pémpō, Strong's G3992)

Summary:

, The word pempo means to dispatch, transmit, or cause someone to go from one place to another under authority or with a specific purpose.

In Philippians 2:28, Paul uses this word to express his deliberate decision to send Epaphroditus back to the church at Philippi. By dispatching Epaphroditus, Paul seeks to relieve the Philippians' anxiety over their brother's recent life-threatening illness and restore joy to their fellowship, demonstrating deep pastoral care for both the messenger and the congregation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Greek verb pempo refers to the act of sending someone or something from one place to another. In ancient Greek literature and everyday communication, the word was used for sending letters, dispatching messengers, commissioning ambassadors, or ordering servants on errands.

In the New Testament setting, sending was an essential part of apostolic ministry and church life. Because ancient communication depended entirely on physical travel, sending a trusted brother was the primary way leaders maintained contact with congregations, delivered letters, and met practical needs across distances. In Philippians 2, Epaphroditus had originally been sent by the Philippian church to bring financial aid and support to Paul while he was imprisoned. After falling gravely ill, Epaphroditus recovered through God's mercy, prompting Paul to dispatch him back to Philippi.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means to send, send forth, or dispatch. It describes directing a person or object to travel to a designated destination to accomplish an objective.

Unlike general motion verbs that simply mean to move or go, pempo emphasizes the initiator who issues the sending directive. The word can refer to:

  • Sending a messenger or delegation to deliver news or represent an authority.

  • Sending physical items, such as gifts or letters.

  • Sending a person on a divine or practical mission.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, pempo carries several practical and theological shades of meaning depending on who is doing the sending:

  • God sending Jesus: The verb frequently describes God the Father dispatching His Son into the world to accomplish salvation.

  • Jesus sending the Holy Spirit or His disciples: The Lord dispatches the Spirit to comfort believers and sends His followers out to preach.

  • Apostles sending co-workers: Leaders like Paul regularly sent trusted associates to strengthen, teach, and comfort local churches.

The verb focuses on the act of dispatching and the goal of the journey rather than the official office of the person sent.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical pattern of sending highlights fundamental truths about God and ministry:

  • God is a sending God: Redemption originates with God initiating contact with humanity by sending His Son and His Spirit.

  • Christian ministry is relational and sent: Ministry is not carried out in isolation. Leaders and churches send trusted individuals to serve others, build unity, and meet practical needs.

  • Sending requires sacrifice: When Paul sent Epaphroditus back to Philippi, he voluntarily gave up a valued companion who was assisting him in prison, putting the emotional and spiritual wellbeing of the Philippian church above his personal convenience.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:28, Paul writes, "Therefore I sent him the more eagerly, that when you see him again you may rejoice, and I may be less sorrowful."

The active voice presents Paul as the direct agent making the decision to send Epaphroditus. The singular form identifies Paul's personal leadership and care in dispatching his fellow worker. The aorist indicative form describes the act of sending as a completed decision made from Paul's vantage point as he prepares the letter. As the Philippians read these words, Epaphroditus would be arriving before them with the letter in hand.

Paul's motivation for sending Epaphroditus was rooted in pastoral compassion. Epaphroditus had been deeply distressed because the Philippians heard he had been near death. By sending him back promptly, Paul aimed to replace their anxiety with joy and ease his own sorrow by knowing his beloved friends were comforted.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 15:22 — "Then it pleased the apostles and elders, with the whole church, to send chosen men of their own company to Antioch with Paul and Barnabas..."

  • Romans 8:3 — "For what the law could not do in that it was weak through the flesh, God did by sending His own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, on account of sin: He condemned sin in the flesh..."

  • Ephesians 6:22 — "...whom I have sent to you for this very purpose, that you may know our affairs, and that he may comfort your hearts."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Reciprocal Christian Partnership: The journey of Epaphroditus forms a complete circle of care. The church at Philippi sent him to care for Paul, and Paul sent him back to care for Philippi.

  • Emotional Openness in Leadership: Paul openly reveals his motivation for sending Epaphroditus—to lessen his own sorrow and increase their joy. True gospel ministry values the emotional and relational health of God's people.
