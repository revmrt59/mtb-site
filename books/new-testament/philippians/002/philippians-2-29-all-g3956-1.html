ALL - (pâs, Strong's G3956)

Summary:

The word refers to totality, completeness, or the full measure of something. When modifying an abstract quality like joy or gladness, it denotes the highest degree or a complete, unreserved expression of that quality. In Philippians 2:29, Paul uses the word to urge the church at Philippi to welcome Epaphroditus back with complete, wholehearted joy, leaving no room for coldness, hesitation, or second-guessing.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek, this foundational word expresses total inclusion or maximum degree. Depending on how it is used in a sentence, it can refer to every individual member of a group ("all people"), the entirety of a collective whole ("the whole city"), or the maximum intensity of an abstract quality ("all joy" or "all wisdom").

When the word qualifies an abstract noun such as joy, patience, or gladness, it functions as an intensive marker. Rather than describing a physical count, it describes the full, unreserved expression of that quality. In everyday speech as well as written texts, the word conveyed that nothing was held back or left partial.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of the word is "all," "every," "whole," or "complete."

When used in Scripture, its specific sense depends on the context:

  • As a plural adjective, it means every individual within a specified group ("all the saints").

  • As a singular adjective modifying a collective noun, it means the entire whole ("the whole body").

  • As a singular adjective modifying an abstract quality, it means total, full, or unreserved ("all joy," "all boldness").

In Philippians 2:29, the word modifies "gladness" as a genitive singular feminine adjective. In this grammatical construction, the word means complete or unreserved. Paul is not calling for a measured or reluctant welcome, but for full, overflowing gladness.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, the word appears in a wide variety of settings. Its exact nuance depends entirely on the word it modifies and the surrounding argument.

When describing God's attributes or actions, the word highlights His exhaustive knowledge and boundless grace. For example, God knows all things, and His glory extends across all generations. When describing human actions, the word often sets a standard of wholehearted devotion or comprehensive care, such as loving all the believers or displaying all patience.

In personal exhortations, Paul frequently uses the word to elevate the standard of Christian behavior. Rather than settling for a partial or hesitant effort, believers are called to express virtues in their fullest measure.

4. Theological Implications

The theological force of this word in Philippians 2:29 rests on how the Christian community receives faithful ministers of the gospel. Epaphroditus had risked his life to serve Paul in prison, becoming so ill that he nearly died. Upon his return to Philippi, there might have been a temptation among some believers to view his return as a failure or an incomplete mission.

By calling the church to receive him with "all gladness," Scripture guards against worldly standards of evaluation. True Christian joy is not conditional or halfway. God calls His people to honor sacrificial service with complete, unreserved gladness. The use of this intensive word demonstrates that gospel partnership requires warm, wholehearted fellowship rather than critical appraisal.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

layout of Philippians 2 centers on humble service and selfless ministry. Paul sets forth Christ as the supreme example of self-emptying humility (Philippians 2:5–11), followed by Timothy's proven character (Philippians 2:19–22) and Epaphroditus's costly sacrifice (Philippians 2:25–30).

Epaphroditus was sent by the Philippian church as their messenger to care for Paul's physical needs in prison. While serving there, he fell dangerously ill. God mercifully spared his life, and Paul decided to send him back to Philippi to relieve the church's anxiety.

In verse 29, Paul commands the believers: "Receive him therefore in the Lord with all gladness, and hold such men in esteem." The word "all" modifies "gladness." Paul explicitly directs the Philippians to welcome Epaphroditus back with total, unhindered joy. He removes any potential awkwardness or misunderstanding about Epaphroditus's early return, stressing that their reception should be marked by total gratitude for a brother who risked his life for Christ's work.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In classical and Biblical Greek, pairing this word with an abstract noun of emotion or virtue is a standard way to express maximum degree without limit. Similar expressions appear throughout Paul's letters, such as "all patience," "all peace," and "all humility."

In Philippians 2:29, the word functions as a pastoral safeguard. By instructing the church to show "all gladness," Paul leaves no room for partial joy, quiet disappointment, or second-guessing. It reframes Epaphroditus's arrival not as a returned messenger who came back early, but as a faithful servant of Christ who deserves the church's highest honor.
