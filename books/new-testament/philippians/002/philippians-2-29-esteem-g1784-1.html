ESTEEM - (éntimos, Strong's G1784)

Summary:

The word entimos describes someone or something that is held in high regard, considered precious, or assigned great value and honor. In Philippians 2:29, Paul uses this word to command the church to hold selfless gospel workers like Epaphroditus in high honor, recognizing that those who risk their lives for the work of Christ deserve deep respect and gratitude from God's people.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

When Paul wrote his letter to the Philippians, he was imprisoned for preaching the gospel. The church at Philippi had sent Epaphroditus to bring financial support and care for Paul's personal needs. While with Paul, Epaphroditus became so severely ill that he nearly died. God restored his health, and Paul decided to send him back to Philippi to relieve the congregation's anxiety and restore their joy.

Paul anticipated that some in the congregation might misinterpret Epaphroditus's early return as a failure to complete his mission. To prevent any misunderstanding, Paul gives clear instructions on how the church should welcome him. He calls them to receive Epaphroditus in the Lord with gladness and to hold men like him in high esteem.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

around the core idea of worth and value. The word can refer to a person who holds a position of distinction, a prized servant, or a precious object.

Here the word means held in honor, highly regarded, or treated as valuable. It stands in contrast to treating someone as common, ordinary, or easily replaced. When applied to people, it describes placing a high estimate on their character, labor, and worth to the community.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the New Testament, the word appears in contexts where true worth or distinction is highlighted. It describes personal valuation, social honor, and divine preciousness.

In gospel narratives, the word is used for a servant who is deeply prized by his master, as well as for a distinguished guest seated in a place of honor at a banquet. In apostolic letters, the term describes Jesus Christ as the precious chief cornerstone, chosen by God though rejected by men.

In Philippians 2:29, Paul takes this concept of high value and applies it to gospel ministers. Believers are instructed to view servants who labor selflessly for Christ as individuals of immense worth who ought to be treated with honor.

4. Theological Implications

This passage shows how God's kingdom redefines honor. In human society, high esteem is often reserved for those with wealth, earthly power, or prominent status. In the church, honor is attached to self-giving sacrifice and faithful service to Jesus Christ.

Esteeming faithful servants aligns the church's values with God's valuation. When believers honor those who risk their comfort and lives for the gospel, they honor the work of Christ displayed in them. This mandate does not encourage pride or self-exaltation in leaders; rather, it instructs the Christian community to show tangible gratitude and respect to those who serve the body of Christ at great personal cost.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The command in Philippians 2:29 sits within a larger section focused on Christlike humility. In the second chapter of Philippians, Paul presents Jesus as the ultimate model of self-emptying love (Philippians 2:5–11). He then points to Timothy and Epaphroditus as practical, living examples of that same mindset.

Epaphroditus nearly lost his life trying to serve Paul on behalf of the Philippian church. Paul explicitly notes that Epaphroditus risked his life to supply what was lacking in their service. The grammatical form of entimos in this verse is masculine accusative plural, pointing beyond Epaphroditus alone to a general class of leaders: "such men."

Paul tells the church to place a high value on Epaphroditus and anyone else who displays similar sacrificial devotion. Rather than evaluating ministers by earthly standards of success or status, the church must value them because of their selfless devotion to Christ.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 7:2 — "And a certain centurion’s servant, who was dear to him, was sick and ready to die." Here the word is rendered "dear," illustrating that the centurion held his servant in high personal regard and deeply valued his life.

  • Luke 14:8 — "When you are invited by anyone to a wedding feast, do not sit down in the best place, lest one more honorable than you be invited by him." In this parable, the word is translated "honorable," referring to a guest who holds social distinction or a place of high standing.

  • 1 Peter 2:4 — "Coming to Him as to a living stone, rejected indeed by men, but chosen by God and precious." Here the word describes Jesus Christ as the foundation stone who is highly valued and precious in God's estimation.

  • 1 Peter 2:6 — "Therefore it is also contained in the Scripture, 'Behold, I lay in Zion A chief cornerstone, elect, precious, and he who believes on Him will by no means be put to shame.'" The word is repeated to emphasize the supreme value God places upon Jesus Christ as the cornerstone of salvation.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A clear contrast exists throughout Scripture between human estimation and divine valuation. What human culture often views as ordinary or weak, God identifies as precious. Epaphroditus was a simple messenger whose sickness might have looked like a failure by worldly standards, yet Paul identifies him as a soldier and fellow worker worthy of high honor.

Commanding the church to hold faithful leaders in esteem creates a healthy environment of mutual care. While gospel workers are called to serve with humility without seeking personal glory, congregations are commanded to guard against taking their leaders for granted. Recognizing the value of sacrificial service builds up the entire body of Christ.
