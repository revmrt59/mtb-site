HOLD - (échō, Strong's G2192)

Summary:

The Greek verb echō basic meaning is to have, hold, possess, or regard. Depending on context, it can refer to physical ownership, spiritual possession, or a mental attitude of estimation toward someone. In Philippians 2:29, Paul uses the verb as a direct command to the Philippian believers, instructing them to hold selfless gospel workers like Epaphroditus in high honor and esteem.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In Philippians 2, Paul writes to a church he loves deeply while he is imprisoned for the gospel. The Philippian congregation had sent Epaphroditus to deliver aid to Paul and care for his personal needs. During his service, Epaphroditus fell critically ill and nearly died. After God mercifully spared his life, Paul decided to send Epaphroditus back to Philippi to relieve the congregation's anxieties and bring them joy.

Paul wants to ensure that Epaphroditus is received with deep appreciation rather than suspicion or disappointment. Because Epaphroditus returned before Paul's imprisonment had ended, some might have viewed his return as an unfinished mission. Paul uses the common Greek verb echō ("hold") paired with terms of honor to direct the church on how to evaluate and cherish faithful ministers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb echō is one of the most frequent verbs in the New Testament. It carries a broad range of meanings based on context, including:

  • To hold or retain physical objects in hand.

  • To possess or have as a belonging, quality, or condition.

  • To experience or undergo a state, such as having peace or trouble.

  • To consider, regard, or hold an opinion about a person or matter.

When echō is joined with an expression of value, it means to hold someone in a particular estimation or to regard them with high value. In Philippians 2:29, the word means to hold or maintain a posture of high esteem toward faithful servants of God.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, echō describes various forms of possession and posture. It can describe physical possession, such as having loaves of bread, or spiritual realities, such as possessing eternal life through faith in Christ. It also expresses internal convictions, such as having confidence toward God or holding a particular judgment about others.

In Philippians 2:29, the grammatical form is a present active imperative in the plural number. Present tense describes an ongoing posture, active voice indicates the subject carries out the action, imperative mood issues an authoritative command, and plural number addresses the church community as a whole. Paul is not issuing a temporary suggestion, but commanding the congregation to make high regard for faithful servants their continuous practice.

4. Theological Implications

The command to hold faithful servants in esteem reveals God's value system for gospel ministry. Human cultures often reserve honor for the powerful, wealthy, or prominent. In contrast, the kingdom of God honors those who display Christlike humility and self-sacrifice.

Epaphroditus risked his life for the work of Christ, echoing the self-emptying spirit of Jesus described earlier in Philippians 2:5–11. By commanding the church to hold such workers in high regard, Paul aligns the church's practical values with God's evaluation. Honor in the Christian community is grounded in sacrificial devotion to Christ and His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the immediate context of Philippians 2:25–30, Paul provides a clear justification for why Epaphroditus deserves this posture from the church. Paul describes him as a brother, fellow worker, fellow soldier, and a minister to his needs. Epaphroditus did not suffer illness through carelessness, but because he risked his life to fulfill the service that the distant Philippians could not perform in person.

By telling the Philippians to "hold such men in esteem," Paul protects Epaphroditus from any unfair judgment. The word echō here requires the congregation to deliberately actively hold Epaphroditus—and others like him who display sacrificial devotion—in high honor. It transforms his return into an occasion for joyful reception and collective gratitude.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The combination of echō with terms of respect shows that honor is not merely an emotion, but an intentional attitude maintained by a community. Churches naturally absorb the values of their surrounding culture unless they consciously evaluate leaders and servants by biblical standards.

Furthermore, Paul broadens the scope of his instruction. He does not say to hold only Epaphroditus in esteem, but to hold "such men" in honor. This creates a general principle for local churches across every generation: those who sacrifice their health, comfort, and security for the advance of the gospel and the care of God's people are to be consistently valued and cherished.
