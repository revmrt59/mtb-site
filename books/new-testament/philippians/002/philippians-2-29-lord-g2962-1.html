LORD - (kýrios, Strong's G2962)

Summary:

The word kurios refers to a master, owner, or ruler who possesses legitimate authority and control. In ordinary ancient usage, it described household masters, property owners, and legal superiors. In the Greek translation of the Old Testament and throughout the New Testament, it became the primary title for God and for Jesus Christ as supreme Lord over all creation. In Philippians 2:29, Paul instructs the church to welcome Epaphroditus "in the Lord," meaning their reception of him must be governed by their shared allegiance to Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the Greco-Roman world, kurios was a familiar term used to describe someone with authority, legal ownership, or dominion. It designated heads of households, leaders, landholders, and high officials. When Jewish translators rendered the Old Testament into Greek, they used kurios for the divine name of God. This established the word as an expression of divine majesty, absolute sovereignty, and holy authority.

By the time the New Testament was written, calling Jesus kurios carried profound weight. In a political culture where Roman emperors claimed supreme lordly honor, declaring Jesus as Lord publicly identified believers as subjects of a higher, heavenly King. The title declared that Jesus possessed divine authority over life, death, and the church.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of kurios is an owner, master, or person holding authority. Depending on the context, the word can refer to:

  • A master or owner of servants, property, or livestock.

  • A person holding social or political authority, such as a ruler or guardian.

  • A polite term of respect, equivalent to "sir."

  • God the Father, expressing His power and rule over creation.

  • Jesus Christ, highlighting His divinity, resurrection authority, and sovereign headship over the church.

In Philippians 2:29, kurios refers directly to the Lord Jesus Christ. The expression "in the Lord" marks the spiritual sphere and authority under which Christians interact with one another.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the New Testament, kurios appears in both ordinary and deeply theological settings. In everyday conversations, people used it as a polite address to superiors or strangers. In parables, it describes vineyard owners or masters overseeing household servants.

When applied to Jesus, kurios takes on supreme spiritual importance. It signifies that Jesus was raised from the dead, exalted to God's right hand, and given total authority over heaven and earth. Believers call Him Lord to confess His deity and yield their lives to His command.

The phrase "in the Lord" is frequently used by Paul to describe the spiritual union Christians have with Christ. Actions performed "in the Lord"—whether obeying authorities, serving others, or extending Christian hospitality—are done out of devotion to Christ and in harmony with His authority.

4. Theological Implications

The title kurios emphasizes that salvation involves both trusting Jesus as Savior and submitting to Him as Lord. Confessing Jesus as Lord means recognizing that He owns believers by right of creation and redemption. Every aspect of Christian living flows from this submission to His sovereign will.

Furthermore, Christ's lordship reshapes how Christians treat one another. Because believers belong to the same Lord, their relationships are no longer governed by personal preferences or earthly status. Welcoming a fellow Christian "in the Lord" demonstrates that Christian fellowship is rooted in mutual commitment to Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2, Paul highlights Timothy and Epaphroditus as self-sacrificing servants of the gospel. Epaphroditus had been sent by the Philippian church to assist Paul in prison. While serving, Epaphroditus suffered a severe illness that nearly cost him his life. After God restored his health, Paul sent him back to Philippi so the church could be relieved and joyful.

In Philippians 2:29, Paul tells the believers, "Receive him therefore in the Lord with all gladness, and hold such men in esteem." The word kurios frames their welcome not as a simple social gathering, but as a spiritual act centered on Christ. Grammatically, the word appears in a dative singular masculine form that connects the action of receiving Epaphroditus directly to Christ as the ground and setting of their fellowship.

Welcoming Epaphroditus "in the Lord" meant receiving him as Christ's own servant, honoring the sacrifice he made for the work of Christ. It elevated his return from a routine homecoming into an act of worship and brotherly honor under Christ's authority.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 6:68 — "But Simon Peter answered Him, 'Lord, to whom shall we go? You have the words of eternal life.'"

  • John 20:28 — "And Thomas answered and said to Him, 'My Lord and my God!'"

  • Romans 14:6 — "He who observes the day, observes it to the Lord; and he who does not observe the day, to the Lord he does not observe it."

  • Ephesians 6:9 — "And you, masters, do the same things to them, giving up threatening, knowing that your own Master also is in heaven, and there is no partiality with Him."

  • 1 Peter 2:3 — "if indeed you have tasted that the Lord is gracious."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The directive to receive Epaphroditus "in the Lord" shows that honoring Christian workers is part of honoring Christ. Epaphroditus risked his life to serve Paul, and Paul wanted the Philippians to recognize that faithful service to Christ deserves high honor. When believers welcome and support those who serve the Lord, they express their honor for the Master who sent them.
