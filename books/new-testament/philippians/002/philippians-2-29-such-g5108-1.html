SUCH - (toioûtos, Strong's G5108)

Summary:

The word toioutos is a demonstrative word meaning "of such a kind," "such a person," or "of this character." It points back to a set of qualities or an example already described, grouping individuals into a recognizable category based on shared traits or actions. In Philippians 2:29, Paul uses the word to instruct the church to honor not only Epaphroditus as an individual, but all believers who display the same Christlike devotion, selflessness, and sacrificial service for the gospel.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word toioutos functions as a demonstrative pointer in the New Testament. Rather than naming an isolated individual or a generic concept, it highlights a specific pattern of character, action, or disposition. It communicates "person(s) of this sort" or "things of this kind." When used of people, it draws attention to the visible qualities that define a group or type of character.

In Philippians 2, Paul provides a series of living examples of the mind of Christ. After setting forth Jesus as the supreme model of self-emptying humility (Philippians 2:5–11), Paul points to Timothy as a faithful servant who sincerely cares for the church (Philippians 2:19–24), and then to Epaphroditus (Philippians 2:25–30). Epaphroditus was sent by the Philippians to care for Paul's physical needs in prison. While doing so, he fell so severely ill that he nearly died. Upon his recovery, Paul sent him back to Philippi and told the church to welcome him joyfully and hold "such" men in high honor.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word toioutos refers to a person, thing, or quality of a particular kind or character. It is used when an author wants to transition from a specific description to a general category of similar instances.

Depending on how it functions in a sentence, the word can mean:

  • A person of this particular character or description.

  • People who share a specific set of qualities or behaviors.

  • Things or circumstances of a similar nature.

In Philippians 2:29, the word is expressed in the accusative case, plural number, and masculine gender. Grammatically, it serves as the direct object of the command to hold in honor. The masculine plural form means "people of this character" or "such men," referring to servants who share Epaphroditus's selfless spirit.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, toioutos serves several key functions depending on its context:

  • Identifying Kingdom Character: Jesus uses the word to describe the trust and humility that mark those who belong to God's kingdom, such as when He welcomes little children and declares that the kingdom belongs to "such" as these.

  • Classifying Behavior or Judgments: The word often identifies specific patterns of behavior—whether righteous or sinful—that fall under divine evaluation or apostolic instruction.

  • Calling for Recognition and Honor: Paul uses the word when commanding churches to recognize, submit to, or esteem leaders and fellow workers whose lives display sacrificial labor for Christ.

The word moves the reader's attention beyond one single person or event, setting up that person or event as a representative example for others to recognize or follow.

4. Theological Implications

By using toioutos in Philippians 2:29, Paul establishes an important principle regarding honor and spiritual leadership in the church. Epaphroditus was not an apostle or a famous church planter; he was an ordinary believer dispatched by his local congregation to deliver financial support and minister to Paul's physical needs. Yet, because he risked his life for the work of Christ, Paul elevates his conduct as a model of Christlike humility.

Theologically, this demonstrates that Christlike character—specifically sacrificial love and faithful service—is the true standard for spiritual honor in the body of Christ. Honor is not reserved exclusively for prominent leaders or miraculous giftedness. God values those who quietly lay down their lives, comfort, and safety for the sake of the gospel and the good of the saints.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:25–30, Paul explains his decision to send Epaphroditus back to his home church in Philippi. Epaphroditus had fallen ill to the point of death, and he was deeply distressed because the Philippians had heard about his sickness. Paul describes God's mercy in sparing Epaphroditus's life, which also spared Paul from overwhelming grief.

When Paul instructs the church, "Receive him therefore in the Lord with all gladness, and hold such in esteem," the word toioutos performs a vital transition. If Paul had only commanded them to receive Epaphroditus, the church might have viewed Epaphroditus's sacrifice as a unique, isolated act of devotion. By adding "and hold such in esteem," Paul broadens the instruction. He tells the Philippians to create a culture within the church that recognizes, values, and honors anyone who demonstrates this kind of gospel-centered selflessness.

The accusative plural masculine form directly identifies the group to be honored: those men and servants who consistently walk in self-sacrificing service.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 19:14 — "But Jesus said, 'Let the little children come to Me, and do not forbid them; for of such is the kingdom of heaven.'"

  • Acts 26:29 — "And Paul said, 'I would to God that not only you, but also all who hear me today, might become both almost and altogether such as I am, except for these chains.'"

  • 2 Corinthians 3:4 — "And we have such trust through Christ toward God."

  • 1 Corinthians 16:16 — "that you also submit to such, and to everyone who works and labors with us."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The placement of Epaphroditus in Philippians 2 creates a deliberate literary structure. Paul begins the chapter with the doctrine of Christ's humiliation and exaltation (Philippians 2:5–11), proceeds to Timothy's proven character (Philippians 2:19–24), and finishes with Epaphroditus's life-threatening service (Philippians 2:25–30).

By using toioutos at the conclusion of this section, Paul connects Epaphroditus directly back to the overarching theme of the chapter: having the mind of Christ. Epaphroditus became a living, visible picture of Christ's self-giving love in ordinary church life. Paul insists that churches must actively treasure and honor servants who live out the gospel in this practical, self-sacrificing way.
