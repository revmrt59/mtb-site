CONCEIT - (kenodoxía, Strong's G2754)

Summary:

The word kenodoxia refers to empty pride, groundless conceit, or the pursuit of hollow glory and recognition. In Philippians 2:3, Paul uses this word to warn the church against letting a desire for personal praise drive their actions, calling them instead to lay aside self-promotion in favor of humble concern for one another.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word kenodoxia is built from two common Greek roots: kenos, meaning empty or devoid of reality, and doxa, meaning glory, praise, or honor. Historically, the word described a pursuit of status or acclaim that possessed no real substance, value, or lasting foundation. It pointed to an inflated opinion of oneself that demanded recognition from others despite having nothing of eternal worth to offer.

In the New Testament, Paul writes to the believers in Philippi to encourage unity and mutual care within the church family. Their fellowship was threatened by subtle temptations toward rivalry, self-assertion, and personal prominence. By introducing this term into his instruction, Paul highlights the underlying motive that often fuels division among God's people: an appetite for hollow personal honor.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Kenodoxia can refer to empty pride, excessive self-conceit, vainglory, or a desire for recognition that lacks genuine value. It describes an attitude that seeks to draw attention to oneself, demanding praise and respect from human observers.

In Philippians 2:3, the noun appears in the accusative case, singular number, and feminine gender. Grammatically, it functions as one of the negative motives that must be completely absent from Christian action. Paul does not merely warn against open conflict; he exposes the internal motive of seeking empty glory as a root cause of disharmony.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

This specific term is rare in biblical literature, appearing only once in the Greek New Testament. While related terms describe empty things or legitimate glory, kenodoxia focuses precisely on the foolishness of human self-exaltation.

Scripture frequently contrasts human opinion with divine truth. Genuine honor comes from God and reflects His character, whereas kenodoxia represents a chasing after human applause. It is an empty glory because it depends on passing human praise, rests on an exaggerated view of self, and achieves nothing of eternal worth.

4. Theological Implications

Theological reflection on kenodoxia emphasizes the deep discrepancy between human pride and divine truth. Seeking empty glory demonstrates a heart that measures worth by visible recognition rather than God's approval. When personal praise becomes a primary motivation, believers begin to view fellow Christians as rivals rather than brothers and sisters in Christ.

Furthermore, seeking empty glory directly contradicts the pattern of Jesus Christ revealed in the surrounding passage. While humans naturally tend to puff themselves up to gain empty honor, Christ possessed true divine glory yet emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant. The pursuit of hollow pride is therefore fundamentally incompatible with the heart of the gospel and the self-giving example of the Savior.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:3, Paul writes, "Let nothing be done through selfish ambition or conceit, but in lowliness of mind let each esteem others better than himself." The word translated "conceit" (kenodoxia) works alongside "selfish ambition" (eritheia) to paint a complete picture of self-centered ministry:

  • Selfish ambition represents the aggressive drive to achieve personal advantage or factional dominance.

  • Conceit represents the internal vanity and desire for hollow praise that feeds that drive.

Together, these two motives poison Christian fellowship. Paul’s instruction is thorough: "Let nothing be done" through these corrupting influences. The antidote Paul provides in the same sentence is "lowliness of mind"—a deliberate, humble mindset that views others as worthy of honor and care. Rather than reaching upward for empty recognition, the believer is called to step downward in humble service.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • This term is unique to this context and does not appear elsewhere in the biblical corpus.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The danger of empty glory lies in its subtlety. A person may engage in outwardly good acts, public leadership, or sacrificial service, yet remain internally motivated by a desire to be noticed and praised. Kenodoxia exposes the truth that the value of an action is deeply tied to the motive behind it. When ministry or good works are performed for personal applause, they lose their spiritual integrity and produce friction rather than unity.
