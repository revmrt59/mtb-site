ESTEEM - (hēgéomai, Strong's G2233)

Summary:

The Greek verb hegeomai primarily means to guide or lead, and in a mental sense, to evaluate, consider, or count something to be true after careful thought. In Philippians 2:3, Paul uses the word to instruct believers to deliberately evaluate others as better than themselves in lowliness of mind, shaping how Christians value and treat one another in the church community.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The apostle Paul wrote his letter to the Philippians to encourage joy, endurance, and practical unity within the local church. A central obstacle to church unity is human pride, which leads to division and rivalry. In Philippians 2:1–4, Paul confronts this problem directly by urging believers to share the same mind and love.

The word translated "esteem" in Philippians 2:3 is hegeomai. In ancient usage, this verb carried two related ideas: leading or governing people on the one hand, and leading one's own mind to a settled conclusion on the other. It describes an active mental process where a person weighs evidence or principles and arrives at a deliberate assessment. Paul uses this word to show that Christian humility is not merely a passing feeling, but a intentional way of thinking about and valuing others.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb hegeomai can refer to outward leadership, describing a chief officer, ruler, or leader who guides others.

It also refers to an inward mental judgment. In this sense, it means to consider, count, regard, think, or hold a settled opinion about something. It implies a conscious decision to judge a person, event, or object in a specific way based on intentional evaluation rather than unthinking impression or emotional impulse.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, hegeomai is used in two primary ways:

First, it is used for governing or leading. In historical and administrative settings, it describes those who hold leadership roles, such as rulers, governors, or church overseers who guide a congregation.

Second, it is used for mental evaluation. In these passages, the word describes an intentional act of counting or considering something to be true. For example, a person might count earthly privileges as loss, consider a circumstance an opportunity, or judge God to be faithful.

In Philippians 2:3, the word appears as a present middle participle in the nominative plural masculine form. The present tense describes a continuous attitude that ought to mark the Christian life, while the middle voice describes the subjects as actively participating in this mental assessment.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical call to "esteem" others in humility carries significant theological meaning:

  • Humility is cognitive and intentional. True Christian humility is not an involuntary feeling or a lack of confidence. It is a deliberate choice to evaluate people through the lens of God's grace.

  • Christian unity requires active value judgments. Believers must intentionally decide to value their brothers and sisters in Christ, intentionally putting aside selfish ambition and empty conceit.

  • Human self-centeredness is displaced by Christlike assessment. Left to themselves, human beings naturally evaluate themselves as primary and others as secondary. Scripture calls for a deliberate reversal of this natural pride.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:3, Paul contrasts two opposing mindsets: "let nothing be done through selfish ambition or conceit, but in lowliness of mind let each esteem others better than himself."

The surrounding passage makes clear what Paul means by "esteeming" others as better than oneself. He is not demanding that believers pretend others possess skills, knowledge, or moral perfection that they do not actually have. Instead, "better" in this context carries the sense of placing higher importance on the needs, dignity, and interests of others.

The present participle "esteeming" works directly with Paul's call for lowliness of mind. Humility begins in how a person thinks. By commanding believers to "esteem" others in this way, Paul requires a conscious mental decision to treat fellow believers as worthy of priority, setting the stage for the supreme example of Christ's self-giving love in Philippians 2:5–11.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Acts 26:2 — NKJV Span: "think"

Paul uses the word when he tells King Agrippa that he "thinks" himself happy to make his defense, expressing a deliberate mental assessment of his opportunity.

  • Philippians 2:6 — NKJV Span: "consider"

In the hymn describing Christ's incarnation, Jesus did not "consider" equality with God something to be seized or held onto for self-interest.

  • Philippians 3:8 — NKJV Span: "count"

Paul explains how he "counts" all things as loss for the excellence of the knowledge of Christ Jesus his Lord.

  • Hebrews 11:26 — NKJV Span: "esteeming"

Moses is described as "esteeming" the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures in Egypt, making a conscious decision of value based on faith.

  • Hebrews 13:17 — NKJV Span: "rule"

The writer uses the word in its leadership sense to refer to spiritual leaders who "rule" over the flock and watch out for their souls.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding hegeomai helps correct common misconceptions about Christian humility. Worldly thinking often defines humility as feeling bad about oneself or minimizing one's God-given abilities. However, because hegeomai involves deliberate mental evaluation, biblical humility is actually an accurate, God-centered assessment of reality.

It acknowledges one's own dependence on divine grace while actively choosing to value others and serve their practical needs. How Christians treat one another in the local church flows directly from how they deliberately choose to evaluate one another in their minds.
