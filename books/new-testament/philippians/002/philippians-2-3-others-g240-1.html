OTHERS - (allḗlōn, Strong's G240)

Summary:

Allelōn is a Greek reciprocal pronoun that indicates mutual or shared action between parties, translated as "one another," "each other," or "others." In Philippians 2:3, Paul uses this word in a direct instruction to the church, calling believers to actively esteem "others" as better than themselves. By using this reciprocal pronoun as the object of high regard, Paul highlights the essential mindset required for Christian unity: believers are called to turn their focus away from selfish ambition and direct honor and care toward their brothers and sisters in Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word allelōn functions in Greek as a reciprocal pronoun. It naturally points to a relationship where actions, attitudes, or responsibilities are directed mutually among members of a group. Unlike standard personal pronouns that simply identify a specific individual, a reciprocal pronoun highlights interpersonal interaction.

In the ancient Greco-Roman world, social life was heavily structured around personal honor, civic status, and self-promotion. In that environment, individuals were encouraged to seek recognition and advancement for themselves above others. In contrast, New Testament writers frequently used this pronoun to define a distinct way of living within the church—one characterized by mutual love, honor, service, and humility among believers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The pronoun allelōn means "one another," "each other," or "others." It describes action that moves between individuals within a community. Depending on the surrounding context, it can describe mutual love, shared encouragement, mutual forbearance, or the directional focus of a believer's care. In Philippians 2:3, the word is translated "others" and serves as the object of how believers should evaluate their fellow members in the body of Christ.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the New Testament, allelōn plays a prominent role in describing community life among Christians. It is the underlying word in the familiar "one another" commands that form the blueprint for church fellowship. Scripture uses the word when commanding believers to love one another, forgive one another, bear one another's burdens, and speak truth to one another.

The pronoun itself does not create the moral duty; rather, it identifies the target toward which the action must be directed. It demonstrates that Christian faith is never intended to be lived in isolated individualism. The word constantly directs the Christian's attention outward toward fellow believers in the local assembly.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical instruction to prioritize "others" reflects the character of God's kingdom and the example of Jesus Christ. Left to itself, human nature naturally leans toward self-interest and self-preservation. The Gospel breaks this self-centered focus. When Scripture commands believers to value others, it grounds this ethic in the self-giving sacrifice of Christ, who laid down His life for His people.

This reveals that true spiritual maturity is measured in how Christians relate to those around them. Godly love is not merely an internal feeling or an abstract ideal; it expresses itself in concrete acts of honor, patience, and practical service toward fellow members of the body of Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:3, Paul writes: "Let nothing be done through selfish ambition or conceit, but in lowliness of mind let each esteem others better than himself." Grammatically, the target occurrence of allelōn is a masculine plural form in the accusative case, functioning as the direct object of the verb translated "esteem" or "value."

Paul sets up a direct contrast in this verse. On one side are "selfish ambition" and "conceit"—attitudes that put personal glory at the center. On the other side is "lowliness of mind," a humble disposition that looks outward.

In this context, Paul uses allelōn to specify where this humble evaluation must land: on fellow believers in the congregation. Believers are not called to despise themselves, but rather to intentionally elevate the dignity and needs of others above their own personal preferences. In a local church facing potential friction, Paul calls every Christian to view their brothers and sisters with high regard, placing their welfare ahead of self-interest.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • John 13:34 — "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another; as I have loved you, that you also love another."

  • Romans 12:10 — "Be kindly affectionate to another with brotherly love, in honor giving preference to one another."

  • Ephesians 4:25 — "Therefore, putting away lying, 'Let each one of you speak truth with his neighbor,' for we are members of another."

  • Hebrews 10:24 — "And let us consider another in order to stir up love and good works."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The instruction regarding "others" in Philippians 2:3 provides the practical pathway toward true Christian unity. Just prior to this verse, Paul urges the Philippian believers to be "like-minded, having the same love, being of one accord, of one mind" (Philippians 2:2).

Unity in a local church is not produced through external coercion or superficial agreement. It grows when individual members adopt genuine humility. When believers consistently value others above themselves, personal rivalries lose their power. Directing attention and honor toward others builds a healthy, Christ-centered fellowship where the gospel is clearly demonstrated to the watching world.
