THAN - (hyperéchō, Strong's G5242)

Summary:

The Greek verb hyperechō means to surpass, excel, exceed, or hold a position above another. In Philippians 2:3, translated in the phrase "better than," the word describes fellow believers as excelling or surpassing oneself in value and consideration, calling Christians to view others as deserving of higher honor and care than themselves.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word hyperechō combines two basic terms: hyper, meaning "above" or "beyond," and echō, meaning "to hold" or "to have." In ancient Greek usage, the word carried the idea of rising above, excelling, standing out, or holding a position of higher rank or superior quality.

In civic and political settings, ancient writers used the word for rulers and officials who held elevated rank or authority over others. In general conversation, it described anything that surpassed ordinary limits or excelled in beauty, value, or strength. In the New Testament, the term describes earthly authorities, the supreme worth of knowing Christ, God's peace that exceeds human understanding, and the humble opinion Christians ought to hold regarding fellow believers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Hyperechō means to surpass, excel, be superior, or hold a position above another.

When applied to civil rulers or leaders, it means to hold governing authority or supreme position. When applied to qualities or concepts, it means to exceed, surpass, or far outweigh in value or power. In Philippians 2:3, the word appears as a participle describing "others." It refers to regarding other people as excelling, surpassing, or standing above oneself in personal worth, dignity, and practical importance.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses hyperechō across a few distinct contexts, each highlighting a form of superiority or surpassing value:

  • Civil Authority: It describes governmental powers that occupy higher office and exercise authority within human society.

  • Matchless Value: It describes the supreme, unsurpassable worth of knowing Jesus Christ, compared to which all human achievements and credentials are loss.

  • Divine Transcendance: It describes the peace of God, which far exceeds human comprehension and mental capacity.

  • Relational Humility: In church life, it turns worldly social competition upside down. Instead of striving to rise above others, Christians are called to view others as surpassing themselves in honor and priority.

4. Theological Implications

Human nature in a fallen world is prone to pride, self-promotion, and social rivalry. People naturally measure themselves against others to claim personal superiority. In Philippians 2, Paul confronts this self-centered tendency by forbidding conduct driven by selfish ambition or empty conceit.

Gospel humility reverses this worldly pattern. By calling believers to regard others as "surpassing" (hyperechontas), Scripture does not ask Christians to deny their own God-given gifts or pretend that others are perfect. Rather, it requires a heart attitude that actively recognizes the worth of others, delights in their strengths, and prioritizes their needs. This mindset reflects the self-giving love of Jesus Christ, who put the needs of others above His own privilege.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:3, Paul writes: "in lowliness of mind let each esteem others better than themselves."

The target word hyperechontas is an accusative participle that grammatically modifies "others" (allēlous). It describes the condition or standing attributed to others in the believer's estimation—namely, that others are regarded as surpassing or excelling oneself. The action of "esteeming" or "considering" comes from a neighboring verb (hēgoumenoi). The target word specifies how those others are to be evaluated: as having higher worth and priority.

This instruction directly counters the factions and personal rivalries that threatened the unity of the Philippian congregation. When every member of a local church views others as surpassing themselves in importance, pride is humbled, selfless service flourishes, and gospel unity is preserved.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 13:1 — "Let every soul be subject to the governing authorities." Here the word describes civil rulers who hold higher position and authority in society.

  • Philippians 3:8 — "Yet indeed I also count all things loss for the excellence of the knowledge of Christ Jesus my Lord." Here the word highlights the supreme, surpassing value of knowing Christ.

  • Philippians 4:7 — "and the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and minds through Christ Jesus." Here the word refers to divine peace exceeding human mental comprehension.

  • 1 Peter 2:13 — "Therefore submit yourselves to every ordinance of man for the Lord’s sake, whether to the king as supreme." Here the word highlights the king's top political rank.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In first-century Greco-Roman culture, social life revolved around honor, public recognition, and personal status. Winning honor over rivals was considered the ultimate goal of public life.

Paul's instruction in Philippians 2:3 radically redefines social interaction within the body of Christ. Instead of seeking personal advancement at the expense of others, Christians are called to accord higher honor to their brothers and sisters. This Christlike posture transforms human relationships and protects the church from friction and division.
