CHRIST - (Christós, Strong's G5547)

Summary:

The word Christos means "Anointed One" and is the Greek counterpart to the Hebrew term for Messiah. In the New Testament, it designates Jesus as God's chosen Prophet, Priest, and King. In Philippians 2:30, the word describes the sacrificial mission undertaken by Epaphroditus, showing that his dangerous physical service to the apostle Paul was ultimately performed directly for the cause and realm of King Jesus.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Greek noun Christos comes from a verb meaning to anoint or rub with oil. In the ancient world, anointing was a sacred ceremony used to consecrate individuals for special leadership roles. In Old Testament history, kings, priests, and occasional prophets were anointed with oil to mark them as appointed by God for sacred service. When Jewish scholars translated the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, they used Christos to render the Hebrew term Mashiach (Messiah). By the first century, Christos pointed specifically to the promised Deliverer who would bring God's kingdom, fulfill His covenant promises, and reign as eternal King.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Christos refers to one who has been anointed and appointed by God. In the New Testament, it functions both as an official title ("the Christ") and as a personal name ("Jesus Christ" or simply "Christ"). The word carries the combined ideas of divine selection, authority, and consecration. Rather than describing a general spiritual leader, Christos specifies Jesus of Nazareth as God's unique, anointed Savior. In gospel proclamation and apostolic letters, the term emphasizes Jesus' supreme authority over the church and creation.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Gospels, Christos often appears with the definite article as a title that people debated: "Is this the Christ?" In the Epistles, the word is used extensively to define the identity, ministry, and lordship of Jesus. Paul frequently pairs the word with prepositional phrases, such as "in Christ" or "servant of Christ," to describe the absolute authority Jesus holds over those who belong to Him. The term highlights that Christian life and ministry are not merely tied to an abstract philosophy or institution, but to a living, anointed King.

4. Theological Implications

The title Christos anchors the identity of Jesus in the overarching story of redemption. Calling Jesus the Christ affirms that He fulfills the law, the prophets, and the royal promises made to David. Because Jesus is the Anointed One, all true Christian labor derives its authority and value from Him. Service rendered to His ministers or His church is recognized by Scripture as service rendered to Christ Himself. Laboring for the gospel is therefore understood as participation in the work of God's appointed King.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:30, Paul commends Epaphroditus, a messenger sent by the Philippian church to bring financial relief and personal aid to Paul in prison. Epaphroditus became gravely ill during this journey, coming near death. Paul describes his life-threatening mission as "the work of Christ."

Grammatically, the word appears in the genitive case, singular number, and masculine gender, indicating that the work belongs to and centers upon Christ. Epaphroditus was carrying out practical, physical care for a human apostle, yet Paul elevates this act by calling it the work of Christ. This choice of vocabulary demonstrates that practical acts of mercy, financial support, and personal assistance toward gospel ministry are counted by God as direct service to King Jesus. Epaphroditus risked his physical survival because he viewed the work of the Anointed King as more valuable than his own comfort.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Philippians 2, Paul sets forth Jesus as the supreme example of self-emptying humility and service (Philippians 2:5–11). He then provides two real-life examples of ministers who reflected this same selfless heart: Timothy and Epaphroditus. By explicitly calling Epaphroditus’s labor "the work of Christ," Paul ties human sacrificial service directly back to the supreme model of Christ's own sacrifice. The self-giving spirit of the Anointed One reproducing itself in His followers turns ordinary acts of service into sacred gospel work.
