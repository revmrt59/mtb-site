CLOSE - (engízō, Strong's G1448)

Summary:

The verb engizō means to draw near, approach, or come close to a person, place, time, or state. In Philippians 2:30, the word describes the severe physical crisis of Epaphroditus, who came to the very brink of death while carrying out his mission of service to Paul on behalf of the Philippian church.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb engizō is derived from the Greek word engus, which means "near." In ancient Greek, the term expressed physical movement that reduced the distance between two points, whether approaching a geographical location, a person, or a specific moment in time.

In the New Testament setting, the word carries both spatial and temporal meanings. It describes Jesus approaching a town, a crowd coming near to listen to teaching, the kingdom of God drawing near to humanity, or a believer approaching God in worship. In Philippians 2:30, the word is used in a physical sense to describe someone reaching the threshold of a critical bodily condition—drawing near to death itself.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Engizō means to draw near, approach, or come close. Depending on the surrounding context, the word refers to:

  • Physical approach: Moving spatially toward a person, city, or destination.

  • Temporal arrival: An event, time, or era coming near or arriving.

  • Reaching a physical boundary: Coming close to a specific health condition or state of body.

In Philippians 2:30, engizō is used in this third sense. Epaphroditus did not merely experience light sickness; his illness progressed until he came close to dying. The verb establishes the physical reality that he reached the outer boundary of life.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses engizō across several distinct settings:

  • Spatial proximity: The word frequently describes people moving toward a destination or coming close to Jesus during His earthly ministry.

  • Kingdom arrival: In the Gospels, the word marks the arrival of God's reign brought near in the person and ministry of Jesus Christ.

  • Spiritual access: In the New Testament letters and Hebrews, the verb describes the movement of the believer's heart drawing near to God in prayer, faith, and fellowship.

  • Physical extremity: In Philippians 2:30, the word marks physical vulnerability, recording how close a servant of God came to losing his life in gospel work.

In all these settings, engizō highlights the narrowing of space or time until the subject stands at the very edge of what is being approached.

4. Theological Implications

The mention that Epaphroditus came close to death highlights the genuine human cost and physical strain that often accompanied gospel ministry in the early church. Epaphroditus traveled a long distance from Philippi to Rome to bring support to Paul in prison. His life-threatening illness was not a sign of divine disfavor, but a consequence of sacrificial service.

Paul's description shows that God's servants are not immune to sickness or physical exhaustion. At the same time, the broader passage records that God had mercy on Epaphroditus and spared his life. This demonstrates both human weakness in the work of the ministry and God's compassionate care over His workers. Epaphroditus's experience reflects the self-giving mindset modeled by Christ earlier in Philippians 2, where faithful service involves risking comfort and life for the sake of others.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:25–30, Paul writes to prepare the Philippians for Epaphroditus's return. Epaphroditus had been sent by the church in Philippi to care for Paul's needs in prison, but he became gravely ill. Paul instructs the congregation to receive Epaphroditus with all gladness and to honor men like him.

In verse 30, Paul explains why Epaphroditus deserves such honor: "because for the work of Christ he came close to death, not regarding his life, to supply what was lacking in your service toward me."

The verb engizō provides the factual basis for Paul's commendation: Epaphroditus physically drew near to death. The broader sentence supplies the devotion behind the act, explaining that he risked his life to fulfill the service the Philippians could not perform in person. The verb itself simply records how near Epaphroditus came to dying, emphasizing the severity of his illness during his mission.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 10:9 — "The kingdom of God has come near to you." Here engizō describes the presence and arrival of God's reign through the ministry of Jesus' messengers.

  • Luke 18:40 — "Then Jesus stood and commanded him to be brought to Him. And when he had come near, He asked him..." Here the verb indicates physical approach as a blind man is brought close to Jesus.

  • Acts 9:3 — "As he journeyed he came near Damascus, and suddenly a light shone around him from heaven." Here the word marks spatial proximity as Saul approached the city.

  • Hebrews 7:19 — "...on the other hand, there is the bringing in of a better hope, through which we draw near to God." Here engizō describes spiritual movement into fellowship with God through Christ.

  • James 4:8 — "Draw near to God and He will draw near to you." Here the verb calls believers to seek God in repentance and devotions, promising His responsive presence.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's explanation of Epaphroditus's near-fatal illness serves an important pastoral function. It reassures the Philippians that Epaphroditus was not returning home early due to failure, fear, or lack of effort. Instead, he was returning because he had poured out his energy to the point of extreme physical jeopardy.

By noting that Epaphroditus came close to death for the work of Christ, Paul gives a practical example of the sacrificial unity and humbleness of mind described in Philippians 2:1–11. Epaphroditus lived out the principle of looking out for the interests of others above his own.
