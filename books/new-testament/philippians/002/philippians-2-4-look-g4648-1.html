LOOK - (skopéō, Strong's G4648)

Summary:

The Greek word skopeo means to fix one's eyes upon, pay close attention to, or keep a target in view. In Philippians 2:4, Paul commands believers to direct their attention toward the needs and interests of others rather than focusing exclusively on themselves.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek, skopeo described the action of looking attentively at a goal or target. It suggested intentional focus, like a watcher keeping a close eye on an horizon or an archer looking at a mark. The word implies a conscious directing of one's sight and mind toward a specific object or outcome rather than a casual glance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Skopeo means to look at, give attention to, consider, or observe carefully. Depending on the context, the word can refer to watching out for potential dangers, keeping a spiritually worthy goal in view, or paying close attention to the needs of fellow believers.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the New Testament, skopeo consistently describes intentional mental or spiritual focus. It is used when Jesus warns hearers to pay close attention to their inner light, when Paul exhorts believers to focus on invisible eternal realities, and when Christians are instructed to observe godly examples in the church. The term highlights what a person chooses to keep in front of their eyes as a priority or guiding concern.

4. Theological Implications

Paul places skopeo within a passage that presents the posture of Jesus Christ as the supreme pattern for Christian community. In Philippians 2, living in Christian unity requires a fundamental reorientation of attention. Naturally, human beings focus primarily on their own safety, comfort, and preferences. Paul shows that spiritual unity grows when believers direct deliberate care and interest toward the spiritual and physical well-being of their brothers and sisters in Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:4, Paul writes, "Let each of you look out not only for his own interests, but also for the interests of others." The plural command addresses the whole congregation, calling every member to participate in mutual care.

Paul does not demand that Christians completely neglect legitimate personal responsibilities. The phrasing "not only... but also" assumes that people naturally care for their own survival and daily needs. However, Paul instructs believers to extend that same active attention to the needs of others in the local fellowship. By doing so, the church models the self-giving humility of Christ described in the verses that follow.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 11:35 — "Take heed therefore that the light which is in you is not darkness." Here the word is rendered "heed," calling for careful spiritual examination of one's own heart and understanding.

  • 2 Corinthians 4:18 — "while we do not look at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen." Here translated "look," describing a conscious focus on unseen eternal truths despite temporary physical affliction.

  • Galatians 6:1 — "considering yourself lest you also be tempted." Translated "considering," directing a believer who restores a fallen brother to keep a watchful eye on their own spiritual vulnerability.

  • Philippians 3:17 — "note those who so walk, as you have us for a pattern." Translated "note," instructing the church to pay attention to faithful believers whose lives provide a trustworthy example.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The English word "scope" comes from skopeo, preserved in words like "telescope" or "microscope," which describe instruments designed to bring specific objects into clear view. This background highlights how Paul uses the word in Philippians 2:4. Spiritual selfishness functions like blinders, keeping a person's view restricted to their own life. By contrast, Paul instructs believers to widen their scope, keeping the needs of the church body clearly in view.
