OTHERS - (héteros, Strong's G2087)

Summary:

The Greek word heteros means "other," "another," or a distinct person or thing. In Philippians 2:4, it refers to fellow human beings, specifically fellow believers in the church community. Paul uses this word to direct the believers' care and attention outward beyond themselves, establishing that genuine Christian unity requires actively looking out for the needs and interests of fellow members of Christ's body.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Paul wrote his letter to the church at Philippi while imprisoned for the gospel. Despite his physical confinement, his primary pastoral concern was the spiritual health, steadfastness, and unity of the Philippian congregation. In the second chapter, Paul urges the believers to live in harmony, warned against selfish ambition and conceit, and calls them to adopt the humble mindset of Jesus Christ.

In Philippians 2:4, Paul addresses the practical focus of daily relationships within the Christian community. Unity is not merely an abstract concept; it is expressed when individuals stop focusing exclusively on their own concerns and turn their attention to the welfare of those around them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

around the fundamental idea of distinction. The word can mean another person or thing besides the one already mentioned. In some contexts, it refers to another of a different kind or quality, while in other contexts it simply distinguishes one individual or group from another.

When used as a noun in the plural, as it is here, the word refers to "others"—distinct human beings who share a common environment, setting, or community. In this verse, the word functions as a plural noun in the genitive case, masculine gender, designating fellow believers whose lives and needs stand alongside one's own.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the New Testament, the word is used in several distinct settings:

  • Distinguishing between two individuals or choices, where one person or path is contrasted with another.

  • Identifying additional members of a group or mission, such as workers or companions sent out together.

  • Pointing to the neighbor or fellow human being in moral and ethical instructions.

In teaching contexts, the word often serves to pivot a listener's attention away from the self and toward the neighbor. It reminds the reader that God's moral commands rarely operate in isolation; they consistently involve how one relates to the distinct individuals God has placed nearby.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical command to care for others roots Christian behavior in the character and work of Jesus Christ. Human nature naturally defaults to self-preservation and personal advancement. By calling believers to consider others, Scripture challenges self-centered living and reorients the heart toward sacrificial love.

This focus on others immediately prepares the reader for the passage that follows in Philippians 2:5–11. There, Paul presents Jesus Christ as the supreme example of selflessness. Christ did not insist on His own privileges, but emptied Himself and took the form of a servant for the salvation of others. Therefore, looking out for the interests of fellow believers is not a human strategy for social harmony; it is the visible reflection of Christ's redeeming love at work in the church.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:4, Paul commands: "Let each of you look out not only for his own interests, but also for the interests of others." The phrasing is carefully balanced. Paul does not condemn proper attention to one's legitimate personal responsibilities ("not only for his own interests"). Rather, he prohibits a narrow, self-absorbed mindset that ignores the burdens, needs, and spiritual growth of fellow Christians.

The target word "others" stands in the genitive plural, identifying the group whose interests are to be actively considered. In a church body where personal rivalries or differing opinions could threaten fellowship, Paul directs each member to look outside their own desires. By placing the needs of others alongside personal concerns, the church guards its unity and demonstrates the humbling grace of the gospel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 10:1 – "After these things the Lord appointed seventy others also, and sent them two by two before His face into every city and place where He Himself was about to go."

  • Acts 4:12 – "Nor is there salvation in any other, for there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved."

  • Romans 13:8 – "Owe no one anything except to love one another, for he who loves another has fulfilled the law."

  • 1 Corinthians 6:1 – "Dare any of you, having a matter against another, go to law before the unrighteous, and not before the saints?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul’s instruction in verse 4 serves as a practical bridge between the call to humility in verse 3 ("let each esteem others better than himself") and the portrait of Christ's incarnation in verses 5–11. Caring for others is the practical expression of humility.

Without active concern for fellow believers, humility remains merely a theoretical idea. By directing the Philippians to look out for the interests of others, Paul shows that genuine faith manifests itself in active, outward-focused care within the fellowship of the church.
