OWN - (heautoû, Strong's G1438)

Summary:

The word heautou is a reflexive pronoun in ancient Greek, used to refer back to the subject of a sentence, meaning "himself," "herself," "itself," "themselves," or "one's own." In Philippians 2:4, it is rendered "own" in the phrase "his own interests." Here Paul uses the word to represent a person's individual affairs, concerns, and needs. Rather than forbidding legitimate attention to personal responsibilities, Paul uses the word to challenge believers to look beyond their own interests and actively care for the spiritual and practical needs of others in the Christian community.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word heautou is the standard Greek reflexive pronoun. It directs attention back to the actor or subject performing the action. Depending on its context and grammatical form, it is translated into English as "himself," "themselves," "yourselves," "ourselves," or "one's own."

In the Greco-Roman world of the first century, culture often emphasized personal honor, social standing, and looking after one's own household or faction. In writing to the church at Philippi, Paul addresses a community facing potential disunity and selfish ambition. To encourage harmony, Paul calls the church to adopt an attitude that stands in stark contrast to self-centered ambition, urging believers to consider others above themselves.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Heautou functions as a reflexive pronoun. It can mean:

  • "himself," "herself," "itself," or "themselves"

  • "of oneself" or "belonging to oneself"

  • "one's own" when referring to personal matters, possessions, or concerns

The word itself is descriptive rather than moral. It simply identifies that an action, concern, or object belongs to or centers on the subject. Whether focusing on oneself is constructive or selfish depends entirely on how the surrounding passage uses the term.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, heautou appears in a wide variety of contexts:

  • Personal identity and self-examination: It is used when people evaluate or examine themselves, as when Jesus asks His listeners to judge for "yourselves" what is right.

  • Private affairs and property: It refers to personal space or private belongings, such as disciples returning to their "own" homes after visiting the empty tomb.

  • Self-humbling and sacrifice: It describes personal actions directed inward or done willingly, such as Christ humbling "Himself" on the cross.

In pastoral instruction, the word helps draw a clear line between responsible self-care and exclusive self-preoccupation.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of heautou in ethical passages highlights key spiritual truths:

  • Balance in Christian care: Scripture recognizes that people naturally look after their basic food, clothing, and safety. Paul does not condemn personal responsibility. Instead, he uses natural personal concern as a reference point, calling Christians to display that same attentiveness toward others.

  • The posture of Christ: Self-interest left unchecked leads to rivalry and division. The gospel presents Jesus as the ultimate model of one who did not insist on His own privileges, but willingly gave Himself up for the good of His people.

  • Mutual care in the body of Christ: Healthy church life requires believers to step outside their immediate circle of personal concerns so that the entire fellowship is supported, valued, and loved.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:4, the word appears in the genitive plural masculine form. The verse reads: "Let each of you look out not only for his own interests, but also for the interests of others."

Paul uses a careful "not only... but also" structure. He assumes that believers must oversee their daily duties, work, family obligations, and personal well-being. The word "own" recognizes this practical necessity.

However, Paul warns against confining one's attention exclusively to personal concerns. When believers focus entirely on their own agenda, rivalry and empty conceit quickly ruin church unity. By placing "his own" side by side with "the interests of others," Paul shows that true Christian love expands a believer's focus to include the burdens, needs, and well-being of fellow Christians.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 12:57 — "Yes, and why, even of yourselves, do you not judge what is right?"

  • John 20:10 — "Then the disciples went away again to their own homes."

  • Romans 8:23 — "Not only that, but we also who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, eagerly waiting for the adoption, the redemption of our body."

  • Philippians 2:8 — "And being found in appearance as a man, He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross."

  • 2 Thessalonians 3:12 — "Now those who are such we command and exhort through our Lord Jesus Christ that they work in quietness and eat their own bread."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Philippians 2:4 serves as the transition to one of the most magnificent passages in the New Testament concerning the person of Christ (Philippians 2:5-11).

There is a meaningful literary contrast in how heautou is used in this chapter:

  • In verse 4, believers are warned not to look exclusively at their "own" interests.

  • In verse 8, Jesus provides the supreme demonstration of this teaching when He humbled "Himself" to die on the cross.

The exact same reflexive pronoun connects the practical command for church harmony with the profound act of redemption accomplished by Christ.
