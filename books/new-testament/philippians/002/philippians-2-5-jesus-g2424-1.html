JESUS - (Iēsoûs, Strong's G2424)

Summary:

The name Iēsous is the Greek form of the Hebrew name Yeshua or Yehoshua (Joshua), which means "Yahweh is salvation" or "Yahweh saves." In the New Testament, it serves as the personal name given to the incarnate Son of God at His birth by divine command. In Philippians 2:5, Paul uses the name within the phrase "Christ Jesus" to ground his call for humble, self-giving love in the historical person and actual earthly example of the Savior.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Iēsous carries deep roots in Israel's Old Testament history. It is the Greek transliteration of the Hebrew name Yeshua, a shortened form of Yehoshua, known in English as Joshua. Throughout the Old Testament, men bearing this name led God's people in key moments of deliverance, most notably Joshua the successor of Moses who led Israel into the Promised Land.

In first-century Jewish culture, the name was relatively common, reflecting a widespread longing among the Jewish people for God to intervene and deliver them from earthly oppression. However, when the angel commanded Joseph and Mary to name the child born in Bethlehem "Jesus," the name took on its full and ultimate purpose. It was given not as a expression of general human hope, but as a specific declaration of divine purpose: He was named Jesus because He would save His people from their sins.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name means "Yahweh saves" or "Yahweh is salvation." As a proper noun, it identifies a specific historical individual, Jesus of Nazareth.

While the name etymologically highlights salvation, its standard lexical function in text is simply to designate the person of Jesus. It identifies the real, historical human being who walked the earth, suffered, died, and rose again. When combined with titles like "Christ" (Anointed One) or "Lord," the personal name "Jesus" anchors those exalted titles to the real history of the incarnate Son.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, the name Iēsous appears in several distinct ways depending on the author and the context:

  • Historical Narrative: In the Gospels, "Jesus" is used continually to record His earthly ministry, highlighting His authentic humanity, His miraculous works, and His direct interactions with people.

  • Apostolic Preaching: In the Book of Acts, the apostles repeatedly proclaim "this Jesus" who was crucified, whom God raised from the dead and made both Lord and Christ.

  • Epistolary Designations: In the NT letters, the name often combines with His title to form "Christ Jesus" or "Jesus Christ." The title "Christ" highlights His divine office as Messiah, while "Jesus" preserves the reality of His personal earthly life and suffering.

4. Theological Implications

The proper name "Jesus" stands as a constant reminder of the Incarnation. God did not send an abstract principle or a distant set of moral guidelines to redeem humanity; He sent His Son into real human history as a real man.

The name connects divine initiative with human experience. Salvation is entirely the work of God ("Yahweh saves"), yet that salvation was accomplished through a genuine human life lived in perfect obedience. By bearing this human name, the Son of God identified fully with those He came to redeem.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:5, Paul writes, "Let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus." Grammatically, the name appears here as a masculine singular noun in the dative case, serving as the object of the preposition that links the believer's mindset directly to Christ.

Paul is urging the church at Philippi toward unity, selflessness, and mutual care. To show them what that looks like, he points directly to the historical example of "Christ Jesus." By using this compound designation right before entering the magnificent hymn of verses 6–11, Paul prevents the Philippians from viewing humility as a vague moral ideal. Instead, humility is defined by the actual mindset of the person Jesus, who exchanged heavenly glory for the form of a bondservant and submitted to death on a cross.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:1 — "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the Son of David, the Son of Abraham:"

  • John 19:5 — "Then Jesus came out, wearing the crown of thorns and the purple robe. And Pilate said to them, 'Behold the Man!'"

  • Acts 17:3 — "...explaining and demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise again from the dead, and saying, 'This Jesus whom I preach to you is the Christ.'"

  • Hebrews 6:20 — "...where the forerunner has entered for us, even Jesus, having become High Priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Paul's letters, the order of titles often carries subtle pastoral focus. While "Jesus Christ" emphasizes the historical Jesus who was revealed as the Messiah, "Christ Jesus" places the messianic role upfront, highlighting the exalted, resurrected Lord who lived a true human life.

For the believer, having the mind of "Christ Jesus" means that Christian behavior is formed by looking at a real Person. Spiritual transformation is not merely adopting a system of values, but taking on the very attitude displayed by Jesus when He walked among us.
