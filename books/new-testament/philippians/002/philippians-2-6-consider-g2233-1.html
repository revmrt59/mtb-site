CONSIDER - (hēgéomai, Strong's G2233)

Summary:

The Greek word hēgeomai means to evaluate, count, judge, or consider something after careful thought, or to hold a position of leadership. In Philippians 2:6, the word describes how Jesus Christ evaluated His divine status, declaring that He did not regard His equality with God as something to be exploited or held onto for self-advantage, but instead chose the path of humble self-giving.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word hēgeomai comes from a root that originally referred to leading, guiding, or going before others on a path. In ancient Greek, this basic idea developed in two primary directions. First, it referred to people who lead or govern, such as military officers, rulers, or chief speakers. Second, it came to describe mental guidance or evaluation—how the mind guides a person to evaluate facts, weigh options, and reach a settled opinion or verdict.

In the New Testament, both of these meanings appear. When applied to leaders, it describes those who rule or guide a community. When applied to thought, it means to count, reckon, judge, or consider something to be true based on reasoned evaluation. In Philippians 2:6, Paul uses the word in this second sense to reveal the conscious evaluation made by Jesus Christ regarding His eternal dignity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb can refer to leading or governing, or to arriving at a firm conclusion through mental evaluation. When used of thought or judgment, it means to account, regard, reckon, or consider. It describes a deliberate decision or judgment of value rather than a passing emotion or unexamined opinion.

In Philippians 2:6, the verb appears in the aorist tense, middle voice, and indicative mood, describing a singular, decisive valuation. Here the word means that Christ evaluated His status of equality with God and judged that His divine equality was not something to be seized or exploited for self-interest.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across New Testament writings, hēgeomai carries two main nuances depending on how it functions in a sentence:

First, it often functions as a title or description for leaders, rulers, or influential figures who guide others.

Second, it describes a mental accounting where a person weighs two things and determines their relative worth. In this sense, a person compares worldly values against spiritual truths and decides which one is truly valuable. For instance, a believer might count former personal achievements as loss, or judge God to be faithful to His promises. This mental valuation reflects a deliberate realignment of priorities, where divine truth completely transforms how one evaluates gain, honor, and sacrifice.

4. Theological Implications

The use of hēgeomai in Philippians 2:6 highlights the voluntary nature of Christ's incarnation and humiliation. Jesus did not enter human lowliness through compulsion, weakness, or accident. Instead, His self-humbling flowed from a deliberate mindset and clear evaluation.

Before taking on human flesh, Christ possessed full equality with God as His eternal, inherent right. Yet He evaluated that divine status not as a privilege to be used for personal ease or self-aggrandizement, but as the foundation for redeeming love. This reveals that true divine glory is expressed not in selfishly holding onto rights, but in humbling oneself to serve others.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:1–11, Paul addresses a Christian community that needed unity, harmony, and mutual care. To accomplish this, he instructs believers to do nothing through selfish ambition, but in humility to esteem others better than themselves.

Paul then grounds this command in the example of Jesus Christ. When Paul states in verse 6 that Christ did not "consider" equality with God something to be grasped, he presents Christ as the ultimate model of unselfish thinking. Though Christ occupied the supreme position of heavenly majesty, His valuation of status was completely selfless. Instead of insisting on His rights, He emptied Himself by taking the form of a servant.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Philippians 2:3 — "Let nothing be done through selfish ambition or conceit, but in lowliness of mind let each esteem others better than himself."

  • Philippians 3:7 — "But what things were gain to me, these I have counted loss for Christ."

  • Acts 26:2 — "I think myself happy, King Agrippa, because today I shall answer for myself before you concerning all the things of which I am accused by the Jews."

  • Hebrews 11:11 — "By faith Sarah herself also received strength to conceive seed, and she bore a child when she was past the age, because she judged Him faithful who had promised."

  • Hebrews 11:26 — "esteeming the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures in Egypt; for he looked to the reward."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The connection between Philippians 2:3 and Philippians 2:6 is essential for understanding Paul's message. In verse 3, Paul instructs believers to "esteem" others better than themselves. Three verses later, he uses the exact same Greek word to describe how Christ did not "consider" His equality with God as something to be used for His own advantage. The mindset required of the church is the very mindset Christ displayed in redemption. Christian humility is simply adopting the Savior's own way of evaluating value and service.
