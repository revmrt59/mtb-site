GOD - (theós, Strong's G2316)

Summary:

The word theos is the standard word for God or a deity. In biblical Scripture, it identifies the one true God, the self-existent Creator and supreme Ruler of all creation. In Philippians 2:6, the word describes the divine reality of Jesus Christ, establishing that before His incarnation He existed in the very nature and character of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek society, theos was used broadly to refer to any pagan god, goddess, or heavenly power. However, when the New Testament writers wrote in Greek, they filled this word with biblical content based on the Old Testament revelation of God. Rather than referring to the pantheon of Greek deities, theos in the New Testament almost always points to the God of Israel—the living, personal Creator who reigns over heaven and earth.

In Philippians 2:6, the Apostle Paul writes to a church facing spiritual opposition and internal friction. To encourage unity, humility, and selfless service, Paul points the Philippian believers to the example of Jesus Christ. Paul introduces Christ by describing His eternal pre-existence, declaring that He existed in the "form of God."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word theos refers to God in the absolute sense: the sovereign Deity who possesses all divine attributes, authority, and glory.

Depending on the context in Scripture, theos can refer to:

  • God the Father, the divine source and initiator of redemption.

  • The divine nature itself, encompassing the infinite holiness, power, and glory of the Lord.

  • Jesus Christ, the eternal Son who shares the exact essence and nature of God.

In Philippians 2:6, the target occurrence is a masculine singular noun in the genitive case ("of God"), serving as a descriptive possessive that modifies "form." It indicates that the nature possessed by Christ prior to His earthly birth was that of God Himself.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, theos is used primarily for God the Father, but it is also used directly of Jesus Christ to affirm His true deity. Scripture uses theos to distinguish the true Creator from idols, to highlight God's sovereign initiative in salvation, and to reveal the personal distinction and divine unity within the Trinity.

When applied to Jesus Christ in passages like Philippians 2:6, theos does not mean Jesus was merely divine-like or morally godly. Instead, it places Christ on the divine side of reality. Joined with the word "form" (morphe), which denotes outward expression coming from inner essential nature, theos highlights that Jesus possessed the real essence and majesty of God.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of theos in Philippians 2:6 provides foundational truth regarding the deity and person of Jesus Christ:

  • Eternal Deity: Christ did not become God at His baptism or resurrection; He eternally existed in the form of God.

  • Equal Glory: Being in the form of God meant Christ possessed total equality with God, holding all divine privileges and rights.

  • Sovereign Condescension: The miracle of the incarnation is that the One who possessed the full nature of God emptied Himself by taking on human nature as a servant.

This passage demonstrates that Christ's self-emptying was not a loss of His divine nature, but a deliberate act of grace. He set aside the visible manifestation of His heavenly glory to save sinners.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:5–11, Paul presents a profound description of Christ's humiliation and exaltation to serve as a practical model for Christian behavior. Paul urges the Philippians to put others before themselves and to adopt the mind of Christ.

In verse 6, Paul establishes the supreme starting point of Christ’s humility: "who, being in the form of God, did not consider it robbery to be equal with God." The target word "God" identifies the divine standard from which Christ stooped. Christ possessed full deity by right, not by robbery or seizure. Yet, He did not view His equality with God as something to be held onto for selfish advantage.

Grammatically, the genitive singular masculine form ("of God") anchors the phrase "form of God." It establishes that Christ’s nature prior to human birth was completely, truly divine. Understanding Christ's infinite glory as God makes His willing sacrifice on the cross infinitely precious and humbling to believers.

  • John 6:27 – "’Do not labor for the food which perishes, but for the food which endures to everlasting life, which the Son of Man will give you, because God the Father has set His seal on Him.’"

  • Acts 3:15 – "’and killed the Prince of life, whom God raised from the dead, of which we are witnesses.’"

  • Romans 10:9 – "that if you confess with your mouth the Lord Jesus and believe in your heart that God has raised Him from the dead, you will be saved."

  • 1 John 4:7 – "Beloved, let us love one another, for love is of God; and everyone who loves is born of God and knows God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A helpful contrast appears between verse 6 and verse 7 in Philippians 2. Verse 6 states that Christ existed in the "form of God," while verse 7 states that He took the "form of a bondservant." The same word for "form" (morphe) is used in both statements.

Just as taking the "form of a servant" meant Jesus became a real, living human being with a servant's status, existing in the "form of God" meant He was truly and fully God. His deity was not an appearance or mask, but His eternal essential nature. This deliberate contrast magnifies the depth of Christ's love: the sovereign God took on human flesh to serve and redeem helpless sinners.
