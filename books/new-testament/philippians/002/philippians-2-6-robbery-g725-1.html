ROBBERY - (harpagmós, Strong's G725)

Summary:

The word harpagmos refers to the act of snatching something away by force, or treating a status or privilege as a prize to be held onto for selfish advantage. In Philippians 2:6, the word describes how Jesus Christ regarded His divine position. Though He existed in the form of God, He did not view His equality with God as something to be exploited for personal benefit, but chose instead to humble Himself in self-giving love for human redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Paul wrote his letter to the church at Philippi while imprisoned. The church faced internal friction and external opposition. To cultivate unity, Paul urged believers to put away selfish ambition and value others above themselves.

To ground this pastoral instruction, Paul pointed to the ultimate example of selflessness: Jesus Christ. In Philippians 2:6–11, often called the hymn of Christ, Paul outlined the path of Jesus from divine glory to human humility, and finally to heavenly exaltation. The word harpagmos appears at the critical turning point of this passage, describing the mindset of Christ before His incarnation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word harpagmos builds on a root idea of taking, seizing, or snatching something away. In general usage, it can refer to two related concepts:

  • An active action: the physical act of robbery, plunder, or snatching.

  • A descriptive condition: a prized treasure, a lucky find, or an advantage to be held onto tightly and exploited for self-interest.

In Philippians 2:6, the context clarifies the meaning. Because Christ already possessed full equality with God from eternity, He did not need to snatch or steal divine status. Therefore, the word here means treating a privileged position as something to be held onto or used for personal advantage. Jesus possessed full divine majesty, yet He did not view His divine rights as a privilege to be retained for His own ease or glory.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Grammatically, the word appears here as an accusative singular masculine noun. It functions as the object describing how Christ evaluated His equality with God.

Rather than clinging to the external privileges of deity, Christ voluntarily emptied Himself by taking on human nature. The contrast in the passage is between holding tightly to one's rights and surrendering those rights to serve others.

4. Theological Implications

This word plays an important role in understanding the incarnation of Christ:

  • It confirms the pre-existence and deity of Christ. Jesus was already "in the form of God" before taking human flesh. He did not need to gain deity, because He already possessed it fully.

  • It highlights the voluntary nature of Christ's humility. Jesus was not forced to lay down His heavenly glory; He willingly chose not to exploit His divine rights for personal gain.

  • It reveals the character of God. The true nature of divine greatness is displayed not in selfish grasping, but in self-giving love and sacrificial service.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:1–5, Paul called believers to walk in humility, asking them to look out not only for their own interests, but also for the interests of others. Verse 6 introduces Christ as the living demonstration of this command.

The surrounding contrast makes the meaning vivid. Where Adam and fallen humanity sought to grasp equality with God through disobedience, Christ—who possessed true divine equality—did not treat His status as a prize to be exploited. Instead, He emptied Himself, taking the form of a servant, becoming obedient to the point of death on a cross.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • This term is unique to this context and does not appear elsewhere in the biblical corpus.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding this word prevents two theological mistakes. First, it refutes the idea that Jesus was a created being who tried to reach divine status. The text states He was already in the form of God. Second, it shows that Christ did not surrender His divine nature during the incarnation, but rather set aside the independent exercise of His divine privileges to walk in obedience as a man.
