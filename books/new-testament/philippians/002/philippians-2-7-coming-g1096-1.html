COMING - (gínomai, Strong's G1096)

Summary:

The Greek verb ginomai basic meaning is to become, to come into being, to happen, or to enter into a new state or condition. Unlike verbs that describe continuous, static existence, this word denotes a transition or arrival in historical reality. In Philippians 2:7, the word describes the Son of God taking on genuine human nature, entering human history in true human likeness as part of His self-humiliation for our salvation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In Philippians 2, the apostle Paul writes to a local church experiencing internal friction and external pressure. To cultivate unity, humility, and self-giving love among believers, Paul sets before them the supreme example of Jesus Christ. In Philippians 2:5–11, often called the "Christ Hymn," Paul traces the downward path of Christ from eternal glory to the humiliation of the cross, followed by His exaltation by God the Father.

The word translated "coming" in verse 7 marks a pivotal transition in this passage. Up to this point, Paul has affirmed Christ's eternal, uncreated identity in the form of God. Now, Paul shifts to describe how the eternal Son stepped into time and historical reality. The use of this verb anchors the incarnation in concrete human history rather than abstract myth or allegory.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ginomai refers to something taking place, coming into existence, or arriving at a new condition. It can be used for natural occurrences, such as a storm arising or night falling, for events taking place in time, or for a person assuming a new role, status, or form.

A clear distinction exists in New Testament Greek between verbs of being and verbs of becoming. While verbs of being describe continuous, uncreated, or ongoing existence, ginomai indicates a beginning point or entry into a state that was not previously present. When applied to Jesus Christ in Philippians 2:7, the word does not mean He began to exist as a divine person, but rather that He entered into a new mode of existence—genuine humanity—at a specific point in time. Here the word means that the eternal Son truly entered human likeness.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the New Testament, ginomai appears in a wide variety of contexts to describe historical events, personal transformations, and fulfillments of prophecy. It carries several primary shades of meaning depending on the surrounding sentence:

  • Event occurrence: Describing things that happen, take place, or come to pass in historical time.

  • Personal transition: Describing a person becoming something they were not previously, such as becoming a disciple, a witness, or an enemy.

  • Creation and arrival: Describing entry into physical existence or arrival in a specific place or condition.

Grammatically, the form in Philippians 2:7 functions as an aorist middle participle in the nominative masculine singular. The aorist aspect views the entry of Christ into human likeness as a historical reality within the overarching sequence of His self-humiliation. The middle voice reflects the grammatical relationship between the subject and the action. While the surrounding context clearly shows Christ's loving, self-giving motive, the grammatical voice itself simply identifies Him as the one undergoing this historical transition into human likeness.

4. Theological Implications

The arrival of God's Son in human likeness is central to the Christian faith. The use of ginomai in Philippians 2:7 safeguards two critical doctrines regarding the incarnation:

First, it affirms the reality of Christ's pre-existence. Before He was "coming in the likeness of men," He already existed in the form of God. His identity did not begin in Bethlehem; rather, His entry into human nature began there.

Second, it affirms the reality of His humanity. Jesus did not merely pretend to be human or temporarily put on a human appearance like a costume. He entered into full, authentic human existence, experiencing human development, physical limitations, hunger, weariness, and suffering, yet remaining without sin. He who was eternally God became man, uniting two distinct natures in one divine Person forever.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:7, "coming in the likeness of men" works in close harmony with the surrounding statements: "made Himself of no reputation" (literally, emptied Himself) and "taking the form of a bondservant."

Paul stacks these descriptive clauses together to emphasize the astonishing extent of Christ's self-humiliation. The eternal Lord did not enter human history as a earthly king surrounded by worldly splendor. Instead, His entry into humanity took the specific form of a bondservant.

The contrast in the passage is profound:

  • In verse 6, Christ possesses the eternal, uncreated form of God.

  • In verse 7, through "coming in the likeness of men," He takes on the created, humble form of a servant.

Paul uses this dramatic contrast not merely to inform the Philippians' theology, but to transform their daily attitudes toward one another. If the Lord of glory was willing to step down into human likeness and serve others, believers ought to put aside selfish ambition and consider others better than themselves.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition expressed by ginomai in Philippians 2:7 helps resolve a common misconception about the incarnation. Some have mistakenly believed that when Christ "emptied Himself," He gave up His divine attributes or ceased to be God.

However, the passage shows that Christ's self-emptying was not a matter of subtraction, but of addition. He did not surrender His deity; rather, He took on a servant's form by "coming in the likeness of men." He concealed His unveiled divine glory beneath the veil of true human flesh. The one who spoke the universe into being entered creation as a dependent infant, demonstrating that true greatness is expressed through humble service.
