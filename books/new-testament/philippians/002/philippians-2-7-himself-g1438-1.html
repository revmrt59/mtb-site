HIMSELF - (heautoû, Strong's G1438)

Summary:

The Greek word heautou is a reflexive pronoun that refers back to the subject of a sentence, translated as "himself," "herself," "itself," or "themselves." It shows that the person performing an action is also the recipient or target of that action. In Philippians 2:7, heautou highlights that Jesus Christ voluntarily performed the act of self-giving, choosing to make Himself of no reputation as He took on human flesh and servant hood for our salvation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The New Testament frequently uses reflexive pronouns to show that an action turns back upon the subject. Instead of an action being directed toward someone else or forced upon a person by an outside power, the subject actively carries out the action regarding himself.

In Paul's letter to the Philippians, this word appears in a central passage that celebrates the humiliation and exaltation of Jesus Christ (Philippians 2:5-11). Paul wrote to a local church struggling with interpersonal friction and pride. To guide them toward true unity, Paul pointed believers to the attitude of Christ. Rather than holding onto status or personal rights, Jesus intentionally humbled Himself to serve others.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Heautou functions as a standard reflexive pronoun. Depending on its grammatical gender, number, and case in a given verse, it translates into English as "himself," "herself," "itself," "yourself," "ourselves," or "themselves."

The word links the action of a verb directly back to the grammatical subject. It does not possess complex theological meaning within its dictionary definition. Instead, it serves the basic function of showing that the subject and the direct object of the verb are the same. For the target occurrence in Philippians 2:7, the word is in the accusative case, singular number, and masculine gender, identifying Jesus as the one who carried out this act of self-renunciation upon Himself.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, heautou is used in everyday instructions, warnings, and theological statements. It can describe a physical action a person takes, an attitude someone holds toward himself, or an act of personal commitment.

When Scripture applies this reflexive word to human beings, it often points out human choices, responsibility, or limitations. For example, people can test themselves, deceive themselves, or rely on themselves. However, when applied to Jesus Christ, the pronoun highlights His voluntary initiative. Jesus was not compelled by external force, nor was He a passive participant in the incarnation. He freely took these actions upon Himself out of love for the Father and for humanity.

4. Theological Implications

The theological depth of Philippians 2:7 comes from who the subject is and what He chose to do. The One who existed eternally in the form of God chose to act upon Himself by taking the place of a servant.

This truth underscores the voluntary nature of the incarnation and the cross. Jesus was not a victim caught in historical circumstances beyond His control, nor was He coerced into human existence. He actively stepped down into human history. By showing that Christ acted upon Himself, Scripture demonstrates both His divine authority and His boundless grace. His willingness to lower Himself reflects the sacrificial nature of God's love.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:7, Paul writes that Christ "made Himself of no reputation" (literally "emptied Himself"). The reflexive pronoun heautou is vital here because it identifies Jesus as the active participant in this step. He was not stripped of His divine essence by an external authority. Rather, He voluntarily set aside the outward display of His heavenly glory to take on human nature.

Paul sets this example before the church to reshape how Christians treat one another. Right after telling believers not to look out merely for their own interests, Paul presents Jesus as the supreme example of selflessness. In the very next verse (Philippians 2:8), the same word is used again when Paul writes that Christ "humbled Himself." This repetition emphasizes a continuous, undivided commitment to lowly service, offering believers a living model of humility.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 18:4 — "Therefore whoever humbles himself as this little child is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven."

  • John 7:18 — "He who speaks from himself seeks his own glory; but He who seeks the glory of the One who sent Him is true, and no unrighteousness is in Him."

  • 2 Corinthians 1:9 — "Yes, we had the sentence of death in ourselves, that we should not trust in ourselves but in God who raises the dead."

  • Philippians 2:8 — "And being found in appearance as a man, He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross."

  • 1 Peter 4:8 — "And above all things have fervent love for one another, for 'love will cover a multitude of sins.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Recognizing the grammatical role of this pronoun helps prevent misunderstandings about Christ's incarnation. Some readers mistakenly assume that "made Himself of no reputation" means Jesus surrendered His deity or ceased to be God when He became human.

The grammar clarifies the true picture. Because Jesus is the subject acting upon Himself, the passage shows His continued active divine authority. Taking the form of a bondservant involved adding a genuine human nature, not subtracting His divine nature. He willingly veiled His divine majesty in human flesh, showing that true divine greatness is expressed in loving service.
