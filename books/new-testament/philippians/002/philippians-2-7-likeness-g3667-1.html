LIKENESS - (homoíōma, Strong's G3667)

Summary:

The word homoiōma refers to a form, image, copy, or outward resemblance. It describes something made in the likeness of something else or appearing in a form that resembles another object or person. In Philippians 2:7, the word describes how the eternal Son of God entered human history by being made in the "likeness" of men. It emphasizes that Christ took on a genuinely human physical form and life, appearing to human eyes as a real man, while remaining truly divine.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word homoiōma comes from a Greek root meaning "like" or "similar." In ancient writing, it refers to a visible representation, a copy, a image, or an appearance that resembles something else. It was used when describing statues, patterns, or outward forms that looked like a specific person or object.

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament, the word was used for images or physical representations, such as idols fashioned to look like created things or visual representations of divine visual revelations. The word highlights visible similarity and outward appearance.

When Paul writes to the believers in Philippi, he includes an early Christian hymn or declaration about Jesus Christ. Paul wants to teach the Philippians how to live in humility and selflessness toward one another. To do this, he points to Jesus Christ as the ultimate example of self-giving humility. Though Christ was in the form of God from all eternity, He humbled Himself by taking the form of a bondservant and coming in the likeness of human beings.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

bound to an exact outward form or copy, the word can refer to a visible resemblance, an image, or the outward shape and presentation of a person or thing.

Here the word means taking on the real form and visible appearance of humanity. When applied to Christ in Philippians 2:7, it does not mean He merely pretended to be human or put on a superficial disguise. Rather, it indicates that Jesus entered the full human experience, presenting Himself in genuine human physical form, growing, hungering, tiring, and living in every way as a true human being.

At the same time, the word allows for a distinction between resemblance and total identity in every moral sense. While Jesus was truly human in His body and physical life, He did not inherit human sinfulness. The word homoiōma highlights that He was genuinely like us in human nature and form, yet without sin.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In New Testament usage, homoiōma appears in contexts where similarity, representation, or shared physical form is described.

It is used to describe physical images or idols that humans make to resemble earthly creatures instead of worshipping the Creator. It is also used to describe shared human experiences, such as the common death humanity shares following the pattern of Adam's transgression.

In Paul's letters, the word becomes especially important for explaining the incarnation and redemption. When describing Christ's mission, Paul uses the word to explain how God sent His Son in the likeness of sinful flesh to address human sin. The word stresses that Jesus took on genuine physical flesh, fully identifying with human weakness, yet without sharing in human sinfulness.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of homoiōma in Philippians 2:7 touches on the core of the incarnation. Jesus did not come as a spirit or a phantom. He took on real flesh and lived as a real man. He was born, grew up, suffered, and died in human form. His humanity was complete and authentic.

Yet the choice of this word also safeguards the truth of Christ's identity. Jesus was not merely a ordinary man who came into existence at His birth; He was the pre-existent Son of God who willingly entered human existence. By saying He was made in the "likeness of men," the text affirms that He truly joined Himself to human nature while remaining who He had always been as God.

This truth gives believers comfort in knowing that Jesus understands human weakness and temptation. He did not remain distant from human suffering, but fully embraced human bodily life to redeem humanity from within.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:7, the noun appears in the dative case, singular number, and neuter gender. It sits within a carefully constructed statement about Christ's voluntary humiliation: He "made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant, and coming in the likeness of men."

Notice the progression in Paul's argument. Before His incarnation, Christ possessed the "form" (morphē) of God. When He humbled Himself, He took the "form" (morphē) of a servant and was made in the "likeness" (homoiōma) of men.

The immediate context emphasizes both His servant posture and His visible human entry into the world. Being found in appearance as a man, He humbled Himself further by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. The word homoiōma serves as the bridge between His pre-existent divine glory and His lowliness on earth, showing how the eternal Son became visible, accessible, and mortal for our salvation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 5:14 — "Nevertheless death reigned from Adam to Moses, even over those who had not sinned according to the likeness of the transgression of Adam, who is a type of Him who was to come."

  • Romans 6:5 — "For if we have been united together in the likeness of His death, certainly we also shall be in the likeness of His resurrection."

  • Romans 8:3 — "For what the law could not do in that it was weak through the flesh, God did by sending His own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, on account of sin: He condemned sin in the flesh."

  • Revelation 9:7 — "The shape of the locusts was like horses prepared for battle. On their heads were crowns of something like gold, and their faces were like the faces of men."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The distinction between how homoiōma is used of idols and how it is used of Christ in Philippians 2:7 highlights a striking biblical contrast. Idols are man-made copies of earthly creatures that attempt to represent God, resulting in false worship. In contrast, Jesus Christ is the true Son of God who took on the actual likeness of humanity to reveal God perfectly and save human beings.

Furthermore, Paul uses this truth in Philippians 2 to address personal relationships within the local church. He does not present the incarnation merely as a doctrine to be believed, but as a model of self-giving love to be followed. Just as Christ did not hold onto His privileges but took on human likeness to serve, believers are called to lay down their selfish ambitions to serve one another.
