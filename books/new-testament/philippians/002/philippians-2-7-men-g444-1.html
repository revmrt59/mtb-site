MEN - (ánthrōpos, Strong's G444)

Summary:

Anthrōpos refers generally to a human being, a person, or humanity as a distinct class of created beings. In Philippians 2:7, the plural form refers to human beings in general, emphasizing that when the eternal Son of God humbled Himself and entered human history, He truly took on real human nature and appeared in the genuine likeness of humanity.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Philippians 2:5–11 contains one of the most profound declarations in the New Testament regarding the person and work of Jesus Christ. Paul writes to the believers in Philippi to encourage unity, humility, and selflessness within the church community. To motivate them toward this mindset, he points to the supreme example of Jesus Christ.

Before His incarnation, Christ existed eternally in the form of God, possessing divine glory with the Father. Yet He did not cling to His divine privileges. Instead, He emptied Himself by taking the form of a bondservant and coming in the likeness of humanity. In this setting, the word for human beings highlights the vast distance between heavenly glory and earthly human existence—a distance Christ willingly crossed to accomplish redemption.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word anthrōpos is the standard biblical term for a human being, a person, or mankind. Unlike terms that specifically designate an adult male, anthrōpos focuses broadly on human nature and human identity in distinction from God, angels, or animals.

Depending on its context, the word can refer to:

  • An individual human being or person.

  • Human beings collectively, or mankind as a whole.

  • The human species in contrast to the divine sphere.

In Philippians 2:7, the word is used in the plural to refer to human beings generally. The focus is not on a single individual, but on human nature itself. Christ was made in the likeness of human beings, meaning He stepped into genuine human reality.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the New Testament, anthrōpos is used in a wide variety of settings. It frequently marks the contrast between humanity's frailties and limitations and God's power and holiness. Scripture often contrasts what human beings can accomplish on their own against what God can do.

The word also serves to describe the shared experience, weakness, and nature of the human race. When used in connection with Jesus Christ, anthrōpos emphasizes the reality of His incarnation. He did not merely put on a human disguise; He became a true participant in human nature, sharing in physical, mental, and emotional human life while remaining without sin.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of anthrōpos in Philippians 2:7 lies at the heart of the doctrine of the incarnation. Biblical salvation required a Redeemer who was both fully divine and fully human. If Christ had not truly become human, He could not have represented humanity or borne the penalty for human sin.

At the same time, Paul uses the phrase "likeness of men" to preserve both the reality and the uniqueness of Christ. By taking on the likeness of human beings, Christ shared fully in human nature, yet without inheriting human sinfulness. He was truly human, but not merely human. The eternal Son did not lay aside His deity; rather, He took on a genuine human nature, veiling His glory so He could serve, suffer, and lay down His life.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:7, the target word functions as a genitive plural masculine noun. This grammatical form connects directly to "likeness," creating the phrase "in the likeness of men."

The surrounding context contrasts two distinct states. First, Christ existed in the "form of God" (Philippians 2:6). Then, He took the "form of a bondservant" and was made in the "likeness of men." The contrast is striking. The One who was equal with God chose to lower Himself to the level of human creatures.

Paul's purpose in this passage is pastoral as well as theological. He is not merely explaining Christology; he is showing believers what true humility looks like. If the Lord of glory was willing to step down into human nature to serve others, Christians should likewise lay aside selfish ambition and serve one another.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Luke 18:27 — "The things which are impossible with men are possible with God."

  • Acts 14:11 — "The gods have come down to us in the likeness of men!"

  • Romans 1:18 — "For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who suppress the truth in unrighteousness."

  • 1 Timothy 2:5 — "For there is one God and one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Early Christian teachers defended both the true deity and true humanity of Christ against competing errors. Some ancient teachings claimed Christ only appeared to be human, while others denied His divine nature. Philippians 2:7 provides a clear biblical balance. By stating that Christ came in the likeness of human beings, Paul affirms that His earthly human existence was entirely real, even as He remained the eternal divine Son.
