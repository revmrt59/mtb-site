REPUTATION - (kenóō, Strong's G2758)

Summary:

The word kenoo means to make empty, render void, or deprive of force. In Philippians 2:7, where the NKJV translates the phrase as "made Himself of no reputation," it describes Jesus Christ's voluntary act of humility in becoming human. Rather than giving up His divine nature, Christ emptied Himself by laying aside His heavenly rights and outward glory, taking on the form of a servant to accomplish the salvation of mankind.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Greek verb kenoo fundamentally means to empty out, render void, or make without effect. In everyday ancient Greek, the term could refer literally to draining a vessel or vacating a space. Figuratively, it meant rendering a claim, a law, or an expectation powerless or meaningless.

In the New Testament, the apostle Paul uses this word in two primary ways. Most often, he uses it to speak of abstract concepts being rendered void, such as faith being rendered useless or a personal boast being made empty. However, in Philippians 2:7, Paul uses the verb with a personal reflexive pronoun to describe the unique action of Jesus Christ in the Incarnation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to making something empty, deprivative of value, or null and void. When applied to an abstract noun like a promise or faith, it means robbing that item of its intended power or fulfillment.

When paired with a reflexive pronoun ("Himself") as it is in Philippians 2:7, the verb means to humble oneself, lower one's status, or willingly set aside position and privileges. The NKJV captures this idiomatic sense with the phrase "made Himself of no reputation." The verb itself does not mean losing divine attributes or ceasing to be God. Instead, it describes a voluntary lowering of status—pouring out heavenly prerogative to take on lowliness.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Paul's writings, kenoo usually highlights the potential loss of force or validity in spiritual matters. For example, Paul expresses concern that the cross might be rendered powerless through human cleverness, or that his spiritual boasting might be rendered meaningless by poor circumstances.

In Philippians 2:7, the usage is distinct because Christ is the subject performing the action upon Himself. The verb is in the aorist tense, active voice, indicative mood, and singular number. Grammatically, this presents Christ's action as a decisive historical reality where He actively chose to lower Himself rather than insisting on holding onto His visible heavenly glory.

4. Theological Implications

Philippians 2:7 serves as a key text regarding the doctrine of the Incarnation. Misunderstandings of this verse have sometimes led to the claim that Jesus shed His divine essence or gave up His divine attributes when He became man. However, divine nature cannot cease to be divine.

Scripture shows that Christ's "emptying" was not a subtractive loss of deity, but an additive taking on of humanity. He did not become less than God; rather, He took on human flesh and servant status. He concealed His divine majesty under the veil of humble human nature, submitting to earthly limitations and voluntary dependence on the Father. His action displays the extreme depth of divine love and voluntary condescension.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:1–11, Paul writes to encourage unity, selflessness, and mutual care within the church at Philippi. To correct selfish ambition and conceit, Paul points believers to the mind of Christ.

Before calling believers to consider the interests of others above themselves, Paul demonstrates how Christ Himself operated. Although existing in the form of God, Christ did not consider equality with God something to be held onto for personal advantage. Instead, He "made Himself of no reputation." The context immediately explains how He did this: by "taking the form of a bondservant, and coming in the likeness of men." The lowering of Himself reached its climax when He humbled Himself even further unto death on a cross.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Romans 4:14 — "For if those who are of the law are heirs, faith is made void and the promise made of no effect."

  • 1 Corinthians 1:17 — "For Christ did not send me to baptize, but to preach the gospel, not with wisdom of words, lest the cross of Christ should be made of no effect."

  • 1 Corinthians 9:15 — "But I have used none of these things, nor have I written these things that it should be done so to me; for it would be better for me to die than that anyone should make my boasting void."

  • 2 Corinthians 9:3 — "Yet I have sent the brethren, lest our boasting of you should be in vain in this respect, that, as I said, you may be ready;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast in Philippians 2 between the "form of God" and the "form of a bondservant" clarifies the nature of Christ's self-emptying. In both expressions, the word "form" refers to the true inner character and nature of a thing expressed outwardly. Christ did not swap His divine nature for a human nature; rather, while remaining truly God, He truly entered human existence as a bondservant.

Paul presents this profound Christological truth not merely for intellectual instruction, but to show local church members how to treat one another. Believers display the mind of Christ when they willingly lay aside personal prestige, recognition, and rights to serve the body of Christ.
