TAKING - (lambánō, Strong's G2983)

lambanō (lambanō, Strong’s G2983)

Summary:

The Greek word lambanō fundamentally means to take, receive, or lay hold of something. It can describe physically picking up an object, accepting a gift or message, or taking on a specific role or status. In Philippians 2:7, the word describes Christ "taking" the form of a bondservant in His incarnation. By using this word, Paul highlights that the eternal Son of God assumed a genuine human nature and servant status when He came to earth, adding humanity to His deity without laying aside His divine nature.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Lambanō is one of the most common verbs in the New Testament. In everyday ancient Greek, it had a wide semantic range covering both active taking (such as grabbing an object, seizing a opportunity, or adopting a role) and passive receiving (such as accepting a gift, receiving authority, or welcoming a guest).

In the New Testament, lambanō appears across every major genre, from the Gospels to the Epistles. It carries no inherent, technical theological definition by itself; rather, its precise meaning in any given passage is shaped by the context and the direct object attached to it. When used with physical objects like bread or clothes, it means to take hold of or pick up. When used with abstract qualities, blessings, or status, it means to receive or assume that condition.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of lambanō centers on two main actions depending on whether the subject is taking something or receiving something:

  • To take hold of, take up, or lay hold of physically.

  • To take upon oneself, assume, or adopt a status, posture, or obligation.

  • To receive or accept what is offered, given, or imparted by another.

In Philippians 2:7, lambanō refers to taking upon oneself or assuming a condition. Here, Jesus Christ is described as "taking the form of a bondservant" (morphē doulou). The verb indicates an addition: the Son of God took to Himself a complete human nature, stepping into human history in the status of a servant.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, lambanō expresses both the act of taking initiative and the act of receiving what is provided. For example, in the Gospel accounts of Jesus' miracles and the Last Supper, the verb often describes Jesus taking bread in His hands before blessing it. Elsewhere, it describes people receiving grace, mercy, or a gift from God.

In Philippians 2:7, the word appears as a masculine nominative singular aorist active participle. Grammatically, the active voice indicates that the subject performs the action of taking upon Himself the nature of a servant. The participle functions alongside the main verb ("made Himself of no reputation" or "emptied Himself") to explain how that self-emptying took place. It was not through subtracting His divine essence, but through taking on human nature. The surrounding passage—which speaks of Christ's humility and obedience—establishes the voluntary nature of His condescension, while the verb lambanō specifically names the action of assuming servant humanity.

4. Theological Implications

The use of lambanō in Philippians 2:7 carries several vital theological implications for the doctrine of the incarnation:

  • Incarnation by Addition: Christ did not cease to be God when He became man. Instead, He "took" something He did not previously possess—a complete human nature and the position of a bondservant. The incarnation involved the addition of human nature to divine nature, not the loss of divinity.

  • Genuine Servant Status: By taking the "form of a bondservant," Christ entered human experience in genuine lowliness. He did not come as an earthly king surrounded by royal splendor, but in humble obedience to the Father and in service to humanity.

  • The Groundwork of Redemption: To save human beings, the eternal Son had to take on human flesh and blood. By assuming true humanity, Christ became qualified to serve as the representative and substitute for sinners.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Philippians 2:5–11 forms a profound passage often called the "Christ Hymn." Paul writes this passage to encourage unity, humility, and selflessness among the believers at Philippi. To motivate them, Paul points to Jesus as the supreme example of humble service.

In verse 6, Paul states that Christ existed in the "form of God" and did not view His equality with God as something to be selfishly held onto or exploited for personal advantage. Then in verse 7, Paul presents the contrast: Christ "made Himself of no reputation, taking the form of a bondservant, and coming in the likeness of men."

The contrast between "form of God" and "form of bondservant" is central to the passage. The word "form" (morphē) denotes the essential characteristic or expression of a reality. Christ, who possesses the essential reality of God, took on the essential reality of a bondservant. The word lambanō bridges these two truths, showing that the One who eternally possessed divine glory took upon Himself true humanity and servant status for our salvation.

  • 1 Corinthians 9:25 — "And everyone who competes for the prize is temperate in all things. Now they do it to obtain a perishable crown, but we for an imperishable crown."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A meaningful connection in Philippians 2:6–7 exists between the ideas of grasping and taking. Verse 6 notes that Christ did not consider equality with God something to be "grasped" (harpagmos) or exploited. Instead, verse 7 shows Him voluntarily "taking" (lambanō) the form of a servant.

While Adam attempted to grasp equality with God through self-exaltation and disobedience, Christ—who was already equal with God—humbled Himself by taking the posture of a bondservant. This contrast highlights the beauty of Christ's humility and provides the ultimate pattern of selfless love for the church.
