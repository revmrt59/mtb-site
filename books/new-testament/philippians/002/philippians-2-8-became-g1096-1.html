BECAME - (gínomai, Strong's G1096)

Summary:

The word means to come into existence, to happen, or to enter into a new state or condition. In Philippians 2:8, the word describes how Jesus Christ, having taken on human nature, entered fully into a state of personal submission to the Father's will, carrying out that obedience all the way to His death on the cross.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Apostle Paul wrote his letter to the church at Philippi while in prison, urging the believers to live together in harmony, humility, and selfless love. To show what true humility looks like, Paul pointed to the supreme example of Jesus Christ.

In Philippians 2:5–11, often called the hymn of Christ, Paul traces the movement of Jesus from His eternal glory alongside the Father down to the lowest depths of human suffering and death. Before taking on human flesh, Christ shared divine equality with God. Yet He did not cling to that status for His own advantage. Instead, He emptied Himself by taking the form of a bondservant and taking on human likeness. Within this portrait of Christ's voluntary humiliation, Paul uses the word "became" to highlight how Jesus actively carried out His submission in human history.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to something coming into being, happening, arriving, or turning out to be a certain way. Unlike words that describe a static state of remaining the same, this word highlights movement into a condition or role.

Depending on the context, the word can mean:

  • To happen or take place as an event.

  • To come into existence or be made.

  • To enter into a status, role, or condition.

In Philippians 2:8, the word carries this third sense of entering into a state or condition. Jesus did not merely possess an attitude of willingness in heaven; He entered into the active reality of human submission, taking on the condition of an obedient servant in His earthly life.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the New Testament, this word appears in a wide variety of settings. It frequently describes historical events taking place in fulfillment of God's plans, such as prophecies coming to pass or key moments in the ministry of Jesus unfolding. It is also used to describe changes in personal status or relationships, such as someone becoming a disciple, a teacher, or an enemy.

When used of people entering a state or disposition, the word emphasizes reality rather than pretense. It marks a clear transition into that state. When Scripture speaks of Christ in relation to this word, it stresses the genuine reality of His incarnation and work. He did not pretend to be human or merely wear humanity like a costume. He entered into actual human experience, subject to human limitations, testing, and suffering.

4. Theological Implications

The statement that Christ "became obedient to the point of death" sits at the heart of Christian doctrine regarding the work of salvation.

First, it highlights the active nature of Christ's obedience. Salvation required more than the Son of God taking on human flesh; it required Him to live a life of complete submission to the Father's holy law and will. From His birth through His earthly ministry, Jesus yielded His human will to the Father, choosing submission over self-assertion at every step.

Second, it underlines the extent of His sacrifice. His obedience was not partial or temporary. He carried His submission to the absolute outer limit—dying on a Roman cross. Death by crucifixion was reserved for criminals and subversives, involving public shame and extreme physical torment. By entering into obedience that led even to this agonizing death, Jesus fulfilled the Father's redemptive purpose and bore the penalty for human sin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:8, Paul constructs a careful sequence to describe the humiliation of Christ. First, Jesus was "found in appearance as a man." Then, "He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross."

The word "became" forms the structural bridge between Christ's humble mindset and His ultimate act of sacrifice. Humility is not merely an internal feeling; it expresses itself in concrete action. Christ's decision to humble Himself resulted in His practical submission to the Father's redemptive plan.

Grammatically, the word appears here as a participle describing Christ as the one who entered this state of submission. The surrounding sentence makes clear that this was not a passive fate forced upon an unwilling victim. Jesus freely accepted this pathway. His obedience extended all the way to death, and Paul adds the phrase "even the death of the cross" to highlight the intense humiliation of the execution He endured. The point of the passage is to show the Philippians that if the Lord of glory was willing to enter into such profound lowliness for others, believers ought to lay down their own pride and serve one another.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding how this word functions in Philippians 2 helps guard against two common misunderstandings about Christ's submission.

First, Christ's obedience does not imply any inferiority in His divine essence. The early verses of the passage affirm that He exists in the very form of God. His choice to enter a state of servant-like obedience was a voluntary economic submission for the purpose of redemption, not a loss of His true deity.

Second, Paul presents Christ's action as the ultimate pattern for Christian fellowship. Immediately before this hymn, Paul instructs believers to "let nothing be done through selfish ambition or conceit, but in lowliness of mind let each esteem others better than himself" (Philippians 2:3). By showing that Christ "became obedient" to the point of maximum sacrifice, Paul provides the ultimate example of what selfless love looks like when lived out in daily life.
