CROSS - (staurós, Strong's G4716)

Summary:

The Greek noun stauros refers physically to an upright stake or cross used by ancient Romans as an execution timber. In Greco-Roman culture, crucifixion was designed to inflict maximum physical suffering and public humiliation, reserved primarily for slaves, rebels, and violent criminals. In Philippians 2:8, the word specifies the extreme depth of Christ's self-humiliation, showing that Jesus did not merely undergo physical death, but surrendered Himself to "even the death of the cross"—the most shameful and painful form of execution in the ancient world.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Mediterranean world, stauros originally referred to a sharp wooden stake or vertical post. By the Roman period, the term came to describe the execution apparatus consisting of a vertical post and a crossbeam. Crucifixion was deliberately public and gruesome. Roman citizens were generally protected from this form of punishment, which was reserved for political rebels, runaway slaves, and severe offenders. The social shame associated with the cross was so intense that society avoided discussing it in polite conversation. When early Christian writers used this word, their readers immediately recognized the shocking contrast between divine majesty and the public shame of execution.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

bound or nailed until death.

  • Figuratively, when applied to discipleship, it describes a follower's willingness to deny self, accept dishonor, and suffer for the sake of Christ.

  • Doctrinally, in the epistles, it represents the entire saving work of Jesus accomplished through His sacrificial death.

In Philippians 2:8, the noun is in the genitive case, singular number, and masculine gender ("death of the cross"). It functions grammatically to clarify the exact nature of the death Christ endured in His obedience to the Father.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the New Testament, stauros carries three main shades of usage:

First, gospel narratives use the word for the physical wooden beam carried through the city to the site of execution.

Second, Jesus uses the word in His teaching on discipleship prior to His arrest. He commands His followers to take up their cross, framing true discipleship as complete submission to God and a willingness to face rejection or loss for His sake.

Third, the apostles use the word as a summary term for God's redemptive work. Rather than viewing the cross as a tragic defeat, early believers recognized it as the place where sin was judged and salvation was secured.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of stauros in Philippians 2:8 highlights the depth of Christ's incarnation and obedience. Jesus did not merely take on human flesh; He lowered Himself to the lowest legal and social status. The phrase "even the death of the cross" emphasizes that Christ accepted a cursed and degrading death. Under Jewish legal tradition, anyone hung on a tree was considered under a curse, while under Roman custom, crucifixion was the ultimate degradation. By willingly submitting to this execution, Jesus took upon Himself the penalty and curse of human sin, transforming a symbol of Roman violence into the supreme display of divine mercy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:5-11, Paul describes the humbling and subsequent exaltation of Jesus Christ. The passage traces a downward sequence of humility: existing in the form of God, taking the form of a bondservant, coming in the likeness of men, and becoming obedient to the point of death. The final detail—"even the death of the cross"—marks the absolute bottom of this descent. The word "even" highlights the contrast between Christ's divine dignity and the indignity of His execution. In context, Paul uses this demonstration of self-giving love to urge the Philippian church to abandon selfish ambition and adopt the same humble attitude toward one another.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 16:24 — "Then Jesus said to His disciples, 'If anyone desires to come after Me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow Me.'"

  • John 19:17 — "And He, bearing His cross, went out to a place called the Place of a Skull, which is called in Hebrew, Golgotha."

  • 1 Corinthians 1:18 — "For the message of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God."

  • Galatians 5:11 — "And I, brethren, if I still preach circumcision, why do I still suffer persecution? Then the offense of the cross has ceased."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The stark contrast between the shame of the cross and the exaltation of Christ forms the backbone of Paul's message in this passage. Immediately after describing Jesus' death on the cross in verse 8, Paul writes in verse 9 that God has highly exalted Him and given Him the name above every name. The cross was not the ultimate end, but the path to God's vindication. For believers living in Philippi—a proud Roman military colony where status, honor, and citizenship were valued above all else—Paul's emphasis on the cross inverted the world's standard of greatness, proving that true honor is found in sacrificial service.
