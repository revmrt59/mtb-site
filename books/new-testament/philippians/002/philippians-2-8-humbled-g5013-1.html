HUMBLED - (tapeinóō, Strong's G5013)

Summary:

The word tapeinoō means to lower, bring down, or reduce in status and position. In ancient Greek culture, lowliness was viewed as a dishonorable condition, but Scripture turns this perspective upside down by presenting self-humbling as an essential spiritual virtue. In Philippians 2:8, the word describes how Jesus lowered Himself in status during His earthly life, submitting to human limitations and accepting even the shame of death on a cross.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greco-Roman culture, the concept of lowliness carried negative connotations. Society praised high social status, noble birth, public honor, and self-assertion. Being brought low was considered a misfortune or a sign of weakness, servitude, and shame. A humble posture was seen as fitting only for slaves or those forced into subjugation.

Scripture completely transformed this view. Throughout the Old and New Testaments, God resists the proud and exalts those who lower themselves before Him. Rather than viewing lowliness as weakness, biblical teaching reveals that lowering oneself before God is the proper response of a created being. When Paul writes to the believers in Philippi, he uses this word to describe the astounding journey of Jesus Christ, who set aside heavenly glory to take on the position of a servant.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word tapeinoō means to make low, bring down, or lower in elevation, status, or condition.

In physical contexts, the word refers to making a high place low, such as levelling a mountain or hill to clear a path. In personal and social contexts, it refers to lowering someone's rank, status, or self-estimation. The word can describe external circumstances that bring a person low, such as poverty, hardship, or humiliation. It also describes a deliberate action where someone lowers their position, steps down from privilege, or adopts a posture of meekness and submission.

When used of human behavior toward God or others, it means setting aside pride, self-aggrandizement, and demands for status in order to submit humbly to God's will and serve others.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Scriptures, tapeinoō is used in two primary ways: God bringing down the proud, and people lowering themselves in humility.

In the Greek Old Testament, God is repeatedly praised as the One who humbles the arrogant while lifting up the needy. Human attempts to exalt themselves above God inevitably end in being brought low. Conversely, humbling oneself in sackcloth and ashes was a recognized sign of genuine repentance.

In the New Testament, Jesus places this word at the heart of kingdom discipleship. He repeatedly teaches that whoever exalts himself will be brought low, but whoever humbles himself will be exalted. The apostles carry this theme forward, commanding believers to humble themselves under God's hand so that He may lift them up in due time. Paul also uses the word to describe enduring modest physical conditions or facing humbling circumstances during his ministry.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical usage of tapeinoō reveals a fundamental truth about God's kingdom: true greatness is found through lowering oneself in service. Human pride seeks upward mobility, self-exaltation, and personal glory. In contrast, divine grace flows downward to those who occupy a lowly place.

This truth reaches its climax in the person and work of Jesus Christ. Salvation does not come through human achievement reaching up to heaven, but through God stepping down into human history. The Son of God did not demand the honor due to Him, but stepped into a lower position to accomplish redemption. His journey demonstrates that self-humbling is not a loss of dignity, but the path through which God brings life, salvation, and ultimate exaltation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:8, Paul writes that Jesus "humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death, even the death of the cross."

The surrounding passage (Philippians 2:5–11) forms a magnificent description of Christ's condescension and exaltation. After highlighting that Christ possessed the very nature of God and took the form of a bondservant in human likeness, Paul uses tapeinoō in verse 8 to describe a further step in His earthly walk.

Jesus did not merely stop at taking human nature; He lowered Himself further by submitting to obedience all the way to death. In Roman society, death on a cross was the most disgraceful form of execution reserved for criminals and slaves. By accepting such a death, Jesus descended to the lowest possible level of earthly shame and suffering.

The statement that He "humbled Himself" reflects the reflexive phrasing in the text, showing that His lowliness was not an unexpected fate forced upon Him, but a willing path of submission to the Father's plan. Rather than asserting His rights as God, He chose the way of lowliness and cross-bearing for the sake of sinners.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 18:4 — "Therefore whoever humbles himself as this little child is the greatest in the kingdom of heaven."

  • Matthew 23:12 — "And whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted."

  • Philippians 4:12 — "I know how to be abased, and I know how to abound."

  • 1 Peter 5:6 — "Therefore humble yourselves under the mighty hand of God, that He may exalt you in due time."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Paul's purpose in explaining Christ's self-humbling in Philippians 2 is pastorally practical. He introduces this section by commanding believers to "let this mind be in you which was also in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 2:5). The self-lowering of Christ serves as the supreme model for relationships within the church. When believers abandon self-centered ambition and choose to humble themselves before one another, church unity is preserved and Christ's character is made visible to the world.
