EXALTED - (hyperypsóō, Strong's G5251)

Summary:

The Greek verb hyperupsoō means to exalt to the supreme height, to lift up above all others, or to raise to the highest place of honor. In Philippians 2:9, the word describes God elevating the Lord Jesus Christ to the supreme place of honor and authority following His voluntary humiliation and death on the cross. It marks the pivotal turn in Paul's description of Christ, showing that Christ's self-emptying leads directly to His divine exaltation above every created power.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb hyperupsoō is built from two common Greek components: hyper, meaning "above" or "beyond," and hypsoō, meaning "to lift up" or "to exalt." Combining these elements creates an intensified verb that expresses supreme exaltation or elevation far above everything else.

In the New Testament context, Paul uses this compound word in his letter to the Philippians as part of a classic early passage concerning Christ (Philippians 2:5–11). The surrounding passage contrasts the profound humility of Jesus—who emptied Himself, took the form of a bondservant, and became obedient to death on a cross—with the decisive response of God the Father, who lifted Him to the ultimate place of glory.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Hyperupsoō means to exalt to the highest position, to raise to supreme majesty, or to honor beyond measure.

While the basic verb hypsoō means "to lift up" or "to exalt," adding the prefix hyper intensifies the standard meaning. It describes an elevation that reaches the highest conceivable level. In this passage, the word does not merely indicate that Jesus was given higher status than before; it describes Him being placed in supreme authority above all existing powers, authorities, and names.

Here the word refers to God raising the resurrected Lord Jesus to heavenly glory and giving Him the name that is above every name.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The verb appears in Philippians 2:9 in the aorist tense, active voice, indicative mood, and singular form.

The aorist tense presents the exaltation as a complete historical reality, bringing into view the resurrection, ascension, and heavenly enthronement of Jesus. The active voice grammatically identifies God as the subject performing the action of exalting His Son. The contrast in the passage is direct: Jesus lowered Himself in servant-hearted obedience, and God exalted Him to the highest place.

4. Theological Implications

The exaltation of Christ reveals a fundamental biblical principle: God exalts those who humble themselves in obedience to Him. Jesus did not insist on holding onto His visible heavenly rights; instead, He submitted completely to the Father's plan. God's act of exalting Him demonstrates divine vindication of Christ's sacrificial work on the cross.

This supreme elevation also demonstrates the divine identity and universal authority of Jesus Christ. By placing Jesus at the highest level of honor and giving Him "the name which is above every name," God identifies Jesus as Lord (Kyrios), before whom every knee will bow and every tongue confess.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Philippians 2:9 begins with the word "Therefore," directly connecting Christ's exaltation to His humble submission described in the preceding verses. The movement of the passage travels from highest glory down to servant status on earth, down to death on a cross, and then back up to supreme exaltation over all creation.

In the broader argument of Philippians, Paul presents Christ's mindset as the supreme pattern for the Christian community. He encourages believers toward selflessness and unity by keeping their eyes on Jesus. By showing that God highly exalted Jesus after He lowered Himself, Paul demonstrates that humble service in God's kingdom is never in vain. Suffering and humble obedience are followed by divine vindication and honor.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • This term is unique to this context and does not appear elsewhere in the biblical corpus.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The compound structure of hyperupsoō reflects Paul's standard practice of adding hyper to standard verbs when describing the extraordinary works and attributes of God. Throughout his letters, Paul frequently uses hyper-compounds to express realities that exceed ordinary human measurement, such as overflowing grace or extraordinary victory. In this passage, the compound verb underscores that Christ's vindication was not a simple return to His previous condition, but a decisive, public display of His supreme cosmic lordship.
