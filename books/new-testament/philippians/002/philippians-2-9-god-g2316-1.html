GOD - (theós, Strong's G2316)

Summary:

The word theos refers to a god or deity, and throughout the New Testament it serves as the standard name for the one true God, the sovereign Creator and Ruler of the universe. While the term could refer to pagan deities in broader ancient culture, biblical writers used it primarily for the living God who reveals Himself in redemptive history. In Philippians 2:9, the word identifies God the Father as the active subject who vindicates and exalts Jesus Christ after His humble obedience and death on the cross.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Greek literature, theos was used broadly for any deity or divine being worshipped in the pagan world. However, when Jewish and early Christian authors wrote in Greek, they used theos to translate the Hebrew terms for God (Elohim and YHWH). In doing so, they filled the word with the rich truth of biblical monotheism.

In the New Testament, theos usually refers to God the Father, the personal Creator and Covenant Lord who initiates salvation. In specific passages, it is also applied directly to Jesus Christ to assert His true divinity. In Philippians 2:9, Paul uses the word within a grand declaration of Christ's humiliation and exaltation, identifying God the Father as the One who responds to the Son's earthly sacrifice by raising Him to supreme glory.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word theos denotes God or a deity. In biblical usage, its primary meaning is the supreme, uncreated Creator and Governor of all things.

  • The word can refer to the one true God, highlighting His supreme power, authority, and holiness.

  • When used with a divine title or context, it often specifies God the Father as distinct in person from the Son and the Holy Spirit, while sharing the same divine nature.

  • In rare contexts, the word can refer to false gods or idols worshipped by nations outside the biblical faith.

In Philippians 2:9, theos functions as a proper subject, designating God the Father who acts directly to honor His Son.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the New Testament, theos carries several distinct nuances depending on the context:

  • Divine Sovereign: The word identifies God as the ultimate ruler over history, nature, and redemption.

  • Father and Author of Salvation: It frequently highlights the Father's role in sending the Son, extending grace, and orchestrating eternal redemption.

  • Divinity of Christ: In key passages, New Testament writers call Jesus theos to affirm that He possesses full deity alongside the Father.

Grammatically, in Philippians 2:9, the noun is masculine singular in the nominative case, identifying God as the explicit subject performing the main action of the sentence. He is the One who exalts Christ and bestows upon Him the highest name.

4. Theological Implications

The use of theos in Philippians 2:9 highlights essential truths about God's character and His redemptive plan:

  • Fatherly Vindication: Jesus voluntarily lowered Himself to the point of dying on a cross. Human rulers condemned Him, but God the Father reversed human judgment by raising and exalting Him.

  • Sovereign Initiative: Salvation is established through God's active purpose. The Father does not leave the Son in death; He crowns His work with glory.

  • Trinitarian Harmony: The passage shows the distinction of persons and complete unity of purpose within the Godhead. The Son willingly humbles Himself to accomplish the Father's plan, and the Father exalts the Son so that all glory ultimately redounds to God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Philippians 2:5-11, Paul presents what is widely known as the Christ Hymn. Verses 6–8 describe the downward path of Christ: though existing in the form of God, He emptied Himself, took the form of a servant, and became obedient unto death on a cross.

Verse 9 marks the decisive turning point in the hymn, signaled by the word "Therefore." Human action ended at the cross, but divine action begins at the exaltation. Here, theos introduces God the Father as the One who breaks into the narrative to elevate the crucified Jesus. The surrounding contrast emphasizes that while human beings rejected Christ, God super-exalted Him. The Father gives Him the name above every name so that every knee should bow and every tongue confess Jesus Christ as Lord.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Matthew 1:23 – "Behold, the virgin shall be with child, and bear a Son, and they shall call His name Immanuel," which is translated, "God with us."

  • Acts 3:15 – "...and killed the Prince of life, whom God raised from the dead, of which we are witnesses."

  • Romans 10:9 – "...that if you confess with your mouth the Lord Jesus and believe in your heart that God has raised Him from the dead, you will be saved."

  • 1 John 4:7 – "Beloved, let us love one another, for love is of God; and everyone who loves is born of God and knows God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Comparing Philippians 2:6 and Philippians 2:9 illuminates the full scope of Christ's identity. In verse 6, Paul notes that Jesus existed in the form of God (theos), affirming His eternal divine nature. In verse 9, God (theos) exalts the incarnate Son in His resurrected humanity.

This dramatic sequence demonstrates that God's kingdom operates on a principle opposite to earthly pride: self-humbling obedience leads to divinely granted honor. The Father takes delight in glorifying the Son who humbled Himself for the redemption of humanity.
