Book Overview

At a Glance

Author: Paul, “a bondservant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ” (1:1).
Author

Paul, “a bondservant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ” (1:1).

Audience

Titus, Paul’s coworker in Crete, and through him the Cretan churches (3:15).

Date

Commonly placed in the mid-60s AD; the letter gives no date.

Historical Setting

Young churches across Crete needed qualified leaders, and deceptive teachers were overturning households.

Purpose

To equip Titus to set the Cretan churches in order with faithful elders, firm correction of false teaching, and instruction in the conduct that God’s saving grace produces.

Central Message

The saving grace of God in Jesus Christ creates a people whose lives agree with sound doctrine and are zealous for good works. Titus must establish faithful leadership, confront corrupt teaching, and instruct believers to display that grace in their households, church life, and conduct toward the wider world.

Key Themes

Truth and godliness; healthy teaching; qualified leadership; good works; grace that teaches; God our Savior; the blessed hope; adorning the doctrine; gentleness toward all; guarding unity.

Book Flow

  • Paul’s Commission and Greeting to Titus (Titus 1:1–4). Paul’s apostleship serves the truth that accords with godliness.

  • Establish Faithful Elders and Silence Corrupt Teachers (Titus 1:5–16). Qualified elders must hold the faithful word and answer those who upset households.

  • Teach the Conduct That Flows from Saving Grace (Titus 2:1–3:11). Grace and the Spirit’s renewal ground godly living in every relationship.

  • Arrange the Continuing Work and Close with Grace (Titus 3:12–15). Paul plans the next steps, urges good works, and closes with grace.

Go Deeper

Background

Author

The letter opens with its writer’s name and calling: “Paul, a bondservant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ” (1:1). Some modern scholars question whether Paul wrote the letters to Timothy and Titus, but the letter presents itself as Paul’s, and the church has received it that way from early times. Paul’s self-introduction already announces the letter’s concern. His apostleship serves “the faith of God’s elect and the acknowledgment of the truth which accords with godliness.” From the first verse, truth is something that shows up in how people live.

Audience

Paul writes “to Titus, a true son in our common faith” (1:4). Titus was a Greek believer whom Paul took with him to Jerusalem, and there, Paul says, not even Titus “was compelled to be circumcised” (Galatians 2:1–3). Paul later sent him to Corinth during a strained season in that church and called him “my partner and fellow worker” (2 Corinthians 8:23). He was a proven coworker whom Paul trusted with hard assignments.

Now Titus is in Crete, and part of the trouble there comes from teachers “of the circumcision” (1:10). A Gentile coworker whose own case had been part of the circumcision question in Jerusalem was well placed to answer them. The letter is personal, but it was meant to be heard more widely. It closes, “Grace be with you all” (3:15), and its instructions to older men, older women, young women, young men, and bondservants were meant for the Cretan churches themselves.

Date

The letter gives no date or place of writing. Acts 27 records Paul’s stop at Fair Havens on Crete during his voyage to Rome as a prisoner (Acts 27:7–13), but it does not describe the ministry implied by Titus 1:5, where Paul says he left Titus there to finish a work already begun. Many therefore place that ministry, and this letter, in a period after the events Acts records, commonly in the mid-60s AD, near the time of 1 Timothy. That is a probable reconstruction, not something the letter states, and the letter’s message does not depend on fixing the date more precisely.

Historical Setting

Crete is a large island in the Mediterranean Sea, south of Greece, with many towns. Paul tells Titus to appoint elders “in every city” (1:5), which suggests believing communities scattered across the island. Cretans were among the crowd in Jerusalem who heard the gospel at Pentecost (Acts 2:11), so the message may have reached the island early, but the churches still lacked order and leadership.

The church in this period met in homes, and Christian households were the setting where the gospel was lived day by day. That is why the danger in Crete was so serious. The false teachers “subvert whole households” (1:11), and much of Paul’s instruction addresses life inside the home: husbands and wives, parents and children, older and younger believers, and bondservants and their masters.

Outsiders were watching. Paul wants the young women to live so “that the word of God may not be blasphemed” (2:5), Titus to speak so “that one who is an opponent may be ashamed” (2:8), and bondservants to serve so “that they may adorn the doctrine of God our Savior in all things” (2:10). The Cretan churches lived among neighbors who would judge the gospel by the lives of the people who claimed it.

Occasion

Paul states the reason plainly: “For this reason I left you in Crete, that you should set in order the things that are lacking, and appoint elders in every city as I commanded you” (1:5). The work had begun, but the churches were unfinished.

The need was urgent. Paul charges that there were “many insubordinate, both idle talkers and deceivers, especially those of the circumcision” (1:10), teaching “for the sake of dishonest gain” (1:11). They were promoting “Jewish fables and commandments of men” (1:14) and stirring up “foolish disputes, genealogies, contentions, and strivings about the law” (3:9). They claimed to know God, but “in works they deny Him” (1:16). Paul writes to equip Titus for this work until Artemas or Tychicus arrives to relieve him, when Titus is to come to Paul at Nicopolis (3:12).

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Titus is a short pastoral letter about putting a young church in order. It tells Titus to appoint elders whose character and teaching are sound, and it describes the qualifications for that office. It exposes the false teachers who are upsetting households and calls Titus to rebuke them. It gives instructions for older men, older women, young women, young men, and bondservants, and then for the whole church’s conduct toward rulers and neighbors. It warns against fruitless disputes and divisive people, and it closes with travel plans, a final call to good works, and greetings.

What keeps this from being a manual of rules is that Paul twice stops to explain the gospel behind every instruction. In 2:11–14 he describes “the grace of God that brings salvation,” which teaches believers to say no to ungodliness and to live for God while they wait for Christ’s appearing. In 3:3–7 he recalls what believers once were and how God saved them “not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy” (3:5). These two passages are the foundation of the letter. Every call to good conduct rests on what God has already done in Christ and by His Spirit.

The result is a letter in which doctrine and daily life cannot be separated. Saving grace forms a people whose lives make the truth attractive. The false teachers “profess to know God, but in works they deny Him” (1:16); believers are to “adorn the doctrine of God our Savior in all things” (2:10).

Why This Book Matters

Titus shows what a healthy church looks like while it is still being set in order. Paul does not begin with programs or buildings. He begins with qualified elders who hold “the faithful word” (1:9), healthy teaching that produces healthy believers, households where faith is lived out, and a church whose gentleness and good works speak to its neighbors. Every generation of the church needs that picture, especially when it is starting something new or repairing what has gone wrong.

The letter also holds together two things the church has often pulled apart: sound doctrine and godly living. For Paul, teaching is not sound simply because its vocabulary is correct. It is sound when it is healthy, when it produces sober, loving, patient, and faithful people (2:1–2). The false teachers in Crete had religious words without changed lives. Titus insists that the truth about God must be seen in the way His people speak, work, and treat one another.

Finally, Titus contains two of the New Testament’s clearest summaries of salvation, and it places them in the middle of practical instructions. Salvation is entirely God’s mercy, “not by works of righteousness which we have done” (3:5), yet the same grace teaches believers how to live (2:12) and redeems a people “zealous for good works” (2:14). Titus answers both the person who thinks good works earn salvation and the person who thinks grace makes good works unimportant. In this letter even a bondservant’s honest work can “adorn the doctrine of God our Savior” (2:10).

Book Flow

Titus 1:1–4: Paul’s Commission and Greeting to Titus

Paul introduces himself as “a bondservant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ,” and he ties his calling to “the acknowledgment of the truth which accords with godliness” and to the “hope of eternal life which God, who cannot lie, promised before time began” (1:1–2). God has now made His word known through the preaching entrusted to Paul. He greets Titus as “a true son in our common faith,” with grace, mercy, and peace from God the Father and “the Lord Jesus Christ our Savior” (1:4).

Titus 1:5–16: Establish Faithful Elders and Silence Corrupt Teachers

Paul left Titus in Crete to “set in order the things that are lacking, and appoint elders in every city” (1:5). An elder must be “blameless,” faithful in his marriage and household, free from self-will, anger, drunkenness, violence, and greed, and marked by hospitality, self-control, and holiness. Above all he must be “holding fast the faithful word,” so that he is able “both to exhort and convict those who contradict” (1:9). Such leaders are needed because deceivers are subverting whole households for dishonest gain. Titus must “rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith” (1:13), for though they profess to know God, “in works they deny Him” (1:16).

Titus 2:1–3:11: Teach the Conduct That Flows from Saving Grace

“But as for you, speak the things which are proper for sound doctrine” (2:1). Paul describes the life that fits healthy teaching for older men, older women, young women, young men, and bondservants, and he calls Titus to be “a pattern of good works” himself (2:7). Then comes the heart of the movement: “the grace of God that brings salvation has appeared to all men,” teaching believers to deny ungodliness and live godly lives while “looking for the blessed hope and glorious appearing of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ” (2:11–13). The instruction continues across the chapter break into public life: believers are to be subject to rulers, “ready for every good work,” and gentle toward all (3:1–2), because “we ourselves were also once foolish” until “the kindness and the love of God our Savior toward man appeared” and He saved us “according to His mercy” (3:3–5). Paul wants these things affirmed so that believers “maintain good works,” while foolish disputes are avoided and a divisive person is warned and then rejected (3:8–11).

Titus 3:12–15: Arrange the Continuing Work and Close with Grace

Paul will send Artemas or Tychicus, and then Titus is to come to him at Nicopolis, where Paul has decided to spend the winter. Titus must help Zenas the lawyer and Apollos on their way so “that they may lack nothing” (3:13). Paul returns one last time to his main concern, “let our people also learn to maintain good works, to meet urgent needs, that they may not be unfruitful” (3:14), and closes, “Grace be with you all” (3:15).

Key Themes to Watch For

  • Truth and godliness. Truth that is known must be seen in life; denial of God can be done “in works” (1:1, 16).

  • Healthy teaching. “Sound doctrine” produces sound faith, love, and patience (1:9, 13; 2:1–2, 8).

  • Qualified leadership. Elders are chosen for character, household faithfulness, and grip on the faithful word (1:5–9).

  • Good works. Good works are the fruit and purpose of redemption, never its price (1:16; 2:7, 14; 3:1, 8, 14).

  • Grace that teaches. God’s grace not only saves but trains His people to live for Him (2:11–12).

  • God our Savior. Paul gives the title “Savior” both to God and to Jesus Christ (1:3–4; 2:10, 13; 3:4, 6).

  • The blessed hope. Believers live now in light of Christ’s appearing and the hope of eternal life (1:2; 2:13; 3:7).

  • Adorning the doctrine. Ordinary conduct commends the gospel to outsiders (2:5, 8, 10).

  • Gentleness toward all. Believers who remember what they once were treat others with humility (3:1–3).

  • Guarding unity. Fruitless disputes and persistent divisiveness must be confronted (3:9–11).

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

  • Elders and bishop. Paul tells Titus to “appoint elders in every city” (1:5) and then continues, “For a bishop must be blameless, as a steward of God” (1:7). The two words name the same office: “elder” points to maturity, and “bishop,” or overseer, to the work of oversight. The pattern built on 1:5 is one church community in each city, even if it met in several homes, shepherded by a plurality of qualified elders rather than a single leader.

  • Blameless. The word that governs the elder’s qualifications (1:6–7) means a life of established integrity that gives no grounds for accusation, not sinless perfection.

  • The husband of one wife. A faithful husband, a one-woman man marked by sexual integrity and marital fidelity (1:6). The phrase does not automatically exclude a man who is unmarried, widowed and remarried, or divorced under circumstances Scripture permits, though a man’s history and present character are still weighed by Scripture.

  • Faithful children. The NKJV’s “faithful children not accused of dissipation or insubordination” (1:6) is best understood as children whose conduct is faithful and orderly, not a requirement that every child has professed saving faith (compare 1 Timothy 3:4–5). The NLT takes it the other way: “his children must be believers.”

  • Sound doctrine. The word “sound” (Greek hygiainō) means “to be healthy.” Sound doctrine is healthy teaching, teaching that produces spiritually healthy, mature, and godly believers (1:9, 13; 2:1–2). Doctrine and transformation belong together.

  • Those of the circumcision. Teachers from a Jewish background, or influenced by one, who promoted “Jewish fables and commandments of men” (1:14), genealogies, and disputes “about the law” (3:9). The letter does not describe their teaching in detail. It judges them by its fruit: division, greed, and denial of God “in works” (1:16).

  • The Cretan saying. “Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons” (1:12) quotes “a prophet of their own,” commonly identified as the Cretan poet Epimenides. Paul uses a proverb the Cretans themselves knew to describe the false teachers’ behavior. His aim is restoration, “that they may be sound in the faith” (1:13), not contempt for a people.

  • Bondservants. The bondservants of 2:9–10 were enslaved people, not simply employees. Paul addresses them as full members of the church whose faithful conduct can “adorn the doctrine of God our Savior.” His instructions do not endorse the ownership of people or excuse any mistreatment of them.

  • Appearing. Paul says that God’s grace “has appeared” (2:11) and that “the kindness and the love of God our Savior toward man appeared” (3:4) in Christ’s first coming. Believers now look for the “glorious appearing” of Christ (2:13). The letter places believers between these two appearings without setting out a detailed timetable of the end.

  • The washing of regeneration (3:5). God “saved us, through the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit.” The washing is the inward cleansing and new birth the Holy Spirit works in a person. Where baptism is in view, it is the outward sign and seal of that inward cleansing; the water itself does not cause new birth. Whether Paul refers directly to baptism here is debated, but salvation rests on God’s mercy and the Spirit’s work, not on any human act.

  • A divisive man (3:10). Someone who persists in stirring up division after a first and second warning. The instruction protects the gospel and the church’s unity; it is not aimed at honest questions or legitimate discussion of doctrine.

Christ and Life

In the middle of instructions about household conduct, Paul describes Jesus Christ as “our great God and Savior” (2:13). The letter prepares for that confession. In each chapter Paul gives the title “Savior” first to God and then to Jesus Christ: “God our Savior” and “the Lord Jesus Christ our Savior” (1:3–4), “God our Savior” and “our great God and Savior Jesus Christ” (2:10, 13), “God our Savior” and “Jesus Christ our Savior” (3:4, 6). The salvation Paul preaches is God’s own work, and Jesus Christ stands fully within it. The NLT reads 2:13 the same way: “our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ.”

This Savior “gave Himself for us, that He might redeem us from every lawless deed and purify for Himself His own special people, zealous for good works” (2:14). Christ did not die only to cancel a debt. He gave Himself to have a people who belong to Him and who are made clean. The words recall God’s promise to Israel that they would be “a special treasure to Me above all people” (Exodus 19:5), and Peter applies similar language to the church, “His own special people” (1 Peter 2:9). Good works in Titus come from belonging to Christ; they are the life of people He has purchased and purified.

The fullest picture of salvation in the letter is in 3:4–7, and the whole Trinity is at work in it. “The kindness and the love of God our Savior toward man appeared.” He saved us “according to His mercy,” through “the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit, whom He poured out on us abundantly through Jesus Christ our Savior,” so that, “having been justified by His grace,” we “should become heirs according to the hope of eternal life.” The Spirit’s inward cleansing and new life are given through Christ, and they make believers heirs of God. Read alongside Ezekiel 36:25–27, where God promised to “sprinkle clean water on you,” give “a new heart,” and “put My Spirit within you,” Titus 3:5–6 describes the kind of cleansing and renewal the prophets looked for. Paul does not cite Ezekiel here, but the prophet’s promise and Paul’s description fit together.

That is why grace in Titus is never a license to live carelessly. The grace that brings salvation is the same grace that is “teaching us” to deny ungodliness and to live “soberly, righteously, and godly in the present age” (2:12). Believers are saved “not of works,” and they are also God’s “workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works” (Ephesians 2:9–10). The Spirit who renews them produces a life that agrees with the truth they confess.

Titus also keeps believers humble toward others. “For we ourselves were also once foolish, disobedient, deceived” (3:3). Those who remember where God’s mercy found them have reason to be “gentle, showing all humility to all men” (3:2). They live in the present age with their eyes on “the blessed hope” (2:13), and the hope of Christ’s appearing gives them patience and purpose in ordinary places like Crete.

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Read the commands in light of the gospel passages. The instructions of 2:1–10 and 3:1–2 rest on 2:11–14 and 3:3–7. Titus is not a list of rules for earning God’s approval; it describes the life that God’s grace produces.

Remember that the household instructions, including those to bondservants, were given to real people in a first-century setting. Their stated purpose is that “the word of God may not be blasphemed” (2:5). They do not endorse every social arrangement of that world. In the same way, the call to be “subject to rulers and authorities” (3:1) stands alongside the apostles’ answer, “We ought to obey God rather than men” (Acts 5:29).

Read the letter’s severity in light of its aim. Titus is to rebuke sharply so that people become “sound in the faith” (1:13), and divisive people are warned more than once before they are rejected (3:10). The goal is healthy believers and a united church.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

  • The Pastoral Letters. Titus, 1 Timothy, and 2 Timothy are often called the Pastoral Letters because they are written to Paul’s coworkers about caring for churches. The qualifications for elders in Titus 1:5–9 closely parallel 1 Timothy 3:2–7.

  • Titus later. Paul’s last letter mentions that Titus had gone to Dalmatia, on the eastern coast of the Adriatic Sea (2 Timothy 4:10).

  • Nicopolis (3:12). Several cities bore this name, which means “city of victory.” The one Paul means is most likely Nicopolis in Epirus, on the western coast of Greece. Paul writes that he “decided to spend the winter there,” which does not mean he had already arrived.

  • Paul’s coworkers. Tychicus was a trusted messenger, “a beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord” (Ephesians 6:21; compare Colossians 4:7; 2 Timothy 4:12). Artemas is mentioned only here. Apollos was the eloquent teacher from Alexandria known from Acts 18:24 and 1 Corinthians 3:6. Zenas “the lawyer” (3:13) may have been an expert in the Jewish law or in Roman law; the letter does not say which.

  • Translation differences. In 3:5 the NLT paraphrases, “He washed away our sins, giving us a new birth and new life through the Holy Spirit.” In 2:1 the NLT renders sound doctrine as “wholesome teaching.”
