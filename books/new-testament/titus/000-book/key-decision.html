Book Questions

Who wrote Titus? (Titus 1:1–4)

The letter opens, “Paul, a bondservant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ... To Titus, a true son in our common faith” (1:1, 4). MTB receives it as Paul’s own letter to Titus, his coworker, whom he had left in Crete to finish setting the churches in order (1:5). Some scholars think a later writer used Paul’s name. MTB takes the letter’s own claim at face value.

The letter’s directions about the churches, the elders, and Paul’s travel plans (3:12–13) read as one coworker’s practical instructions to another.

Read: Titus 1:1–5, Titus 3:12–15, 2 Corinthians 8:23, Galatians 2:1–3

If we are saved by mercy and not by works, why does Titus stress good works so often? (Titus 2:14; 3:5, 8)

Titus is clear that God saved us “not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy” (3:5). Yet the letter keeps returning to good works (1:16; 2:7, 14; 3:1, 8, 14). Believers “should be careful to maintain good works” (3:8).

MTB reads the letter this way: saving grace forms a people whose good works display the beauty of the truth. Christ “gave Himself for us, that He might redeem us from every lawless deed and purify for Himself His own special people, zealous for good works” (2:14). Good works are the purpose and fruit of salvation, never the way to earn it.

That is why Paul says the false teachers “profess to know God, but in works they deny Him” (1:16), while faithful servants “adorn the doctrine of God our Savior in all things” (2:10), showing its beauty by how they live. Truth and godliness belong together (1:1).

Read: Titus 1:1, Titus 1:16, Titus 2:10–14, Titus 3:3–8, Titus 3:14, Ephesians 2:8–10 · Related: What is “the washing of regeneration”? Is it baptism?, Does “salvation has appeared to all men” mean everyone will be saved?

Titus 1 · 6 questions

What is “the faith of God’s elect”? (v. 1)

Paul calls himself an apostle “according to the faith of God’s elect and the acknowledgment of the truth which accords with godliness” (v. 1). MTB’s preferred reading takes “faith” in both senses: the truth God’s chosen people believe, and their trust in God Himself. Paul’s ministry serves both.

That truth “accords with godliness.” Right belief and real trust lead to a godly life, the theme that runs through the whole letter (1:16; 2:11–12; 3:8).

Read: Titus 1:1–3, Titus 2:11–14, Titus 3:8 · Related: If we are saved by mercy and not by works, why does Titus stress good works so often?

Should each church be led by a group of elders, and is a “bishop” a separate office? (vv. 5–7)

Paul left Titus in Crete to “set in order the things that are lacking, and appoint elders in every city” (v. 5). Then he continues, “For a bishop must be blameless, as a steward of God” (v. 7). The word “bishop” means overseer. Paul moves from “elders” to “bishop” while describing the same men, so MTB understands them as one office, not a higher bishop over the elders.

MTB strongly holds that the New Testament pattern for local church government is a plurality of biblically qualified elders led by a set-man, a lead elder who is first among equals. In the early years each city had one church. It might meet in one home or several because it was still small, but it was shepherded by a team of qualified elders. Verse 5 does not spell all of this out; it is a firm MTB conviction built on Paul’s command to appoint elders, plural, in every city.

This was Titus’s first step in putting the Cretan churches in order, and it was urgent, because false teachers “subvert whole households” (v. 11). Titus was to appoint men qualified in character and faithful to the word, not merely gifted speakers. Paul says “blameless” twice (vv. 6–7). It means a man of proven integrity whom no one can rightly accuse, not a man without sin.

Read: Titus 1:5–11, Acts 14:23, Acts 20:17, 28, 1 Timothy 3:1–7, 1 Peter 5:1–2 · Related: What does “the husband of one wife” mean?, Must an elder’s children all be believers?

What does “the husband of one wife” mean? (v. 6)

An elder must be “the husband of one wife” (v. 6). The Greek phrase reads literally as a one-woman man. Readers have taken it in five main ways: an elder must be married; a widower who remarries is excluded; polygamy is forbidden; a divorced man is excluded; or an elder must be a man of sexual purity and complete faithfulness to his wife.

MTB holds the last view. Paul is describing a faithful husband whose life shows sexual integrity and proven godly character. The phrase is not an automatic bar against a man who is single, who remarried after his wife died, or who was divorced under biblically legitimate circumstances.

That does not make a man’s marriage history irrelevant. His history and his present character must still be weighed by Scripture, history, time and witness.

Read: Titus 1:6, 1 Timothy 3:2 · Related: Should each church be led by a group of elders, and is a “bishop” a separate office?, Must an elder’s children all be believers?

Must an elder’s children all be believers? (v. 6)

The NKJV says an elder must have “faithful children not accused of dissipation or insubordination” (v. 6). The Greek word can mean “believing” or “faithful,” and some translations read it as children who are believers.

MTB understands it as faithful, orderly conduct. The rest of the phrase points that way: children not known for wild living or rebellion. Paul’s parallel in 1 Timothy 3:4–5 asks whether a man rules his own house well. The qualification tests whether he leads his household faithfully. It does not make him responsible for producing his children’s conversion.

Read: Titus 1:6, 1 Timothy 3:4–5 · Related: What does “the husband of one wife” mean?

Who were the false teachers in Crete? (vv. 10–14)

Paul describes “many insubordinate, both idle talkers and deceivers, especially those of the circumcision” (v. 10). They gave heed to “Jewish fables and commandments of men” (v. 14) and stirred up “strivings about the law” (3:9). MTB’s preferred reading is that they were mainly Jewish-Christian teachers who pressed law-keeping, purity rules, and legends about genealogies. Other influences may have mixed in.

Their teaching also gave license to sin and did not change how people lived. Though they “profess to know God,” they deny Him by their works (v. 16), and they upset “whole households” for “dishonest gain” (v. 11). Paul answers with the grace of God, “teaching us that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly in the present age” (2:12). Sound teaching changes lives.

Read: Titus 1:10–16, Titus 2:11–12, Titus 3:9–11, 1 Timothy 1:3–7 · Related: Does “To the pure all things are pure” set aside the food and purity laws?, Who is the “divisive man,” and what does “reject” mean?

Does “To the pure all things are pure” set aside the food and purity laws? (vv. 14–15)

“To the pure all things are pure, but to those who are defiled and unbelieving nothing is pure” (v. 15). Paul is answering teachers who pressed “commandments of men” about outward purity (v. 14). For the believer, food and other things God created are not unclean in themselves. Jesus taught that what enters a person’s stomach does not defile his heart (Mark 7:18–23), and Paul says, “All things indeed are pure” (Romans 14:20). Purity rules, whether drawn from the old ceremonial laws or added by men, do not make anyone clean before God.

What defiles is a defiled mind and conscience. The verse is about food and purity rules, not moral conduct. The same letter condemns lawless deeds and calls believers to deny “ungodliness and worldly lusts” (2:12).

Read: Titus 1:14–16, Mark 7:14–23, Romans 14:14–20, Titus 2:11–12 · Related: Who were the false teachers in Crete?

Titus 2 · 4 questions

Is “sound doctrine” just correct theology? (v. 1)

“Speak the things which are proper for sound doctrine” (v. 1). The word “sound” means healthy or whole. Sound doctrine is healthy teaching, and it produces spiritually healthy, mature, and godly believers. MTB strongly believes that teaching from the Bible must produce spiritual health, lived out in Christlike character and fruit in daily life and in our closest relationships. Sound doctrine should not result in strife, arguments, contentiousness, or division over secondary issues (1 Timothy 1:5–9).

That is why Paul immediately applies it to older men, older women, younger women, younger men, and servants (vv. 2–10). The source of that life is God’s grace, which teaches us to live godly lives (vv. 11–14). Doctrine and transformation belong together. Godliness is not mere outward behavior apart from grace.

Read: Titus 2:1–14, Titus 1:9, 1 Timothy 1:5–9

Does Titus 2 approve of slavery? (vv. 9–10)

“Bondservants” (v. 9) were enslaved people in the Roman world, not simply employees. Paul tells them to serve faithfully “that they may adorn the doctrine of God our Savior in all things” (v. 10). Their faithful work would make the teaching about God attractive to those who watched them. His aim is the gospel’s witness in the situation they were in.

These instructions do not endorse owning people, deny their dignity, or authorize abuse. Paul elsewhere encourages a slave who can gain freedom to take it (1 Corinthians 7:21), and he calls masters to answer to their own Master in heaven (Colossians 4:1). The principles of submission, excellent work, and faithful service in these verses still apply to the relationship between employees and employers.

Read: Titus 2:9–10, 1 Corinthians 7:21–23, Colossians 4:1, Philemon 1:15–16

Does “salvation has appeared to all men” mean everyone will be saved? (v. 11)

“For the grace of God that brings salvation has appeared to all men” (v. 11). Grace has been revealed to every kind of person, including the slaves Paul has just addressed. That is not a claim that every person is automatically saved.

The same grace “teaches” us to deny ungodliness and live for God while we wait for Christ (vv. 12–13). Grace that saves also trains.

Read: Titus 2:11–14, 1 Timothy 2:3–6 · Related: If we are saved by mercy and not by works, why does Titus stress good works so often?

Does verse 13 call Jesus “God”? (v. 13)

Yes. Believers look for the “glorious appearing of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ” (v. 13). MTB reads “great God and Savior” as one Person, Jesus Christ. The next verse continues with Him alone: He “gave Himself for us” (v. 14). Some have read the phrase as speaking of the Father and of Jesus separately, but the wording most naturally names Jesus as both.

The “blessed hope” is Christ’s return. This verse does not supply a detailed timetable of end-time events.

Read: Titus 2:13–14, 2 Peter 1:1, John 20:28

Titus 3 · 3 questions

How should believers live under rulers who do not honor God? (vv. 1–2)

The churches of Crete lived under pagan Roman rule. Paul tells Titus, “Remind them to be subject to rulers and authorities, to obey, to be ready for every good work, to speak evil of no one, to be peaceable, gentle, showing all humility to all men” (vv. 1–2). Believers are to be good citizens who actively do good in their communities, not rebels and not people who withdraw.

Paul grounds this in humility: “For we ourselves were also once foolish” (v. 3), until God’s kindness appeared (v. 4). Obedience to rulers has a limit. When they command what God forbids, “We ought to obey God rather than men” (Acts 5:29).

Read: Titus 3:1–7, Romans 13:1–7, 1 Peter 2:13–17, Acts 5:29

What is “the washing of regeneration”? Is it baptism? (v. 5)

God saved us “through the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit, whom He poured out on us abundantly through Jesus Christ our Savior” (vv. 5–6). Christians understand this in several ways. Some believe baptism is God’s appointed means of new birth. Others see baptism as the outward sign and seal of the Spirit’s inward cleansing. Others read the washing as the Spirit’s cleansing itself, with no direct reference to baptism.

MTB’s reading is that the Holy Spirit gives the inward cleansing and new life. Where baptism is in view, it is the outward sign and seal of that work.

Either way, the source of salvation is plain: “not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy.” God’s promise to cleanse His people and put His Spirit within them stands behind this verse (Ezekiel 36:25–27).

Read: Titus 3:4–7, Ezekiel 36:25–27, John 3:5, Ephesians 5:26 · Related: If we are saved by mercy and not by works, why does Titus stress good works so often?

Who is the “divisive man,” and what does “reject” mean? (vv. 9–11)

Paul tells Titus to “avoid foolish disputes, genealogies, contentions, and strivings about the law; for they are unprofitable and useless” (v. 9). Then: “Reject a divisive man after the first and second admonition” (v. 10).

MTB understands this in two parts. Believers should avoid fruitless theological quarrels. Church leaders must warn a divisive person, and if he keeps dividing after repeated correction, refuse his divisive influence. If he remains unrepentant, leaders are to mark him as divisive and separate him from the flock. This protects the gospel, the church’s unity, and its witness.

The command is not aimed at every honest question or every doctrinal discussion. Legitimate discussion and needed correction are still part of healthy church life.

Read: Titus 3:8–11, Romans 16:17, 2 Timothy 2:23–26
