Titus 1:1 Paul, a bondservant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ, according to the faith of God's elect and the acknowledgment of the truth which accords with godliness,(NKJV)

Titus 1:1 This letter is from Paul, a slave of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ. I have been sent to proclaim faith to those God has chosen and to teach them to know the truth that shows them how to live godly lives. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul opens his letter by describing himself as a servant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ, sent to build up the faith of God's chosen people and lead them into truth that produces godly living. True knowledge of God is never just information; it is meant to change how people live.

Verse Explanation

Paul opens his letter by describing himself as a servant of God and an apostle of Jesus Christ, sent to build up the faith of God's chosen people and lead them into truth that produces godly living. True knowledge of God is never just information; it is meant to change how people live.

"Paul, a bondservant of God"

Paul was once Saul of Tarsus, a Pharisee who persecuted the church until the risen Jesus confronted him on the road to Damascus (Acts 9:1–6). He most likely wrote this letter late in his ministry, after many years of preaching and planting churches. The Greek word translated "bondservant" means a slave, someone who belongs entirely to a master and lives to do his will. In his other letters Paul usually calls himself a bondservant "of Jesus Christ" (Romans 1:1; Philippians 1:1). Only here does he call himself a bondservant "of God." That title recalls the great servants of the Old Testament. Moses, for example, is called "the servant of the LORD" (Deuteronomy 34:5). Paul does not begin by presenting his own importance. He begins by saying whose he is.

"and an apostle of Jesus Christ,"

An apostle is someone sent out with authority to represent the one who sent him. Paul's commission came directly from Christ, not from any human appointment. He describes himself elsewhere as an apostle "not from men nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father" (Galatians 1:1). From the beginning the Lord had said of him, "he is a chosen vessel of Mine to bear My name before Gentiles, kings, and the children of Israel" (Acts 9:15).

Titus already knew Paul well, so Paul did not need to prove his credentials to him. But Titus faced resistance in the churches on Crete, and this letter gave him Paul's full authority as he worked to set those churches in order. Later Paul tells him, "Speak these things, exhort, and rebuke with all authority. Let no one despise you" (2:15). The two titles belong together. Paul has authority as an apostle because he is first a slave who belongs wholly to God.

"according to the faith of God's elect"

Here "according to" points to the purpose of Paul's apostleship. He was sent for the sake of the faith of God's chosen people. "Elect" means chosen. In the Old Testament Israel was God's chosen people, and in the New Testament the term describes all who belong to God through Christ (Colossians 3:12). God's choosing does not make preaching and teaching unnecessary. It is the reason Paul labored, as he told Timothy: "Therefore I endure all things for the sake of the elect, that they also may obtain the salvation which is in Christ Jesus with eternal glory" (2 Timothy 2:10). Paul's work was to bring God's people to faith and to strengthen that faith once it began.

"and the acknowledgment of the truth which accords with godliness,"

The Greek word translated "acknowledgment" means more than knowing facts. It describes recognizing the truth and personally embracing it. Paul also says this truth "accords with godliness." In other words, it fits with a life that honors God and leads to one. "Godliness" means reverence for God that shows up in how a person actually lives.

Paul never treats the gospel as information people can hold without being changed by it. Later in this letter he says that the grace of God teaches us that, "denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly in the present age" (2:12). That conviction runs through the whole letter. It is why the elders Titus appoints must be men of proven character, and why Paul gives such practical instruction to older men, older women, young people, and servants in chapter 2. On Crete, the teachers troubling the churches talked about God but did not live for Him. Paul's ministry aimed at the opposite: truth that is believed, embraced, and lived out.

NLT

Titus 1:2 This truth gives them confidence that they have eternal life, which God — who does not lie — promised them before the world began. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul's ministry rests on the confident expectation of eternal life, which God promised before the world was created. Because God cannot lie, this promise is completely trustworthy, and believers can stake their lives on it.

Verse Explanation

Paul's ministry rests on the confident expectation of eternal life, which God promised before the world was created. Because God cannot lie, this promise is completely trustworthy, and believers can stake their lives on it.

"in hope of eternal life"

In the previous verse Paul described his calling as an apostle, serving the faith of God's people and their knowledge of the truth that leads to godly living. Now he names what that faith and godliness rest on and look toward: the hope of eternal life. In the Bible, hope is not wishful thinking. It is a confident expectation of something God has promised but that has not yet fully arrived.

Eternal life means more than living forever. Jesus defined it in His prayer to the Father: "And this is eternal life, that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent" (John 17:3). Believers begin to share in that life now, yet its fullness lies ahead. Paul returns to this hope later in the letter. Believers are "looking for the blessed hope and glorious appearing of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (2:13), and "having been justified by His grace we should become heirs according to the hope of eternal life" (3:7). Everything Paul asks of Titus and the Cretan churches, from appointing godly elders to living self-controlled lives, takes place in the light of this hope.

"which God, who cannot lie,"

The hope is certain because of who made the promise. Paul uses a single Greek word here, meaning "free from lies" or "unlying." It is not simply that God chooses not to lie. Lying is completely contrary to His nature. Moses declared, "God is not a man, that He should lie" (Numbers 23:19). Hebrews ties this truth directly to the believer's hope: "it is impossible for God to lie," so that those who have fled to Him "might have strong consolation" as they "lay hold of the hope set before us" (Hebrews 6:18). A promise is only as good as the one who makes it, and this promise comes from the God who has never spoken a false word.

That description stands out in a letter written to an island where deceivers were already troubling the churches and whose people had a reputation for lying (1:10, 12).

"promised before time began,"

The phrase literally reads "before the times of the ages." Paul uses the same expression in 2 Timothy 1:9, where God's "purpose and grace" were "given to us in Christ Jesus before time began." Before God created the world, He had already determined to give eternal life to His people. Since no human being existed yet to receive the promise, many interpreters understand it as God's settled purpose, a pledge made within God Himself in eternity, much like Paul's statement that God "chose us in Him before the foundation of the world" (Ephesians 1:4). Some take the phrase to mean "long ages ago," pointing to the promises God gave throughout the Old Testament. Either way, the hope of eternal life was not a later idea or a backup plan. God planned it from the very beginning.

The promise made before time began was not kept hidden forever. In the next verse Paul explains that God has now revealed it through the preaching entrusted to him (1:3).

Titus 1:3 but has in due time manifested His word through preaching, which was committed to me according to the commandment of God our Savior;(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 1:3 And now at just the right time he has revealed this message, which we announce to everyone. It is by the command of God our Savior that I have been entrusted with this work for him. (NLT)

Main Idea

At exactly the right time, God made His promise known publicly through the preaching of the gospel, and Paul describes this preaching as a responsibility entrusted to him by God's direct command. The message he carries is not his own idea but God's word revealed according to God's plan.

Verse Explanation

At exactly the right time, God made His promise known publicly through the preaching of the gospel, and Paul describes this preaching as a responsibility entrusted to him by God's direct command. The message he carries is not his own idea but God's word revealed according to God's plan.

"but has in due time manifested His word through preaching,"

In the previous verse Paul spoke of "eternal life which God, who cannot lie, promised before time began" (1:2). "But" turns from that ancient promise to the present day. God made the promise before history began, and now He has brought it out into the open. "In due time" means "at His own proper time." God chose the moment, just as Paul says elsewhere: "when the fullness of the time had come, God sent forth His Son" (Galatians 4:4). The gospel did not arrive late or by accident. It came exactly when God intended.

The word "manifested" means to make visible or bring into plain view. What had been a promise is now openly announced. Paul says the same thing to Timothy about the grace "given to us in Christ Jesus before time began, but has now been revealed by the appearing of our Savior Jesus Christ, who has abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Timothy 1:9–10).

God made His word known "through preaching." The Greek word describes the message a herald proclaims publicly on behalf of a king. It refers both to the act of preaching and to the message itself. God chose to make His promise of eternal life known by having it announced, so that people would hear and believe. As Paul writes, "faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God" (Romans 10:17). The message about Christ, preached aloud, is how God now brings His promise to people like the believers in Crete.

"which was committed to me"

"Committed" means entrusted, the way something valuable is placed in a person's care. Paul did not invent this message or own it. God handed it to him, and he was responsible to deliver it faithfully. He describes "the glorious gospel of the blessed God which was committed to my trust" (1 Timothy 1:11), and he felt the weight of that responsibility: "woe is me if I do not preach the gospel!" (1 Corinthians 9:16). This matters for Titus's work in Crete. The teaching Titus must protect, and the teaching the elders must "hold fast" (1:9), is God's word passed down through His apostle, not one opinion among many. That sets it apart from what the false teachers troubling the churches were spreading.

"according to the commandment of God our Savior;"

Paul preached because God commanded him to, not because he volunteered or was appointed by people. He made the same point to the Galatians, calling himself "an apostle (not from men nor through man, but through Jesus Christ and God the Father who raised Him from the dead)" (Galatians 1:1). His authority came from the same God who gave the promise.

That God is "our Savior." A savior is one who rescues people from danger they cannot escape on their own. In the Old Testament God claimed this title for Himself alone: "I, even I, am the LORD, and besides Me there is no savior" (Isaiah 43:11). In this short letter Paul calls God the Father "our Savior" (1:3; 2:10; 3:4), and he gives the same title to Jesus Christ (1:4; 2:13; 3:6). He even speaks of "our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (2:13). The Father and the Son share the work of saving, and later Paul explains how that rescue happens: "not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy He saved us" (3:5).

The word "our" makes the title personal. Paul and Titus, along with the believers in Crete, had all been rescued by this God. The God who commanded the preaching is the same God who saves through it. That is why the message cannot be changed or treated lightly. It is His means of bringing people to eternal life, and He "desires all men to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Timothy 2:4).

NKJV

Titus 1:4 To Titus, a true son in our common faith: Grace, mercy, and peace from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ our Savior.(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 1:4 I am writing to Titus, my true son in the faith that we share. May God the Father and Christ Jesus our Savior give you grace and peace. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul addresses Titus as a true son in the faith they share, showing the close spiritual bond between the older apostle and his younger coworker. He greets him with grace, mercy, and peace from God the Father and from Jesus Christ, whom he again calls Savior, highlighting that salvation comes from both the Father and the Son.

Verse Explanation

Paul addresses Titus as a true son in the faith they share, showing the close spiritual bond between the older apostle and his younger coworker. He greets him with grace, mercy, and peace from God the Father and from Jesus Christ, whom he again calls Savior, highlighting that salvation comes from both the Father and the Son.

"To Titus,"

Titus was one of Paul's closest and most dependable coworkers. He was a Greek, a Gentile believer, and when Paul went up to Jerusalem to meet with the church leaders, he brought Titus along. There Titus "was not compelled to be circumcised" (Galatians 2:3). That made him living proof that Gentiles are saved through faith in Christ without first becoming Jews. Acts never mentions him, but Paul's letters show how much he relied on him. Paul sent Titus to Corinth during a painful conflict with that church. When Paul could not find him in Troas, he "had no rest" in his spirit (2 Corinthians 2:13). When Titus finally arrived with good news, Paul was deeply comforted (2 Corinthians 7:6–7). Paul called him "my partner and fellow worker" (2 Corinthians 8:23). Now Paul had entrusted him with the difficult work of bringing order to the young churches on Crete. Near the end of this letter Paul asks him to come to Nicopolis once a replacement arrives (3:12). Later Titus went on to Dalmatia (2 Timothy 4:10).

"a true son in our common faith:"

The word translated "son" is the ordinary Greek word for a child, and "true" means genuine or legitimate. It was used for a child born within a marriage, a rightful son. Paul uses the same expression for Timothy, "a true son in the faith" (1 Timothy 1:2). Paul was not Titus's physical father, so he means a spiritual relationship. Many interpreters think Paul may have led Titus to Christ, though Scripture does not say so directly. At the very least, Paul had trained him, worked beside him, and trusted him the way a father trusts a grown son who shares his values. By calling Titus his true son, Paul also confirms Titus's standing before the Cretan churches. Titus was not acting on his own authority. He came as the trusted representative of the apostle.

The word "common" means shared, held together by both. Paul was a Jew trained as a Pharisee, and Titus was an uncircumcised Gentile, yet they held one and the same faith in Christ. That shared faith joined them as father and son in the gospel. It also matters for the letter that follows. Titus would have to answer false teachers on Crete, especially "those of the circumcision" (1:10), and he himself was one of the clearest examples that Jew and Gentile are saved the same way.

"Grace, mercy, and peace"

Paul usually opens his letters with "grace" and "peace." Here, as in his letters to Timothy (1 Timothy 1:2; 2 Timothy 1:2), he adds "mercy." Grace is God's undeserved kindness, His favor given freely to people who have not earned it. Mercy is His compassion toward those who are weak, needy, or suffering, His willingness to help rather than treat us as our sins deserve. Peace reflects the Hebrew idea of shalom, a wholeness and well-being that comes from being made right with God. Paul is not offering a polite formality. He is asking God to give Titus these blessings, which every believer needs and which Titus would certainly need for the hard work ahead of him on Crete.

"from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ our Savior."

These blessings come from both the Father and the Son, joined together as their one source. Paul would never name a mere man alongside God as the giver of grace and peace, so this wording places Jesus Christ on equal footing with the Father.

In the previous verse Paul spoke of "God our Savior." Now he calls Jesus Christ "our Savior." The letter keeps moving back and forth this way. Paul speaks of "God our Savior" again in 2:10 and 3:4, and of "Jesus Christ our Savior" in 3:6. He even joins the two ideas in "our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (2:13). The rescue of sinners is the work of the Father, who planned and promised it, and of the Son, who accomplished it. "Lord" names Jesus as the Master whom believers serve. "Savior" names Him as the one who rescued them.

Main Idea

Paul had left Titus on the island of Crete to finish organizing the young churches and to appoint elders in every city, following the New Testament pattern of each city's believers being led by a group of qualified spiritual leaders. Healthy leadership was the first step toward correcting what was still unhealthy in these churches.

Verse Explanation

Paul had left Titus on the island of Crete to finish organizing the young churches and to appoint elders in every city, following the New Testament pattern of each city's believers being led by a group of qualified spiritual leaders. Healthy leadership was the first step toward correcting what was still unhealthy in these churches.

"For this reason I left you in Crete,"

Crete is a long, mountainous island in the Mediterranean, southeast of Greece. Cretans were among the crowd in Jerusalem on the day of Pentecost who heard the apostles speaking "the wonderful works of God" in their own languages (Acts 2:11), so the gospel may have reached the island early. The book of Acts does not record Paul ministering there. His ship only sailed along Crete's coast on the way to Rome as a prisoner (Acts 27:7–13). Many believe this work took place after his release from that first imprisonment. Paul and Titus, his "true son in our common faith" (1:4), had labored there together. When Paul moved on, he did not take Titus with him but left him behind on purpose. The assignment was not permanent. Later in the letter Paul tells Titus to join him at Nicopolis once a replacement arrives (3:12), so Titus was there to finish a specific task.

"that you should set in order the things that are lacking,"

The words "left" and "lacking" come from the same Greek root. Paul left Titus behind because some things had been left undone. The verb "set in order" appears only here in the New Testament. It means to straighten something out further, carrying on a correction that was already underway. The churches in Crete had believers, but they did not yet have everything they needed to be healthy. Some of what was missing was organization. Much of it was spiritual. False teachers were already at work among them (1:10–11), and the Cretan way of life was the opposite of godliness (1:12). Titus was to bring order to these churches so that they could stand firm.

"and appoint elders in every city"

The first thing missing was leadership. The Greek word for "elder" originally meant an older man, and it carried the idea of maturity and experience. Israel had long been guided by elders (Numbers 11:16), and Jewish synagogues had them as well. In the church, elders are the men responsible for shepherding God's people and teaching them His word. Paul uses the same office under the name "bishop," or overseer, a few verses later (1:7).

The word "appoint" means to put someone in charge, to place him officially in a position of responsibility. Titus was not simply to wait for leaders to emerge. He was to install qualified men so the churches would have recognized, accountable oversight.

Paul speaks of elders, plural, "in every city." The believers in each town made up one church, even if they met in different homes (compare Romans 16:5), and that church was to be led by a group of qualified elders sharing the work together. This was Paul's consistent practice. On his first missionary journey he and Barnabas "appointed elders in every church" (Acts 14:23), and later he called for "the elders of the church" at Ephesus (Acts 20:17). Leadership by a plurality of qualified elders kept any single man from ruling the church alone, and it gave each congregation shepherds to guide it. This was the starting point for setting right what was lacking in Crete.

"as I commanded you—"

Titus was carrying out instructions Paul had already given him in person. The word "commanded" means to give clear orders or arrangements. By putting those directions in writing, Paul likely also gave Titus backing before the churches, so that no one could treat the appointments as Titus's own idea. The dash leads straight into the qualifications that follow (1:6–9). Paul was concerned not only that elders be appointed but that the right men be chosen. Those qualifications deal mainly with character and faithfulness to God's word.

Study Notes

Acts never describes Paul planting churches across Crete. Its only mention of the island comes during his voyage to Rome as a prisoner, when the ship anchored briefly at Fair Havens near Lasea (Acts 27:8) and then left to escape the winter storms. That stop gave no opportunity to establish churches "in every city." Acts closes with Paul under house arrest in Rome for two years (Acts 28:30–31) and does not say how his case ended.

The traditional explanation is that Paul was released, travelled again, and was later arrested a second time. Paul expected release when he wrote Philippians (Philippians 1:25–26), and he asked Philemon to prepare a guest room for him (Philemon 22). Clement of Rome, writing about AD 95, says Paul reached "the limits of the west." The church historian Eusebius later reported a release followed by a second imprisonment. The travel details in Titus also fit this picture. A winter at Nicopolis (3:12) has no place in Acts. 2 Timothy then finds Paul imprisoned again and expecting death. On this view, Titus dates to roughly AD 62–66.

Many modern scholars argue instead that a later follower of Paul wrote the letters to Timothy and Titus. They point to these travel difficulties and to differences in vocabulary. But the letter names Paul as its writer and includes ordinary personal arrangements (3:12–13). A release from the first imprisonment explains the evidence without strain.

Verse Explanation

An elder must be a man of established integrity who is above legitimate accusation, faithful and devoted to his wife as a "one-woman man," and leading a household where his children are not known for wild or rebellious living. The focus is on proven character and marital faithfulness rather than a technical rule about marital status, and on a man's ability to guide his family well rather than a guarantee that every child has personally believed.

"if a man is blameless,"

The "if" introduces the test Titus was to apply when appointing elders in every city (1:5). Not every willing man qualified. The Greek word translated "blameless" means "not able to be accused," someone against whom no legitimate charge can be brought. It does not mean sinless perfection, since no one but Christ could meet that standard. It means a man whose life is marked by proven integrity, so that when people look at how he lives, there is nothing they can rightly hold against him.

Paul repeats the word in the next verse: "For a bishop must be blameless, as a steward of God" (1:7). Being blameless is the overall requirement, and the qualities that follow show what it looks like. Paul begins with the man's home, where his character is seen most clearly and over the longest time. He gives Timothy the same starting point: "A bishop then must be blameless, the husband of one wife" (1 Timothy 3:2).

"the husband of one wife,"

In Greek the phrase reads literally "a one-woman man." It describes what kind of husband he is. He is sexually pure and completely devoted to his wife, in his conduct and in his heart. This mattered in the world Paul wrote to. In Greek and Roman society, married men were rarely condemned for unfaithfulness. A man who leads God's people must be faithful in a way that stands out from that culture.

Some have read the phrase as a requirement that an elder be married, or as a rule against a widower remarrying, against polygamy, or against any man who has been divorced. But Paul's wording points to a man's faithfulness rather than a technical rule about his marital status. He uses the same kind of phrase for widows, who must have been "the wife of one man" (1 Timothy 5:9), describing a woman known for her faithfulness in marriage. A man's marital history still matters and must be weighed by Scripture, but the question Paul is asking is whether this man is known for faithfulness to his wife.

"having faithful children"

The Greek word translated "faithful" can mean either "believing" or "faithful." Here it describes children who are trustworthy and well-ordered in their behavior, which is exactly how the rest of the verse defines them. Paul is not making an elder responsible for his children's salvation, which only God can give. He is asking whether the man has shown he can lead his family well. Paul says the same to Timothy, describing a man "who rules his own house well, having his children in submission with all reverence (for if a man does not know how to rule his own house, how will he take care of the church of God?)" (1 Timothy 3:4–5). A man who cannot guide his own family in an orderly way is not ready to guide God's household.

"not accused of dissipation or insubordination."

"Dissipation" means wild, reckless living, the kind that wastes money, health, and reputation on indulgence. Paul uses the same word for drunkenness: "do not be drunk with wine, in which is dissipation" (Ephesians 5:18). A closely related word describes the prodigal son, who "wasted his possessions with prodigal living" (Luke 15:13). "Insubordination" means being unruly and refusing to accept authority. An elder's children should not have a reputation for either kind of behavior.

The word "accused" connects back to "blameless." Just as no legitimate charge should stand against the elder, no such charge should stand against the household he leads. Paul will use the same word, "insubordinate," a few verses later for the false teachers troubling the churches in Crete (1:10). The elder's orderly home was meant to set him clearly apart from the men he would have to correct.

Study Notes

In the Greek phrase mias gunaikos anēr, "one" stands first for emphasis: literally "of one wife husband." The phrase describes the kind of husband a man is. Each alternative reading faces difficulties.

A requirement that elders be married sits poorly with Paul's own life. He was unmarried and commended singleness for undistracted service to the Lord (1 Corinthians 7:7–8, 32–35). A ban on widowers who remarry conflicts with Paul's plain teaching that death ends a marriage and leaves the surviving spouse free to marry again (Romans 7:2–3; 1 Corinthians 7:39). Polygamy did exist among some Jews of the period. However, the matching phrase for widows, "the wife of one man" (1 Timothy 5:9), cannot be aimed at a woman with several husbands at once, because that was not practiced. The parallel suggests both phrases concern faithfulness, not the number of spouses.

The divorce reading deserves the most serious attention, because Jesus treated divorce and remarriage as grave matters (Matthew 19:9). Even so, Paul does not say "not divorced," though he easily could have. The phrase asks whether the man is known for exclusive devotion to his wife. His past, including any divorce, must still be weighed honestly by Scripture.

On "faithful children," the same Greek word pistos appears again in 1:9, where the elder holds fast "the faithful word," meaning the trustworthy word, and again in 3:8, "This is a faithful saying." In these letters it often means trustworthy rather than believing. That supports reading it here as a description of the children's conduct.

Verse Explanation

Because an elder, also called an overseer, manages God's household on God's behalf, he must be free from the kinds of faults that would disgrace that trust. Being stubborn, hot-tempered, controlled by alcohol, violent, or greedy for money are all incompatible with shepherding God's people.

"For a bishop must be blameless,"

"For" explains why Paul has just insisted on such careful standards for the elders Titus is to appoint (1:5–6). The word "bishop" translates the Greek episkopos, meaning "overseer," someone who watches over and takes responsibility for others. Paul is not introducing a new, higher office. He is describing the same men he has just called "elders" (1:5). "Elder" points to the maturity and respect these men must have, and "overseer" points to the work they do. Paul uses both terms for the same leaders elsewhere. He called for "the elders of the church" at Ephesus and then told them that the Holy Spirit had made them "overseers, to shepherd the church of God" (Acts 20:17, 28). Centuries later, "bishop" came to mean one church official ruling over many congregations, but that is not what Paul means here. Each city's church was to be cared for by a group of qualified men sharing this oversight.

Paul repeats "blameless" from the previous verse. An elder must be a man no one can rightly accuse of wrongdoing. This is not sinless perfection, but a settled pattern of integrity. Here Paul ties the requirement to the reason that follows: the kind of trust this man is given.

"as a steward of God,"

A steward was a household manager, often a trusted servant put in charge of his master's property, supplies, and other servants. Joseph served this way in Potiphar's house, where everything was placed in his hands (Genesis 39:4–6). The steward had real authority, but nothing he managed belonged to him. Paul calls the church "the house of God" (1 Timothy 3:15), so an elder manages God's household on God's behalf. The people he oversees are not his. Paul has just required that an elder lead his own household well (1:6), and now he shows why: the church is a household too, and its Owner is God. "It is required in stewards that one be found faithful" (1 Corinthians 4:2).

The five faults that follow are exactly the ways a steward betrays that trust. He might run the house for his own way, his own anger, his own appetites, or his own profit.

"not self-willed,"

The Greek word describes someone who is self-pleasing, stubborn, and determined to have his own way regardless of others. Peter uses the same word for the arrogant false teachers who despise authority (2 Peter 2:10). A self-willed man treats the church as his to run. But a steward's whole job is to carry out his master's wishes, not his own. Since elders lead together, a man who cannot listen, yield, or work alongside others is unfit to share oversight. Peter warns elders against "being lords over those entrusted to you" (1 Peter 5:3).

"not quick-tempered,"

This is a man easily provoked, whose anger flares quickly and controls his responses. Elders deal with difficult people, including opponents who must be corrected (1:9), and a short temper will turn correction into a quarrel. "A servant of the Lord must not quarrel but be gentle to all, able to teach, patient" (2 Timothy 2:24). James adds that "the wrath of man does not produce the righteousness of God" (James 1:20).

"not given to wine, not violent,"

"Given to wine" describes a man who lingers over wine and comes under its control. Paul is not demanding total abstinence, since he told Timothy to "use a little wine" for his stomach (1 Timothy 5:23). He is ruling out a man whose drinking masters him. "Violent" translates a word for a striker, someone who lashes out and hits. The two faults naturally go together, because drunkenness often leads to brawling. A man who strikes others cannot shepherd them. Jesus, the Chief Shepherd, "when He was reviled, did not revile in return" (1 Peter 2:23).

"not greedy for money,"

The Greek word refers to someone eager for shameful gain, willing to profit in ways that dishonor God. Elders could receive financial support for their work (1 Timothy 5:17–18), and that made the temptation real: a man could use the ministry to enrich himself. Peter tells elders to shepherd the flock "not for dishonest gain but eagerly" (1 Peter 5:2). This was no distant danger on Crete. The false teachers there were teaching "for the sake of dishonest gain" (1:11), displaying the very fault that disqualifies a man from leading God's household.

Study Notes

The New Testament's use of "bishop" and "elder" for the same leaders is confirmed by early Christian writing outside the Bible. Paul greets the church at Philippi "with the bishops and deacons" (Philippians 1:1). That means one city's church had several overseers and no single bishop over them. Clement of Rome, writing to Corinth near the end of the first century, uses "overseers" and "elders" for the same group of leaders.

The later pattern began fairly early. By about AD 110, the letters of Ignatius of Antioch show some cities with one bishop placed above a group of elders. Jerome, writing around AD 400 in his commentary on this very passage, still recognized the earlier arrangement. He argued that elders and bishops were originally the same and that churches were governed by a shared council of elders. In his view, one man was later set over the rest to prevent division, and that change came from church custom rather than from an arrangement the Lord Himself established. Titus 1:5–7 was one of the main passages Jerome used to make that point.

The word "but" turns from the five faults Paul has just ruled out, being self-willed, quick-tempered, given to wine, violent, or greedy for money (1:7), to the qualities an elder must have. The first is hospitality. The Greek word literally means "a lover of strangers." In the ancient world, inns were often expensive, dirty, or morally dangerous, so traveling believers, including missionaries and teachers, depended on Christians who would open their homes to them. Paul gives Titus that same kind of responsibility later in this letter, asking him to send Zenas and Apollos on their journey so "that they may lack nothing" (3:13). Churches also commonly met in homes (Romans 16:5).

Hospitality is required of every believer (Romans 12:13; 1 Peter 4:9), but an elder must be known for it. It sits naturally beside the warning against greed in the previous verse. A man who loves money guards what he has, while a hospitable man shares his table, his time, and his home. A shepherd who keeps people at a distance cannot really care for them.

"a lover of what is good,"

This is a single Greek word, used only here in the New Testament. It describes a man who loves goodness in every form: good people, good deeds, and whatever is right and worthwhile. Paul is describing more than outward behavior. An elder's desires are drawn toward what is good. This fits a theme that runs through the whole letter. The truth of the gospel produces godly living, and Christ "gave Himself for us, that He might redeem us from every lawless deed and purify for Himself His own special people, zealous for good works" (2:14). An elder should show plainly what that kind of life looks like.

"sober-minded,"

The Greek word describes a sound, sensible mind. It refers to clear thinking and balanced judgment, not drinking, which Paul already addressed in verse 7. A sober-minded man is not ruled by impulse, extremes, or emotion. He thinks carefully before he speaks or acts. Paul uses words from this same family repeatedly in the next chapter, urging older men, young women, and young men toward this kind of self-governed good sense (2:2–6). He also says God's grace teaches all believers to "live soberly, righteously, and godly in the present age" (2:12). The elder must first live out what grace is meant to produce in the whole church.

"just, holy,"

"Just" means righteous or upright. Here it especially points to how a man treats other people, dealing honestly and fairly and doing what is right by God's standard. Elders must make decisions, settle conflicts, and correct error, so a man who plays favorites or bends the truth for his own advantage cannot be trusted with that responsibility.

"Holy" here is not the usual New Testament word for holiness. It describes a man who is devout and reverent toward God and devoted to pleasing Him. Placed side by side, the two words cover a man's conduct toward people and his devotion toward God. Paul reminded the Thessalonians "how devoutly and justly and blamelessly" he and his companions had lived among them (1 Thessalonians 2:10). The same word for "holy" describes Christ Himself as our High Priest, "holy, harmless, undefiled" (Hebrews 7:26). The men who lead Christ's people are to reflect His character.

"self-controlled,"

The Greek word describes a man who has mastery over himself and holds his desires, appetites, and impulses firmly in check. Every fault listed in verse 7, such as anger, drunkenness, violence, and greed, is a failure of self-control, so this quality closes the list by addressing what lies underneath them all. Such self-control is not simply willpower. Paul names it as part of "the fruit of the Spirit" (Galatians 5:22–23), the result of God's work in a believer's life. A man who cannot govern himself is not ready to help govern the church of God.

An elder must be that kind of loyal man toward God's word. He does not hold it loosely or let it go when it becomes unpopular.

The word he holds is "faithful," meaning trustworthy and reliable. Paul uses this same idea elsewhere when he introduces a key truth with "This is a faithful saying" (Titus 3:8; 1 Timothy 1:15). The message is reliable because it comes from the God "who cannot lie" (1:2) and was entrusted to Paul by God's own command (1:3). An elder can stake his life and his ministry on it.

"as he has been taught,"

Paul gave Timothy the same instruction: "And the things that you have heard from me among many witnesses, commit these to faithful men who will be able to teach others also" (2 Timothy 2:2). The truth was handed from Christ to the apostles, from the apostles to men like Titus, and from them to the elders in each church. An elder must first be a faithful learner before he can be a faithful teacher.

"that he may be able, by sound doctrine,"

Holding fast to the word has a purpose. It is what makes an elder "able" to serve the church. Most of the qualifications before this describe character, but this one also requires the ability to teach (1 Timothy 3:2). That ability grows out of a firm grip on the truth.

The Greek word translated "sound" means healthy. Our word "hygiene" comes from it. Sound doctrine is healthy teaching, the kind that makes believers spiritually strong and whole. Paul uses the word repeatedly in his letters to Titus and Timothy because false teaching works like a disease. He warned that one false teacher's message "will spread like cancer" (2 Timothy 2:17). The false teachers troubling the Cretan churches were spreading that kind of sickness. Healthy teaching also produces healthy lives. In the next chapter Paul tells Titus to "speak the things which are proper for sound doctrine" (2:1) and then describes how older men, older women, young people, and servants should live. Right teaching and godly living belong together.

"both to exhort and convict those who contradict."

An elder uses sound doctrine in two ways. To "exhort" means to urge, encourage, and appeal. It describes the elder's ongoing work of building up the believers in his care, strengthening them and calling them to live out what they believe. To "convict" means to expose error and show someone clearly where he is wrong. It is the same word Jesus used for the Holy Spirit's work of convicting the world of sin (John 16:8).

Those who "contradict" are people who speak against the truth and oppose it. Paul knew such people would come. He warned the elders at Ephesus that "savage wolves will come in among you, not sparing the flock" (Acts 20:29). A shepherd must feed the sheep and also guard them from wolves. An elder who can encourage but never correct leaves the church exposed. One who can only argue leaves it hungry. Both tasks depend on the same thing: the faithful word he has been taught and holds firmly.

The man who leads the church must have an orderly household, while those troubling the church show the very unruliness that would disqualify him. They would not submit to the apostle's teaching or to the leaders God had placed over the churches, and that is why Titus needed elders whose own homes showed they could lead well.

"both idle talkers and deceivers,"

"Idle talkers" means people whose words are empty and useless. They talked a great deal, but their talk produced nothing good. Paul described the same problem in Ephesus, where some had "turned aside to idle talk" (1 Timothy 1:6). Later in this letter he tells Titus to avoid "foolish disputes, genealogies, contentions, and strivings about the law; for they are unprofitable and useless" (3:9). That is the kind of talk he has in mind: endless arguments that sound important but do not lead anyone closer to God.

"Deceivers" goes further. The Greek word refers to someone who misleads the mind, drawing people away from the truth. These men were not only wasting the church's time with empty words. They were leading others astray. This is why the elders' grip on "the faithful word" mattered so much. Empty talk and deception had to be answered with teaching that was true and reliable.

"especially those of the circumcision,"

"Those of the circumcision" refers to people of Jewish background. Paul uses this phrase for Jewish believers (Colossians 4:11; Galatians 2:12). Here it points to a group within the churches who taught that Gentile believers had to be circumcised and follow the Jewish law and customs. This was not a new problem. Earlier, men from Judea had taught Gentile believers, "Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved" (Acts 15:1). The church leaders in Jerusalem rejected that teaching.

Crete had a Jewish population. Cretans were among the crowd in Jerusalem at Pentecost who heard the apostles speak "the wonderful works of God" (Acts 2:11). So it is not surprising that the Cretan churches included Jewish members, and that some of them pressed this teaching on others. "Especially" shows they were not the only troublemakers, but they were the leading group.

Titus understood this issue well. He was a Greek, and when Paul brought him to Jerusalem, Titus "was not compelled to be circumcised" (Galatians 2:3). He himself was living proof that Gentiles are fully accepted by God without becoming Jews first. Adding circumcision or law-keeping as a requirement takes away from the gospel Paul preached, that God "saved us, not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy" (3:5). Paul describes the content of their teaching more fully later in the chapter (1:14). Here he names who they were and why the churches needed faithful elders to stand against them.

Study Notes

Writings outside the Bible confirm a long-standing Jewish community on Crete. In 1 Maccabees 15:23, Gortyna, one of the island's chief cities, appears among the places that received a Roman letter on behalf of the Jews, around 139 BC. The Jewish writer Philo, a contemporary of Paul, lists Crete among the regions with Jewish settlements. Josephus tells of a man who impersonated a son of Herod the Great and won support from Jews on Crete before going on to Rome. A Jewish community this established gives a clear setting for a group within the churches who pressed circumcision, Jewish purity rules, and speculative stories about the law on Gentile believers.

"One of them, a prophet of their own, said,"

"One of them" means one of the Cretans, the same people among whom the false teachers were troubling the churches. Early Christian writers identified this "prophet" as Epimenides, a Cretan poet who lived about six centuries before Paul. The Greeks honored him as a religious seer. Paul calls him a prophet because that is how the Cretans themselves regarded him, not because he spoke for God. The point is that this judgment did not come from an outsider with a grudge. It came from one of their own celebrated figures.

Paul was willing to quote pagan writers when they said something true. In Athens he quoted a Greek poet to make a point about God: "For we are also His offspring" (Acts 17:28). Here he uses a well-known line to describe the kind of behavior Titus was facing.

"'Cretans are always liars,"

The Cretans' reputation for dishonesty was famous in the ancient world. The Greeks even made a verb out of the island's name, "to Cretanize," meaning to lie or cheat. Another Greek poet, Callimachus, used this same line and tied it to the Cretans' claim that the tomb of Zeus was on their island, a claim the Greeks considered a lie because they believed their chief god could not die.

This reputation stands in sharp contrast to the opening of the letter. Paul's message rests on the hope of eternal life "which God, who cannot lie, promised before time began" (1:2). The Cretans were known for lying, and the false teachers among them were "deceivers" (1:10), but the God whose truth Titus was sent to teach cannot lie. In a culture where people's words could not be trusted, the churches needed to hold firmly to the One whose promises are completely reliable.

"evil beasts, lazy gluttons.'"

"Evil beasts" pictures wild, dangerous animals, people who act on savage instinct rather than self-control. "Lazy gluttons" is literally "idle bellies," people who avoid work and live to satisfy their appetites. Paul describes false teachers elsewhere in similar terms, saying they "do not serve our Lord Jesus Christ, but their own belly" (Romans 16:18) and that their "god is their belly" (Philippians 3:19). The false teachers in Crete fit the picture, since they taught "for the sake of dishonest gain" (1:11). By contrast, an elder must not be violent or greedy for money, and he must be self-controlled (1:7–8).

Paul is not saying that every Cretan was beyond hope. Titus was appointing elders from among Cretan believers, and Paul goes on to affirm that "this testimony is true" (1:13) so that those caught up in these patterns might be corrected. Paul admits that he and other believers were once "foolish, disobedient, deceived, serving various lusts and pleasures" (3:3). The gospel that changed them could change Cretans too.

The word "fables" translates the Greek mythos, a made-up story or myth. These were "Jewish" fables because they came from the false teachers Paul has just described, "especially those of the circumcision" (1:10). Scripture does not spell out what these stories contained. Since Paul later tells Titus to avoid "foolish disputes, genealogies, contentions, and strivings about the law" (3:9), many interpreters think they were imaginative tales built around Old Testament names, family lines, and laws. Whatever their exact content, they were human inventions dressed up as spiritual insight. The apostles took care to separate the gospel from such stories. Peter wrote, "we did not follow cunningly devised fables" when they made known the power and coming of Jesus Christ (2 Peter 1:16).

"and commandments of men"

Along with their stories, the false teachers imposed rules of their own making. Jesus used this same expression when He rebuked the Pharisees for setting aside God's commands to keep their traditions: "And in vain they worship Me, teaching as doctrines the commandments of men" (Mark 7:7). Paul warned the Colossians about similar rules, "Do not touch, do not taste, do not handle," which he called "the commandments and doctrines of men" (Colossians 2:21–22). In Crete these rules apparently dealt with what was clean and unclean, since Paul answers them right away by saying, "To the pure all things are pure" (1:15). Such rules can look like serious devotion to God, but they shift attention from what God has done to what people must do to stay pure.

The source of these commandments is the real problem. Paul preached his message "according to the commandment of God our Savior" (1:3). The false teachers offered commandments that came only from men. One message came from God and carried His authority; the other carried no more authority than the people who made it up.

"who turn from the truth."

The men behind these fables and rules are people who turn away from the truth. Paul's whole ministry aimed at bringing God's people to "the acknowledgment of the truth which accords with godliness" (1:1). These teachers moved in the opposite direction, away from the truth and toward stories and regulations of their own making. Paul warned Timothy that a time would come when people "will turn their ears away from the truth, and be turned aside to fables" (2 Timothy 4:4).

This is why the believers in Crete could not treat such teaching as harmless. Paying attention to people who have turned from the truth will draw the listeners away with them. The sharp rebuke Titus was to give aimed to bring the believers back to healthy faith before these teachers led them any further from the truth.

Titus 1:15 To the pure all things are pure, but to those who are defiled and unbelieving nothing is pure; but even their mind and conscience are defiled.(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 1:15 Everything is pure to those whose hearts are pure. But nothing is pure to those who are corrupt and unbelieving, because their minds and consciences are corrupted. (NLT)

Main Idea

For people whose hearts have been cleansed by God, ordinary things like food are pure, but for those who are corrupt and unbelieving, nothing is pure because their very thinking and sense of right and wrong have been corrupted. Purity is a matter of the heart, not of outward rules.

Verse Explanation

For people whose hearts have been cleansed by God, ordinary things like food are pure, but for those who are corrupt and unbelieving, nothing is pure because their very thinking and sense of right and wrong have been corrupted. Purity is a matter of the heart, not of outward rules.

"To the pure all things are pure,"

Paul is answering the false teachers troubling the Cretan churches, whose "commandments of men" (1:14) apparently included rules about what was clean and unclean. Since many of them were "of the circumcision" (1:10), these rules likely drew on Jewish purity laws about food and ritual cleanness, with human additions on top. Paul's answer is that purity does not come from the things a person eats or touches. It begins in the person.

"The pure" are those whom God has made clean. Peter said God was "purifying their hearts by faith" (Acts 15:9), and later in this letter Paul says Christ gave Himself "that He might redeem us from every lawless deed and purify for Himself His own special people" (2:14). For people cleansed this way, ordinary things like food are not a source of defilement. Jesus taught the same thing: "There is nothing that enters a man from outside which can defile him; but the things which come out of him, those are the things that defile a man" (Mark 7:15). Mark adds that by saying this, Jesus was "purifying all foods" (Mark 7:19). Paul writes elsewhere, "every creature of God is good, and nothing is to be refused if it is received with thanksgiving" (1 Timothy 4:4).

"All things" does not mean that sin becomes acceptable for a believer. Paul is speaking about the good things God created, which the false teachers were treating as unclean. He is not speaking about moral evil, which this same letter firmly condemns.

"but to those who are defiled and unbelieving nothing is pure;"

The Greek word translated "defiled" means stained or polluted. These people were the ones most concerned about avoiding contamination, yet they were defiled themselves. Their problem was not what they ate but what they were. They were also "unbelieving." They did not truly trust and obey the truth of the gospel, which is how a person is made clean. Without that inner cleansing, nothing is pure to them. Careful rules cannot make them clean, and whatever they touch is handled by an unclean heart. A person can avoid every forbidden food and still remain defiled before God.

"but even their mind and conscience are defiled."

The "mind" is a person's ability to think and understand. The "conscience" is the inner sense that judges right and wrong. These are the very faculties a person needs in order to tell what is clean from what is unclean, and in these teachers both were polluted. That is why their judgments about purity could not be trusted. They called good things unclean while failing to see the corruption within themselves. Paul describes similar teachers elsewhere as "having their own conscience seared with a hot iron" (1 Timothy 4:2).

Rules cannot cleanse a corrupted conscience. Only Christ can do that. Hebrews asks, "how much more shall the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered Himself without spot to God, cleanse your conscience from dead works to serve the living God?" (Hebrews 9:14). The difference between the pure and the defiled is not who keeps the stricter rules, but whose heart God has made clean.

NLT

Titus 1:16 Such people claim they know God, but they deny him by the way they live. They are detestable and disobedient, worthless for doing anything good. (NLT)

Main Idea

These false teachers claim to know God, yet the way they live proves they do not truly know Him. Their lives are described as detestable and disobedient, making them useless for any good work, a sobering reminder that genuine faith always shows itself in how a person lives.

Verse Explanation

These false teachers claim to know God, yet the way they live proves they do not truly know Him. Their lives are described as detestable and disobedient, making them useless for any good work, a sobering reminder that genuine faith always shows itself in how a person lives.

"They profess to know God,"

These are the false teachers troubling the Cretan churches, especially "those of the circumcision" (1:10). The Greek word for "profess" means to declare openly or publicly confess. They said plainly that they knew God, and as teachers who prided themselves on the Law, they likely claimed to know Him better than anyone. But in Scripture, knowing God is never just having correct information about Him. It is a real relationship that changes how a person lives. John puts it bluntly: "He who says, 'I know Him,' and does not keep His commandments, is a liar, and the truth is not in him" (1 John 2:4).

"but in works they deny Him,"

Their words said one thing, and their lives said another. "Deny" means to disown or refuse to acknowledge. They never said they rejected God, but their conduct did. They were upsetting whole households and teaching for money (1:11), and their minds and consciences were defiled (1:15). Paul describes the same kind of people to Timothy: "having a form of godliness but denying its power" (2 Timothy 3:5).

This is the opposite of what Paul says about his own ministry at the start of the letter. He serves "the acknowledgment of the truth which accords with godliness" (1:1). The truth of God, when it is truly known, produces a godly life. These men claimed the truth but showed none of its fruit, and Jesus said that fruit is how false teachers are recognized: "Therefore by their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:20).

"being abominable,"

The Greek word means detestable or disgusting, something that causes revulsion. It appears only here in the New Testament, but it is related to the word "abomination," which the Bible uses for things God finds repulsive. Jesus used that related word when He told the Pharisees, "For what is highly esteemed among men is an abomination in the sight of God" (Luke 16:15). These teachers were fixated on human rules about what was clean and unclean (1:14–15), yet in God's sight they were the ones who were detestable.

"disobedient,"

This word describes someone who refuses to be persuaded and will not obey. They claimed to know God while refusing to submit to Him. It fits the description of them as "insubordinate" (1:10). Their rebellion was not only against Paul and Titus but against God Himself.

"and disqualified for every good work."

The Greek word for "disqualified" means failing the test, rejected after examination. It was used for metal or coins that were tested and found to be fake. Paul uses the same word for himself when he speaks of disciplining his body "lest, when I have preached to others, I myself should become disqualified" (1 Corinthians 9:27). These men presented themselves as teachers of others, but when their lives were tested, they proved worthless for any good work at all.

That stands in sharp contrast to the elders Titus was to appoint. An elder must be "a lover of what is good" (1:8), while these men were useless for anything good. Good works do not save anyone, since God saved us "not by works of righteousness which we have done" (3:5). But they do show that a person truly knows God. Christ "gave Himself for us, that He might redeem us from every lawless deed and purify for Himself His own special people, zealous for good works" (2:14). The false teachers claimed the name of God, but they lacked the very evidence that His grace had changed them.
