Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 1
Main Idea:

Paul charges Titus to set the churches of Crete in order by appointing qualified elders who hold fast the faithful word, because teachers who profess to know God but deny Him by their works are overturning whole households.

Setting:

Paul writes to Titus, “a true son in our common faith” (1:4), whom he left on the island of Crete to finish the work among young churches scattered across its cities (1:5).

Chapter Outline:

  • Titus 1:1–4 — Greeting to Titus

  • Titus 1:5–9 — Appointing Qualified Elders

  • Titus 1:10–16 — Rebuking False Teachers in Crete

Key Verse:

“holding fast the faithful word as he has been taught, that he may be able, by sound doctrine, both to exhort and convict those who contradict.” (Titus 1:9)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Titus 1 begins and ends with the same concern. Paul ties his apostleship to “the acknowledgment of the truth which accords with godliness” (1:1), and the chapter closes with teachers who “profess to know God, but in works they deny Him” (1:16). Everything rests on God Himself: the hope of eternal life comes from the God “who cannot lie” (1:2), and Paul gives the title “our Savior” first to God and then to the Lord Jesus Christ (1:3–4).

Paul’s first instruction concerns people. Titus is to “set in order the things that are lacking” by appointing “elders in every city” (1:5), a plurality of qualified shepherds for each city’s church, the same men Paul calls a “bishop” and “a steward of God” (1:7). Paul weighs them by their character and above all by their hold on the faithful word, which equips them to encourage the church and to correct those who contradict it (1:9).

Such men are urgently needed, because deceivers are subverting whole households for dishonest gain (1:10–11). Even Paul’s sharp rebuke has a healing purpose, “that they may be sound in the faith” (1:13). The chapter shows a God who guards His people by giving them faithful shepherds and who calls even the deceived back to health.

Chapter Sections

Titus 1:1–4 — Greeting to Titus

Paul introduces himself as God’s bondservant and Christ’s apostle, whose calling serves the faith of God’s people, their knowledge of truth that leads to godliness, and the hope of eternal life promised by the God who cannot lie. He greets Titus as a true son in their shared faith and calls both God and Jesus Christ “our Savior.”

Titus 1:5–9 — Appointing Qualified Elders

Titus’s first task in setting the Cretan churches in order is to appoint qualified elders in every city, men who are blameless in their homes, free from the traits that disqualify a steward of God, and marked by godly character. Above all, an elder must hold fast the faithful word so that he can both build up believers and correct those who oppose the truth.

Titus 1:10–16 — Rebuking False Teachers in Crete

Many deceivers, especially those of the circumcision, are overturning households with Jewish fables and human commands for the sake of dishonest gain. Titus must rebuke them sharply so that they may become sound in the faith, because their works deny the God they claim to know.

Why This Chapter Matters

Titus 1 shows that the health of a church begins with its leadership. Before Paul says a word to older men, young women, or bondservants, he tells Titus to appoint elders, and he measures them first by character, home life, and faithfulness to God’s word. A church can have gifted speakers and still lack what it most needs.

The chapter also teaches believers to judge teaching by its fruit. The deceivers in Crete spoke about God, yet their greed and the damage they did to families exposed them (1:11, 16). Because the truth Paul preaches “accords with godliness” (1:1), a message that leaves people greedy and divided has already shown what it is. Paul answers it with firm correction aimed at restoration (1:13).

Key Themes to Watch For

Truth and godliness. Knowing the truth and living a godly life belong together, from the greeting to the closing verdict (1:1, 16).

The God who cannot lie. The hope of eternal life is secure because it rests on God’s own promise and truthful character (1:2–3).

Qualified leadership. Elders are chosen for blameless character, faithful homes, and a firm hold on the faithful word (1:5–9).

Healthy faith. Sound doctrine and even sharp rebuke aim at believers who are spiritually healthy, “sound in the faith” (1:9, 13).

Profession and practice. The false teachers are exposed when their works contradict their words (1:11, 15–16).

Key Words & Concepts

Elders and bishop. Paul tells Titus to appoint “elders” (1:5) and then calls the same leader a “bishop,” or overseer (1:7). The two words name one office: “elder” points to maturity, and “bishop” to the work of oversight. Paul’s charge to appoint elders, plural, “in every city” points to a shared, qualified leadership for each city’s church.

Blameless. The word that governs the whole list of qualifications (1:6–7). It describes a man of established integrity who gives no just ground for accusation, not a man without sin.

The husband of one wife; faithful children (1:6). The first phrase is best understood as a faithful husband, a one-woman man marked by sexual integrity, rather than an automatic bar to a man who is unmarried, widowed and remarried, or divorced under circumstances Scripture permits; his history and present character are still weighed. Faithful children most likely describes children whose conduct is faithful and orderly. The NLT reads it differently: “his children must be believers.”

Those of the circumcision. Teachers from a Jewish background, or influenced by one, who promoted “Jewish fables and commandments of men” (1:14). Paul judges them by their fruit: greed, damaged households, and lives that deny God.

“A prophet of their own” (1:12). Paul quotes a Cretan saying, commonly attributed to the poet Epimenides, that called his own people liars, evil beasts, and lazy gluttons. Paul uses it to name the false teachers’ conduct, and his goal is their restoration, not contempt for the Cretan people.

“To the pure all things are pure” (1:15). Paul answers human purity rules by locating defilement in the mind and conscience rather than in the things God made. The verse does not permit whatever a person wants; it shows where real purity and real corruption begin.
