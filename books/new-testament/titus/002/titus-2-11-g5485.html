Summary:

GRACE - ( charis , Strong’s [5485])

The unmerited favor and divine influence of God. In Titus 2:11, it refers to the benevolent mercy of God manifested in the person and work of Jesus Christ, providing the means for salvation and the power for godly living.

Full Study:

1. Greek Roots / Derivation

Derived from the verb chairo, which means "to rejoice" or "to be glad."

It is closely related to chara (joy) and charisma (a gift of grace).

In its root sense, it describes that which causes joy, pleasure, or delight, moving from the objective "loveliness" of a thing to the subjective "favor" shown by a giver.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Favor, kindness, or goodwill.

In the New Testament, it signifies the "free gift" of God’s favor toward those who deserve the opposite (judgment).

It describes both the state of being in God’s favor (justification) and the empowering presence of God’s Spirit in the soul (sanctification).

Lexically, it is the antithesis of opheilema (a debt) or ergon (works).

3. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures

- The Royal Bounty: In Hellenistic culture, charis described a king's "unilateral favor" toward a subject, where the king grants a request or a gift purely out of his own generosity, not because the subject earned it.

- The Gracious Gesture: It pictured a "beautiful" or "charming" act that brought light and joy into a dark situation, emphasizing the character of the giver.

- The Gift and Response: In social contracts, it referred to a "favor" that creates a bond of gratitude between the benefactor and the recipient, where the recipient’s life becomes a "thank-offering" for the gift received.

4. Theological Implications

Grace is the "source" of salvation (Ephesians 2:8) and the "means" of our justification (Titus 3:7).

It represents the "appearance" of God's character in human history, specifically through the Incarnation of Christ.

Theologically, grace is not just a passive sentiment in the mind of God; it is a "dynamic power" that teaches, trains, and transforms the believer to deny ungodliness.

5. Contextual Nuance in Titus 2:11

In the NKJV of Titus 2:11, Paul writes, "For the grace (charis) of God that brings salvation has appeared to all men."

- The Manifestation: The word "appeared" (epephane) suggests the breaking forth of light, as in a sunrise. Grace is not an abstract concept here; it is personified in Jesus Christ.

- The Scope: It is "saving grace" (soterios), defining its primary function as the delivery of man from the penalty and power of sin.

- The Universality: It has appeared to "all men," meaning the offer of grace is no longer restricted to one nation but is heralded to the entire world.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

- John 1:17: “For the law was given through Moses, but grace (charis) and truth came through Jesus Christ.”

- Romans 3:24: “being justified freely by His grace (chariti) through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus,”

- Romans 5:20: “...But where sin abounded, grace (charis) abounded much more,”

- Ephesians 2:8: “For by grace (chariti) you have been saved through faith...”

- 2 Timothy 2:1: “You therefore, my son, be strong in the grace (chariti) that is in Christ Jesus.”

- Titus 3:7: “that having been justified by His grace (chariti) we should become heirs...”

7. Other Relevant Insights

This word highlights that the "maintenance of good works" (Titus 3:8) is the result of grace, not the cause of it.

It suggests that a "denying of ungodliness" (Titus 2:12) is only possible when a person is "instructed" by the grace that saved them.

By identifying grace as something that "appeared," Paul emphasizes that the Gospel is a historical "matter" (Titus 3:9) based on the visible intervention of God in the world.
