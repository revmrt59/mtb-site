Summary:

LUSTS - ( epithymias , Strong’s [1939])

Strong desires, cravings, or yearnings. It refers to the intense internal longings of the soul directed toward what is forbidden or worldly, acting as the primary driving force behind sinful conduct.

Full Study:

1. Greek Roots / Derivation

Derived from the preposition epi, which functions as an intensive (meaning "upon" or "focused"), and thymos, which means "passion," "mind," or "breath."

It literally describes a "passion set upon" something. It carries the image of the soul reaching out with great intensity toward a specific object or goal.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

A desire, craving, or longing.

While the word can be used neutrally for any strong desire, in the New Testament it is almost exclusively used in a negative sense to describe "evil desires" or "concupiscence."

Lexically, it is the impulse of the heart toward an object that is outside the will of God, representing the internal source of external temptation.

3. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures

- The Internal Wind: In ancient Greek thought, it was sometimes pictured as a strong wind that drives a ship; if the wind is uncontrolled, it drives the vessel toward the rocks.

- The Hungry Beast: It pictured the "appetite" of the lower nature that is never satisfied, constantly demanding more of what the world offers to the senses.

- The Chains of the Flesh: It was used to describe the "shackles" that bind a person to their base impulses, preventing them from pursuing the higher virtues of the spirit.

4. Theological Implications

Lusts are identified as the "conception" point of sin (James 1:14–15). They are the internal conditions that make external temptation effective.

They characterize the "former life" of the believer and the "old man" that was crucified with Christ.

Theologically, lusts are the fruit of the fallen nature, seeking fulfillment in the creation rather than in the Creator.

5. Contextual Nuance in Titus 2:12

In the NKJV of Titus 2:12, Paul qualifies these as "worldly lusts (epithymias)."

- The Worldly Connection: The adjective "worldly" (kosmikas) identifies these desires as belonging to the present age and the system of the world that is passing away.

- The Object of Denial: Grace "teaches" the believer to "deny" these cravings. This implies that the Christian life involves an ongoing refusal to let these internal impulses dictate one's behavior.

- The Contrast: These lusts stand in direct opposition to the "sober, righteous, and godly" life that grace produces.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

- Galatians 5:16: “...and you shall not fulfill the lust (epithymian) of the flesh.”

- Ephesians 2:3: “...among whom also we all once conducted ourselves in the lusts (epithymiais) of our flesh...”

- 1 Timothy 6:9: “...fall into temptation and a snare, and into many foolish and harmful lusts (epithymias)...”

- 2 Timothy 2:22: “Flee also youthful lusts (epithymias); but pursue righteousness, faith, love, peace...”

- 1 John 2:16: “...the lust (epithymia) of the flesh, the lust (epithymia) of the eyes, and the pride of life...”

7. Other Relevant Insights

This word highlights that the spiritual battle is primarily internal. The "denying" must happen at the level of the "desire" before it reaches the level of the "deed."

It suggests that "worldliness" is not just about external locations or activities, but about the "lusts" that connect a heart to the world's values.

By teaching us to deny these lusts, grace empowers the believer to live for the "blessed hope" (verse 13) rather than for the temporary gratification of the senses.
