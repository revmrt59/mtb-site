Summary:

PRESENT - ( nyn , Strong’s [3568])

At this moment or in the current time. In Titus 2:12, it refers to the current era or "age" in which the believer lives, characterizing the specific historical and moral environment where the training of grace must be applied.

Full Study:

1. Greek Roots / Derivation

A primary particle of time. It is a foundational adverb in the Greek language used to identify the immediate point of time in contrast to the past or the future.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Now, at this time, or the present.

When used with a noun (like "age"), it functions as an adjective to describe the current state of things or the era currently in progress.

It signifies the "here and now," emphasizing the immediate reality and the temporal nature of the world before the return of Christ.

3. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures

* The Point of Transition: In Greek thought, it was the "razor's edge" between what has been and what will be, representing the only time in which a person can actually take action.

* The Current Administration: It was used in legal and political contexts to describe the "now-existing" laws or rulers, distinguishing them from former regimes.

* The Theater of Life: It pictured the "stage" upon which the current generation of actors performs their roles, emphasizing that this "scene" is temporary and will eventually be replaced.

4. Theological Implications

The "present" is the period of the "already but not yet." The grace of God has appeared in the past, but the glory has not yet appeared in the future.

It represents the time of testing and training. The "present age" is a fallen system, yet it is the very place where God’s light is to shine through His people.

Theologically, the "now" is the time of salvation and the opportunity for the practice of good works.

5. Contextual Nuance in Titus 2:12

In the NKJV of Titus 2:12, it is part of the phrase "present (nyn) age."

- The Distinction: By adding "present," Paul acknowledges that there is another age—the age to come.

- The Urgency: The commands to live soberly and godly are not for a future utopia, but for the messy, ungodly reality of the current era.

- The Contrast: The "present age" is the domain of "worldly lusts," yet grace enables the believer to live differently within it.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

* Romans 8:18: “For I consider that the sufferings of this present (nyn) time are not worthy to be compared...”

* 2 Corinthians 6:2: “...Behold, now (nyn) is the accepted time; behold, now (nyn) is the day of salvation.”

* 2 Timothy 4:10: “for Demas has forsaken me, having loved this present (nyn) world...”

* Titus 2:12: “...we should live soberly, righteously, and godly in the present (nyn) age,”

* 1 Peter 1:12: “...things which have now (nyn) been reported to you through those who have preached the gospel...”

7. Other Relevant Insights

This word highlights that the Christian life is a duty for today; it is the immediate response to the "teaching" of grace.

It suggests that a life of faith is not lived in a vacuum but in a specific temporal context that demands alertness.

By focusing on the "present age," the text reminds the believer that their conduct is intended to be visible to the world in its current state, serving as a testimony to the grace that has appeared.

Regarding the transliteration nun vs. nyn: Both are correct depending on the system used.

- Nun is the common transliteration used in Strong’s Concordance and many English-based study tools.

- Nyn is the more academic transliteration favored by lexicons like Zodhiates’ or Thayer’s, because the Greek letter upsilon ($\upsilon$) is traditionally represented by the English letter 'y' to distinguish it from the 'u' sound of other vowels.

Regardless of the spelling, the word always refers to the same Greek term for the "immediate present."
