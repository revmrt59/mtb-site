Summary:

WORKS - ( ergon , Strong’s [2041])

An action, deed, or labor. In Titus 2:14, it refers to the visible, noble, and beneficial activities that believers are empowered to perform as a direct result of being redeemed and purified by Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Greek Roots / Derivation

Derived from the primary root ergo (to work). It is a foundational Greek term for any deed or action, whether physical, mental, or spiritual. The root focuses on the output or the product of an individual's energy and will.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

A business, employment, that which any one is occupied, or that which one undertakes to do.

It signifies an act, deed, or anything accomplished by hand or art.

In the New Testament, it often refers to the "works" of a person as the evidence of their character, or "good works" as the fruit of a life transformed by the Holy Spirit.

3. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures

- The Artisan’s Product: In secular Greek, it pictured the finished work of a craftsman—the table made by the carpenter or the statue carved by the sculptor—representing the visible proof of the maker's skill.

- The Farmer’s Labor: It was used for the "toil" of the field, specifically the tasks required to produce a harvest, representing the effort necessary to bring about a result.

- The Public Benefactor: It described the "noble deeds" done for the benefit of a city or state, representing actions that were not only functional but also honorable and beneficial to others.

4. Theological Implications

Works are the "fruit" of faith, not the "root" of salvation. While we are not saved by works, we are saved for works (Ephesians 2:10).

They serve as the external evidence of an internal reality. A "living faith" is one that is accompanied by "works" (James 2:17).

Theologically, the "good works" of a believer are the "excellent" and "profitable" outcomes (Titus 3:8) of the "grace" that has appeared to all men.

5. Contextual Nuance in Titus 2:14

In the NKJV of Titus 2:14, the believer is to be "zealous for good works (kala erga)."

- The Adjective: Paul uses kalos, which means "beautiful," "noble," or "excellent." These works are not just morally correct; they are attractive and draw others to the Gospel.

- The Relationship: These works are the direct consequence of being "purified" for Christ's own possession. One cannot do "good works" until they are redeemed from "lawless deeds."

- The Motivation: The zeal for these works comes from the "blessed hope" (verse 13) and the awareness that we belong to the "Great God and Savior."

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

- Matthew 5:16: “Let your light so shine before men, that they may see your good works (kala erga) and glorify your Father...”

- John 6:29: “Jesus answered and said to them, ‘This is the work (ergon) of God, that you believe in Him whom He sent.’”

- Ephesians 2:10: “For we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works (ergois agathois)...”

- Titus 3:1: “...to be ready for every good work (ergon agathon),”

- Titus 3:8: “...those who have believed in God should be careful to maintain good works (kalon ergon).”

- Titus 3:14: “And let our people also learn to maintain good works (kalon ergon)...”

7. Other Relevant Insights

This word highlights that the Christian life is an "active" life. The "grace of God" does not produce a passive person but one who is "occupied" with that which is good.

It suggests that "lawless deeds" (verse 14) are a perversion of the "work" God intended for humanity.

By maintaining "good works," the believer provides a "profitable" (Titus 3:8) testimony to the world, proving that the Gospel has the power to change a person's conduct as well as their destiny.
