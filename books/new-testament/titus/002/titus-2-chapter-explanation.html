Titus 2:1
NKJV

Titus 2:1 But as for you, speak the things which are proper for sound doctrine:(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:1 As for you, Titus, promote the kind of living that reflects wholesome teaching. (NLT)

Main Idea

Unlike the false teachers who upset whole households with empty talk, Titus is to teach what fits with "sound doctrine," meaning healthy teaching that produces spiritually healthy, mature believers. Right teaching is not just correct ideas; it shapes how God's people actually live.

Verse Explanation

Unlike the false teachers who upset whole households with empty talk, Titus is to teach what fits with "sound doctrine," meaning healthy teaching that produces spiritually healthy, mature believers. Right teaching is not just correct ideas; it shapes how God's people actually live.

"But as for you,"

Paul had left Titus on the island of Crete to finish organizing the young churches there and to appoint elders in every city (1:5). Titus was a Gentile convert whom Paul called "a true son in our common faith" (1:4), and he was serving as Paul's personal representative among the Cretan believers. The Greek wording here is emphatic and sets Titus apart from the people Paul has just described. Those false teachers were "insubordinate, both idle talkers and deceivers," and they were upsetting "whole households, teaching things which they ought not, for the sake of dishonest gain" (1:10–11). Their message centered on "Jewish fables and commandments of men" (1:14). Paul's summary of them is blunt: "They profess to know God, but in works they deny Him" (1:16). Their talk sounded religious, but their lives contradicted it. Titus was to be different.

"speak the things which are proper"

The false teachers' "mouths must be stopped" (1:11), but Titus's mouth was to stay open. The Greek verb points to ongoing speaking. Titus was to keep teaching, not deliver a single correction and move on. The answer to bad teaching was not silence but faithful, steady instruction in the truth.

The word "proper" means fitting or suitable, what naturally belongs with something. Titus was to teach the things that fit sound doctrine. As the instructions that follow show, these are mostly about everyday life: how older men and women carry themselves, how young wives love their families, how young men exercise self-control, and how bondservants serve their masters (2:2–10). Paul is not changing the subject from doctrine to behavior. The life that grows out of true teaching is part of what Titus must teach. The false teachers claimed to know God while living in a way that denied Him. Titus was to teach a faith that shows up in how God's people actually live.

"for sound doctrine:"

"Doctrine" simply means teaching. The Greek word translated "sound" means healthy or whole, and our English word "hygiene" comes from it. Sound doctrine is healthy teaching. It is certainly true and correct, but the word means more than that. Healthy teaching produces spiritually healthy people, believers who grow mature and live godly lives. Paul has already told Titus that an elder must be able "by sound doctrine, both to exhort and convict those who contradict" (1:9), and that the Cretans needed sharp correction "that they may be sound in the faith" (1:13). Elsewhere he warns that false teaching "will spread like cancer" (2 Timothy 2:17). False teaching works like a disease, spreading and doing damage. Sound doctrine works like good health, strengthening God's people from the inside out.

That is why Paul can follow a command about doctrine with a list of character qualities for ordinary believers. Teaching that is truly healthy cannot stay only in the mind. It shapes homes, speech, work, and relationships, and Paul will soon show that it rests on God's saving grace, which teaches believers how to live (2:11–12).

Titus 2:2
Titus 2:2
NKJV

Titus 2:2 that the older men be sober, reverent, temperate, sound in faith, in love, in patience;(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:2 Teach the older men to exercise self-control, to be worthy of respect, and to live wisely. They must have sound faith and be filled with love and patience. (NLT)

Main Idea

Older men are to be clear-headed, dignified, and self-controlled, and to be healthy in their faith, their love, and their steady endurance. Their years should show mature, well-rounded Christian character that younger believers can look up to.

Verse Explanation

Older men are to be clear-headed, dignified, and self-controlled, and to be healthy in their faith, their love, and their steady endurance. Their years should show mature, well-rounded Christian character that younger believers can look up to.

"that the older men be sober,"

Paul is telling Titus what to teach each group in the Cretan churches, beginning with the older men. The Greek word here describes men advanced in years, not the church office of elder from chapter 1, though many of these men may have served as elders. Paul uses the same word for himself as "Paul, the aged" (Philemon 9). Their age gave them influence in their families and congregations, and their lives would show younger believers what healthy Christian teaching looks like over a lifetime.

The word "sober" originally meant free from the effects of wine, and it includes that. Older women are told not to be "given to much wine" (2:3), and the overseer must be "not given to wine" (1:7). But the word came to describe a clear, alert mind more broadly, the opposite of being dulled or carried away by anything. Paul uses it for church leaders, who must be "temperate" (1 Timothy 3:2), and for their wives (1 Timothy 3:11). On Crete, where Paul's quotation called the people "lazy gluttons" (1:12), older men who kept a clear head and steady habits would stand out.

"reverent,"

"Reverent" describes a person whose conduct is dignified and worthy of respect. Deacons must be "reverent" in the same way (1 Timothy 3:8). It does not mean stiff or humorless. It describes a man who takes God and life seriously, so his words and behavior carry weight. Such a man is not silly, careless, or crude, and others can respect him because his life matches the gravity of what he believes.

"temperate,"

This word means sensible, self-controlled, and sound in judgment. It describes a mind kept in order, so a person thinks clearly and acts wisely instead of being ruled by impulses, appetites, or moods. The same quality is required of elders, who must be "sober-minded" (1:8). This word family runs through the whole chapter. The older women are to "admonish," literally to train in this self-control, the younger women (2:4). The younger women are to be "discreet" (2:5), and the younger men "sober-minded" (2:6). Grace itself teaches all believers to live "soberly" (2:12). Paul asks this of everyone, but he begins with the older men, who should have had the most years to learn it.

"sound in faith, in love, in patience;"

"Sound" means healthy. Paul wants these men spiritually healthy in three areas. Paul has already told Titus to rebuke those misled by the false teachers, "that they may be sound in the faith" (1:13). The older men should already be what those others need to become.

"Faith" here is their trust in God and their firm hold on the truth He has revealed. "Love" is their care for God and for others. "Patience" means steady endurance, the ability to keep going under hardship without giving up. Paul often joins faith and love with hope, as in "your work of faith, labor of love, and patience of hope in our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Thessalonians 1:3). Here patience takes hope's place. Endurance is what hope produces in a person who has trusted God through many years. Long life brings disappointments and losses, and faith can turn into cynicism, love can cool, and patience can wear thin. Paul wants older men whose faith has stayed firm, whose love has grown warmer, and whose endurance has been strengthened by all they have been through.

Titus 2:3 the older women likewise, that they be reverent in behavior, not slanderers, not given to much wine, teachers of good things"”(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:3 Similarly, teach the older women to live in a way that honors God. They must not slander others or be heavy drinkers. Instead, they should teach others what is good. (NLT)

Main Idea

Older women are to live in a way that shows they take God seriously, avoiding gossip and drunkenness and instead using their experience to teach what is good. Their influence is meant to be a positive force in the church family.

Verse Explanation

Older women are to live in a way that shows they take God seriously, avoiding gossip and drunkenness and instead using their experience to teach what is good. Their influence is meant to be a positive force in the church family.

"the older women likewise,"

Paul has just told Titus what to teach the older men (2:2), and "likewise" carries the same seriousness over to the older women. They were not an afterthought in the church. The healthy teaching that produces godly lives was meant to shape them as much as anyone, and they had an important role in passing it on. Paul gives no specific age. He means the mature women of the congregation, those whose years and experience gave them a natural place of influence among the younger women.

"that they be reverent in behavior,"

This is not the same word for "reverent" used of the older men in verse 2. The Greek word here describes what is fitting for something holy, and it was used for what was proper in a temple or for those who served there. "Behavior" refers to a person's whole manner and bearing, how she carries herself day to day. Paul is saying that an older woman's ordinary life should look like it belongs to God. Her conduct at home, in conversation, and in the community should fit someone who lives in God's presence and takes Him seriously.

"not slanderers,"

The Greek word for "slanderers" is diabolos, the same word used for the devil, who is the great accuser (Revelation 12:10). A slanderer spreads damaging talk about others, whether it is false or simply not hers to share. Paul uses the same word when he says women serving in the church must be "not slanderers" (1 Timothy 3:11), and he lists "slanderers" among the marks of people in the last days (2 Timothy 3:3). Women with time, experience, and close ties to many households could easily become the center of harmful talk. In a church already troubled by the false teachers of chapter 1, who were upsetting whole households (1:11), careless words could do great damage. Instead, the older women's speech was to build others up.

"not given to much wine,"

The Greek literally says "not enslaved to much wine." Paul is not describing someone who simply has a drink. He is describing someone who has become dependent on wine and is controlled by it. Paul had already described the Cretans' own reputation with the words "lazy gluttons" (1:12), so this was a real danger in that culture. A woman ruled by wine cannot be self-controlled, and she cannot guide younger women well.

"teachers of good things—"

In Greek this is a single word meaning "teachers of what is good." After the two warnings, Paul turns to what the older women should do. The next verse shows what he has in mind: they are to "admonish the young women to love their husbands, to love their children" (2:4). This was not public teaching in the church gathering. It was the kind of teaching that happens through friendship, example, and wise counsel in everyday life. The contrast with the false teachers on Crete is clear. They were "teaching things which they ought not" and tearing households apart (1:11). The older women were to teach good things and help build households up. Their years were not a time to step back. They were a time to pass on to the next generation what they had learned of a life shaped by God.

Titus 2:4
Titus 2:4
NKJV

Titus 2:4 that they admonish the young women to love their husbands, to love their children,(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:4 These older women must train the younger women to love their husbands and their children, (NLT)

Main Idea

One main way older women teach good things is by training the younger women to love their husbands and children. Love for family is something that can be learned and encouraged, and mature women are well placed to pass it on.

Verse Explanation

One main way older women teach good things is by training the younger women to love their husbands and children. Love for family is something that can be learned and encouraged, and mature women are well placed to pass it on.

"that they admonish the young women"

"They" are the older women, whom Paul has just called "teachers of good things" (2:3). This verse shows what that teaching looks like in practice. The Greek word translated "admonish" means to train someone to think sensibly, to help her grow wise and self-controlled. It belongs to the same word family as "temperate" in verse 2 and "discreet" in verse 5. It is not a word for scolding. It describes patient, practical training, the way an experienced woman helps a younger one learn to handle life well. The "young women" here are mainly young wives and mothers, women in the stage of life the older women had already passed through.

Paul gives this training to the older women rather than assigning it to Titus. Titus was to teach and set an example for everyone, but older women could speak to the daily realities of marriage and motherhood from experience. Many interpreters also see wisdom in having women instruct women, which would have protected Titus from suspicion in a setting where the false teachers of chapter 1 were already upsetting whole households (1:11).

"to love their husbands, to love their children,"

In Greek each of these phrases is a single word: "husband-loving" and "children-loving." Neither word appears anywhere else in the New Testament. Both are built on a word for warm, affectionate love, the kind of love that enjoys and cherishes the people closest to us.

Paul does not assume this love will grow on its own. Living with a husband and raising children every day can wear down affection, and selfishness, weariness, and conflict can slowly replace it. So Paul says this love can be taught and strengthened. Older women who had kept loving their husbands through hard years, and who had poured themselves into their children, could help younger women do the same. This is part of what Paul means by "sound doctrine" (2:1): healthy teaching produces healthy lives, and one of the first places that health shows up is the home. Marriage and motherhood are not small or secondary matters next to spiritual things. They are among the main places where God's grace trains believers to live godly lives (2:11–12).

Titus 2:5
Titus 2:5
NKJV

Titus 2:5 to be discreet, chaste, homemakers, good, obedient to their own husbands, that the word of God may not be blasphemed.(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:5 to live wisely and be pure, to work in their homes, to do good, and to be submissive to their husbands. Then they will not bring shame on the word of God. (NLT)

Main Idea

Younger women are encouraged to be sensible, pure, devoted to caring for their homes, kind, and respectful toward their own husbands, so that no one can speak badly of God's message because of how Christians live. The way believers handle their closest relationships either honors God's word or gives others a reason to mock it.

Verse Explanation

Younger women are encouraged to be sensible, pure, devoted to caring for their homes, kind, and respectful toward their own husbands, so that no one can speak badly of God's message because of how Christians live. The way believers handle their closest relationships either honors God's word or gives others a reason to mock it.

"to be discreet,"

This verse continues the list of what the older women are to teach the younger women, who have just been urged "to love their husbands, to love their children" (2:4). "Discreet" translates the same Greek word rendered "temperate" for the older men (2:2). It describes a sensible, self-controlled mind, someone who thinks clearly and does not let feelings or impulses take charge. The verb in verse 4 that describes how the older women "admonish" the younger comes from the same word family. The older women were to train younger women in this same steady good sense.

"chaste,"

The Greek word means pure or morally clean. It includes sexual faithfulness, but it also describes a heart and life free from what defiles. For a young wife this meant keeping herself wholly for her husband in a culture where sexual immorality was common and widely accepted.

"homemakers,"

The word describes a woman who works at home, busy and diligent in caring for her household. The opposite picture appears in 1 Timothy 5:13, where some younger widows "learn to be idle, wandering about from house to house." Paul wanted them instead to "marry, bear children, manage the house, give no opportunity to the adversary to speak reproachfully" (1 Timothy 5:14). The point is faithful care for the family, not confinement or idleness. The wife of Proverbs 31 "watches over the ways of her household, and does not eat the bread of idleness" (Proverbs 31:27), and her diligence also reached into buying a field and trading. On Crete, where false teachers were overturning "whole households" (1:11), homes that were well cared for gave a strong testimony.

"good,"

"Good" here means kind and generous, showing goodness toward others. A woman can keep a busy home and still be harsh or self-centered. Paul wanted the younger women's diligence marked by warmth toward their families, guests, and neighbors. Dorcas is remembered as a woman "full of good works and charitable deeds" (Acts 9:36).

"obedient to their own husbands,"

The Greek word means to submit, to willingly place oneself under another's leadership. It is something a wife chooses to do, not something forced on her. "Their own" matters: Paul is not saying women must submit to men in general, but that a wife should respect and follow her own husband's leadership. Ephesians 5:22 gives the same instruction, "Wives, submit to your own husbands, as to the Lord," and it is followed at once by the command for husbands to love their wives "just as Christ also loved the church and gave Himself for her" (Ephesians 5:25). Submission does not mean the wife is of less worth, since husband and wife are "heirs together of the grace of life" (1 Peter 3:7).

"that the word of God may not be blasphemed."

This purpose clause explains why all these qualities matter. "Blasphemed" means slandered, spoken against. "The word of God" is the gospel message the churches had received. Outsiders in the Roman world watched new religious groups closely, and Christianity was often suspected of breaking up families and undermining good order. If Christian wives neglected their homes, lived impurely, or treated their husbands with contempt, their neighbors would not blame only the women. They would blame the message. Paul's sharpest warning on this point comes from the prophets: "The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you" (Romans 2:24).

The same concern runs through the whole chapter. Paul gives a similar reason for bondservants elsewhere, "so that the name of God and His doctrine may not be blasphemed" (1 Timothy 6:1). Here it shows what healthy teaching looks like in ordinary life. The false teachers of chapter 1 "profess to know God, but in works they deny Him" (1:16). The young wives on Crete were to show the opposite, a life where the gospel they believed was visible in how they loved and served at home. Peter even speaks of unbelieving husbands being "won by the conduct of their wives" without a word (1 Peter 3:1). A Christian home lived this way protects the gospel from slander and helps it gain a hearing.

Titus 2:6
Titus 2:6
NKJV

Titus 2:6 Likewise, exhort the young men to be sober-minded,(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:6 In the same way, encourage the young men to live wisely. (NLT)

Main Idea

Young men are urged to be self-controlled and sensible in how they think and act. In every age group, healthy teaching is meant to produce the same kind of steady, godly character.

Verse Explanation

Young men are urged to be self-controlled and sensible in how they think and act. In every age group, healthy teaching is meant to produce the same kind of steady, godly character.

"Likewise, exhort the young men"

"Likewise" carries the pattern of the previous verses forward. Paul has described the godly character Titus should teach older men, older women, and young women (2:2–5), and now he turns to the young men. The same healthy teaching applies to them.

The Greek word for "exhort" (parakaleō) literally means to call someone to your side. It can mean to urge, to encourage, to appeal, or to comfort. It is firm, but it is also personal. Paul told Timothy to "exhort" an older man "as a father, younger men as brothers" (1 Timothy 5:1), so this is how a brother speaks to a brother, not how a master orders a servant. Paul had already said an elder must be able, "by sound doctrine, both to exhort and convict those who contradict" (1:9). Here Titus himself does that work, pressing the truth on young men in a way meant to move them to act. The young women were to be trained by the older women (2:4), but Titus is to exhort the young men directly, and the next verse shows he must also set the example for them (2:7).

"to be sober-minded,"

Paul gives the young men only one quality, but it is a broad one. "Sober-minded" translates a Greek word that means to have a sound, sensible mind that keeps itself under control. It comes from the same family of words used for the older men being "temperate" (2:2) and the young women being "discreet" (2:5). A young man with this quality thinks clearly and does not let his desires, temper, ambition, or tongue rule him. He has self-control.

Young men often have strength, energy, and confidence, and those gifts can be wasted or turned to harm without self-control. Paul warned Timothy, "Flee also youthful lusts; but pursue righteousness, faith, love, peace with those who call on the Lord out of a pure heart" (2 Timothy 2:22). Proverbs puts a high value on this kind of strength: "He who is slow to anger is better than the mighty, and he who rules his spirit than he who takes a city" (Proverbs 16:32).

The same word appears among the qualifications for elders, who must be "sober-minded" (1:8). On Crete, where people were known as "lazy gluttons" (1:12) and false teachers were upsetting whole households (1:11), self-controlled young men would grow into the steady leaders the churches needed. This self-control does not come from willpower alone. Paul soon explains that the grace of God itself teaches believers to live "soberly" (2:12).

Titus 2:7
Titus 2:7
NKJV

Titus 2:7 in all things showing yourself to be a pattern of good works; in doctrine showing integrity, reverence, incorruptibility,(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:7 And you yourself must be an example to them by doing good works of every kind. Let everything you do reflect the integrity and seriousness of your teaching. (NLT)

Main Idea

Titus himself is to be a living example of good works, and his teaching is to be marked by honesty, seriousness, and purity of motive. A leader's life and teaching must match if people are going to take the message seriously.

Verse Explanation

Titus himself is to be a living example of good works, and his teaching is to be marked by honesty, seriousness, and purity of motive. A leader's life and teaching must match if people are going to take the message seriously.

"in all things showing yourself to be a pattern of good works;"

Paul has just told Titus to urge the younger men to be self-controlled (2:6). Now he turns to Titus himself. As Paul's delegate in Crete, Titus was responsible for teaching every group in the church, and what he said would carry weight only if his own life matched it. "In all things" leaves nothing out. His conduct, his work, and the way he treated people were all part of his ministry.

The Greek word translated "pattern" first described the mark left by a blow or a stamp, like an image pressed into wax. It came to mean a model that others could look at and copy. Paul gave Timothy the same charge: "be an example to the believers in word, in conduct, in love, in spirit, in faith, in purity" (1 Timothy 4:12). Paul lived this way himself, telling the Philippians, "join in following my example, and note those who so walk, as you have us for a pattern" (Philippians 3:17). Christians learn how to live largely by watching others who already live that way, and Titus was to be someone the believers on Crete could safely imitate.

"Good works" are acts of goodness, kindness, and service that grow out of a life changed by God. This stands against the false teachers of chapter 1, of whom Paul said, "They profess to know God, but in works they deny Him, being abominable, disobedient, and disqualified for every good work" (1:16). Their lives contradicted their words. Titus's life was to show what healthy teaching produces when it is actually lived, the same good works Christ redeemed His people to pursue (2:14).

"in doctrine showing integrity, reverence, incorruptibility,"

"Doctrine" here refers to Titus's teaching, both what he taught and the way he taught it. Paul has already told him to "speak the things which are proper for sound doctrine" (2:1), meaning healthy teaching that produces godly lives. Now Paul describes the character that teaching must have.

"Integrity" translates a word meaning unspoiled or uncorrupted. Titus's teaching was to be pure in both content and motive. He was not to twist God's word to please people or use it for his own advantage. That mattered on Crete, where the false teachers were "teaching things which they ought not, for the sake of dishonest gain" (1:11). People can usually tell when a teacher is serving himself, and Titus was to give them no reason to suspect it.

"Reverence" describes dignity and seriousness, the same quality Paul wanted in the older men (2:2). Titus was handling the word of God, and his manner was to show that he took God seriously. He was not to be careless, flippant, or showy with sacred things. "Incorruptibility" carries the idea of something that does not rot or spoil, and older English versions rendered it "sincerity." Titus's teaching was to remain genuine and wholesome, the same truth delivered with the same honest heart whether people welcomed it or resisted it.

Taken together, the verse shows that Titus's life and his teaching were not two separate parts of his ministry. The believers on Crete would learn what God's truth looks like by listening to what Titus taught and by watching how he lived.

Study Notes

The NKJV lists three qualities for Titus's teaching: "integrity, reverence, incorruptibility." Most modern translations list only two, such as "integrity, dignity" (ESV). The difference comes from the Greek manuscripts. The text behind the NKJV, which follows the majority of later manuscripts, includes a third word, aphtharsia. The KJV rendered it "sincerity," and the NKJV renders it "incorruptibility." The oldest surviving manuscripts lack this word. Many scholars think it began as a note in the margin explaining the rare first term, which has a nearly identical meaning, and was later copied into the text itself. Others hold that it belongs to the original wording.

The variant hardly changes the meaning. All three terms describe teaching that is pure, honest, and untainted by self-interest. The first and third words are so close in meaning that the NKJV's longer reading mainly adds emphasis. Paul's point stands in either text: Titus's teaching must be free of the corruption that marked the Cretan false teachers, who taught "for the sake of dishonest gain" (1:11).

Titus 2:8
NKJV

Titus 2:8 sound speech that cannot be condemned, that one who is an opponent may be ashamed, having nothing evil to say of you.(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:8 Teach the truth so that your teaching can’t be criticized. Then those who oppose us will be ashamed and have nothing bad to say about us. (NLT)

Main Idea

His words are to be healthy and above criticism, so that anyone who opposes the gospel will be embarrassed to find nothing bad to say. A blameless life and careful speech take away the ammunition of those looking for a reason to attack.

Verse Explanation

His words are to be healthy and above criticism, so that anyone who opposes the gospel will be embarrassed to find nothing bad to say. A blameless life and careful speech take away the ammunition of those looking for a reason to attack.

"sound speech that cannot be condemned,"

This phrase finishes the description Paul began in verse 7 of how Titus himself must teach. "Sound" is the same word Paul used for "sound doctrine" (2:1), healthy teaching that produces godly lives. Here it describes Titus's "speech," his preaching and teaching above all, but also the way he talks with people generally. His words were to be wholesome and spiritually nourishing, the kind that strengthen people rather than confuse or harm them.

"Cannot be condemned" means beyond criticism, giving no fair grounds for blame. This set Titus apart from the false teachers on Crete. They were "idle talkers and deceivers" who taught "for the sake of dishonest gain" (1:10–11), and though they claimed to know God, "in works they deny Him" (1:16). Titus could not answer them effectively if his own words were careless, harsh, or inconsistent. What he said and how he said it had to hold up under close examination.

"that one who is an opponent may be ashamed,"

The "opponent" is anyone standing on the other side, whether one of the false teachers or an outsider hostile to the gospel. Paul assumes that such people will be watching and listening for something to attack. When they look closely at Titus and find his teaching and conduct above reproach, their accusations fall flat, and they are left embarrassed. Peter makes the same point to believers facing slander, urging them to keep a good conscience, "that when they defame you as evildoers, those who revile your good conduct in Christ may be ashamed" (1 Peter 3:16).

The goal is not to humiliate an opponent for its own sake. Paul told Timothy to correct opponents "in humility," hoping that "God perhaps will grant them repentance, so that they may know the truth" (2 Timothy 2:25). Shame that comes from finding no fault may be the very thing that causes a critic to reconsider.

"having nothing evil to say of you."

The word "evil" here means anything bad or worthless, any fault at all that could be used against Titus. Paul has already said that believers' conduct affects how others view the gospel, so "that the word of God may not be blasphemed" (2:5). The same is true of Titus. If the man appointed to teach the churches could be fairly accused of wrongdoing, his opponents would use it to discredit the message itself. Paul held himself to the same standard: "We give no offense in anything, that our ministry may not be blamed" (2 Corinthians 6:3). Opponents might still criticize Titus, but if his speech and life were sound, they would have nothing true to say against him.

Study Notes

In verse 7 Paul speaks to Titus personally ("showing yourself"). At the end of verse 8, however, the word "you" is plural in the Greek text behind the NKJV. Opponents are to have "nothing evil to say" about Titus and the believers together, not about Titus alone. The oldest manuscripts, followed by most modern translations, read "us" instead of "you." That reading would include Paul and the whole Christian community. In the Greek of that period, the words for "you" (plural) and "us" were spelled almost alike and pronounced almost the same, so copyists easily confused them.

Both readings point the same way. Titus's sound speech and blameless conduct protect more than his own reputation. Opponents looking for ammunition would treat any fault in the church's leader as a fault in the church and its message. This fits the concern running through the chapter, that the gospel itself not be "blasphemed" because of how believers live (2:5, 10).

NKJV

Titus 2:9 Exhort bondservants to be obedient to their own masters, to be well pleasing in all things, not answering back,(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:9 Slaves must always obey their masters and do their best to please them. They must not talk back (NLT)

Main Idea

Bondservants, who were a large part of society in that day, are urged to obey their masters, to work in a way that genuinely pleases them, and not to talk back. Even in a hard and unfair position, a believer's attitude can reflect the gospel.

Verse Explanation

Bondservants, who were a large part of society in that day, are urged to obey their masters, to work in a way that genuinely pleases them, and not to talk back. Even in a hard and unfair position, a believer's attitude can reflect the gospel.

"Exhort bondservants"

The command to "exhort," or urge, carries over from Paul's instructions to the young men (2:6). Titus is to speak to believers in every station of life, and now he turns to bondservants, the slaves who made up a large part of the population in the Roman world. People became slaves through capture in war, through debt, by being sold, or simply by being born to a slave mother. Many worked in fields and mines, but many others lived in households as cooks, nurses, craftsmen, teachers, managers, and even doctors. Some could eventually earn or be granted their freedom. Even so, a slave was legally the property of his master and could be bought, sold, or beaten, and he had very little control over his own life.

Paul does not approve of this system. He lists "kidnappers," people who stole others to sell them, among the lawless (1 Timothy 1:10). He encouraged slaves to gain their freedom if they could (1 Corinthians 7:21), and he told masters to give their bondservants "what is just and fair, knowing that you also have a Master in heaven" (Colossians 4:1). In Christ, "there is neither slave nor free" (Galatians 3:28). But many believers on Crete were slaves with no way out, and Paul shows them how to live for God where they were. The word for "bondservant" is the same one Paul uses for himself at the start of the letter: "Paul, a bondservant of God" (1:1). The apostle and the household slave share the same identity before the Lord.

"to be obedient to their own masters,"

The word translated "obedient" means to place oneself under another's authority. It is the same word Paul uses for wives being "obedient to their own husbands" (2:5). "Their own" points to each slave's particular master, the person God had placed over him in his actual situation. That master might be a believer or an unbeliever, and Paul gives no exception for a master who did not know Christ.

"to be well pleasing in all things,"

Paul goes beyond bare obedience. A slave could do what he was told while working slowly, carelessly, or resentfully. Paul calls believing slaves to serve in a way that actually satisfies their masters. The word translated "well pleasing" is one Paul usually uses for what pleases God, as when he says, "we make it our aim... to be well pleasing to Him" (2 Corinthians 5:9). That fits his teaching elsewhere: bondservants were to work "in sincerity of heart, fearing God," doing their work "heartily, as to the Lord and not to men" (Colossians 3:22–23). Serving an earthly master well became a way of serving Christ.

"In all things" describes the whole range of a slave's ordinary duties. It does not mean a believer must obey a command to sin. When human authority demands disobedience to God, "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).

"not answering back,"

"Answering back" means talking back, arguing, or contradicting. Paul is not forbidding honest or respectful words. He is forbidding the defiant, quarrelsome talk that a hard life easily produces. The same word appears in chapter 1 for "those who contradict" sound teaching (1:9). The false teachers spoke against the truth, but believers, even those in the lowest position, were to be marked by a different spirit. Peter told slaves to submit "not only to the good and gentle, but also to the harsh" (1 Peter 2:18). He pointed them to Christ, "who, when He was reviled, did not revile in return; when He suffered, He did not threaten" (1 Peter 2:23). A slave who held his tongue under unfair treatment was following the example of his Lord.

Titus 2:10
NKJV

Titus 2:10 not pilfering, but showing all good fidelity, that they may adorn the doctrine of God our Savior in all things.(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:10 or steal, but must show themselves to be entirely trustworthy and good. Then they will make the teaching about God our Savior attractive in every way. (NLT)

Main Idea

Instead of stealing from their masters, bondservants are to prove completely trustworthy, so that their lives make the teaching about God our Savior look attractive to others. Faithful, honest living is like a beautiful setting that shows off the truth of the gospel.

Verse Explanation

Instead of stealing from their masters, bondservants are to prove completely trustworthy, so that their lives make the teaching about God our Savior look attractive to others. Faithful, honest living is like a beautiful setting that shows off the truth of the gospel.

"not pilfering,"

Bondservants often handled their master's household goods, money, and supplies, which made it easy to keep back small amounts for themselves. Petty theft by slaves was so common that many people simply expected it. The Greek word translated "pilfering" means to set something aside for oneself, taking a portion of what belongs to someone else. The same word describes Ananias, who "kept back part of the proceeds" from the land he sold (Acts 5:2). Paul is not describing open robbery but quiet, small-scale dishonesty that a servant might hope would never be noticed.

"but showing all good fidelity,"

"Fidelity" translates the Greek word usually rendered "faith." Here it means faithfulness: being a person who can be trusted. "Showing" means demonstrating it openly, so that the master can see it over time. "All good fidelity" describes trustworthiness that is complete and genuine, not merely careful when someone is watching. Paul told other Christian bondservants to obey "not with eyeservice, as men-pleasers, but in sincerity of heart, fearing God" (Colossians 3:22). A servant entrusted with small things had daily chances to prove faithful, and Jesus taught that "He who is faithful in what is least is faithful also in much" (Luke 16:10).

"that they may adorn the doctrine of God our Savior in all things."

The Greek word for "adorn" means to arrange something beautifully or decorate it. It was used for putting on jewelry or fine clothing. Paul uses it when he says women should "adorn themselves in modest apparel" (1 Timothy 2:9). An honest, faithful servant does not add anything to God's teaching or make it more true. Instead, his life shows how beautiful it is, the way a setting displays a jewel. Earlier, Paul warned that wrong conduct could cause "the word of God" to be "blasphemed" (2:5). Here he gives the positive side: a believer's life can make God's teaching attractive to the people who watch it.

"The doctrine of God our Savior" is the sound doctrine Paul told Titus to teach (2:1), the healthy teaching that produces godly lives. It comes from God, and it is the message of how He saves. When a slave who once might have stolen becomes completely trustworthy, his master can see what that salvation does in a person. Paul gives this role to people who had almost no standing in their society. A master might never read a word of Scripture, but he could see God's teaching displayed every day in the honesty of a servant. "In all things" extends this to every part of the servant's work, so that nothing in his conduct contradicts the gospel he believes.

Titus 2:11
Titus 2:11
NKJV

Titus 2:11 For the grace of God that brings salvation has appeared to all men,(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:11 For the grace of God has been revealed, bringing salvation to all people. (NLT)

Main Idea

The reason all these groups can live this way is that God's grace, His undeserved kindness that rescues people from sin, has appeared in Jesus Christ and is offered to every kind of person. Salvation is God's gift, not something earned by good behavior.

Verse Explanation

The reason all these groups can live this way is that God's grace, His undeserved kindness that rescues people from sin, has appeared in Jesus Christ and is offered to every kind of person. Salvation is God's gift, not something earned by good behavior.

"For the grace of God"

The word "For" gives the reason behind everything Paul has just told Titus to teach. Older men, older women, younger women, younger men, and bondservants (slaves, a large part of that society) were all called to live in ways that honor God (2:2–10). Paul has just said that even slaves could "adorn the doctrine of God our Savior in all things" (2:10). Now he explains what makes such lives possible. These instructions are not a list of rules for earning God's approval. They rest on what God has already done.

The Greek word for "grace" means favor or kindness freely given. God's grace is His kindness toward people who have not earned it and could never deserve it. Paul says the same thing later in this letter: "But when the kindness and the love of God our Savior toward man appeared, not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy He saved us" (3:4–5). The godly lives Paul describes in this chapter flow out of that grace. They do not replace it.

"that brings salvation"

This phrase translates a single Greek word meaning "saving." God's grace is more than a kind attitude. It actually rescues people from sin, both from the guilt and judgment their sins deserve and from sin's power over their lives. This is why Paul can call God "our Savior" (2:10). Salvation is His gift, as Paul wrote elsewhere: "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works, lest anyone should boast" (Ephesians 2:8–9).

"has appeared"

The Greek verb means to shine forth or become visible, like light breaking into darkness. It is the same verb Paul uses in 3:4 when he says God's kindness and love "appeared." God's grace was always in His heart, but it became visible in history when Jesus Christ came. Paul told Timothy that this grace "was given to us in Christ Jesus before time began, but has now been revealed by the appearing of our Savior Jesus Christ, who has abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel" (2 Timothy 1:9–10). In Jesus's life, death, and resurrection, God's saving kindness stepped into plain view. Paul will soon point to a second appearing still to come (2:13).

"to all men,"

This does not mean that every person is automatically saved. It means God's saving grace has been revealed and offered to every kind of person. Paul has just addressed old and young, men and women, free people and slaves, and grace reaches all of them on the same terms. That included the people of Crete, whom even one of their own poets called "liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons" (1:12). No one's age, background, reputation, or social position puts them beyond God's reach. God "desires all men to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Timothy 2:4). A slave in a Cretan household could receive the same grace as his master, and he could display it as fully in his daily work.

Titus 2:12
Titus 2:12
NKJV

Titus 2:12 teaching us that, denying ungodliness and worldly lusts, we should live soberly, righteously, and godly in the present age,(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:12 And we are instructed to turn from godless living and sinful pleasures. We should live in this evil world with wisdom, righteousness, and devotion to God, (NLT)

Main Idea

This same grace that saves also trains believers, like a teacher, to say no to godless living and sinful desires and to live with self-control, right conduct, and devotion to God in this present world. God's grace does not just forgive sin; it changes the way people live.

Verse Explanation

This same grace that saves also trains believers, like a teacher, to say no to godless living and sinful desires and to live with self-control, right conduct, and devotion to God in this present world. God's grace does not just forgive sin; it changes the way people live.

"teaching us that,"

The grace of God that "has appeared, bringing salvation" (2:11) does not stop at forgiveness. It goes on teaching. The Greek word here means more than passing along information. It describes the training a parent gives a child, including correction and discipline, and it is related to the word Paul uses for Scripture's "instruction in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16). The tense points to continuing work. Grace keeps training believers over a lifetime.

Paul has just told the older women to be "teachers of good things" so they can train the younger women (2:3–4). Now he shows where that teaching comes from: grace itself is the first teacher. What believers teach one another in the church follows what God's grace is already teaching all of them. Paul says "us," placing himself among the learners.

"denying ungodliness and worldly lusts,"

To "deny" means to refuse, to say a firm no. The same word appears at the end of chapter 1, where Paul describes the false teachers on Crete: "They profess to know God, but in works they deny Him" (1:16). Those men said no to God by how they lived. Grace teaches believers to say no to ungodliness instead.

"Ungodliness" means living without reverence for God, as though He did not matter. "Worldly lusts" are the desires that belong to a life organized apart from Him: the cravings for pleasure, possessions, and status that drive people who do not know God. Paul admits believers once lived that way, "serving various lusts and pleasures" (3:3). Grace does not merely excuse that old life. It trains God's people to turn away from it.

"we should live soberly, righteously, and godly"

After the no comes the yes. Many interpreters see these three words as covering a believer's whole life: how he governs himself, how he treats others, and how he relates to God.

"Soberly" means living with self-control and good sense. Paul has used words from this same family for every group in the chapter. Older men are to be "temperate" (2:2), younger women "discreet" (2:5), and younger men "sober-minded" (2:6). The self-control Titus is to urge on each group is exactly what grace teaches all of them.

"Righteously" means dealing rightly with other people: being honest, fair, and faithful in what one owes them. This is the kind of conduct Paul asked of bondservants, who were not to steal from their masters but to show "all good fidelity" (2:10).

"Godly" means living with reverence and devotion toward God. It is the opposite of the "ungodliness" just rejected. Paul began the letter by speaking of "the truth which accords with godliness" (1:1). True teaching and a godly life belong together, and grace produces both. This is not outward good behavior put on from the outside. It is the fruit of a heart God has saved and continues to train.

"in the present age,"

This life is to be lived now, in the ordinary world of homes, work, and neighbors, not postponed until heaven. Believers on Crete were surrounded by people known as liars and lazy gluttons (1:12), and their homes were being upset by false teachers. Grace did not call them out of that world. It taught them to live differently within it, while they wait for Christ's return (2:13).

Titus 2:13
Titus 2:13
NKJV

Titus 2:13 looking for the blessed hope and glorious appearing of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ,(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:13 while we look forward with hope to that wonderful day when the glory of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ, will be revealed. (NLT)

Main Idea

Believers live this way while eagerly waiting for the "blessed hope," the future return of Jesus Christ in glory. This verse plainly calls Jesus "our great God and Savior," affirming that He is fully God.

Verse Explanation

Believers live this way while eagerly waiting for the "blessed hope," the future return of Jesus Christ in glory. This verse plainly calls Jesus "our great God and Savior," affirming that He is fully God.

"looking for"

The Greek word means to wait for something with eager expectation, ready to welcome it when it arrives. Luke uses it of Simeon, who was "waiting for the Consolation of Israel" (Luke 2:25), and of Joseph of Arimathea, who was "waiting for the kingdom of God" (Luke 23:51). This kind of waiting is active, not idle. It continues the thought of verse 12. Grace teaches believers to "live soberly, righteously, and godly in the present age," and they live that way while looking ahead to what is coming. John makes the same connection: "everyone who has this hope in Him purifies himself, just as He is pure" (1 John 3:3). What a Christian expects in the future shapes how he lives now.

"the blessed hope and glorious appearing"

In the Bible, "hope" is not wishful thinking but confident expectation of something God has promised. This hope is "blessed" because it brings great joy to those who belong to Christ. The hope and the appearing are not two separate events. The blessed hope is the glorious appearing: the future return of Jesus Christ. The phrase "glorious appearing" reads literally "the appearing of the glory." When Jesus returns, His full glory will be openly displayed. He will not come again as He did the first time, in humility and weakness. The world will see "the Son of Man coming on the clouds of heaven with power and great glory" (Matthew 24:30).

The word "appearing" ties this verse to verse 11, where "the grace of God that brings salvation has appeared." The two words come from the same root. Paul uses this word for both of Christ's comings. God's grace "has now been revealed by the appearing of our Savior Jesus Christ" (2 Timothy 1:10), and Paul speaks of a crown laid up for "all who have loved His appearing" (2 Timothy 4:8). Believers live between these two appearings. Grace has already appeared in Christ's first coming, bringing salvation and teaching God's people how to live. His glory has not yet appeared, but it will. "The present age" (2:12) is the time between them, and grace trains believers to live godly lives during it.

"of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ,"

The one whose glory will appear is "our great God and Savior," and Paul identifies Him by name: Jesus Christ. These are not two persons, God the Father and then Jesus. Both titles, "God" and "Savior," belong to one person, and that person is Jesus. Paul plainly calls Jesus God, just as Thomas did when he saw the risen Lord and said, "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28). A "Savior" is one who rescues. Jesus is the one who rescues His people from sin and its judgment.

Only three verses earlier, Paul spoke of "the doctrine of God our Savior" (2:10). He uses that same title, God our Savior, for the Father in 1:3 and 3:4. Here he gives it to Jesus. Throughout this short letter he moves back and forth easily between "God our Savior" and "Jesus Christ our Savior" (1:3–4; 3:4–6), because the salvation the Father planned is the salvation the Son accomplished. The Jesus who came in humility and "gave Himself for us" (2:14) is the great God whose return believers eagerly await.

Study Notes

Older English readers may notice that the KJV reads "the great God and our Saviour Jesus Christ," wording that can suggest two persons, the Father and the Son. The NKJV and most modern translations read "our great God and Savior Jesus Christ," naming one person. The Greek favors the NKJV. A single article ("the") governs both titles, which are joined by "and." In ordinary Greek usage, this pattern presents both descriptions as belonging to the same person. The pattern is often called "Granville Sharp's rule," after the English scholar who described it in 1798. Peter uses the identical construction: "the righteousness of our God and Savior Jesus Christ" (2 Peter 1:1).

Other evidence points the same way. Elsewhere in Paul's letters, the "appearing" always refers to Christ, never to the Father (1 Timothy 6:14; 2 Timothy 4:1, 8). Verse 14 continues with "who gave Himself for us," which fits one person. The main argument for two persons is that Paul usually distinguishes the titles, as in "God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ our Savior" (1:4). Most interpreters find the grammatical evidence stronger.

The language also had a sharp edge in Paul's world. Greeks used "epiphany" (appearing) for a god visibly stepping in to help, and for the arrival or accession of rulers. Emperors were publicly hailed as "god" and "savior." Paul may well have meant a deliberate contrast, though this cannot be proven. In his view, the only one truly worthy of those titles is Jesus Christ.

Titus 2:14
NKJV

Titus 2:14 who gave Himself for us, that He might redeem us from every lawless deed and purify for Himself His own special people, zealous for good works.(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:14 He gave his life to free us from every kind of sin, to cleanse us, and to make us his very own people, totally committed to doing good deeds. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jesus gave His own life to set us free from the power and guilt of sin and to make us clean, so that we would be His own treasured people, eager to do good. Good works are not the price of salvation but the natural result of belonging to Christ.

Verse Explanation

Jesus gave His own life to set us free from the power and guilt of sin and to make us clean, so that we would be His own treasured people, eager to do good. Good works are not the price of salvation but the natural result of belonging to Christ.

"who gave Himself for us,"

"Who" points back to "our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (2:13). The One whose glorious return believers await is the same One who came the first time to die. Paul often speaks of Christ's death this way. Jesus "gave Himself for our sins" (Galatians 1:4), and Paul could say personally that He "loved me and gave Himself for me" (Galatians 2:20). The wording stresses that Jesus was not simply a victim. He gave Himself willingly, as He said of His own mission: "the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). "For us" means on our behalf and in our place. What He gave was not a gift outside Himself. He gave His own life.

"that He might redeem us from every lawless deed"

To "redeem" means to set someone free by paying a price. Paul has just spoken to bondservants (2:9–10), slaves who made up a large part of that society, and in his world the word called to mind a slave whose freedom had been bought. Christ paid that price with His own life (1 Timothy 2:6).

He redeems His people "from every lawless deed." "Lawless" describes living as though God's commands do not matter. The wording recalls the psalmist's hope: "And He shall redeem Israel from all his iniquities" (Psalm 130:8). Jesus fulfills that hope. He does not only cancel the guilt of sin. He frees people from its grip so they no longer have to live under it. That is why grace teaches believers to deny "ungodliness and worldly lusts" (2:12). The One who bought their freedom did not free them so they could go on serving the old master.

"and purify for Himself His own special people,"

To "purify" is to cleanse, to make clean what sin has stained. Through Ezekiel, God had promised to cleanse His people from their transgressions, and then "they shall be My people, and I will be their God" (Ezekiel 37:23). Christ cleanses people so that they belong to Him.

"His own special people" translates a Greek word that means a personal possession, something treasured as one's own. The Greek translation of the Old Testament uses this word when God tells Israel at Sinai that they would be "a special treasure to Me above all people" (Exodus 19:5), and again in Deuteronomy 14:2. Peter applies the same title to the church: "His own special people" (1 Peter 2:9). What God once said of Israel, Christ now says of everyone He has redeemed, including Gentile believers on an island like Crete. "For Himself" shows that Jesus is doing what the LORD did for Israel, claiming a people as His own treasure.

"zealous for good works."

"Zealous" means eager and enthusiastic, not merely willing. The people Christ has cleansed are meant to be eager to do good. This is the purpose of His redemption, not the cause of it. Paul will soon say that God saved us "not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy" (3:5). Good works do not purchase belonging to Christ. They grow out of it, just as Paul wrote elsewhere that we are "created in Christ Jesus for good works" (Ephesians 2:10).

This phrase gathers up the whole chapter. The self-control of older men, the teaching of older women, the love younger women show their families, the integrity of younger men, and the faithfulness of bondservants (2:2–10) are all examples of what these good works look like. Titus himself was to be "a pattern of good works" (2:7), modeling the very life Christ died to create in His people. The contrast with the false teachers of chapter 1 is striking. They claimed to know God but were "disqualified for every good work" (1:16). Christ's people, cleansed and claimed by Him, are marked by an eager love for doing good.

Titus 2:15
NKJV

Titus 2:15 Speak these things, exhort, and rebuke with all authority. Let no one despise you.(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 2:15 You must teach these things and encourage the believers to do them. You have the authority to correct them when necessary, so don’t let anyone disregard what you say. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul closes the section by telling Titus to teach these truths boldly, encouraging believers and correcting them when needed, with the full authority God has given him. Because the message is from God, Titus should not let anyone brush him off or look down on him.

Verse Explanation

Paul closes the section by telling Titus to teach these truths boldly, encouraging believers and correcting them when needed, with the full authority God has given him. Because the message is from God, Titus should not let anyone brush him off or look down on him.

"Speak these things,"

Paul began this section with the same command: "But as for you, speak the things which are proper for sound doctrine" (2:1). Now he ends it the same way, so "these things" covers everything in between. That includes his instructions to older men, older women, younger women, younger men, and bondservants (2:2–10). It also includes the message about God's grace, which brings salvation and teaches believers to deny ungodliness, live godly lives, and wait for Christ's return (2:11–14). Titus was not to treat the practical instructions and the gospel as separate subjects. The healthy teaching he spoke was meant to produce healthy, godly lives. In contrast to the false teachers of chapter 1, who were "teaching things which they ought not" (1:11), Titus was to keep on speaking what God had given.

"exhort, and rebuke with all authority."

Paul has already used "exhort" in this chapter for urging and encouraging believers to live as they should (2:6). "Rebuke" goes further. The Greek word means to expose what is wrong and correct it, to show someone their error so they can turn from it. Paul used the same word when he said an elder must be able "to exhort and convict those who contradict" (1:9), and when he told Titus to "rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith" (1:13). The goal of rebuke was restoration, not humiliation. Some believers simply needed encouragement, while others needed to be confronted, and Titus was to do both.

The word translated "authority" means a command or an official order. Paul used the same word for his own ministry, which was "committed to me according to the commandment of God our Savior" (1:3). Titus's authority did not come from his personality, his rank, or his ability to win arguments. It came from God, who had given the message and sent Paul, who in turn left Titus in Crete with instructions (1:5). "All authority" means Titus was not to soften God's word or speak as though it were merely his opinion.

"Let no one despise you."

The Greek word for "despise" means to disregard someone or look down on him, brushing him aside as unimportant. In Crete, where many were "insubordinate" and loved to argue (1:10), some people would try to dismiss Titus and what he said. Paul gave Timothy a similar charge, "Let no one despise your youth" (1 Timothy 4:12), though nothing in this letter says Titus was young.

Titus could not control how others treated him, but he could refuse to back down, and he could leave them no good reason to dismiss him. That is why Paul told him to be "a pattern of good works," with integrity in his teaching and "sound speech that cannot be condemned," so that an opponent would have "nothing evil to say" (2:7–8). Bold teaching and a godly life together would give the message the hearing it deserved.
