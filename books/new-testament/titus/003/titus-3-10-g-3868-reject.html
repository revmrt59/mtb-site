Summary:

REJECT - ( paraiteomai , Strong’s [3868]) Summary: To beg off, to excuse oneself from, or to shun. It describes a deliberate and decisive refusal to have further association or engagement with someone or something, signifying a formal turning away for the sake of spiritual safety.

Full Study:

1. Greek Roots / Derivation A compound verb derived from the preposition para, meaning "from" or "beside," and aiteo, meaning "to ask," "to beg," or "to request." In the middle voice (as used in Titus 3:10), it carries the sense of "asking for oneself" that a matter or person be set aside, effectively saying, "count me out" or "excuse me from this."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition To deprecate, to decline, to refuse, or to avoid. It signifies a polite but firm refusal of an invitation or, in a disciplinary context, a decisive shunning of a persistent troublemaker. In the New Testament, it is used for "excusing" oneself from a banquet, "refusing" to hear a message, or "avoiding" certain types of people and arguments.

3. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures - The Declined Invitation: In secular Greek, it was the standard word for someone who sent an "excuse" for why they could not attend a social gathering or fulfill a duty. - The Legal Appeal: It pictured a defendant "begging off" a certain judge or requesting to be excused from a specific legal obligation. - The Shunning of the Sick: It was used to describe staying "beside" or "away from" a contaminated area or a person with a contagious disease to prevent infection.

4. Theological Implications Rejection is a necessary part of spiritual discernment; a believer cannot "maintain good works" while simultaneously entertaining "foolish disputes." It is a protective act for the church, recognizing that a "divisive" spirit is contagious and must be quarantined if it refuses to be cured by "admonition." The act of "rejecting" is not rooted in personal animosity but in a commitment to the "faithful saying" of the Gospel and the peace of the body.

5. Contextual Nuance in Titus 3:10 In Titus 3:10, Paul commands Titus to "reject (paraitou)" a divisive man after two attempts at correction. The present imperative suggests a settled and ongoing policy of refusal: once the process of admonition has failed, Titus is to "have nothing more to do with" that person's divisive activities. This "rejection" is the logical conclusion of the "avoidance" mentioned in verse 9; if one must avoid the topics, they must eventually avoid the person who insists on those topics.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV) - Luke 14:18: “But they all with one accord began to make excuses (paraiteisthai)...” - 1 Timothy 4:7: “But reject (paraitou) profane and old wives’ fables, and exercise yourself toward godliness.” - 1 Timothy 5:11: “But the younger widows refuse (paraitou)...” - 2 Timothy 2:23: “But avoid (paraitou) foolish and ignorant disputes, knowing that they generate strife.” - Hebrews 12:25: “See that you do not refuse (paraitesthe) Him who speaks...”

7. Other Relevant Insights This word highlights that some people are "beyond the reach" of verbal instruction because they have chosen their own "hairesis" (opinion). It suggests that a leader’s time is a stewardship; to continue arguing with a "warped" person is to waste the time that belongs to the "profitable" care of the flock. The goal of this "rejection" is also for the benefit of the offender, as being "left alone" by the church may lead them to realize their "self-condemned" state (verse 11).
