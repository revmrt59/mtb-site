Summary:

SELF-CONDEMNED - ( autokatakritos , Strong’s [843]) Summary: Judging oneself or condemned by one's own actions. It describes a person whose persistent behavior and rejection of correction serve as the very evidence and verdict of their own guilt.

Full Study:

1. Greek Roots / Derivation A compound word derived from autos, meaning "self," kata, meaning "against" or "down," and krino, meaning "to judge" or "to separate." It literally means "judged by oneself" or "down-judged by one's own hand," suggesting that no external judge is needed to prove the person's error because their own conduct provides the testimony.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition Self-condemned, or condemned by one's own conscience and actions. In the New Testament, it signifies a person whose refusal to heed repeated warnings and whose commitment to divisive behavior leaves them without excuse. It denotes a state where the individual's "choice" (hairesis) of error is so blatant that the verdict of guilt is obvious to everyone, including the offender.

3. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures - The Witness and Judge: In secular Greek thought, it pictured a person who stands in a courtroom and provides the very testimony that leads to their own conviction. - The Signature: It was used for a document that carried the author's own seal or signature, making them personally responsible for everything written within it. - The Inescapable Net: It described a person who has "woven their own net" and become entangled in it, where the consequences of their actions are the direct result of their own design.

4. Theological Implications Self-condemnation is the result of a "warped" nature that has been given every opportunity to return to the truth. It highlights the fairness of God's discipline; the person is not being rejected based on a whim, but based on a "self-authored" record of rebellion. This condition is the ultimate end of "foolish disputes" and "contentions"-the mind becomes so darkened that it effectively passes judgment on itself by continuing in sin.

5. Contextual Nuance in Titus 3:11 In Titus 3:11, Paul concludes his instruction on the divisive man by noting he is "being self-condemned (autokatakritos)." This provides the justification for the "rejection" commanded in verse 10; Titus does not need to feel guilty for removing the man, because the man has already "judged himself" unworthy of the fellowship. The presence of this word emphasizes that the "first and second admonition" were the final tests that revealed the man’s heart, making the subsequent discipline a simple recognition of his own self-verdict.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV) - Job 15:6 (LXX): “Your own mouth condemns (elenchei-related concept) you, and not I; yes, your own lips testify against you.” - Matthew 12:37 (Related Concept): “For by your words you will be justified, and by your words you will be condemned (katadikazo).” - Luke 19:22: “And he said to him, ‘Out of your own mouth I will judge (krino) you, you wicked servant...’” - Romans 2:1: “...for in whatever you judge another you condemn (katakrino) yourself; for you who judge practice the same things.” - 1 John 3:20 (Related Concept): “For if our heart condemns (kataginosko) us, God is greater than our heart...”

7. Other Relevant Insights This word highlights that "heresy" is a suicidal act for the soul; the divisive person destroys their own standing in the church. It suggests that the goal of the "admonition" is to prevent this self-condemnation by bringing the person to repentance. By identifying the man as autokatakritos, Paul protects the church leaders from accusations of being "judgmental," as the man's own "sinning" has already settled the case.
