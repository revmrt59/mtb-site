Summary:

LACK NOTHING - ( leipo , Strong’s [3007]) Summary: To be wanting, to fall short, or to be destitute. In this context, it describes the absence of any necessary provision, ensuring that a person is fully equipped and sustained so their progress is not hindered by physical need.

Full Study:

1. Greek Roots / Derivation Derived from the primary verb leipo, which signifies leaving behind, quitting, or forsaking. It carries the core idea of a "gap" or a "deficiency" where something that should be present is missing.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition To leave, to fail, or to be in want. In the passive or intransitive sense (as used here), it means "to be lacking" or "to be destitute." In the New Testament, it is used to describe a lack of resources, a lack of character, or a failure to meet a specific requirement or standard.

3. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures - The Deserted Post: In secular Greek, it was used for a soldier who "leaves" his station, creating a dangerous "gap" or "lack" in the defensive line. - The Unfinished Building: It pictured a construction project that had "stalled" because it "lacked" the necessary funds or materials to continue. - The Empty Hand: It described a traveler who reaches into their bag and finds "nothing," representing the frustration and danger of being unequipped for a journey.

4. Theological Implications God's grace is intended to ensure that the believer "lacks nothing" in their spiritual standing or their equipment for "good works" (2 Timothy 3:17). However, the "lack" of physical provision for those in ministry is a responsibility given to the church to solve through generosity. Ensuring that others "lack nothing" is a practical way for the church to reflect the "generosity" and "abundant grace" of God mentioned in verses 5–7.

5. Contextual Nuance in Titus 3:13 In the NKJV of Titus 3:13, the command is to send Zenas and Apollos so that "nothing be wanting (leipe) to them." This is the negative goal of the "haste" and "sending forward" mentioned earlier in the verse. Paul is instructing the church to perform a "gap analysis" on the needs of these ministers and to fill every "lack" so that their work for Christ can be performed without distraction or distress.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV) - Luke 18:22: “...‘You still lack (leipei) one thing. Sell all that you have...’” - Titus 1:5: “For this reason I left you in Crete, that you should set in order the things that are wanting (leiponta-lacking),” - James 1:4: “But let patience have its perfect work, that you may be perfect and complete, lacking (leipomenoi) nothing.” - James 1:5: “If any of you lacks (leipetai) wisdom, let him ask of God...” - James 2:15: “If a brother or sister is naked and destitute (leipomenoi-lacking/wanting) of daily food,”

7. Other Relevant Insights This word highlights that "good works" (verse 14) are often the "provision" that fills the "lack" of others. It suggests that a church is "maintaining" its mission only when it is "diligent" (verse 12) to ensure its laborers are not in want. By preventing Zenas and Apollos from "lacking," Titus proves that the "profitable" work of the Gospel (verse 8) is being successfully lived out in Crete.
