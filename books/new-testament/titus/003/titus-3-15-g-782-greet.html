Summary:

GREET - ( aspazomai , Strong’s [782]) Summary: To salute, welcome, or embrace. It describes an outward expression of affection and unity, signifying a heartfelt acknowledgement of a shared bond in Christ and a desire for the well-being of others.

Full Study:

1. Greek Roots / Derivation Derived from the alpha intensive and spao, meaning "to draw" or "to pull." It literally means "to draw to oneself," suggesting a close and personal interaction where two people are brought together in a physical or verbal embrace.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition To salute, to welcome, to pay respects, or to embrace. In the New Testament, it is the standard term for a Christian greeting, used both for personal encounters and for the closing salutations in apostolic letters. It denotes a formal but warm acknowledgement that reinforces the "brotherhood" and "love" within the body of Christ.

3. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures - The Open Arms: In secular Greek, it was used for the "welcome" given to a returning traveler or a guest, where the host would "draw them in" to the safety and hospitality of the home. - The Public Salute: It pictured a citizen "hailing" an official or a friend in the marketplace, recognizing their presence and status with a specific word or gesture of honor. - The Final Farewell: It was also used for the "parting embrace," where friends would "cling" to each other one last time before a long separation, representing a bond that remains even when they are apart.

4. Theological Implications A Christian "greeting" is a manifestation of the "kindness and love of God" (verse 4) working through the believers. It is not a mere social formality; it is a spiritual act that acknowledges the "hope of eternal life" (verse 7) shared by both the sender and the receiver. It identifies that the church is a "family" of those who "love us in the faith," where every member is valued and welcomed.

5. Contextual Nuance in Titus 3:15 In the NKJV of Titus 3:15, Paul uses the word twice: "All who are with me greet (aspazontai) you. Greet (aspasai) those who love us in the faith." The first use is a present indicative, showing the ongoing affection of the team with Paul toward Titus. The second is an aorist imperative, commanding Titus to personally and specifically "welcome" those in Crete who remain faithful to the "faithful saying" of the Gospel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV) - Matthew 10:12: “And when you go into a household, greet (aspasasthe) it.” - Acts 20:1: “...Paul called the disciples to himself, embraced (aspasamenos) them, and departed...” - Romans 16:16: “Greet (aspasasthe) one another with a holy kiss...” - 1 Corinthians 16:20: “All the brethren greet (aspazontai) you.” - Philippians 4:21: “Greet (aspasasthe) every saint in Christ Jesus...” - Hebrews 11:13: “...but having seen them afar off were assured of them, embraced (aspasamenoi-welcomed/greeted) them...”

7. Other Relevant Insights This word highlights that "good works" (verse 14) often begin with a "good greeting"; the way we welcome others sets the tone for our service. It suggests that a "divisive" person (verse 10) is one who has forfeited the right to this "holy greeting" by rejecting the unity of the faith. By "greeting" those who love in the faith, Titus maintains the "profitable" bonds of the church against the "useless" disputes of the false teachers.
