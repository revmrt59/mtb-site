Summary:

SPEAK EVIL - ( blasphemeo , Strong’s [G989]) Summary: To defame, revile, or speak reproachfully of someone. It refers to injurious speech that is intended to damage a person's reputation or character, often manifested as slander or railing.

Full Study:

1. Greek Roots / Derivation Derived from the verb blapto, meaning "to injure" or "to hinder," and pheme, meaning "speech" or "reputation." It literally describes "injurious speech" that is intended to harm the name or standing of another person.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition To revile, defame, or rail at individuals. While often used in a religious sense regarding God (blasphemy), in social and civic contexts it refers to slander, calumny, or the act of speaking lightly of those who should be honored. It denotes a verbal assault that seeks to degrade the worth or character of the person being discussed.

3. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures - Social Defamation: In secular Greek, it was used to describe the act of slandering a person’s reputation in the public square or in a court of law. - The Verbal Weapon: It pictured speech as something that "strikes" or "hinders" the progress and honor of another person's life through false or harsh accusations. - Mockery: It was used for the biting sarcasm or mocking language directed toward those in positions of authority or those holding opposing viewpoints.

4. Theological Implications The word highlights the weight of human speech and its power to dishonor the image of God in others. Scripture warns that those who "speak evil" are acting in opposition to the law of love and the character of God. It identifies a heart that is not yet fully under the control of the Spirit, as the tongue reflects the internal state and maturity of the believer.

5. Contextual Nuance in Titus 3:2 In Titus 3:2, believers are explicitly commanded "to speak evil of no one." This command follows the instruction to be subject to authorities, implying that even if rulers are unjust or pagan, the believer is prohibited from using reviling language against them. The prohibition is universal, encompassing both the household of faith and the unbelieving world.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV) - Romans 3:8: “And why not say, ‘Let us do evil that good may come’?-as we are slanderously reported (blasphemeo) and as some affirm that we say.” - 1 Timothy 6:4: “...he is proud, knowing nothing... from which come envy, strife, reviling (blasphemeo), evil suspicions...” - Titus 2:5: “...to be discreet, chaste, homemakers... that the word of God may not be blasphemed (blasphemeo).” - 1 Peter 4:4: “...they think it strange that you do not run with them... speaking evil (blasphemeo) of you.”

7. Other Relevant Insights This word serves as a test of the reality of one's transformation and practical holiness. A life characterized by gentle and peaceable speech provides a contrast to the contentious and reviling speech patterns common in a rebellious culture. Refusing to "speak evil" is a primary way to maintain a clear testimony and avoid giving others a reason to reproach the faith.
