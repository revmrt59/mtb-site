Summary:

MALICE - ( kakia , Strong’s [2549]) Summary: The internal disposition of ill-will or viciousness toward others. It describes a heart that harbors badness and seeks to inflict harm or trouble, representing a fundamental lack of moral goodness.

Full Study:

1. Greek Roots / Derivation Derived from the adjective kakos, meaning "bad," "evil," or "worthless." It denotes the quality of being evil in character or a general state of depravity. While kakos describes the nature of a thing, kakia focuses on the state or the manifestation of that badness in the soul.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition Malice, ill-will, malignity, or wickedness. It refers to the desire to injure others or a settled state of mind that takes pleasure in the misfortune of neighbors. In certain contexts, it can also refer to "trouble" or "calamity," signifying the evil that befalls a person.

3. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures - The Vicious Disposition: In secular Greek literature, it was the standard word for moral badness and was considered the opposite of arete (virtue). - The Root of Harm: It pictured a person whose character was so warped that they naturally produced harmful actions toward their community. - The Internal Poison: It described a "bitterness of spirit" that poisoned relationships and destroyed social harmony through a refusal to be kind or forgiving.

4. Theological Implications Malice is listed among the characteristic sins of the unregenerate life that must be "put away" or "laid aside" by the believer. It is the antithesis of the love and kindness that should characterize those who have experienced the mercy of God. The presence of kakia indicates a heart that is still operating under the principles of the fallen nature rather than the influence of the Holy Spirit.

5. Contextual Nuance in Titus 3:3 In Titus 3:3, Paul describes the former life of believers as "living in malice (kakia) and envy." This identifies a lifestyle where relationships were defined by a desire for others to suffer or a resentment of their success. Acknowledging this past state of viciousness highlights the magnitude of the transformation brought about by the "kindness and love of God our Savior."

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV) - Matthew 6:34: “...Sufficient for the day is its own trouble (kakia).” - Acts 8:22: “Repent therefore of this your wickedness (kakia), and pray God...” - 1 Corinthians 5:8: “...not with the leaven of malice (kakia) and wickedness, but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth.” - Ephesians 4:31: “Let all bitterness, wrath, anger... be put away from you, with all malice (kakia).” - 1 Peter 2:1: “Therefore, laying aside all malice (kakia), all deceit, hypocrisy, envy...”

7. Other Relevant Insights This word highlights that the problem of sin is not just about outward actions, but about the inward "badness" of the heart's intentions. It suggests that a life lived apart from grace is inevitably a life of conflict, as malice prevents genuine fellowship and peace. The removal of malice is essential for spiritual growth, as it clears the way for the "implanted word" to take root and produce fruit.
