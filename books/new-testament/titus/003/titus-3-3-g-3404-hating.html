Summary:

HATING - ( misountes / miseo , Strong’s [3404])

To detest, abhor, or feel a settled animosity toward another. In Titus 3:3, it describes the active, reciprocal malice and social friction that characterize unregenerate life, where individuals are consumed by mutual ill-will and enmity.

Full Study:

  1. Greek Roots / Derivation

Derived from the primary verb miseo, which is the standard term for "to hate" throughout the New Testament. It is the direct opposite of the verbs agapao (to love with commitment) and phileo (to love with affection). The root suggests a deep-seated aversion or a willful choice to reject and oppose someone or something.

  1. Meaning / Lexical Definition

To hate, to pursue with hatred, or to detest. Lexically, it signifies a feeling of strong dislike or enmity. In some biblical contexts, it can also carry a comparative sense, meaning "to love less" (as in Matthew 10:37 compared with Luke 14:26), but in Titus 3:3, the context of "malice and envy" points to the literal sense of active, malicious hatred.

  1. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures

    • The State of Enmity: In secular Greek, it described the condition of being an "enemy" (echthros), representing a relationship defined by conflict, avoidance, and the desire for the other’s harm.

    • The Rejection of the Good: It was often used to describe the "hatred" of a specific law, city-state, or moral principle, where the person actively worked against the influence of that entity.

    • The Social Fracture: It pictured a community where the "glue" of social harmony had dissolved, leaving behind a collection of individuals who viewed one another with suspicion and contempt.

  2. Theological Implications

Theologically, miseo is presented as a fundamental characteristic of the fallen world and the "kingdom of darkness." It is the natural response of the sinful heart to the light of God (John 3:20) and to those who represent Him. In the unregenerate state, hatred is not merely an occasional emotion but a prevailing social atmosphere. It is the antithesis of the "new commandment" given by Christ, and its presence in the heart is a marker of spiritual death (1 John 3:14–15).

  1. Contextual Nuance in Titus 3:3

In the NKJV of Titus 3:3, Paul describes the former life as "living in malice and envy, hateful and hating (misountes) one another."

  • Reciprocal Malice: The word is used as a present participle, indicating an ongoing, habitual state of mind. It is "one another" (allelous), showing that the hatred was mutual and pervasive throughout their social interactions.

  • The Climax of Depravity: Paul places "hating" at the end of a list that begins with "foolish" and "disobedient." It shows that intellectual and moral rebellion inevitably leads to the breakdown of human relationships.

  • The Evidence of Slavery: This hatred is shown to be the result of being "slaves to various lusts and pleasures." When individuals live only for their own satisfaction, they naturally come to detest anyone who stands in the way of their desires.

  1. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

    • Matthew 5:44: “...bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate (misousin) you...”

    • John 3:20: “For everyone practicing evil hates (misei) the light...”

    • John 15:18: “If the world hates (misei) you, you know that it hated (memiseken) Me before it hated you.”

    • 1 John 2:9: “He who says he is in the light, and hates (mison) his brother, is in darkness...”

    • 1 John 3:15: “Whoever hates (mison) his brother is a murderer...”

  2. Other Relevant Insights

This word highlights that the transformation brought by the Gospel is essentially a transition from "hatred" to "love."

It suggests that a society without Christ will eventually become a society of mutual detestation, as individual "lusts and pleasures" (v. 3) collide.

By using misountes, Paul provides a "profitable" (Titus 3:8) reminder of the social misery that the believers have escaped through the "kindness and love of God" (v. 4).
