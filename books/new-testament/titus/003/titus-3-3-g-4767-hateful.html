Summary:

HATEFUL - ( stygetos , Strong’s [4767])

To be odious, detestable, or worthy of hatred. In Titus 3:3, it describes the repulsive moral state of the unregenerate person, depicting a character that is so corrupted by sin that it is loathsome both to God and to other men.

Full Study:

  1. Greek Roots / Derivation

Derived from the verb stygeo, which means "to hate" or "to abhor." It is related to the root used in Greek mythology for the River Styx—the cold, dark river of the underworld that was considered "hateful." The word carries a sense of shivering or shrinking back from something that is disgusting or repulsive.

  1. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Odious, detestable, or loathsome. Lexically, it refers to a person or thing that is deserving of hatred. While many Greek words for "hate" focus on the active emotion of the hater, stygetos focuses on the quality of the object being hated. It describes someone who has become so offensive in their behavior and nature that they are inherently repulsive.

  1. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures

    • The Repulsive Character: In secular Greek literature, it was used to describe people who were morally bankrupt or physically disgusting to the point that others could not bear their presence.

    • The Shadow of Styx: Because of its linguistic connection to the River Styx, it pictured a character that was "deathly" and "dark," lacking the warmth of human kindness and thus becoming loathsome to the society around them.

    • The Shudder of Disgust: It conveyed the physical reaction of recoiling from something foul, representing the natural human response to the "hateful" nature of an unregenerate heart.

  2. Theological Implications

Theologically, stygetos serves as a stark indictment of the human condition apart from the grace of God. It shows that sin does not just make a person "wrong" or "mistaken"; it makes them "hateful." It underscores the total depravity of the heart that serves "various lusts and pleasures." This term establishes the necessity of the "kindness and love of God" (verse 4), because the condition it describes is so repulsive that only divine grace could choose to look upon it with favor.

  1. Contextual Nuance in Titus 3:3

In the NKJV of Titus 3:3, Paul lists "hateful" as one of the final characteristics of the believers' former lives.

  • Passive vs. Active: Paul pairs "hateful" (stygetos—being worthy of hate) with "hating one another" (misountes—the active emotion of hate). This suggests a vicious cycle: people whose character is repulsive, living in a society where everyone is actively hating each other.

  • The Universal "We": Paul includes himself ("For we ourselves were also once..."), emphasizing that no one is exempt from this loathsome state apart from Christ.

  • The Contrast of Grace: The "hatefulness" of man provides the dark background against which the "kindness" (chrestotes) of God shines more brightly in the subsequent verses.

  1. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

    • Stygetos is a hapax legomenon, meaning it occurs only once in the entire New Testament (Titus 3:3). Its unique appearance emphasizes the specific, intense loathsomeness that Paul wants to highlight regarding the pre-Christian state.

  2. Other Relevant Insights

This word highlights that sin is fundamentally "ugly." It distorts the image of God into something that others find detestable.

It suggests that a "hateful" character is the inevitable result of living for "lusts and pleasures."

By using stygetos, Paul provides a "profitable" (Titus 3:8) reminder of the depth from which the believer has been rescued, fostering humility and a greater appreciation for the "washing of regeneration."
