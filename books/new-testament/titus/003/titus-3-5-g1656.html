Summary:

MERCY - ( eleos , Strong’s [1656]) Summary: Compassion or pity directed toward the miserable and afflicted, joined with a desire to relieve their suffering. It is the active expression of God’s goodness that addresses the wretched state of the sinner.

Full Study:

1. Greek Roots / Derivation Derived from the verb eleeo, meaning "to have pity" or "to be compassionate." In the Septuagint (LXX), it is frequently used to translate the Hebrew word chesed, which refers to God's steadfast, covenant-keeping love and mercy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition Kindness or good will toward the miserable and the afflicted, joined with a desire to help them. It signifies the outward manifestation of pity; it assumes need on the part of him who receives it and resources on the part of him who shows it. It is the specific aspect of God's love that reaches out to those who are in a state of helplessness and ruin.

3. Usage in Greek Culture & Word Pictures - The Courtroom Appeal: In secular Greek contexts, it was used for the pity felt by a judge for a defendant who was in a miserable condition, though often it was only shown if the suffering was deemed "undeserved." - The Healing Touch: It pictured the response of a physician to a patient in deep pain, moving beyond a diagnosis to the application of a remedy. - The Outstretched Hand: It identifies a heart that is "moved to the bowels" (a Greek idiom for deep emotion) by the sight of another's distress, resulting in an act of rescue.

4. Theological Implications Mercy is the ground upon which God acts to save humanity; because He is "rich in mercy," He intervenes in the death and darkness of sin. It is often distinguished from grace (charis): grace is God’s favor to the undeserving, while mercy is His compassion to the miserable. Grace deals with the guilt of sin, while mercy deals with the misery and consequences of sin.

5. Contextual Nuance in Titus 3:5 In Titus 3:5, mercy is contrasted with "works of righteousness which we have done." The text emphasizes that salvation is not a reward for human effort but is "according to His mercy" (kata to autou eleos). It establishes that the motive for God's saving act is found entirely within His own compassionate nature, not in the merit of the one being saved.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV) - Matthew 9:13: “...‘I desire mercy (eleos) and not sacrifice.’ For I did not come to call the righteous...” - Ephesians 2:4: “But God, who is rich in mercy (eleos), because of His great love with which He loved us,” - 1 Timothy 1:2: “To Timothy, a true son in the faith: Grace, mercy (eleos), and peace from God our Father...” - Hebrews 4:16: “Let us therefore come boldly to the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy (eleos)...” - James 2:13: “For judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy. Mercy (eleos) triumphs over judgment.”

7. Other Relevant Insights This word highlights the depth of the human problem; humanity is not just "wrong," but is in a state of misery and ruin from which only divine mercy can provide an exit. It suggests that the believer’s life should be characterized by a similar mercy toward others, having been the recipient of such great compassion from God. Properly understanding eleos prevents legalism, as it reminds the believer that their standing before God is based on His pity rather than their own performance.
