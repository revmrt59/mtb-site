Titus 3

Verse-by-Verse
Titus 3:1
Titus 3:1
NKJV

Titus 3:1 Remind them to be subject to rulers and authorities, to obey, to be ready for every good work,(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 3:1 Remind the believers to submit to the government and its officers. They should be obedient, always ready to do what is good. (NLT)

Main Idea

Believers are to respect and obey the governing authorities and to be ready to do whatever is good. Paul tells Titus to keep reminding the Cretan Christians of this, because a life of good conduct in society is part of what it means to follow Christ.

Verse Explanation

Believers are to respect and obey the governing authorities and to be ready to do whatever is good. Paul tells Titus to keep reminding the Cretan Christians of this, because a life of good conduct in society is part of what it means to follow Christ.

"Remind them"

Paul wrote this letter to Titus, a trusted coworker and a Gentile believer (Galatians 2:3) whom he called "my partner and fellow worker" (2 Corinthians 8:23). Paul had left Titus on the island of Crete to "set in order the things that are lacking" in the young churches and to appoint elders in every city (1:5). Chapter 2 gave instructions for older men, older women, young women, young men, and servants. Now Paul turns to how all the believers are to live in the wider world. The word "remind" shows that this was not new teaching. The Cretan Christians had already heard it, but they needed to keep hearing it, and the Greek verb carries the sense of reminding them again and again.

"to be subject to rulers and authorities,"

"Rulers and authorities" refers to the people who held governing power. Crete was part of the Roman Empire, so these would include Roman officials as well as local leaders. To "be subject" means to willingly place oneself under someone's authority. Paul uses the same word for servants and their masters (2:9). He taught the same thing in Romans: "Let every soul be subject to the governing authorities. For there is no authority except from God, and the authorities that exist are appointed by God" (Romans 13:1). Christians respect earthly rulers because God Himself has given them their place.

"to obey,"

This is a separate word from "be subject," and it moves from attitude to action. Believers are not only to respect authority in principle but to actually do what is required of them. This obedience has a limit, though. When the Jewish council ordered the apostles to stop preaching about Jesus, Peter and the others answered, "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29), using the same Greek word for "obey." Christians obey their rulers as part of obeying God, so they are never bound to obey a command to sin.

"to be ready for every good work,"

Paul wants more than law-abiding citizens. "Ready" describes people who are prepared and willing, eager to do good whenever an opportunity comes, not reluctant to help. In this setting it points especially to doing good in public life, being the kind of neighbors and citizens whose conduct benefits the community around them. Peter wrote the same way about submitting to rulers: "For this is the will of God, that by doing good you may put to silence the ignorance of foolish men" (1 Peter 2:15).

The phrase "every good work" appears earlier in the letter in a very different light. The false teachers troubling Crete claimed to know God but denied Him by their actions, being "disqualified for every good work" (1:16). True believers are to be just the opposite, ready for every good work. That is the result of what Christ came to do, since He gave Himself to "purify for Himself His own special people, zealous for good works" (2:14).

Titus 3:2
Titus 3:2
NKJV

Titus 3:2 to speak evil of no one, to be peaceable, gentle, showing all humility to all men.(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 3:2 They must not slander anyone and must avoid quarreling. Instead, they should be gentle and show true humility to everyone. (NLT)

Main Idea

Christians should not speak badly of anyone but should be peaceful, gentle, and humble toward everyone, not just toward fellow believers. This kind of behavior stands in sharp contrast to the harsh and quarrelsome culture around them.

Verse Explanation

Christians should not speak badly of anyone but should be peaceful, gentle, and humble toward everyone, not just toward fellow believers. This kind of behavior stands in sharp contrast to the harsh and quarrelsome culture around them.

"to speak evil of no one,"

Paul is still listing what Titus is to remind the believers on Crete (3:1). The Greek word translated "speak evil" means to slander, insult, or speak abusively about someone. It is the same word Paul used a few verses earlier when he urged Christian wives to live in a way "that the word of God may not be blasphemed" (2:5). Believers who refuse to slander others take away an outsider's reason to slander the gospel. Paul also wanted the church's conduct to leave an opponent "ashamed, having nothing evil to say of you" (2:8).

"No one" leaves no exceptions. It includes the rulers and authorities Paul has just mentioned (3:1), the false teachers troubling the churches, and the unbelieving neighbors who may have treated Christians badly. This would not have come naturally on Crete. Paul had already quoted a Cretan poet who called his own people "liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons" (1:12), and he described many in the area as "insubordinate, both idle talkers and deceivers" (1:10). In a culture known for lying and rough talk, Christians were to be known for guarding their tongues.

"to be peaceable, gentle,"

"Peaceable" translates a Greek word that literally means "not a fighter." It describes someone who does not pick quarrels or turn every disagreement into a battle. Paul requires the same quality of church leaders, who must be "not quarrelsome" (1 Timothy 3:3). Here he asks it of every believer.

"Gentle" describes a person who is reasonable and willing to yield, who does not insist on every right or demand the harshest response. Paul used the same word when he told the Philippians, "Let your gentleness be known to all men" (Philippians 4:5). Such a person can disagree without being harsh. Among quarrelsome people, a Christian who would not fight back and did not demand his own way would stand out clearly.

"showing all humility to all men."

The Greek word translated "humility" is often rendered "meekness." It describes strength held under control, a gentle spirit that does not lash out or look down on others. Jesus described Himself this way: "I am gentle and lowly in heart" (Matthew 11:29). Paul once appealed to the Corinthians "by the meekness and gentleness of Christ" (2 Corinthians 10:1), joining the same two qualities found in this verse. When believers treat others this way, they reflect the character of their Lord.

Paul doubles the emphasis with "all humility to all men." Believers are to show the full measure of this gentle spirit, and to show it to everyone, not only fellow Christians or people who treat them kindly. That includes outsiders, opponents, and the hard and dishonest people of Crete.

Titus 3:3
Titus 3:3
NKJV

Titus 3:3 For we ourselves were also once foolish, disobedient, deceived, serving various lusts and pleasures, living in malice and envy, hateful and hating one another.(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 3:3 Once we, too, were foolish and disobedient. We were misled and became slaves to many lusts and pleasures. Our lives were full of evil and envy, and we hated each other. (NLT)

Main Idea

Before coming to Christ, believers themselves were foolish, rebellious, misled, controlled by their own desires, and full of hatred and envy toward others. Remembering what we once were should make us humble and patient with people who are still living that way.

Verse Explanation

Before coming to Christ, believers themselves were foolish, rebellious, misled, controlled by their own desires, and full of hatred and envy toward others. Remembering what we once were should make us humble and patient with people who are still living that way.

"For we ourselves were also once foolish,"

"For" gives the reason behind the instructions just before this verse. Paul had told Titus to remind the believers "to speak evil of no one, to be peaceable, gentle, showing all humility to all men" (3:2). That was not easy in the harsh, quarrelsome culture of Crete. Paul now explains why believers should treat even difficult people gently: they themselves were once no better. "We ourselves" includes Paul. He described his own past this way: "I was formerly a blasphemer, a persecutor, and an insolent man" (1 Timothy 1:13). Christians have no reason to look down on unbelievers, because they remember being in the same condition.

The Greek word for "foolish" means lacking understanding. It is the word Paul used when he wrote, "O foolish Galatians!" (Galatians 3:1). It does not describe low intelligence. It describes people who do not understand God or His ways. Paul says elsewhere that unbelievers have "their understanding darkened, being alienated from the life of God" (Ephesians 4:18).

"disobedient,"

Believers are now to "obey" (3:1), but once they were "disobedient," unwilling to submit to God or to rightful authority. Paul used the same Greek word in chapter 1 for the false teachers troubling the Cretan churches: they "profess to know God, but in works they deny Him, being abominable, disobedient, and disqualified for every good work" (1:16). The people Titus must confront are living the way the believers themselves once lived. That is why believers can stand firm against error without feeling superior to those caught in it.

"deceived,"

The Greek word means led astray or made to wander off the right path. The form is passive. The deceived person is misled by something outside himself, and he is also willing to follow it. People without Christ are wrong about God, about themselves, and about what will make them happy, and they do not know they are wrong.

"serving various lusts and pleasures,"

"Serving" means serving as a slave. Their desires were not just things they enjoyed. They were masters giving orders. Paul says the same in Romans, reminding believers that "you were slaves of sin" (Romans 6:17). "Various" shows that these masters were many and different: cravings of the body, greed, ambition, the pursuit of whatever felt good at the moment. In the previous chapter Paul said God's grace teaches believers "denying ungodliness and worldly lusts" to "live soberly, righteously, and godly in the present age" (2:12). The desires that once ruled them are now to be refused.

"living in malice and envy,"

"Living" describes a settled way of life, not an occasional slip. "Malice" is ill will, a desire to see others hurt. "Envy" is resentment over what someone else has. Both poison relationships, because both treat other people as rivals rather than neighbors.

"hateful and hating one another."

"Hateful" means detestable, the kind of person others find hard to love. "Hating one another" shows that the hostility ran both ways. People living for themselves end up resenting and resisting each other. This is the opposite of being "peaceable, gentle, showing all humility to all men" (3:2). The way believers once lived led to hostility on every side.

Paul does not describe this former life to shame believers. He describes it so they will remember what they were. Believers did not become different because they were better than their neighbors. In the very next verse Paul shows that the change came from God's kindness and love. Remembering this makes it possible to be gentle with those who still live the way believers once did.

Titus 3:4
Titus 3:4
NKJV

Titus 3:4 But when the kindness and the love of God our Savior toward man appeared,(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 3:4 But — When God our Savior revealed his kindness and love, (NLT)

Main Idea

The turning point in every believer's story is not something they did but something God did: His kindness and love for people appeared in the coming of Jesus. God's love for humanity took visible form in His Son, our Savior.

Verse Explanation

The turning point in every believer's story is not something they did but something God did: His kindness and love for people appeared in the coming of Jesus. God's love for humanity took visible form in His Son, our Savior.

"But when"

These two words mark the turning point. Paul has just described what believers were before Christ: "foolish, disobedient, deceived, serving various lusts and pleasures, living in malice and envy, hateful and hating one another" (3:3). Nothing in that list suggests people slowly getting better on their own. The change did not begin with them. It began when God acted. The sentence that starts here reaches its main point in the next verse: "He saved us" (3:5). Everything between "But when" and "He saved us" is about what God did, not what people did.

"the kindness and the love of God our Savior toward man"

The Greek word for "kindness" describes goodness that shows itself in generous action, a kindness that does good to others. The phrase "love toward man" translates a single Greek word, philanthrōpia, from which we get "philanthropy." It means love and kindness toward human beings. Luke uses the same word for the people of Malta, who welcomed shipwrecked strangers and "showed us unusual kindness; for they kindled a fire and made us all welcome" (Acts 28:2). It is warm, practical care for people who have no claim on it.

That is the sharp contrast with the verse before. People were "hateful and hating one another," yet God's attitude toward them was kindness and love. He did not wait for them to become lovable. Paul makes the same point in Romans: "But God demonstrates His own love toward us, in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8).

A "Savior" is one who rescues people from danger they cannot escape on their own. In the Old Testament, God called Himself Israel's Savior: "I, even I, am the LORD, And besides Me there is no savior" (Isaiah 43:11). Paul uses the title often in this short letter, sometimes for God the Father (1:3; 2:10) and sometimes for Jesus Christ (1:4; 2:13). Here it refers to God the Father, whose love set salvation in motion. The next verses show that this salvation came through the Holy Spirit, whom God poured out "through Jesus Christ our Savior" (3:5–6).

"appeared,"

The Greek verb means to shine forth or become visible, the way light breaks into darkness. Paul used the same verb earlier in the letter: "For the grace of God that brings salvation has appeared to all men" (2:11). God's kindness and love were not new. He had always been kind and loving. But they appeared, becoming visible in history, in the coming of Jesus. In His birth, His life among people, His death on the cross, and His resurrection, God's love for humanity was put on display where people could see it. God did not merely tell the world He loved it. He showed it in His Son.

Study Notes

The words Paul uses here had a public, political ring in his day. Inscriptions from across the Roman world called rulers "savior." One at Ephesus honored Julius Caesar as "god manifest" and "common savior of human life." The noun related to "appeared" was often used for the visit or arrival of a ruler or a god. Philanthrōpia, the "love toward man" in this verse, was a virtue that official decrees praised in kings who showed generosity to their subjects.

Paul gathers this vocabulary around the true God. He used the same "appearing" language twice in chapter 2 (2:11, 13). Interpreters disagree about whether he meant a deliberate contrast with imperial claims or was simply using the ordinary religious language of his world. Either way, the effect is striking. Paul has just told believers to submit to earthly rulers (3:1). Now he describes the only Savior whose kindness toward people actually rescues them.

Titus 3:5
Titus 3:5
NKJV

Titus 3:5 not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy He saved us, through the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit,(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 3:5 he saved us, not because of the righteous things we had done, but because of his mercy. He washed away our sins, giving us a new birth and new life through the Holy Spirit. (NLT)

Main Idea

God saved us because of His mercy, not because of any good deeds we had done. He did this by washing us clean and giving us new life through the Holy Spirit, who renews us from the inside out.

Verse Explanation

God saved us because of His mercy, not because of any good deeds we had done. He did this by washing us clean and giving us new life through the Holy Spirit, who renews us from the inside out.

"not by works of righteousness which we have done,"

Paul begins by ruling out the reason people most often assume God accepts them. "Works of righteousness" are good and upright deeds: obeying God's commands, doing what is right, living a moral life. Paul does not say such deeds are worthless. He says they were not the reason God saved us. In Greek, the word "we" is emphatic, stressing what we ourselves did. Paul has just described what believers were before God saved them: "foolish, disobedient, deceived, serving various lusts and pleasures" (3:3). People in that condition had no righteous record to offer God. Even at their best, as Isaiah confessed, "all our righteousnesses are like filthy rags" (Isaiah 64:6).

This mattered on Crete. Some there were teaching that keeping parts of the Jewish law was necessary (1:10, 14). Others claimed to know God while their lives denied Him (1:16). Paul answers both problems the same way he does elsewhere: "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works, lest anyone should boast" (Ephesians 2:8–9). Good deeds still have their place in the Christian life, but they come after salvation as its result, not before it as its price.

"but according to His mercy He saved us,"

"He" is God our Savior (3:4). The measure of what God did was His mercy, not our merit. Mercy is God's compassion toward people in misery who deserve nothing from Him. He did not look for something good in us and reward it. He saw our helpless condition and acted out of pity and love. Paul says the same in Ephesians: "But God, who is rich in mercy, because of His great love with which He loved us" made us alive even when we were dead in sins (Ephesians 2:4–5).

"He saved us" describes something God has already done. Paul does not say God helped us save ourselves or made salvation possible for us to complete. God is the one who acted, and the result is settled. Believers are those whom God has rescued from sin's guilt and power.

"through the washing of regeneration"

Paul now explains how God saved us. The word "washing" means a bath, a complete cleansing. "Regeneration" means a new birth, being born again. God did not simply clean up the old life. He washed away the filth of sin and gave a new life that was not there before. Jesus told Nicodemus that a person must be "born again" to see the kingdom of God (John 3:3), and this washing is that new birth.

This is the Spirit's work within a person. Baptism pictures this cleansing outwardly, but the washing Paul describes is what God does inside the heart. God had promised it long before through Ezekiel: "Then I will sprinkle clean water on you, and you shall be clean; I will cleanse you from all your filthiness and from all your idols. I will give you a new heart and put a new spirit within you" (Ezekiel 36:25–26). Cleansing and new life come together, just as Paul joins them here.

"and renewing of the Holy Spirit,"

"Renewing" means making new. The Holy Spirit does not merely improve a person's behavior. He makes the person new from the inside. Paul uses the same word in Romans 12:2, "be transformed by the renewing of your mind," which shows that this new life keeps working its way out as believers grow. The washing and the renewing are both the Spirit's work. The people who once lived "hateful and hating one another" (3:3) have been changed by God Himself: "if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation; old things have passed away; behold, all things have become new" (2 Corinthians 5:17).

In this one sentence, salvation flows from the Father's mercy and is carried out by the Spirit's work. Paul goes on to say that God poured out this Spirit "through Jesus Christ our Savior" (3:6).

Study Notes

Christians have long discussed whether "the washing of regeneration" refers to baptism. Roman Catholic, Orthodox, and Lutheran traditions understand it as baptism, through which God gives new birth. Many others hold that Paul is describing the Spirit's inward cleansing. On that view, baptism is its outward picture, or Paul may not be thinking of baptism at all. The MTB understanding, given in the explanation above, is that the washing is God's inward work, which baptism pictures.

The grammar supports reading the verse as one work of the Spirit. In Greek a single "through" governs both "washing" and "renewing," and "of the Holy Spirit" most naturally belongs to both. Paul seems to describe one saving act from two sides, cleansing and making new, rather than a rite and a separate work of the Spirit. The word for "regeneration," meaning "new birth" or "new beginning," appears elsewhere in the New Testament only in Matthew 19:28. There Jesus uses it for the renewal of all things when He reigns. The new birth of a believer is a personal beginning of that coming renewal.

Titus 3:6
Titus 3:6
NKJV

Titus 3:6 whom He poured out on us abundantly through Jesus Christ our Savior,(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 3:6 He generously poured out the Spirit upon us through Jesus Christ our Savior. (NLT)

Main Idea

God gave the Holy Spirit to believers generously, not in small measure, and He did this through Jesus Christ our Savior. The Father, Son, and Spirit all work together in bringing a person to salvation.

Verse Explanation

God gave the Holy Spirit to believers generously, not in small measure, and He did this through Jesus Christ our Savior. The Father, Son, and Spirit all work together in bringing a person to salvation.

"whom He poured out on us"

"Whom" refers to the Holy Spirit, just named in the previous verse as the One who renews believers. "He" is God the Father, called "God our Savior" in verse 4, whose kindness and love moved Him to save. The Spirit is not a reward believers earn. God gives Him.

"Poured out" is the same verb used for the promise God made through the prophet Joel, which Peter quoted on the Day of Pentecost: "I will pour out of My Spirit on all flesh" (Acts 2:17). The Spirit's coming at Pentecost was the fulfillment of that promise. Paul says the Spirit was poured out "on us." That means not only on the apostles in Jerusalem but on every believer, including the young Christians on Crete. Each person God has saved has received the Spirit. Paul says something similar in Romans: "the love of God has been poured out in our hearts by the Holy Spirit who was given to us" (Romans 5:5).

"abundantly"

The Greek word means richly or generously. God did not hand out His Spirit in small, careful portions. He poured Him out in full measure. Paul uses the same word when he says God "gives us richly all things to enjoy" (1 Timothy 6:17). The cleansing and new life described in verse 5 are not partial or fragile. God gave believers everything they needed to begin a new life, and He gave it freely.

"through Jesus Christ our Savior,"

The Father gave the Spirit through His Son. Jesus had told His disciples, "It is to your advantage that I go away; for if I do not go away, the Helper will not come to you; but if I depart, I will send Him to you" (John 16:7). On the Day of Pentecost, Peter explained what had happened: Jesus, "being exalted to the right hand of God, and having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, He poured out this which you now see and hear" (Acts 2:33). Because Jesus died, rose, and returned to the Father, the Spirit came to live in His people.

In verse 4, the Father is called "God our Savior." Here Jesus is called "our Savior." Paul gives the same title to both throughout this letter. He speaks of "God our Savior" and then "the Lord Jesus Christ our Savior" (1:3–4), and later of "God our Savior" (2:10) and then "our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (2:13). The Father and the Son are not two separate rescuers with different roles in competition. They share one saving work.

Verses 4–6 show the whole of God at work in rescuing sinners. The Father, moved by kindness and love, planned salvation and gave the Spirit. The Son is the One through whom the Spirit was given. The Spirit washes and renews the believer from within. Salvation does not depend on anything we contribute. From beginning to end, it is the work of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit together.

Titus 3:7
Titus 3:7
NKJV

Titus 3:7 that having been justified by His grace we should become heirs according to the hope of eternal life.(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 3:7 Because of his grace he made us right in his sight and gave us confidence that we will inherit eternal life. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because God has declared us right with Him as a free gift of His grace, we have become His heirs, people who will receive the eternal life He has promised. This hope is certain because it rests on what God has done, not on what we have earned.

Verse Explanation

Because God has declared us right with Him as a free gift of His grace, we have become His heirs, people who will receive the eternal life He has promised. This hope is certain because it rests on what God has done, not on what we have earned.

"that having been justified by His grace"

The word "that" shows the purpose behind what God did in the previous verses. He saved us through the Spirit's work of cleansing and new life, the Spirit He poured out through Jesus Christ (3:5–6), so that we would be justified and become His heirs. To be "justified" means to be declared righteous. It is a courtroom word. It describes a judge's ruling that a person stands in the right before the law. Here God Himself is the Judge, and He declares guilty people to be right with Him. The verse speaks of this as something already done: believers have been justified.

That ruling rests on "His grace." Grace is God's undeserved favor, kindness given freely to people who have not earned it and could never repay it. Paul has just described what believers once were: "foolish, disobedient, deceived," living "in malice and envy, hateful and hating one another" (3:3). God did not declare such people righteous because of anything they had done, since He saved us "not by works of righteousness which we have done" (3:5). Paul says the same in Romans: believers are "justified freely by His grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Romans 3:24). Earlier in this letter he wrote that "the grace of God that brings salvation has appeared to all men" (2:11). That grace appeared in Jesus Christ, and through Him God gives a right standing that no one could earn.

"we should become heirs"

An heir is someone who receives an inheritance because he belongs to the family. This fits what Paul has just said about the "washing of regeneration" (3:5). Regeneration means being given new life, being born again. Those whom God has given new life become His children, and His children receive what He has promised them. Paul makes the same connection in Romans: "and if children, then heirs—heirs of God and joint heirs with Christ" (Romans 8:17). God does more than clear guilty people of their guilt. He also brings them into His family and gives them a share in His inheritance.

"according to the hope of eternal life."

In the Bible, "hope" is not wishful thinking. It is a confident expectation of something God has promised but has not yet fully given. The inheritance believers look forward to is eternal life. That life begins now through the Spirit's work within them. Its fullness waits for the day Paul calls "the blessed hope and glorious appearing of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ" (2:13). Peter describes this inheritance as "incorruptible and undefiled and that does not fade away, reserved in heaven for you" (1 Peter 1:4).

Paul opened this letter with the same hope, calling it the "hope of eternal life which God, who cannot lie, promised before time began" (1:2). The believer's hope is certain because it depends on God's promise and God's grace, not on the believer's own record. The same God who declared them righteous is the One who will give them the inheritance.

Titus 3:8
Titus 3:8
NKJV

Titus 3:8 This is a faithful saying, and these things I want you to affirm constantly, that those who have believed in God should be careful to maintain good works. These things are good and profitable to men.(NKJV)

NLT

Titus 3:8 This is a trustworthy saying, and I want you to insist on these teachings so that all who trust in God will devote themselves to doing good. These teachings are good and beneficial for everyone. (NLT)

Main Idea

Paul calls this teaching about salvation a trustworthy saying that Titus should keep stressing, so that those who believe in God will make it their aim to do good works. Good deeds do not earn salvation, but they are the natural result of it and benefit everyone.

Verse Explanation

Paul calls this teaching about salvation a trustworthy saying that Titus should keep stressing, so that those who believe in God will make it their aim to do good works. Good deeds do not earn salvation, but they are the natural result of it and benefit everyone.

"This is a faithful saying,"

"Faithful" means trustworthy, something that can be fully relied on. Paul uses this expression several times in his letters to Timothy and Titus to mark a truth of special importance, such as "This is a faithful saying and worthy of all acceptance, that Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners" (1 Timothy 1:15). Here it points back to the teaching about salvation Paul has just given (3:4–7). God's kindness and love appeared. He saved us "not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy" (3:5). He gave new life through the Holy Spirit, and "having been justified by His grace," we became "heirs according to the hope of eternal life" (3:7). Paul is telling Titus that these truths can be depended on completely.

"and these things I want you to affirm constantly,"

The word translated "affirm" means to speak with confidence and insist on something. Titus was not to mention these truths once and move on. He was to keep stressing them. False teachers on Crete were already "subverting whole households" (1:11), so the churches needed to hear the true message of salvation clearly and often.

"that those who have believed in God"

These are the people whose salvation Paul has just described, those who have come to trust in God and continue to trust Him. The order matters. Good works do not lead up to faith or earn God's favor. They come after a person has believed and been saved by God's mercy.

"should be careful to maintain good works."

"Be careful" translates a word that means to think about something, to give serious attention to it. "Maintain" translates a word that literally means to stand in front of something. It can mean to lead or take charge, and Paul uses it elsewhere for a man who "rules his own house well" (1 Timothy 3:4). Here it means to devote oneself to good works and make them a priority. Believers are not to do good only now and then, when it happens to suit them. They are to make it their steady aim.

Good works are a major concern in this letter. They include honest work, kindness, generosity, self-control, and faithful service to others. The false teachers on Crete claimed to know God, but "in works they deny Him" (1:16). Titus himself was to be "a pattern of good works" (2:7). Christ "gave Himself for us, that He might redeem us from every lawless deed and purify for Himself His own special people, zealous for good works" (2:14). Earlier in this chapter Paul told believers to be "ready for every good work" in public life (3:1). That included being peaceable, gentle, and humble toward everyone (3:2).

Paul's wording shows that the good works he has just ruled out as the way of salvation are the expected result of it. In verse 5 he insists that God did not save us because of our works. Here he insists that the saved must devote themselves to good works. Paul teaches the same order in Ephesians. After saying salvation is "not of works, lest anyone should boast" (Ephesians 2:9), he adds, "For we are His workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, which God prepared beforehand that we should walk in them" (Ephesians 2:10). God's grace that saves people also changes how they live. Those who were once "foolish, disobedient, deceived" (3:3) now show by their conduct that God has made them new.

"These things are good and profitable to men."

"These things" most naturally refers to what Titus is to keep teaching: God's saving mercy and the good works that come from it. The word "good" is the same word used in "good works." It describes what is excellent and right. "Profitable" means useful and beneficial. This teaching does real good to people. It builds believers up, and the good works it produces bless neighbors, families, and whole communities. Paul has already shown that godly living can "adorn the doctrine of God our Savior in all things" (2:10), making the message of salvation attractive to those who see it lived out.

Study Notes

The phrase "maintain good works," repeated in verse 14, has a possible alternative translation. The verb can mean "be engaged in" an activity, and "good works" can also mean "honorable occupations." Some scholars and translation footnotes therefore render it "engage in honorable occupations." On that reading, Paul is telling believers to earn an honest living. That would suit an island Paul described as full of "lazy gluttons" (1:12) and "idle talkers" (1:10). It would also suit verse 14, where hard work supplies what is needed. Paul gives similar teaching elsewhere, urging believers to "work with your own hands" (1 Thessalonians 4:11).

Most translations keep "good works," and with good reason. The phrase runs throughout Titus with its broader meaning (2:7, 14; 3:1). In this verse it comes right after the teaching on salvation by grace, so the contrast with "works of righteousness" (3:5) is hard to miss. The broader meaning is more likely, though honest work is certainly one of the good works Paul has in mind.

Verse Explanation

In contrast to teaching that produces good lives, Titus is to stay away from pointless arguments, debates about family trees, quarrels, and fights over the law, because they accomplish nothing worthwhile. This does not forbid serious discussion of truth; it warns against controversies that distract from the Gospel and bear no fruit.

"But avoid foolish disputes,"

"But" sets this warning against what Paul has just told Titus to stress: the faithful teaching about salvation, which leads believers to "maintain good works" (3:8). The Greek verb for "avoid" pictures stepping around something to keep clear of it, and the command is ongoing: keep on staying away. The word for "disputes" refers to questions or speculations, the kind of issues people debate for the sake of debating. Paul calls them "foolish" because they may sound deep but have no real wisdom in them. He gave Timothy the same instruction: "But avoid foolish and ignorant disputes, knowing that they generate strife" (2 Timothy 2:23).

"genealogies,"

These are not the family lines recorded in Scripture, which have real purpose. Matthew and Luke trace the ancestry of Jesus to show that He is the promised Son of David (Matthew 1:1; Luke 3:23–38). The problem was the way some people used genealogies. Paul has already warned about troublemakers in Crete, especially "those of the circumcision" (1:10), and told Titus that believers must not give heed to "Jewish fables and commandments of men" (1:14). Many interpreters think these teachers built elaborate theories and legends on the family lists of the Old Testament, or used family descent to claim special standing before God. Paul warned Timothy about the same kind of thing: "endless genealogies, which cause disputes" (1 Timothy 1:4). Such speculation could keep people busy for years without bringing anyone closer to God.

"contentions, and strivings about the law;"

"Contentions" are quarrels and rivalries, and they are what foolish debates produce. The church was meant to be different. Believers are to be "peaceable, gentle, showing all humility to all men" (3:2), because they themselves were once "hateful and hating one another" (3:3). Quarreling inside the church pulls them back toward that old way of life.

"Strivings about the law" are fights over the law of Moses, most likely arguments pushed by those who wanted to add Jewish rules and rituals to faith in Christ. Paul was not against the law itself. He wrote, "But we know that the law is good if one uses it lawfully" (1 Timothy 1:8). What he rejected were endless arguments over the law's details that led people away from the grace that saves them. Such teachers could sound very religious, yet Paul said of them, "They profess to know God, but in works they deny Him" (1:16).

"for they are unprofitable and useless."

Paul gives the reason in words that point back to the previous verse. The teaching about God's mercy and the good works that come from it is "good and profitable to men" (3:8). These controversies are the opposite. They are "unprofitable," because they help no one, and "useless," meaning empty and futile, because they produce nothing that lasts. They do not lead anyone to Christ, strengthen faith, or move believers to do good. Instead they waste time, stir up strife, and pull attention away from the Gospel.

This does not mean Titus should avoid every hard question or refuse to deal with error. Paul expected him to be able to "convict those who contradict" (1:9) and even to "rebuke them sharply, that they may be sound in the faith" (1:13). Careful discussion of truth and needed correction are part of protecting the church. What Titus must avoid are arguments that go in circles, divide believers, and bear no fruit.

Study Notes

Beginning with Irenaeus in the second century, some interpreters connected the "genealogies" in Titus and 1 Timothy 1:4 with Gnostic teaching. The Gnostics were a later movement that described long chains of spiritual beings coming out from God. Fully developed Gnostic systems, however, appear only after Paul's time. The context in Titus also points in a Jewish direction. The troublemakers are "of the circumcision" (1:10), they spread "Jewish fables" (1:14), and their quarrels are "about the law."

Jewish writings from this period show the kind of speculation Paul probably meant. Works such as Jubilees and the Biblical Antiquities (written under the name of Philo) retell Genesis and fill in its family lists with invented names, wives, and legends. Pride in ancestry was another use of genealogy. John the Baptist rebuked people who relied on saying, "We have Abraham as our father" (Matthew 3:9). The exact form of the Cretan teaching cannot be known, but the Jewish background fits the evidence best.

Verse Explanation

A person who keeps stirring up division in the church should be warned once, and then a second time, and if he still refuses to change, the church should have nothing more to do with him. This protects the unity and health of the church while still giving the person a fair chance to repent.

"Reject a divisive man"

The Greek word translated "divisive" is the word from which we get "heretic," and this is the only place it appears in the New Testament. Its root meaning has to do with choosing, and from there it came to describe a party or faction. The related noun is used of the "sect" of the Sadducees and of the Pharisees (Acts 5:17; 15:5), and Paul lists "dissensions, heresies" among the works of the flesh (Galatians 5:20). Here the word describes a person who chooses his own way and keeps stirring up division in the church, drawing others into his side. Paul has just told Titus to avoid "foolish disputes, genealogies, contentions, and strivings about the law" (3:9). The divisive man is the one who will not let those fruitless arguments go. He keeps pressing them until the church splits into camps.

On Crete this was a real danger. False teachers were already "subverting whole households" (1:11), and the believers lived among a harsh and quarrelsome culture. Yet Paul had just called them to be "peaceable, gentle, showing all humility to all men" (3:2). A church that is constantly divided cannot show that kind of life to its neighbors. Jesus prayed that His people would be one, "that the world may believe that You sent Me" (John 17:21). Division does more than make a church unpleasant. It weakens its grasp of the truth, harms its spiritual health, and damages its witness.

The word "reject" means to refuse or decline something. Paul uses the same word when he tells Timothy to "reject profane and old wives' fables" (1 Timothy 4:7) and to "avoid foolish and ignorant disputes, knowing that they generate strife" (2 Timothy 2:23). To reject the divisive man means refusing to accept his divisive influence. He is no longer given a hearing, and he is no longer given room to keep spreading division among God's people. Paul gives similar instructions elsewhere: "note those who cause divisions and offenses, contrary to the doctrine which you learned, and avoid them" (Romans 16:17).

This does not mean the church should shut down every disagreement or treat everyone who raises a hard question as a troublemaker. Believers can discuss Scripture honestly, and leaders must still teach and correct. Paul himself wanted elders who could "exhort and convict those who contradict" (1:9). The man in view is not someone who simply disagrees. He keeps causing division even after he has been corrected.

"after the first and second admonition,"

The Greek word for "admonition" means a warning meant to set someone right. It is the same word Paul uses when he tells fathers to raise their children "in the training and admonition of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4). The goal of a warning is not to drive a person away but to call him back. The divisive man is to be warned once, and then a second time. That gives him a fair and clear opportunity to see what he is doing and to change.

This patient approach fits Jesus' own instruction for dealing with a brother who sins: go to him privately, then bring one or two others, and only then bring the matter to the church (Matthew 18:15–17). Paul shows the same spirit when he tells the Thessalonians not to keep company with a disobedient person, yet adds, "do not count him as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother" (2 Thessalonians 3:15). The church is not quick to reject anyone. But the warnings do have a limit. If a person refuses correction after being warned twice, the problem is no longer a misunderstanding. He has shown that he is set on his course, and the church must protect itself from the harm he is doing.

Verse Explanation

Paul tells Titus that he plans to send either Artemas or Tychicus to take over the work in Crete, and that Titus should then hurry to join him at Nicopolis, where Paul intends to spend the winter. The verse gives a glimpse of how Paul organized his team of coworkers for ministry across many places.

"When I send Artemas to you, or Tychicus,"

Paul had not yet decided which of the two men to send, but he wanted Titus to know that a replacement was coming. Titus was not to leave Crete until someone arrived to carry on the work. The churches there still needed to be set in order and given elders (1:5), and they faced false teachers who were upsetting whole households (1:10–11). Paul would not pull Titus away and leave them without help.

Artemas is mentioned nowhere else in the New Testament, so nothing more is known about him. That Paul would trust him with Titus's work in Crete shows he was a capable and reliable worker. Tychicus is better known. He came from the province of Asia and traveled with Paul as he carried the offering from the churches to Jerusalem (Acts 20:4). Paul later sent him to deliver the letters to Ephesus and Colosse and to report on Paul's circumstances, describing him as "a beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord" (Ephesians 6:21). Near the end of his life, Paul writes, "And Tychicus I have sent to Ephesus" (2 Timothy 4:12). For that reason, many interpreters think Artemas was the one who eventually went to Crete.

"be diligent to come to me at Nicopolis,"

The Greek word translated "be diligent" means to hurry or make every effort. Once his replacement arrived, Titus was not to linger. Paul uses the same word when he urges Timothy, "Be diligent to come to me quickly" (2 Timothy 4:9). He valued having his coworkers with him, both for their companionship and for the work they could do together.

Several cities in the ancient world were called Nicopolis, a name meaning "city of victory." The one Paul most likely meant stood on the western coast of Greece. Caesar Augustus founded it to celebrate his naval victory at Actium. It had a harbor and was a well-connected city, a good place for Paul to stay and to direct the work in the surrounding region. The word "there" suggests that Paul had not yet arrived when he wrote this letter. Later, Titus went to Dalmatia (2 Timothy 4:10), a region farther north along the same coast.

"for I have decided to spend the winter there."

Travel by sea on the Mediterranean became dangerous in the winter months, and ships generally stayed in port until spring. Paul knew those dangers firsthand. On his voyage to Rome, his ship sailed late in the season, when "sailing was now dangerous" (Acts 27:9), and it was wrecked in a storm. By choosing a city for the winter, Paul made sure Titus could reach him before travel shut down and that the two could spend those months together.

Paul's wording shows that he was free to make his own travel plans, unlike his situation as a prisoner at the end of Acts. Many interpreters believe he wrote this letter after his release from that first imprisonment in Rome, while he continued to strengthen the churches with the help of trusted coworkers.

The Greek word for "lawyer" was used in the Gospels for experts in the Law of Moses (Luke 10:25), but it was also a common term for a lawyer in Greek or Roman law. "on their journey with haste,"

"With haste" can also mean diligently or earnestly. Paul wanted Titus to give this task prompt and careful attention. He was not asking for a token gesture.

The goal was full provision, so that Zenas and Apollos would not be held back or have to scramble for basic needs along the way. Meeting the real needs of these traveling servants of the Lord was one practical way to do that.

"that they may not be unfruitful."

A fruit tree that produces nothing has failed at the very thing it exists to do. Jesus used this picture when He said, "By this My Father is glorified, that you bear much fruit; so you will be My disciples" (John 15:8). Good works do not earn salvation, since God saved us "not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy" (3:5). But a life changed by His mercy is meant to produce visible fruit. That fruit sets believers apart from the false teachers on Crete, who "profess to know God, but in works they deny Him" and are "disqualified for every good work" (1:16), and from the arguments Paul calls "unprofitable and useless" (3:9). Their talk produced nothing, but believers who meet the real needs of others show that their faith is genuine.

Paul does not name the people with him, but they were his coworkers in the ministry. Some of them, such as Artemas or Tychicus, may have been the very men he planned to send to Crete (3:12). Their greeting reminded Titus that he was not working alone. While he dealt with false teachers and divisive people on the island, a whole circle of believers elsewhere stood with him and cared about the churches he was serving.

"Greet those who love us in the faith."

Paul asks Titus to pass his greetings on to the believers on Crete. Their love for Paul and his companions rested on the faith they shared in Jesus Christ. It was not merely personal friendship. Paul had opened the letter by calling Titus "a true son in our common faith" (1:4), and that common faith bound together believers who had never met.

The wording is also fitting for Crete. Not everyone connected with the churches there was a friend of Paul's teaching. Some were "insubordinate, both idle talkers and deceivers" who were upsetting whole households (1:10–11), and others were stirring up division (3:10). Many interpreters see a quiet distinction in Paul's words. His warm greeting goes to those who loved the truth and those who taught it.

"Grace be with you all."

The word "you" here is plural. The letter was written to Titus, but Paul expected its instructions to reach the churches, so his closing blessing reaches all the believers on Crete. Paul regularly ended his letters with a prayer for grace. He even called it his personal "sign in every epistle" (2 Thessalonians 3:17).

This prayer for grace carries special weight in Titus. Grace is God's undeserved favor, and earlier in the chapter Paul explained that believers were "justified by His grace" (3:7). Grace had also been "teaching us" to turn from ungodliness and live godly lives (2:11–12). The same grace that saved the Cretan believers would carry them forward as they lived out everything Paul had written: submitting to authorities, speaking gently to all people, avoiding fruitless quarrels, and devoting themselves to good works. The letter that opened with "Grace, mercy, and peace" (1:4) closes with grace as well.

"Amen."

"Amen" comes from a Hebrew word meaning firm or sure. It expresses agreement and confidence: "So let it be." Paul closes his prayer for the believers by affirming that God's grace is certain and that he wants it for all of them.

Study Notes

The closing "Amen" is missing from several of the oldest Greek manuscripts. It was likely added as the letter came to be read aloud in worship, which is why most modern translations leave it out. The NKJV follows the Greek text that includes it.

Older editions of the King James Version also printed a note after this verse saying the letter was written to Titus, "ordained the first bishop of the church of the Cretians, from Nicopolis of Macedonia." Notes like this were added by later copyists and are not part of Scripture. This one is probably mistaken on two counts. The Nicopolis Paul most likely meant was on the western coast of Greece, not in Macedonia. And Paul's words "I have decided to spend the winter there" (3:12) suggest he had not yet arrived when he wrote.
