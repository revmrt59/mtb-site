Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 3
Main Idea:

Paul calls believers to gentle, good conduct toward rulers and neighbors because God saved them by His kindness and mercy, not by their works, and he tells Titus to keep affirming this gospel while avoiding fruitless disputes and firmly warning the divisive.

Setting:

The instruction of chapter 2 now moves into the believers’ public life on Crete, and Paul closes the letter with plans for Titus and his coworkers (3:12–15).

Chapter Outline:

  • Titus 3:1–2 — Reminders for Life Among Others

  • Titus 3:3–8 — Saved by God’s Mercy

  • Titus 3:9–11 — Avoid Disputes and Warn the Divisive

  • Titus 3:12–15 — Final Instructions and Greetings

Key Verse:

“not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy He saved us, through the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit” (Titus 3:5)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Titus 3 turns Christian conduct outward. Believers on Crete are to be subject to rulers, “ready for every good work,” and gentle, “showing all humility to all men” (3:1–2), including neighbors who are hard to love. Paul grounds this in memory: “we ourselves were also once foolish, disobedient, deceived” (3:3). Believers who remember where God found them have no ground to look down on anyone.

Then Paul tells how everything changed. “When the kindness and the love of God our Savior toward man appeared,” God saved His people “not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to His mercy” (3:4–5). He washed and renewed them by the Holy Spirit, whom He “poured out on us abundantly through Jesus Christ our Savior,” so that, justified by grace, they became heirs of eternal life (3:5–7). The Father’s kindness, the Spirit’s renewal, and the Son through whom the Spirit is given all appear in this one saving work. Paul wants this “faithful saying” affirmed constantly so that believers will “maintain good works” (3:8).

The chapter then guards the church from what is “unprofitable and useless”: foolish disputes, and the divisive person who refuses repeated warnings (3:9–11). Paul closes with practical care for his coworkers, a final call to good works that “meet urgent needs,” and grace for all (3:12–15).

Chapter Sections

Titus 3:1–2 — Reminders for Life Among Others

Believers are to respect governing authorities, be ready for every good work, speak evil of no one, and treat everyone with gentleness and humility. Paul turns the godly living of chapter 2 outward into public life on Crete.

Titus 3:3–8 — Saved by God’s Mercy

Believers were once foolish and enslaved to their passions, but God saved them by His mercy, not by their works, through the washing of regeneration and renewing of the Holy Spirit poured out through Jesus Christ. This faithful saying is to be affirmed constantly so that those who believe will be careful to maintain good works.

Titus 3:9–11 — Avoid Disputes and Warn the Divisive

Titus must avoid foolish disputes, genealogies, and quarrels about the law because they are unprofitable and useless. A divisive person is to be warned twice and then rejected, since his refusal shows that he is warped and self-condemned.

Titus 3:12–15 — Final Instructions and Greetings

Paul plans to send Artemas or Tychicus and asks Titus then to join him at Nicopolis, to supply Zenas and Apollos for their journey, and to teach the believers to meet urgent needs. He closes with greetings and grace for them all.

Why This Chapter Matters

Titus 3 gives one of the clearest pictures of salvation in the New Testament and sets it among instructions for everyday conduct. God saves “according to His mercy” (3:5), and the Spirit’s renewal is the source of a changed life. No one can boast of earning that salvation, and no one is beyond the reach of a mercy that saved the people described in 3:3.

The chapter also shows how grace shapes conduct toward outsiders and within the church. Those who remember their own past can be gentle with people still living that way (3:2–3). And the church must protect its unity: honest questions and careful doctrinal discussion are welcome, but fruitless controversy and persistent divisiveness after repeated warnings must be confronted (3:9–11).

Key Themes to Watch For

Good works. Paul calls for good works three times in this chapter, always as the fruit of salvation rather than its cause (3:1, 8, 14).

Gentleness toward all. Believers who remember what they once were treat everyone with humility (3:1–3).

Mercy, not merit. Salvation comes from God’s kindness and mercy, and believers are justified by His grace (3:4–5, 7).

The Spirit’s renewal. God washes and renews His people by the Holy Spirit, poured out abundantly through Jesus Christ (3:5–6).

Guarding unity. Fruitless disputes are avoided, and persistent divisiveness is confronted after repeated warnings (3:9–11).

Key Words & Concepts

Subject to rulers and authorities (3:1). Believers honor civil authority and are ready to do good in public life. This stands alongside the apostles’ answer when obedience to people would mean disobeying God: “We ought to obey God rather than men” (Acts 5:29).

The washing of regeneration (3:5). The washing is the inward cleansing and new birth the Holy Spirit works in a person. Where baptism is in view, it is the outward sign and seal of that cleansing; the water itself does not cause new birth. Whether Paul refers directly to baptism here is debated, but salvation rests on God’s mercy and the Spirit’s work, not on any human act.

Justified and heirs (3:7). To be justified is to be declared right with God, here “by His grace.” Believers become heirs “according to the hope of eternal life,” the same hope Paul named in the letter’s first verses (1:2).

A faithful saying (3:8). Paul uses this phrase to mark a trustworthy summary of the gospel, as he does elsewhere in his letters to Timothy (1 Timothy 1:15). Here it seals the teaching of 3:4–7.

A divisive man (3:10). Someone who keeps stirring up division after a first and second warning. The instruction protects the gospel and the church’s unity; it is not aimed at honest questions or legitimate discussion of doctrine.

Nicopolis and Paul’s coworkers (3:12–13). Paul will send Artemas or Tychicus, one of the two, to relieve Titus. Nicopolis is most likely the city in Epirus on the western coast of Greece, where Paul had decided to spend the winter. Zenas “the lawyer” may have been an expert in Jewish or Roman law; Apollos is the teacher known from Acts and 1 Corinthians.
