Main Idea

Chronicles begins with Adam, then Seth and Enosh, tracing the line from the first man God created. Opening with Adam places Israel’s story within the history of all humanity and identifies Israel’s God as the Creator.

Verse Explanation

Chronicles begins with Adam, then Seth and Enosh, tracing the line from the first man God created. Opening with Adam places Israel’s story within the history of all humanity and identifies Israel’s God as the Creator.

“Adam,”

First Chronicles opens with a single name and no introduction. There is no title, no setting, and no sentence, only “Adam.” For a people rebuilding their life after exile, the first word of the book reaches back to the very beginning. Adam was the first man, formed by God from the dust and given life by His breath (Genesis 2:7). By starting here, Chronicles places Israel's story inside the story of all humanity. Israel's God is not a local deity whose concern begins with one nation. He is the Creator of every human being, and the history Chronicles will tell belongs within His purposes for the whole world.

“Seth, Enosh,”

The list moves directly from Adam to “Seth,” the son born after Abel's death (Genesis 4:25), and then to Seth's son “Enosh.” Chronicles passes over Cain and Abel without mention. It is following the line that Genesis 5 traces from Adam to Noah, the line through which the narrative of Genesis continues. Genesis notes that in the days of Enosh “men began to call on the name of the LORD” (Genesis 4:26). Chronicles gives no such comment. The names stand on their own, and their bare listing asks the reader to receive them as real people in a real line of descent.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Literary form. Verses 1–4 consist entirely of names without connecting words such as “begot” or “the son of.” The reader is expected to know the fuller account in Genesis 5, where each generation is introduced with ages and the formula “and he died.” Chronicles compresses that chapter into thirteen names.

Christ connection. Luke's genealogy of Jesus traces His ancestry back through Enosh and Seth to “Adam, the son of God” (Luke 3:38). Chronicles' opening and Luke's conclusion both place Israel's story, and finally the story of Jesus, within the history of the whole human race. This is a canonical connection, not a statement made by Chronicles itself.

Theological context. Opening with Adam identifies the God of Israel with the Creator revealed in Genesis. Paul makes a related point in Athens: God “has made from one blood every nation of men” (Acts 17:26).

Main Idea

The line continues through Cainan, Mahalalel, and Jared, following the descent recorded in Genesis 5. The names are given without narrative, presenting them as real generations in a continuing line of descent.

Verse Explanation

The line continues through Cainan, Mahalalel, and Jared, following the descent recorded in Genesis 5. The names are given without narrative, presenting them as real generations in a continuing line of descent.

“Cainan, Mahalalel, Jared,”

The line continues through three more generations. “Cainan” was the son of Enosh, “Mahalalel” the son of Cainan, and “Jared” the son of Mahalalel (Genesis 5:9–18). Genesis tells us little about these men beyond their ages and the fact that each had sons and daughters and died. Chronicles tells us even less. Yet their place in the list matters. Each name is a link in an unbroken chain from the first man toward Noah, and through Noah toward Abraham and Israel. The genealogy treats them as historical people, not as symbols. Their lives were part of the history God was directing, even though nothing they did is recorded.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Spelling. The NKJV spells the name “Cainan” here and in Genesis 5:9, while some translations use ‘Kenan.’ This Cainan, son of Enosh, is a different person from the Cainan whom Luke lists between Arphaxad and Shelah (Luke 3:36); see the note at 1:18.

Genesis comparison. Genesis 5 supplies ages at each birth and total lifespans. Chronicles omits all numbers. It is concerned with the line of descent itself rather than with chronology.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Enoch, Methuselah, and Lamech carry the line toward Noah. Chronicles names them without the stories or ages that Genesis supplies, keeping attention on the continuity of the line.

“Enoch, Methuselah, Lamech,”

The names carry the line closer to Noah. “Enoch” is the most remarkable figure in the Genesis list. Genesis says twice that he “walked with God,” and adds, “he was not, for God took him” (Genesis 5:24). “Methuselah” his son is remembered as the longest-lived man in Scripture (Genesis 5:27). “Lamech” was Noah's father (Genesis 5:28–29).

Chronicles mentions none of this. It does not stop to tell Enoch's story or to note Methuselah's great age. Its purpose here is to show continuity, not to tell individual stories. The reader who knows Genesis supplies the background; the reader who does not still learns that these men stood in the line of descent that led to Noah.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Name distinction. This Lamech, the son of Methuselah in Seth's line, is a different man from Lamech the descendant of Cain, who boasted of violence (Genesis 4:18–24). Genesis also has two men named Enoch: Cain's son (Genesis 4:17) and Jared's son, who walked with God (Genesis 5:18–24). Chronicles follows only Seth's line.

Cross-reference. The New Testament remembers Enoch's faith (Hebrews 11:5) and quotes a prophecy attributed to him (Jude 14–15).

Main Idea

The first sequence ends with Noah and his three sons, Shem, Ham, and Japheth. These sons become the heads of the families of the nations listed in the verses that follow.

Verse Explanation

The first sequence ends with Noah and his three sons, Shem, Ham, and Japheth. These sons become the heads of the families of the nations listed in the verses that follow.

“Noah, Shem, Ham, and Japheth.”

The first sequence ends with “Noah,” the man through whom God preserved humanity in the flood, and his three sons, “Shem, Ham, and Japheth.” The list names ten generations from Adam through Noah, as in Genesis 5. With Noah the human family begins again. Every nation listed in the following verses traces back to one of these three sons, as Genesis 10 records: “from these the nations were divided on the earth after the flood” (Genesis 10:32).

The verse does not say that Shem, Ham, and Japheth were Noah's sons; it simply lists their names after his. The reader is expected to know the relationship from Genesis (Genesis 5:32; 10:1). The order “Shem, Ham, and Japheth” follows Genesis. In the verses that follow, however, Chronicles will treat the families in reverse order, Japheth first, then Ham, and finally Shem, so that the line leading to Abraham comes last and can be followed forward.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Structure. The genealogy of the nations (1:5–23) follows the arrangement of Genesis 10, which lists Japheth, Ham, and then Shem. Placing Shem last allows the record to move smoothly from Shem's family into the line from Shem to Abraham (1:24–27). This pattern of treating secondary lines first and the main line last recurs in the chapter: Ishmael and Keturah's sons before Isaac, and Esau before Israel.

Textual note. The Septuagint adds the words ‘the sons of Noah’ before the three names, making the relationship explicit. The Hebrew text followed by the NKJV simply lists them.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

The sons of Japheth are named: Gomer, Magog, Madai, Javan, Tubal, Meshech, and Tiras. This begins Japheth’s branch of humanity, following the order of Genesis 10.

“The sons of Japheth were”

The genealogy now turns from a single line to the families of the nations, beginning with “Japheth.” The expression “the sons of” introduces the peoples descended from him. In genealogies like this, “sons” can refer to individual sons, to the peoples who descended from them, or to both.

“Gomer, Magog, Madai, Javan, Tubal, Meshech, and Tiras.”

Seven names follow, matching Genesis 10:2 exactly. Several are known from later Scripture and from ancient history. “Madai” is the Hebrew name for the Medes, who later joined the Persians in conquering Babylon. “Javan” corresponds to the Ionians, a name that came to stand for the Greeks; Daniel speaks of “the kingdom of Greece” using this name (Daniel 8:21). “Magog,” “Meshech,” and “Tubal” appear together in Ezekiel's prophecy against Gog (Ezekiel 38:2). In general, Japheth's descendants are associated with peoples to the north and west of Israel. Chronicles records them without comment, but their presence at the head of the list shows that the nations far beyond Israel's horizon belong to the one human family God made.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Identifications. Proposed historical identifications include Gomer with the Cimmerians, Madai with the Medes, and Javan with the Ionian Greeks. Tubal and Meshech are often associated with peoples in Asia Minor known from Assyrian records. These identifications vary in certainty, and the names should not be treated as a precise map of modern nations.

Interpretive caution. The grouping of peoples under Noah's sons concerns ancestry and the arrangement of the ancient world. It provides no basis for ranking human worth or for claims of racial superiority.

Verse Explanation

Gomer’s sons are Ashkenaz, Diphath, and Togarmah. The record follows one of Japheth’s lines into the next generation.

“The sons of Gomer were Ashkenaz, Diphath, and Togarmah.”

Chronicles follows one of Japheth's sons into the next generation. “Gomer” had three sons. “Ashkenaz” appears in Jeremiah as a kingdom summoned against Babylon (Jeremiah 51:27). “Togarmah” is mentioned in Ezekiel as a people trading horses and mules with Tyre and as an ally of Gog (Ezekiel 27:14; 38:6). “Diphath” is otherwise unknown. Chronicles traces only Gomer's and Javan's lines among Japheth's sons, just as Genesis does. The names remind the reader that God knew these distant peoples by name long before Israel encountered them.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Name difference. Genesis 10:3 reads “Riphath” instead of “Diphath.” In Hebrew script the letters for d and r look very similar, and the difference is best explained as a copying variation between the two texts. Many manuscripts of Chronicles and the Septuagint read “Riphath.” Such variations involve spelling, not the truthfulness of the record.

Later usage. In medieval Jewish tradition “Ashkenaz” became a name for Germany and gave rise to the term “Ashkenazi” for Jews of central and eastern Europe. This later usage does not determine the identity of the ancient people.

Javan’s sons are Elishah, Tarshishah, Kittim, and Rodanim. Japheth’s descendants spread to peoples that Genesis 10:5 associates with the coastlands.

“The sons of Javan were Elishah, Tarshishah, Kittim, and Rodanim.”

The second line traced from Japheth is “Javan,” associated with the Greeks. His four sons are linked with peoples and places around the Mediterranean. “Kittim” is commonly identified with Cyprus; the NKJV translates the same Hebrew name as ‘Cyprus’ in passages such as Isaiah 23:1 and Daniel 11:30. “Tarshishah” corresponds to Tarshish, a distant seaport famous for ships and trade, the place to which Jonah tried to flee (Jonah 1:3). “Rodanim” is often connected with the island of Rhodes. “Elishah” is less certain but is also associated with the Mediterranean. Genesis says of these peoples, “From these the coastland peoples of the Gentiles were separated into their lands” (Genesis 10:5). Israel, a small nation in the hills of Canaan, was reminded that the God who called them also knew the peoples of the islands and distant seas.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Name differences. Genesis 10:4 reads “Tarshish” and “Dodanim.” “Tarshishah” is a variant form of the same name. “Rodanim” and “Dodanim” differ again by the similar Hebrew letters d and r. Many scholars prefer “Rodanim” as the original reading because of its likely connection with Rhodes, and the Septuagint of Genesis also reads it so.

Main Idea

The sons of Ham are Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan. Ham’s branch includes peoples well known in Israel’s later history, including Mizraim, the Hebrew name for Egypt, and Canaan.

Verse Explanation

The sons of Ham are Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan. Ham’s branch includes peoples well known in Israel’s later history, including Mizraim, the Hebrew name for Egypt, and Canaan.

“The sons of Ham were Cush, Mizraim, Put, and Canaan.”

The genealogy turns to Ham's descendants. His four sons represent peoples who loomed large in Israel's history. “Cush” refers to the region south of Egypt along the upper Nile, often translated “Ethiopia” in English Bibles. “Mizraim” is the ordinary Hebrew name for Egypt, the land where Israel had been enslaved. “Put” is usually connected with Libya or a people near Egypt. “Canaan” is the ancestor of the peoples who lived in the land God later gave to Israel. The list names Israel's former oppressor and the inhabitants of the promised land in the same breath, as members of one human family descended from Noah.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Interpretive caution. Genesis 9:25–27 records Noah's curse on Canaan. That curse has been tragically misused to justify racial slavery, especially of African peoples descended from Cush. Scripture's curse falls on Canaan, not on Cush or on Ham's descendants generally, and it was never a basis for racial oppression. Chronicles itself lists Ham's sons without any word of curse.

Geography. Egypt (Mizraim), Cush, and Put are associated with northeast Africa; Canaan with the eastern Mediterranean coast. The geographical arrangement in Genesis 10 is broadly regional rather than strictly racial or linguistic.

“The sons of Cush were Seba, Havilah, Sabta, Raama, and Sabtecha.”

The record follows Cush into the next generation. His five sons are generally associated with peoples in Africa and across the Red Sea in Arabia. “Seba” is mentioned with Sheba as a distant land that would bring gifts to the king (Psalm 72:10). “Havilah” appears elsewhere in Genesis as a region (Genesis 2:11; 25:18). The names “Sabta” and “Sabtecha” are otherwise little known.

“The sons of Raama were Sheba and Dedan.”

The line continues through “Raama” to his sons “Sheba” and “Dedan,” peoples known for trade in Arabia. The queen who later visited Solomon came from Sheba (1 Kings 10:1). The genealogy follows these trading peoples without comment, but their inclusion shows how widely the human family had spread and how many peoples Israel would encounter through commerce and history.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Repeated names. Sheba and Dedan appear again as sons of Jokshan, Abraham's son by Keturah (1:32), and another Sheba appears among Joktan's sons in Shem's line (1:22). The same names occur in different branches, possibly because tribal groups intermarried or settled together in Arabia, or because names were reused. The lists record these relationships without explaining them, and they should not be forced into a single identification.

Spelling. Genesis 10:7 reads “Sabtah,” “Raamah,” and “Sabtechah.” These are spelling variants.

Main Idea

Cush fathered Nimrod, who began to be a mighty one on the earth. This brief notice marks Nimrod’s prominence without offering a full portrait of his character, for which Genesis 10:8–12 supplies more background.

Verse Explanation

Cush fathered Nimrod, who began to be a mighty one on the earth. This brief notice marks Nimrod’s prominence without offering a full portrait of his character, for which Genesis 10:8–12 supplies more background.

“Cush begot Nimrod;”

The list pauses on one man. “Nimrod” is named as a descendant of “Cush.” “Begot” can refer to a direct father-son relationship or to more distant descent; Genesis gives no further detail.

“he began to be a mighty one on the earth.”

This brief notice interrupts the list to describe Nimrod's prominence. “A mighty one” describes a man of strength, power, and prominence. “He began” introduces his rise to prominence. Genesis adds that he was “a mighty hunter before the LORD” and that his kingdom began in Babel and Shinar and extended to Assyria, where he built Nineveh (Genesis 10:9–11). Chronicles repeats only the first statement. It records Nimrod's power without telling his whole story or giving a moral verdict. The notice shows that the rise of great powers belongs to the history God was recording, a history that would later include the empires of Assyria and Babylon, which conquered Israel and Judah.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Interpretation. Nimrod's character is debated. Some Jewish and Christian traditions portray him as a rebel against God and associate him with the tower of Babel, since his kingdom began in Babel (Genesis 10:10). Genesis does not explicitly make that connection, and the phrase “before the LORD” (Genesis 10:9) can be read positively or negatively. Chronicles' brief notice does not settle the question.

Later reference. Micah calls Assyria “the land of Nimrod” (Micah 5:6), reflecting the tradition that Nimrod founded cities in that region.

“Mizraim begot Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, Naphtuhim,”

Chronicles returns to Ham's second son, “Mizraim,” Egypt. The names that follow end in -im, the Hebrew plural, showing that they describe peoples or clans rather than individual sons. “Begot” here expresses the origin of peoples, not a single birth. “Ludim” may be a people in Africa, distinct from Lud in Shem's line (1:17). “Lehabim” may be connected with the Libyans. “Naphtuhim” has been associated with the Nile Delta region. “Anamim” is otherwise unknown. The list treats these groups as part of Egypt's family, showing how the peoples of northeast Africa were related in the ancient understanding preserved in Genesis 10:13–14.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Plural names. The use of plural gentilic names (Ludim, Anamim, and so on) is a clear sign that ancient genealogies could describe relationships among peoples using the language of fathers and sons. This is part of what the genealogies mean by “begot” in these contexts, and it should be read accordingly.

Verse Explanation

The list of Mizraim’s descendants ends with Pathrusim and Casluhim, adding that the Philistines and the Caphtorim came from them. The note places the Philistines, later Israel’s great enemy, within the one human family.

“Pathrusim, Casluhim”

Two more peoples complete the list of Mizraim's descendants. “Pathrusim” refers to the people of Pathros, a name for Upper Egypt in the south (Isaiah 11:11; Jeremiah 44:1). “Casluhim” is otherwise unknown.

“(from whom came the Philistines and the Caphtorim).”

The parenthesis adds a note of great significance for Israel's later history. “The Philistines” were to become Israel's fiercest enemy in the days of Saul and David. Here they are placed in the family of nations descended from Noah. “The Caphtorim” were the people of Caphtor, usually identified with Crete or the Aegean region. The note does not single the Philistines out for condemnation. It simply records their origin, reminding Israel that even its enemies were part of the human family God made and governs.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Textual and historical question. Other passages say that the Philistines came from Caphtor (Amos 9:7; Jeremiah 47:4; Deuteronomy 2:23). Because of this, some scholars suggest that the parenthetical note originally stood after “Caphtorim” rather than after “Casluhim,” or that the Philistines had connections with both groups. Chronicles closely parallels Genesis 10:14. The two passages have small wording differences in the NKJV, but both place the Philistine-origin notice after Casluhim and include Caphtorim within its parentheses. The relationship may involve migration through more than one region, and the evidence does not settle the details.

Cross-reference. Amos 9:7 places the Philistines' migration from Caphtor alongside Israel's exodus from Egypt as acts of the same sovereign God: “Did I not bring up Israel from the land of Egypt, The Philistines from Caphtor, And the Syrians from Kir?”

From this point the names are given as peoples in the singular form with “the,” a common way of naming a people in Hebrew. “The Jebusite” refers to the inhabitants of Jebus, the city later captured by David and made his capital, Jerusalem (1 Chronicles 11:4). “The Amorite” was one of the largest and most widespread peoples of Canaan, often used as a name for the land's inhabitants generally. “The Girgashite” is a smaller group named in the lists of Canaan's peoples (Joshua 3:10). For readers of Chronicles, the mention of the Jebusites would recall that the city at the center of their worship had once belonged to Canaan's descendants before God gave it to David.

Lists of peoples. The peoples of Canaan often appear in lists of six or seven in the Pentateuch and Joshua (for example, Deuteronomy 7:1; Joshua 3:10). Genesis 10 and Chronicles name a fuller range, including peoples of Phoenicia and Syria to the north.

The record turns from family lists to a list of rulers. “The land of Edom” was the territory south and east of the Dead Sea, including Mount Seir. Edom had a form of kingship early in its history. “before a king reigned over the children of Israel:”

This phrase gives the list its significance for Chronicles. Edom had kings long before Israel did. The words look back from a time when Israel had kings, and they anticipate the book's central concern with Israel's own kingship under David and his house. God had promised Abraham and Jacob that kings would come from them (Genesis 17:6; 35:11). The note does not imply that Edom was more blessed because it had kings first. It simply gives the relative order of events and prepares the reader for the story of Israel's kingdom.

“Bela the son of Beor, and the name of his city was Dinhabah.”

The first king was “Bela the son of Beor,” who ruled from “Dinhabah.” The location of Dinhabah is unknown. Each king is identified by his father or his city.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Chronology. The phrase “before a king reigned over the children of Israel” comes from Genesis 36:31. It gives relative historical order, not an absolute date. The kings of Edom cannot be dated precisely from this list.

Name distinction. Bela the son of Beor is not Balaam the son of Beor, the prophet hired by Balak (Numbers 22:5), though the similarity of the names has led some interpreters to connect them. Scripture does not identify them.

Nature of Edomite kingship. The list does not present a father-to-son dynasty. Each king comes from a different family or city, which may indicate an elective or tribal kingship.

“When Saul died, Baal-Hanan the son of Achbor reigned in his place.”

The seventh king was “Baal-Hanan the son of Achbor.” He is identified by his father's name rather than a city. His name means ‘Baal is gracious,’ reflecting the worship of Baal common among the peoples around Israel. The list records his reign without comment on his religion or character. Chronicles includes these kings as historical rulers, not as examples to be followed or condemned.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Name distinction. Another Baal-Hanan, a Gederite, served as David's official over olive and sycamore trees (1 Chronicles 27:28). The names are the same; the men are different.

Verse Explanation

Hadad’s death is noted, and the record turns to the chiefs of Edom, beginning with Timnah, Aliah, and Jetheth. Edom’s history is summarized through both its kings and its clan chiefs.

“Hadad died also.”

The list of kings closes with the death of the last king. Genesis 36:39 does not record Hadad's death; Chronicles adds this note, completing the list. Whether the note implies that kingship in Edom ended with Hadad, or simply rounds off the record, is not stated.

“And the chiefs of Edom were Chief Timnah, Chief Aliah, Chief Jetheth,”

Chronicles turns to the “chiefs of Edom.” The word “Chief” refers to the head of a clan or tribal division. Genesis introduces this list as “the names of the chiefs of Esau, according to their families and their places, by their names” (Genesis 36:40). The names may refer both to clan leaders and to the clans or regions they led. “Timnah” recalls Timna, Lotan's sister and Amalek's mother (1:36, 39). “Aliah” and “Jetheth” are otherwise unknown. Edom's history is summarized through both its kings and its clan chiefs.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Vocabulary. “Chief” translates alluph, a term used especially of Edom's clan leaders (Genesis 36:15–43; Exodus 15:15). The word is related to eleph, which can mean ‘thousand’ or ‘clan,’ suggesting the leader of a clan unit.

Spelling. Genesis 36:40 reads “Alvah” for “Aliah.”

Relationship to kingship. Whether the chiefs ruled after the kings, alongside them, or in different regions is not clear. The phrase “according to their dwelling places” (Genesis 36:43) suggests that the list may be geographical as well as genealogical.

Verse Explanation

The list ends with Magdiel and Iram and the summary, “These were the chiefs of Edom.” With Edom’s record complete, the genealogy is ready to turn to the sons of Israel.

“Chief Magdiel, and Chief Iram.”

The last two chiefs are named. Magdiel and Iram also appear in Genesis 36:43, but these parallel lists supply no further personal history.

“These were the chiefs of Edom.”

The summary closes Edom's record. The chapter that began with “Adam” (1:1) ends with the leaders of Edom. Between them, Chronicles has surveyed all humanity, the families of Noah's sons, the line from Shem to Abraham, and the families of Abraham, Ishmael, Keturah, Esau, and Seir. Edom's record is given in full before the genealogy turns to Israel. The next verse will begin, “These were the sons of Israel” (2:1). Esau's family has received its place; now the record follows Israel.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Structure. Chapter 1 moves from the universal to the particular in three stages: from Adam to Abraham (1:1–27), Abraham's families (1:28–34), and Esau's family and Edom (1:35–54). By completing the record of Esau before turning to Israel, Chronicles follows the pattern of Genesis, where Esau's genealogy in Genesis 36 precedes the story of Jacob's sons in Genesis 37.

Later history. Edom's relationship with Israel was often bitter. David subdued Edom (1 Chronicles 18:12–13), and the prophets denounced Edom's cruelty when Jerusalem fell (Obadiah 10–14; Psalm 137:7). Chronicles' genealogy, however, simply records Edom as part of the family of nations descended from Abraham and Isaac.
