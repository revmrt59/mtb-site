Verse Explanation

The sons of Israel are named, beginning with Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, and Zebulun. Chronicles presents Israel as a family of twelve sons before focusing on any one tribe.

“These were the sons of Israel:”

Chapter 1 closed with the chiefs of Edom. Chapter 2 opens with the family whose story the whole book will tell. “The sons of Israel” are Jacob's twelve sons, the ancestors of Israel's tribes. Chronicles has already called Jacob by his God-given name, Israel (1:34), and the name now passes to the family that will become the nation. For readers after the exile, many of whose tribal connections had been shaken by deportation and loss, this line restated who they were: the descendants of the twelve sons of Israel.

“Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Zebulun,”

The first six names are the sons of Leah, listed in order of birth (Genesis 35:23). “Reuben” was the firstborn, though he lost the rights of the firstborn because of his sin against his father (1 Chronicles 5:1). “Levi” became the priestly tribe, whose service fills much of Chronicles. “Judah,” the fourth son, is the tribe whose genealogy will occupy the rest of this chapter and much of the next two, because from Judah came David. Even here, at the start, the whole family is named before attention narrows to Judah.

Study Notes

Chronicles keeps the birth order of Leah’s six sons from Genesis 35:23 but does not follow Genesis 35:24–26 in grouping the other six sons by their mothers.

Chapters 2–8 trace the tribes of Israel, with Judah first (2:3–4:23), Levi at the center (chapter 6), and Benjamin last (chapter 8).

The community after the exile centered on Judah and Benjamin together with priests and Levites, though some from Ephraim and Manasseh also lived in Jerusalem (9:3). The placement of Judah and Benjamin at the beginning and end may reflect that setting.

Verse Explanation

The list of Israel’s sons concludes with Dan, Joseph, Benjamin, Naphtali, Gad, and Asher. Naming all twelve keeps the whole people in view, even though Judah will receive the most attention.

“Dan, Joseph, Benjamin, Naphtali, Gad, and Asher.”

The remaining six sons are named. “Joseph” and “Benjamin” were the sons of Rachel, Jacob's beloved wife. “Dan” and “Naphtali” were born to Bilhah, Rachel's servant, and “Gad” and “Asher” to Zilpah, Leah's servant (Genesis 35:24–26). By naming all twelve, Chronicles keeps the whole people of Israel in view. Judah will receive the most attention, but it never stands alone. The book's later concern with “all Israel” begins here, with twelve brothers named together as one family.

Study Notes

Dan is placed before Joseph and Benjamin rather than with his full brother Naphtali. The reason for the arrangement is not stated.

Some suggest that Dan, Rachel’s son through her maid Bilhah (Genesis 30:3–6), is placed with Rachel’s own sons because Rachel counted him as her own; others connect his position with his seventh place in Jacob’s blessing (Genesis 49:16).

Joseph is named as one son, though his descendants became two tribes, Ephraim and Manasseh (Genesis 48:5). Chronicles later explains that the birthright passed to Joseph's sons while leadership came through Judah (1 Chronicles 5:1–2).

“The sons of Judah were Er, Onan, and Shelah.”

The genealogy now turns to Judah, the tribe of David. Judah's first three sons are named. Their story is told in Genesis 38.

“These three were born to him by the daughter of Shua, the Canaanitess.”

Their mother was “the daughter of Shua,” a Canaanite woman (Genesis 38:2). Chronicles notes her Canaanite background without comment. The royal tribe of Judah included Canaanite ancestry from its earliest generation, a reminder that Israel's family history was never as tidy or isolated as later pride might wish.

“Er, the firstborn of Judah, was wicked in the sight of the LORD; so He killed him.”

The verse adds a sobering note. “Er, the firstborn” was “wicked in the sight of the LORD,” and the LORD put him to death (Genesis 38:7). Scripture does not say what Er's wickedness was. What matters is the verdict: the LORD saw it, judged it, and acted. The first notice of moral evaluation in the tribal genealogies concerns a son of Judah, the royal tribe. Belonging to Judah did not place anyone beyond God's judgment. The genealogy that leads toward David begins by reminding the reader that God is holy and that privilege does not excuse sin.

Study Notes

“The daughter of Shua” renders the Hebrew bat-shua', which can also be read as a personal name, Bathshua. The same form is used for Bathsheba in 1 Chronicles 3:5. Genesis 38:2 indicates that Shua was the name of her father.

Chronicles does not mention that Onan was also put to death by the LORD (Genesis 38:8–10). It records only Er's judgment, perhaps because Er was the firstborn.

Shelah's descendants are listed later, in 4:21–23, and became the clan of the Shelanites (Numbers 26:20).

Verse Explanation

Tamar, Judah’s daughter-in-law, bore him Perez and Zerah, bringing Judah’s sons to five. The brief note recalls the troubled events of Genesis 38 through which the family continued, without approving them.

“And Tamar, his daughter-in-law, bore him Perez and Zerah.”

The line continues through one of the most uncomfortable episodes in Genesis. “Tamar” was the widow of Judah's son Er. When Judah failed to give her his son Shelah as he had promised, she disguised herself and conceived twins by Judah himself (Genesis 38:11–30). Chronicles compresses that story into a single phrase: “his daughter-in-law.” The words are enough to recall what happened. Chronicles does not hide the circumstances and does not approve them. It simply states that “Perez and Zerah” were born to Judah through Tamar.

The line through Perez will lead to David (2:5, 9–15). Ruth 4 also traces David's ancestry through Perez and remembers Tamar by name (Ruth 4:12, 18). Matthew names her in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:3). God worked His purposes through a family whose history included deception and failure, and the genealogies do not pretend otherwise.

“All the sons of Judah were five.”

The verse totals Judah's sons: Er, Onan, Shelah, Perez, and Zerah. The count includes the two who died, so all five are remembered.

Study Notes

Judah himself acknowledged of Tamar, “She has been more righteous than I” (Genesis 38:26).

“The sons of Zerah were Zimri, Ethan, Heman, Calcol, and Dara—five of them in all.”

Before following Perez's line, the record lists the family of “Zerah,” Perez's twin. His five sons are named. “Zimri” is probably the same man called Zabdi in Joshua 7:1, the grandfather of Achan. “Ethan, Heman, Calcol,” and “Dara” have names that appear in 1 Kings 4:31, where Solomon's wisdom is said to surpass that of “Ethan the Ezrahite, and Heman, Chalcol, and Darda.” Psalm 88 is attributed to Heman the Ezrahite and Psalm 89 to Ethan the Ezrahite. “Ezrahite” may mean ‘descendant of Zerah.’ If these are the same men, Zerah's family was remembered for wisdom. The record follows Judah's other line through Tamar before returning to Perez.

Study Notes

“Dara” corresponds to “Darda” in 1 Kings 4:31. Some Hebrew and Greek manuscripts, along with the ancient Syriac translation and the Latin Vulgate translation, read “Darda” in this verse as well, and some translations (for example, the NIV and NLT) adopt that form. The standard Hebrew text reads Dara.

1 Kings 4:31 describes the wise men it names as “the sons of Mahol.” Mahol may be a personal name. Because the same Hebrew word (machol) means ‘dance’ (Psalm 30:11), some have taken the phrase to mean members of a guild of musicians or dancers rather than literal sons, though few interpreters have adopted this view.

Heman and Ethan are also names of Levitical singers in David's time (1 Chronicles 6:33, 44; 15:19). The relationship among these figures is uncertain.

Zimri is a different man from Zimri the Simeonite (Numbers 25:14) and Zimri the king of Israel (1 Kings 16:9–20).

Verse Explanation

Carmi’s son Achar is remembered as the troubler of Israel, who transgressed in the accursed thing. He is the Achan of Joshua 7, and the form of his name here fits his description, showing that the family record also preserves accountability for unfaithfulness.

“The son of Carmi was Achar,”

The genealogy moves to a notorious member of Zerah's family. “Carmi” was apparently the son of Zimri (Joshua 7:1 traces Achan's descent from Zerah through Zabdi and Carmi). His son was “Achar.” Joshua calls him Achan. Chronicles uses the form “Achar,” which sounds like the Hebrew word for ‘trouble.’

“the troubler of Israel,”

His name fits his description. Achar is remembered as “the troubler of Israel.” At Jericho he took for himself silver, gold, and a garment that God had devoted to destruction, and Israel was defeated at Ai because of his sin (Joshua 7:1–26). Joshua said to him, “Why have you troubled us? The LORD will trouble you this day” (Joshua 7:25). The place of his execution was called the Valley of Achor, ‘valley of trouble.’

“who transgressed in the accursed thing.”

Chronicles states the nature of his sin. “Transgressed” describes unfaithfulness, a breach of trust against God. “The accursed thing” refers to the spoil of Jericho that God had devoted to Himself and forbidden Israel to take (Joshua 6:17–19). Achar's sin brought trouble on the whole nation. The family record of Judah preserves his name as a warning. A genealogy that leads to David does not omit those whose unfaithfulness harmed God's people.

Study Notes

In Hebrew, the name Achar ('akar) and the word translated “troubler” ('oker) come from the same word family. The name Achor, used for the valley in Joshua 7:26, belongs to that word family as well. Some translations of Chronicles read “Achan” here, as in Joshua.

“Transgressed” translates the Hebrew verb ma'al, a key word in Chronicles for unfaithfulness toward God. The same verb describes the unfaithfulness behind the exile of the northern tribes (5:25) and Saul’s death (10:13), and the related noun describes the unfaithfulness for which Judah was exiled (9:1). Its first appearance in the book is in connection with Achar.

This brief note completes the record of Zerah's descendants. “Ethan,” one of Zerah's sons (2:6), had a son named “Azariah.” Nothing more is known of him. Azariah was a common name in Israel, meaning ‘the LORD has helped.’ With this note, the record of Zerah's family closes, and the genealogy returns to Perez's son Hezron, whose line leads to David. Even a single name like Azariah's is preserved; the record remembers people about whom Scripture tells us nothing else.

Study Notes

The Old Testament mentions more than twenty men named Azariah, and roughly seventeen of them appear in Chronicles. This Azariah, a descendant of Zerah, is different from the priests, kings, and others who bore the same name.

The genealogy now reaches the three sons of “Hezron” who organize most of the rest of the chapter. “Jerahmeel” was the firstborn (2:25). The chapter will trace all three lines: Ram's line to David (2:10–17), Caleb's family (2:18–24, 42–55), and Jerahmeel's family (2:25–41). David's ancestry is placed within the wider family of Judah, among brothers and cousins who also had their place in the tribe.

A related form of the name, “Chelub,” appears in 4:11.

Ram fathered Amminadab, and Amminadab fathered Nahshon, leader of the children of Judah. The line toward David passes through a recognized tribal leader of the wilderness generation (Numbers 1:7; 2:3).

“Ram begot Amminadab, and Amminadab begot Nahshon,”

The line toward David begins with “Ram,” Hezron's son. Ram fathered “Amminadab,” and Amminadab fathered “Nahshon.” Amminadab was the father-in-law of Aaron, since Aaron married “Elisheba, daughter of Amminadab, sister of Nahshon” (Exodus 6:23). The family of Judah's future kings was linked by marriage to the family of Israel's first high priest.

“leader of the children of Judah;”

Nahshon is given a title: “leader of the children of Judah.” In the wilderness, Nahshon led the tribe of Judah. He is named among the tribal leaders who assisted Moses with the census (Numbers 1:7), and he led Judah's camp when Israel marched (Numbers 2:3). He brought the first offering at the dedication of the tabernacle altar (Numbers 7:12). The line toward David passed through a recognized leader of the wilderness generation.

Study Notes

From Nahshon to David the line has only four more names (Salma, Boaz, Obed, Jesse), spanning the period from the wilderness to the early monarchy.

Some interpreters suggest the list may be selective, though Ruth 4:20–22 gives the same sequence. The genealogy does not supply ages, so its chronological span cannot be calculated from the names alone.

“Ozem the sixth,”

“Ozem” is the sixth son. Like Nethanel and Raddai, he is otherwise unknown.

“and David the seventh.”

The list reaches its goal with “David the seventh.” David, the shepherd chosen by God, anointed by Samuel, and made king over all Israel, is the reason this line has been traced. Chronicles will devote most of the remainder of the book to his reign (chapters 10–29). Here, in the genealogy, he is simply the youngest son of Jesse, numbered after his brothers. God's choice of the youngest son echoes His pattern of choosing those whom people overlook (1 Samuel 16:11–13).

1 Samuel indicates that Jesse had eight sons. Seven passed before Samuel, and David was the eighth (1 Samuel 16:10–11; 17:12). Chronicles counts David as the seventh. The chapter does not explain the difference.

Study Notes

Several explanations have been proposed for the difference between this verse and 1 Samuel 16:10–11 and 17:12. One brother may have died young or without children and so was omitted from the genealogy.

1 Chronicles 27:18 names “Elihu, one of David’s brothers,” who is not listed here, though Elihu may be another form of Eliab, and the ancient Greek translation known as the Septuagint reads Eliab in that verse.

An ancient translation of this verse into the Syriac language adds Elihu as the seventh son and counts David as the eighth.

“Now their sisters were Zeruiah and Abigail.”

The genealogy names Jesse's daughters, an unusual inclusion. “Zeruiah” and “Abigail” were David's sisters. Their names matter because of the sons they bore, who played major roles in David's reign.

“And the sons of Zeruiah were Abishai, Joab, and Asahel—three.”

Zeruiah's three sons were among David's most important military men. “Joab” became commander of David's army, fierce, capable, and often ruthless (1 Chronicles 11:6; 18:15). “Abishai” was a leading warrior and commander (1 Chronicles 11:20; 18:12). “Asahel” was famous for his speed, “as fleet of foot as a wild gazelle” (2 Samuel 2:18), and was killed by Abner. David's own nephews were among the men who helped establish his kingdom. Chronicles identifies them as part of Judah's family record.

“And the families of the scribes who dwelt at Jabez”

The chapter ends with “the families of the scribes” who lived at “Jabez,” a town apparently in Judah. “Scribes” were people skilled in writing, copying, and record-keeping. The location of Jabez is unknown. The name is also borne by the man whose prayer is recorded in 4:9–10, but the text does not connect them.

“were the Tirathites, the Shimeathites, and the Suchathites.”

Three scribal families are named. Their names are otherwise unknown.

“These were the Kenites who came from Hammath, the father of the house of Rechab.”

The scribal families are identified as “Kenites.” The Kenites were a non-Israelite people associated with Moses's father-in-law, who settled among the people of Judah (Judges 1:16). They came “from Hammath, the father of the house of Rechab.” The Rechabites were later known for their faithfulness to their ancestor's commands; Jeremiah held them up as an example of obedience (Jeremiah 35:1–19). Judah's record makes room for these scribal families and their associated peoples among its households. The chapter that began with the twelve sons of Israel ends with Kenite scribes, people of non-Israelite origin who were counted among Judah's families.

Study Notes

The meaning of the three family names is uncertain. The Latin Vulgate translates them as descriptions rather than names (‘singing and resounding and dwelling in tents’), and some later interpreters took the first to mean gatekeepers. Translations differ.

Jehonadab the son of Rechab supported Jehu against the worship of Baal (2 Kings 10:15–23), and his descendants refused to drink wine or build houses because of his command (Jeremiah 35:6–10). The relationship between Hammath, Rechab, and these scribal families is not explained further.
