NLT

1 Chronicles 3:1 These are the sons of David who were born in Hebron: The oldest was Amnon, whose mother was Ahinoam from Jezreel.The second was Daniel, whose mother was Abigail from Carmel. (NLT)

Main Idea

The sons of David born in Hebron begin with Amnon, the firstborn, by Ahinoam the Jezreelitess, and Daniel, the second, by Abigail the Carmelitess. Chronicles now turns from David’s ancestry to his own household.

Verse Explanation

The sons of David born in Hebron begin with Amnon, the firstborn, by Ahinoam the Jezreelitess, and Daniel, the second, by Abigail the Carmelitess. Chronicles now turns from David’s ancestry to his own household.

“Now these were the sons of David who were born to him in Hebron:”

Chapter 2 traced Judah's families and brought the line to David, the seventh son of Jesse (2:15). Chapter 3 turns from David's ancestry to his own household. This is the royal center of Judah's genealogy. The reason the family matters is stated later in the book, when God promises David, “I will set up your seed after you, who will be of your sons; and I will establish his kingdom” (1 Chronicles 17:11). The list of David's sons is the record of the family to which that promise was given.

“Hebron” was the city in the hill country of Judah where David first reigned as king, before he captured Jerusalem (2 Samuel 5:5). The first six sons were born there.

“The firstborn was Amnon, by Ahinoam the Jezreelitess;”

David's firstborn was “Amnon,” son of “Ahinoam the Jezreelitess,” whom David married during his years as a fugitive (1 Samuel 25:43). Her hometown, Jezreel, was a town in the hill country of Judah (Joshua 15:56), not the better-known Jezreel in the north. Amnon's story is one of the darkest in David's household: he violated his half-sister Tamar and was later killed by her brother Absalom (2 Samuel 13). Chronicles does not retell those events here, but the name recalls them.

“the second, Daniel, by Abigail the Carmelitess;”

The second son was “Daniel,” born to “Abigail the Carmelitess.” Abigail was the wise and courageous woman who turned David from taking revenge on her husband Nabal, and who became David's wife after Nabal's death (1 Samuel 25). She is called “the Carmelitess” because Nabal's business was in Carmel in Judah (1 Samuel 25:2). Nothing is recorded about Daniel's life.

Study Notes

2 Samuel 3:3 calls Abigail's son “Chileab.” Daniel and Chileab may be two names for the same son. The reason for the difference is not given. This Daniel is unrelated to the prophet Daniel.

The Hebron list names every son together with his mother. This was important in a household with several wives, since a son's mother affected his standing and relationships in the family, as the later conflicts over succession show (1 Kings 1:5–11).

Verses 1–4 closely follow 2 Samuel 3:2–5, with the note on David's reign drawn from 2 Samuel 5:5.

Verse Explanation

The third son is Absalom, whose mother Maacah was the daughter of Talmai king of Geshur, and the fourth is Adonijah the son of Haggith. These names recall sons whose later conflicts are narrated in Samuel and Kings, though Chronicles does not retell them here.

“the third, Absalom the son of Maacah, the daughter of Talmai, king of Geshur;”

The third son was “Absalom.” His mother, “Maacah,” was a princess, the daughter of “Talmai, king of Geshur,” a small Aramean kingdom northeast of the Sea of Galilee. David's marriage to her was likely a political alliance. Absalom's story is told at length in 2 Samuel 13–18: he killed Amnon to avenge Tamar, fled to his grandfather in Geshur (2 Samuel 13:37–38), returned, and later led a rebellion that drove David from Jerusalem. He was killed by Joab, and David mourned him bitterly (2 Samuel 18:33). Chronicles names him without retelling that history.

“the fourth, Adonijah the son of Haggith;”

The fourth son was “Adonijah,” son of “Haggith.” At the end of David's life, Adonijah tried to make himself king, saying, “I will be king” (1 Kings 1:5). David instead confirmed Solomon as his successor, and Adonijah was later executed (1 Kings 1–2). These names recall sons whose later conflicts are narrated in Samuel and Kings. A royal household with a troubled history stands at the heart of the line through which God promised to work.

Study Notes

Geshur appears again in 2:23: “Geshur and Syria took from them the towns of Jair.”

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

These six sons were born in Hebron, where David reigned seven years and six months, and he then reigned thirty-three years in Jerusalem. The verse anchors David’s family in the two stages of his reign.

“These six were born to him in Hebron.”

The verse sums up the Hebron sons. Six sons were born during David's years in Hebron.

“There he reigned seven years and six months,”

David reigned in Hebron “seven years and six months,” first over Judah only, while Saul's son Ishbosheth reigned over the northern tribes (2 Samuel 2:4, 8–11). Chronicles passes over that period of divided rule, but the figure preserves its length.

“and in Jerusalem he reigned thirty-three years.”

After the tribes of Israel came to Hebron and made David king over all Israel, he captured Jerusalem and reigned there “thirty-three years” (1 Chronicles 11:1–9). The total reign was forty years (1 Chronicles 29:27). The verse anchors David's family in the two stages of his reign. It also provides one of the few explicit chronological figures in the genealogies.

Study Notes

2 Samuel 5:4–5 gives the same figures and adds that David “was thirty years old when he began to reign.” 1 Chronicles 29:27 rounds the Hebron period to seven years.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

In Jerusalem four sons were born to David by Bathshua the daughter of Ammiel: Shimea, Shobab, Nathan, and Solomon. Bathshua is another form of the name Bathsheba, and Solomon, named last among her sons, is the one through whom the royal line continues.

“And these were born to him in Jerusalem:”

The list continues with the sons born in Jerusalem, David's capital. The city that became the center of Israel's worship was also where most of David's family grew up.

“Shimea, Shobab, Nathan, and Solomon—four by Bathshua the daughter of Ammiel.”

Four sons were born to David by “Bathshua the daughter of Ammiel.” “Bathshua” is another form of the name Bathsheba, and “Ammiel” corresponds to Eliam, her father's name in 2 Samuel 11:3. The way David took Bathsheba from Uriah the Hittite, and the death of their first child, are told in 2 Samuel 11–12. Chronicles does not mention those events, but neither does it deny them. Bathsheba is remembered here as the mother of four sons.

“Nathan” became the ancestor of Jesus' line in Luke's genealogy (Luke 3:31). “Solomon,” named last among Bathshua's sons, is the one through whom the royal line continues (3:10). God's love for Solomon was announced at his birth (2 Samuel 12:24–25), and God chose him to build the temple (1 Chronicles 22:9–10; 28:5–6). From the household of David's gravest sin came the son who would build the LORD's house.

Study Notes

Solomon is listed last among Bathshua's sons, but 2 Samuel 12:24 suggests that he was born soon after the death of the first child. The order here may reflect something other than birth order, such as listing the most important son last to lead into the royal line. The text does not explain it.

2 Samuel 5:14 and 1 Chronicles 14:4 call the first son “Shammua.”

The Hebrew forms are bat-sheva' (Bathsheba) and bat-shua' (Bathshua). The same Hebrew form bat-shua' appears in 2:3 for Judah’s Canaanite wife, where the NKJV translates it “the daughter of Shua.”

Verse Explanation

The list ends with Elishama, Eliada, and Eliphelet, bringing these Jerusalem sons to nine in all. The names Elishama and Eliphelet appear twice in the list, a detail the text records without explanation.

“Elishama, Eliada, and Eliphelet—”

The list ends with three more sons. The names “Elishama” and “Eliphelet” appear a second time in the list (see 3:6). The text records the repetition without explanation. Families sometimes gave a name to a later son after an earlier son of the same name had died, which may explain the repetition here.

“nine in all.”

The count totals the sons in this second Jerusalem group: Ibhar, Elishama, Eliphelet, Nogah, Nepheg, Japhia, Elishama, Eliada, and Eliphelet. With the four sons of Bathshua, David had thirteen sons born in Jerusalem, besides the six born in Hebron.

Study Notes

1 Chronicles 14:7 reads “Beeliada” where this verse and 2 Samuel 5:16 read “Eliada.” Beeliada means ‘the lord knows’ (the Hebrew word ba'al means ‘lord’), and Eliada means ‘God knows.’

Some scholars propose that Beeliada is the earlier form and that Baal was later replaced with El because of the name’s association with the Canaanite god. Other names show a similar avoidance of Baal with a different substitute: Saul’s son Esh-Baal (1 Chronicles 8:33) is called Ishbosheth in 2 Samuel 2:8, with the Hebrew word bosheth (‘shame’) in place of Baal.

The summary closes the list of David’s named sons, acknowledges further sons by his concubines who are not named here, and adds Tamar their sister, whose painful story is told in 2 Samuel 13.

“These were all the sons of David,”

The summary closes the list of David's named sons, nineteen in all.

“besides the sons of the concubines,”

The verse acknowledges that David also had sons by his “concubines,” secondary wives (2 Samuel 5:13; 15:16). They are not named. Chronicles is selective, naming the sons of David's wives and passing over others.

“and Tamar their sister.”

The verse ends by naming one daughter, “Tamar their sister.” David had other daughters (2 Samuel 5:13), but only Tamar is named. Her painful story is told in 2 Samuel 13: her half-brother Amnon violated her, and her full brother Absalom avenged her by killing him. By naming her, Chronicles remembers her. It does not retell what happened, but readers who knew the history would recognize the name.

Study Notes

Chronicles names women in its genealogies more often than might be expected, including mothers, wives, and daughters (2:4, 16–17, 26, 29, 34–35, 46, 48–49; 3:1–5, 9). Tamar's inclusion is consistent with that pattern.

Main Idea

The royal line from Solomon begins with Rehoboam, Abijah, Asa, and Jehoshaphat, each named as the son of the one before. The genealogy now follows the kings of Judah descended from David.

Verse Explanation

The royal line from Solomon begins with Rehoboam, Abijah, Asa, and Jehoshaphat, each named as the son of the one before. The genealogy now follows the kings of Judah descended from David.

“Solomon's son was Rehoboam;”

The list now follows the royal line from “Solomon.” His son “Rehoboam” became king after him. Early in his reign the ten northern tribes rejected him and made Jeroboam king, dividing the kingdom (2 Chronicles 10). From this point, the kings of David's line ruled only Judah, with Benjamin.

“Abijah was his son, Asa his son, Jehoshaphat his son,”

The line continues with “Abijah,” “Asa,” and “Jehoshaphat,” each named as the son of the one before. The repeated words “his son” give the list its steady rhythm. Asa and Jehoshaphat were among the kings who sought the LORD and led reforms in Judah (2 Chronicles 14–20), though both also had failures. The genealogy now follows the kings of Judah descended from David, the line through which God kept His promise to give David a lasting house.

Study Notes

1 Kings 15:1 calls Abijah “Abijam.”

Verses 10–14 list fifteen kings from Rehoboam to Josiah in an unbroken chain of fathers and sons, without the years of reign or evaluations supplied in Kings and 2 Chronicles.

Main Idea

Joram, Ahaziah, and Joash continue the royal line. The list traces descent and therefore does not include Athaliah, who ruled Judah for a time between Ahaziah and Joash (2 Kings 11).

Verse Explanation

Joram, Ahaziah, and Joash continue the royal line. The list traces descent and therefore does not include Athaliah, who ruled Judah for a time between Ahaziah and Joash (2 Kings 11).

“Joram his son, Ahaziah his son, Joash his son,”

The royal line continues. “Joram” (also called Jehoram) married Athaliah, the daughter of Ahab, and “did evil in the sight of the LORD” (2 Chronicles 21:6). His son “Ahaziah” reigned only one year before he was killed by Jehu (2 Kings 9:27). “Joash” was Ahaziah's son. When Ahaziah died, his mother Athaliah seized power and tried to destroy all the royal heirs (2 Kings 11:1). The infant Joash was hidden in the temple for six years, while “Athaliah reigned over the land” (2 Kings 11:3). The list traces descent and therefore does not include Athaliah. Her absence from this list does not mean she never ruled. It means she was not part of David's line of descent.

The survival of Joash is one of the most dramatic moments in the history of David's house. The entire royal line nearly ended. God preserved it through one hidden child.

Study Notes

Matthew 1:8 reads “Joram begot Uzziah,” omitting Ahaziah, Joash, and Amaziah, whom Chronicles names (3:11–12).

Kings uses both forms for this king: “Jehoram the son of Jehoshaphat” (2 Kings 8:16) and “Joram” (2 Kings 8:21, 24). 2 Chronicles 21 uses “Jehoram.”

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Ahaz, Hezekiah, and Manasseh follow in the royal succession. Faithful and unfaithful kings stand side by side in the same line, showing that royal descent did not guarantee obedience to God.

“Ahaz his son, Hezekiah his son, Manasseh his son,”

Three kings follow. “Ahaz” was one of Judah's worst kings. He worshiped other gods, sacrificed his children in the fire, and closed the doors of the temple (2 Chronicles 28:1–4, 24). His son “Hezekiah” was one of Judah's best. He reopened and cleansed the temple, restored worship, and trusted the LORD when Assyria threatened Jerusalem (2 Chronicles 29–32). Hezekiah's son “Manasseh” undid his father's reforms and led Judah into worse evil than the nations, yet when he was taken captive he humbled himself, and God restored him (2 Chronicles 33:1–13).

Faithful and unfaithful kings stand side by side in the same line. Royal descent did not guarantee obedience to God. Each king was responsible for his own response to the LORD, and the genealogy alone does not tell the reader which kings walked in God's ways.

Study Notes

Only Chronicles records Manasseh's repentance and restoration (2 Chronicles 33:12–13). Kings records his evil and its consequences without mentioning his repentance (2 Kings 21:1–18).

His son Josiah became king at eight years old and became one of Judah's most faithful kings. 1 Chronicles 3:15
NKJV

1 Chronicles 3:15 The sons of Josiah were Johanan the firstborn, the second Jehoiakim, the third Zedekiah, and the fourth Shallum.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Chronicles 3:15 The sons of Josiah were Johanan (the oldest), Jehoiakim (the second), Zedekiah (the third), and Jehoahaz (the fourth). (NLT)

Main Idea

Josiah’s sons are listed as Johanan the firstborn, Jehoiakim the second, Zedekiah the third, and Shallum the fourth. They are named as a family, not in the order they reigned, and Shallum is the king known as Jehoahaz (Jeremiah 22:11).

Verse Explanation

Josiah’s sons are listed as Johanan the firstborn, Jehoiakim the second, Zedekiah the third, and Shallum the fourth. They are named as a family, not in the order they reigned, and Shallum is the king known as Jehoahaz (Jeremiah 22:11).

“The sons of Josiah were Johanan the firstborn,”

The form of the list changes. Instead of a single son following each king, Josiah's sons are listed together. “Johanan the firstborn” is otherwise unknown. He never reigned, and he may have died before his father.

“the second Jehoiakim,”

“Jehoiakim,” originally named Eliakim, was made king by Pharaoh Necho after his brother Jehoahaz was deposed (2 Kings 23:34). He reigned eleven years and did evil (2 Kings 23:36–37).

“the third Zedekiah,”

“Zedekiah,” originally named Mattaniah, was the last king of Judah. Nebuchadnezzar made him king after Jehoiachin was taken to Babylon (2 Kings 24:17). His rebellion led to the destruction of Jerusalem and the temple (2 Kings 25:1–7).

“and the fourth Shallum.”

“Shallum” is the king known as Jehoahaz, who reigned three months after Josiah's death before Pharaoh Necho deposed him and took him to Egypt (2 Kings 23:30–34). Jeremiah calls him “Shallum the son of Josiah” (Jeremiah 22:11).

The sons are named as a family, not in the order they reigned. The actual order of their reigns was Shallum (Jehoahaz), then Jehoiakim, then Jehoiakim's son Jehoiachin, and finally Zedekiah.

Study Notes

The ages given in Kings show that the list is not strictly in birth order either. Jehoahaz (Shallum) was twenty-three when he became king (2 Kings 23:31), and Jehoiakim was twenty-five when he succeeded him three months later (2 Kings 23:36), so Jehoiakim was older. Zedekiah was twenty-one when he became king eleven years later (2 Kings 24:18), so he was younger than Shallum. Shallum and Zedekiah had the same mother, Hamutal (2 Kings 23:31; 24:18). The reason for the order in this verse is not stated.

“The sons of Jehoiakim were Jeconiah his son”

The record follows “Jehoiakim” into the next generation. His son “Jeconiah” is also called Jehoiachin and, by Jeremiah, Coniah (Jeremiah 22:24). He reigned three months before Nebuchadnezzar took him captive to Babylon along with the royal household and many of Jerusalem's leaders (2 Kings 24:8–15). He remained a captive for thirty-seven years until he was released and given a place at the king's table (2 Kings 25:27–30).

“and Zedekiah his son.”

A second name follows: “Zedekiah his son.” The phrase “his son” may refer to Jehoiakim, making this Zedekiah another son of Jehoiakim, or to Jeconiah, making him Jeconiah's son. The relationship of this Zedekiah to others of that name in the surrounding list should not be assumed. King Zedekiah was Josiah's son (3:15) and Jehoiachin's uncle (2 Kings 24:17).

Study Notes

Some interpreters identify the Zedekiah of verse 16 with King Zedekiah, taking “his son” to mean Jeconiah’s successor rather than his child. Others regard him as a different, otherwise unknown Zedekiah. The text does not settle the question.

In 2 Chronicles 36:10 the Hebrew calls King Zedekiah ‘his brother’ (achiv) in a verse about Jehoiachin, although Zedekiah was Jehoiachin’s uncle (2 Kings 24:17). The Hebrew word for brother can also mean ‘relative.’ The NKJV prints “Jehoiakim’s brother,” which gives Zedekiah’s actual relationship as a son of Josiah (3:15). This use of the word does not by itself show who the Zedekiah of verse 16 is.

The names Jeconiah, Jehoiachin, and Coniah all combine the divine name with the verb ‘establish,’ meaning ‘the LORD establishes’ or ‘the LORD will establish.’

“And the sons of Jeconiah were Assir,”

With this verse the record moves into the time of the exile. “Jeconiah,” the king taken captive to Babylon, had sons. The first word after his name is “Assir.” The NKJV reads it as a personal name. Many translations take the Hebrew word as describing Jeconiah himself, translating ‘Jeconiah the captive.’ The word means ‘prisoner’ or ‘captive.’ Either way, the verse places David's royal family in captivity.

“Shealtiel his son,”

The next son was “Shealtiel.” He is the father of Zerubbabel in Ezra and Haggai (Ezra 3:2; Haggai 1:1) and appears in both Gospel genealogies of Jesus (Matthew 1:12; Luke 3:27). The royal line continued in exile.

This continuation is significant. Jeremiah had pronounced judgment on Jeconiah: “Write this man down as childless,” for “none of his descendants shall prosper, Sitting on the throne of David, And ruling anymore in Judah” (Jeremiah 22:30). Jeconiah did have sons, as this verse shows. Jeremiah's word concerned the throne. None of his descendants would reign as king in Judah. Yet God did not allow the family itself to disappear.

Study Notes

Reading the Hebrew word 'assir as a name, as the NKJV does, adds a son not mentioned elsewhere; reading it as a description avoids counting an extra generation.

Babylonian administrative tablets discovered in Babylon record rations of oil issued to ‘Ya'ukin, king of the land of Judah,’ and to his five sons, during the reign of Nebuchadnezzar. These texts confirm that Jehoiachin lived in Babylon with sons during his captivity.

He led the first return from Babylon (Ezra 2:2), helped rebuild the altar and lay the foundation of the temple (Ezra 3:2, 8), and was encouraged by the prophets Haggai and Zechariah to complete the work (Haggai 1:1; Zechariah 4:9). Haggai calls him “governor of Judah” (Haggai 1:1), not king. “The sons of Zerubbabel were Meshullam, Hananiah, Shelomith their sister,”

Study Notes

Several explanations have been proposed for the difference between this verse and Ezra 3:2 and Haggai 1:1.

Shealtiel may have died childless, and Pedaiah his brother may have fathered Zerubbabel by marrying his deceased brother Shealtiel's widow (Deuteronomy 25:5–6), making Zerubbabel legally Shealtiel's son and biologically Pedaiah's. Alternatively, Zerubbabel may have been adopted, or he may have been raised by Shealtiel and counted as his heir.

None of these explanations is stated in the text. Ezra, Haggai, Matthew, and Luke all use “son of Shealtiel” (Ezra 3:2; Haggai 1:1; Matthew 1:12; Luke 3:27), which may reflect Zerubbabel's legal standing.

For readers in the community after the exile, this list mattered. They had lost their king, their independence, and for a time their temple. But David's family had not vanished. Chronicles will soon record God's promise that He would establish David's throne forever (1 Chronicles 17:11–14). The survival of David's descendants kept that promise in view. Christian readers see its fulfillment in Jesus, the Son of David, of whom the angel said, “the Lord God will give Him the throne of His father David. And He will reign over the house of Jacob forever, and of His kingdom there will be no end” (Luke 1:32–33). That fulfillment rests on God's promise and the testimony of the New Testament, not on the mere survival of names in a list.
