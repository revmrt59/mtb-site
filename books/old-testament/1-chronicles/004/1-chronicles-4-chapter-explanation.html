Main Idea

Verse Explanation

The sons of Judah are listed as Perez, Hezron, Carmi, Hur, and Shobal. These names span several generations rather than one set of brothers, introducing Judah’s wider families beyond the royal line.

“The sons of Judah were Perez, Hezron, Carmi, Hur, and Shobal.”

After the royal genealogy of chapter 3, the record returns to Judah's other families. The heading “The sons of Judah” introduces names that span several generations rather than one set of brothers. “Perez” was Judah's son (2:4), “Hezron” was Perez's son (2:5), “Hur” was a descendant of Hezron through Caleb (2:19), and “Shobal” was Hur's son (2:50). The list therefore traces a line of descent within Judah, using “sons” in the broad sense of descendants. “Carmi” is unexpected here, since the Carmi of 2:7 belonged to Zerah's line. Many interpreters think the name stands for Caleb (Chelubai), Hur's father, which would make the list a straight line from Perez to Shobal. Either way, the verse introduces Judah's wider families beyond the royal household. After giving the line of kings its place, Chronicles turns to the ordinary households of the tribe.

Study Notes

The Hebrew name Carmi (karmi) and Chelubai (kelubai, 2:9) share some letters, and a copying change is possible.

Another possibility is that Carmi represents a different clan here. Translations generally retain “Carmi,” and the question remains open.

Joshua 7:1 gives Carmi's own descent in full: “Achan the son of Carmi, the son of Zabdi, the son of Zerah.”

“These were the families of the Zorathites.”

These families made up “the Zorathites,” the people of Zorah, a town in the lowland of Judah (2:53). Chronicles links named ancestors with a clan known by its place of settlement. Zorah was the hometown of Samson's father Manoah (Judges 13:2), though Samson belonged to the tribe of Dan. These lists also associate families of Judah with Zorah.

Study Notes

Chapter 2:53 says that the Zorathites came from the families of Kirjath Jearim, descended from Shobal. This verse gives more detail about the same branch.

Chronicles often returns to a family to add information, which suggests that the genealogies draw on more than one source or record.

“These were the sons of the father of Etam:”

The list names the family of “the father of Etam.” Etam was a town near Bethlehem, later fortified by Rehoboam (2 Chronicles 11:6). As elsewhere, “the father of” a town likely means its founding or leading ancestor.

“Jezreel, Ishma, and Idbash;”

Three sons are named. “Jezreel” is also the name of a town in the hill country of Judah (Joshua 15:56), the hometown of David's wife Ahinoam (3:1). “Ishma” and “Idbash” are otherwise unknown.

“and the name of their sister was Hazelelponi;”

The verse adds a sister, “Hazelelponi.” A daughter is remembered by name alongside her brothers. Her name is unusual and its meaning uncertain. Chronicles consistently includes women in its genealogies, and here a daughter whose life is otherwise unknown is given her own place.

Study Notes

The Hebrew reads literally ‘These were the father of Etam,’ which is awkward. The Septuagint, an ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, reads ‘the sons of Etam.’ The NKJV supplies “the sons of the father of Etam.” The exact relationship is uncertain, but the names clearly belong to a family associated with Etam.

1 Chronicles 4:9–10 — The Prayer of Jabez

1 Chronicles 4:9
1 Chronicles 4:9
NKJV

1 Chronicles 4:9 Now Jabez was more honorable than his brothers, and his mother called his name Jabez, saying, "Because I bore him in pain."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Chronicles 4:9 There was a man named Jabez who was more honorable than any of his brothers. His mother named him Jabez because his birth had been so painful. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jabez is introduced as more honorable than his brothers, and his mother named him Jabez, saying she bore him in pain. His name tied his birth to pain, which gives special force to the prayer that follows.

Verse Explanation

Jabez is introduced as more honorable than his brothers, and his mother named him Jabez, saying she bore him in pain. His name tied his birth to pain, which gives special force to the prayer that follows.

“Now Jabez was more honorable than his brothers,”

Suddenly the list of names pauses for a man named “Jabez.” His family relationship is not given. He is introduced with a verdict: he “was more honorable than his brothers.” The word “honorable” comes from the root associated with weight, glory, and honor. Jabez was respected, a man of weight and good standing among his family. The text does not say how he earned that honor. It is not a description of wealth or power. It is a description of character and standing, set before the prayer that follows.

“and his mother called his name Jabez, saying, ‘Because I bore him in pain.’”

His name came from his birth. His mother named him “Jabez,” saying, “Because I bore him in pain.” The name sounds like the Hebrew word for ‘pain,’ with letters rearranged. Every time someone called him by name, they recalled the pain of his birth. Names in Israel were often thought to say something about a person. A name tied to pain might have seemed to mark Jabez for sorrow. The mother's statement recalls the word spoken to Eve: “In pain you shall bring forth children” (Genesis 3:16). The pain of childbirth was part of life in a fallen world. Jabez's name carried that reminder.

His name tied his birth to pain, which gives special force to the prayer that follows. The man whose name meant pain asked God to keep him from pain.

Study Notes

The Hebrew name Jabez (ya'betz) and the Hebrew word for pain ('otzev) are closely related: the name uses the consonants of 'otzev with the last two reversed. The mother's explanation, “I bore him in pain” (be'otzev), uses the noun itself.

The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia explains the wordplay by reading the name as if it stood for the Hebrew expression ya'tzev, ‘he causes pain.’

Genesis 3:16, “In pain you shall bring forth children,” uses a related Hebrew noun from the same root, 'etsev, rather than 'otzev.

“More honorable” translates the Hebrew word nikbad, from kabed, ‘to be heavy’ or ‘to be honored.’ Here it describes someone held in high regard.

A town named Jabez appears in 2:55 as the home of scribal families. The text does not connect the man and the town, and Keil and Delitzsch leave open whether this Jabez was the town's founding ‘father.’

Main Idea

Jabez calls on the God of Israel to bless him indeed, enlarge his territory, keep His hand with him, and keep him from evil so that he may not cause pain, and God grants what he requested. His dependent prayer is directed to God Himself, and the answer reported here is not a formula promising the same outcome to everyone who repeats his words.

Verse Explanation

Jabez calls on the God of Israel to bless him indeed, enlarge his territory, keep His hand with him, and keep him from evil so that he may not cause pain, and God grants what he requested. His dependent prayer is directed to God Himself, and the answer reported here is not a formula promising the same outcome to everyone who repeats his words.

“And Jabez called on the God of Israel saying,”

Jabez turned to God. He “called on the God of Israel,” the God of his fathers, the God who had made promises to Israel and given them their land. His prayer is directed to God Himself, not to a technique or a formula. The phrase “called on” describes earnest prayer, crying out to God for help.

“Oh, that You would bless me indeed,”

His first request was for God's blessing. “Bless me indeed” is emphatic: he asked God to bless him truly and fully. In the Old Testament, God's blessing included life, fruitfulness, provision, and His favor (Numbers 6:24–26). Jabez did not ask for blessing in general terms. He asked for God's blessing on his own life.

“and enlarge my territory,”

His second request was for land. “Territory” refers to the boundaries of his property, the land his family held. In Israel, land was the inheritance God had given to each tribe and family, and it provided for the family's life and future. Jabez asked God to enlarge it. The request had a concrete setting among Israel's families and lands. It was not a request for influence or ministry in a modern sense, though later readers may apply it by analogy to the responsibilities God entrusts to them.

“that Your hand would be with me,”

His third request was for God's presence and power. “Your hand” speaks of God's active help, guidance, and protection. Ezra later testified that the king granted his requests “according to the hand of the LORD his God upon him” (Ezra 7:6). Jabez wanted more than land. He wanted God's hand with him in whatever he received.

“and that You would keep me from evil, that I may not cause pain!”

His fourth request was for protection. “Keep me from evil” asks God to guard him from harm, trouble, or wrongdoing. “That I may not cause pain” echoes his name. The man named for pain asked God to keep him from pain, whether the pain of harm that might come to him or the pain he might cause others. His prayer reached beyond his name and the circumstances of his birth to the God who could change them.

“So God granted him what he requested.”

The account ends with God's answer. “God granted him what he requested.” The verse does not say how soon the answer came or how it was given. It simply reports that God heard and answered. A man whose name was associated with pain sought the God of Israel, and God granted his request.

The prayer of Jabez shows dependent prayer directed to God for blessing, provision, His accompanying hand, and protection. The answer reported here is not a formula promising the same outcome to everyone who repeats his words. Scripture teaches believers to ask according to God's will (1 John 5:14) and to pray, as Jesus did, “not My will, but Yours, be done” (Luke 22:42). What Jabez models is not a phrase to repeat but a heart that turned to God.

Study Notes

The prayer opens with the Hebrew particle 'im, usually ‘if.’ Here it expresses a strong wish, which the NKJV renders “Oh, that”; Gesenius lists this verse among places where 'im introduces a wish. The KJV margin gives the literal form, ‘If thou wilt.’

An alternative reading takes the prayer as a vow whose conclusion is left unstated, like Jacob's vow, “If God will be with me” (Genesis 28:20), but without the promise that follows there. Keil and Delitzsch, following Bertheau, read the verse this way.

The last clause is rendered “that I may not cause pain” (NKJV), ‘so that it might not bring me pain’ (ESV), and ‘so that I will be free from pain’ (NIV). The Hebrew wording ('otzbi) includes ‘me’ but does not state who causes the pain.

Study Notes

The Encyclopaedia Judaica suggests that the town was originally called ‘Copper City’ (Ir Nehoshet), since the Hebrew word nahash (‘serpent’) resembles the Hebrew word nehoshet (‘copper, bronze’). The proposal is not confirmed, and the site of Ir-Nahash is uncertain.

Some manuscripts of the ancient Greek translation known as the Septuagint, including Codex Vaticanus, read ‘Rechab’ instead of “Rechah,” which would connect these families with the Rechabites (2:55); others (Codex Alexandrinus) read ‘Repha.’ The Hebrew reading is retained in the NKJV.

“And their villages were Etam, Ain, Rimmon, Tochen, and Ashan—five cities—”

Smaller settlements are recorded alongside the main cities. “Etam” here is a different town from the Etam near Bethlehem (4:3). “Ain” and “Rimmon” may have been neighboring towns, later known together as En Rimmon (Nehemiah 11:29). “Ashan” later became a Levitical city (1 Chronicles 6:59). The total is “five cities.” Chronicles preserves the full extent of Simeon's dwelling places.

Study Notes

The Septuagint, an ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, reads ‘Gerar’ instead of “Gedor.” Gerar was a region in the western Negev between Gaza and Beersheba, where Abraham and Isaac had lived among the Philistines (Genesis 20:1; 26:1), and where Asa pursued the Cushites (2 Chronicles 14:13). The reference to Hamites in verse 40 would fit Gerar. Its exact site is debated. The NKJV follows the Hebrew reading.

“These recorded by name came in the days of Hezekiah king of Judah;”

The verse anchors this episode in history. The leaders “recorded by name” in verses 34–37 came “in the days of Hezekiah king of Judah,” who reigned in the late eighth and early seventh centuries BC. The reference dates this particular episode. It does not date every genealogy in the chapter.

“and they attacked their tents and the Meunites who were found there,”

They attacked the inhabitants, who lived in “tents,” suggesting a semi-nomadic population, along with “the Meunites who were found there.” The Meunites were a people of the southern desert, against whom King Uzziah also fought (2 Chronicles 26:7).

“and utterly destroyed them, as it is to this day.”

They “utterly destroyed them.” The phrase describes total destruction, the same language used for the conquest of Canaan. The verse records the violence of the conquest plainly. “As it is to this day” speaks from the writer's historical standpoint.

“So they dwelt in their place, because there was pasture for their flocks there.”

The Simeonites settled in the land they had taken, “because there was pasture for their flocks there.” The verse records the violence plainly, without making it a pattern for God's people today. These reports do not authorize modern seizure of land, ethnic hostility, or violence in God's name.

Study Notes

“Utterly destroyed” translates the Hebrew verb haram, used for devoting something to destruction, especially in the conquest narratives (Joshua 6:21; 1 Samuel 15:3). The text does not say whether this action was commanded by God.

Hezekiah's reign is commonly dated about 715–686 BC, though some chronologies place it about a decade earlier.

The Simeonites “defeated the rest of the Amalekites who had escaped.” The Amalekites were Israel's longstanding enemies, descendants of Esau's grandson Amalek (1:36). They attacked Israel in the wilderness, and God declared war on Amalek “from generation to generation” (Exodus 17:16). Saul fought them and failed to carry out God's judgment completely (1 Samuel 15:7–9). David also fought them, and some escaped (1 Samuel 30:17). These accounts provide background for the reference to surviving Amalekites, but Chronicles does not identify the particular conflict from which this group had escaped.

“They have dwelt there to this day.”

The Simeonites settled in the region of Seir and “have dwelt there to this day,” that is, up to the writer's own time. The chapter closes with Simeonites settled in that region, recalling Israel's long conflict with Amalek. The closing statement speaks from the writer's historical standpoint; it does not promise that the same families occupy those places today.
