Book Overview

At a Glance

1 Samuel
Author

First Samuel does not name its author, and Samuel cannot have written all of it, since his death is recorded in 25:1 while the story continues well beyond it. Scripture does point to the kind of records that stood behind it. Chronicles refers to accounts kept by “Samuel the seer,” “Nathan the prophet,” and “Gad the seer” (1 Chronicles 29:29), and Samuel himself wrote down “the behavior of royalty” and laid it up before the LORD (10:25). Such prophetic records may have contributed to the book, though its final writer or compiler is unknown. That background fits the book’s character, since the history of Israel’s first kings is told from the standpoint of the prophets who brought them the LORD’s word.

Audience

First Samuel was written for Israel, for generations looking back on how the nation received its first kings. The book says little directly about its first readers, but its reference to “the kings of Judah” (27:6) points to people who already lived under a monarchy. That would have changed how the book sounded to them. When Samuel warned that a king would take their sons for his chariots, the best of their fields for his servants, and a tenth of their flocks (8:11–17), he was describing a world those readers knew from experience. What they needed was to understand what the LORD required of a king, and why the kingdom’s first chapter unfolded as it did.

Date

The events of 1 Samuel run from the closing years of the judges to the end of Saul’s reign, roughly the eleventh century BC; on common estimates, Samuel was born around 1100 BC and Saul died around 1010 BC. The book itself was completed later. Its remark that Ziklag “has belonged to the kings of Judah to this day” (27:6) suggests that it reached its final form, or passed through an editorial stage, after the kingdom divided around 930 BC. A more precise date cannot be determined.

Historical Setting

First Samuel opens at the end of the period of the judges, when “everyone did what was right in his own eyes” (Judges 21:25). Israel was a loose union of tribes without a king, held together by its worship of the LORD and by the leaders God raised up in times of crisis. The center of that worship was the tabernacle at Shiloh, where Eli served as priest, and that is what makes the opening chapters so serious. Eli’s sons were corrupting the priesthood at the very place where Israel came to meet with God, and “the word of the LORD was rare in those days” (3:1). The nation’s spiritual life was failing at its source.

Purpose

The book does not state why it was written, but its content suggests its purpose: to record and interpret the transition from the judges to the monarchy. The interpretation is built into the story itself, because at each turning point a prophetic word explains what the LORD is doing. When the elders demand a king, the LORD tells Samuel that the request is a rejection of His own rule (8:7). When Saul fails, Samuel tells him why his kingdom will not continue and, later, why he himself is rejected (13:13–14; 15:23). When David is chosen, it is only because the LORD says, “Arise, anoint him; for this is the one!” (16:12). Israel needed to understand that its kings ruled under the LORD and would stand or fall by their response to His word.

Central Message

First Samuel reveals that the LORD remains Israel’s true King as He moves His people from the judges to the monarchy. He raises up those who hear His word and depend on Him, brings down the proud and self-willed, and through Samuel’s ministry, Saul’s rejection, and David’s anointing and testing prepares the kingdom for a man after His own heart.

Key Themes

  • The LORD as Israel’s true King. Human kings rule under His authority and answer to His word (8:7; 12:12–25).

  • God brings low and lifts up. Hannah’s song announces the reversals the book then traces: Eli’s house and Saul brought low, Samuel and David raised up (2:7–8).

  • Hearing and obeying the word of the LORD. Samuel’s “Your servant hears” and Saul’s refusal to obey mark the difference between the two men (3:10; 15:22–23).

  • God looks at the heart. God chooses by what He sees within, which is why Eliab, the impressive firstborn, is passed over and the youngest son is anointed (16:7).

  • God forms His leaders. Samuel, Saul, and David are each called and tested, and each is marked by how he responds to God (1–3; 9–16; 17–31).

  • God’s anointing and appointing. The anointing marks God’s choice and empowerment, but faithful service requires continued obedience (10:1, 6; 16:13–14).

  • The contrast between Saul and David. Fear, jealousy, and self-will set against dependence on God, though neither man is one-dimensional (18:12; 24:6; 30:6).

  • The holiness of God’s presence. The ark cannot be used as a charm, and the God who dwells among His people is holy (4:3–11; 6:20).

  • Waiting for God’s time. David is anointed long before he reigns and refuses to seize the kingdom by force (16:13; 24:6; 26:11).

Book Flow

1 Samuel 1:1–3:21: Samuel Raised Up Before the LORD

God answers Hannah’s prayer and gives her Samuel, whom she gives back to the LORD “as long as he lives” (1:28). Her song praises the God who “brings low and lifts up” and who will “exalt the horn of His anointed” (2:7, 10), and the chapters that follow put that pattern on display. Eli’s sons treat the worshipers’ sacrifices as theirs to seize, and a man of God announces the LORD’s verdict on Eli’s house in words that echo through the rest of the book: “those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed” (2:30). Meanwhile the boy Samuel grows before the LORD. His call comes when “the word of the LORD was rare” (3:1) and Eli’s eyes had grown dim. Samuel answers, “Speak, for Your servant hears” (3:10), and the first word he receives is judgment on the house that raised him. By the end, all Israel knows that the LORD has established him as a prophet (3:20).

1 Samuel 4:1–7:17: The Ark, Judgment, and Return to the LORD

After a defeat, Israel’s elders ask the right question, “Why has the LORD defeated us today before the Philistines?”, and then reach for the wrong answer: bring the ark, “that when it comes among us it may save us” (4:3). They want God’s help without returning to God. The ark is captured, Eli’s sons die, Eli falls dead at the news, and his daughter-in-law names her son Ichabod: “The glory has departed from Israel!” (4:21). Yet the LORD, who would not be used by His people, is not defeated by their enemies. Dagon falls before the ark, the LORD’s hand strikes the Philistine cities, and the ark comes home on a cart whose cows head straight for Israel, proof that the LORD Himself had struck them (6:9–12). At Beth Shemesh His holiness strikes those who look into it, and the survivors ask, “Who is able to stand before this holy LORD God?” (6:20). Renewal comes only when Israel laments after the LORD, puts away its foreign gods, and confesses, “We have sinned against the LORD” (7:6). The LORD thunders against the Philistines, and Samuel gives the name of Israel’s place of defeat, Ebenezer, to a stone of victory: “Thus far the LORD has helped us” (7:12).

1 Samuel 8:1–12:25: Israel Asks for a King

With Samuel old and his sons taking bribes, the elders demand a king to judge them “like all the nations” (8:5). Kingship had been anticipated; the problem was the motive, and the LORD names it plainly: “they have rejected Me, that I should not reign over them” (8:7). Even so, He directs Samuel to give them a king. The man God chooses begins well. Saul calls his tribe “the smallest of the tribes of Israel” (9:21), hides among the equipment when the lot falls on him (10:22), and receives “another heart” (10:9). Empowered by the Spirit, he rescues Jabesh Gilead and spares the men who had despised him, for “today the LORD has accomplished salvation in Israel” (11:13). The kingdom is renewed at Gilgal, but Samuel’s farewell sets the terms on which it now depends: if king and people obey the LORD’s voice, they will go on following Him; if they rebel, “the hand of the LORD will be against you” (12:14–15). And Samuel will not “sin against the LORD in ceasing to pray for you” (12:23).

1 Samuel 13:1–15:35: Saul’s Disobedience and Rejection

At Gilgal comes the failure that costs Saul his dynasty. With the Philistines massing and his army deserting, he waits the seven days Samuel had appointed, then offers the burnt offering himself, and Samuel arrives just as he finishes. Saul’s explanation, “I felt compelled” (13:12), sounds reasonable, but he has “not kept the commandment of the LORD,” and Samuel tells him his kingdom will not continue: “The LORD has sought for Himself a man after His own heart” (13:13–14). At Michmash, Jonathan’s confidence that “nothing restrains the LORD from saving by many or by few” (14:6) stands against his father’s rash oath. The failure that costs Saul the throne itself comes with Amalek. Saul spares Agag and the best of the livestock, sets up a monument for himself, and greets Samuel with “I have performed the commandment of the LORD” while the sheep bleat behind him (15:12–14). Samuel answers, “to obey is better than sacrifice” (15:22). Saul’s own words then expose the problem: “I feared the people and obeyed their voice” (15:24), and “honor me now, please, before the elders of my people” (15:30). The LORD grieves that He made Saul king (15:11, 35), yet “the Strength of Israel will not lie nor relent” (15:29). His grief is real, and His verdict stands.

1 Samuel 16:1–20:42: David Anointed and Saul’s Jealousy

The LORD sends Samuel to Bethlehem, and when Samuel is sure the impressive firstborn must be the one, the LORD corrects him: “man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart” (16:7). He chooses the youngest, the son left out with the sheep. This is the pivot of the book: “the Spirit of the LORD came upon David from that day forward,” and “the Spirit of the LORD departed from Saul” (16:13–14). From here the two men’s paths run in opposite directions. Facing Goliath, David sees what the army does not, that the giant has defied “the armies of the living God” (17:26), and he fights so “that all the earth may know that there is a God in Israel” (17:46). The women’s song, “Saul has slain his thousands, and David his ten thousands” (18:7), turns Saul’s favor into suspicion, and the narrator names the root of his fear: “the LORD was with him, but had departed from Saul” (18:12). Jonathan, the heir to the throne, binds himself to David in covenant, defends him, and finally sends him away in peace (20:42).

1 Samuel 21:1–26:25: David the Fugitive

Driven from Saul’s presence, David becomes a fugitive in his own land. His deception of the priest at Nob costs the priests their lives when Doeg kills them at Saul’s command, and David owns it: “I have caused the death of all the persons of your father’s house” (22:22). At the cave of Adullam “everyone who was in distress, everyone who was in debt, and everyone who was discontented” gathers to him (22:2), and from these outcasts come the men who will stand with him. The contrast with Saul sharpens. David inquires of the LORD and saves Keilah from the Philistines (23:1–5), while Saul slaughters the LORD’s priests. In the wilderness Jonathan “strengthened his hand in God,” telling him, “You shall be king over Israel” (23:16–17). Twice David has Saul within reach, and twice he refuses to stretch out his hand against “the LORD’s anointed” (24:6; 26:11), leaving judgment with God. Between these accounts Samuel dies, and Abigail keeps David from avenging himself on Nabal, a reminder that the man who would not strike Saul could still be tempted to take revenge.

1 Samuel 27:1–31:13: David Among the Philistines and the Death of Saul

Convinced that “I shall perish someday by the hand of Saul” (27:1), David takes refuge among the Philistines at Ziklag, raiding surrounding peoples and deceiving Achish so thoroughly that he is nearly drawn into battle against Israel (28:1–2). The Philistine lords refuse to have him, and their refusal keeps him away from Gilboa (29:4–11). Saul, receiving no answer from the LORD, turns to a medium at En Dor, the very practice he had put out of the land (28:3), and hears the verdict Samuel announced in his lifetime spoken again: the kingdom has been given to David, and Israel will fall to the Philistines. The book then sets the two men’s final crises side by side. David returns to a burned Ziklag and men ready to stone him, yet he “strengthened himself in the LORD his God” (30:6), inquires of Him, and recovers all. Saul and his sons fall on Mount Gilboa, and the men of Jabesh Gilead, whom Saul had rescued at the start of his reign (11:1–11), honor their bodies.

Eli’s sons were corrupting the priesthood at the very place where Israel came to meet with God, and “the word of the LORD was rare in those days” (3:1). The nation’s spiritual life was failing at its source.

Israel’s most dangerous enemy was the Philistines, a well-organized people settled along the southern coastal plain. They controlled metalworking in the land: Israel had no blacksmith, went to the Philistines even to sharpen farm tools, and on the day of battle had almost no swords or spears (13:19–22). That detail explains much of what follows. Israel’s defeat at Aphek, its demand for a king who would “go out before us and fight our battles” (8:20), and the careers of both Saul and David all unfold under Philistine pressure. It was one source of the wider insecurity behind Israel’s demand for a king, alongside Samuel’s age, his sons’ corruption, and the threat of Nahash the Ammonite (8:1–5; 12:12).

Kingship itself was not foreign to God’s purposes. The Law had anticipated a king “whom the LORD your God chooses” (Deuteronomy 17:15), and Hannah’s song speaks of “His king” before Israel had one (2:10). The question 1 Samuel presses is therefore what kind of king Israel would have, and whether the nation would go on trusting the LORD as its true King.

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

First Samuel begins with a barren woman whose prayer is so intense that the priest mistakes it for drunkenness, and it ends with Israel’s first king falling on his own sword on Mount Gilboa. Between those two scenes, God carries His people from the era of the judges into the monarchy through three lives that overlap rather than follow one another: Samuel, the prophet given in answer to Hannah’s prayer; Saul, the king Israel demanded; and David, the shepherd God chose. Much of the book’s tension arises from that overlap. Samuel continues to speak into Saul’s reign until his death (25:1), and David is anointed years before Saul’s reign comes to an end.

Hannah’s grief is real and prolonged. She is barren, provoked year after year by her rival, and so troubled that she weeps and will not eat. At Shiloh she prays and weeps in anguish (1:10), and out of that prayer comes her vow: if the LORD gives her a son, she will give him to the LORD all the days of his life (1:11). Scripture does not record the LORD’s words to her, but the narrative points to a real encounter in that prayer. The LORD made known something of His own desire for Israel, a prophet through whom He would speak and anoint His king, and Hannah’s vow was her response. She leaves the house of the LORD and “her face was no longer sad” (1:18), and Elkanah’s words, “Only let the LORD establish His word” (1:23), fit a word from the LORD concerning this child. When Samuel is weaned she keeps her vow (1:27–28), and her song reaches far beyond her own home to the LORD’s holiness, His judgment, the reversals He brings, and the strength He will give to “His king” (2:10). She wanted a son; God was raising up the prophet who would anoint Israel’s first two kings.

What Hannah sings, the rest of the book shows. Samuel grows up before the LORD while Eli’s sons treat His offering with contempt, and the house that despised the LORD is brought low while the boy who listened is raised up. The ark is captured when Israel tries to use it as a guarantee of victory and comes back only when the LORD Himself humbles the Philistines and their god, so that His presence is shown to be beyond Israel’s control. Saul, though truly chosen, anointed, and empowered, loses the kingdom by disobeying the word of the LORD, first at Gilgal and then concerning Amalek. David, the youngest son, was overlooked when Jesse brought his sons before Samuel and was sent for only when Samuel asked whether another remained (16:10–11); his absence suggests no one thought him worth bringing forward. He is anointed, defeats Goliath, and then spends years as a hunted man, twice refusing to kill the king who is trying to kill him.

Woven through the narrative are the speeches that interpret it: Hannah’s song (2:1–10), the man of God’s word against Eli’s house (2:27–36), Samuel’s warning about the king Israel was asking for (8:11–18), his farewell to the nation (12:1–25), and his verdict on Saul after Amalek (15:22–23). These are the places where the book pauses to tell the reader what the events mean. The book ends with Saul’s death on Mount Gilboa, while David, anointed but not yet king, waits for God’s time.

Why This Book Matters

Whenever the New Testament calls Jesus “the Son of David” (Matthew 1:1), it reaches back to the events of this book. First Samuel records how Israel received its first king, how that king forfeited the throne, and how God chose a young shepherd from Bethlehem whose family line would lead to Christ. Without this account, the history of Israel’s monarchy would have no foundation, and the hope of a King descended from David, which runs through the rest of the Old Testament, would have no beginning.

The book also shows, with unusual clarity, that God does not judge as people do. Eli’s sons hold priestly office, yet “they did not know the LORD” (2:12). Saul looks every inch a king, taller than anyone in Israel (9:2; 10:23), yet he will not obey. David is the son his own father did not bring to the sacrifice, and he is chosen because “the LORD looks at the heart” (16:7). Again and again, position, appearance, and strength fail to predict what God will do, which shows how differently He weighs people from the way Israel did.

Finally, 1 Samuel shows how God forms those He calls, and why a calling must be lived out in obedience. Samuel learns to hear the LORD’s voice as a boy and keeps speaking His word for the rest of his life. Saul receives a real calling, “another heart,” and the Spirit’s power (10:6–10), yet loses the kingdom through fear of people and disobedience (15:24). David is anointed long before he reigns and is prepared through service, opposition, and years of waiting. God’s purposes also advance through others besides Samuel, Saul, and David. The LORD remembers Hannah’s prayer (1:19). After Jonathan’s attack at Michmash, the people say that “he has worked with God this day” (14:45). David blesses the LORD “who sent you this day to meet me” when Abigail turns him back from bloodshed (25:32–33). The book therefore speaks to every believer, not only to those who lead.

  • God brings low and lifts up. Hannah’s song announces the reversals the book then traces: Eli’s house and Saul brought low, Samuel and David raised up (2:7–8).

  • Hearing and obeying the word of the LORD. Samuel’s “Your servant hears” and Saul’s refusal to obey mark the difference between the two men (3:10; 15:22–23).

  • God looks at the heart. God chooses by what He sees within, which is why Eliab, the impressive firstborn, is passed over and the youngest son is anointed (16:7).

  • God forms His leaders. Samuel, Saul, and David are each called and tested, and each is marked by how he responds to God (1–3; 9–16; 17–31).

  • God’s anointing and appointing. The anointing marks God’s choice and empowerment, but faithful service requires continued obedience (10:1, 6; 16:13–14).

  • The contrast between Saul and David. Fear, jealousy, and self-will set against dependence on God, though neither man is one-dimensional (18:12; 24:6; 30:6).

  • The holiness of God’s presence. The ark cannot be used as a charm, and the God who dwells among His people is holy (4:3–11; 6:20).

  • Waiting for God’s time. David is anointed long before he reigns and refuses to seize the kingdom by force (16:13; 24:6; 26:11).

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

  • Judge. Before Israel had kings, God raised up judges to deliver and lead His people. Samuel is the last of the judges and also a prophet, which places him at the hinge between the old order and the new.

  • Prophet. One called by God to speak His word. Samuel guides Israel through the transition to kingship and confronts its first king, showing from the start that Israel’s kings would answer to the word of the LORD.

  • The ark of the covenant. The sacred chest that represented the LORD’s throne and presence among His people. Israel’s attempt to use it in battle (4:3) shows that God’s presence cannot be controlled.

  • Anointing. Pouring oil on a person to set him apart for a God-given office. Samuel anoints Saul from a flask (10:1) and David from a horn (16:13). “Anointed” translates the Hebrew word behind “Messiah.”

  • The LORD’s anointed. The king God had appointed. David refused to kill Saul to seize the throne and entrusted Saul’s fate to the LORD (24:6; 26:9–11). His restraint does not mean that leaders are exempt from correction; David also confronts Saul openly (24:9–15; 26:18–20).

  • The Spirit of the LORD came upon. In the Old Testament, the Spirit came upon individuals to empower them for a task (10:6; 16:13). Saul’s loss of the Spirit (16:14) concerns this empowerment for office, not the indwelling Spirit given to believers in the New Covenant.

  • A distressing spirit from the LORD (16:14). The text places even this troubling spirit under God’s sovereign authority as judgment on Saul. It does not mean that evil originates in God.

  • A man after His own heart (13:14). God’s own choice of a man devoted to Him, “who will do all My will” (Acts 13:22). The phrase does not mean David was sinless.

  • Urim, the ephod, and inquiring of the LORD. Priests sought God’s direction through the ephod and the Urim (14:3; 23:6–9; 28:6). David inquires and is answered; Saul inquires and receives no answer.

  • Ebenezer. “Stone of help.” The name first marks the place of Israel’s defeat (4:1); Samuel later gives it to a stone of victory (7:12).

  • Philistines. A powerful people on the coastal plain, organized around five cities, each with its own lord: Ashdod, Gaza, Ashkelon, Gath, and Ekron (6:17). The five lords act together. They decide where the ark will go (5:8–11), send it back with offerings “according to the number of the lords of the Philistines” (6:4), and overrule Achish when they send David home before the battle at Gilboa (29:2–7).

Christ and Life

Hannah’s song looks beyond her own answered prayer: “He will give strength to His king, and exalt the horn of His anointed” (2:10). She speaks of God’s anointed king before Israel has a king at all. The word “anointed” is the root of “Messiah,” and the hope her song introduces finds its fulfillment in Jesus, the Christ. Mary’s song echoes Hannah’s when she praises the God who “has put down the mighty from their thrones, and exalted the lowly” (Luke 1:52; compare 1 Samuel 2:7–8). The pattern of God bringing low and lifting up reaches its fullest expression in Christ, who humbled Himself to the cross and whom God has highly exalted (Philippians 2:8–11).

David, the youngest son of Jesse of Bethlehem, is anointed king and the Spirit comes upon him (16:13). The New Testament presents Jesus as the Son of David, born in the city of David (Matthew 1:1; Luke 2:4, 11), the descendant through whom God brought a Savior (Acts 13:22–23). Jesus was anointed “with the Holy Spirit and with power” (Acts 10:38), and believers share in His anointing (2 Corinthians 1:21; 1 John 2:20, 27). The New Covenant gift of the Spirit is greater than the Old Testament empowerment for office, but the pattern of God’s servant anointed by His Spirit points toward Christ.

Jesus Himself drew on 1 Samuel. When the Pharisees objected that His disciples were picking grain on the Sabbath, He reminded them of what David did at Nob, when he and his men were hungry and ate the showbread (21:1–6; Matthew 12:3–4; Mark 2:25–26; Luke 6:3–4). Then He went beyond anything David could claim: “For the Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath” (Matthew 12:8). The Son of David answered His critics from David’s own story and placed the Sabbath itself under His authority.

The book also shows life with God through the contrast between those who hear and those who do not. Hannah brought what was on her heart before the LORD and met something of what was on His. Samuel’s answer, “Speak, for Your servant hears” (3:10), is the readiness of a servant who wants God’s word. Saul’s tragedy grows from fear of people and failure to obey (15:22–24). David, at his lowest point at Ziklag, with the city burned, the families taken, and his own men speaking of stoning him, “strengthened himself in the LORD his God” (30:6) and then inquired of the LORD before he acted (30:8). Those who belong to Christ live from the same dependence: life with God begins in hearing Him, and when every other support gives way, strength is found in Him.

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Read 1 Samuel as the story of God’s kingship, not only of human kings. Even where people seem to drive events, the narrator credits the LORD: “the LORD remembered” Hannah (1:19), “the LORD thundered” against the Philistines at Mizpah (7:10), Samuel presents Saul as the one “whom the LORD has chosen” (10:24), and “the LORD struck Nabal” (25:38).

Do not read Saul merely as a villain or David merely as a hero. God truly chose and anointed Saul, and the Bible records David’s deceptions and failures honestly. The difference between them lies in their response to God’s word.

Remember that narrative description is not approval. The book reports many actions without endorsing them. And remember that 1 Samuel ends unfinished: Saul is dead, but David is not yet king. Read it as the first half of a story that continues in 2 Samuel.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

  • One book in two parts. In the Hebrew Bible, 1 and 2 Samuel formed a single work; the division into two books came later. Reading them together preserves the continuity from Samuel’s birth to David’s reign.

  • The Hebrew text. The text of Samuel has more difficult places than many Old Testament books. The Septuagint and the Dead Sea Scrolls sometimes preserve different readings. These affect individual details, not the book’s message.

  • Saul’s age and reign (13:1). The numbers in the Hebrew text of this verse appear to have been lost in copying, and translations handle the verse in different ways. Acts 13:21 says Saul reigned forty years.

  • Goliath’s height (17:4). The NKJV, following the Hebrew text, reads “six cubits and a span,” roughly nine and a half feet. A Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel from Qumran, major Septuagint manuscripts, and Josephus read “four cubits and a span,” roughly six and a half feet. The conversions are approximate, since the length of a cubit varied.

  • Beth Shemesh (6:19). The NKJV reads “fifty thousand and seventy men.” Some ancient witnesses read seventy, and the number is textually difficult.

  • Saul’s two rejections. At Gilgal Saul’s dynasty is rejected (13:13–14); after Amalek, Saul himself is rejected as king (15:23, 26). They are two distinct steps.

  • The medium at En Dor (28). The narrator calls the figure who appears “Samuel” (28:12, 15–16, 20). Interpreters differ on how this happened. The passage gives no approval to consulting the dead, which the Law forbids (Deuteronomy 18:10–12), and 1 Chronicles 10:13–14 records it among the reasons for Saul’s death.

  • Jonathan and David. Their bond is covenant loyalty and friendship before the LORD (18:1–4; 20:14–17, 42). The same language of love is used of the people’s loyalty to David (18:16).

  • Saul’s death. The narrator’s account in chapter 31 governs. The Amalekite’s different report in 2 Samuel 1 is best read as a false claim made to win David’s favor.
