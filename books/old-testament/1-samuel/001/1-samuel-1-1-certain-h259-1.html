CERTAIN - (ʼechâd, Strong's H259)

Summary:

The Hebrew word echad basic meaning is "one" or "a single." It can function as a cardinal number, denote unity, or serve as an indefinite designation equivalent to "a certain." In 1 Samuel 1:1, echad is translated "certain" and introduces Elkanah, an individual previously unknown to the reader. By using this word at the opening of the book, the author singles out one particular man and his family out of all Israel, establishing the specific historical setting through which God would raise up the prophet Samuel and transform the nation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Book of 1 Samuel opens during a dark and chaotic period in Israel's history. The era of the judges was marked by spiritual compromise, political disunity, and widespread moral decline. Instead of beginning with a grand royal decree or a sweeping nation-wide revival, the narrative turns its focus to a quiet, ordinary household in the hill country of Ephraim.

By introducing Elkanah as a "certain man" from Ramathaim Zophim, the text directs the reader's attention away from the general turmoil of the nation toward one specific family. God's major works in redemptive history frequently begin in obscurity. The historical setting demonstrates that even when the broader culture is in collapse, God continues His work through specific individuals in everyday places.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of echad is "one." Depending on how it is used in a sentence, it can refer to a single numerical count, the first item in a series, a unified group, or an indefinite person or thing ("a certain one").

When used with a singular noun in narrative storytelling, echad often functions much like the English phrase "a certain." Rather than emphasizing the mathematical count of one, it signals the arrival of a specific individual who has not yet been named in the account. In 1 Samuel 1:1, the word identifies Elkanah as a real, distinct individual entering the narrative horizon.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament historical narratives, echad serves several distinct purposes:

  • Counting or Enumeration: It is used simply to specify a single quantity as opposed to two or more.

  • Priority or Ordering: It can denote the "first" in a sequence, such as the initial day of creation.

  • Corporate Unity: It can describe distinct items or people brought together into a single cohesive whole.

  • Narrative Introduction: It introduces a particular person, place, or object into a story, directing the reader's focus to that specific subject.

In 1 Samuel 1:1, echad operates in this final narrative sense. It brings a single, specific household into sharp relief against the background of the entire nation of Israel.

4. Theological Implications

The choice to begin 1 Samuel with a "certain man" underscores how God executes His sovereign plan. The kingdom of Israel was in dire need of spiritual renewal, yet God did not begin by raising an army or sending a visible display of divine judgment. Instead, He began with one man, his two wives, and their domestic grief.

This reveals that God operates through real human history and through specific, ordinary lives. He does not act merely in vague generalities. The God of Israel notices and calls single individuals to play a role in His redemptive plan. The divine answer to a national crisis began in the humble home of one man in Ramathaim Zophim.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:1, echad modifies the word for "man," yielding the translation "a certain man." The word is grammatically singular and common in gender, perfectly matching the individual subject being described.

This opening phrasing anchors the narrative in concrete reality. The text does not begin like a fable with vague places or mythical figures. It names a specific man (Elkanah), his lineage, his home region, and his family situation. The word echad acts as a spotlight, moving across the vast landscape of Israel and stopping upon this single family. The immediate context shows that this "certain man" had a wife named Hannah who was barren. Thus, echad narrows the cosmic and national story of redemption down to the deeply personal tears and prayers of one household.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew literature, beginning a story with "a certain man" is a recognized technique for launching a significant new chapter in Israel's history. Similar openings appear at the beginning of the book of Ruth and the book of Job. This literary feature prepares the reader for an extended narrative focused on God's providence working through individual lives.

The grammatical agreement of echad as a singular form reinforces the personal focus of the passage. Out of all the men living in Israel at the close of the period of the judges, the narrative deliberately singles out this one man to trace the lineage of the prophet who would anoint Israel's first kings.
