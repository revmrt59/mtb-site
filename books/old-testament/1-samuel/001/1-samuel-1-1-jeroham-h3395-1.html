JEROHAM - (Yᵉrôchâm, Strong's H3395)

Summary:

The name Jeroham is a Hebrew proper name meaning "may he find compassion" or "God shows compassion," derived from a Hebrew root referring to tender mercy. In 1 Samuel 1:1, it functions as a masculine proper noun in the genealogy of Elkanah, the father of Samuel. By tracing Elkanah's lineage back through Jeroham, Elihu, Tohu, and Zuph, the opening sentence of 1 Samuel grounds Samuel's origins in a real, historically traceable Israelite family living in the hill country of Ephraim.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Jeroham comes from the Hebrew root racham, which relates to deep love, mercy, or tender compassion. Names in ancient Israel often expressed a prayer, an expression of faith, or a recognition of God's character at the time of a child's birth. The name Jeroham translates to "may he find mercy" or "God will show compassion."

Grammatically, the word functions as a masculine proper noun. In 1 Samuel 1:1, it appears in the opening sentence of the First Book of Samuel. The narrative introduces Elkanah, a man living in Ramathaim Zophim in the mountains of Ephraim, and establishes his lineage by naming his father Jeroham, his grandfather Elihu, his great-grandfather Tohu, and his great-great-grandfather Zuph.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Jeroham is a personal name used exclusively for men in the Old Testament. As a proper noun, its basic meaning reflects the concept of divine mercy and compassion. It belongs to a common category of Hebrew personal names that combine a verbal action or prayer with an implicit reference to God's merciful care.

In 1 Samuel 1:1, the word functions as a patronymic link in a family line: Elkanah is identified directly as "the son of Jeroham." The term provides a specific historical marker for Elkanah's immediate ancestry.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, Jeroham appears in several family registries to identify specific men within Israel's tribal structures. Outside of 1 Samuel 1:1, the name identifies individuals within the tribes of Levi, Benjamin, and Judah, as well as key military leaders, tribal heads, and priests.

In every case, the name anchors a person within a known family tree. This established family connections, tribal inheritance, and historical continuity. Rather than presenting figures as legendary or isolated individuals, historical narratives in Scripture use names like Jeroham to show that God's work unfolds through genuine human families across successive generations.

4. Theological Implications

Scripture routinely introduces major eras in salvation history by planting them in detailed human genealogies. By recording Elkanah's line through Jeroham back to Zuph, 1 Samuel 1:1 demonstrates that Samuel—the prophet who would guide Israel through the transition to the monarchy and anoint King David—came from a real family line preserved by God.

The meaning of the name Jeroham—derived from the root for compassion—may add a quiet thematic resonance to the opening chapter of 1 Samuel. The chapter focuses heavily on Hannah's deep sorrow over her childlessness and God's compassionate response to her prayer. While the text introduces Jeroham simply as an ancestor in Elkanah's lineage, the name's association with mercy fits naturally alongside the narrative of divine favor that unfolds in Hannah's life.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:1, the inclusion of Jeroham serves a clear historical and literary purpose. The preceding period of the Judges was marked by widespread spiritual decay and political instability. The opening line of 1 Samuel signals a turn toward a new chapter in Israel's history by focusing on a godfearing family in the hill country of Ephraim.

Elkanah's family tree spans four generations: Jeroham, Elihu, Tohu, and Zuph. Identifying Elkanah as "the son of Jeroham" locates him within a specific house and tradition. This multi-generational background sets the stage for Hannah's grief, her silent vow at the tabernacle in Shiloh, and the ultimate birth of Samuel. The text moves smoothly from this formal ancestral chain into the intimate, personal struggle of a household, demonstrating how divine providence works through generational lines to fulfill God's purposes.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Genealogical details elsewhere in Scripture supply broader background for Jeroham's family. While 1 Samuel 1:1 mentions Elkanah living in the territory of Ephraim, the genealogy in 1 Chronicles 6 reveals that Jeroham and Elkanah were Levites from the family line of Kohath. Levites were distributed among various tribal territories rather than receiving a single continuous land grant, which explains why a Levite family lived in the hill country of Ephraim. This Levite heritage helps clarify how Samuel was later dedicated to serve at the sanctuary under Eli the priest.
