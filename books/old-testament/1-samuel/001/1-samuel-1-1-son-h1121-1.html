SON - (bên, Strong's H1121)

Summary:

The Hebrew word ben primarily refers to a male child, son, or direct descendant, but it can also describe members of a group or offspring of animals. In 1 Samuel 1:1, the word identifies Elkanah as the direct male offspring of Jeroham, tracing his ancestral line across four generations to establish his family heritage and historical setting before the birth of Samuel.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the Old Testament, family heritage and ancestral lineage were essential for establishing a person's legal identity, tribal affiliation, and right to ancestral land. The word ben is the foundational Hebrew noun used to express the relationship between a father and his male child or descendant. Tracing line of descent through the phrase "son of" served as the primary way Israelites recorded history, preserved tribal heritage, and established legal identity within the covenant community.

In the opening verse of 1 Samuel, this family designation introduces Elkanah, the father of Samuel. By tracing Elkanah's lineage back through four generations, the book begins by grounding its historical events in a real family living in the hill country of Ephraim.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

distributions of meaning for the word include:

  • A biological son or male child.

  • A grandson or distant male ancestor within a genealogy.

  • A member of a specific nation, tribe, or group (such as "sons of Israel").

  • The young or offspring of animals.

  • A person characterized by a particular age, quality, or condition.

In 1 Samuel 1:1, the word carries its primary meaning of direct male offspring. It links Elkanah directly to his father, Jeroham, as part of a longer ancestral chain.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, ben is used in both literal and figurative ways. Most commonly, it designates direct family succession from father to son. Because Hebrew genealogical lists often highlight key ancestors rather than every single historical generation, the term can mean either an immediate son or a later male descendant.

The word emphasizes continuity and connection. It shows how identity, blessing, land inheritance, and covenant obligations pass from one generation to the next. When used of animals, it simply means their young or offspring. When used of groups, it highlights a shared identity derived from a common ancestor or authority.

4. Theological Implications

Genealogies in Scripture demonstrate that God carries out His redemptive purpose through actual human history and real families. By identifying Elkanah through his ancestral lineage, the text shows that God's work in Israel was anchored in established covenant households.

The term also highlights the cultural and spiritual importance of children in ancient Israel. Sons were seen as a blessing from God, vital for continuing the family line and preserving the inheritance Lord had given each family. This legal and generational background sets up the central drama at the beginning of 1 Samuel: a well-established family line faces the pain of barrenness, showing that human lineage alone cannot bring about God's deliverance without His miraculous grace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:1, the target word appears in the phrase identifying Elkanah as the "son of Jeroham." Grammatically, the word is singular and of common gender, identifying Elkanah as an individual male descendant in this lineage.

The verse repeats the word four times to trace Elkanah's line: Elkanah, son of Jeroham, son of Elihu, son of Tohu, son of Zuph. Tracing the family back four generations establishes Elkanah as a man of honorable and established standing in Ephraim. This detailed genealogy creates a deliberate contrast with the personal suffering introduced in the very next verses: despite Elkanah's distinguished lineage, his beloved wife Hannah has no children. The emphasis on Elkanah's family line magnifies the tragedy of Hannah's barrenness and underlines her earnest prayer for God to grant her a male child.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 13:14 – "son" – In instructions for teaching future generations, a father is called to explain the Passover when his "son" asks about the meaning of God's mighty acts.

  • 1 Kings 1:30 – "son" – King David confirms his royal decree regarding succession, declaring that Solomon his "son" will reign after him.

  • 1 Chronicles 10:14 – "son" – Summary of Israel's royal history notes that God turned the kingdom over to David the "son" of Jesse.

  • Ezekiel 18:2 – "children’s" – Addressing a popular proverb about generational guilt, the word appears in the expression "children's" teeth to contrast personal accountability with ancestral heritage.

  • Genesis 32:15 – "colts" – Demonstrating the word's application to animal offspring, Jacob prepares a gift for Esau that includes female camels with their "colts".

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Israel, naming and identifying a person by their father's name provided security, property rights, and community standing. To be named as someone's son meant carrying the responsibility and reputation of the household.

In the narrative of 1 Samuel, this generational focus highlights God's sovereign power. While Elkanah's line is established through human birth, the son who will change Israel's history—Samuel—comes as a direct answer to Hannah's prayer. God takes a family line and works through a miraculous birth to raise up a prophet who will guide His people.
