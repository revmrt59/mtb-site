ANGUISH - (bâkâh, Strong's H1058)

Summary:

Bakah means to weep, shed tears, or cry out in sorrow or emotional intensity. In 1 Samuel 1:10, translated within the NKJV phrase "wept in anguish," the verb describes Hannah’s tearful prayer before the Lord as she grieved her childlessness and the mockery of her rival. Her weeping was not a sign of hopeless surrender, but an honest pouring out of her soul to God in prayer.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root bakah is the primary Hebrew word for weeping and expressing grief through tears. In ancient Israelite culture, shedding tears was a natural, outward expression of deep emotion. Expressing grief, distress, or intense desire through weeping was widely understood as a healthy response to heavy burdens.

In 1 Samuel 1, Hannah experienced the ongoing sorrow of childlessness in a society that placed high value on bearing children. Making her trial harder, her husband’s second wife, Peninnah, provoked her continually to distress her. During the family's annual trip to worship at Shiloh, Hannah was so grieved that she wept and could not eat. In her distress, she went to the tabernacle to lay her pain before God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of bakah is to weep, shed tears, or lament. Depending on who is weeping and the situation taking place, the word can express various settings:

  • Deep personal sorrow or pain arising from affliction or loss.

  • Mourning for the dead or over a tragedy affecting a group of people.

  • Emotional relief or gladness, such as when family members reunite after a long separation.

  • Sorrow before God when recognizing spiritual failure or seeking divine mercy.

In 1 Samuel 1:10, the verb refers to Hannah's physical weeping as she brought her petition to the Lord.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, bakah marks moments where emotional distress reaches its limit. Tears often signify the boundary where human capability ends and reliance on God begins. When words feel insufficient, weeping expresses what the heart cannot easily put into sentences.

In Scripture, weeping serves several clear purposes:

  • A cry for help in life-threatening or desperate situations.

  • A shared expression of grief during times of national distress.

  • A posture of humble entreaty that accompanies earnest prayer.

The verb bakah itself describes the action of weeping rather than the act of prayer. However, when tears are lifted up to the Lord, weeping becomes part of earnest supplication.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s weeping in 1 Samuel 1 highlights how God receives the honest grief of His people:

  • God welcomes transparent prayer. Hannah did not mask her sorrow or try to sound polished. She brought her genuine grief into the presence of the Lord.

  • Tears during trials do not indicate a lack of faith. Hannah wept while praying directly to God. Her sorrow drew her to the tabernacle rather than driving her away from God.

  • God sees what human observers miss. While Eli the priest misunderstood Hannah’s silent moving lips and accused her of being drunk, God saw her heart and heard her tearful prayer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:10, Hannah's weeping occurs alongside deep inner heartache: "she was in bitterness of soul, and prayed to the LORD and wept in anguish."

The passage highlights a sharp contrast between human misunderstanding and divine attentiveness. Her husband Elkanah could not fully comfort her, and the high priest misjudged her posture. Yet Hannah explained that she was pouring out her soul out of great grief and affliction.

Her weeping in verse 10 marks a decisive moment in her life. Instead of giving way to despair or retaliating against Peninnah, Hannah released her sorrow to the Lord. The grammatical construction—a feminine singular imperfect form—describes her weeping as an action taking place during her prayer at Shiloh.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 21:16 — "Then she went and sat down across from him at a distance of about a bowshot; for she said to herself, 'Let me not see the death of the boy.' So she sat opposite him, and lifted her voice and wept."

  • Genesis 45:15 — "Moreover he kissed all his brothers and wept over them, and after that his brothers talked with him."

  • 1 Samuel 30:4 — "Then David and the people who were with him lifted up their voices and wept, until they had no more power to weep."

  • Nehemiah 8:9 — "And Nehemiah, who was the governor, Ezra the priest and scribe, and the Levites who taught the people said to all the people, 'This day is holy to the LORD your God; do not mourn nor weep.' For all the people wept, when they heard the words of the Law."

  • Lamentations 1:2 — "She weeps bitterly in the night, her tears are on her cheeks; among all her lovers she has no one to comfort her."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's tearful prayer at Shiloh led to the birth of Samuel, whose name records that God hears prayer. Her heavy sorrow at the sanctuary eventually gave way to exultant praise in 1 Samuel 2, where her prayer shifted from quiet weeping to joyful thanksgiving. This movement from weeping in chapter 1 to joy in chapter 2 illustrates how God listens to the cries of the afflicted and turns sorrow into praise.
