PRAYED - (pâlal, Strong's H6419)

Summary:

In 1 Samuel 1:10, the Hebrew verb palal describes Hannah bringing her deep personal heartache directly to the Lord in prayer. The word generally means to entreat, make supplication, intercede, or ask God to step into a situation. In this verse, it captures Hannah's desperate petition at the tabernacle in Shiloh, where she pours out her sorrow over childlessness and asks the Lord to intervene on her behalf.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The opening chapter of 1 Samuel introduces Hannah, a woman trapped in severe emotional distress. Though loved by her husband Elkanah, she was unable to bear children, while his second wife, Peninnah, had children and provoked Hannah mercilessly year after year. This anguish peaked during the family's annual pilgrimage to worship at Shiloh.

Rather than allowing bitterness to turn her away from God, Hannah took her pain to the place of worship. Standing near the doorpost of the tabernacle, she channeled her deep grief into focused entreaty. In this historical setting, her prayer was not a routine ritual, but a vital appeal from a suffering believer who knew that only the Lord could alter her circumstances.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb palal fundamentally means to ask, plead, intercede, or seek divine intervention. When a person is the subject and God is the object, the word refers to appealing to God for help, mercy, or guidance.

Depending on the context, palal can carry distinct shades of meaning:

  • Entreating or petitioning God for personal or communal needs.

  • Interceding on behalf of another person or group.

  • Arbitrating, judging, or intervening in a legal or social conflict.

In 1 Samuel 1:10, the word functions in its primary petitionary sense. Hannah is not arbitrating a legal dispute; she is entreating the Lord to hear her cry and grant her request for a son.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, palal appears in several distinct settings that highlight its spiritual and social depth.

First, the word frequently describes powerful intercession. God commands leaders or prophets to pray on behalf of others so that judgment might be turned away or healing granted. In these contexts, palal involves standing in the gap for people who need divine mercy.

Second, the word is deeply linked with the temple and tabernacle. Solomon’s dedication of the temple centers around the expectation that when God’s people face war, drought, exile, or personal affliction, they can pray toward God's dwelling place and be heard.

Third, the word occasionally carries the sense of dynamic intervention or legal arbitration, where someone steps between two parties to execute judgment or stop a crisis.

In terms of grammar, the form used in 1 Samuel 1:10 is a feminine singular imperfect verb with a sequential narrative prefix (wayyiqtol). This grammatical form presents Hannah's action as the direct, active response flowing out of her inner distress.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of palal carries profound theological lessons about how believers relate to God in prayer:

  • God is accessible to the afflicted: Hannah’s prayer demonstrates that God does not ignore the grief of the brokenhearted. Prayer is the means God provides for His people to bring real human pain into His presence.

  • Prayer recognizes God’s sovereignty: By praying, a person acknowledges that human strength is insufficient and that God alone has the power to change circumstances, give life, and reverse hardship.

  • True prayer involves the heart: Palal is not merely reciting empty formulas. As Hannah’s experience shows, true petition involves opening one's innermost longings before the Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:10, palal works together with the surrounding words to paint a vivid picture of Hannah's condition. The text states that she was "in bitterness of soul" and "wept anguishly" as she prayed to the Lord. Her outward tears and inward pain were completely directed toward God.

This internal intensity explains the misunderstanding that follows in the passage. As Hannah prayed, her lips moved, but her voice was not heard, leading Eli the priest to assume she was drunk. When Hannah corrected Eli, she explained that she had been pouring out her soul and speaking out of the abundance of her complaint and grief.

The contextual force of palal here shows that effective prayer does not require loud, public performance. Hannah's silent, soul-baring petition was genuine intercession, reaching the heart of God even when human leaders completely misjudged her motives.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 20:7 — "Now therefore, restore the man’s wife; for he is a prophet, and he will pray for you and you shall live."

Here the word refers to Abraham interceding for Abimelech, showing how prayer can bring divine healing and restoration to others.

  • 1 Samuel 2:25 — "If one man sins against another, God will judge him. But if a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?"

In this passage, the word carries the nuance of arbitration or legal intervention, contrasting human judgment with the lack of an intercessor when someone willfully defies God.

  • 2 Kings 19:15 — "Then Hezekiah prayed before the LORD, and said: 'O LORD God of Israel, the One who dwells between the cherubim, You are God, You alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth.'"

Facing national destruction by Assyria, King Hezekiah turns to God in earnest appeal, demonstrating how rulers brought critical national emergencies to the Lord.

  • Job 42:10 — "And the LORD restored Job’s losses when he prayed for his friends."

This occurrence highlights intercessory prayer, showing that Job's full restoration occurred as he sought God's mercy on behalf of those who had misjudged him.

  • Psalms 106:30 — "Then Phinehas stood up and intervened, and the plague was stopped."

Here the verb describes decisive action that turns away divine wrath, illustrating the word's shade of meaning related to judgment and mediation.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's prayer in 1 Samuel 1:10 marks a pivotal turning point in redemptive history. Out of her personal grief and sincere appeal came the birth of Samuel, who would serve as Israel's final judge, establish the prophetic office, and anoint King David.

Furthermore, the narrative highlights a striking contrast between the spiritual state of Eli's priesthood and the quiet faith of Hannah. While Eli's sons were corrupt and Eli himself was spiritually dim, an ordinary, afflicted woman laying her burden before God through prayer became the instrument through which God began renewing His people.
