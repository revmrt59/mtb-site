WEPT - (bâkâh, Strong's H1058)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bakah means to shed tears, cry, or express grief aloud. It describes physical weeping resulting from deep emotion, including pain, sorrow, fear, joy, or intense longing. In 1 Samuel 1:10, bakah describes Hannah's deep overflow of sorrow at the tabernacle in Shiloh as she pours out her heartache over childlessness before the Lord. Here, the word highlights her unreserved grief turned directly into earnest prayer, showing that weeping before God is a genuine and humble posture of faith.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, emotional expression was open, visible, and natural in public and religious life. Shedding tears was not viewed as weakness or a lack of spiritual resolve; rather, tears were recognized as a normal human response to affliction, loss, injustice, and sincere longing.

The verb bakah captures the physical act of crying, often accompanied by vocal weeping, groaning, or bowing down. In the opening chapter of 1 Samuel, Israel is in a period of spiritual weakness under the judges. Against this national backdrop, the story introduces the personal sorrow of Hannah. She suffered from barrenness and endured persistent provocation from Peninnah, her husband's other wife. At the central sanctuary in Shiloh, Hannah's distress reaches a high point, leading her to weep openly before God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Bakah means to weep, cry, shed tears, or lament. The primary sense refers to the physical expression of strong emotion through tears.

  • When used of personal loss, it refers to mourning over sorrow, hardship, or death.

  • When used in times of danger or distress, it describes crying out for help, relief, or deliverance.

  • When used in contexts of joy or reunion, it describes emotional weeping upon seeing family members after a long separation.

The word itself denotes the physical act of crying. It does not carry an inherent moral or spiritual quality on its own; whether weeping reflects faithful trust, deep sorrow, or bitter complaint depends on the heart of the person and the context in which it occurs.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout Scripture, bakah describes a broad range of human experiences.

  • Familial Grief and Joy: Patriarchs cry over the loss of loved ones, such as Jacob mourning for Joseph, or weep with gladness during emotional family reunions, as when Joseph reveals himself to his brothers.

  • Corporate Lament: The nation of Israel weeps together during times of national tragedy, military defeat, or collective repentance before God.

  • Penitential Sorrow: Prophets and psalmists speak of weeping as an expression of true sorrow for sin or intense sorrow under divine discipline.

In Hebrew grammar, bakah is sometimes paired as an infinitive absolute with another form of the same verb to emphasize intensity or persistence ("weeping bitterly" or "weeping greatly"). In 1 Samuel 1:10, the word appears in the form of an infinitive absolute, emphasizing the deep, unrestrained nature of Hannah's crying.

4. Theological Implications

Scripture reveals several truths through passages featuring bakah:

  • God Notices Human Sorrow: The biblical narrative repeatedly demonstrates that tears shed before God are seen and remembered by Him. He is attentive to the real grief of His people.

  • Honesty in Prayer: Weeping before God is a vital part of biblical prayer. Faith does not require hiding grief or pretending to be strong. Bringing tears to the Lord reflects trust that He alone can bring comfort and relief.

  • The Hope of Final Deliverance: Tears mark life in a broken world, but biblical revelation points to a future hope where God will wipe away every tear from the eyes of His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:10, Hannah stands at the tabernacle in deep "bitterness of soul," praying to the Lord and weeping intensely.

The context places her weeping in sharp contrast to her circumstances. Year after year, Peninnah provoked her to make her miserable because her womb was closed. Rather than venting her bitterness toward others or turning away from God, Hannah directs her grief straight to the sanctuary. Her weeping is intertwined with prayer.

The grammatical form of bakah in this verse—an infinitive absolute—highlights the depth of her crying. She was not shedding a quiet, restrained tear; her sorrow was poured out completely. Eli the priest mistook her tearful, silent movement of lips for drunkenness, demonstrating how isolated Hannah was in her grief. No human around her understood her pain, but God heard her silent prayer and saw her tears. After pouring out her soul and receiving Eli's blessing, her burden lifted, showing that bringing her tears to God brought true peace.

  • Nehemiah 8:9 — "And Nehemiah, who was the governor, Ezra the priest and scribe, and the Levites who taught the people said to all the people, 'This day is holy to the LORD your God; do not mourn nor weep.' For all the people wept, when they heard the words of the Law."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Tears as Vocal Prayer: In Hebrew thought, tears function almost like a language when spoken words fail. Psalm 56:8 expresses this truth poetically by asking God to collect tears in a bottle, showing that sincere grief brought to Him is precious.

  • Faithful versus Unbelieving Weeping: Scripture records instances where Israel wept out of unbelief or complaint, such as during wilderness wanderings. In contrast, Hannah’s weeping in 1 Samuel 1 is an example of faithful weeping—taking actual pain to the Lord in submission and trust.
