DAYS - (yôwm, Strong's H3117)

Summary:

The basic Hebrew noun yom refers to a period of daylight, a twenty-four-hour day, or a broader period of time marked by specific events. In the plural, when combined with "life," it frequently describes the complete length of a person's existence. In 1 Samuel 1:11, Hannah uses the word to vow that any son God gives her will be given back to the Lord for "all the days of his life," expressing a total, lifelong dedication to God's service rather than a temporary commitment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew word yom is one of the most fundamental terms for time in the Old Testament. It appears across all eras of Biblical Hebrew to mark time in various ways, from the daylight hours of a single morning to broad historical epochs. In ancient Israel, daily life was ordered by the natural rhythm of sunrise and sunset, annual feast days, and the unfolding years of human life.

In 1 Samuel 1:11, the story unfolds near the end of the period of the judges, a time marked by spiritual decline and political turmoil in Israel. Hannah, who was childless and enduring severe emotional distress, traveled with her family to worship at the tabernacle in Shiloh. While praying at the sanctuary, she made a sacred vow to God. She promised that if the Lord answered her plea and gave her a male child, she would dedicate him fully to divine service. Her vow uses yom in the plural to define the permanent scope of her promise.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word yom can refer to:

  • The daylight hours as distinct from night.

  • A standard twenty-four-hour day.

  • A specific point in time, date, or event.

  • A broader era, season, or period of time.

  • A person's full lifespan when used in the plural with "life."

In 1 Samuel 1:11, the word appears in its plural form linked with "his life." Here it means the entire duration of a person's earthly existence. Hannah is not offering her prospective child for a short term or a temporary season of service. She is promising a complete lifetime of devotion to God.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, yom takes its precise meaning from its surrounding context. When joined with numbers or terms like "evening" and "morning," it routinely describes a literal day. When used in the plural alongside terms like "life," "years," or "old age," it highlights continuity and the full length of a human life.

In vows and commitments, measuring duty by "days" stresses ongoing, regular faithfulness. Under the Mosaic system, priests and Levites often served in limited shifts or temporary rotations. By pledging her child for "all the days of his life," Hannah raises her vow above standard temporary obligations. The word emphasizes an unbroken span of time that extends from early childhood until death.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's vow reflects a clear understanding of divine sovereignty and human stewardship. She recognizes that the child she longs for can come only as an unmerited gift from God. In response, she resolves to give that gift back to the Giver completely.

Her promise of "all the days of his life" prepares the way for Samuel's crucial ministry in Israel. Samuel would serve as a lifelong prophet, priest, and judge, guiding the nation through the transition from the judges to the monarchy. While typical Nazirite vows were often undertaken for a limited duration, Hannah's pledge established a lifelong consecration. This demonstrates that true devotion to God is not merely an occasional duty, but an enduring life commitment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:11, the surrounding narrative gives deep solemnity to Hannah's phrase "all the days of his life." Hannah stands in the sanctuary, weeping freely out of deep grief. She asks God to look upon her affliction, remember her, and grant her a son. Her vow carries two explicit conditions: surrendering the child to the Lord for his full lifetime and ensuring no razor ever touches his head, marking him out as dedicated to God.

The phrase "all the days of his life" highlights the magnitude of Hannah's sacrifice. She petitions God for a child she will not keep at home, choosing instead to hand over every single day of his future to the tabernacle. Once the boy was weaned, she fulfilled her word, leaving young Samuel at Shiloh to minister before the Lord permanently.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:5 – "God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night." Here the word is used in its primary sense to distinguish daylight from nighttime.

  • Deuteronomy 16:8 – "Six days you shall eat unleavened bread, and on the seventh day there shall be a sacred assembly to the LORD your God." The word designates specific consecutive days within Israel's annual feast calendar.

  • 1 Chronicles 22:9 – "I will give peace and quietness to Israel in his days." In this context, the plural form refers to the full span of King Solomon's reign over the nation.

  • Job 10:20 – "Are not my days few? Cease! Leave me alone, that I may take a little comfort." Job uses the plural form to describe the brevity and frailty of human lifespan.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In 1 Samuel 1:11, yom carries masculine plural grammar, joined directly to the phrase meaning "his life." In biblical Hebrew, combining the word for "all" with the plural of yom and a personal possessive is the standard idiom for expressing an entire earthly lifespan.

This lifetime vow contrasts sharply with the spiritual atmosphere of the passage. At a time when the priesthood under Eli's sons was corrupt and failing, God accepted Hannah's vow to raise up a leader whose entire life—every single day—would be devoted to faithful ministry before the Lord.
