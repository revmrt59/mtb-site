FORGET - (shâkach, Strong's H7911)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shakach means to forget, ignore, neglect, or lose sight of someone or something. In biblical usage, forgetting is rarely a simple lapse of memory; it refers to a failure of care, attention, or covenantal commitment. In 1 Samuel 1:11, Hannah uses this word in her earnest prayer at the tabernacle, pleading that God will not "forget" her in her distress. Her request is an appeal for God to look upon her grief, show her active mercy, and grant her a son.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Near East and the Old Testament world, memory and forgetting carried strong relational weight. To remember someone meant to show active concern, favor, or assistance, while to forget meant to overlook, neglect, or leave someone without help. In Israel's history, the Lord repeatedly commanded His people not to forget His covenant, His laws, or His saving acts. Conversely, when God's people suffered oppression, distress, or barrenness, they cried out to Him asking that He would not forget them.

When 1 Samuel opens, Israel was living through a period of spiritual weakness and personal distress. Hannah, married to Elkanah, suffered deep sorrow because she was childless, while her rival Peninnah provoked her constantly. During the annual gathering for worship at Shiloh, Hannah went to the tabernacle in deep bitterness of soul. Her prayer in 1 Samuel 1:11 pairs a negative plea—not to forget her—with positive requests to look on her affliction and remember her.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shakach means to forget, ignore, neglect, or cease to care for. It describes both human memory lapses and deliberate acts of disregard. When used of human beings toward God, shakach describes forsaking God's commandments, ignoring His past faithfulness, or turning to idols.

When worshippers pray that God will not shakach them, the word does not imply that God is capable of mental distraction or forgetfulness. Instead, it is a personal petition. For God to "forget" would mean leaving a person in suffering without intervention. Asking God not to forget is a request for Him to turn His attention toward the worshipper, exercise His mercy, and act on their behalf. In 1 Samuel 1:11, the word appears in a masculine singular imperfect form as part of Hannah's direct address to God.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, shakach appears in several distinct settings:

  • Human forgetfulness toward God: Israel is repeatedly warned against forgetting the Lord who brought them out of Egypt or forgetting His covenant law (Deuteronomy 4:23; 8:14). This kind of forgetting involves moral apathy and spiritual neglect.

  • Human forgetfulness toward others: People forget their commitments, past kindnesses, or brethren in need (Genesis 27:45; Deuteronomy 24:19).

  • The cry of the sufferer to God: Sufferers in the Psalms frequently pray that God will not forget their plight or their lives (Psalms 9:18; 44:24; 74:19). In these prayers, "forgetting" describes the experience of feeling overlooked during times of trouble.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical usage of shakach illuminates how Scripture speaks about divine memory and attention. God's memory is not a matter of cognitive recall, but an exercise of His sovereign will and faithful care. When God remembers, He acts; when He does not forget, He demonstrates His enduring mercy toward the weak and vulnerable.

Hannah's plea reveals that God invites His people to bring their deepest burdens directly to Him. Pleading with God not to forget is an expression of faith in His personal character. It acknowledges that God is the ruler of all creation while remaining attentive to the quiet prayers of a humble servant.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:11, Hannah's vow is framed by three requests: "look on the affliction of Your maidservant," "remember me," and "not forget Your maidservant." This threefold petition highlights her complete reliance on God's care.

Hannah refers to herself three times in this single verse as "Your maidservant." By taking the posture of a humble servant, she presents her need before the Lord. Her request not to be forgotten contrasts directly with her experience in her household, where Peninnah mocked her and made her feel discarded. Humanly speaking, Hannah was overlooked because of her barrenness. Yet she appeals to the Lord, confident that while human society may disregard her, the God of Israel does not forget those who cry out to Him.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 4:23 — "Take heed to yourselves, lest you forget the covenant of the LORD your God which He made with you..."

  • Psalms 9:18 — "For the needy shall not always be forgotten; the expectation of the poor shall not perish forever."

  • Psalms 44:24 — "Why do You hide Your face, and forget our affliction and our oppression?"

  • Psalms 119:93 — "I will never forget Your precepts, for by them You have given me life."

  • Isaiah 51:13 — "And you forget the LORD your Maker, who stretched out the heavens and laid the foundations of the earth..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's prayer in 1 Samuel 1:11 marks a key moment in Israel's history. Her petition for God not to forget her leads to the birth of Samuel, who would serve as Israel's judge, priest, and prophet, eventually anointing King David. God's response to Hannah's personal grief became the means of spiritual renewal for the nation. This shows that God's non-forgetfulness operates on both personal and national levels, using the answered prayer of a suffering woman to advance His larger redemptive purpose.
