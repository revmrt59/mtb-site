GIVE - (nâthan, Strong's H5414)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb natan fundamentally means to give, grant, place, set, or deliver. It is the primary Old Testament word used to describe the transfer of a gift, object, authority, or person from one hand to another. In 1 Samuel 1:11, Hannah uses this word to declare her solemn vow to God, promising that if the Lord gives her a male child, she will give that son back to the Lord for lifelong service at the tabernacle.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb natan is one of the most frequent action words in the Hebrew Scriptures. In ancient Israelite daily life, the word covered a wide range of actions—from placing an object in a vessel, setting boundaries, or appointing leaders, to handing over an inheritance or presenting an offering to God.

In the opening

chapter of 1 Samuel, Israel is in a period of spiritual decay and instability under the leadership of the priest Eli and his corrupt sons. Hannah, one of the wives of Elkanah, suffers from childlessness and social distress. During her visit to the sanctuary at Shiloh, she pours out her heart to God in prayer. Her speech culminates in a dramatic vow in 1 Samuel 1:11, where natan serves as the pivot of her petition and commitment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, natan means to give, place, or hand over. Depending on the sentence structure and context, the word can mean:

  • To grant or bestow a gift, blessing, or favor.

  • To place, put, or set something in a physical position.

  • To deliver or hand over control, custody, or authority to someone else.

  • To assign, dedicate, or appoint a person to a task or role.

The word itself does not carry a single specialized ritual or theological definition; rather, its specific sense comes from who is performing the action, what is being handed over, and the purpose of the transfer.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses natan in two primary directions: divine giving and human giving.

When God is the subject, natan describes Him granting land to Israel, bestowing children upon parents, providing food for His creatures, or placing His Spirit or law within His people. Divine giving reflects God's freedom, generosity, and sovereign authority over creation.

When human beings are the subject, natan describes individuals presenting gifts, paying wages, making vows, or placing items in specific locations. In worship settings, human giving is always a response to God's prior giving. Because God owns the earth and everything in it, people do not enrich God when they give to Him; rather, they yield back what He has entrusted to them.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s prayer highlights a crucial biblical pattern regarding divine gifts and human stewardship. Throughout Scripture, blessings granted by God are meant to be held in open hands rather than gripped with self-centered ownership.

By using natan to describe both what she asks from God and what she promises to return to God, Hannah demonstrates that true devotion involves surrendering the very gifts we receive. The passage shows that God's answers to prayer are not merely meant to satisfy personal desires, but can be dedicated back to Him to fulfill His larger redemptive purposes in the world.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:11, Hannah makes a vow: "O LORD of hosts, if You will indeed look on the affliction of Your maidservant and remember me, and not forget Your maidservant, but will give Your maidservant a male child, then I will give him to the LORD all the days of his life."

In this single sentence, the concept of giving operates in two directions:

  • First, Hannah asks God to grant (natan) her a son.

  • Second, she pledges to yield (natan) that child back to the Lord forever.

The contextual force of the word here moves beyond a temporary gift or standard tithe. Hannah promises a complete and permanent transfer of custody and life purpose. Samuel will not belong to Elkanah's household line in the standard economic sense, nor will he remain at home to care for Hannah in her old age. Instead, he will be set apart for lifelong service at the sanctuary. Through this surrender, God raises up Samuel to be the prophet and judge who will guide Israel out of spiritual dark times.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 18:7 – God speaks to Aaron regarding the sanctuary duties: "I give your priesthood to you as a gift for service."

  • Deuteronomy 26:2 – Israel is commanded to take the firstfruits of the land that the LORD is "giving" them and bring it to the place of worship.

  • 1 Chronicles 16:18 – In David's psalm of thanksgiving, he recalls God's covenant promise to "give" the land of Canaan as an inheritance.

  • Ecclesiastes 2:26 – Solomon notes that God "gives" wisdom, knowledge, and joy to the person who pleases Him.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast in 1 Samuel 1–2 between Hannah and the family of Eli is stark. Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, used their priestly status to take forcefully from the people's sacrifices for themselves. In contrast, Hannah, an afflicted woman with no social standing, asks God for a son only to return him fully to God's service.

Hannah's willing gift of Samuel stands at the transition point of Israel's history, paving the way for the establishment of the prophetic ministry and the Davidic kingdom.
