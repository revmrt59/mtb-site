LIFE - (chay, Strong's H2416)

Summary:

The word chay refers to physical existence, the state of being alive, or the active life force that animates living beings. In 1 Samuel 1:11, Hannah promises that if God grants her a male child, she will give him to the LORD for "all the days of his life." Here, the word describes the total earthly existence of her son Samuel, marking a vow of lifelong service and complete dedication to God.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In 1 Samuel 1, Hannah suffers deep grief because she is childless while her husband's second wife taunts her. In ancient Israelite culture, childlessness was viewed not only as a personal tragedy but as a bitter trial. Hannah goes to the tabernacle at Shiloh to pray directly to God.

Her prayer is notable for its raw honesty and absolute devotion. Rather than asking for a child simply to relieve her personal shame, Hannah vows that if God gives her a son, she will hand him back to God for his entire lifetime. In 1 Samuel 1:11, her pledge that the child will serve God for all his days sets the stage for Samuel to become a pivotal prophet, priest, and judge in Israel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of chay is "living," "alive," or "life." It stands in direct contrast to death and non-existence. The word can describe:

  • The state of physical life in humans, animals, or plants.

  • The duration of a person's earthly lifespan.

  • Active, moving, or flowing physical elements, such as fresh spring water ("living water").

  • The living character of God Himself in solemn oaths ("as the LORD lives").

Grammatically, the term occurs here in a masculine plural form. In Hebrew, this plural form is standard when expressing the continuous experience or complete span of physical life.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, chay carries several shades of meaning depending on how it is used:

First, it denotes physical vitality and animated existence. To be alive is to possess the breath and energy given by God.

Second, when joined with words like "days" or "years," it refers to a person's lifetime. This is the exact usage in 1 Samuel 1:11, where "all the days of his life" means the complete duration of Samuel's earthly existence.

Third, the word describes things that are fresh and active rather than stale or dormant. For example, running spring water is described using this same word to emphasize its refreshing, active quality.

Finally, the word appears in oaths where someone swears by the life of God or the life of a king. Promising by the "life" of the LORD affirms that just as surely as God lives, the speaker's statement is true.

4. Theological Implications

In the biblical worldview, life is not an accident of nature; it is a direct gift from God. God is the source and sustainer of all living things. Because God gives life, human existence belongs ultimate honor and devotion back to Him.

Hannah’s vow highlights an important theological principle: what God gives in grace can be joyfully returned to Him in worship. By dedicating her son’s life to God, Hannah acknowledges that the child she sought was never ultimately hers to keep for personal ambition. Her son’s entire life becomes an instrument for God's holy work.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:11, Hannah’s use of the word occurs within a solemn vow. Under the Mosaic Law, Levitical priests typically served in tabernacle duties during specific years of adulthood. Hannah’s promise goes far beyond normal requirements. She vows to surrender her child for "all the days of his life"—from his earliest childhood after weaning until his death.

Furthermore, Hannah couples this lifelong commitment with a vow that "no razor shall come upon his head," reflecting elements of a Nazirite consecration. While Nazirite vows were usually taken voluntarily for a temporary season, Hannah consecrates her unborn son for a permanent, lifetime service. The phrase "all the days of his life" reinforces that Samuel will belong to God's sanctuary service without interruption or expiration.

  • Deuteronomy 6:2 — "that you may fear the LORD your God, to keep all His statutes and His commandments which I command you, you and your son and your grandson, all the days of your life, and that your days may be prolonged."

  • Jeremiah 2:13 — "For My people have committed two evils: They have forsaken Me, the fountain of living waters, and hewn themselves cisterns—broken cisterns that can hold no water."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Later in 1 Samuel 1:28, Hannah fulfills this vow after weaning Samuel, stating that "as long as he lives he shall be lent to the LORD." Her action stands as a profound model of faithful commitment.

The radical nature of Hannah's surrender stands out even more when compared to the corrupt priesthood of Eli’s sons at Shiloh in the same era. While Eli's sons used sanctuary service for personal gain, Samuel’s entire life was set apart to serve God with integrity from his early childhood.
