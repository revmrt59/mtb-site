LOOK - (râʼâh, Strong's H7200)

Summary:

The word ra'ah means to see, look at, observe, or perceive. In 1 Samuel 1:11, Hannah uses this word in her desperate prayer at the tabernacle, asking God to "look" on her affliction. Her request is not merely for God to notice her visually, but for Him to turn His active attention toward her grief, recognize her helpless condition, and respond with mercy.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

1 Samuel 1 opens during the turbulent era of Israel's judges, a time marked by widespread spiritual weakness and moral decay. Hannah, one of the two wives of Elkanah, suffered from barrenness while her rival, Peninnah, taunted her relentlessly. During the family's annual pilgrimage to the sanctuary at Shiloh, Hannah brought her unbearable sorrow directly to the Lord. Weeping bitterly outside the tabernacle, she made a solemn vow, crying out for God to look on her affliction. The verb ra'ah forms the starting point of her petition, expressing her deep desire that Israel's God would pay personal, divine attention to her private pain.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of ra'ah is physical sight—perceiving an object or person through the eyes. Beyond literal vision, the word naturally extends to mental perception, evaluation, and deliberate attention. When used of human beings, it means to look, notice, inspect, or understand. When used of God turning His eyes toward human distress, it describes His attentive awareness of a person's situation. The word itself does not automatically guarantee rescue or miraculous action; rather, it denotes turning one's vision and focus directly upon a person or situation, fully taking in their reality.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, ra'ah appears across every setting of daily life. It describes individuals observing physical landscapes, leaders inspecting buildings or armies, and people witnessing major historical events. In divine contexts, God's "seeing" carries immense significance. When God looks upon something, nothing is hidden from Him. At times, His looking leads to judgment when He beholds human evil; at other times, His looking leads to salvation when He sees the oppression of His people. In prayers of lament, asking God to "look" is an urgent appeal for Him to end His apparent silence and direct His active care toward the sufferer.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's prayer reflects a core truth revealed throughout Scripture: God is not distant, blind, or unconcerned with human suffering. When a worshipper asks God to "look," the prayer acknowledges His supreme authority and His capability to act. God's seeing is never passive or indifferent. While the word ra'ah fundamentally means to look or perceive, its placement within prayer shows that divine vision precedes divine intervention. Before God delivers, He looks; before He restores, He sees. The assurance that God beholds human anguish offers profound comfort to those who feel invisible or forgotten by the world around them.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:11, Hannah addresses God as the "LORD of hosts," acknowledging Him as the supreme ruler over all heavenly armies. Yet in the very same breath, she asks this grand Sovereign to "look" on her individual suffering. The verb form here is masculine singular imperfect, expressing a direct, continuous plea directed to the Lord. Hannah pairs her request to look with the verbs "remember" and "not forget." In ancient Israelite culture, childlessness brought severe social stigma and personal grief. By pleading with God to look at her affliction, Hannah lays bare her heart, trusting that the Almighty who sees her misery will not leave her in despair.

  • Psalms 128:6 – "Yes, may you see your children's children. Peace be upon Israel!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's cry in 1 Samuel 1:11 stands in sharp contrast with the human leadership present at Shiloh. While Eli the high priest looked at Hannah and wrongly concluded she was drunk, God looked at her heart and recognized her deep grief. Human eyes frequently misinterpret outer appearances, but divine vision sees with total clarity and true compassion. Hannah's confidence in God's seeing highlights the difference between human spiritual blindness and God's perfect, attentive care.
