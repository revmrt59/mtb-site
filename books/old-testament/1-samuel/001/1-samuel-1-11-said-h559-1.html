SAID - (ʼâmar, Strong's H559)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb amar means to speak, say, declare, or utter. It is the primary verb used throughout the Old Testament to introduce direct speech, spoken thoughts, formal declarations, and personal prayers. In 1 Samuel 1:11, the word introduces Hannah's solemn vow to God, marking the moment where her deep personal grief is voiced directly to the Lord in a prayer that alters her life and Israel's history.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb amar is the foundational expression for speech in biblical Hebrew narrative. Rather than merely describing the act of vocalization, Hebrew writers use this word to introduce dialogue, reveal inner thoughts, record divine decrees, and chronicle human responses. In ancient Hebrew narrative, reported speech drives the storyline forward and reveals character far more than abstract description.

In the opening chapter of 1 Samuel, Israel is in a period of spiritual weakness under the leadership of the judge and priest Eli. Hannah, living in a polygamous household, suffers severe distress due to childlessness and perpetual provocation. When she goes to the tabernacle at Shiloh, her grief moves her to approach God directly. The narrative uses amar at the moment her quiet suffering turns into an articulated vow before the Lord of hosts.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, amar means to say, tell, or speak. It carries several primary shades of meaning depending on who speaks and the setting of the speech:

  • Simple speech: Expressing words directly to another person in everyday conversation.

  • Internal speech: Thinking or resolving something within oneself, often translated as saying "in one's heart."

  • Divine command: Speaking with authoritative power, as when God commands creation or reveals His word to prophets.

  • Formal declaration or vow: Stating a binding promise, prayer, or covenantal commitment before witnesses or before God.

In 1 Samuel 1:11, the word functions in this last sense. It introduces the precise words of Hannah's vow, bringing her internal sorrow into clear, spoken commitment before the Lord.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament narrative and poetry, amar appears in a wide variety of communicative contexts:

  • When God speaks, His speech carries divine authority and creative energy.

  • When leaders or prophets speak, the word introduces instructions, warnings, or proclamations.

  • When individuals pray, the verb records human petitions directed toward heaven.

The word itself is flexible and simple, acquiring its specific emotional or formal tone from the surrounding context. Whether introducing a brief greeting, a royal decree, or an agonizing prayer, the verb serves as the doorway into the speaker's mind and heart.

4. Theological Implications

The use of amar in prayer highlights a central biblical truth: God is a listener who receives human speech. Hannah does not address a distant or uncaring deity. By recording what she "said," Scripture demonstrates that God welcomes the concrete, honest words of His people.

Furthermore, Hannah’s spoken vow shows how verbal prayer aligns personal grief with God's broader purposes. Hannah asks for a son, but through her vow she offers him back to God for lifelong service. Her spoken words transform a private burden into a servant for God's kingdom, illustrating how honest vocal prayer opens the door for God to work in and through human trial.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:11, the grammatical form is a feminine singular imperfect verb with a narrative prefix (wayyiqtol). This form carries the sequential narrative action forward: Hannah wept bitterly, made a vow, and "said" her prayer.

The context highlights a striking contrast between human misunderstanding and divine attention. As Hannah prayed, her lips moved, but her voice was not heard aloud, leading Eli the priest to accuse her of drunkenness. Yet the text explicitly records what she "said" in her vow to the Lord. Even when human leaders fail to understand quiet or broken prayer, God hears the words spoken to Him in faith.

Her spoken vow asking for a male child and promising to dedicate him as a Nazirite forms the turning point of the chapter. What Hannah said in her distress laid the groundwork for the birth of Samuel, the prophet who would guide Israel out of spiritual darkness.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The recorded prayer in 1 Samuel 1:11 is the first time in Scripture that God is addressed as the "LORD of hosts" (YHWH Tseva'ot). Hannah's choice of words shows that even in her personal powerlessness, she spoke to the God who commands all the armies of heaven. The simple verb "said" introduces one of the most faith-filled and historically significant prayers in the Old Testament.
