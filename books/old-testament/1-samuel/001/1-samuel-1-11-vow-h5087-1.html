VOW - (nâdar, Strong's H5087)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nadar means to make a solemn promise or vow to God, voluntarily committing oneself to give an offering, perform a service, or devote something to Him, often in connection with a specific prayer. In 1 Samuel 1:11, this word describes Hannah's desperate yet faithful action as she vows that if the Lord grants her a male child, she will yield him completely back to the Lord's service for all the days of his life.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, a vow was a sacred, voluntary commitment made directly to God. People frequently made vows during times of severe distress, danger, or deep personal grief, asking God for deliverance, healing, or help. A person was under no divine command to make a vow, but once spoken before the Lord, the promise became a holy obligation that had to be faithfully fulfilled.

The word appears in key moments across Israel's history—from patriarchs making pledges on journeys to leaders making vows before battle and worshippers offering sacrifices at the sanctuary. In the narrative of 1 Samuel, Israel was experiencing a period of spiritual lowliness under the priesthood of Eli's corrupt sons. Hannah's private, heartfelt vow stands out as a bright light of sincere worship during a spiritually dark time, leading to the birth of Samuel, who would help shepherd Israel into a new era.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Nadar refers to the act of making a binding verbal pledge to God. When a person uses this word, they are formally declaring an intent to devote something of value to the Lord—such as a sacrifice, a portion of property, a period of special devotion, or a dedicated life.

The word can mean:

  • To promise something to God conditionally (asking God for aid and pledging a response upon receiving His help).

  • To make a voluntary pledge of devotion or worship without attached conditions.

  • To bind oneself legally and spiritually before the Lord to fulfill a specific word spoken in prayer.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture consistently distinguishes between required obligations under the law and the voluntary nature of nadar. God never commanded His people to make vows, but He strictly commanded them to honor any vow they freely chose to speak.

The Old Testament highlights two important aspects of nadar:

  • Absolute Integrity: A spoken vow could not be treated lightly or retracted at convenience. To vow meant placing one's moral integrity directly before God.

  • Worshipful Gratitude: Vows were typically paired with praise and thanksgiving. When God answered the request made during a vow, the worshipper would return to the sanctuary to fulfill the promise and publicly testify to God's faithfulness.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of nadar reveals significant truths about prayer, human stewardship, and divine sovereignty:

  • Prayer Driven by Faith: Making a vow to God demonstrates a deep conviction that God is listening and possesses the power to intervene in human suffering.

  • Surrender of Blessing: True biblical vows do not attempt to bribe or manipulate God. Rather, they acknowledge that every blessing comes from Him, and they express a willingness to give back to God what He graciously provides.

  • God's Respect for Human Words: God holds human speech accountable. Sacred promises made to the Almighty reflect the seriousness of walking in truth and reverent fear.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:11, Hannah is deeply distressed because of her barrenness and the persistent provocation she faces from Peninnah. As she prays silently at the tabernacle in Shiloh, the text records that she "vowed a vow" (expressed by the verb nadar in narrative form). Her prayer is not an attempt to bargain selfishly with God for social standing. Instead, her heart's desire is centered on God's glory and the needs of His house.

The grammatical form of the verb presents her action as a direct, sequential part of her prayer before the Lord. She addresses God as the "LORD of hosts"—a title highlighting His supreme power—and asks Him to look upon her affliction. Her vow commits the requested son to perpetual service at the sanctuary, marked by the Nazirite dedication of never having a razor touch his head. In this passage, nadar highlights the transition from Hannah's personal grief to her magnificent surrender, laying the foundation for Samuel's crucial ministry as prophet and judge.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 30:2 — "If a man makes a vow to the LORD, or swears an oath to bind himself by some agreement, he shall not break his word; he shall do according to all that proceeds out of his mouth."

  • Deuteronomy 23:21 — "When you make a vow to the LORD your God, you shall not delay to pay it; for the LORD your God will surely require it of you, and it would be sin to you."

  • Psalms 132:2 — "How he swore to the LORD, and vowed to the Mighty One of Jacob..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's use of nadar in 1 Samuel 1:11 is unique in the Old Testament because she vows to dedicate an unborn child to lifelong service. Typical vows under the Law of Moses involved animal sacrifices, money, or temporary periods of consecration, such as a temporary Nazirite vow. Hannah’s promise went far beyond temporary devotion, asking for a child only to surrender him permanently to God's sanctuary. This extraordinary surrender illustrates how God can transform deep personal suffering into a blessing that serves an entire nation.
