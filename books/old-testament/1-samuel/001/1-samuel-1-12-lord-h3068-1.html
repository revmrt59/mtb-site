LORD - (YHWH, Strong's H3068)

Summary:

YHWH (rendered as "LORD" in capital letters in English Bibles) is the personal, covenant name of the God of Israel. Rather than a generic title like "God" or "Master," it designates the specific, living God who revealed Himself to Moses and entered into a personal relationship with His people. In 1 Samuel 1:12, this sacred name identifies the true God before whom Hannah pours out her heart in silent prayer, showing that her petition is directed to the faithful God of Israel who hears and remembers His people.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the Old Testament, the name YHWH represents the unique personal name of God. English translations traditionally render this name in small capital letters as "LORD." This translation practice reflects a long-standing Jewish tradition where, out of deep reverence for the divine name, readers substituted the title Adonai ("Lord") whenever YHWH appeared in the text.

Unlike general titles such as Elohim (which means "God" or "Mighty One"), YHWH functions as God's proper name. God revealed this name in a foundational way to Moses at the burning bush, identifying Himself as "I AM WHO I AM." The name marks God as the self-existent, living God who reaches out to form lasting covenants with human beings.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

YHWH is the personal name of Israel's God.

The word refers to:

  • The personal identity of the living God of Israel.

  • The God who revealed Himself to Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, and Moses.

  • The sovereign Lord who initiates and keeps His covenant promises.

While descriptive terms like Elohim highlight God's power as Creator, YHWH identifies who God is in personal relationship with His worshipers. It is not a title shared with earthly kings or idols, but belongs exclusively to the God of Israel.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, YHWH appears regularly when the text emphasizes prayer, worship, covenant promises, and personal relationship.

When people cry out in distress, make vows, or give thanks, Scripture frequently uses this personal name. This highlights that God is not a distant force, but a living person who listens and responds. God's people do not offer prayers to an unknown deity; they pray to YHWH, the God who has bound Himself to them in grace.

4. Theological Implications

The name YHWH grounds the believer's trust in God's faithful character. Because it is God's covenant name, any promise made under this name is backed by God's unchangeable nature.

The revelation of this name demonstrates that God desires to be known. Unlike the gods of neighboring ancient nations—which were tied to physical idols or local natural forces—YHWH is the supreme Lord of creation who graciously welcomes His people into fellowship with Himself.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:12, Hannah stands at the tabernacle in Shiloh, offering persistent, silent prayer. The text highlights that "she continued praying before the LORD."

Grammatically, the name YHWH appears here as a proper noun, directly identifying the recipient of Hannah's prayer. Its use in this passage brings out several key points:

  • Direct Access: Hannah does not direct her anguish to an abstract power; she presents her petition straight to the personal God of Israel.

  • Covenant Refuge: Living during a period of spiritual decline in Israel, Hannah seeks refuge in the God who cares for the humble and afflicted.

  • True Presence: Praying "before the LORD" indicates that Hannah deliberately places herself in God's presence, confident that YHWH sees her sorrow even when human observers fail to understand her.

Hannah anchors her petition in the personal care and faithfulness of YHWH.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:4 — "This is the history of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens."

  • Exodus 13:5 — "And it shall be, when the LORD brings you into the land of the Canaanites..."

  • Joshua 14:12 — "Now therefore, give me this mountain of which the LORD spoke in that day..."

  • Psalms 25:1 — "To You, O LORD, I lift up my soul."

  • Jeremiah 33:1 — "Moreover the word of the LORD came to Jeremiah the second time..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In 1 Samuel 1:12, Eli the priest sees Hannah moving her lips without speaking aloud and mistakenly accuses her of drunkenness. The human spiritual leadership at Shiloh was insensitive and quick to judge. However, the text contrasts Eli's misunderstanding with Hannah's real activity: she is standing before YHWH. Human leaders may misinterpret a believer's deep grief, but YHWH knows the silent cries of the heart and responds with mercy.
