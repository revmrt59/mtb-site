DRUNK - (shikkôwr, Strong's H7910)

Summary:

The Hebrew word shikkor describes someone who is intoxicated, drunken, or overcome by alcoholic drink. It refers to a state of mental confusion, physical staggering, and loss of self-control caused by excessive wine. In 1 Samuel 1:13, Eli wrongly assumes that Hannah is drunk because her lips move in silent, earnest prayer without her voice being heard. The word highlights Eli's superficial, mistaken judgment, contrasting human spiritual insensitivity with Hannah's deep, heartbroken devotion before the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, religious feasting was a regular part of sacrificial worship at Shiloh, where the tabernacle was located. Israelite families would travel to Shiloh to offer sacrifices, give thanks, and share a sacrificial meal that often included food and wine. Because wine was part of these worship celebrations, excessive drinking could sometimes occur during festival times.

Eli, the high priest, sat by the doorpost of the tabernacle watching the worshipers. When Hannah approached the tabernacle in deep emotional distress because of her childlessness, her posture was unfamiliar to Eli. Rather than praying aloud, as was common practice in ancient Israelite worship, Hannah prayed silently from her heart. Seeing her lips moving continuously with no sound emerging, Eli jumped to the conclusion that she was intoxicated from the sacrificial feasting.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word describes a person who is intoxicated or impaired by alcohol. It indicates a physical and mental state where strong drink distorts speech, judgment, and bodily coordination. The term can refer to temporary drunkenness or describe someone characterized by habitual intoxication.

In Scripture, the word carries a consistently negative meaning. It is associated with loss of self-control, shame, spiritual dullness, and physical instability. When applied to a person, it points to a condition where normal reasoning and self-governance are disrupted by drink. In 1 Samuel 1:13, the grammatical form is feminine singular, describing Hannah as Eli perceived her.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, the word appears in two main contexts: literal intoxication and figurative staggering under divine judgment.

Literally, Scripture uses the word for individuals overcome by wine during feasts or binges, such as Nabal or King Elah. In these historical accounts, being drunk leads to vulnerability, foolish decision-making, or sudden tragedy.

Metaphorically, the word describes physical instability or spiritual confusion. Mariners tossed by violent ocean waves stagger like a drunken man, unable to keep their footing. Prophets and rulers who lack spiritual discernment are described as drunken, reeled by confusion rather than wine. Similarly, nations facing God's judgment are pictured as drinking a cup of wrath that leaves them staggering and helpless.

4. Theological Implications

Eli's accusation against Hannah illustrates the sharp contrast between outward appearance and inner spiritual reality. Human leaders can misjudge deeply spiritual devotion because they evaluate only what is visible to the eye. Hannah was pouring out her soul to God in pure faith, yet the high priest mistook her deep grief for shameful drunkenness.

This passage also highlights the danger of spiritual dullness in religious leadership. Eli was quick to condemn a hurting woman, yet slow to discipline his own sons, who were committing genuine wickedness at the tabernacle. Misinterpreting spiritual passion as carnal excess reflects a failure of discernment.

Furthermore, the passage sets up a contrast later echoed in the New Testament: the physical disturbance of drunkenness stands in opposition to the genuine movement of the Holy Spirit. While drunkenness clouds the mind and leads to unrestrained foolishness, true spiritual prayer brings clear, honest pouring out of the heart before God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:13, Hannah's grief over her bareness and the ongoing provocation from her rival Peninnah drove her to intense prayer. She was weeping bitterly and making a vow to the Lord. Her mouth moved in response to the overwhelming emotion in her heart, but she uttered no audible words.

Eli observed her from a distance. Expecting standard vocalized prayers or perhaps accustomed to worshipers indulging too freely in wine during the feasts, he interpreted her silent, moving lips as the incoherent mumblings of a drunken woman. He confronted her directly, demanding that she put away her wine.

Hannah's respectful reply exposes Eli's error. She was not a drunken woman, but a woman of a sorrowful spirit who had consumed neither wine nor strong drink. Her physical movements were not produced by intoxication, but by the abundance of her grief and fervor before the Lord.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 25:36 — "Now it happened, in the morning, when the wine had gone out of Nabal, and his wife had told him these things, that his heart died within him, and he became like a stone." (Here Nabal was holding a royal feast and was holding himself "drunk".)

  • 1 Kings 16:9 — "Now his servant Zimri, commander of half his chariots, conspired against him as he was in Tirzah drinking himself "drunk" in the house of Arza, steward of his house in Tirzah."

  • Psalms 107:27 — "They reel to and fro, and stagger like a "drunken" man, and are at their wits’ end."

  • Jeremiah 23:9 — "My heart within me is broken because of the prophets; all my bones shake. I am like a "drunken" man, and like a man whom wine has overcome, because of the Lord, and because of His holy words."

  • Joel 1:5 — "Awake, you "drunkards", and weep; and wail, all you drinkers of wine, because of the new wine, for it has been cut off from your mouth."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's experience shows that silent prayer is fully recognized and honored by God. Even though no human voice was heard and the high priest misunderstood her actions, the Lord heard her quiet petition and remembered her.

There is a striking irony in the narrative structure of early 1 Samuel. Eli accused Hannah of being a wicked, drunken woman, yet it was Eli's own sons, Hophni and Phinehas, who were genuinely vile men taking advantage of the sacred offerings. Eli wrongly judged a righteous worshiper while failing to correct true corruption in his own sanctuary. God vindicated Hannah by granting her request and giving her a son, Samuel, who would grow up to be a true prophet in Israel.
