HANNAH - (Channâh, Strong's H2584)

Summary:

The feminine proper name Channah comes from a Hebrew root meaning "grace" or "favor." In 1 Samuel 1:13, the name identifies the childless, grief-stricken woman who prays silently from her heart inside the tabernacle. In this passage, the name highlights Hannah as the heartbroken worshiper seeking God's favor in quiet sincerity, standing in sharp contrast to the high priest Eli, who misjudges her silent devotion as drunkenness.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Hannah belongs to the wife of Elkanah who lived during the turbulent period of the judges in Israel. During this era, central leadership was lacking, spiritual devotion across the nation had declined, and the priesthood at Shiloh was compromised by corruption.

Hannah suffered from barrenness, a condition that carried severe social stigma and deep personal grief in ancient Israel. Compounding her heartache, her husband's second wife, Peninnah, provoked her constantly about her inability to bear children. Each year, when the family traveled to the sanctuary at Shiloh for annual worship and sacrifice, Hannah brought her heavy burden before the Lord. It is in this setting of personal grief and spiritual earnestness that Hannah approaches God in prayer.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Channah is a feminine proper name derived from the Hebrew verb meaning "to show favor" or "to be gracious." The name translates as "grace," "favor," or "gracious one."

In biblical history, names often reflected significant spiritual themes or personal characteristics. While Channah functions simply as a proper personal name, its root meaning connects directly to the core need of her life: her reliance on God's unmerited favor. Hannah was a woman who lacked earthly power or societal prestige, yet she threw herself entirely upon the gracious character of the Lord.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Old Testament, this personal name refers exclusively to the mother of the prophet Samuel. The root of her name (ch-n-n) shares an immediate connection with the common Hebrew noun for favor (chen).

This verbal connection runs throughout the opening narrative of 1 Samuel. When Hannah speaks to Eli after he realizes his mistake, she asks that she might "find favor" in his eyes. Her very name serves as a subtle reminder of what she seeks from God and people alike. She does not come demanding what she deserves; she comes asking the God of all grace to look upon her humble condition and extend His favor.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's experience illustrates how God interacts with the brokenhearted and forgotten. While human society often favored women with many children, God listened to the desperate cry of a woman whom others pitied or mocked.

Her life demonstrates that true prayer does not depend on loud words or public recognition. Hannah prayed quietly from the depths of her soul. God heard her silent petition and responded graciously, showing that He exalts the humble and provides for those who trust in Him. Furthermore, God used Hannah's personal grief and subsequent joy to bring about a turning point in redemptive history, giving Israel her last great judge and first major prophet, Samuel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:13, the narrator focuses on the inner nature of Hannah's prayer: "Now Hannah spoke in her heart; only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard." In standard ancient worship, prayers were typically recited aloud. Hannah's silent, heartfelt communication was so unusual that Eli assumed she was drunk and rebuked her.

The passage uses her name to direct full attention to her individual posture before God. While Eli judged her strictly by outward appearance, Hannah was actually pouring out her soul in deep grief. The presence of her name here emphasizes the tragedy of the moment: the high priest at Shiloh failed to recognize a truly godly, sorrowful woman seeking divine grace, mistaking her silent, earnest prayer for ungodly behavior.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah’s silent prayer provides a profound example of interior worship in the Old Testament. It demonstrates that God hears the unexpressed groans of the heart just as clearly as loud public petitions.

Additionally, the tragic contrast between Eli’s quick willingness to condemn Hannah and his inability to restrain his own wicked sons exposes the spiritual blindness of the priesthood at that time. Hannah’s quiet, persistent faith stands out as a bright light against the backdrop of spiritual decay, showing that genuine faith can flourish even when spiritual leaders fail.
