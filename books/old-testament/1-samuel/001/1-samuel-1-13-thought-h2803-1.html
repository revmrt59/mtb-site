THOUGHT - (châshab, Strong's H2803)

Summary:

The Hebrew word chashav means to think, calculate, reckon, or form a judgment about something. It describes the mental process of weighing facts or appearances to arrive at a conclusion. In 1 Samuel 1:13, the word describes how the high priest Eli observed Hannah’s silent prayer and formed a wrong opinion, mistakenly judging her deep grief to be drunkenness.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The opening chapter of 1 Samuel records the sorrow of Hannah, who suffered from barrenness and severe emotional distress. While at the sanctuary in Shiloh, Hannah poured out her soul before God. She prayed silently, moving her lips without making an audible sound. Sitting nearby, Eli the priest watched her actions closely.

The verb chashav is an everyday Hebrew word used in various aspects of life, including commercial counting, artistic design, legal accounting, and personal evaluation. In this passage, it describes a mental assessment: an observer looking at visual evidence and making a determination about what is happening.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can mean to consider, calculate, estimate, or form an opinion.

  • When a person is evaluating a situation, the word means forming a mental judgment based on observable evidence or reasoning.

  • When used in context with plans or schemes, it means devising or plotting a course of action.

  • When used in administrative, financial, or sacrificial contexts, it means reckoning, crediting, or accounting a status to a person or item.

In all its uses, the word involves an active mental process of weighing information to assign a value, intent, or meaning to something.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses chashav for both human and divine assessments. The word itself describes the mental act of calculating, but the accuracy of the calculation depends entirely on who is doing the thinking.

When God is the subject, His evaluation is always true and righteous. God reckons righteousness based on faith, or computes true spiritual value without error. When human beings are the subject, their thinking relies on outward appearances, limited perspective, or flawed motives. A human calculation can be accurate, but it can also be a complete misjudgment.

4. Theological Implications

Eli’s assessment of Hannah highlights the severe limits of human discernment. Eli looked at the physical signs—moving lips and silence—and used his mind to process what he saw. He reached a firm conclusion, but his conclusion was wrong.

This passage demonstrates that human evaluation often misinterprets true spiritual devotion. While human leaders assess outward actions and standard practices, God directly sees the internal state of the heart. Hannah’s quiet, desperate prayer was invisible to Eli’s spiritual perception, but it was fully known and accepted by God. Worship and prayer cannot always be judged by external conventions.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:13, the narrative emphasizes the contrast between Hannah’s inner life and Eli’s external assessment. Hannah spoke in her heart, meaning her prayer was entirely internal and unvoiced.

Eli observed her moving lips and absence of sound, and he calculated what this behavior meant. The verb appears in a masculine singular imperfect form with a sequential conjunction, indicating the immediate action Eli took upon seeing her: he formed the opinion that she was drunk. He took a legitimate visual observation and turned it into an erroneous moral charge. This misjudgment sets up Hannah’s gentle, respectful correction in the following verses, where she explains her intense anguish, turning Eli’s harsh assumption into a blessing.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 15:6 — "And he believed in the LORD, and He accounted it to him for righteousness."

  • Esther 9:25 — "...his wicked plot which he had devised against the Jews should return on his own head..."

  • Psalms 106:31 — "And that was accounted to him for righteousness to all generations forevermore."

  • Isaiah 10:7 — "Yet he does not mean so, nor does his heart think so; but it is in his heart to destroy..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Eli was accustomed to standard, audible worship at the tabernacle. Because Hannah’s intense, quiet petition did not fit the customary pattern he expected to see, he defaulted to a negative assumption.

This passage serves as a valuable reminder for spiritual leadership. It cautions believers against hastily judging the spiritual condition or emotional pain of others based on external gestures or non-traditional expressions of grief. God understands the silent cries of the heart even when earthly observers misinterpret them.
