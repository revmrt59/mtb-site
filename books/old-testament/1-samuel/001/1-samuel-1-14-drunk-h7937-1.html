DRUNK - (shâkar, Strong's H7937)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shakar means to become intoxicated, to drink deeply, or to display the effects of strong drink. In 1 Samuel 1:14, Eli uses the word to accuse Hannah of being drunk, foolishly mistaking her silent, grief-stricken prayer before the Lord for public intoxication.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, wine and fermented drink were common parts of daily life, agricultural celebrations, and sacrificial feasts. While drinking wine in moderation during religious feasting was customary, public drunkenness was viewed as a loss of self-control and moral clarity. At the tabernacle sanctuary in Shiloh, worshippers gathered annually to offer sacrifices and share in sacred meals.

During the period of the judges, spiritual discernment in Israel had fallen into severe decline. The priesthood at Shiloh under Eli was characterized by lax discipline, and Eli's own sons were known for flagrant ungodliness. Feasting at the sanctuary sometimes devolved into unseemly revelry. This corrupt atmosphere forms the backdrop of 1 Samuel 1, where Eli observes Hannah praying near the doorpost of the tabernacle and quickly presumes the worst about her behavior.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shakar basic meaning is to become intoxicated, to drink to excess, or to act drunk. Depending on context, it can describe literally drinking strong drink until physical impairment occurs, or it can be used figuratively to describe staggering under divine judgment or being deeply satiated.

When describing human behavior, the word focuses on the physical and mental state caused by overindulgence in alcohol—loss of normal bodily control, impaired speech, and foolish conduct. In 1 Samuel 1:14, the verb form is feminine singular and imperfect, describing Eli's perception of Hannah's ongoing condition as she continues to move her lips without making a sound.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses shakar across several distinct settings:

  • Literal intoxication: The word describes physical drunkenness resulting from excessive wine, often bringing shame or vulnerability, as seen in the accounts of Noah and Uriah.

  • Festive abundance: In celebratory contexts, the word can refer to drinking freely or being merry with companions during a feast, without necessarily conveying moral condemnation.

  • Figurative judgment: Prophetic passages frequently employ the word to depict God forcing rebellious nations to drink the cup of His wrath until they stagger, lose their footing, and fall helpless.

  • Physical consumption: In contexts regarding basic physical sustenance, the verb can refer to drinking to one's fill or satisfaction.

4. Theological Implications

The imagery of drunkenness in Scripture carries significant theological weight. Literally, drunkenness represents a lack of sobriety and self-restraint. Figuratively, it serves as a picture of spiritual stupor, confusion, and divine retribution.

In 1 Samuel 1, Eli’s use of shakar reveals a profound spiritual irony. While Eli failed to restrain his own sons from rampant wickedness, he was quick to misjudge a godly woman pouring out her heart to God. His inability to distinguish true intercession from intoxication exposes the spiritual daze that had overtaken the priesthood. Drunkenness dulls the senses, but Eli’s spiritual dullness caused him to mistake deep, sober prayer for the very excess he failed to discipline among his own family.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

When Hannah came to Shiloh, she was burdened by childlessness and the persistent provocation of her rival. Desperate for God's mercy, she prayed silently in the tabernacle. Her lips moved as she poured out her soul, but her voice was inaudible.

Watching from his seat by the doorpost, Eli saw her moving lips and concluded she was intoxicated. He confronted her bluntly: "How long will you be drunk? Put away your wine from you!" Eli used shakar to accuse her of lingering in a state of drunkenness right at the sanctuary.

Hannah’s respectful response highlights the contrast between appearance and reality. She explained that she was neither drunk with wine nor strong drink, but was speaking out of the abundance of her grief and fervor. Eli’s hasty accusation serves as a warning against fleshly judgment, while Hannah’s quiet dignity demonstrates true spiritual devotion in the midst of misunderstanding.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah directly contrasts her physical state with her spiritual act. While Eli accused her of being full of wine (shakar), Hannah explained that she was "pouring out" her soul before the Lord. The juxtaposition between pouring wine into oneself and pouring out one's grief to God provides a striking contrast in the narrative.

Additionally, Hannah's gentle, humble clarification turned Eli's harsh rebuke into a pastoral blessing. Once Eli realized his mistake, he pronounced a blessing over her petition, which God subsequently answered with the birth of Samuel.
