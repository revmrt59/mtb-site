LONG - (mâthay, Strong's H4970)

Summary:

The word matay is a Hebrew interrogative term that asks a question about time, meaning "when?" When combined with the preposition meaning "until," it forms the common expression "how long?" or "until when?"

In 1 Samuel 1:14, Eli uses the word to confront Hannah, mistakenly believing she is lingering in a state of drunkenness at the tabernacle. His question highlights his quick, erroneous judgment of a grieved woman who was actually praying silently before the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew word matay serves as a basic question word regarding time. It is used throughout the Old Testament in everyday conversations, prophetic warnings, royal appeals, and temple prayers. In ancient Israel, asking "when?" or "how long?" was a natural way to inquire about the timing, duration, or end of an event or condition.

In 1 Samuel 1, the setting is the sanctuary at Shiloh during an annual feast. Hannah, grieved by years of childlessness and bitter provocation, came to pray before God. Eli, the aged high priest, sat near the doorpost of the tabernacle. Observing Hannah moving her lips without making any audible sound, Eli jumped to a wrong conclusion about her behavior and challenged her directly.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Matay refers strictly to time. On its own, it means "when?" When paired with the preposition 'ad ("until"), as it is in 1 Samuel 1:14, it forms the phrase 'ad-matay, which means "until when?" or "how long?"

The term does not carry moral or emotional weight by itself. Instead, it functions as a tool for inquiry, asking about:

  • The point in time when something will occur.

  • The duration of an ongoing action or state.

  • The expected end of a difficult or controversial situation.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, matay appears in several distinct conversational settings:

  • Human Dialogue: People use it to ask when a task will be completed, when a meeting will occur, or how long a conflict will drag on.

  • Prophetic and Divine Confrontation: God or His prophets use it to challenge persistent sin, asking how long people will refuse to obey, humble themselves, or turn from evil.

  • Prayers of Lamentation: Worshipers and psalmists frequently cry out, "How long, O Lord?" to ask when God will intervene, bring deliverance, or end their suffering.

In every case, the word draws attention to time and duration, reflecting the speaker's impatience, concern, or urgent desire for resolution.

4. Theological Implications

While matay is simply a word about time, its biblical usage touches on important spiritual themes:

  • Human Impatience and Divine Timing: The question "how long?" often arises when human expectations clash with God's timing. Believers frequently ask God when relief will come, learning trust through waiting.

  • The Limits of Human Judgment: Eli's use of the word shows how easily human religious leaders can misinterpret spiritual realities. Eli saw outward movement without sound and assumed drunkenness, whereas God saw a devoted heart offering silent prayer.

  • Divine Patience and Human Delay: When God asks "how long?", it highlights human stubbornness in delaying repentance, contrasting God's patience with human reluctance to obey.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:14, Eli addresses Hannah with sharp accusation: "How long will you be drunk? Put away your wine from you!"

Here, the word "long" reflects Eli's belief that Hannah was lingering at the tabernacle in an unfit, intoxicated condition. He assumed her silent posture and trembling lips were the result of excessive drinking during the sacrificial feast.

This creates a striking irony in the narrative:

  • Eli, the high priest responsible for discerning spiritual matters, completely misjudges Hannah's godly grief.

  • Hannah, a humble and oppressed woman, possesses the true spirit of prayer that Eli fails to recognize.

Eli's harsh question exposes the spiritual decay of the priesthood at Shiloh at that time, setting the stage for God to raise up Samuel as a faithful priest and prophet.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 10:3 — Moses and Aaron ask Pharaoh on God's behalf how "long" he will refuse to humble himself before the Lord.

  • 2 Samuel 2:26 — Abner asks Joab how "long" the sword will devour before calling off the pursuit of fellow Israelites.

  • Isaiah 6:11 — Isaiah asks the Lord how "long" his message of judgment must be proclaimed to an unyielding nation.

  • Habakkuk 2:6 — The prophet pronounces woe upon the plunderer, asking how "long" he will continue to heap up stolen goods.

  • Zechariah 1:12 — The Angel of the Lord asks how "long" God will withhold mercy from Jerusalem and the cities of Judah.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's quiet, dignified response to Eli's accusation is noteworthy. Rather than reacting with anger at his misjudgment, she respectfully corrects his mistaken assumption, explaining that she was pouring out her soul before the Lord out of great grief.

Eli immediately retracts his harsh assumption and offers a blessing: "Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition." This exchange shows how honest, soft-spoken clarification can turn an unjust accusation into an occasion for blessing.
