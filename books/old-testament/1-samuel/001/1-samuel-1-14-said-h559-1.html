SAID - (ʼâmar, Strong's H559)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar means to say, speak, declare, or express a thought. It is the primary word used in the Old Testament to report direct speech, whether uttered aloud, spoken inwardly to oneself, or declared as an authoritative command. In 1 Samuel 1:14, the word introduces High Priest Eli's spoken rebuke of Hannah, where he rashly accuses her of drunkenness while she is actually praying silently in the sanctuary.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb 'amar is the basic Hebrew word for verbal communication. It appears across all genres of Old Testament literature, from simple everyday dialogue to divine decrees in creation and prophetic announcements.

In the narrative setting of 1 Samuel 1, Israel was experiencing a time of spiritual lowliness near the end of the period of the judges. Worship took place at the tabernacle in Shiloh under the aging priest Eli and his wicked sons. The chapter contrasts two distinct forms of communication before God: Hannah’s silent, desperate prayer from the heart, and Eli’s misguided spoken confrontation from the seat of authority.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

centered around the expression of thought into words, 'amar can mean:

  • To speak or say something directly to another person.

  • To declare or proclaim an instruction or decree.

  • To think or say within one's heart (expressing internal thought).

  • To answer or respond in conversation.

Depending on who is speaking and the context of the statement, the verb can introduce a simple greeting, an imperial command, a prayer, or an accusation. In 1 Samuel 1:14, the verb introduces Eli’s immediate verbal reprimand directed toward Hannah.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Biblical Hebrew storytelling, direct speech introduced by 'amar drives the plot forward and discloses the character and heart of the speaker. Rather than merely describing events from a distance, biblical narrative frequently relies on dialogue to show how individuals relate to God and one another.

When God is the subject, the word introduces authoritative commands that bring creation into being or reveal His holy will to prophets. When human beings speak, the word demonstrates human wisdom, fear, faith, or spiritual blindness. The verb often sets up a contrast between human perception and divine reality, showing that what people say often reflects their limited or flawed understanding.

4. Theological Implications

Eli's spoken word in 1 Samuel 1:14 highlights the fallibility of human spiritual leaders. Even an ordained priest serving in the house of the Lord can look at outward behavior, misinterpret a godly heart, and utter an unjust accusation. Human speech based strictly on surface observations can easily wound the innocent.

By contrast, the passage reveals that God hears the unuttered prayers of the heart. Hannah's silent plea was heard by the Lord, even though Eli misjudged it as the slurred speech of a drunken woman. This demonstrates that true communication with God depends not on loud or public speech, but on the genuine intent and sorrow of the soul.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:13, the text notes that Hannah "spoke in her heart; only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard." Because Eli saw her lips moving without sound, he concluded she was intoxicated after the feast.

In verse 14, Eli turns his mistaken assumption into direct speech: "So Eli said to her, 'How long will you be drunk? Put away your wine from you!'" The narrative form of the verb presents his reaction as immediate—Eli observed her external appearance and immediately spoke words of condemnation. This tragic misjudgment illustrates the spiritual decay at Shiloh. The priest who should have offered comfort and shepherd care to a suffering soul instead spoke harsh words of false accusation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:3 — Then God "said", "Let there be light"; and there was light.

  • Genesis 45:28 — Then Israel "said", "It is enough. Joseph my son is still alive. I will go and see him before I die."

  • 1 Samuel 20:42 — Then Jonathan "said" to David, "Go in peace, since we have both sworn in the name of the LORD..."

  • Psalms 27:8 — When You "said", "Seek My face," My heart "said" to You, "Your face, LORD, I will seek."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The opening chapters of 1 Samuel place a heavy emphasis on voice, speaking, and hearing. Eli's rash words to Hannah in chapter 1 contrast sharply with God's later speech to the young boy Samuel in chapter 3, where Eli fails to recognize God's voice until it is repeated several times. The spiritual decline of Shiloh is marked by priests who speak hastily without spiritual discernment and fail to hear the true word of the Lord.
