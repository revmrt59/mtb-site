WINE - (yayin, Strong's H3196)

Summary:

The Hebrew word yayin refers to fermented wine made from grapes. Throughout the Old Testament, it describes a common beverage used in daily life, sacred worship, and celebratory gatherings, while also carrying potential for abuse when consumed in excess. In 1 Samuel 1:14, high priest Eli wrongly accuses Hannah of intoxication, demanding that she put away her wine. In this passage, the word exposes Eli's tragic lack of spiritual discernment, as he mistake Hannah's intense, grief-stricken prayer for drunken disorder.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word yayin is the most frequent Hebrew term for fermented grape wine in ancient Israel. Winemaking was an integral part of agricultural and social life in the ancient Near East. Grapes were harvested, trodden in winepresses, and fermented in jars or wineskins. Wine served as a standard household beverage, a symbol of agricultural prosperity, and an element in formal offerings at the sanctuary.

During the period of the Judges, the central sanctuary was located at Shiloh. Hannah traveled there with her husband Elkanah to worship the Lord. Deeply distressed by her barrenness and provoked by her rival, Hannah entered the tabernacle area to pray. As she wept and poured out her heart, her silent prayer was misinterpreted by Eli, the aging high priest whose household was falling into spiritual ruin.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

yayin refers directly to fermented wine. Depending on the context, the word can mean:

  • A standard daily beverage consumed at meals and family gatherings.

  • A sacred element used as a drink offering accompanying sacrifices.

  • An intoxicating drink that, when overconsumed, leads to loss of self-control, slurred speech, and ungodly behavior.

Here in 1 Samuel 1:14, the word refers specifically to the intoxicating beverage Eli presumed Hannah had consumed before arriving at the sanctuary.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture presents wine with clear balance. God gave the grapevine, and wine is often mentioned alongside grain and oil as a sign of earthly blessing and refreshment. In liturgical settings, wine was poured out before the Lord as an expression of worship and gratitude.

At the same time, Old Testament writers repeatedly warn against the dangers of drunkenness. Intoxication dulls moral judgment, leads to mockery, and brings shame. The word yayin itself is morally neutral; the context determines whether it represents God's provision or human folly.

4. Theological Implications

Eli's accusation in 1 Samuel 1:14 carries severe theological irony. Eli was quick to rebuke a brokenhearted woman engaged in sincere, silent supplication, assuming she was drunk on wine. Yet Eli failed to discipline his own sons, Hophni and Phinehas, who were committing blatant sacrilege at the tabernacle.

This interaction shows that religious position does not guarantee spiritual insight. Eli looked only at outward appearances—moving lips without audible sound—and jumped to a condemnation. Hannah's condition was not produced by wine, but by a spirit burdened with grief seeking comfort in God alone. The passage reveals how God honors the genuine cry of the afflicted even when spiritual leaders fail to understand them.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:13, Hannah was speaking in her heart; only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard. Eli observed her and concluded she was drunk. He confronted her sharply in verse 14: "How long will you be drunk? Put away your wine from you!"

Eli used yayin as the direct explanation for her strange posture and Silent lip movements. The word appears as a common noun in the singular form, pointing directly to the wine Eli assumed was impairing her speech and conduct. Hannah's gentle response overturned his accusation entirely: she had drunk neither wine nor strong drink, but was pouring out her soul before the Lord.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 9:21 — "Then he drank of the wine and was drunken, and became uncovered in his tent."

  • Leviticus 23:13 — "Its drink offering shall be of wine, one-fourth of a hin."

  • Proverbs 31:4 — "It is not for kings, O Lemuel, it is not for kings to drink wine, nor for princes intoxicating drink."

  • Jeremiah 35:6 — "But they said, 'We will drink no wine, for Jonadab the son of Rechab, our father, commanded us, saying, "You shall drink no wine, you nor your sons, forever."'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A striking contrast exists between what Eli assumed and what Hannah was actually doing. Eli accused her of being full of wine, but Hannah described her action as "pouring out" her soul before the Lord. Where Eli saw the wasteful excess of drunkenness, God received a profound liquid offering of heartfelt prayer. This misunderstanding marks the beginning of a major transition in Israel's history: Eli's discerning power was fading, while God was preparing to answer Hannah's prayer by raising up Samuel, a faithful prophet who would truly hear God's voice.
