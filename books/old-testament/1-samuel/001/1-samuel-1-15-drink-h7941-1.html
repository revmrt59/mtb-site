DRINK - (shêkâr, Strong's H7941)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun shekhar refers to strong, intoxicating drink distinct from ordinary grape wine. It frequently appears alongside wine to represent all forms of alcoholic beverages that can produce intoxication. In 1 Samuel 1:15, Hannah uses the word to refute High Priest Eli's unjust accusation of drunkenness, clarifying that her silent, emotionally intense prayer came from a sorrowful heart poured out before God, not from the effects of intoxicating drink.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, everyday beverages included water, milk, and wine pressed from grapes. Beyond grape wine, ancient Near Eastern cultures produced fermented alcoholic drinks made from barley, pomegranate, dates, or honey. The noun shekhar designates these strong, fermented beverages. The term comes from a root associated with drinking to fullness or becoming intoxicated.

In Israel's religious life, strong drink occupied a distinct place. Priests were strictly forbidden from consuming wine or strong drink while ministering in the sanctuary, and Nazirites voluntarily renounced all fermented beverages as part of their vow of consecration. While not prohibited in every setting, strong drink was recognized as a substance capable of clouding judgment, impairing spiritual discernment, and leading to foolish behavior.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word shekhar refers to fermented, intoxicating liquor other than standard grape wine. While standard wine was produced from grapes, shekhar generally denoted beer or potent fermented fruit drinks.

When paired with the Hebrew word for wine (yayin), the phrase "wine or drink" functions as a comprehensive expression for any alcoholic beverage capable of causing intoxication. The word itself does not automatically imply sinful behavior in every context, as Scripture mentions it in connection with certain offerings and festal meals. However, it regularly carries a warning regarding the loss of self-control, altered perception, and moral compromise that excess consumption causes.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses shekhar across several distinct settings. In the Law of Moses, it appears in instructions governing sanctuary worship and special vows, where refraining from intoxicating drink preserved holy discernment and personal devotion. In wisdom literature and the prophets, the word is frequently used to warn against the dangers of excess, showing how strong drink leads to mockery, violence, and spiritual apathy.

Grammatically, in 1 Samuel 1:15, the noun appears in the singular number and common gender, referring directly to the physical substance Hannah affirms she has not consumed.

4. Theological Implications

The contrast between physical intoxication and genuine spiritual passion runs throughout the Bible. Intoxicating drink promises temporary escape or artificial joy, but it weakens moral clarity and dulls spiritual sensitivity. True devotion, by contrast, involves clear-minded commitment and honest communication with God.

Hannah's interaction with Eli highlights this truth. Her physical appearance during intense, silent prayer looked strange to a spiritually dull priest, leading him to assume she was drunk. Yet her posture was the result of deep spiritual anguish, not physical intoxication. Scripture reveals that while worldly escape numbs the soul without fixing the problem, bringing honest grief before the Lord yields divine comfort and real peace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1, Hannah endures heavy emotional distress due to her childlessness and the bitter provoke of her rival, Peninnah. Visiting the sanctuary at Shiloh, Hannah prays silently, her lips moving without making a sound as she pours out her soul to God. High Priest Eli observes her and mistakenly accuses her of being drunk.

Hannah respectfully corrects Eli, explaining that she is a woman of a sorrowful spirit who has consumed "neither wine nor intoxicating drink." By specifying both wine and shekhar, Hannah covers every kind of intoxicating beverage, leaving no doubt about her sobriety.

Her defense sets up a powerful contrast between two kinds of "pouring out." Rather than pouring strong drink down her throat to drown her sorrow, she poured out her heart to the Lord. Her outward appearance was not the stupor of intoxication, but the unrestrained urgency of a suffering believer seeking God's help.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 10:9 — "Do not drink wine or intoxicating drink, you, nor your sons with you, when you go into the tabernacle of meeting, lest you die." God commands the priests to abstain from strong drink during sanctuary service so they can distinguish between the holy and the unholy.

  • Numbers 6:3 — "He shall separate himself from wine and similar drink; he shall drink no vinegar made from wine or vinegar made from similar drink." A Nazirite surrendered intoxicating drink as an outward marker of complete dedication to God.

  • Proverbs 20:1 — "Wine is a mocker, Strong drink is a brawler, And whoever is led astray by it is not wise." The sage warns that strong drink deceives people, producing loud folly and lack of restraint.

  • Proverbs 31:6 — "Give strong drink to him who is perishing, And wine to those who are bitter of heart." In severe situations, strong drink is described as a physical pain reliever for those who are dying or in extreme physical suffering.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Eli's misjudgment of Hannah offers a sobering warning for leaders. Eli lived during a period of spiritual decline in Israel when his own sons acted corruptly in the sanctuary. Because Eli was accustomed to spiritual coldness and public disorder, he failed to recognize genuine, heart-crying prayer when he saw it, mistaking holy anguish for drunken misbehavior.

Hannah's response remains an exemplary model of grace under false accusation. She did not respond in anger to Eli's harsh charge. Instead, she clearly stated her sobriety and explained her true condition. Her example shows believers how to process deep suffering: rather than turning to worldly numbing agents like strong drink, the most fruitful choice is to lay one's burdens openly before the Lord in prayer.
