DRUNK - (shâthâh, Strong's H8354)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shatah refers to the basic physical act of drinking or consuming a liquid, such as water, wine, or milk. It describes taking in nourishment or participating in a feast. In 1 Samuel 1:15, Hannah uses this word in the negative to defend her integrity before Eli the priest. She explains that she has not consumed wine or any strong drink, clearing herself of the charge of drunkenness and revealing that her quiet weeping was actually a desperate prayer before God.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, gathering at the sanctuary for annual feasts was a central part of religious life. Families traveled to places like Shiloh to offer sacrifices, give thanks, and share a communal meal. These meals included food and drink, such as wine, as part of sacred celebration.

Because drinking wine was normal during these annual feasts, Eli the priest assumed Hannah was intoxicated when he saw her lips moving without sound. However, Hannah was enduring severe emotional anguish over her childlessness and the taunts of her rival. Rather than joining in the festive eating and drinking, she abstained and went to the tabernacle to bring her grief directly to God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shatah means to drink, swallow, or consume liquid. It is the primary word in the Old Testament for the physical action of drinking. Depending on the passage, it can refer to drinking water for basic survival, drinking milk for nourishment, or drinking wine during a feast.

The word itself simply describes the act of consuming a beverage. It does not automatically mean getting drunk or overindulging; other context words indicate whether a person is drinking moderately, drinking water in the wilderness, or drinking to excess. In 1 Samuel 1:15, the word describes the act of drinking alcohol, which Hannah explicitly denies doing.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses shatah in several distinct settings:

  • Physical Sustenance: It describes human and animal life being sustained by water, such as travelers drinking from a well or Israel drinking water provided by God in the wilderness.

  • Feasting and Hospitality: It appears frequently alongside eating to describe social gatherings, covenant meals, and sacred celebrations where food and drink were shared.

  • Fasting and Abstinence: In contexts of deep grief, repentance, or intense prayer, individuals often abstained from drinking liquid altogether.

While the word usually describes bodily thirst being satisfied, it can also contrast physical consumption with spiritual longing. When people fast or refrain from drinking during prayer, physical necessity is set aside to focus entirely on God.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's use of shatah brings out a powerful contrast between outward ritual participation and inward heart worship. Eli looked at her physical movements and assumed she was participating in the feast in a worldly or uncontrolled way. Hannah clarified that far from dousing her sorrow in alcohol, she was bringing her pain directly to the Lord.

Her testimony highlights that true relief for a heavy heart is found not in earthly remedies or physical consumption, but in pouring out one's soul to God. Physical drinking temporarily quenches bodily thirst or dulls pain, but transparent prayer brings the soul into direct contact with the God who hears and remembers His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:15, Hannah uses shatah in a common, singular, perfect verb form as she answers Eli's mistaken accusation. Eli had confronted her, telling her to put away her wine. Hannah responded respectfully but firmly, explaining that she had consumed no wine or strong drink at all.

Her statement forms a striking parallel contrast: she had not poured wine down her throat; instead, she was pouring out her soul before the Lord. The word highlights her complete abstinence during a time when others were feasting. Her physical mouth was still, and no liquid had crossed her lips, yet her heart was crying out in profound, unspoken prayer.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Eli's misjudgment of Hannah offers a sobering lesson for spiritual leaders. He mistook the physical expression of intense grief and silent prayer for the carelessness of a drunkard. Hannah’s defense showed that her silent lip movement was actually a sign of concentrated, heartfelt prayer.

Furthermore, Hannah’s refusal to drink alcohol during her grief demonstrates her devotion. Instead of seeking temporary distraction or escape through wine, she turned her unmet longing into earnest supplication. Her honesty before God laid the groundwork for the birth of Samuel, a prophet who would lead Israel during a pivotal era in its history.
