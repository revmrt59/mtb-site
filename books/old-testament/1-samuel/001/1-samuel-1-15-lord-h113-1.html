LORD - (ʼâdôwn, Strong's H113)

Summary:

The Hebrew word adon refers to a master, owner, ruler, or superior who holds authority or deserves honor. In social and civil settings, it serves as a term of polite respect toward someone in a position of leadership or religious oversight. In 1 Samuel 1:15, Hannah uses the word to address Eli the high priest as "lord," expressing restraint, reverence, and composure as she defends herself against his mistaken charge of drunkenness.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israelite society, social order and community life relied heavily on clear lines of respect and authority. The noun adon was used throughout everyday life to acknowledge leadership, ownership, or position. A servant addressed a master as adon, a subject addressed a king as adon, and a worshiper or citizen addressed a civic or religious leader with the same title of respect.

In 1 Samuel 1, the narrative opens at the tabernacle in Shiloh during a time of widespread spiritual decay in Israel. Hannah, grieved by her childlessness and provoked by her rival, prayed silently in deep anguish. Eli, the ruling priest at Shiloh, observed her lips moving without sound and wrongly concluded that she was drunk. When Eli confronted her, Hannah did not respond with anger or contempt. Instead, she addressed Eli as "my lord," maintaining respectful speech toward the priest of Israel while correcting his error.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Adon refers to an owner, master, ruler, or superior. It carries the basic sense of one who exercises authority, holds responsibility, or occupies a position of dignity. When people use the word in human relationships, it functions as a title of respect, similar to addressing a ruler, supervisor, or leader as "sir" or "my lord."

When applied to human beings, the term does not automatically imply divine status or absolute control. It indicates the recognized leadership or higher standing of the person being addressed. When applied to God, the word highlights His supreme lordship and rule over creation. In 1 Samuel 1:15, the word is a singular noun of common gender, functioning as Hannah's respectful title for Eli.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, adon appears in several distinct contexts:

  • Domestic and civil authority: Servants use the word for their masters, and subjects use it for kings and local officials.

  • Family honor: Family members sometimes use it as a title of respect for family heads or elder leaders.

  • Religious and civic leaders: Individuals use the word when addressing priests, prophets, or judges holding official positions in Israel.

  • Divine lordship: Worshipers use the title for God to exalt His sovereign majesty over all earthly powers.

The word itself does not carry intrinsic moral goodness or badness; its meaning depends on who holds authority and how that authority is used. When a godly person addresses an authority figure as adon, it reflects proper humility, order, and courteous speech.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s use of adon in 1 Samuel 1:15 highlights the distinction between human authority and God's supreme authority. Eli held the honored office of priest in Israel, and Hannah properly recognized his office by calling him "lord." However, Eli was human and capable of poor judgment, as shown by his hasty and incorrect assessment of Hannah's spiritual grief.

While Hannah addressed Eli as her earthly superior ("my lord"), her heart was directed toward the true God ("the LORD"). She recognized Eli's place as a religious leader without allowing his mistaken accusation to drive her away from seeking God. This distinction shows that honoring human authority and bringing ultimate grief directly to God can exist side by side.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:15, Hannah’s words reveal a striking contrast between outer appearances and inner spiritual reality. Eli had just accused her of being intoxicated while praying at the tabernacle. A natural reaction to such an unfair accusation from a religious leader might have been resentment or harsh argument.

Instead, Hannah responded with calm dignity: "No, my lord, I am a woman of a sorrowful spirit." By addressing Eli as adon, she acknowledged his priestly office and kept her tone polite and controlled. Her gentle response diffused the confrontation and moved Eli from suspicion to granting a priestly blessing. The word underscores Hannah's humility and grace, showing that her deep emotional grief had not turned into bitter disrespect for Israel's priest.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 18:12 – Sarah uses the word "lord" to refer to Abraham, showing traditional respect within domestic life.

  • 1 Samuel 24:8 – David addresses Saul as "lord" as he steps out of the cave, demonstrating respect for the anointed king even while being hunted.

  • 1 Kings 3:26 – A desperate mother speaks to King Solomon, calling him "lord" as she pleads for her child's life in the royal court.

  • Psalms 136:3 – The psalmist calls believers to give thanks to the "Lord of lords," exalting God above every earthly master and authority.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The opening chapter of 1 Samuel establishes a subtle literary contrast regarding priesthood and leadership. While Eli held the title of "lord" as priest, his own sons were corrupt and dishonored God's sanctuary. Hannah, a non-priestly woman suffering in silence, displayed greater spiritual composure, faith, and respectful speech than the male leaders around her.

Additionally, the passage sets up a helpful contrast between two Hebrew words translated into English: Hannah addresses Eli as adon ("lord"), acknowledging human leadership, but she pours out her soul before YHWH ("the LORD"), appealing to the eternal, covenant God who alone could answer her prayer and grant her a son.
