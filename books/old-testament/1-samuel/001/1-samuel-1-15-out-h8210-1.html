OUT - (shâphak, Strong's H8210)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shaphak means to pour out, spill, or empty a liquid container. When applied figuratively to human emotion, it describes the complete unburdening of a person's inner spirit before God. In 1 Samuel 1:15, Hannah uses this word to refute Eli's charge of drunkenness, explaining that her silent lips and tearful posture were not caused by wine, but by the exhaustive release of her deep sorrow and earnest prayer before the LORD.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Hannah traveled with her family to the sanctuary at Shiloh for the annual sacrifice. Her life was marked by deep emotional distress because of her barrenness and the relentless harassment of Peninnah, her rival. While at Shiloh, Hannah went to the sanctuary to lay her grief before God.

Her prayer was intense and internal; her lips moved, but no sound came from her mouth. Eli, the high priest, saw her trembling lips and assumed she was drunk from the festival feast. In ancient Israelite culture, the verb shaphak was a common word used in farming, sacrificial worship, daily life, and warfare. It described pouring out liquids like water, blood, or oil, spilling blood in violence, or throwing up dirt for military siege works. In devotional settings, it described the total emptying of one's thoughts and emotions in prayer.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic physical meaning of shaphak is to pour out, spill, shed, or empty.

When applied to physical items, the word can refer to:

  • Pouring out liquid offerings or water onto the ground or altar.

  • Shedding blood, whether through animal sacrifices or human violence.

  • Piling up earth or dirt to form a mound.

When applied figuratively to a person's inner life—such as the soul or the heart—it means to hold nothing back. To pour out the soul is to release every hidden grief, fear, desperation, and hope into the presence of God until the person is entirely emptied before Him.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses shaphak in several distinct settings, all sharing the common concept of total emptying:

  • Sacrificial and Ritual Contexts: The priest would pour out the blood of a bull at the base of the sacrificial altar. In these settings, the pouring out of blood represents the giving of life.

  • Violence and Crime: The word frequently describes shedding innocent blood. To shed blood is to spill human life onto the earth.

  • Divine Judgment: The prophets often speak of God pouring out His wrath or burning anger upon rebellious nations, picturing divine judgment as a overflowing cup emptied upon the wicked.

  • Prayer and Supplication: Psalmists and prophets use the word for the afflicted soul who pours out his complaint or heart before the LORD. Just as a vessel is turned upside down until every drop of liquid runs out, the grieving worshipper empties his inner life before God.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's use of shaphak highlights the nature of true prayer. Worship is not merely reciting structured sentences or maintaining outward decorum. Real supplication involves bringing the raw, unfiltered distress of the soul into God's presence.

This usage also shows that God welcomes the uncensored grief of His children. Hannah did not try to polish her anguish or pretend to be strong. She emptied her heart completely at the sanctuary. In Biblical theology, this total emptying of the soul functions like a personal offering to God. When a believer has no strength or answers left, laying that emptiness before the LORD is itself an act of faith.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:15, Hannah responds to Eli's harsh accusation ("How long will you be drunk? Put away your wine from you!"). Her answer sets up a clear contrast between physical intoxication and spiritual outpouring.

Eli thought Hannah was filled with wine, but Hannah explains that she was actually emptying her soul before God. Intoxication fills a person with wine and leads to a loss of self-control; spiritual prayer empties a person of self and relies entirely on God's help. Hannah describes herself as a woman of a "sorrowful spirit" who has drunk neither wine nor strong drink. Her physical silence and moving lips were not the stupor of drunkenness, but the silent, agonizing release of her innermost grief (nephesh). By using shaphak, she makes clear that her prayer was an exhaustive act of worship before Yahweh.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In 1 Samuel 1:15, the verb appears in the common gender, singular number, and imperfect wayyiqtol form. This grammatical form presents Hannah's prayer as a completed action within her explanation to Eli, carrying the force of "so I poured out."

There is also a striking irony in this scene. Eli, the high priest of Israel, fails to recognize the difference between drunkenness and deep prayer. Yet God, who looks on the heart rather than outward appearances, heard Hannah's silent outpouring. The very act of pouring out her heart in secret distress resulted in God remembering her, ultimately leading to the birth of Samuel, who would usher in a new era for the nation of Israel.
