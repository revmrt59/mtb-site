WINE - (yayin, Strong's H3196)

Summary:

The Hebrew word yayin refers to fermented grape juice or wine, the standard word for wine throughout the Old Testament. It describes beverage wine used in daily life, festive celebrations, and formal sacrificial drink offerings, while also carrying moral warnings about intoxication and spiritual dulness when misused. In 1 Samuel 1:15, Hannah uses yayin to refute Eli's accusation of drunkenness, explaining that her silent, weeping posture was not caused by drinking wine, but by intense sorrow and sincere prayer as she poured out her heart to God.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, yayin was a staple agricultural product alongside grain and oil. Derived from grape harvests, wine was enjoyed at social gatherings, served at royal banquets, and commanded as part of the daily drink offerings at the tabernacle and temple.

The setting of 1 Samuel 1 takes place at the sanctuary in Shiloh during the judges period. Hannah, distressed by year after year of childlessness and harsh provoke from her rival, went to pray before God. High Priest Eli observed her lips moving silently without hearing any audible voice and concluded she was drunk on wine. Hannah responded by asserting that she had consumed no wine or intoxicating drink, but was simply bringing her deep grief directly to the Lord.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Yayin refers specifically to fermented wine made from grapes. Depending on the context, the word carries several nuances:

  • Ordinary table wine consumed during regular meals or harvest celebrations.

  • Sacrificial wine poured out on the altar as an acceptable offering to God.

  • An intoxicating agent that can impair judgment, weaken self-control, and cause spiritual blindness when consumed in excess.

In 1 Samuel 1:15, yayin denotes an intoxicating beverage. Hannah uses the term in a negative declaration, clarifying that her physical demeanor was completely sober and born out of deep personal anguish, not alcohol consumption.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture presents yayin with balanced nuance. On one hand, wine is described as a gift from God that gladdens the heart and represents earthly abundance and blessing. It was routinely included in covenant worship as a drink offering paired with animal and grain sacrifices.

On the other hand, Scripture frequently warns against the excessive consumption of yayin. Overindulgence leads to loss of physical control, foolish behavior, dishonor, and faulty decision-making. Certain groups, such as Nazirites or priests while performing sacred duties, abstained from wine entirely as a sign of special consecration or holy vigilance. Hannah's denial of drinking wine situated her among those who approached God with sober reverence rather than worldly distraction.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's use of yayin in her defense reveals several key theological truths:

  • Human misjudgment versus divine awareness: Eli, Israel's high priest, misread the outward signs of intense spiritual sorrow as profane drunkenness. God, however, looks beyond appearance to examine the genuine motives of the heart.

  • Spiritual passion is distinct from emotional excess or intoxication: True, fervent prayer can look unusual or overwhelming to an outside observer, but it flows from a clear mind and a sorrowful, earnest spirit.

  • The proper outlet for deep pain: Rather than seeking comfort or escape in wine, Hannah brought her unbearable grief directly into the presence of the Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The target word yayin appears in 1 Samuel 1:15 as part of Hannah's self-defense against Eli's mistaken rebuke. Eli had said to her, "How long will you be drunk? Put your wine away from you!" (1 Samuel 1:14).

Hannah’s reply in verse 15 forms a deliberate contrast:

  • She denies physical drinking: "I have drunk neither wine nor intoxicating drink."

  • She explains her actual action: "but have poured out my soul before the LORD."

There is a striking contrast implied in her words. A person who drinks too much wine pours liquid into themselves and becomes empty of wisdom and self-control. Hannah, by contrast, poured out her inner soul to God in honest supplication. Her silent lip movements were not the slurred motions of a drinker, but the silent, desperate communications of a broken spirit pleading for divine mercy.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 9:21 — "Then he drank of the wine and was intoxicated, and became uncovered inside his tent."

  • Leviticus 23:13 — "Its drink offering shall be of wine, one-fourth of a hin."

  • Judges 13:14 — "She may not eat anything that comes from the vine, nor may she drink wine or similar drink..."

  • Proverbs 31:4 — "It is not for kings, O Lemuel, it is not for kings to drink wine, nor for princes intoxicating drink..."

  • Jeremiah 35:6 — "But they said, 'We will drink no wine, for Jonadab the son of Rechab, our father, commanded us, saying, "You shall drink no wine, you nor your sons, forever."'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Grammatically, yayin in 1 Samuel 1:15 is a singular, common noun functioning as the direct object of Hannah's denial ("I have drunk neither wine...").

  • The contrast between wine and prayer in 1 Samuel 1 closely parallels New Testament teaching regarding spiritual fullness, where believers are instructed not to be drunk with wine, but to be filled with the Holy Spirit.

  • Once Eli recognized his error and understood that Hannah had not been drinking wine, his harsh accusation transformed into a pastoral blessing, demonstrating how honest communication restores holy fellowship.
