WOMAN - (ʼishshâh, Strong's H802)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun 'ishshah is the primary Old Testament word for an adult human female, translated as "woman" or "wife" depending on the context. In 1 Samuel 1:15, Hannah uses 'ishshah when defending herself before Eli the priest, describing herself as a "woman of a sorrowful spirit." In this context, the word establishes her identity as a deeply grieving individual pouring out her heart to God, contrasting her genuine spiritual devotion with Eli's mistaken assumption that she was intoxicated.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word 'ishshah is the basic Hebrew term used throughout the Old Testament for a female person or a married woman. It appears across every genre of Old Testament literature, from narrative histories and legal codes to wisdom literature and prophetic books.

In the opening chapter of 1 Samuel, the narrative presents Hannah facing intense personal distress. She suffers from barrenness and endures constant provocation from Peninnah, her husband's other wife. During an annual pilgrimage to the sanctuary at Shiloh, Hannah enters the tabernacle to pray. Overwhelmed by grief, she prays silently, her lips moving without producing an audible voice. Eli, the high priest, observes her from his seat near the doorpost and misinterprets her silent agony as drunkenness. When Eli confronts her, Hannah responds respectfully, using 'ishshah to identify herself and explain her spiritual state.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'ishshah refers fundamentally to an adult female human being. Its exact meaning in a passage depends on how it is used in the sentence:

  • It refers to a woman as an individual female person, distinguished from a man.

  • It denotes a wife when used in a relational or marital setting.

  • It combines with descriptive words to indicate a woman's character or condition, such as a "wise woman" or a "virtuous woman."

In 1 Samuel 1:15, the word carries its primary sense of a female person, modified by a descriptive phrase to communicate her internal state: "a woman of a sorrowful spirit."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament narrative and law, 'ishshah expresses both the general category of female humanity and specific relational roles. It marks women in public life, domestic settings, legal protections, and religious worship.

When Hannah uses 'ishshah before Eli, her choice of words carries a clear nuance of personal dignity and vulnerability. She does not address the priest with hostility despite his harsh and incorrect accusation. Instead, she presents herself simply as a woman bearing a heavy grief. Her statement grounds her explanation in her shared humanity and her personal burden before God, inviting the priest to see her true situation rather than judging by outward appearances.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's use of 'ishshah in 1 Samuel 1:15 illustrates several clear biblical truths regarding prayer and individual worship:

  • God hears and values the sincere prayers of suffering individuals, regardless of their societal standing or silent presentation.

  • True worship is an expression of the heart. Hannah describes her prayer as pouring out her soul before the Lord, showing that prayer consists of inward truth rather than outward noise.

  • The narrative contrasts human misjudgment with divine understanding. While Eli, the high priest, judges Hannah based on incomplete observation, God looks upon her heart and answers her petition.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:15, the grammatical form of 'ishshah is singular and feminine, directly identifying Hannah as an individual woman. Eli had just rebuked her sharply, telling her to put away her wine. Hannah's response begins with a respectful refusal: "No, my lord, I am a woman of a sorrowful spirit."

This statement works in tandem with her next words: "I have drunk neither wine nor intoxicating drink, but have poured out my soul before the LORD." In verse 16, she adds a further clarification, asking Eli not to consider her a "daughter of Belial" (a wicked or worthless woman).

The contrast between Eli's assumption and Hannah's reality is striking. Eli assumed she was a dishonorable woman indulging in drunkenness at the sanctuary; Hannah reveals that she is a woman carrying profound sorrow directly to God. The word 'ishshah anchors her self-defense, helping to turn Eli's mistaken judgment into a priestly blessing.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's description of herself as a "woman of a sorrowful spirit" introduces an important literary theme in 1 Samuel. Her quiet, faith-filled dependence on God stands in stark contrast to the spiritual insensitivity of Eli and the corruption of his sons, Hophni and Phinehas.

While the priestly establishment at Shiloh was failing, God was preparing to bring renewal to Israel through the child born to this grieving woman. Hannah's honest prayer in her weakness becomes the turning point that leads to the birth of Samuel, who would serve as God's prophet and transition Israel into the era of the monarchy.
