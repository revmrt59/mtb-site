GRIEF - (kaʻaç, Strong's H3708)

Summary:

The Hebrew word ka'as refers to intense inner turmoil, grief, vexation, sorrow, or provocation. It describes the deep emotional pain and agitation that arise when a person faces persistent hardship, cruel treatment, or overwhelming distress. In 1 Samuel 1:16, the word describes Hannah's deep sorrow and inner pain as she prays desperately to the Lord. Her use of ka'as explains that her moving lips and silent groanings were not caused by drunkenness, as the priest Eli mistakenly assumed, but flowed from an overflowing heart heavy with grief.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The first chapter of 1 Samuel introduces Hannah, a woman enduring profound emotional distress. Her struggle was twofold: she was unable to bear children, and her husband's second wife, Peninnah, relentlessly taunted and provoked her because of her barrenness. Year after year, when the family went up to the tabernacle at Shiloh to worship, Peninnah used the occasion to mock Hannah, driving her to tears and keeping her from eating.

During one of these visits to Shiloh, Hannah went to the tabernacle alone to pray. Overcome with pain, she wept bitterly and made a vow to God, asking Him to remember her and grant her a son. As she prayed silently in her heart—her lips moving while her voice was silent—the high priest Eli observed her. Lacking discernment in that moment, Eli incorrectly concluded that she was drunk and rebuked her. Hannah defended herself respectfully, explaining that her strange appearance was not the result of wine or wickedness, but stemmed from an abundance of complaint and grief.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word ka'as refers to emotional agitation, grief, sorrow, vexation, or provocation. It describes an intense feeling produced by external pressure, insult, or persistent sorrow.

The word is used in Scripture in two distinct ways depending on who is experiencing it:

  • When applied to human beings, it means grief, sorrow, or deep frustration caused by bitter life circumstances, loss, or personal attack.

  • When applied to God or used in relation to divine holiness, it refers to righteous anger or provocation caused by human sin and rebellion.

In the case of human suffering, ka'as is not merely mild annoyance or fleeting sadness. It describes an internal churning of the soul where anger, deep hurt, and pain meet.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, ka'as appears in wisdom literature, poetic books, and historical narratives. Its usage reflects a blend of emotional agitation and heavy grief.

In poetry and wisdom literature, the word frequently describes the emotional toll of suffering. Job uses the word to express the weight of his misery under severe trials. In the Psalms, David connects ka'as with physical exhaustion, noting that deep sorrow and grief can dim a person's eyes and wear down their strength. In Ecclesiastes, Solomon notes that increasing human wisdom often brings increased grief or frustration as one observes the fallen nature of the world.

In historical books and prophetic writings, the word often highlights the provocation of God's holy anger. Israel's persistent idolatry and covenant breaking are said to cause ka'as, provoking the Lord to judgment.

Whether describing human heartache or divine response, the word carries the core idea of an intense emotional reaction triggered by wrong, hardship, or provocation.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's response in 1 Samuel 1 reveals important biblical truths about prayer and grief. Rather than allowing her ka'as to turn into bitter resentment against Peninnah or hardhearted rebellion against God, she brought her raw emotions directly into the presence of the Lord.

Scripture shows that God does not require His people to hide their brokenness or pretend to be emotionless when facing hardship. Pouring out one's soul, complete with tears, agony, and deep sorrow, is a valid expression of faith. Hannah's grief was intense, but bringing that grief to the sanctuary demonstrated her trust in God's power and mercy.

Her example shows that true prayer often arises not from neat, polished words, but from a troubled spirit seeking refuge in the God who hears and cares.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:16, Hannah uses ka'as to clear her name before Eli. Eli had wrongly labeled her a "wicked woman" (literally, a "daughter of Belial"), assuming her silent, intense movements were caused by drunkenness.

Hannah corrects Eli's misjudgment by identifying the true source of her behavior. She explains that she has spoken out of the "abundance" (the overwhelming overflow) of her complaint and grief (ka'as).

The word functions as a singular noun of common gender in this sentence, describing her overall state of emotional pain. It shows that her speech to God was not the loose talk of a intoxicated person, but the earnest cry of a soul crushed under prolonged taunting and unfulfilled longing.

This contrast is striking: Eli saw external disorder, but God saw a deeply faithful woman laying down her heavy sorrow at the altar.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 6:2 — "Oh, that my grief were fully weighed, and my calamity laid with it on the scales!"

Here Job uses the word to describe the immense weight of his physical and emotional agony, comparing his deep suffering to a heavy burden that could be weighed on scales.

  • Psalms 6:7 — "My eye wastes away because of grief; it grows old because of all my enemies."

In this psalm of lament, David describes how severe grief and sorrow caused by opposing enemies physically drain his vitality and weaken his sight.

  • Psalms 31:9 — "Have mercy on me, O LORD, for I am in trouble; my eye wastes away with grief, yes, my soul and my body!"

David again links his internal grief to physical weariness, showing how deep soul-sorrow impacts the whole person.

  • Proverbs 17:25 — "A foolish son is a grief to his father, and bitterness to her who bore him."

This proverb uses the word to describe the personal distress, heartache, and vexation a rebellious child brings upon loving parents.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Cause and the Result: Earlier in the chapter, the text notes that Peninnah provoked Hannah severely to make her miserable. The root idea of provocation appears at the start of the story, and by verse 16, Hannah describes the resulting emotional state as ka'as ("grief"). What was inflicted as provocation turned into deep heart-sorrow before God.

  • Misjudging the Hurting: Eli's hasty condemnation serves as a quiet warning to religious leaders and worshipers. It is easy to mistake the unpolished, desperate groans of a hurting person for spiritual disorder or disrespect.

  • From Grief to Peace: After Hannah poured out her ka'as to the Lord and received Eli's blessing, her countenance changed. She ate, and her face was no longer sad. The heavy grief that brought her to her knees was lifted when she entrusted her situation into God's hands.
