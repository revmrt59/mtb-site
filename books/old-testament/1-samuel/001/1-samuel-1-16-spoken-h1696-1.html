SPOKEN - (dâbar, Strong's H1696)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb dabar means to speak, talk, declare, or express in words. In 1 Samuel 1:16, Hannah uses this word ("spoken") to explain her silent prayer to the high priest Eli. After Eli mistakenly accused her of being drunk, Hannah clarified that her lip movements were not slurred words of intoxication, but genuine speech addressed to God out of the overflow of her deep grief and anguish.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The first chapter of 1 Samuel records the deep sorrow of Hannah, who was childless and provoked by her husband’s second wife, Peninnah. During the annual family pilgrimage to the sanctuary at Shiloh, Hannah brought her pain directly to the Lord. She stood near the doorpost of the tabernacle and prayed silently. Her heart was in anguish, and while her lips were moving, her voice made no sound.

Eli the priest sat nearby observing her. Seeing her lips move without hearing any words, Eli assumed she was intoxicated and rebuked her sharply. Hannah responded to his harsh misjudgment with humility and clarity. She explained that she had drunk no wine or strong drink; rather, she was pouring out her soul before the Lord. In verse 16, she asked Eli not to view her as a worthless woman, explaining that out of the abundance of her grief and trouble she had spoken until now.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of dabar is to speak, declare, converse, or communicate in words. It is one of the foundational verbs for verbal expression in the Old Testament. The word can describe simple personal conversation, official proclamations by leaders, divine communication from God, or interior speech expressed to oneself or to God in prayer.

When used of human speech, dabar focuses on articulate communication. It implies that definite thoughts and words are being formed, even if those words are spoken quietly or inwardly. In 1 Samuel 1:16, the word indicates that Hannah was not merely experiencing an emotional outburst; she was communicating clear, intentional words to God in prayer.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, dabar appears in a wide variety of settings to describe communication:

  • Divine Revelation: God speaks His commandments, promises, and warnings to His people and through His prophets.

  • Human Declarations: People use the word to describe making promises, entering agreements, offering counsel, or giving reports.

  • Heartfelt Prayer: Individuals use the verb to describe expressing their needs, grievances, and praise directly to God.

The word itself covers the whole spectrum of vocalized speech and intended communication. Context determines whether the speech is public or private, loud or silent, authoritative or suppliant.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s explanation to Eli offers an encouraging biblical insight into the nature of prayer. Real prayer is genuine communication with God. It does not require a public audience or loud vocalization to be valid speech before the Lord.

This passage also highlights the contrast between human perception and divine hearing. Eli looked only at the outward appearance—the moving lips without sound—and jumped to a false, critical conclusion. God, however, received Hannah's silent words as true speech (dabar). God hears the deliberate, formed prayers of a broken heart even when human observers completely misinterpret the situation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:16, Hannah uses a singular, common, perfect form of dabar ("I have spoken") to summarize what she had been doing during her time at the sanctuary. The perfect grammatical form describes her completed action of pouring out her petition up to that point.

Hannah connects her speech to two specific internal conditions: her "complaint" (siah, meaning deep concern or meditation) and her "grief" (ka'as, meaning vexation or anguish). Her outward speech—though inaudible to Eli—was driven directly by the heavy burden in her heart. By stating that she had "spoken," Hannah defended her character against Eli's accusation of drunkenness. Drunken babble is chaotic and ungodly, but Hannah’s speech was purposeful, reverent, and addressed directly to the God of Israel.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's use of dabar in 1 Samuel 1:16 reveals a striking irony in the sanctuary setting. Eli, the spiritual leader of Israel, failed to recognize true prayer when he saw it, mistaking intense devotion for public drunkenness. Yet Hannah, an afflicted and desperate woman, maintained a clear and respectful witness. Her gentle correction of Eli changed his tone entirely, leading him to offer a blessing over her petition (1 Samuel 1:17). Her articulate prayer from the heart resulted in God answering her with the birth of Samuel, whose name itself recalls that God hears prayer.
