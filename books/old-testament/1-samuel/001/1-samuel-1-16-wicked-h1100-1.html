WICKED - (bᵉlîyaʻal, Strong's H1100)

Summary:

The Hebrew word beliyya'al carries the basic meaning of worthlessness, lawlessness, or moral ruin. In the Old Testament, it often describes people whose character and actions lack spiritual value, moral restraint, or integrity. In 1 Samuel 1:16, Hannah uses the word when asking Eli not to view her as a "daughter of wickedness," assuring the high priest that her silent, emotional prayer stems from deep personal grief rather than brazen disrespect or drunken irreverence at the sanctuary.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The term beliyya'al is formed from two concepts: a word meaning "without" and a word meaning "profit," "usefulness," or "value." Taken together, the word describes something or someone that is without value, ungodly, or destructive to moral order.

In ancient Israelite society, describing a person as a "child of worthlessness" or a "daughter of wickedness" was a severe moral indictment. It pointed to an individual who rejected God's commands, ignored societal boundaries, and lived without spiritual accountability. The word was applied to idolaters, lying witnesses, lawless troublemakers, and those who showed open contempt for God and neighbor.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Beliyya'al refers to worthlessness, corruption, lawlessness, or evil. It describes a state of moral failure that yields no righteous fruit.

When applied to human actions or thoughts, the word indicates a spirit of rebellion or ungodliness. When applied directly to a person, it functions as a descriptive noun for a scoundrel, a corrupt individual, or a reckless troublemaker. In 1 Samuel 1:16, Hannah uses the word as a descriptive noun to deny that she is an ungodly, disrespectfully drunken woman showing contempt in the house of the LORD.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, beliyya'al frequently appears in formal phrases such as "son of worthlessness," "man of wickedness," or "daughter of wickedness." Rather than indicating biological lineage, these phrases describe a person's dominant moral character.

The word covers several specific expressions of evil:

  • Lawless violence and sexual depravity, such as the corrupt men who disturbed the peace in Israel's early history.

  • Deceitful or treasonous speech, including false witnesses hired to destroy innocent lives.

  • Spiritual rebellion and contempt for worship, seen in individuals who treat sacred things with profanity or disdain.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of beliyya'al emphasizes that sin strips human life of genuine value and moral purpose. Created in the image of God, human beings are designed to yield fruit through righteousness and obedience. Sin reverses this design, turning actions into spiritual uselessness and destruction.

The term also illustrates the contrast between outward appearances and true spiritual devotion. Irreverence and rebellion desecrate worship, but honest expression of grief before God is sacred. Misjudging genuine sorrow as moral corruption reveals a lack of spiritual discernment, reminding God's people that true righteousness is rooted in the heart rather than superficial assumptions.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1, Hannah suffers under the double burden of barrenness and continuous provocation from her rival Peninnah. Deeply distressed, she visits the sanctuary at Shiloh to pour out her heart before the LORD. As she prays, her lips move while her voice remains silent, a posture that was misunderstood by Eli the priest.

Eli rashly concludes that Hannah is drunk and rebukes her publicly. Hannah defends her character with remarkable grace and humility. She addresses Eli respectfully and begs, "Do not consider your maidservant a daughter of wicked," using beliyya'al as a singular, common noun to describe the kind of lawless, irreverent person Eli assumes she is.

Hannah clarifies that her silent trembling is not caused by wine or lawless contempt for God's house, but by the abundance of her deep anguish and grief. Her explanation refutes Eli's false accusation and demonstrates her profound piety.

  • Psalms 18:4 — "The pangs of death surrounded me, and the floods of ungodliness made me afraid."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A powerful irony unfolds across the early chapters of 1 Samuel involving this exact word. Eli falsely accuses the faithful, grieving Hannah of being a "daughter of wickedness" (beliyya'al) in 1 Samuel 1:16. However, in 1 Samuel 2:12, the text uses this exact same word to describe Eli's own biological sons, Hophni and Phinehas, calling them "corrupt" men who showed total contempt for the LORD's offerings.

Eli mistakenly saw moral ruin where there was only humble prayer, while failing to restrain true spiritual corruption within his own family and priestcraft.
