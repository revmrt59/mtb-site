WOMAN - (bath, Strong's H1323)

Summary:

The Hebrew word bath primary refers to a "daughter," identifying a female child or offspring within a family. Beyond literal family relations, Hebrew frequently uses bath idiomatically to describe a person's character, local identity, or connection to a place. In 1 Samuel 1:16, the word is translated as "woman" within the phrase "a wicked woman" (literally "a daughter of Belial"). Here, it contributes to Hannah's passionate defense of her godly character, assuring the high priest Eli that her silent, weeping prayer stems from deep grief rather than godless rebellion or unmanly drunkenness.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

1 Samuel opens during a spiritually dark period in Israel's history. The tabernacle at Shiloh was administered by the aging high priest Eli and his corrupt sons. Hannah, childless and deeply distressed, traveled to Shiloh to pour out her heart before Yahweh. As she prayed silently in her heart, moving her lips without making a sound, Eli misjudged her posture and accused her of being drunk.

In her respectful response, Hannah uses the word bath to reject Eli's harsh charge. She asks him not to view her as a worthless or rebellious person. The setting highlights the contrast between outward religious appearance and true inner devotion. Hannah's use of this word forms part of a direct plea for her character and spiritual integrity to be rightly understood.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of bath is a female offspring or "daughter." It is the feminine counterpart to the Hebrew word for "son." In its most literal sense, it refers to a biological daughter born to a father or mother.

In biblical Hebrew, bath also functions idiomatically in several regular ways:

  • Character or Quality: When combined with an abstract noun, bath identifies a woman who possesses or exhibits that specific character trait.

  • Geographic Identity: The word is used to personify the inhabitants of a region or city, such as "daughter of Zion" or "daughter of my people."

  • Dependent Settlements: When describing geography, the plural form of bath refers to smaller surrounding villages attached to a main city.

In 1 Samuel 1:16, bath carries the idiomatic sense of character. The word itself means "daughter," but when linked with the term for worthlessness (beliya'al), the phrase describes a woman marked by godless, lawless behavior.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Hebrew Scriptures, bath appears in various contexts that showcase its range of meaning:

First, it marks literal family lineage and inheritance. A daughter belongs to a household, receives parental care, and carries forward family connections.

Second, it personifies entire communities or nations under trial. Prophets often speak of the "daughter of my people" to express deep grief over the suffering and captivity of Israel. In these passages, the word conveys vulnerability, tenderness, and collective experience.

Third, it describes physical geography. Surrounding towns and villages attached to a primary fortified city are called its "daughters," showing their structural and administrative dependence on the mother city.

Finally, as seen in 1 Samuel 1:16, bath combines with descriptive terms to outline a person's spiritual or moral condition. Calling someone a "daughter of" a quality indicates that their life is thoroughly shaped by that characteristic.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's use of bath in 1 Samuel 1:16 reveals important theological truths about prayer, judgment, and spiritual character:

First, spiritual distress must not be confused with godless rebellion. A believer praying under intense sorrow may look overwhelmed or misunderstood by onlookers, but God sees the true condition of the heart. Hannah was not walking in lawless self-indulgence; she was taking her desperate need directly to the Lord.

Second, the passage exposes the danger of spiritual blindness in religious leaders. Eli was quick to suspect drunkenness in a devout woman, yet he failed to restrain the genuine, ongoing wickedness of his own sons (whom Scripture elsewhere labels as worthless men). This contrast shows that outward assessment can be deeply flawed when separated from spiritual discernment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:16, Hannah addresses Eli with notable respect, calling herself his "maidservant." She uses bath within the idiomatic phrase translated "a wicked woman" (literally "a daughter of Belial"). Belial signifies worthlessness, profitlessness, or utter lawlessness.

By insisting that she is not a "daughter of Belial," Hannah defends her spiritual standing. She explains that her silent lip movement was not the slurred behavior of a drunken reveler, but the earnest movement of a soul speaking "out of the abundance of my complaint and grief." Grammatically, bath is singular and feminine, specifically tailoring her statement to her personal identity as a woman standing before God and priest.

Her defense turns Eli's harsh rebuke into a pastoral blessing. Once Eli understands that her posture comes from faith rather than ungodliness, he commends her to God's favor.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Hebrew idiom linking bath with a qualifying noun is a vivid feature of Old Testament expression. Just as a physical daughter reflects the lineage of her parents, a "daughter of" a spiritual quality reflects the nature of that quality in daily life.

Hannah's choice of words established a firm boundary between genuine worship and profanity. Her sorrow was real, but her devotion was pure. The Lord honored her sincere cry, answering her prayer with the birth of Samuel, whom He would use to bring spiritual renewal to Israel.
