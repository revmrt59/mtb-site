ASKED - (shâʼal, Strong's H7592)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb sha'al means to ask, request, petition, or inquire. It spans everyday questioning between people, formal requests presented to kings, and earnest prayers brought before God. In 1 Samuel 1:17, the word describes Hannah's heartfelt prayer for a child, where Eli reassures her that the God of Israel will grant the personal petition she asked of Him.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The opening chapter of 1 Samuel introduces Hannah, a woman suffering deeply from childlessness in a culture where barrenness brought intense grief and social reproach. While worshipping at the sanctuary in Shiloh, Hannah pours out her soul to God in silent, weeping prayer. She vows that if the Lord gives her a son, she will dedicate him back to His service for his entire life.

Eli the priest sees her lips moving without any sound and initially assumes she is drunk. When Hannah explains that she is speaking out of great anguish and grief, Eli realizes his mistake and speaks a blessing over her. He pronounces peace and prays that the God of Israel will answer the petition she presented. The word translated "asked" in verse 17 captures this pivotal moment where human weakness brings a specific, personal request directly to God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb sha'al means to ask, request, petition, or inquire. In Scripture, it covers several related actions:

  • Inquiring to gain information from another person.

  • Requesting a gift, favor, or permission from someone in authority.

  • Consulting God or seeking divine guidance for a course of action.

  • Bringing an earnest petition or prayer to God in times of need.

In 1 Samuel 1:17, sha'al describes bringing a deeply felt personal request to the Lord. The word does not mean demanding or taking by force; rather, it describes presenting a specific desire to God with the understanding that He alone has the power and authority to fulfill it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses sha'al across many contexts. In everyday human interactions, it describes ordinary conversation, such as asking a neighbor for news or inquiring about someone's health. In governmental settings, citizens or royal guests use the word when bringing requests before a monarch.

In spiritual contexts, sha'al carries two main nuances:

  • Inquiring for direction: Seeking God's guidance before making important decisions or going into battle.

  • Supplication in prayer: Asking God to meet personal or communal needs.

When used in prayer, sha'al places the speaker in a position of humble reliance. The focus is not on elaborate language, but on the simple fact that the person asking depends entirely on God's willingness and ability to answer.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's act of asking highlights important truths about prayer and God's character. God invites His people to bring their genuine desires and emotional burdens directly to Him. Prayer is presented as real communication where a believer brings specific needs before the sovereign Lord.

Furthermore, God's willingness to hear Hannah reveals that He cares for those who are distressed and humble. He does not turn away from those who bring their pain to Him in faith. Asking God acknowledges His authority and admits that human effort alone cannot produce what is needed.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:17, Eli says to Hannah, "Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition which you have asked of Him."

The grammatical form of the verb is feminine, singular, and perfect. The feminine singular form directly addresses Hannah, highlighting her individual act of prayer. The perfect form presents her act of asking as a completed plea that she has already laid before God. This matches the flow of the story: Hannah's silent, agonizing prayer at the altar was complete, and Eli's priestly blessing assures her that her request was heard by the God of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 40:7 — "So he asked Pharaoh’s officers who were with him in custody in his lord’s house, saying, 'Why do you look so sad today?'" Here the word describes a thoughtful, everyday inquiry into someone's well-being.

  • 1 Samuel 1:20 — "So it came to pass in the process of time that Hannah conceived and bore a son, and called his name Samuel, saying, 'Because I have asked for him from the LORD.'" This verse connects directly back to verse 17, as Hannah names her child to remember the specific petition she asked of God.

  • 1 Samuel 12:17 — "Is the wheat harvest not today? I will call to the LORD, and He will send thunder and rain, that you may perceive and see that your wickedness is great which you have done in the sight of the LORD, in asking a king for yourselves." Here the word describes Israel's misplaced request for a human king, showing that what people ask for reveals where their hearts put their trust.

  • 2 Samuel 5:23 — "Therefore David inquired of the LORD, and He said, 'You shall not go up; circle around behind them, and come upon them in front of the mulberry trees.'" In this military setting, the word describes seeking divine direction from God before taking action.

  • 1 Kings 3:13 — "And I have also given you what you have not asked: both riches and honor, so that there shall not be any like you among the kings all your days." God commends Solomon because he asked for wisdom rather than personal wealth, promising to grant unasked blessings as well.

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a clear connection in the narrative of 1 Samuel 1 built around sha'al. Hannah asks (sha'al) for a son, presents her petition (she'elah), and names the child Samuel (Shemu'el), reflecting that he was asked of God. Later in the chapter, Hannah dedicates the child back to the Lord, using a related form of the word to show that he is given over to God's service for life. What began as an earnest personal request ends in complete devotion back to the God who answered her prayer.
