GOD - (ʼĕlôhîym, Strong's H430)

Summary:

The Hebrew word elohim is the primary general term for God or deity in the Old Testament. Depending on context, it can refer to the one true God, foreign gods, or earthly judges. When used for the true God, it presents Him as the supreme ruler and power over all creation. In 1 Samuel 1:17, Eli uses elohim alongside the title "of Israel" to assure the grieving Hannah that her personal prayer has been placed before the living God, who holds both the authority and faithful goodwill to grant her request.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

1 Samuel opens during the dark and turbulent period of Israel's judges. The worship center at Shiloh was marred by spiritual compromise among the priesthood. Against this backdrop, Hannah traveled with her family to Shiloh carrying immense personal sorrow due to her barrenness and the persistent torment of her rival, Peninnah.

In her deep distress, Hannah prayed silently before the Lord, moving her lips without making a sound. The high priest Eli observed her and mistakenly assumed she was drunk. After Hannah corrected his harsh misjudgment and explained her profound grief, Eli spoke a word of pastoral encouragement, asking that "the God of Israel" grant the petition she had poured out before Him.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word elohim is a general Hebrew term for divine beings or authority. It can mean:

  • The one true God who created and rules the universe.

  • Foreign or pagan gods worshipped by neighboring nations.

  • Heavenly beings or earthly authorities acting under divine mandate.

Grammatically, elohim is a masculine plural noun. However, when Scripture refers to the true God of Israel, it consistently pairs elohim with singular verbs and singular adjectives. This grammatical feature expresses divine majesty, absolute fullness, and supreme authority, while maintaining that God is one.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, elohim highlights God's sovereign authority over nature, history, and humanity.

When Scripture describes creation, elohim demonstrates His power to bring the cosmos into existence purely by His word. In legal and covenantal settings, the word distinguishes the true God from worthless idols made by human hands.

When paired with geographical or relational descriptions—such as "God of Israel" or "God of your fathers"—the word connects the supreme Creator of the universe with His specific, historical commitment to His chosen people.

4. Theological Implications

The title elohim establishes that the ultimate ruler of creation is not an impersonal force or an isolated local deity. He possesses absolute power over all human circumstances, including human reproduction and personal suffering.

At the same time, the broader passage shows that His supreme power does not render Him distant. While pagan deities were viewed as unpredictable or indifferent, the God of Israel hears the quiet, inner cries of the brokenhearted. His sovereignty means that no human situation is beyond His capability to transform.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:17, the specific phrase used is "the God of Israel." This phrase carries strong contextual weight:

  • It shifts the focus from human failure to divine faithfulness. Human leadership at Shiloh had failed to recognize Hannah's genuine devotion, but the "God of Israel" knew her heart.

  • Eli's statement functions as a priestly blessing. By invoking "the God of Israel," Eli directed Hannah's faith away from his own flawed human authority and toward the living God.

  • The title recalled God's covenant loyalty. Hannah was not praying to a distant, unknown spirit, but to the God who had pledged Himself to care for His people.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:1 — "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth." The word introduces the supreme Creator exercising absolute power over all initial creation.

  • Leviticus 26:1 — "You shall not make idols for yourselves... to bow down to it; for I am the LORD your God." Here the term identifies the divine authority who claims exclusive allegiance from His redeemed people.

  • Psalms 42:1 — "As the deer pants for the water brooks, so pants my soul for You, O God." The psalm uses the word to express a deep personal longing for fellowship with the living God.

  • Jeremiah 23:36 — "...for you have perverted the words of the living God, the LORD of hosts, our God." The word highlights divine ownership and absolute truth over against false human assertions.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In 1 Samuel 1:17, the grammatical form of elohim is masculine plural. This standard plural form highlights God's supreme majesty without multiplying His person.

The impact of Eli's blessing using this name is evident in the immediate aftermath. In 1 Samuel 1:18, Hannah accepted the word spoken in the name of the God of Israel, went her way, ate, and was no longer sad. Her burden was lifted even before the physical answer arrived, because her request had been committed to the sovereign God.
