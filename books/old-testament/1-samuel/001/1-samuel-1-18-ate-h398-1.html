ATE - (ʼâkal, Strong's H398)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb akal means to eat, consume, or devour. It is the primary word in the Old Testament for taking food into the body for physical nourishment. In 1 Samuel 1:18, Hannah's act of eating marks the end of her sorrowful fasting and demonstrates her renewed peace and trust after receiving Eli's blessing.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The action takes place at Shiloh, where the tabernacle rested during the period of the judges. Elkanah traveled there yearly with his family to worship and offer sacrifices. In ancient Israel, peace offerings involved a sacrificial meal shared by the family in the presence of the Lord.

For years, Hannah was unable to participate in these joyful meals because of her grief over her barrenness and the harsh provocations of Peninnah. Her sorrow was so severe that she wept and refused to eat. The sanctuary at Shiloh had become a place of deep personal pain for her until she poured out her soul before God in prayer.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb akal refers to the basic human act of eating or consuming food. It covers a wide range of everyday and symbolic situations in Scripture:

  • Taking daily physical food and nourishment.

  • Partaking of ceremonial or sacrificial meals.

  • Figurative consumption, such as fire devouring wood, a sword destroying enemies, or land consuming its inhabitants.

The word itself describes the physical action of eating. Context determines whether the eating represents daily survival, a religious feast, or a symbol of emotional and spiritual restoration.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, akal is used in several distinct settings:

  • Everyday Meals: It describes normal human nourishment and hospitality, such as eating bread or enjoying the fruit of the land.

  • Covenant and Sacrificial Feasts: Eating in the presence of God signified fellowship, peace, and covenant communion among God's people.

  • Fasting and Mourning: The deliberate refusal to eat (akal) was a standard sign of grief, repentance, or severe distress. Resuming the act of eating marked the end of mourning and the restoration of gladness.

  • Metaphorical Devouring: In prophetic and poetic literature, akal describes complete destruction, such as divine judgment sweeping away a nation.

4. Theological Implications

While akal simply means to eat, its spiritual meaning in times of sorrow is significant. Refusing food in Scripture reflects a heavy heart, while breaking a fast signals faith and acceptance of God's care.

Hannah’s refusal to eat stemmed from overwhelming sorrow. When she finally ate, it showed that true peace comes through prayer and trust in God's promises. Her physical appetite returned because her internal burden had been lifted. This demonstrates how genuine spiritual rest affects a person's whole being, including physical health and routine actions.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:7, Hannah's sorrow was so intense that "she wept and did not eat." Her husband Elkanah tried to comfort her, asking why her heart was grieved and why she was not eating.

After praying silently in the tabernacle and receiving Eli’s blessing—"Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition"—Hannah expressed faith in God's mercy. The narrative records that she went her way, "ate," and her face was no longer sad (1 Samuel 1:18).

The grammatical form used for "ate" in this verse is a feminine singular verb in the narrative past sequence, carrying the story directly from her prayer to her action. The sequence is vital: Hannah ate before there was any physical proof that her prayer was answered. She was not yet pregnant, yet her grief was gone. Her act of eating was a physical demonstration that she had placed her burden in God's hands and trusted His care.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 31:15 — "Are we not considered strangers by him? For he has sold us, and also completely consumed our money."

  • Joshua 24:13 — "I have given you a land for which you did not labor, and cities which you did not build, and you dwell in them; you eat of the vineyards and olive groves which you did not plant."

  • Psalms 78:25 — "Men ate angels' food; He sent them food to the full."

  • Ecclesiastes 5:18 — "Here is what I have seen: It is good and fitting for one to eat and drink, and to enjoy the good of all his labor in which he toils under the sun all the days of his life which God gives him; for it is his heritage."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The sacrificial environment at Shiloh adds depth to Hannah's act. Sacrificial meals were intended to be occasions of joy and thanksgiving for God's blessings. Peninnah had turned these meals into occasions of grief for Hannah.

When Hannah finally ate in verse 18, she reclaimed her seat at the table not because her circumstances had changed, but because her heart had changed. Her act of eating transformed what had been a scene of yearly torment into a genuine meal of peace and faith in God's promise.
