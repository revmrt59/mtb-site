LONGER - (ʻôwd, Strong's H5750)

Summary:

The Hebrew word ‘ōd expresses continuation, duration, or repetition, translated into English as "still," "yet," "again," or "while." When combined with a negative particle, it indicates that an ongoing state or action has come to an end, translated as "no longer" or "not anymore." In 1 Samuel 1:18, the word describes the dramatic change in Hannah's emotional state after she received Eli's blessing, marking the exact moment her long-standing burden of grief ceased and her face was "no longer" sad.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew word ‘ōd is a common noun and adverb used throughout Old Testament narrative, poetry, and prophecy. It comes from a root idea of turning back, repeating, or continuing. The word communicates that something is still happening, still lingering, or taking place once again.

In biblical Hebrew, ‘ōd serves as a key temporal marker. It tells the reader whether an action is persisting over time or whether a situation is being extended. When paired with a negative particle, it serves a critical narrative function by marking the definitive end of an established condition.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word ‘ōd can refer to several related concepts depending on how it functions in a sentence:

  • Continuation: "still," "yet," or "while" (describing an action or state that continues without interruption).

  • Repetition or addition: "again," "further," or "another" (describing a repeated action or an added element).

  • Duration: "as long as" or "all life long" (describing the whole extent of a time period).

  • Cessation (when negated): "no longer," "not anymore," or "never again" (describing the abrupt stopping of a previous condition).

In 1 Samuel 1:18, the word is joined with a negative particle. Here it means "no longer," signaling that Hannah's long period of downcast sorrow had officially ended.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, ‘ōd provides vivid time markers that help structure biblical stories and songs.

In personal stories, it often highlights lingering conditions or ongoing hope. Characters ask if a relative is "still" alive, or narrative descriptions note that a person was "still" speaking or waiting. In poetic literature, the word expresses ongoing devotion, such as praising God "as long as" life endures. In prophetic texts, it frequently announces future changes, promising that judgment or blessing will "yet" come, or that a humbled people will "no longer" suffer oppression.

When used with a negative statement, ‘ōd draws a sharp line between past suffering or practice and a new reality. The past condition was real and continuous, but the new word or action brings it to a complete halt.

4. Theological Implications

The use of ‘ōd with negation in 1 Samuel 1:18 highlights a major theme in biblical faith: God's word breaks the continuity of human suffering.

Hannah had endured years of distress, mockery, and misunderstanding. Her sorrow was not a fleeting mood; it was an ongoing, year-after-year reality. Yet human suffering, no matter how prolonged, is not ultimate. When God intervenes through His word, continuous grief gives way to peace.

This shift also shows the nature of faith. Hannah’s face was "no longer sad" before she visibly saw the answer to her prayer. She had not yet conceived a child, nor had her home circumstances physically altered. Her burden lifted because she received the priestly blessing in faith, demonstrating that true spiritual peace relies on God's promise rather than immediate physical sight.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The context of 1 Samuel 1 makes the statement in verse 18 deeply moving. For years, Hannah had suffered childlessness and the persistent provocation of Peninnah. Her pain was so intense that when she prayed silently at the tabernacle in Shiloh, the priest Eli mistook her silent weeping and moving lips for drunkenness.

After Hannah explained her deep anguish, Eli realized his mistake and pronounced a word of peace: "Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition." Hannah responded with humble gratitude, went on her way, ate food, and her face was "no longer sad."

The word ‘ōd highlights the complete contrast between Hannah's past and her present. Her grief had been an ongoing, continuous presence in her life. The moment she rested on the word of peace spoken in God's sanctuary, that continuous grief was broken. The sorrow that had marked her countenance for years was brought to an immediate end.

7. Other Relevant Insights

From a grammatical standpoint, ‘ōd in 1 Samuel 1:18 is parsed as a common noun with singular number. Its function here in combination with the negative particle serves as an adverbial modifier of time, describing the complete stopping of the action or state described by the following word.

The narrative places Hannah's restored appetite and altered expression immediately after her prayer and Eli's blessing. By stating that her face was "no longer sad," the biblical text emphasizes that internal peace produces visible outward transformation long before external circumstances change.
