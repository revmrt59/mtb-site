EARLY - (shâkam, Strong's H7925)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shakam means to get up early in the morning, to start early, or to act with promptness and zeal. In biblical narratives, it describes rising at dawn to begin a journey, fulfill a command, or gather for worship. In 1 Samuel 1:19, the word describes Elkanah and his household rising at first light to worship the Lord before returning home to Ramah, highlighting their shared priority of praising God following Hannah's answered prayer.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israelite daily life, major activities began at daybreak. Because the heat of the day made labor and travel difficult, rising early was essential for completing long journeys, preparing sacrifices, or organizing military actions.

Beyond its practical necessity, rising early carried cultural and spiritual meaning. Setting out at dawn demonstrated earnestness, resolve, and devotion. When someone rose early to carry out a task, it showed that the matter was urgent and held top priority.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shakam refers to getting up early in the morning or starting an action at daybreak. The word focuses on prompt action at the start of the day.

When shakam is combined with another verb, it describes doing that second action early or diligently. In narrative accounts, it often points to literal morning activity. In poetic and prophetic passages, it develops an idiomatic sense meaning to act eagerly, persistently, or repeatedly.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses shakam across historical narratives and prophetic books to convey eager compliance, disciplined readiness, or persistent effort.

In narrative accounts, leaders and patriarchs rise early to obey divine instructions, construct altars, prepare for battle, or seek God in worship. Starting at dawn indicates that the individual did not delay or hesitate.

In prophetic texts, particularly in Jeremiah, the word frequently describes God's persistent efforts to warn His people. The phrase describes God "rising early and sending" His prophets, expressing divine patience, relentless mercy, and urgent initiative to turn Israel back to righteousness.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of shakam highlights the connection between priority and obedience. When God gives a direction, faithful servants respond without procrastination. Rising early to obey shows a heart aligned with God's will.

The word also illuminates God's own character. When Scripture describes God as "rising early" to speak or send messengers, it uses human language to convey God's eager desire for fellowship and His untiring patience toward His covenant people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1, Hannah had travelled to Shiloh in deep grief over her childlessness. After pouring out her heart in secret prayer before the Lord, Eli the priest spoke a word of blessing, assuring her that God would grant her petition. Hannah received this blessing with faith, ate, and was no longer sad.

Verse 19 opens with Elkanah and his household rising early in the morning. Before embarking on their journey back to Ramah, the family gathered to worship before the Lord. The choice of shakam emphasizes that worship was their first thought at dawn. Rather than rushing off to travel, they prioritized giving thanks and seeking God's presence together. The prompt morning worship reflects a household united in faith and gratitude for God's goodness.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 21:14 — "So Abraham rose early in the morning, and took bread and a skin of water..."

  • Exodus 34:4 — "So he cut two tablets of stone like the first ones. Then Moses rose early in the morning and went up on Mount Sinai..."

  • 2 Chronicles 36:15 — "And the LORD God of their fathers sent warnings to them by His messengers, rising up early and sending them, because He had compassion on His people..."

  • Jeremiah 26:5 — "...to heed the words of My servants the prophets whom I sent to you, both rising up early and sending them..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In 1 Samuel 1:19, the verb appears in a masculine plural, imperfect wayyiqtol form. This grammatical form presents the action as part of the ongoing narrative sequence, depicting the family acting together in prompt obedience.

The immediate transition from morning worship to their journey home underlines that true worship prepares believers for daily life and responsibilities. Elkanah and Hannah did not view worship as a burdensome obligation before travel, but as the primary start to their day.
