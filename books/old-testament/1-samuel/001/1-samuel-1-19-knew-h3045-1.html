KNEW - (yâdaʻ, Strong's H3045)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yada means to know, perceive, experience, acknowledge, or enter into personal relationship. Depending on the context, it can describe intellectual recognition, personal familiarity, or physical marital intimacy. In 1 Samuel 1:19, the word refers to physical intimacy between husband and wife within marriage. It highlights Elkanah coming together with his wife Hannah in marital closeness, providing the quiet human setting in which God remembered Hannah and answered her prayer for a child.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

1 Samuel opens with the painful sorrow of Hannah, a woman who suffered long years of childlessness. Despite her husband Elkanah's genuine affection, Hannah faced deep grief and persistent taunting from Elkanah's second wife, Peninnah. During an annual family pilgrimage to worship at the tabernacle in Shiloh, Hannah poured out her soul in silent, tearful prayer before the Lord, asking for a son and vowing to dedicate him to divine service.

Eli the priest observed her and, after realizing she was praying from deep anguish rather than drunkenness, pronounced a blessing that God would grant her petition. Encouraged, Hannah worshiped, ate, and returned home with her family to Ramah. The narrative then records that early the next morning, Elkanah knew Hannah his wife, setting the stage for the birth of Samuel, who would become a pivotal prophet and judge in Israel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yada has a rich variety of uses in Scripture. It can mean to acquire information, recognize a fact, gain practical skill, or experience something directly. It is not limited to theoretical knowledge in the mind. Instead, it frequently emphasizes personal connection, practical experience, and mutual relationship.

When used regarding human relationships, yada describes deep familiarity and personal recognition. When used specifically of a husband and wife, the word refers to the physical sexual union of marriage. In this setting, the word highlights physical union as a personal, honorable, and intimate bond between two people, rather than a mere biological act.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, yada appears in several distinct settings:

  • Practical and Intellectual Awareness: It describes observing facts, gaining understanding, or acquiring technical skill.

  • Personal Recognition and Loyalty: It refers to acknowledging authority, respecting a covenant, or knowing someone personally.

  • Divine Awareness: It describes God's complete knowledge of human hearts, actions, and needs.

  • Marital Physical Union: It expresses physical intimacy between a husband and a wife.

By using the same word for personal acquaintance, deep understanding, and marital union, Scripture presents marital intimacy as an expression of personal commitment and complete mutual self-giving.

4. Theological Implications

The narrative in 1 Samuel 1 highlights how God's redemptive work intersects with ordinary human life. Marriage and physical intimacy are part of God's good creation design. Scripture does not view physical marital relations as unholy or detached from spiritual life. Immediately after worshiping the Lord at Shiloh, Elkanah and Hannah returned home and engaged in normal marital life.

Furthermore, the passage brings together human responsibility and divine action. Elkanah knew his wife, and the Lord remembered her. Human physical union alone could not overcome Hannah's long-standing barrenness; God opened her womb. Yet God chose to fulfill His promise through the ordinary, sacred means of faithful marriage.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:19, the word appears in a masculine, singular, imperfect form with a wayyiqtol narrative structure, continuing the chronological sequence of events. The action follows their morning worship at Shiloh and their return home to Ramah.

The placement of this word creates a meaningful transition in the story. After years of barrenness and weeping, Hannah moved from the sanctuary of God back into her home. Her husband Elkanah knew her in the privacy of their marriage. The text immediately adds that the Lord remembered her.

This sequence shows that God's answer to Hannah's prayer did not require extraordinary legal ceremonies or pagan rituals. In contrast to surrounding ancient near-eastern religions, which used ritual prostitution to seek fertility from idols, Israel's God works through normal, quiet, holy marital union. Elkanah's act was one of covenant faithfulness toward his wife, and God blessed that union with the gift of a child.

  • Isaiah 51:7 — "Listen to Me, you who know righteousness, You people in whose heart is My law..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The biblical use of yada for marital intimacy guards against two opposite errors regarding marriage. It rejects the idea that physical intimacy is dirty or unspiritual, placing it instead immediately after acts of holy worship. At the same time, it rejects the view that physical intimacy is merely mechanical or commercial, framing it instead as a deep, personal union between husband and wife.

In Hannah's story, this quiet act of marital faithfulness became the setting through which God provided a leader who would turn the nation back to Himself. Samuel's birth began not with political fanfare, but with humble prayer, holy worship, and faithful love within a household.
