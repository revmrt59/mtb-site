REMEMBERED - (zâkar, Strong's H2142)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb zakar means to recall, bring to mind, mention, or keep someone or something in view. When applied to God, the word does not imply that He previously forgot, but rather that He turns His focused attention toward a person to act on their behalf. In 1 Samuel 1:19, the statement that "the LORD remembered her" signals the dramatic turning point in Hannah's story, where God answers her earnest prayer and moves to fulfill her plea for a child.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the Old Testament, the concept of remembering goes far beyond simple mental recall. In daily human experience, memory can fade or fail, but in the biblical mindset, memory is closely connected to action. To remember someone is to keep that person in view and respond to their situation accordingly.

When God is said to remember, it never means He had slipped into forgetfulness. Instead, it marks a moment in redemptive narrative when God actively intervenes in human history based on His character, His promises, or a person's earnest cry. In 1 Samuel 1, Hannah suffers under the burden of childlessness and the distress of ongoing provocation. Her silent, tear-filled prayer at the tabernacle at Shiloh asks God to look upon her affliction and remember her. The arrival of this word in verse 19 demonstrates that God was attentive to her all along and had set the exact moment to respond.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb zakar can refer to several related actions depending on who is performing the action:

  • To keep in mind or call to mind.

  • To mention or speak of someone by name.

  • To bring a past event or promise into present view.

When people are the subject, the word often describes honoring God's deeds, keeping His commandments in mind, or calling upon His name in prayer. When God is the subject, the word refers to His active favor and intervention. He sets His attention upon a person or situation, bringing help, deliverance, or the fulfillment of what was requested.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, zakar appears in several distinct settings:

  • Covenant Action: God remembers His promises made to His people, moving to deliver them from trouble or exile.

  • Prayer and Worship: Believers call upon God to remember them in times of distress or righteousness, trusting in His mercy rather than their own merits.

  • Personal Deliverance: Individual narratives highlight God remembering a person in desperate or isolated circumstances, marking a turn from sorrow to blessing.

Human memory is fragile and subject to neglect, but God's memory is constant. When Scripture notes that God remembered an individual, it highlights the transition from a period of waiting or suffering to a period of active divine blessing.

4. Theological Implications

The statement that God remembers reveals fundamental truths about His character and His relationship with His people:

  • God Hears the Afflicted: God is aware of hidden grief and private prayers. Hannah prayed in her heart, moving her lips without making a sound. The outcome proves that God heard her silent longing.

  • Timing and Faithfulness: Divine remembrance takes place according to God's wise timing. The period of waiting before God acts is not a sign of His absence, but a time in which faith is refined.

  • Prayer Moves God to Act: Hannah explicitly asked God to remember her in verse 11. God's response in verse 19 highlights the direct connection between persistent, honest prayer and divine action.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:19, Elkanah and his family rise early in the morning, worship before the LORD at Shiloh, and return to their home in Ramah. The text then records that Elkanah knew Hannah his wife, "and the LORD remembered her."

Grammatically, the target word appears in this verse as a masculine singular form in the imperfect wayyiqtol, which drives the narrative forward by linking Hannah's act of worship and return home directly to God's response.

The context emphasizes a striking contrast between human perception and divine reality. Eli the priest initially misjudged Hannah, thinking she was drunk when she was praying. Peninnah tormented her because of her barrenness. Yet while others misunderstood or provoked her, the LORD was attending to her prayer. When the text states that the LORD remembered her, it marks the end of her sorrow and the beginning of Samuel's life—a child who would play a crucial role in the history of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 8:1 — "Then God remembered Noah, and every living thing, and all the animals that were with him in the ark."

  • Exodus 2:24 — "So God heard their groaning, and God remembered His covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob."

  • Nehemiah 5:19 — "Remember me, my God, for good, according to all that I have done for this people."

  • Psalms 103:14 — "For He knows our frame; He remembers that we are dust."

  • Psalms 111:5 — "He has given food to those who fear Him; He will ever be mindful of His covenant."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The turning point marked by God remembering Hannah extends far beyond her personal household. Samuel's birth, which resulted from God remembering Hannah, transformed the nation of Israel. Samuel grew up to be a prophet and judge who anointed Israel's first kings, Saul and David.

This shows how God's response to an individual's private prayer can have vast kingdom implications. What began as a mother's tearful plea in the tabernacle resulted in leadership that guided an entire nation back to God.
