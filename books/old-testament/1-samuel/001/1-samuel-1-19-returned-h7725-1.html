RETURNED - (shûwb, Strong's H7725)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub means to turn back, return, go back, or restore. In 1 Samuel 1:19, the word describes Elkanah and his household completing their annual worship at Shiloh and traveling back home to Ramah. In this passage, the verb marks the transition from sanctuary worship to ordinary home life, showing that Hannah returned home in peace, trusting God to work in answer to her prayer.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The book of 1 Samuel opens during a spiritually dark period in Israel's history, near the end of the era of the judges. Each year, Elkanah traveled with his family from their home town of Ramah to the central sanctuary at Shiloh to offer sacrifices to the Lord of hosts.

During one of these annual visits, Elkanah's wife Hannah poured out her soul in deep grief before the Lord because she was barren. After the priest Eli spoke a word of blessing and peace over her, Hannah's sorrow lifted. Early the next morning, after worshipping before the Lord once more, the family "returned" to their home in Ramah. The word shub captures this physical journey back home after an intense encounter with God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Shub can refer to turning around, going back, returning to a place, or restoring something to a previous state.

The verb is used in several primary ways across Scripture:

  • Physical movement: turning back or traveling back to a place of origin, such as returning home from a journey.

  • Relational or spiritual direction: turning back to God in repentance, or turning away from evil.

  • Reversal or restoration: returning property to its rightful owner, turning back anger, or bringing something back to a former condition.

  • Repeated action: turning back to do something again.

In 1 Samuel 1:19, the word describes physical travel: Elkanah and his household turned back from Shiloh and traveled home to Ramah.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

across the Old Testament narrative and prophetic books, shub serves as a key word for movement—both physical and spiritual. In story passages, it frequently marks transitions between scenes, as characters finish a duty or journey and return to their regular dwellings.

In prophetic texts, the word often carries spiritual depth, calling people who have strayed to turn back to the Lord. Whether describing a literal footsteps home or a spiritual turning of the heart, the word emphasizes a change of direction toward where one belongs.

4. Theological Implications

The narrative use of shub in 1 Samuel 1:19 highlights how faith operates in daily life. Faith does not require staying perpetually at the holy site; true worship leads back into regular daily responsibilities. Elkanah and Hannah worshipped God at Shiloh and then returned home, taking the blessing and peace of God back into their ordinary routine.

Furthermore, God's divine work often unfolds in quiet, routine places. Hannah prayed at the sanctuary, but the answer to her prayer took place after they returned to Ramah. God works through the ordinary rhythms of home, marriage, and family life just as powerfully as He does at the altar.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:19, the grammar presents a sequence of actions undertaken by the household together: they rose early, worshipped before the Lord, and returned to their house at Ramah. Grammatically, the verb appears in the wayyiqtol masculine plural imperfect form, carrying the narrative forward into the next stage of the story.

This return to Ramah is full of quiet significance. Before Hannah prayed, her barrenness caused her bitterness and tears. After Eli's blessing, her countenance was no longer sad, and she ate food. Her willingness to turn back and return home demonstrates that her heart was at rest. She did not linger at Shiloh out of anxiety, but returned home in quiet confidence. Immediately after they returned, the text notes that the Lord remembered her, setting the stage for Samuel's birth.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 3:19 — "In the sweat of your face you shall eat bread till you return to the ground..." Here "return" speaks of physical mortality, as human bodies go back to the dust from which they were made.

  • Genesis 43:10 — "For if we had not delayed, surely we would have returned this second time." The word "returned" refers to literal physical travel back from a journey.

  • Job 7:10 — "He shall return no more to his house..." Here "return" describes the finality of earthly death, after which a person no longer goes back to his home.

  • Jeremiah 3:22 — "Return, you backsliding children, and I will heal your backslidings." The word "Return" is used spiritually to invite wandering people back into fellowship with God.

  • Hosea 14:2 — "Take words with you, and return to the LORD..." Here "return" functions as a command to turn back to God through sincere confession and faith.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The movement in 1 Samuel 1 underscores household unity in worship. Elkanah did not return alone, nor did Hannah stay behind in sorrow. The entire family worshipped together and returned together. The spiritual peace Hannah received at Shiloh refreshed the entire household as they returned to Ramah.
