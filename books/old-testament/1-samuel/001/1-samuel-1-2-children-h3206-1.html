CHILDREN - (yeled, Strong's H3206)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun yeled refers to a child, offspring, or young person born to parents. In 1 Samuel 1:2, the masculine plural form refers to the offspring born to Peninnah, establishing the central personal contrast in Elkanah's household between Peninnah's fertility and Hannah's barrenness.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word yeled comes from a basic Hebrew verbal root meaning to bear, bring forth, or father children. It directly connects a child to the act of birth and parentage. The term appears across the Old Testament to describe children at various stages of growth—from newborn infants to young boys, older youths, and even young men raised together. In ancient Israel, household lineage and heritage were central to family identity, memory, and spiritual life. Being blessed with offspring was seen as a profound sign of divine favor and provision for a family's future, while childlessness brought emotional sorrow and societal hardship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun yeled fundamentally refers to a child or offspring. Depending on the setting, the word can mean:

  • An infant or newborn baby

  • A young child or youth

  • A son or male descendant

  • Young men associated with one another by generation or rank

In 1 Samuel 1:2, the word is used in its masculine plural form to refer to the offspring born to Peninnah. It identifies her children directly as her physical descendants within Elkanah's household.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament narrative, poetry, and law, yeled takes on distinct shades of meaning based on the context:

  • Physical Offspring and Heritage: The primary usage identifies children born to parents. It highlights the natural progression of generations and the biological gift of physical descendants within a family.

  • Vulnerability and Protection: In stories of severe trial or flight, the word often emphasizes the weakness and dependence of young children who require parental care and protection.

  • Generational Groupings: In some historical contexts, the word refers to young men who grew up together, such as royal advisors of a king's generation who lack the maturity and wisdom of older elders.

4. Theological Implications

While yeled itself is a standard term for offspring, its placement in redemptive history highlights key biblical truths:

  • God as the Source of Life: Offspring are never presented in Scripture as purely natural accidents. Children are gifts from God, who opens and closes the womb according to His sovereign purpose.

  • Comfort for the Distressed: Stories involving children in difficult circumstances show that God hears the cries of distressed mothers and forgotten families.

  • Preparation for Sovereign Deliverance: Major developments in salvation history frequently begin with the birth of a specific child. When God raises up a leader or prophet, He often works through a surprising birth to demonstrate that deliverance comes from Him alone.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:2, the word children plays a crucial role in setting up the entire narrative of Samuel's birth and calling:

  • Establishing Household Contrast: The verse introduces Elkanah's two wives: Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children. The simple statement establishes a stark domestic contrast.

  • Illuminating Hannah's Grief: Peninnah's children served as a constant visible reminder of Hannah's empty arms and societal shame. Peninnah used her fertility to provoke Hannah bitterly year after year.

  • Setting the Stage for Faith: The painful contrast between having children and having none drove Hannah not to bitterness against God, but to earnest, persistent prayer at the tabernacle.

  • Highlighting Sovereign Grace: Humanly speaking, Peninnah possessed the biological fruitfulness, but God was preparing to turn Hannah's deep pain into the instrument of blessing for all Israel through the birth of Samuel.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The grammatical form of the word in 1 Samuel 1:2 is a masculine plural noun, referring collectively to the children born to Peninnah.

  • The contrast in 1 Samuel 1 between Peninnah having children and Hannah having none reflects a common pattern in Old Testament narrative, where physical barrenness becomes the backdrop for God's miraculous power to grant life and advance His purpose.

  • The grief caused by childlessness in ancient Israel was tied not only to personal affection, but to household inheritance and social standing. Hannah's ultimate victory in receiving a child led to her celebrated song of praise in 1 Samuel 2, which shapes the theological theme of the entire book.
