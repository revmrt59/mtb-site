PENINNAH - (Pᵉninnâh, Strong's H6444)

Summary:

The Hebrew name Peninnah is a feminine proper noun that means "pearl," "jewel," or "coral." It comes from a Hebrew root associated with precious gems or red coral. In 1 Samuel 1:2, Peninnah identifies the second wife of Elkanah. Her presence introduces the painful household conflict between fertility and barrenness, setting the stage for God's gracious intervention in Hannah's life and the birth of Samuel.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Peninnah appears at the beginning of 1 Samuel during the closing years of the period of the judges. This was a era marked by spiritual corruption and social instability in Israel. Elkanah, a Levite living in the hill country of Ephraim, had two wives: Hannah and Peninnah.

Polygamy was practiced in the ancient Near East and tolerated under Mosaic civil law, but Scripture consistently presents it as a source of domestic strife, jealousy, and heartache. The opening narrative of 1 Samuel highlights this pattern. Peninnah had borne children to Elkanah, whereas Hannah was childless.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Peninnah is a feminine proper noun. The name connects to the Hebrew noun peninim, which refers to pearls, red coral, or precious stones prized for their beauty and worth. Thus, the name Peninnah literally means "pearl," "coral," or "jewel."

In 1 Samuel 1:2, the word functions simply as the personal proper name of Elkanah's second wife. The name itself carries no inherently negative or hostile meaning. Its difficult associations in biblical history come entirely from Peninnah's actions and heart attitude toward Hannah within the household narrative.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses the name Peninnah exclusively in the opening chapter of 1 Samuel to identify the fertile wife of Elkanah. Although her name means "jewel" or "pearl," her conduct toward her fellow wife stands in sharp contrast to the beauty her name implies.

The narrative uses Peninnah's presence to establish a clear contrast between two women in the family: Peninnah, who enjoyed natural fruitfulness, and Hannah, who experienced temporary barrenness. This contrast shows that outward abundance can exist alongside a harsh, proud spirit, while deep sorrow can drive a devout soul to earnest prayer.

4. Theological Implications

Several theological truths emerge from the passage in which Peninnah appears:

  • The pain of departing from God's ideal: Peninnah's role in the family demonstrates the tension and rivalry that regularly resulted when human practices diverged from God's original creation model for marriage.

  • God's sovereignty over human circumstances: Peninnah's fertility was not a reward for superior righteousness, nor was Hannah's childlessness a sign of divine rejection. God permitted this hardship to guide Hannah toward total reliance on Him.

  • Outward prosperity versus inward godly character: A person may possess external blessings, such as Peninnah's large family, yet lack kindness and spiritual grace. God looks past external status to judge the condition of the heart.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:2, Peninnah is introduced immediately beside Hannah. The text states that "the name of one was Hannah, and the name of the other Peninnah. Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children."

This verse sets up the central conflict of the opening narrative. Peninnah is named second, yet she is the one who has borne children. The verse highlights her fertility right before describing Hannah's barrenness, creating immediate narrative tension. In the verses that follow, Peninnah uses her fruitfulness to provoke Hannah bitterly, seeking to make her miserable over her empty womb. The statement in verse 2 explains the background of Peninnah's pride and Hannah's silent pain, which ultimately drove Hannah to pour out her soul before the LORD at Shiloh.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 1:4 — "And whenever the time came for Elkanah to make an offering, he would give portions to Peninnah his wife and to all her sons and daughters."

In this passage, Peninnah receives portions of the sacrificial meal for herself and her children. The distribution of the sacrificial meat brings the family dynamic into sharp focus, as Elkanah gives a double portion to Hannah because he loves her, even though Peninnah has borne him sons and daughters.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Contrast between name and character: The literal meaning of Peninnah ("pearl" or "jewel") creates a subtle narrative irony. While her name suggests something precious and delicate, her behavior toward Hannah was severe and provoking.

  • Greek translation (Septuagint): In the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, the name is rendered as Phenanna.

  • Canonical pattern: Peninnah fits a broader pattern in the Old Testament narrative where a fertile rival wife taunts a childless wife, similar to Hagar and Sarah in Genesis 16 or Leah and Rachel in Genesis 29–30. In every instance, God hears the cry of the afflicted and accomplishes His divine plan through the humbled wife who relies upon Him.
