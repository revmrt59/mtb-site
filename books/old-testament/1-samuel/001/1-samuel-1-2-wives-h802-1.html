WIVES - (ʼishshâh, Strong's H802)

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ishshah means "woman" or "wife." In 1 Samuel 1:2, it appears in the plural to refer to the two wives of Elkanah, Hannah and Peninnah. This word introduces a household shaped by polygamy, where jealousy and barrenness set the stage for God's merciful intervention in answering Hannah's prayer and raising up the prophet Samuel.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew noun 'ishshah is the standard Old Testament word for an adult female person ("woman") or a married woman ("wife"). The surrounding sentence determines whether the word refers to a woman generally or a wife specifically. In 1 Samuel 1:2, the story opens during the period of the judges, a time marked by spiritual decline and social instability in Israel.

The verse introduces Elkanah, a Levite living in the hill country of Ephraim, and states that he had two wives. Although God's original creation design established marriage as a union between one man and one woman, polygamy was practiced in the ancient Near East and tolerated in Israel's civil laws. The introduction of two wives immediately signals to the reader that domestic strife is about to follow.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of the word is a female human being ("woman") or a spouse ("wife"). When the word is linked to a specific man, it means his wife. In the plural form, it refers to multiple women or wives.

The word itself is a descriptive title rather than a moral assessment. It simply identifies a person's biological sex or marital status. Any moral character, faith, or spiritual condition attributed to a wife comes from the surrounding narrative, not from the word itself.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, Scripture uses 'ishshah in many everyday, legal, and historical settings. It appears in the creation account for the first woman, in God's laws governing family rights, and in historical genealogies.

When used of wives, the word emphasizes the marital relationship. In monogamous settings, it highlights companionship, family life, and household unity. In polygamous settings, such as Elkanah's household, the plural form identifies multiple women married to one husband, setting up narratives that focus on domestic conflict and spiritual lessons.

4. Theological Implications

While Old Testament narratives record instances of men taking multiple wives without always stating explicit condemnation in the text, Scripture consistently shows the troubling results of departing from God's creation plan for marriage. Monogamy was established in Eden, whereas taking multiple wives regularly produced rivalry, favoritism, and grief (as seen in the families of Jacob, David, and Solomon).

In 1 Samuel 1:2, the mention of two wives establishes the broken human situation into which God brought His redemption. Hannah's barrenness and Peninnah's harsh behavior caused deep emotional pain, but God used this distress to lead Hannah into heartfelt prayer. Out of this troubled home, God granted Hannah a son, Samuel, who would help lead Israel back to spiritual devotion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:2, the plural word translated "wives" identifies Hannah and Peninnah as both belonging to Elkanah. The passage immediately contrasts their situations: Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children.

Having two wives created a bitter environment inside the home. Peninnah used her ability to bear children to provoke Hannah year after year, especially when the family traveled to worship at the tabernacle in Shiloh. The reference to the two wives sets up the key human conflict at the beginning of 1 Samuel, showing how Hannah's quiet suffering inside her family led to a prayer that changed Israel's history.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The grammatical form of the target occurrence in 1 Samuel 1:2 is plural and masculine. In Hebrew grammar, this specific noun uses an irregular masculine plural ending to express the plural concept of multiple wives or women.

The story highlights how God works through human difficulty. Even though Elkanah loved Hannah, his decision to take two wives caused continuous pain in his household. God used Hannah's distress within this family structure to draw her to Him in total trust, demonstrating that divine mercy can bring national blessing out of personal heartbreak.
