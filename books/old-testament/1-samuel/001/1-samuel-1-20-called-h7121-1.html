CALLED - (qârâʼ, Strong's H7121)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qara means to call out, cry aloud, summon, proclaim, or assign a name to a person or place. In 1 Samuel 1:20, the word describes Hannah naming her newborn son Samuel as a deliberate testimony to answered prayer, recording her gratitude to the LORD who heard her silent cries in Shiloh.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The opening chapter of 1 Samuel takes place during a dark period in Israel's history near the end of the era of the judges. Israel lacked faithful political leadership, and the priesthood at Shiloh suffered under moral corruption. Against this gloomy national background, the story begins with a personal family tragedy: Hannah's prolonged barrenness and the ongoing taunts she endured from her husband's second wife, Peninnah.

In deep distress, Hannah went to the tabernacle at Shiloh to pray. Rather than complaining or seeking revenge, she poured out her heart to the LORD, asking for a male child and promising to dedicate him entirely to divine service. When God answered her prayer, she conceived and gave birth to a son. In ancient Israelite culture, naming a child was often a deeply spiritual act that commemorated God's intervention, expressed faith, or recorded the circumstances of the child's birth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb qara is a fundamental Hebrew word used across many everyday and formal settings. It carries several primary shades of meaning:

  • To name or designate: Assigning a specific name or title to a person, place, or object.

  • To cry out or invoke: Shouting loudly in distress, or calling upon God in prayer and worship.

  • To summon or invite: Calling someone to come to a location or inviting guests to a feast.

  • To proclaim or read aloud: Announcing a public gathering, declaring a message, or reading sacred texts to a congregation.

In 1 Samuel 1:20, qara refers specifically to bestowing a personal name upon a child ("called his name Samuel").

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, qara appears in a wide variety of narrative, prophetic, and poetic settings. When God is the subject who names something, the word highlights divine authority and sovereign order, such as when God names the light Day and the darkness Night. When a parent names a child, the word marks a key transition in narrative history, often capturing the mother or father's spiritual reflection on what God has done.

When used for worship, qara describes believers calling upon the name of the LORD in prayer, public praise, or sacrifice. When used in social or official contexts, it describes issuing an invitation or calling people together for a holy assembly.

In 1 Samuel 1:20, the grammar presents the verb in a feminine singular form with a narrative past sequence (wayyiqtol). This highlights Hannah carrying out the action of giving her son his name immediately following his birth.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s act of naming her son connects human petition to divine action. She explicitly explained the name Samuel by stating, "Because I have asked for him from the LORD." The Hebrew name Shemu'el sounds like the phrase for "heard by God" or "asked of God."

By using qara to assign this name, Hannah created a permanent testimony to God's covenant attentiveness. Every time Samuel’s name was spoken throughout his life—whether as a boy serving in Shiloh, as a prophet, or as a judge over Israel—it served as a public reminder that God hears the cries of the humble and answers prayer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The use of qara in 1 Samuel 1:20 forms a striking contrast with Hannah's earlier prayer in 1 Samuel 1:13. In that earlier verse, Hannah was praying silently in her heart; her lips were moving, but her voice was not heard, leading Eli the priest to mistake her for a drunken woman. Her private grief was voiced without sound.

When God answered her petition, Hannah’s silence was broken by a public declaration. By naming her child, she openly declared what God had done for her. The mother takes the initiative here in bestowing the name, reflecting her direct, personal experience of divine mercy. The verb moves the narrative from silent sorrow to joyous fulfillment, setting the stage for Hannah to return to Shiloh and fulfill her vow.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:5 — "God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night."

  • Genesis 22:14 — "And Abraham called the name of the place, The-LORD-Will-Provide..."

  • Psalms 3:4 — "I cried to the LORD with my voice, and He heard me from His holy hill."

  • Hosea 11:2 — "As they called them, so they went from them..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Old Testament narratives, mothers frequently played a central role in naming children during pivotal spiritual moments. Examples include Eve naming Seth, Rachel naming her sons, and Hannah naming Samuel. These naming moments served as domestic altars—enduring testimonies woven directly into everyday family life.

There is also a intentional wordplay in 1 Samuel between the verb sha'al (to ask or request) and the name Shemu'el (Samuel). Later in the same chapter, Hannah tells Eli, "For this child I prayed, and the LORD has granted me my petition which I asked (sha'al) of Him" (1 Samuel 1:27). The act of naming the child preserves this connection for all future readers.
