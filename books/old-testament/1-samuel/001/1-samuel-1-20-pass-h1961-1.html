PASS - (hâyâh, Strong's H1961)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb hayah means "to be," "to exist," "to become," or "to come to pass." In biblical narrative, it often introduces a new development in the story, marking the moment when an event actually takes place. In 1 Samuel 1:20, the word marks the fulfillment of Hannah's long period of waiting and prayer, signaling that in God's timing, the promised son was conceived and born.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The opening chapter of 1 Samuel records a time of great spiritual and national transition in Israel. Eli was serving as priest at Shiloh, but the spiritual life of the nation was weak. In the midst of this challenging era, the text focuses on an intimate family dynamic: Hannah was childless, enduring years of grief and provocation.

During an annual visit to Shiloh, Hannah poured out her soul in quiet, weeping prayer to the LORD, promising that if God gave her a male child, she would dedicate him to divine service for his entire life. The priest Eli initially misunderstood her distress, but upon learning her heart, he offered a blessing: "Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition which you have asked of Him." Hannah departed with peace in her heart, believing God had heard her. The word hayah appears in verse 20 as the narrative turns from Hannah's prayer to the concrete reality of its answer.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb hayah is the primary Hebrew word used to express existence and occurrence. It can refer to:

  • Being or existing as a state of reality.

  • Becoming something new or changing into a new state.

  • Happening, taking place, or coming to pass in history.

When hayah opens a sentence in historical narrative, it often functions as an introductory phrase—frequently rendered "it came to pass" or "it happened." This usage does not change the core lexical meaning of the verb ("to happen" or "to be"), but it serves an important structural role in biblical storytelling by moving the plot forward and introducing a major historical event.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, hayah appears in a variety of contexts. It describes the simple presence of something, the occurrence of an event, or the transformation of a person or situation over time.

In narrative literature, the phrase "it came to pass" marks a shift from background dialogue or waiting into active historical reality. It tells the reader that what was previously spoken, promised, or anticipated is now taking place in real time. Rather than describing abstract ideas, the word anchors spiritual hopes to real calendar moments in human history.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of this passage does not come from expanding the definition of hayah itself, but from how the word functions in the flow of redemptive history.

Hannah’s story demonstrates that God's purposes unfold within actual human history. Hannah did not remain indefinitely in a state of unfulfilled longing; her personal pain moved toward a specific resolution ordained by God. The phrase underscores that God works through normal human chronology—through months and seasons—to bring about His redemptive plans. Samuel’s birth was not merely a personal comfort to Hannah; it was a pivotal turn in the history of Israel, setting the stage for the rise of the monarchy and the eventual line of David.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:20, hayah is rendered in English as part of the phrase "it came to pass." Grammatically, the verb appears in the masculine singular, imperfect form with a wayyiqtol construction. This specific grammatical form functions in Hebrew as the main engine for driving narrative action forward from one event to the next.

The text states: "So it came to pass in the process of time that Hannah conceived and bore a son..." The Hebrew phrase translated "in the process of time" literally refers to the revolution or cycle of days—the natural period required for human gestation and birth. The verb hayah links Hannah's faithful prayer at Shiloh to the arrival of her baby months later. The use of the word here highlights the transition from Hannah's private grief to public joy, demonstrating that God had remembered her petition.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:2 — "The earth was without form, and void; and darkness was on the face of the deep."

  • Genesis 37:23 — "So it came to pass, when Joseph had come to his brothers, that they stripped Joseph of his tunic..."

  • 1 Samuel 13:10 — "Now it happened, as soon as he had finished presenting the burnt offering, that Samuel came..."

  • 1 Kings 18:17 — "Then it happened, when Ahab saw Elijah, that Ahab said to him, 'Is that you, O troubler of Israel?'"

  • Jeremiah 3:16 — "'Then it shall come to pass, when you are multiplied and increased in the land in those days,' says the LORD..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

For believers waiting on answers to prayer, the narrative structure of 1 Samuel 1 offers profound comfort. Hannah spent years in sorrow, but the introduction of hayah in verse 20 marks the exact moment her period of waiting concluded. It reminds readers that God's work is not confined to the realm of spiritual promises; it arrives in concrete, visible history. The God who hears silent prayers in Shiloh is the God who brings about tangible answers in the fullness of time.
