ALL - (kôl, Strong's H3605)

Summary:

The Hebrew word kol is a widespread noun indicating totality, translated as "all," "the whole," "every," or "entirety." Depending on how it is constructed with surrounding words, it can describe every member of a group or the entire composition of a single unit. In 1 Samuel 1:21, kol describes Elkanah traveling to Shiloh with "all his house." The word highlights the corporate participation of Elkanah's entire household in covenant worship, establishing the background of family devotion against which Hannah's temporary remaining behind to care for infant Samuel is understood.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word kol comes from an ancient Hebrew root denoting completeness or total quantity. It serves as the standard Old Testament term for expressing the total extent of a person, group, place, time, or concept.

In the social world of ancient Israel, the basic unit of community and worship was the household (bet). Faith was not maintained merely as an individual matter; family leaders led their households together in covenant duties. In 1 Samuel 1, Elkanah made annual trips to the central sanctuary at Shiloh to offer sacrifices. The mention that "all his house" made this journey demonstrates how family life and religious devotion were bound together in Israelite worship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Kol functions lexically as a noun meaning "totality" or "the whole." In English, it is most frequently translated as "all," "every," "whole," or "entire."

When combined with a singular noun or singular suffix, kol generally means "the whole of" or "the entire." When joined with a plural noun, it usually means "all" or "every one of." Grammatically, in 1 Samuel 1:21, the term attaches to the singular noun "house" with common gender classification, giving it the sense of "the whole of his household." It encompasses everyone belonging to Elkanah's domestic unit—family members and household servants alike—as a single collective whole.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses kol in a variety of ways depending on the scope of the passage:

  • Universal totality: It can describe absolute completeness, such as the whole creation or all humanity.

  • Contextual totality: In narrative texts, it frequently means the sum total of a specific group within a defined context rather than an unrestricted global count.

  • Undivided devotion: When paired with terms like "heart" or "soul," it points to complete, wholehearted commitment.

  • Corporate solidarity: When applied to a clan, tribe, or nation, it stresses that the entire group shares in a common action, privilege, or obligation.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of kol in relation to the household reflects God's design for corporate worship. God revealed Himself not only to isolated individuals, but to families and communities called to walk with Him together.

When Elkanah gathered his household to offer sacrifices at Shiloh, he acted as a covenant head bringing his entire family before God. This illustrates the principle that devotion to the LORD is meant to encompass the whole life of a family, shaping every member of the home under shared worship and obedience.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

1 Samuel 1 establishes the birth and dedication of Samuel during a period of spiritual transition in Israel. The chapter contrasts Elkanah's regular household worship with Hannah's personal grief over her childlessness and her silent, desperate prayer to the LORD.

After God answers Hannah's prayer and she gives birth to Samuel, Elkanah prepares for his regular journey to Shiloh. Verse 21 notes that "all his house" went up to perform the annual sacrifice and fulfill his vow. This highlights the standard practice of the entire family worshipping together.

Against this backdrop of the whole household traveling, Hannah requests to stay behind until the child is weaned so she can present him permanently to the LORD (1 Sam 1:22). The emphasis on "all his house" going up clarifies that Hannah's temporary absence from the trip was not spiritual neglect or family division. Rather, it was a purposeful, devotion-driven decision fully supported by her husband to prepare Samuel for his lifelong service to God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 9:19 — "whole" — "These three were the sons of Noah, and from these the whole earth was populated."

  • 1 Samuel 4:5 — "all" — "And when the ark of the covenant of the LORD came into the camp, all Israel shouted so loudly that the earth shook."

  • 1 Samuel 25:16 — "all" — "They were a wall to us both by night and day, all the while we were with them keeping the sheep."

  • 1 Kings 19:18 — "all" — "Yet I have reserved seven thousand in Israel, all whose knees have not bowed to Baal, and every mouth that has not kissed him."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding kol as a contextual totality prevents readers from confusing corporate expressions with modern individualistic assumptions. In 1 Samuel 1:21, "all his house" describes the complete unit going up together, while the surrounding narrative explicitly accounts for Hannah remaining behind for a specific season. The word highlights the baseline of household solidarity while the narrative explains the godly exception.
