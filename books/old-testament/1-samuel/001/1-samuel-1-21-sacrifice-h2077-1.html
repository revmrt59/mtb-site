SACRIFICE - (zebach, Strong's H2077)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun zebach refers to a slain animal offered in worship, often serving as the basis for a sacred communal meal shared among the worshipers. In 1 Samuel 1:21, the word describes the regular annual sacrifice offered by Elkanah at Shiloh, highlighting his household's faithful, steady routine of worship into which God moved to fulfill His divine plans.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, sacrificial worship was the central outward expression of devotion to God. The noun zebach comes from a root verb that means to slaughter or slay an animal for sacrifice. Ancient worship contained several distinct types of offerings. While some sacrifices were burned entirely on the altar to signify complete dedication, a zebach involved dividing the slain animal.

A portion of the fat and blood was offered on the altar to the Lord, specific portions were given to the officiating priest, and the remaining meat was returned to the worshiper. The worshiper and his family would then eat this meat together near the sanctuary in a sacred meal. Consequently, zebach was closely tied to family gatherings, covenant celebrations, joy, and holy fellowship in God's presence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word zebach can refer to:

  • A slain animal sacrifice offered to God.

  • A peace or fellowship offering where meat is consumed in a sacred feast.

  • A sacrificial banquet or religious feast shared among covenant partners or family members.

In 1 Samuel 1:21, the noun is singular and common in gender. It identifies the specific, designated annual offering that Elkanah brought to the tabernacle at Shiloh as an act of obedience and devotion.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses zebach across a wide variety of historical and religious settings. Unlike the 'olah (burnt offering), which was completely consumed by fire to express total consecration or atonement, zebach emphasizes communion and fellowship. Eating in the ancient world was a profound pledge of peace and relationship; thus, sharing a zebach before God signified that the worshiper was at peace with the Lord and with fellow believers.

The word appears in contexts involving annual family pilgrimages, covenant ratification ceremonies, national thanksgiving celebrations, and the personal fulfillment of vows. Because the meat had to be eaten within a specified holy timeframe, these sacrifices were cheerful, feast-like occasions that brought households together before God.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of zebach demonstrates that God desires real, relational communion with His people. Worship in Israel was not merely an abstract mental activity; it was an embodied, shared experience centered on God's altar.

At the same time, Scripture repeatedly warns against treating zebach as a magical ritual or external obligation. God does not need the meat of animals, nor is He pleased by sacrifices offered without sincere faith, repentance, and obedience. The sacrificial meal was meant to express an existing heart of trust and covenant fidelity, pointing forward to the ultimate peace established between God and His people through Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:21, Elkanah travels to Shiloh with his entire household to offer "the yearly sacrifice and his vow." This occurrence comes immediately after God answered Hannah's desperate prayer for a child. While Hannah remained home in Ramah to care for infant Samuel until he was weaning, Elkanah maintained the family's consistent spiritual schedule.

The phrase "yearly sacrifice" points to Elkanah's ongoing habit of worship. During the period of the Judges, Israel experienced widespread moral decay and spiritual compromise, even among the priesthood at Shiloh. Yet Elkanah’s regular presentation of the zebach shows that an ordinary household could maintain genuine devotion to God despite surrounding corruption. The addition of "his vow" reveals that Elkanah was also personally confirming and honoring the spiritual commitments made by his family, aligning his household's devotion with the prescribed worship of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 31:54 – "Then Jacob offered a sacrifice on the mountain, and called his brethren to eat bread."

  • 1 Samuel 15:22 – "Has the LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the LORD?"

  • Psalms 51:17 – "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit, a broken and a contrite heart—these, O God, You will not despise."

  • Proverbs 17:1 – "Better is a dry morsel with quietness, than a house full of feasting with strife."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The translation of zebach as "feasting" in Proverbs 17:1 illustrates how central the shared meal was to the definition of this sacrifice. In that proverb, a house "full of sacrifices" means a home filled with abundant meat from sacrificial celebrations, pointing out that even religious feasts lose their value when accompanied by family division.

Elkanah’s annual zebach in 1 Samuel 1:21 serves as the quiet background for one of the greatest turning points in Israel's history. God brought about the birth of Samuel—the prophet who would anoint King David—not through a dramatic political movement, but within the ordinary, faithful rhythms of a family traveling year after year to offer their zebach at the sanctuary.
