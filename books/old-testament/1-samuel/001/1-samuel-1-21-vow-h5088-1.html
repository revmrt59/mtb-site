VOW - (neder, Strong's H5088)

Summary:

The word neder refers to a solemn promise or pledge made to God, or to the sacred gift or sacrifice brought in fulfillment of that promise. In 1 Samuel 1:21, the word describes the personal offering that Elkanah traveled to Shiloh to perform alongside his family's annual worship. His careful attention to fulfilling his vow highlights the genuine piety of Samuel's household and shows that personal promises made to God were to be faithfully kept in public worship.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israelite worship, a vow was a voluntary obligation made to God. A person would promise to bring a specific sacrifice, dedicate a gift, or perform a service if God granted a request or protected them through a difficult time. Once made, a vow became a binding commitment to be fulfilled at God's central sanctuary.

The setting of 1 Samuel 1 takes place at Shiloh, where the tabernacle and the ark of the covenant were located before the temple was built in Jerusalem. Pious Israelites made annual pilgrimages to Shiloh during festival seasons to offer appointed sacrifices and present their voluntary offerings, including vows made during the preceding year.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun neder refers to a vow, a solemn pledge made to God, or the votive sacrifice offered to fulfill that promise.

The word can mean:

  • A spoken promise made to God, committing oneself to a specific action or offering.

  • The actual sacrifice or gift brought to the sanctuary to fulfill that promise.

Making a vow was entirely voluntary under Israelite law. No one was required to make one, but once spoken aloud to the LORD, the pledge was sacred and required faithful performance.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture treats the vow as a serious matter of personal integrity before God. When an Israelite faced a crisis, illness, or severe distress, they often called upon God in prayer and made a vow to give Him praise, sacrifices, or dedicated service when He answered.

A neder was often associated with peace offerings and festive meals at the tabernacle. Fulfilling a vow was not a private or secret matter; it was performed at the sanctuary so that family, friends, and the worshipping community could celebrate God's goodness together. The law instructed that vows should not be delayed or neglected, reminding worshippers that God takes spoken words seriously.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical teaching on vows emphasizes that God is active, listening, and worthy of total trust. When believers make promises to God in times of need, those promises reflect confidence that He hears prayer.

Fulfilling a vow demonstrates gratitude and integrity. It safeguards worship from becoming hollow or transactional. Worship is not merely asking God for help in trouble; it involves keeping one's word when God answers. True faith leads to tangible acts of thanksgiving and obedience.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:21, the text records that Elkanah and his household went up to Shiloh to offer to the LORD "the yearly sacrifice and his vow." Earlier in the chapter, his wife Hannah had made a vow of her own, promising that if God gave her a son, she would dedicate him to the LORD's service for his entire life.

In verse 21, Elkanah travels to Shiloh to present his regular annual sacrifice along with his own personal vow. This detail shows that Elkanah was a God-fearing husband who took worship seriously. He did not treat religion as a routine obligation, but maintained a personal pattern of devotion that included bringing votive offerings to God.

Although Hannah stayed home on this occasion to nurse the infant Samuel until he was weaned, Elkanah fully supported her dedication of the child. His own practice of paying his vows provided a household context of spiritual faithfulness. Samuel was born into a family where promises made to God were honored with joyful obedience.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 28:20 — "Then Jacob made a vow, saying, 'If God will be with me and keep me in this way that I am going... then the LORD shall be my God.'"

  • Leviticus 7:16 — "But if the sacrifice of his offering is a vow or a voluntary offering, it shall be eaten the same day that he offers his sacrifice..."

  • Numbers 30:3 — "If a man makes a vow to the LORD, or takes an oath to bind himself by some agreement, he shall not break his word..."

  • 2 Samuel 15:7 — "Absalom said to the king, 'Please let me go to Hebron and pay the vow which I made to the LORD.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In 1 Samuel 1:21, the word neder appears as a singular noun. Grammatically, this identifies the specific personal vow Elkanah brought on this pilgrimage, distinguishing his personal pledge from the regular community sacrifice required for the feast.

Elkanah's example shows how personal vows and family worship work together. He did not isolate his private obligations from his family's corporate devotion. By bringing his vow to Shiloh along with his household, he integrated his personal gratitude into the shared spiritual life of his family.
