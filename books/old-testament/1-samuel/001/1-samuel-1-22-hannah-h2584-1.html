HANNAH - (Channâh, Strong's H2584)

Summary:

The Hebrew name Channah comes from a root meaning "grace" or "favor." As a proper personal name, it identifies the mother of the prophet Samuel. In 1 Samuel 1:22, the name marks Hannah's active role and quiet resolve as she stays behind during the annual family pilgrimage to wean young Samuel, preparing to fulfill her vow by presenting him permanently for service to the LORD at Shiloh.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The story of Hannah opens during the turbulent era of the judges, a time characterized by spiritual decay and leadership failure in Israel. The sanctuary at Shiloh was led by the aging priest Eli and his corrupt sons. In the midst of this dark national period, the narrative shifts to a small family from the mountains of Ephraim.

Hannah was one of two wives of a man named Elkanah. While Peninnah had children, Hannah suffered from barrenness, which was a source of deep sorrow and public shame in ancient Israelite culture. Driven by grief and provoked by her rival, Hannah brought her desperate ache directly to God in prayer at the tabernacle. Her personal story of pain, prayer, and divine response becomes the catalyst for a monumental shift in Israel's history—the raising up of Samuel as a faithful prophet, judge, and kingmaker.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Channah is a proper feminine personal name in Hebrew. It is derived from the verbal root chanan, which means to show favor, be gracious, or extend mercy. The name itself can be translated as "grace," "favor," or "gracious one."

As a proper noun, the name specifically designates this historical woman in the Old Testament. While Hebrew names frequently carry rich thematic connections to a person's life or character, the word functions fundamentally as her personal identifier rather than as a general abstract noun in the text.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The name Channah occurs exclusively in the opening chapters of 1 Samuel. Her story is structured around a dramatic progression from grief to deliverance:

  • Personal trial: Hannah experiences the pain of barrenness and the misunderstanding of others, including Eli the priest.

  • Earnest petition: She makes a personal vow to God, asking for a male child and promising to dedicate him fully to the LORD's service.

  • Divine answer: God remembers her, and she gives birth to Samuel.

  • Faithfulness to God: She fulfills her promise by presenting the boy at Shiloh once he is weaned.

  • Joyful praise: She offers a prophetic hymn of thanksgiving celebrating God's power to lift up the lowly.

Throughout her narrative, her name is associated with silent, heart-felt prayer, unyielding devotion, and joyful thanksgiving for divine favor.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's story illustrates key truths about God's character and how He works in human history:

  • Divine attention to the humble: God hears the prayers of the afflicted. Hannah’s experience demonstrates that God values the distressed and answers those who seek Him in truth.

  • Grace and surrender: Hannah receives a son as a direct gift of God's favor, but she does not treat the gift as a personal possession to hold tightly. Instead, she offers him back to the LORD for His broader purposes.

  • Preparing national redemption: God often accomplishes major historical works through hidden, personal moments. Hannah's private prayer and maternal faithfulness prepared the way for Samuel, who would guide Israel out of spiritual darkness into the era of the kingdom.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:22, the text states, "But Hannah did not go up, for she said to her husband, 'Not until the child is weaned; then I will take him, that he may appear before the LORD and remain there forever.'"

Here, the narrative uses her name to highlight her personal leadership and clear conviction within the household. Elkanah and the rest of the family went up for the annual festival, but Hannah deliberately chose to stay behind. This choice was not an attempt to delay or avoid her promise to God. In ancient Israel, weaning typically occurred when a child was two or three years old. Hannah intended to give Samuel her undivided care during his earliest years so that when she brought him to the tabernacle, he would be ready to stay permanently under Eli's care.

Her words reveal a mother whose purpose was settled. She accepted full responsibility for her vow and acted with wisdom, patience, and unwavering faith to execute it completely.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Cultural context of weaning: In the ancient Near East, weaning was a major developmental milestone. By waiting until Samuel was fully weaned, Hannah ensured he was physically independent enough to reside at the tabernacle complex.

  • Connection to her song of praise: The themes of grace and favor embedded in Hannah's name echo strongly in her song in 1 Samuel 2. She extols God for reversing human fortunes—filling the hungry, exalting the weak, and granting children to the barren.

  • A model of integrity: In a culture where vows could sometimes be made rashly or quietly forgotten, Hannah stands out as an example of absolute integrity, keeping her solemn word to the LORD even at immense personal cost.
