BEST - (ṭôwb, Strong's H2896)

Summary:

The Hebrew word tob indicates that which is good, fitting, pleasant, beautiful, or beneficial. It describes intrinsic quality, moral excellence, and practical suitability across various settings in Scripture. In 1 Samuel 1:23, the word forms part of an idiom meaning "what seems right or proper to you." Here, Elkanah uses the word to affirm Hannah's judgment, granting her discretion to nurse and care for young Samuel until he is fully weaned before presenting him to the Lord at Shiloh.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The term tob is the primary word for "good" in ancient Hebrew. It spans a wide range of everyday and religious ideas, from physical attractiveness and agreeable speech to moral purity and practical wisdom. In the cultural setting of ancient Israel, family heads exercised authority over vows made within the household. Decisions regarding pilgrimage and religious devotion required harmony between husband and wife.

When Hannah declared her intention to stay behind from the annual family journey to Shiloh until her son Samuel was weaned, Elkanah addressed her decision directly. His response reflects household agreement and confidence in her godly character during a pivotal moment in Israel's history.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word tob describes that which meets a high standard of quality, beauty, or usefulness. Depending on how an author employs it, the word can mean:

  • Moral goodness or righteousness, describing actions that align with God's character.

  • Practical benefit or advantage, describing what is helpful or wise.

  • Physical beauty or fine quality, describing pleasing appearances or superior materials.

  • Personal satisfaction or fittingness, expressing what is agreeable or proper.

When paired with the Hebrew preposition meaning "in the eyes of," tob forms a common expression that means "what seems best, right, or proper" to a particular person. In 1 Samuel 1:23, the word carries this exact sense of sound personal judgment and fitting timing.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, biblical writers use tob to contrast divine order with human confusion. In the opening chapter of Genesis, God repeatedly evaluates His creation as tob ("good"), signifying that it functions exactly according to His holy design.

In personal and social contexts, the word often highlights thoughtful decision-making. When people express that an action is tob in someone's eyes, it indicates approval, permission, or respect for another person's discretion. The word does not merely convey subjective personal preference; it frequently refers to an outcome that produces genuine practical benefit and keeps peace within a community or home.

4. Theological Implications

God is the ultimate standard of what is tob. Human discernment regarding what is "good" or "best" must remain anchored in God's revealed truth. In 1 Samuel 1:23, Elkanah balances human discretion with dependence on God's sovereignty. After telling Hannah to do what seems best to her, he adds, "Only let the LORD establish His word."

This balance shows that practical human planning and divine fulfillment belong together. Believers are invited to exercise wisdom and good judgment in daily affairs while trusting God to bring His promises to completion. Human decisions called tob are truly good when they honor God's character and yield to His supreme will.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1, Hannah experienced deep emotional pain due to her childlessness and made a vow to dedicate her son to the Lord's service at the tabernacle. After God remembered her and Samuel was born, the time came for the family's annual sacrifice at Shiloh. Hannah asked to remain behind until the boy was weaned, ensuring he would be old enough to live under the care of Eli the priest.

Elkanah's reply—"Do what seems best to you"—uses tob to grant her full freedom in timing the boy's weaning. Under Old Testament law, a husband had the legal right to void a vow made by his wife if he disagreed with it. Instead of overruling Hannah or insisting on an immediate journey, Elkanah recognized her maternal wisdom and spiritual devotion. His use of tob affirms her plan as practical, loving, and entirely appropriate for fulfilling her promise to God.

  • Proverbs 16:19 — "Better it is to be of a humble spirit with the lowly, than to divide the spoil with the proud."

  • Jeremiah 6:20 — "For what purpose to Me comes frankincense from Sheba, and sweet cane from a far country? Your burnt offerings are not acceptable, nor your sacrifices sweet to Me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase in 1 Samuel 1:23 highlights a striking contrast with the general moral climate of the period. The book of Judges, which immediately precedes 1 Samuel chronologically, repeatedly describes a dark spiritual era by stating that "everyone did what was right in his own eyes." In that context, human autonomy led to chaos because people defined "good" apart from God's law.

In contrast, Elkanah and Hannah use their personal judgment in submission to the Lord. When Elkanah tells Hannah to do what seems tob to her, it takes place within a home committed to keeping vows made to the God of Israel. Their exercise of personal discretion stands as an example of godly wisdom operating within the boundaries of faith.
