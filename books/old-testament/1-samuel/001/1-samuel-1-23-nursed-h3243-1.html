NURSED - (yânaq, Strong's H3243)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yanaq means to suckle, nurse, or give milk to a child. In 1 Samuel 1:23, the word describes Hannah remaining at home to nurse and nourish her young son Samuel until he was fully weaned, faithful in her duty to prepare him for a lifetime of divine service.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, nursing an infant was a vital and extended process. Children were typically nursed until two or three years of age. During this period, nursing provided basic nutrition, physical protection, and essential care during the most vulnerable years of early childhood.

The first chapter of 1 Samuel opens with Hannah, a woman burdened by years of childlessness. In her distress, she prayed fervently at the tabernacle in Shiloh, asking God for a son and promising to dedicate him to the Lord for his entire life. God answered her prayer, and she gave birth to Samuel. When the time came for the annual family trip to offer sacrifices at Shiloh, Hannah chose to stay home until the child was old enough to remain at the sanctuary under the care of Eli the priest.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of yanaq is to suck or draw milk. When used for a mother or nurse, it means to feed a baby at the breast.

The word centers on natural sustenance and early physical care. It describes the dependent relationship of an infant relying entirely on a mother or nurse for life-sustaining food. While the word usually refers to human mothers and infants, it can also describe animals nursing their young or be used symbolically to describe drawing rich nourishment from a generous source.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, the word appears in both historical accounts and poetic imagery:

  • Infant Care: The word describes mothers or wet-nurses caring for babies, such as Jochebed nursing young Moses or Sarah nursing Isaac.

  • Identification of Children: In lists of vulnerable groups or during community gatherings, the word identifies nursing infants who depend completely on their parents.

  • Prophetic Imagery: Prophets like Isaiah use the term metaphorically to describe God's people feasting on divine comfort and spiritual abundance, receiving nourishment just as a child draws strength from a mother.

4. Theological Implications

The act of nursing highlights God's design for protecting and sustaining human life at its most fragile stage. God works through natural parental care to preserve children and accomplish His broader purposes.

In the narrative of 1 Samuel, Hannah's nursing of Samuel shows that spiritual devotion works alongside practical parental responsibility. She had made a solemn vow to dedicate her son to the Lord, but she recognized that fulfilling that commitment required giving him dedicated care during his infancy. Preparing a child for God's service involves patient, everyday attention to their physical and emotional growth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:23, Elkanah supports his wife's decision, telling her to do what seems best to her and wait until Samuel is weaned. The narrative records that Hannah stayed behind and nursed her son.

The Hebrew verb form (feminine singular imperfect with wayyiqtol) carries the narrative forward, showing Hannah's direct action in response to her husband's agreement. She remained at home, focusing her energy on nurturing her child.

This passage reveals that Hannah did not view child-rearing as a delay to her spiritual pledge, but as the foundation of it. Her decision to stay behind and nurse Samuel ensured he would be physically strong and mature enough to live at the tabernacle in Shiloh when the time arrived.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Near Eastern culture, weaning marked a significant transition in a child's development. Celebrations were often held when a child was successfully weaned, as surviving the early infant years was a major milestone.

Hannah's choice to nurse Samuel until he was fully weaned meant she spent several uninterrupted years raising him in her home. The strong foundation she built during these quiet early years helped prepare Samuel for his future calling as a prophet, priest, and judge over Israel.
