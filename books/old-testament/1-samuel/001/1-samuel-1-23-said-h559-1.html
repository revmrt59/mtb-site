SAID - (ʼâmar, Strong's H559)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb amar means to say, speak, tell, or declare. It is the primary word used across the Old Testament to introduce direct speech, internal thoughts, and authoritative proclamations. In 1 Samuel 1:23, the word records Elkanah's spoken blessing and affirmation to his wife Hannah, approving her decision to stay home until Samuel is weaned and praying for the Lord to fulfill His word.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Hebrew narrative, spoken dialogue moves the action forward and reveals the inner character and motives of the speakers. In 1 Samuel 1, Hannah had suffered years of childlessness and prayed fervently at the tabernacle in Shiloh, promising that if God gave her a son, she would dedicate him to divine service for his entire life. God answered her prayer, and she gave birth to Samuel.

When the time came for the annual family pilgrimage to worship and sacrifice, Hannah asked her husband Elkanah if she could remain at home until the boy was fully weaned, after which she would present him before the Lord. 1 Samuel 1:23 records Elkanah's spoken reply. His word of consent and prayerful agreement affirms his wife's vow and establishes spiritual harmony within the household.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Amar refers to the act of expressing thoughts or commands through speech. Depending on who is speaking and the nature of the setting, the word can mean:

  • To speak or say something directly to another person in ordinary conversation.

  • To command, instruct, or give orders as an authority figure.

  • To declare or proclaim formally before a group or covenant community.

  • To think or reason inwardly, often described in Hebrew as saying something "in one's heart."

In 1 Samuel 1:23, the verb functions in its most direct sense: introducing spoken conversation from a husband to his wife in response to her request.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The verb amar appears throughout every section of Hebrew Scripture, from narrative and law to wisdom literature and prophecy. Its exact shade of meaning is shaped by the speaker and the context:

  • When God speaks using this verb, His word possesses creative power and absolute authority, calling light into existence or establishing covenant promises.

  • When human leaders, kings, or priests speak, the verb introduces decrees, wisdom, or legal directions.

  • In personal household relationships, the verb records requests, blessings, answers, and commitments.

Because the word simple and versatile, the weight of what is spoken depends entirely on the character of the speaker and the message delivered.

4. Theological Implications

Words in Scripture reflect the commitments of the heart and carry spiritual responsibility. In the covenant setting of ancient Israel, family heads held legal authority regarding vows made within the home. A husband could either confirm or invalidate a vow made by his wife.

Elkanah’s spoken answer shows a leader using his words to support godly devotion rather than to obstruct it. Instead of asserting self-centered control, his spoken words yield to Hannah’s vow to the Lord. Furthermore, his statement links human approval with divine action, adding the prayer: "Only let the LORD establish His word."

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:23, amar introduces Elkanah’s response to Hannah: "Do what seems best to you; wait until you have weaned him. Only let the LORD establish His word."

The grammar of the verb presents Elkanah as masculine singular in a narrative past form (imperfect, wayyiqtol), marking his direct action in carrying the account forward. His spoken reply accomplishes two important things:

  • First, he grants full freedom to Hannah ("Do what seems best to you"), endorsing her maternal care and spiritual plan.

  • Second, he redirects the ultimate outcome to God, expressing his desire that the Lord fulfill what He had promised concerning Samuel.

Rather than creating conflict over domestic routine, Elkanah's speech brings peace and aligns the family's actions with God's higher calling for the young child.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:3 — "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light." Here the word introduces God's divine command that calls creation into existence.

  • Deuteronomy 26:18 — "Also today the LORD has proclaimed you to be His special people..." Here the verb is translated "proclaimed," describing God's formal covenant declaration over Israel.

  • 1 Samuel 20:42 — "Then Jonathan said to David, 'Go in peace...'" Here the word introduces a blessing and pledge of covenant faithfulness between friends.

  • Psalms 27:8 — "When You said, 'Seek My face,' My heart said to You, 'Your face, LORD, I will seek.'" Here the word describes both divine invitation and the worshipper's responsive pledge.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Earlier in 1 Samuel 1, Hannah prayed silently before the Lord at Shiloh—her lips moved, but her voice was not heard aloud. That silent prayer resulted in the gift of Samuel. By contrast, in verse 23, Elkanah’s words are spoken aloud in clear conversation.

This contrast shows how private inner prayer before God eventually bears outward fruit in visible, spoken agreement within the community and family. Spoken words of faith turn private commitments into shared worship and obedience.
