SEEMS - (ʻayin, Strong's H5869)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun 'ayin primarily refers to the physical eye, the organ of sight. By natural extension, ancient Hebrew frequently uses the word idiomatically to describe perception, evaluation, or judgment—how a matter appears "in the eyes" of someone. In 1 Samuel 1:23, Elkanah uses this idiom when he tells his wife Hannah to "do what seems best to you" (literally, "do what is good in your eyes"). In this passage, the word forms part of a Hebrew idiom for personal discernment, expressing Elkanah's trust in Hannah's judgment as she cares for young Samuel before fulfilling her vow to dedicate him to the LORD.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Hebrew thought, physical body parts are frequently used to express internal aspects of human life. Just as the heart often represents the mind or will, the eye ('ayin) represents sight, awareness, and judgment. Grammatically, 'ayin is a common noun that appears here in the dual form. In Hebrew, naturally paired body parts—such as eyes, ears, and hands—standardly take the dual grammatical ending.

The historical setting of 1 Samuel 1 involves Hannah's deep grief over her childlessness, her earnest prayer at the tabernacle in Shiloh, and God's gracious answer in giving her a son named Samuel. Hannah had promised that if God gave her a son, she would dedicate him to the LORD's service for his entire life. After Samuel was born, the time came for the annual family pilgrimage to Shiloh. Hannah requested to stay behind until the child was fully weaned, at which point she would present him at the tabernacle. Elkanah's response in verse 23 affirms her plan, granting her freedom to determine the best timing for the child's care.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'ayin has a few basic senses in Old Testament usage:

  • The literal organ of sight (the physical eye).

  • The visual appearance, aspect, or surface color of an object.

  • Perspective, opinion, or evaluation, especially when combined with prepositions and qualifying words (such as "in the eyes of").

When paired with the Hebrew word for "good" (tob) and the preposition "in" (b-), the phrase literally reads "good in the eyes of." English translations render this idiom as "seems best," "seems good," or "pleases." It describes how a situation or proposed action looks to a person's judgment.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses 'ayin in a wide variety of settings to contrast human sight with divine sight. Human beings evaluate situations based on outward appearances—what seems right "in their own eyes." Sometimes this indicates wise personal judgment, but in other contexts, following what is right "in one's own eyes" leads to moral failure when detached from God's commands.

In contrast, God's eyes see all things perfectly. Scripture repeatedly speaks of actions being either good or evil "in the sight [eyes] of the LORD." This establishes God's evaluation as the absolute standard of righteousness. Human appraisal is subjective and limited, whereas God's sight is objective, complete, and holy.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of 'ayin in 1 Samuel 1:23 highlights the relationship between human discernment and divine authority. Elkanah tells Hannah to do what "seems best" to her, trusting her motherly wisdom and spiritual commitment. Yet he immediately adds a prayer: "Only may the LORD establish His word."

This balance shows an important theological principle. God grants believers freedom and responsibility to exercise wisdom and practical judgment in daily life. However, human judgment—what seems good in human eyes—must always remain submitted to God's revealed word and sovereign purpose. Proper spiritual discernment does not operate independently of God; it operates under His blessing and guidance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:23, the phrase containing 'ayin plays a key role in showing domestic harmony and shared faith between Elkanah and Hannah. Under Old Testament regulations regarding household vows, a husband had the authority to confirm or annul a vow made by his wife. Rather than acting harshly or overrule her plans, Elkanah honors Hannah's devotion.

By telling her to do what "seems best" to her regarding the weaning of Samuel, Elkanah expresses confidence in her judgment. He recognizes that she is fully committed to honoring her vow to God. His encouragement shows that wisdom involves recognizing when to grant freedom to others to execute godly plans in the way they judge best, while surrounding those plans with prayer for God's divine guidance.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The dual form of 'ayin reflects the standard Hebrew grammatical pattern for paired body parts. It simply refers to both physical eyes together, forming the natural basis for the idiom of sight and perception.

Elkanah's response provides a helpful model of godly encouragement within a family. He does not treat Hannah's spiritual vow as a distraction or a burden to his household. Instead, he supports her evaluation of the timing while keeping the ultimate focus on God's will: "Only may the LORD establish His word." True spiritual leadership supports practical wisdom while pointing back to God's faithfulness.
