SON - (bên, Strong's H1121)

Summary:

The word ben refers primarily to a male child, son, or direct descendant, though it can also describe youth, membership in a group, or offspring of animals. In 1 Samuel 1:23, the word designates the young child Samuel, whom Hannah nurses during his early infancy before fulfilling her vow to dedicate him permanently to the service of the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, family lineage and offspring were central to life, inheritance, and God's promises to the nation. The word ben is the foundational Hebrew term used to describe immediate offspring, broader male descendants, and family membership. Family identity passed from generation to generation, making a son essential for carrying on the family name, maintaining tribal inheritances, and participating in the religious life of Israel.

When Hannah prayed in Shiloh, her distress stemmed from her inability to bear children. In ancient Near Eastern culture, childlessness carried heavy emotional and social sorrow. God heard her prayer and granted her a male child. The word ben in 1 Samuel 1:23 marks the concrete answer to Hannah's petition and highlights the physical reality of the child God entrusted to her.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meanings of ben across the Hebrew Scriptures include:

  • A literal male offspring or immediate son.

  • A male descendant or grandson within a broader genealogy.

  • Children or descendants in general when used in the plural.

  • A member of a specific group, nation, or character class (such as "sons of Israel" or "sons of the prophets").

  • The young of an animal or an age marker (such as a creature or person being a certain number of years old).

In 1 Samuel 1:23, the word carries its most direct and foundational sense: a mother's immediate male child.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Hebrew writers use ben across various genres to express distinct ideas:

  • Direct Family Relationships: The word commonly indicates the relationship between parents and their male children, emphasizing obedience, inheritance, and discipline.

  • Legal and Lineage Continuity: Genealogies rely heavily on this word to trace family lines, legal rights, and land holdings across generations.

  • Figurative and Character Idioms: Hebrew frequently connects ben with an abstract noun to describe a person's character or condition. For example, a "son of worth" describes a noble person, while a "son of wickedness" describes a treacherous person.

  • Relationship to God: God occasionally refers to the nation of Israel as His "son" to describe His redeeming care, and Israelite kings are spoken of in father-son terms to emphasize their royal office under God's appointment.

4. Theological Implications

The gift of a son in Scripture often connects directly to God's providence and sovereign purposes. Children are presented not as personal achievements or mere biological outcomes, but as heritage and blessings given by God.

Hannah's experience illustrates that every child ultimately belongs to God. Though Hannah prayed fervently for a male child, she recognized that her son was entrusted to her for a divine purpose. Her willingness to nurse her son during his infant years while preparing to relinquish him into the service of the Lord demonstrates how parental care and complete devotion to God work together.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:23, Elkanah responds to Hannah's decision to remain at home until the boy is weaned. He tells her to do what seems best to her and prays that the Lord will establish His word. The text then states that she stayed and nursed her son until she had weaned him.

The use of ben here draws attention to the intimacy and duty of Hannah's early motherhood. She did not rush her child away prematurely, nor did she go back on her vow. Instead, she spent those crucial early years caring directly for her son, preparing him for the lifelong devotion that lay ahead at Shiloh. The presence of the word underscores the physical reality of the child who would grow up to become Israel's great prophet and judge.

7. Other Relevant Insights

From a grammatical standpoint in 1 Samuel 1:23, the word is singular in number and common in gender, pointing specifically to the individual infant Samuel.

In ancient Israel, weaning typically occurred around two to three years of age. During this period, a mother provided both physical nourishment and basic early guidance. Hannah's dedicated care during her son's early years formed the foundation for his transition into the tabernacle service under Eli the priest.
