WEANED - (gâmal, Strong's H1580)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb gamal means to bring a process to full completion, to yield ripe fruit, to deal bountifully, or to wean a child from nursing. In 1 Samuel 1:23, the word describes Hannah bringing young Samuel's period of infancy and nursing to its complete conclusion. Elkanah encourages Hannah to fulfill this motherly duty fully before presenting Samuel for lifelong service at the tabernacle, showing that quiet domestic faithfulness serves as the foundation for dedicated service to God.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, weaning a child was a major developmental milestone. Infants were typically nursed for two to three years before being fully transitioned to solid food. Because infant mortality was high in the ancient Near East, reaching the point of weaning was a cause for gratitude and family celebration, marking a child's passage into early childhood.

In the opening chapter of 1 Samuel, Hannah suffers deeply from childlessness and prays fervently at the tabernacle in Shiloh. She vows that if the Lord gives her a son, she will give him back to the Lord for all the days of his life. God answers her prayer, and she gives birth to Samuel. When the time comes for the annual family pilgrimage to Shiloh, Hannah decides to stay home until Samuel is fully weaned. In 1 Samuel 1:23, her husband Elkanah supports her choice, urging her to complete this nurture before presenting Samuel to serve at the house of the Lord.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word gamal carries the basic idea of bringing something to completion or dealing fully with someone. Depending on the context, the word can mean:

  • To bring a child through the nursing stage to full weaning.

  • To deal bountifully with or reward a person.

  • To yield or produce fully developed fruit.

  • To repay or render an action toward another.

In 1 Samuel 1:23, the word means weaning—specifically, completing the entire period of infant nursing so the child is ready for the next stage of life.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses gamal to describe both biological development and personal actions. When applied to young children, it refers to the completion of the nursing period, as seen with Isaac in Genesis, Samuel in 1 Samuel, and Genubath in 1 Kings.

When applied to plants, as with Aaron's rod in the wilderness, it describes a branch bringing forth fully ripe fruit. When applied to personal relationships or divine action, it describes dealing fully or generously with someone, whether through God bestowing goodness on His worshiper or a person repaying another for their actions.

The common thread across these settings is the idea of bringing something to its intended fulfillment or giving what is due in full measure. Weaning is not presented as an act of deprivation, but as the natural completion of an essential stage of growth.

4. Theological Implications

The use of gamal in Hannah's story highlights several biblical truths:

  • God Values Ordinary Nurture: Sacred service to God does not bypass ordinary human development. God called Samuel to be a prophet and judge, yet His plan included years of quiet motherly nursing and care.

  • Orderly Faithfulness in Vows: Hannah had made a sacred vow to surrender Samuel to God, but Elkanah recognized that fulfilling the vow required waiting until the child was properly mature. True devotion to God is orderly, wise, and attentive to life's proper seasons.

  • The Maturity of the Soul: The imagery of a weaned child appears elsewhere in Scripture to represent a soul that has learned calm contentment. Just as a weaned child rests peacefully with its mother without desperately crying for milk, the believer learns to rest quietly in God's care.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:23, Elkanah responds to Hannah's decision to remain at home rather than travel immediately to Shiloh. He says, "Do what seems best to you; wait until you have weaned him. Only let the LORD establish His word."

The target word appears in an infinitive construct form, acting as a direct statement of action ("until your weaning him"). Elkanah affirms that nursing Samuel to full completion is the priority for this season. He does not view her delay in traveling to Shiloh as a failure to keep her vow; rather, he sees it as the necessary preparation for fulfilling it rightly.

Hannah then remains at home nursing Samuel until the process is complete. By giving Samuel the full care he required as an infant, Hannah prepared him physically and emotionally to be left at Shiloh under the care of Eli the priest. Contextually, the word emphasizes that devotion to God honors the practical duties of everyday life.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 21:8 – "So the child grew and was weaned. And Abraham made a great feast on the same day that Isaac was weaned."

  • Numbers 17:8 – "Now it came to pass on the next day that Moses went into the tabernacle of witness, and behold, the rod of Aaron, for the house of Levi, had sprouted and put forth buds, had produced blossoms and yielded ripe almonds."

  • Psalms 13:6 – "I will sing to the LORD, because He has dealt bountifully with me."

  • Psalms 116:7 – "Return to your rest, O my soul, for the LORD has dealt bountifully with you."

  • Psalms 131:2 – "Surely I have calmed and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with his mother; like a weaned child is my soul within me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • A Feast of Celebration: In biblical culture, weaning was often celebrated with a feast, as Abraham did for Isaac. This demonstrates that moving from one stage of life to the next was viewed as an answer to prayer and a sign of divine blessing.

  • Hannah's Spiritual Clarity: Hannah knew that surrendering Samuel to the tabernacle required that he be old enough to eat solid food and function apart from continuous nursing. Her choice to stay behind shows spiritual wisdom combined with motherly tenderness.
