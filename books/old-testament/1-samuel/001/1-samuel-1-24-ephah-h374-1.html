EPHAH - (ʼêyphâh, Strong's H374)

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ēpâ refers to a standard dry unit of volume used in ancient Israel for measuring grain, barley, and flour. It served as a common measurement for household food supplies, commercial trade, and grain offerings brought to the Lord's sanctuary. In 1 Samuel 1:24, the word refers to the generous quantity of flour Hannah brought to Shiloh alongside three bulls and a skin of wine when she presented her young son Samuel to the Lord, demonstrating her wholehearted gratitude and commitment to fulfilling her vow.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israelite society, daily life and worship depended on clear, recognizable units of measurement. The ephah was the standard dry measure used across the land to quantify commodities such as barley, wheat, and fine flour. Whether measuring harvested crops, distributing rations, conducting fair trade in the market, or preparing sacrifices for worship at the sanctuary, the ephah provided a consistent standard of volume.

In the opening chapter of 1 Samuel, Hannah prayed in deep sorrow for a child, promising that if the Lord granted her a son, she would dedicate him to God's service for his entire life. After the Lord answered her prayer and the child Samuel was weaned, Hannah made the journey from Ramah to the house of the Lord at Shiloh. She did not come empty-handed. To accompany the presentation of her child and the fulfillment of her vow, she brought sacrificial animals, wine, and an ephah of flour.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'ēpâ designates a specific unit of dry volume. In Israelite weights and measures, one ephah equaled one-tenth of a homer (or ten omers). In modern equivalent terms, an ephah is roughly equal to five or six gallons (about 22 liters), representing a significant volume of dry goods.

The word is used in three main settings:

  • Sacrificial Worship: It designates the required quantity of fine flour used for grain offerings presented alongside animal sacrifices or as individual offerings prescribed by God's law.

  • Daily Life and Domestic Needs: It describes substantial amounts of grain harvested, milled, or transported for family sustenance.

  • Economic Trade: It refers to the standard container or measure used in commercial transactions, where honesty was strictly required by God's law.

Here in 1 Samuel 1:24, the word functions in its basic concrete sense, identifying the specific amount of grain Hannah brought to Shiloh for the sacrificial feast and offering.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, the ephah connects the material world of labor and agriculture with worship and ethics.

In the worship of Israel, grain offerings frequently accompanied animal sacrifices. The law specified exact fractions or whole measures of flour depending on the occasion. By setting precise standards for sacrifices, God showed that worship required order, care, and intentionality.

In daily life, an ephah represented a bounty of grain. When Ruth gathered an ephah of barley after a single day of gleaning in the field of Boaz, it signaled extraordinary generosity and divine provision. Similarly, when Jesse sent David to the army camp with an ephah of dried grain, it served as a substantial food supply for his brothers.

In legal and wisdom texts, the ephah carries strong ethical associations. Merchants were forbidden to use a deceptive "differing ephah"—using a larger container when buying and a smaller container when selling. God demanded absolute honesty in the marketplace, placing commercial integrity under divine scrutiny.

4. Theological Implications

The use of the ephah in Scripture highlights several basic truths about God and His people:

  • True Worship Involves Tangible Devotion: Israel’s covenant relationship with God was expressed through physical offerings drawn from their daily labor. Giving flour from the harvest acknowledged that all provision comes from God's hand.

  • God Values Generosity Over Bare Compliance: Sacrificial worship was meant to reflect the worshipper's heart. Bringing a full measure of grain demonstrated joy, gratitude, and reverence for God's goodness.

  • Faithfulness Integrates Sacred and Secular Life: God cares about how grain is measured at the altar and in the marketplace. Honesty in everyday commerce reflects reverence for the God of truth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:24, the grammatical form is a singular feminine noun, specifying one complete unit of this standard dry measure.

The presence of "one ephah of flour" in Hannah's offering holds clear contextual significance. According to the sacrificial laws of Israel, a single bull sacrifice typically required a meal offering of three-tenths of an ephah of fine flour. Hannah brought three bulls and a full ephah of flour. An entire ephah was more than enough to accompany the sacrifices she offered that day.

This generous provision shows that Hannah did not approach the sanctuary with reluctance or minimal effort. Having received the miraculous gift of a son in answer to her prayers, she returned to Shiloh with lavish thanksgiving. She gave back to God the child she cherished most, supporting that lifelong dedication with a full, ungrudging offering of praise and sacrifice.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 19:36 – In establishing standards for community righteousness, God commands Israel to maintain "an honest ephah," showing that economic fair dealing is rooted in His holy character.

  • Ruth 2:17 – Ruth gleans in the field of Boaz until evening and gathers about an "ephah" of barley, illustrating God's abundant provision through Boaz's protection.

  • 1 Samuel 17:17 – Jesse directs young David to take an "ephah" of dried grain to his brothers at the battle line, demonstrating how this dry measure supplied practical daily nourishment.

  • Proverbs 20:10 – The proverb warns that deceptive "measures" are an abomination to the Lord, stressing that God demands integrity in trade.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's generous offering of an ephah of flour stands in quiet contrast to the corruption that soon appears in the narrative. In 1 Samuel 2, the sons of Eli treat the offerings brought to Shiloh with greed and contempt. While the priests failed in their duties, Hannah demonstrated true heart-piety, bringing her full measure of flour with joy to honor the Lord who heard her prayer.
