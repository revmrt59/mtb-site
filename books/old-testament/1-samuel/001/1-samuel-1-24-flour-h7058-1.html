FLOUR - (qemach, Strong's H7058)

Summary:

The Hebrew word qemach refers to flour or ground meal made from cereal grains such as wheat or barley. It describes standard ground grain used for daily domestic baking, hospitality, emergency sustenance, and sacrificial offerings. In 1 Samuel 1:24, qemach identifies the grain offering that Hannah brought to Shiloh alongside animal sacrifices and wine when presenting young Samuel to the LORD. Her gift of flour expressed deep devotion and generous gratitude as she fulfilled her vow to dedicate her long-awaited son to God's service.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, ground grain was a primary staple of daily life. Milling grain into flour was a constant domestic activity, usually performed by women who crushed wheat or barley between stones. Flour was used to bake daily bread, prepare meals for honored guests, and provision communities during times of peace and national gathering.

In addition to its everyday domestic role, flour held an important place in Israelite worship. Grain offerings accompanied animal sacrifices during sacred feasts, vows, and dedicated acts of worship. In these sanctuary settings, presenting flour acknowledged God as the source of physical life and expressed thanksgiving for His ongoing harvest blessings.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word qemach refers to flour or ground meal. It denotes the ordinary product obtained from milling grain like wheat or barley. While Hebrew contains another word specifically reserved for fine, highly refined flour (solet), qemach describes standard flour or meal used in everyday household baking and general worship offerings.

Depending on the passage, the word can describe flour used for feeding unexpected visitors, preparing bread for sacrificial meals, sustaining households during famine, or supplying large assemblies during royal celebrations.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Scripture, qemach appears in several distinct contexts:

  • Daily Household Life and Hospitality: The word describes flour kneaded quickly to prepare fresh bread for visitors, reflecting ancient customs of hospitality.

  • Sacrificial Offerings: The word appears in worship settings where grain was offered to God alongside meat or wine, such as Hannah's dedication offering at Shiloh or grain offerings in the Mosaic law.

  • Divine Provision and Miracles: The word is featured in historical accounts of prophetic ministry, such as the widow of Zarephath whose remaining flour was miraculously sustained during a drought, and Elisha purifying a toxic pot of stew.

  • Provisions for Assemblies: The word denotes bulk agricultural supplies brought to feed gathered crowds and support national celebrations.

4. Theological Implications

Although qemach is an ordinary agricultural term, its role in biblical worship highlights key spiritual truths. In the sacrificial system, bringing flour represented offering the basic fruit of human labor and daily sustenance back to God. An animal sacrifice represented atonement and the surrender of life, while the grain offering acknowledged that daily physical existence depends on God's ongoing providence.

Furthermore, God's miraculous multiplication of flour during drought demonstrated His sovereignty over creation and His faithful care for those who trust Him. Presenting flour in worship demonstrated that true worship involves honoring God with the practical, daily blessings He has provided.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:24, Hannah travels to the sanctuary at Shiloh after weaning her young son Samuel. Having prayed for years in deep distress because of her barrenness, she received Samuel as God's direct answer to her prayer. She returned to Shiloh to fulfill her solemn vow by dedicating Samuel to lifelong service at the tabernacle.

Hannah does not arrive empty-handed. She brings three bulls, a skin of wine, and an ephah of flour (qemach). An ephah was a substantial agricultural measure—equivalent to several gallons of flour—representing a very generous grain offering for a single family. Grammatically, the noun appears here in the singular number and common gender, specifying this single measured amount of flour brought for the sacred occasion.

Her generous gift of flour accompanied the consecration of her child. It shows that Hannah did not give begrudgingly or offer a bare minimum. She accompanied her ultimate sacrifice—surrendering her long-awaited son—with a lavish expression of thanksgiving through standard worship materials. The flour served as the grain component of her worship, celebrating God's gracious provision of both a son and daily life.

  • Judges 6:19 — "Then Gideon went in and prepared a young goat, and unleavened bread from an ephah of flour; the meat he put in a basket, and he put the broth in a pot; and he brought them out to him under the terebinth tree and presented them."

  • 1 Kings 17:12 — "So she said, 'As the LORD your God lives, I do not have bread, only a handful of flour in a bin, and a little oil in a jar; and see, I am gathering a couple of sticks, that I may go in and prepare it for myself and my son, that we may eat it, and die.'"

  • 1 Chronicles 12:40 — "Moreover those who were near to them, from as far away as Issachar and Zebulun and Naphtali, were bringing food on donkeys and camels, on mules and oxen—provisions of flour and cakes of figs and clusters of raisins, wine and oil and oxen and sheep abundantly, for there was joy in Israel."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Israelite worship, grain offerings composed of flour frequently accompanied burnt offerings and peace offerings. By bringing flour alongside animals and wine, Hannah followed the established worship pattern for a major vow offering and peace offering.

Her presentation of an ephah of flour highlights her household's wholehearted devotion. Preparing an ephah of flour required significant labor and agricultural resources. This detail underscores that Hannah's act of bringing Samuel to Shiloh was a joyful, solemn act of worship supported by her entire household.
