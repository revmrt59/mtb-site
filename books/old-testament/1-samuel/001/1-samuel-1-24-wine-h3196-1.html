WINE - (yayin, Strong's H3196)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun yayin refers to wine, the fermented beverage produced from grapes. Throughout the Old Testament, it describes a common drink of daily life, a symbol of joy and festivity, a potential snare when overconsumed, and a liquid offering presented in formal worship. In 1 Samuel 1:24, yayin refers to a skin of wine brought by Hannah as a sacred drink offering to accompany her sacrifices when presenting her young son, Samuel, to the Lord at Shiloh. The inclusion of wine highlights her deep gratitude, joyous dedication, and full commitment as she faithfully fulfills her vow.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The term yayin is the primary Hebrew word for wine made from grapes. In ancient Israel, viticulture was a central part of agricultural life. Grapes were harvested, pressed in stone vats, and allowed to ferment into wine. Stored in clay jars or leather skins, wine served as a basic beverage, a practical way to preserve the fruit of the vineyard, and a standard element of host hospitality and celebratory feasting.

Beyond its role in everyday life, wine had an established place in Israel’s worship system. Under the Mosaic Law, drink offerings of wine accompanied various burnt offerings and grain offerings presented at the sanctuary. In 1 Samuel 1:24, Hannah travels to the tabernacle at Shiloh to fulfill the vow she made while praying for a child. Having weaned young Samuel, she brings three bulls, an ephah of flour, and a leather skin of wine (yayin) to present her son to God's service for his entire life.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word refers to fermented grape juice. Depending on who is using it and the setting in which it appears, yayin can mean:

  • A regular beverage consumed during meals, hospitality, and public celebrations.

  • A sacred liquid poured out as a drink offering on the altar before God.

  • An intoxicating drink capable of producing drunkenness, loss of self-control, or moral failure if abused.

In 1 Samuel 1:24, the word refers specifically to a sacrificial drink offering brought alongside animal and grain sacrifices to mark a solemn and joyous occasion of worship and dedication.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture speaks of yayin across several distinct settings:

  • Creation Gift and Hospitality: Wine is portrayed as a good gift from God that gladdens the human heart and accompanies physical nourishment, fellowship, and celebration.

  • Sacrificial Worship: In tabernacle and temple rituals, a prescribed measure of wine was poured out before the Lord as a drink offering. Poured out completely at the base or side of the altar, it represented total devotion and thanksgiving.

  • Moral Warning: Wisdom literature and the prophets repeatedly warn against excessive drinking. In these contexts, wine represents a danger that can impair judgment, lead to foolish behavior, and dull spiritual awareness.

  • Prophetic Symbolism: The prophets use wine imagery in two distinct directions—as a picture of divine judgment (drinking the cup of God's wrath) or as a symbol of God's coming kingdom blessing and abundant restoration.

4. Theological Implications

In the setting of worship, bringing wine alongside animal sacrifices and flour signifies complete and generous devotion. A drink offering of wine was not kept or consumed by the worshiper at the altar; it was poured out entirely to God. This physical act demonstrated that the worshiper held nothing back from the Lord.

Hannah’s provision of wine reveals the heart with which she approached God. Giving up her long-awaited son to live at Shiloh was a life-altering sacrifice, yet her spirit was not bitter or reluctant. Bringing a substantial quantity of wine alongside valuable livestock and grain shows that her dedication was characterized by abundance, gladness, and profound gratitude for God’s faithfulness.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The presence of yayin in 1 Samuel 1:24 creates a meaningful contrast with the earlier events of the chapter. In 1 Samuel 1:13–15, while Hannah was praying in deep emotional anguish for a child, Eli the priest saw her lips moving without sound and falsely assumed she was drunk with wine (yayin). Hannah defended herself, clarifying that she had drunk no wine, but was pouring out her soul before the Lord.

When Hannah returns to Shiloh in verse 24, yayin appears again—this time not as a false accusation, but as an actual, holy offering. The woman once wrongly accused of drunkenness now brings a skin of wine to pour out as a sacrifice of praise. Her gift of wine accompanies the ultimate fulfillment of her promise: yielding her son Samuel to the Lord for a lifetime of service.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 23:13 – Describes the drink offering of "wine" required to accompany the grain offering as a sweet aroma to the Lord.

  • Numbers 15:10 – Commands the priest to bring a calculated measure of "wine" as a drink offering for a burnt offering.

  • 1 Samuel 25:18 – Mentions two skins of "wine" among the generous provisions Abigail quickly gathered to bring to David and his men.

  • Proverbs 9:5 – Presents Wisdom inviting the simple to eat her bread and drink the "wine" she has prepared.

  • Jeremiah 35:6 – Highlights the obedience of the Rechabites, who faithfully refused to drink "wine" according to the command of their ancestor.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Leather Skins: In ancient Israel, wine was frequently carried and stored in leather containers (nebel). A skin of wine held a noticeable volume, making Hannah’s contribution a substantial and valuable provision for the sanctuary offerings.

  • Pouring Out: A subtle structural theme connects Hannah's story. In her distress, she poured out her soul to God in prayer; in her answered joy, she brought wine to be poured out on God's altar. True spiritual devotion moves from pouring out one's heart in prayer to pouring out one's resources in joyful service.

  • Generous Worship: Hannah did not bring the minimum required by law. The combination of three bulls, a large quantity of flour, and a full skin of wine reflects a lavish expression of praise that matches the greatness of God's kindness to her.
