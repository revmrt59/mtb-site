BULL - (par, Strong's H6499)

par ( par , Strong’s H6499 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word par refers to a young male bull or steer. In the Old Testament, it describes a valuable agricultural animal often brought as a major sacrificial offering in Israel's worship. In 1 Samuel 1:25, the word identifies the animal slaughtered at the tabernacle in Shiloh as part of the worship ceremony when Hannah and Elkanah presented young Samuel for dedicated service to the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the agrarian economy of ancient Israel, cattle were among the most valuable assets a household could own. A young male bull represented substantial physical strength, agricultural productivity, and livestock value. Because keeping a bull to maturity required significant pasture, time, and resources, these animals were prized possessions. In the Old Testament narrative and worship instructions, the presence of a young bull signals an occasion of importance, whether in agrarian life, national leadership, or sanctuary worship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word par refers to a young bull, steer, or young ox. It specifies a male bovine that has grown past early calfhood into the prime of its vigor. The term identifies the animal itself by species, sex, and stage of maturity. It does not inherent spiritual qualities; rather, it names a specific livestock animal that was widely recognized for its size, strength, and agricultural worth.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament narratives and ritual laws, par functions as a standard term for a young male bovine. It appears frequently in the regulatory texts of Leviticus and Numbers, where God gives instructions for sanctuary sacrifices. Young bulls were designated for solemn religious occasions, including the ordination of priests, sin offerings on behalf of the whole congregation, and major festival burnt offerings. The noun itself remains concrete and descriptive, identifying the creature being presented. The religious significance comes from the divine instructions and ritual setting in which the animal was offered, rather than from the vocabulary word alone.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of young bulls in worship highlights key truths about Israel's sacrificial system. Bringing a young bull meant offering a livestock animal of high material value. In the Old Testament law, major offerings for corporate sin or priest consecration required a bull, showing that formal worship and covenant atonement involved substantial, visible surrender before God. These sacrifices demonstrated the gravity of approaching a holy God and foreshadowed the ultimate, complete sacrifice of Jesus Christ. At the same time, Scripture repeatedly emphasizes that God was not interested in the physical animal for its own sake. As later biblical writers made clear, an offering of a bull pleased God only when accompanied by heart obedience, faith, and genuine praise.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:25, the word describes the animal slaughtered at the sanctuary in Shiloh just before Hannah presents her young son Samuel to Eli the priest. Hannah had prayed in deep distress for a child, promising to dedicate him to the Lord for all his life. After God answered her prayer and the child was weaned, she and her husband brought Samuel to Shiloh along with provisions and livestock for sacrifice.

The text notes that "they slaughtered a bull, and brought the child to Eli." The slaughter of the young bull served as the worship sacrifice that accompanied the presentation of the child. It anchored Hannah's commitment in the established worship procedures of the sanctuary. The physical offering of the bull was completed at the altar, opening the way for the lifelong dedication of Samuel to God's service.

  • Judges 6:26 — "and build an altar to the Lord your God on top of this rock in the proper manner, and take the second bull and offer a burnt sacrifice with the wood of the wooden image which you shall cut down."

  • 1 Kings 18:26 — "So they took the bull which was given them, and they prepared it, and called on the name of Baal from morning even till noon, saying, 'O Baal, hear us!'"

  • Psalms 69:31 — "This also shall please the Lord better than an ox or bull which has horns and hooves."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The grammar of 1 Samuel 1:25 presents the word par in the singular, designating one specific bull slaughtered at this moment in the narrative. This act of sacrifice highlights the connection between formal ritual worship and personal vows in Israel's life. While the bull was slain and consumed on the altar as a burnt offering, the greater gift remaining at the sanctuary was Samuel himself. The sacrifice of the animal was a temporary act of worship, but it marked the beginning of a lifelong gift of service that would shape the history of Israel.
