SLAUGHTERED - (shâchaṭ, Strong's H7819)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shachat means to slaughter or slay, referring specifically to cutting the throat of an animal or human. In the biblical text, it serves as the standard technical word for ritually slaying sacrificial animals at the tabernacle or temple. In 1 Samuel 1:25, the word describes the sacrificial slaying of a bull at Shiloh, which accompanied Hannah and Elkanah's formal presentation of young Samuel to Eli for lifelong service to the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, worship centered around the sanctuary, where God established specific sacrificial procedures for His people to draw near to Him. The verb shachat describes the primary physical act that initiated most animal sacrifices: the slitting of the throat to slay the victim cleanly so its blood could be gathered and used according to divine instructions.

While other Hebrew verbs describe general killing, execution, or butchering, shachat carries a specific connection to ritual slaughter. Within the tabernacle and temple worship, this action was not an arbitrary act of violence, but a prescribed worship step performed at the entrance of the tent of meeting or near the altar.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shachat can refer to:

  • Slaying an animal for ritual sacrifice in divine worship.

  • Slaughtering livestock for human consumption or large gatherings.

  • Executing or killing humans in military contexts, judicial executions, or acts of violence.

When God commands worship in the Law of Moses, the word designates the decisive moment an offering is slain before its blood is sprinkled by the priests. When used outside worship contexts, it describes the swift killing or slaughter of animals or people.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, the word appears heavily in the books of Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers, establishing its role as the primary sacrificial term. In these legal passages, the worshiper or priest performs the act of slaughter before the Lord, highlighting the requirement of shed blood to make atonement or present a gift to God.

In historical narratives, such as Judges, Samuel, and Kings, the word shifts between formal sanctuary offerings and civil or military slaughter. Prophets like Isaiah also use the term when addressing the hypocrisy of people who perform the outward physical act of slaughtering sacrifices while their hearts remain far from God's righteous standards.

4. Theological Implications

The act of slaughtering a sacrifice carries significant biblical meaning:

  • Blood and Life: Scripture teaches that the life of the flesh is in the blood. The act of slaughtering an animal represents the surrendering of life, pointing to the reality that approaching a holy God requires a life given up.

  • Substitution and Approach: Sacrificial slaughter provided a way for Israel to draw near to God. The death of the offering served as a substitute or a formal gift, clearing the way for worshipers to stand in God's presence.

  • Covenant Obligation: Bringing an animal to be slaughtered was an expressive act of faith and costly obedience. It demonstrated that the worshiper acknowledged God's lordship over their possessions and lives.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:25, Hannah and her husband Elkanah have traveled to the sanctuary at Shiloh after young Samuel has been weaned. Hannah had previously prayed in deep anguish for a child, promising that if God gave her a son, she would dedicate him to the Lord for all the days of his life.

The narrative action uses a plural imperfect form with sequential narrative action (wayyiqtol), indicating that the worshipers acted together in bringing and offering the sacrifice. Before Hannah speaks to the high priest Eli and surrenders her young son to his care, the text records that "they slaughtered a bull."

This detail shows that Hannah's dedication of Samuel was grounded in formal covenant worship. She did not merely drop off her child; she and her husband brought substantial offerings, including a bull for sacrifice. The slaughter of the bull marked the sacrifice that accompanied their vow fulfillment, showing that dedicating Samuel to God was an act of worship, gratitude, and devotion.

  • Exodus 12:21 — "Then Moses called for all the elders of Israel and said to them, 'Pick out and take lambs for yourselves according to your families, and kill the Passover lamb.'"

  • 1 Samuel 14:34 — "Then Saul said, 'Disperse yourselves among the people, and say to them, "Bring me every man's ox and every man's sheep, slaughter them here, and eat; and do not sin against the LORD by eating with the blood."' So every one of the people brought his ox with him that night, and slaughtered it there."

  • 2 Chronicles 35:6 — "So slaughter the Passover offerings, sanctify yourselves, and prepare them for your brethren, that they may do according to the word of the LORD by the hand of Moses."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The presentation of Samuel in 1 Samuel 1 provides a striking contrast to the corrupt priestly practices introduced in chapter 2. While Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, would later treat the Lord's sacrifices with contempt by demanding raw meat before the fat was burned, Hannah and Elkanah approached Shiloh with reverence, carefully completing the proper slaughter of the offering before handing over their child. Their reverent obedience stands out against the spiritual decline of the period of the judges.
