PRAYING - (pâlal, Strong's H6419)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb palal means to pray, make entreaty, intercede, or petition God. In some biblical contexts it carries the sense of acting as a mediator, judging, or intervening in a difficult matter, but most frequently it describes bringing one's heart, needs, and requests directly before the LORD. In 1 Samuel 1:26, Hannah uses this word as she identifies herself to Eli the priest, reminding him that she is the very woman who previously stood beside him pouring out her heart to God. Her reference to "praying" connects her past secret anguish to the visible reality of God's faithful answer—the child Samuel standing before them.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The book of 1 Samuel opens during a spiritually dark era in Israel's history, the period of the judges. Elkanah and his two wives, Hannah and Peninnah, traveled yearly to the tabernacle at Shiloh to worship and sacrifice. Peninnah had children, but Hannah was childless because the LORD had closed her womb. Year after year, Peninnah provoked Hannah bitterly, driving her to deep grief and tears.

During one visit to Shiloh, Hannah went into the tabernacle in sheer heartbreak. While Eli the priest sat by the doorpost, Hannah wept bitterly and made a vow to God, promising that if He gave her a male child, she would give him back to the LORD for all the days of his life. Eli saw her lips moving without sound and initially mistook her fervour for drunkenness. After Hannah explained her deep anguish, Eli dismissed her with a blessing. God remembered Hannah, she conceived, and gave birth to Samuel. Once the boy was weaned, Hannah returned to Shiloh to fulfill her vow, standing before Eli once again to present her young son.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of palal is to entreat, intercede, or pray to God. When a person is the subject and God is the object, the verb describes appealing to the LORD for mercy, help, deliverance, or divine intervention.

In its underlying root idea, palal can refer to making a legal assessment, judging, or intervening to set things right. For instance, in rare settings it describes human arbitration or decisive action to halt a plague. However, when used of a person speaking to God, it carries the sense of bringing an explicit plea before the supreme Judge and heavenly Father, asking Him to act on behalf of the needy or helpless.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses palal across many historical contexts and literary forms:

  • Personal Supplication: Individuals use the word when crying out to God in moments of personal crisis, barrenness, sickness, or severe distress.

  • Intercession by Leaders: Prophets, kings, and priests use palal to intercede on behalf of others or the entire nation. Abraham, Moses, Samuel, Hezekiah, and Nehemiah frequently pray for the people using this word.

  • Temple Dedication and Public Worship: The term appears prominently during public gatherings, such as King Solomon's prayer dedicating the temple, where he asked God to hear the prayers offered toward His holy place.

The word emphasizes active appeal to God. It is not mere quiet reflection, but an intentional turning toward the LORD to make a request or confess a need.

4. Theological Implications

The use of palal highlights several foundational truths about God and prayer:

  • God Hears the Afflicted: Hannah had no political power or social leverage, yet her plea reached the King of Kings. God takes notice of the brokenhearted who appeal to Him.

  • Prayer Brings Human Need to Divine Sovereignty: Crying out in prayer acknowledges that human effort alone cannot produce life, victory, or salvation. God must intervene.

  • Intercession Changes Circumstances: Throughout the Old Testament, the intercessory prayers of God's servants turn away wrath, heal sickness, and grant life where there was barrenness.

  • Vows and Answered Prayer: Prayer involves both asking and responding faithfully when God answers. Hannah did not keep the gift for herself; she returned to give praise and fulfill her promise.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:26, Hannah says, "O my lord! As your soul lives, my lord, I am the woman who stood by you here, praying to the LORD."

The word appears here in its infinitive construct form ("praying"), linking her past action of persistent, heartfelt pleading directly to her present posture of thanksgiving. When Eli first saw her in 1 Samuel 1:12–13, her voice was unheard, and he judged her falsely. Now, Hannah stands in the exact same spot, but the silent burden has been replaced by a living testimony.

By declaring that she was the woman "praying to the LORD," Hannah anchors her child's origin firmly in divine grace. Samuel was not simply born of natural processes; he was granted directly by God in answer to persistent prayer. Her reminder to Eli establishes that the God who hears in secret fulfills His promises openly.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 1:10 — "And she was in bitterness of soul, and prayed to the LORD and wept anguish."

  • 1 Kings 8:29 — "That Your eyes may be open toward this temple night and day... to hear the prayer which Your servant makes toward this place."

  • Job 42:10 — "And the LORD restored Job's losses when he prayed for his friends."

  • Psalms 106:30 — "Then Phinehas stood up and intervened, and the plague was stopped."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah’s words in 1 Samuel 1:26 set the stage for her famous song of praise in 1 Samuel 2:1–10. Her act of praying in chapter 1 opens a doorway to grand prophetic praise in chapter 2, where she celebrates God raising the poor from the dust and exalting the helpless. Her personal experience of answered prayer becomes a model of how God acts on behalf of all who humble themselves before Him.
