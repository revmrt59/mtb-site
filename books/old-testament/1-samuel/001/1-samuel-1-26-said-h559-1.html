SAID - (ʼâmar, Strong's H559)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb amar means to say, speak, tell, declare, or command. It is the most common word in the Old Testament for verbal speech, ranging from ordinary everyday conversation to solemn royal decrees and divine declarations. In 1 Samuel 1:26, the word marks the moment Hannah addresses the high priest Eli to identify herself as the woman who once prayed in tears at the tabernacle, declaring that God has granted her request for a child.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root amar is foundational to Hebrew narrative. Ancient Hebrew storytelling relies heavily on direct dialogue to reveal character, intent, and covenant commitments. Rather than describing internal feelings in abstract terms, Hebrew writers show what people think and believe by recording what they say.

In the setting of 1 Samuel 1, Israel was living through a period of spiritual lowliness and personal heartbreak. Hannah, grieved by childlessness and provoked by her rival, had stood near the tabernacle doorpost pouring out her soul in quiet, desperate prayer. Eli the priest had initially mistaken her silent, moving lips for drunkenness. Years later, after the Lord answered her prayer and gave her a son, Hannah returned to Shiloh. The verb amar introduces her spoken declaration to Eli as she presents young Samuel to be dedicated to the service of the Lord.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb amar describes the act of uttering words. Depending on who is speaking and the nature of the statement, it can mean:

  • To speak or say something in conversation.

  • To tell, report, or communicate information to another person.

  • To command, direct, or declare with authority.

  • To say to oneself, describing internal thought or intention.

In 1 Samuel 1:26, the word carries the direct sense of address: Hannah spoke to Eli, initiating a conversation that connects her past trial with her present thanksgiving.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Scripture, amar appears in a wide variety of contexts:

  • Divine Speech: When God speaks, His word carries creative power and authority. God speaks creation into existence and communicates His law to His people.

  • Human Dialogue: In human relationships, the word introduces ordinary speech, requests, advice, and testimony.

  • Internal Thought: Hebrew often expresses internal reasoning as "saying in one's heart."

Grammatically, the form used in 1 Samuel 1:26 is a feminine singular imperfect verb with a sequential prefix (wayyiqtol). This form advances the narrative action, showing that after Hannah and her family completed their sacrifice, she stepped forward and addressed Eli directly.

4. Theological Implications

While amar itself is a simple everyday verb for speaking, its placement in 1 Samuel 1 carries deep theological meaning:

  • God Hears and Answers: Hannah’s speech in verse 26 stands in bright contrast to her silent prayer in chapter 1. Earlier, her voice was not heard; now she speaks aloud in witness to God’s faithfulness.

  • Vows and Integrity: Hannah’s words to Eli show her fulfillment of a solemn promise. She acknowledged that the child in her arms was the answer to her prayer, and she openly brought him to the Lord as promised.

  • The Power of Testimony: By speaking directly to Eli, Hannah reminded the priest of God's capacity to transform silent grief into joyful praise. Her vocal declaration honored God publically in the sanctuary.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:26, Hannah’s words begin with a respectful oath: "O my lord! As your soul lives, my lord, I am the woman who stood by you here, praying to the LORD."

This moment completes a dramatic narrative arc. In chapter 1, Eli had misjudged Hannah, suspecting her of drunkenness because her lips moved without sound. Now, Hannah uses her voice openly. She does not come to boast or accuse Eli of his prior mistake; instead, she comes to testify. The verb amar frames her initiative to ensure the priest recognizes God’s work. Her spoken witness anchors the dedication of Samuel in the reality of answered prayer.

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is worth noting how speech functions throughout the early chapters of 1 Samuel. Israel was in a period when "the word of the LORD was rare" (1 Samuel 3:1). Spiritual apathy had dulled the leadership of Eli’s house. In this atmosphere of silence and spiritual decay, it is a faithful, once-barren woman whose clear voice breaks through at the sanctuary. Hannah’s spoken testimony in 1 Samuel 1:26 prepares the way for her prophetic prayer of praise in chapter 2, setting the tone for God's renewed speech through the young prophet Samuel.
