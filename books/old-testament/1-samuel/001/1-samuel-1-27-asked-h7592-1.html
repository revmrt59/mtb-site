ASKED - (shâʼal, Strong's H7592)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb sha'al means to ask, request, petition, or inquire. It describes the act of seeking something from another person or turning to God in prayer to ask for His help, guidance, or favor. In 1 Samuel 1:27, Hannah uses sha'al to testify that her young child Samuel is the direct answer to her heart's request to God. The word highlights her humble, specific petition before the Lord and sets up her joyful decision to dedicate the child back to His service.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, asking was a normal part of daily life and religious devotion. The word sha'al describes a range of human interactions, from asking a simple question or requesting a daily favor to making an earnest appeal to a king or seeking guidance from God.

When applied to worship and prayer, sha'al highlights human need and dependence. Israel was encouraged to bring specific petitions to the Lord, seeking His direction before going to battle or asking for personal mercy in times of distress.

In the opening chapter of 1 Samuel, Hannah experiences deep emotional grief because of her barrenness and the constant provocation of her rival, Peninnah. During an annual family pilgrimage to the tabernacle at Shiloh, Hannah presents her heart to Yahweh in quiet, tearful prayer, asking Him for a male child.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of sha'al is to ask, request, inquire, or demand. Its exact sense depends on who is asking and what is being sought:

  • When a person asks another person, it can mean asking for information, requesting permission, or asking for a gift.

  • When a person asks God, it refers to making a prayer petition or asking for divine direction.

  • When used for military or political decisions, it means inquiring of the Lord through a prophet, priest, or prayer to discover God's will.

  • In certain contexts, the word can also carry the sense of borrowing or asking for loan of an item.

In 1 Samuel 1:27, the word refers directly to Hannah's personal prayer petition offered to the Lord.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, sha'al appears in several distinct settings:

  • Interpersonal requests: It describes people asking questions about health, family, or travel, as well as making formal requests of rulers or neighbors.

  • Inquiring of God: Leaders like David frequently used sha'al when seeking military instructions from the Lord, asking whether to attack or hold back.

  • Prayer petitions: Individuals used the word when asking God for physical provision, deliverance from enemies, or personal blessings.

In 1 Samuel 1:27, the word appears in a singular, common gender, perfect form ("I asked"). The perfect form presents Hannah's petition as a completed action in her past. She looks back to her desperate moment of prayer at the tabernacle and connects that past request directly to the healthy child now standing beside her.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's use of sha'al highlights several key truths about biblical prayer:

  • God hears personal sorrow: Prayer is not a mechanical ritual. God invites His people to express genuine, specific grief and desire before Him.

  • Answers to prayer demonstrate God's grace: Hannah recognizes that her child is not merely the result of natural circumstance, but a direct gift granted by the God who hears.

  • Stewardship of answered petitions: When God grants a request, the recipient is called to view the answer through the lens of devotion. Hannah requested a child from God, and once the request was fulfilled, she gladly surrendered the child back to the Lord's service.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The use of sha'al in 1 Samuel 1:27 forms the emotional and thematic peak of Hannah's story. Earlier in the chapter, the priest Eli saw her lips moving without sound and initially mistook her for a drunk woman. Upon learning of her deep sorrow, Eli blessed her, asking that God grant the petition she had asked of Him (1 Samuel 1:17).

When Hannah returns to Shiloh years later, she brings the young boy Samuel to Eli and uses the exact same word: "For this child I prayed, and the LORD has granted me my petition which I asked of Him."

There is also a meaningful wordplay in the Hebrew text. The name Samuel (Shemu'el) sounds similar to phrases meaning "heard by God," and in verse 28, Hannah states that she has "given" or "lent" (sha'ul, a form directly derived from sha'al) the child to the Lord. The child who was asked from God is now dedicated to God for his entire life.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 1:17 — "asked" — Eli speaks peace to Hannah, praying that God will grant the petition she has "asked" of Him.

  • 1 Kings 3:13 — "asked" — God grants Solomon wisdom and promises to give him riches and honor that he had not "asked" for.

  • 2 Samuel 5:23 — "inquired" — Before facing his enemies in battle, David "inquired" of the Lord to receive divine guidance.

  • Joshua 15:18 — "ask" — Achsah urges Othniel to "ask" her father Caleb for a fertile field as a blessing.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's prayer journey provides a powerful picture of spiritual transformation. She began in bitterness and weeping, bringing her request (sha'al) to God in private agonizing prayer. She ended in joy and thanksgiving, presenting the answer to her prayer back to the Lord in public worship. This shows that true biblical asking is grounded in faith, looking beyond the gift itself to honor the God who gives it.
