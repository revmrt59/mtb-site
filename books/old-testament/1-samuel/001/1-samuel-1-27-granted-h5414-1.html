GRANTED - (nâthan, Strong's H5414)

Summary:

The basic meaning of the Hebrew verb natan is to give, grant, bestow, or place. It is a fundamental Old Testament word used for handing over physical objects, assigning authority, or bestowing gifts and favors. In 1 Samuel 1:27, the word describes God's direct answer to Hannah's prayer by granting her request for a son. By choosing this word, Hannah joyfully testifies that her child Samuel was received directly as a personal gift from the LORD.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

sequence

The opening chapter of 1 Samuel introduces Hannah, a woman bearing the heavy burden of barrenness and sorrow. Year after year, she endured ridicule and heartache, yet she took her grief directly to God in silent, earnest prayer at the tabernacle in Shiloh.

When Eli the priest saw her praying, he pronounced a blessing over her, asking God to fulfill her petition. God heard her plea and gave her a son, whom she named Samuel. Once the child was weaned, Hannah brought Samuel back to Shiloh to present him to Eli and dedicate him to the LORD's service. In 1 Samuel 1:27, Hannah uses the verb natan to declare that the LORD personally granted the very request she had poured out before Him.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning of natan is to give, grant, bestow, or place. Depending on the subject and setting, the word can refer to:

  • Giving a gift or offering to another person.

  • Placing or setting an object in a physical location.

  • Assigning authority, position, or responsibility.

  • Granting a petition or request in response to an appeal.

When God is the subject of natan, the verb emphasizes His sovereign generosity and His active care for people. In 1 Samuel 1:27, the word means that God graciously granted Hannah's specific request, giving the gift of a child to a woman who had been unable to conceive.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses natan in narrative, law, poetry, and prophecy. It can describe a ruler issuing a decree, a landowner granting property, or a worshiper bringing an offering.

The word takes its exact nuance from the surrounding sentence. When paired with a petition or prayer, natan highlights the fulfillment of a desire that lay completely outside the speaker's ability to accomplish. Grammatically, the verb appears here as a masculine singular imperfect form with wayyiqtol, which carries the story forward by stating the completed result: God acted, and the grant was accomplished. Hannah highlights that her child was not the product of human chance, but the clear result of God giving what was asked.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s declaration using natan brings forward several enduring spiritual truths:

  • God is the giver of life: Human effort could not alter Hannah’s circumstances. God alone holds the power to bestow life.

  • God responds to earnest prayer: By granting her request, God demonstrated that He listens to the quiet grief and prayers of His people.

  • Divine gifts call for faithful stewardship: Because God gave Samuel to Hannah, she recognized that her child ultimately belonged to the LORD. God's generosity invites a heart of grateful dedication.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:27, Hannah stands before Eli holding her young son. She reminds Eli of her former visit when she prayed in deep pain, saying, "For this child I prayed, and the LORD has granted me my petition which I asked of Him."

The verb natan bridges Hannah's past sorrow with her present praise. The text contrasts her earlier tears with her current testimony. By emphasizing that the LORD granted her prayer, Hannah directs all glory to God's mercy rather than her own persistence. This act of giving also sets up the next verse, where Hannah dedicated Samuel back to the LORD for his entire life. Because God was the gracious Giver, she held the gift with open hands.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's experience illustrates how recognizing God as the giver reshapes a person's perspective. In ancient Israel, childlessness carried severe social and personal hardship. Hannah did not allow her hardship to turn into bitterness; instead, she brought her need to God. When God answered her with natan, her heart moved from sorrow to song. Her prayer of praise in 1 Samuel 2 becomes a foundation for Biblical worship, celebrating how God lifts up the needy and satisfies the humble.
