LENT - (shâʼal, Strong's H7592)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb sha'al fundamentally means to ask, request, inquire, or petition. In specific contexts involving requests, it can also mean to grant, deliver, or yield something up in response to a petition. In 1 Samuel 1:28, where English translations often render it as "lent," Hannah presents her young son Samuel to the LORD. Her statement is not a temporary loan, but an act of complete dedication. Having asked God for a child, she now hands that child over entirely to God's service for the rest of his life.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The book of 1 Samuel opens during a dark period in Israel's history. The priest Eli and his wicked sons were serving at the tabernacle in Shiloh, and the nation lacked faithful spiritual leadership. In the midst of this spiritual drought, a woman named Hannah suffered deeply because she was childless and provoked by her rival. Hannah brought her grief directly to the LORD in prayer, asking Him to remember her and grant her a male child. She promised that if God granted her request, she would yield the child back to Him for His service all the days of his life.

When God answered her prayer and gave her a son, she named him Samuel. After the child was weaned, Hannah made the journey back to the sanctuary at Shiloh to fulfill her vow. She brought Samuel to Eli and declared that she was yielding him over to the LORD permanently.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of sha'al is to ask, inquire, request, or petition. People use it when asking questions, seeking guidance, or requesting items or favors from someone else. It is used when an individual asks a favor of a neighbor, when a king asks counsel from God, or when a worshiper brings a petition before the LORD.

In certain settings, the word shifts from the act of asking to the act of responding to a request. In these instances, it means to grant what was requested, to hand over upon petition, or to yield something to another. While older English Bibles often use the word "lent," the action here does not imply a temporary arrangement expecting a return. Instead, it describes giving someone or something over completely because a request was made and answered.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Scripture, sha'al appears in several distinct settings:

  • Seeking information or guidance: A person inquires about a situation, asks for directions, or seeks divine direction before going into battle.

  • Making a personal request: An individual asks another person for assistance, food, or a gift.

  • Petitioning God: A worshiper brings a specific need or desire before the LORD in prayer.

  • Granting or surrendering upon request: A giver yields what was requested back to the one who asked or into dedicated service.

The connection in Hannah's story depends on these shades of meaning. Hannah asked (sha'al) God for a son, God granted her petition, and then she gave (sha'al) him back to the LORD. Her response matches the answer she received.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s surrender of Samuel illustrates essential truths about God's gifts and human stewardship. First, everything God gives remains ultimately His. Hannah recognized that the child born to her was a direct answer to prayer, given by God's grace. Rather than holding tightly to the gift, she recognized God's ultimate ownership over Samuel's life.

Second, true devotion fulfills its promises to God. Hannah did not view her vow as a temporary obligation to be bargained away once her sorrow was relieved. Her total surrender of Samuel shows that worship involves tangible sacrifice and wholehearted trust. Giving Samuel to God meant releasing her daily control over his future, trusting that God would care for him and use him for His holy purposes.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:28, Hannah uses the verb sha'al twice in close succession. She states that she has "lent" (yielded) Samuel to the LORD, and that as long as he lives he shall be "lent" (yielded) to the LORD.

The English word "lent" can easily mislead readers into thinking Hannah intended to take Samuel back later, like loaning an item to a neighbor. But the surrounding context makes her meaning clear. She explicitly states the duration: "as long as he lives." Samuel's surrender to tabernacle service was lifelong and irreversible.

Hannah's words reflect a deliberate contrast and fulfillment. Throughout chapter 1, the narrative repeats this verbal root: Eli promised God would grant what she asked; Hannah named the child Samuel because she asked him of the LORD; Hannah reminded Eli that she prayed and received what she asked; and finally, Hannah gave him back to the LORD. By using the same word for giving that was used for asking, the passage highlights a complete circle of grace and surrender. God gave what Hannah requested, and Hannah yielded back what God provided.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The repetition of sha'al in 1 Samuel 1 also connects to the broader narrative of the book. Samuel's name is closely tied to the concept of being heard or requested from God. Later in the narrative, the verbal root connects conceptually with King Saul, whose name Sha'ul means "asked for."

The story in 1 Samuel contrasts Hannah’s humble asking and faithful surrendering with the nation's later demanding of a human king. Hannah asked God in personal trust and yielded the child back to Him; Israel later insisted on a king out of a desire to be like the surrounding nations.
