WORSHIPED - (shâchâh, Strong's H7812)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shachah means to bow down, prostrate oneself, or lie low on the ground as a physical demonstration of respect, submission, and honor. When directed toward God, it describes the low posture of heart and body in true reverence and worship. In 1 Samuel 1:28, the word marks the act of reverence at Shiloh as Hannah presents young Samuel to the LORD. It shows that yielding what is most precious back to God is ultimately an act of joyful worship and surrender.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Near Eastern culture, bowing flat before a superior was a common physical gesture. A person would lower their head or lie prostrate to show total honor, acknowledge authority, and request favor. In Israel's worship, this outward action expressed inward awe before the majesty and holiness of God.

The physical act of lowering oneself to the dirt reminded the worshipper of human weakness and mortality in contrast to God's supreme exaltation. Worship in the Old Testament was rarely an abstract mental exercise; it involved the entire person, including bodily posture.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word shachah can mean to bow down, prostrate oneself, pay homage, or worship.

When a person is the subject and another human is the object, the word describes showing honor, deference, or royal allegiance to a king, elder, or benefactor. When God is the object, the word means bowing before Him in reverence, honoring His divine authority, and offering true worship. The term combines the outward action of lowliness with the inner attitude of complete submission to the one receiving the homage.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, shachah appears in a variety of settings:

  • Civil and Social Honor: People bow low to show respect to kings, leaders, or benefactors during formal meetings or desperate appeals.

  • Pagan Idolatry: Scripture repeatedly forbids bowing down to idols or false gods, reserving ultimate allegiance for the true God alone.

  • Covenant Worship: God's people bow down when encountering His presence, hearing His word, receiving His promises, or offering sacrifices at the sanctuary.

The word demonstrates that true worship involves an intentional surrender of independence, placing oneself beneath the authority of the one being worshipped.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical practice of bowing down highlights key truths about God and humanity. Bowing before God acknowledges His absolute sovereignty, holiness, and ownership of all creation. It puts the worshipper in the proper position—recognizing that human beings are creatures dependent upon their Creator.

Worship in Scripture is closely tied to surrender. When believers bow before God, they yield their plans, desires, and possessions to His service. True worship acknowledges that every good gift comes from God's hand and ultimately belongs to Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:28, Hannah brings her young son Samuel to the tabernacle at Shiloh. After years of painful barrenness and fervent prayer, God answered her by giving her a son. In fulfillment of her vow, Hannah returns Samuel to the LORD to serve Him at the tabernacle for his entire life.

The narrative concludes by stating that he "worshiped" the LORD there. The grammatical form presents a masculine singular action in narrative sequence, marking an immediate response to Hannah's act of dedication. Whether highlighting young Samuel beginning a lifetime of service, Elkanah standing in agreement with his wife's vow, or the priest receiving the child, the verb anchors the entire scene in humble worship. Giving up a long-awaited child could have been viewed as a loss, but the text frames it as a solemn act of bowing before God in gratitude and submission.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 18:2 — Abraham ran to meet three unexpected visitors and "bowed" himself to the ground, showing deep honor and Middle Eastern hospitality.

  • Ruth 2:10 — Ruth fell on her face and "bowed" down to the ground before Boaz, expressing humble gratitude for his grace and protection.

  • 1 Samuel 15:25 — King Saul begged Samuel to pardon his transgression so that he might return and "worship" the LORD.

  • 2 Samuel 15:32 — King David reached the top of the Mount of Olives, a place where he "worshiped" God, even while fleeing in grief from Absalom.

  • Jeremiah 25:6 — God commanded Israel not to pursue other gods or "worship" them, establishing that true homage belongs to God alone.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The act of worship in 1 Samuel 1:28 serves as a fitting foundation for the rest of 1 Samuel. While the house of Eli the priest was falling into spiritual compromise and disrespect for holy things, Samuel’s life begins at Shiloh in an atmosphere of reverence and surrender.

Hannah's devotion demonstrates that genuine worship costs something. By bowing before the LORD and releasing her son into God's service, she shows that holding back nothing from God is the highest form of praise.
