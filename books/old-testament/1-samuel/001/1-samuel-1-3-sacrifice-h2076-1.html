SACRIFICE - (zâbach, Strong's H2076)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb zabach means to slaughter an animal for the purpose of a sacrifice or a ritual meal. Depending on the context, it describes the act of offering an animal to God as an act of worship, covenant fellowship, or thanksgiving, as well as pagan ritual offerings. In 1 Samuel 1:3, the word identifies the purpose of Elkanah's annual pilgrimage to Shiloh, showing his faithful commitment to worship the LORD of hosts through formal animal sacrifice during a spiritually dark period in Israel's history.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, worship regularly involved animal sacrifice. The root verb zabach refers to the act of slaughtering livestock as an offering. Unlike general slaughtering for daily food consumption, zabach usually carries a sacred or ceremonial meaning, set within the context of a ritual altar.

In the historical setting of 1 Samuel 1:3, Israel was living near the end of the period of the Judges. Centralized worship took place at Shiloh, where the tabernacle and the ark of the covenant resided. Elkanah, the father of Samuel, made a regular journey from his hometown of Ramathaim Zophim to Shiloh. His journey was made specifically to present himself before the LORD and participate in the required sacrificial worship prescribed in the Law of Moses.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb zabach primarily refers to slaughtering an animal for a sacrifice or a formal worship meal. The word can refer to several distinct settings:

  • Offering a lawful sacrifice to the LORD at an approved sanctuary or altar.

  • Slaughtering an animal for a covenant meal or shared communal feast.

  • Offering an unauthorized or pagan sacrifice to false gods.

In 1 Samuel 1:3, the word describes an intentional act of religious worship directed toward the LORD of hosts (YHWH Tsebaoth). The grammatical form functions as an infinitive construct, which expresses purpose—Elkanah went up to Shiloh specifically "to sacrifice" to the LORD.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament narrative and poetry, zabach carries distinct nuances depending on the intent of the worshipper and the recipient of the offering:

When directed toward God, the word often highlights fellowship and covenant devotion. Many sacrifices represented by zabach were peace offerings (shelem), where portions of the animal were burned on the altar, portions were given to the priests, and the remaining meat was eaten by the worshipper's family in a holy meal.

When used negatively, the word describes Israel's recurring fall into idolatry, where the nation slaughtered sacrifices to pagan deities on high places rather than at the tabernacle.

In narrative contexts, zabach can also describe a farewell feast or covenant ratification meal where animals were killed and shared among participants, blending ritual worship with family or community covenanting.

4. Theological Implications

The practice of sacrifice in the Old Testament establishes fundamental biblical truths about approaching a holy God. Worship required an offering, acknowledging God's holiness and humanity's dependence on divine mercy.

The use of zabach in 1 Samuel highlights that personal faith and devotion were preserved through official, physical acts of obedience. Worship was not merely an internal feeling; it required costly devotion, travel, and public submission to God's ordained altar. Furthermore, the sacrifices offered under the Old Covenant pointed forward to the necessity of atonement, pointing ahead to the ultimate sacrifice of Jesus Christ, who offered Himself once for all to remove sin completely.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:3, zabach appears in a passage that establishes the spiritual setting for the birth of Samuel. The verse notes that Elkanah went up from his city yearly to worship and "sacrifice" to the LORD of hosts in Shiloh.

The passage immediately mentions that the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, were priests of the LORD there. Later narrative detail reveals that these priests were corrupt and abused the sacrificial system for personal gain. Yet despite the spiritual corruption in the priesthood, Elkanah maintained his personal loyalty to the LORD and continued his annual duty of sacrifice at the centralized sanctuary.

The purpose-oriented form of the verb shows Elkanah's active initiative. His regular pilgrimage demonstrates that individual godliness endured in Israel even when institutional leadership was deeply compromised.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The location mentioned in 1 Samuel 1:3—Shiloh— served as the major religious center before Jerusalem became the capital under David. The mention of zabach at Shiloh emphasizes continuity with the sanctuary system established in the wilderness under Moses.

It is also noteworthy that Elkanah's household participated in this sacrifice together. Annual sacrifices often involved peace offerings where family members shared in the sacred meal, underscoring the role of the family unit in passing down faithful worship during times of national instability.
