SONS - (bên, Strong's H1121)

Summary:

The Hebrew word ben fundamentally refers to a male offspring, son, or descendant. Beyond direct biological lineage, Scripture uses the term flexibly to describe membership in a family, nation, age group, or category. In 1 Samuel 1:3, the word identifies Hophni and Phinehas as the direct male offspring of Eli the priest. By introducing them as Eli's sons who were serving as priests at Shiloh, the text establishes their official standing and family legacy, setting up a tragic contrast between their sacred position and their upcoming unfaithfulness.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israelite society, family identity, inheritance, and religious duties were passed down through patriarchal lines. A father's lineage determined a person's tribal standing, property rights, and priestly obligations. The word ben serves as the primary Hebrew term for establishing this familial continuity.

In the opening chapter of 1 Samuel, the worship center of Israel is situated at Shiloh. As Elkanah and his family travel yearly to offer sacrifices to the Lord of hosts, the narrator introduces the religious leadership present at the sanctuary. The term is used here to identify Hophni and Phinehas in relation to their father, Eli. As Eli's male offspring, they inherited the rights and responsibilities of the sanctuary service.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word primarily refers to a physical son or immediate male offspring. However, Hebrew usage extends the term across several related categories:

  • Direct Offspring: A biological son born to parents.

  • Descendants: Grandchildren or distant generations within a family line or nation.

  • Young of Animals: Offspring of livestock or creatures.

  • Character or Category: A person characterized by a particular trait, status, or age group.

In 1 Samuel 1:3, the term carries its direct biological sense, identifying Hophni and Phinehas as the immediate sons of Eli.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

across the Old Testament, ben appears in a wide variety of contexts. It frequently forms the building block for genealogies, royal successions, and covenant blessings.

While the word often designates human male children, its broader poetic and legal uses demonstrate its scope. For example, when describing livestock, Scripture applies the term to the young of animals, such as camel colts or young sheep. In legal and ceremonial contexts, it can express age, designating a person or animal as being of a certain age. When applied to human families, it emphasizes the passing of name, authority, and covenant inheritance from one generation to the next.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of Eli's sons in 1 Samuel 1:3 highlights a crucial theological truth: covenant position and family heritage do not guarantee personal godliness.

Hophni and Phinehas possessed every outer privilege. They held the title of priests because of their biological descent from Eli. Yet as the narrative unfolds, their lives reveal a complete lack of personal reverence for God. Their inclusion by name and relation serves as a warning that spiritual devotion is not automatically transmitted through physical birth. Each generation must personally know and honor the Lord, regardless of their family heritage.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:3, the phrase "the two sons of Eli" serves an important structural and narrative purpose. It introduces the ministers who operated at Shiloh when Hannah came to pray in her deep distress.

By linking Hophni and Phinehas directly to Eli, the text establishes the formal authority they held in the sanctuary. At the same time, it subtly prepares the reader for the corruption that would bring down the house of Eli. The text specifies that these two sons were functioning as priests of the Lord, creating an immediate tension between the ideal holiness of the priestly family and the actual spiritual bankruptcy that soon becomes apparent in the story.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 3:16 – God tells Eve that in pain she will bring forth "children," showing the word used for human offspring in the context of the Fall.

  • Genesis 32:15 – Jacob prepares a gift of livestock for Esau, including camel "colts," illustrating how the term can describe the young of animals.

  • 1 Kings 1:30 – King David promises Bathsheba that her "son" Solomon will reign after him, highlighting the word in royal succession.

  • 1 Chronicles 3:9 – The text lists the "sons" of David, demonstrating its standard usage in formal family records.

  • Psalms 114:4 – The psalm poetically describes the hills leaping like "lambs," showing the word applied figuratively to young flock animals.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Grammatically, the word in 1 Samuel 1:3 is a plural, masculine form. It groups Hophni and Phinehas together under their common father Eli and their shared priestly duty.

This shared identity becomes central to the message of 1 Samuel. Both brothers acted together in wickedness, and both faced God's judgment together. Their shared biological sonship placed them in the sanctuary, but their shared rebellion brought disaster upon their father's household.
