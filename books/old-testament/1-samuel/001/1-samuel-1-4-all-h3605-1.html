ALL - (kôl, Strong's H3605)

Summary:

The Hebrew word kol means "all," "every," "whole," or "the totality" of a given group or thing. It serves as a comprehensive term in biblical Hebrew to express complete inclusion without exception. In 1 Samuel 1:4, the word describes how Elkanah distributed sacrificial portions to Peninnah and "all her sons and daughters," emphasizing the complete inclusion of her children in the sacrificial meal and setting up a stark contrast with childless Hannah.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word kol is one of the most common terms in ancient Hebrew for expressing totality and completeness. It appears in every type of biblical literature, from early narrative accounts to detailed sacrificial laws, wisdom sayings, and prophetic announcements.

In ancient Israel, annual family trips to the central sanctuary at Shiloh involved communal worship and peace offerings. After the priest received his portion and the fat was offered on the altar, the remaining meat was returned to the worshiper to host a joyful family feast before the Lord. In 1 Samuel 1, Elkanah brings his entire household to Shiloh for this yearly sacrifice. The passage uses kol to describe how Elkanah distributed portions of the sacrificial meal to his family members.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to the whole of a singular object or the entirety of a collective group. When paired with a plural noun, it means "all" or "every individual" within that group. When paired with a singular noun, it often translates as "whole" or "entire."

In general usage, the word indicates that no part or person is omitted from the statement. It establishes complete coverage within the scope of the sentence. Here in 1 Samuel 1:4, the word modifies Peninnah's children, indicating that every single one of her sons and daughters received an individual portion of the offering.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses kol to emphasize dynamic totality across many contexts:

  • Comprehensive groups: The word frequently designates an entire assembly, nation, or household, showing that an instruction or event applies to every person present.

  • Complete obedience or devotion: Scripture often pairs the word with commandments or the heart to describe full, undivided loyalty to God rather than partial compliance.

  • Geographic or physical scope: The word describes entire regions, whole lands, or complete physical structures to show that nothing within those boundaries is left out.

Grammatically, the word functions here in a singular, common form, attached to a plural concept to indicate that every single member of the family group receives a share.

4. Theological Implications

The term kol often highlights the thoroughness of God's work and the broad reach of His covenant instructions. When God commands His people, He demands devotion with all their heart and soul. When He promises blessing or judgment, the scope of His action covers the entire community.

In the setting of Israel's worship, worship was intended to be a family event where whole households gathered before the Lord. The inclusive nature of the sacrificial feast reflected God's generous provision for every member of the covenant family. At the same time, the inclusion of every child highlighted individual standing and identity within the larger family structure during sacred meals.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:4, kol plays a crucial role in highlighting the quiet tragedy taking place within Elkanah's household. As the head of the house, Elkanah gives portions of the peace offering to his wife Peninnah and to "all her sons and daughters."

The repetition of portions for every single child makes Peninnah's fruitful situation visibly obvious at the sacred table. Every son and daughter receives a portion, multiplying Peninnah's presence and honor during the feast. Right after this, the text notes that Elkanah gave Hannah a double portion (or a single chosen portion) because he loved her, even though the Lord had closed her womb. The complete inclusion of all of Peninnah's children sharpens the contrast between Peninnah's abundance and Hannah's deep pain, establishing the emotional and spiritual setting for Hannah's desperate prayer to God in the sanctuary.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The distribution of portions at the peace offering was governed by principles laid out in Israel's law. Peace offerings were meant to be occasion for shared joy and thanksgiving. By giving portions to all her sons and daughters, Elkanah fulfilled his duty as a godly provider, ensuring that every child participated in the sacred meal.

Yet, this scene illustrates how a moment meant for universal family joy could become a place of personal sorrow. Peninnah used her full table to provoke Hannah, turning a sacred meal into a time of grief. The text uses kol not merely as a statistical detail about Peninnah's family size, but to paint a clear picture of the full table that made Hannah's empty arms so painfully obvious until the Lord answered her prayer.
