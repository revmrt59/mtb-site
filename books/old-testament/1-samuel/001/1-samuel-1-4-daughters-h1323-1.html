DAUGHTERS - (bath, Strong's H1323)

Summary:

The Hebrew word bat primarily refers to a daughter or female descendant. In 1 Samuel 1:4, the word appears in the plural to describe the female children born to Peninnah. In this passage, the mention of Peninnah's daughters highlights her fully established, fruitfully blessed household during the annual sacrifice at Shiloh, creating a painful contrast with Hannah, who was childless.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word bat is the standard Hebrew term for a female child or daughter. It functions as the direct feminine counterpart to ben ("son"). In ancient Israelite culture, children were seen as tangible gifts from God, central to the stability, legacy, and ongoing life of the family unit.

In addition to its direct biological meaning, ancient Hebrew frequently used kinship terms in flexible ways. The word bat could refer to a granddaughter, a young woman in a community, or even outlying rural settlements dependent on a larger central city.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Bat has a few related meanings in Scripture:

  • A biological female child or immediate daughter.

  • A female descendant, such as a granddaughter or woman of a particular lineage.

  • A term of affectionate address toward a younger woman.

  • A figurative term for surrounding towns or dependent villages attached to a primary city.

  • A figurative personification of a city or its inhabitants, such as the expression "daughter of Zion."

In 1 Samuel 1:4, the word carries its most direct literal meaning, referring specifically to the biological female children of Elkanah and Peninnah.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses bat across many historical and literary settings:

  • Direct Family Relationships: The word often identifies a daughter within her immediate family, such as Lot's daughters in Genesis or the daughters of Zelophehad in Numbers.

  • Adoptive and Guardian Care: The word describes formal or functional daughterhood, as when Pharaoh's daughter adopted Moses and raised him as her own son.

  • Community and Affection: Elder figures frequently used the plural form "daughters" as a tender expression of guidance or affection, such as Naomi addressing her daughters-in-law.

  • Geographical and Metaphorical Sense: In historical lists, surrounding agricultural communities surrounding a major fortified city were called its "daughters" (often translated "villages"). In poetic texts, cities facing grief or restoration were personified as a "daughter."

4. Theological Implications

In the Old Testament narrative, children represent divine favor, covenant continuity, and future hope. Having both sons and daughters signified a well-rounded, fully established family.

At the same time, Scripture reveals that God's grace and purpose are not restricted to human social expectations. The presence or absence of children in a household served as a key backdrop through which God demonstrated His power to lift up the barren, fulfill His promises, and bring about unexpected redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:4, Elkanah travels to the sanctuary at Shiloh for the annual sacrifice. When he offers his sacrifice, he gives portions of the sacrificial meal to his second wife Peninnah and "to all her sons and her daughters."

The explicit mention of both sons and daughters emphasizes how complete Peninnah's family was. Each year at the worship festival, Peninnah sat surrounded by her children, receiving multiple portions for her household. In contrast, verse 5 notes that Elkanah gave Hannah a double portion because he loved her, even though she had no children.

The presence of Peninnah's daughters at the sacrificial table made Hannah's empty hands visually obvious to everyone present. This contrast deepened Hannah's grief and Peninnah's provocation, driving Hannah to her knees in desperate prayer before God. Ultimately, this sorrow became the doorway for God to hear her prayer and grant her a son, Samuel.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The grammatical form in 1 Samuel 1:4 is feminine plural, indicating multiple daughters born to Peninnah.

Annual sacrificial meals under the Mosaic law were meant to be joyful occasions shared by the entire household, including sons and daughters. In Elkanah's household, the act of distributing sacrificial portions to every son and daughter made the annual trip to Shiloh an ongoing reminder of who had children and who did not, setting the stage for God's sovereign intervention in Israel's history.
