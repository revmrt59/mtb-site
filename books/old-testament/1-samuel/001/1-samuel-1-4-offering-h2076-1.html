OFFERING - (zâbach, Strong's H2076)

Summary:

The word zabach means to slaughter an animal for sacrifice or ritual feasting. In ancient Israelite worship, it frequently referred to presenting fellowship or peace offerings, where the animal was prepared so that a portion was offered to God while the family shared in a sacred meal. In 1 Samuel 1:4, the word describes Elkanah slaughtering his annual sacrifice at Shiloh, setting the stage for the family feast where portions were distributed and Hannah's silent heartache unfolded before the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Near East and throughout the Old Testament, bringing an animal sacrifice was a central act of divine worship. Before the construction of the temple in Jerusalem, Israel gathered at central sanctuaries such as Shiloh, where the tabernacle and the ark of the covenant rested during the period of the judges.

Israelites traveled to these holy sites during annual festival seasons to present worship offerings. The action of slaughtering and presenting these animals was an essential part of approaching God. It marked moments of thanksgiving, covenant renewal, rededication, and joyful communion with God and community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word zabach refers primarily to slaughtering or killing an animal as a religious sacrifice. Depending on who is bringing the offering and the context of the event, the word carries a few natural shades of meaning:

  • Slaughtering an animal specifically for an altar sacrifice to God.

  • Preparing a fellowship or peace offering, which culminated in a shared sacred meal for the worshippers.

  • Slaughtering livestock for meat or butchering an animal for a feast.

  • Presenting unauthorized or idolatrous sacrifices to false gods.

When used in Israel's worship, zabach often points to a sacrifice that ended in a shared table gathering, where those bringing the offering ate together in God's presence after the sacrificial blood and fat were presented at the altar.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, zabach appears in a wide variety of narrative, legal, and poetic settings. In the books of the Law, the word describes both the individual act of worship and the mandatory national offerings. It distinguishes worshipful sacrifices made to the Lord from pagan rites practiced by surrounding nations.

In narrative accounts, bringing a zabach often marks major moments in family life, national life, or spiritual renewal. A family would travel to the sanctuary, present their sacrifice, and sit down to eat together. Scripture also highlights the danger of mere outward ritual: when people perform the physical act of slaughtering sacrifices without clean hands and a devoted heart, the offering loses its spiritual value before God.

4. Theological Implications

The practice of zabach highlights several key truths about worship:

  • Approach to God requires a appointed sacrifice: Human beings cannot simply barge into the presence of a holy God; worship requires coming according to His revealed instructions.

  • Communion and Fellowship: Unlike burnt offerings that were completely consumed on the altar, fellowship sacrifices allowed the worshipper to partake of the meal. Eating in the sanctuary represented peace, reconciliation, and ongoing table fellowship with God.

  • Heart Worship over Cold Ritual: The physical slaughter of an animal was meant to reflect an inward reality of faith, gratitude, and obedience.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:4, zabach describes Elkanah performing his regular religious duty at Shiloh. The grammatical form used here (a masculine singular imperfect, wayyiqtol) drives the story forward, showing Elkanah actively carrying out his annual sacrifice.

The context reveals a powerful spiritual contrast. Elkanah is acting as a faithful, devout head of household, worshiping God and distributing meat portions to his family from the fellowship meal. Yet inside this scene of sacred celebration, severe personal grief is present. Peninnah receives her portions for her children, while Hannah sits in sorrow over her barrenness. The act of slaughtering and sharing the sacrificial feast brings the family together at the altar, but it also accentuates Hannah's anguish, driving her to pray desperately to the Lord.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 31:54 — "Then Jacob offered a sacrifice on the mountain, and called his brethren to eat bread."

  • Leviticus 22:29 — "And when you offer a sacrifice of thanksgiving to the LORD, offer it of your own free will."

  • 1 Samuel 15:21 — "But the people took of the plunder, sheep and oxen, the best of the things which should have been utterly destroyed, to sacrifice to the LORD your God in Gilgal."

  • Psalms 4:5 — "Offer the sacrifices of righteousness, and put your trust in the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The occurrence of zabach in 1 Samuel 1 helps contextualize the spiritual state of Israel just before the rise of the monarchy. While the priesthood at Shiloh under Eli's sons was becoming corrupt, ordinary families like Elkanah's were still making the journey to slaughter their sacrifices and worship the Lord faithfully. God used this regular context of annual sacrificial feasts to hear Hannah's heart cry, eventually answering her prayer with the birth of Samuel.
