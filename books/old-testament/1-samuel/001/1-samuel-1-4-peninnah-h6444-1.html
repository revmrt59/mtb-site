PENINNAH - (Pᵉninnâh, Strong's H6444)

Summary:

Peninnah is a proper feminine name in Hebrew, traditionally associated with terms for "jewel," "coral," or "pearl." In the Old Testament narrative, Peninnah is identified as the second wife of Elkanah. In 1 Samuel 1:4, the name identifies the wife who had borne sons and daughters to Elkanah and received designated portions of the sacrificial meal, serving as a visible contrast to Hannah's childlessness and highlighting the ongoing friction within Elkanah's household during acts of worship.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name Peninnah appears in the opening chapter of 1 Samuel, establishing the family background of Samuel the prophet. The narrative begins during the turbulent period near the end of the judges, prior to the establishment of Israel's monarchy.

Elkanah lived in Ramathaim Zophim in the mountains of Ephraim and maintained a household with two wives, Hannah and Peninnah. While domestic polygamy occurred in ancient Near Eastern culture, the biblical narrative consistently demonstrates that multi-wife households produced severe competition, jealousy, and domestic strife. Each year, the family made a pilgrimage to Shiloh to worship and offer sacrifices to the Lord of hosts at the Tabernacle.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew word Peninnah is a feminine proper noun used exclusively as a personal name. Etymologically, the name relates to a Hebrew noun denoting precious gems, red coral, or pearls.

As a proper name, Peninnah carries no complex verbal stem meaning of its own; rather, it functions directly within the text to identify a specific historical individual—the wife of Elkanah who bore him children.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The name Peninnah distinguishes Elkanah's two wives based on their maternal status: Peninnah had children, whereas Hannah had none. In ancient Israelite society, children were viewed as signs of God's favor, social security, and family continuity.

Because Peninnah bore sons and daughters while Hannah remained childless, Peninnah possessed domestic status and social standing that Hannah lacked. The narrative records that Peninnah used her fruitfulness to taunt and provoke Hannah, turning Hannah's childlessness into a source of severe grief. The usage of her name highlights how personal status and rivalry disrupted what should have been unified family worship.

4. Theological Implications

The narrative surrounding Peninnah touches on several foundational biblical themes:

  • Divine sovereignty over fruitfulness: The text notes that God had closed Hannah's womb, making Peninnah's maternal success a contrast designed by the narrative to emphasize God's direct role in opening Hannah's womb later in His timing.

  • The reality of human fallenness in worship: Peninnah's rivalry with Hannah shows that participating in religious routines and sacrificial meals does not automatically generate godliness or love within a heart.

  • God's elevated purpose through human trial: Peninnah's ongoing harassment drove Hannah to deep, persistent prayer at the Tabernacle. God used this trial to bring Hannah to the point of dedicating her prospective son entirely to the Lord's service, resulting in the birth and ministry of Samuel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:4, the name Peninnah appears in the context of the annual sacrificial feast at Shiloh. When Elkanah offered his sacrifice, he distributed portions of the peace offering meal to his family members.

Peninnah receives portions for herself and for all her sons and daughters. This scene physically demonstrated her abundance; she sat surrounded by her children, receiving multiple shares of the sacrificial meat. Directly after this, Elkanah gives Hannah a single special portion because he loved her, even though she was childless.

The occurrence of Peninnah's name in verse 4 establishes the public setting where Peninnah's visible fruitfulness stood side by side with Hannah's emptiness, setting the stage for Peninnah's severe provocation of Hannah during the sacred meal.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 1:2 — "And he had two wives; the name of one was Hannah, and the name of the other Peninnah. Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Peninnah disappears from the historical account as soon as God remembers Hannah and Samuel is born. While Peninnah enjoyed immediate physical prosperity and family prominence, the central trajectory of redemptive history flowed through Hannah's faithful persistent prayer. Peninnah's presence in the opening chapter serves to highlight the spiritual contrast between mere external abundance and humble dependence on God.
