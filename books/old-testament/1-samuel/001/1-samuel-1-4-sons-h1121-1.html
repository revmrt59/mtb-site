SONS - (bên, Strong's H1121)

Summary:

The Hebrew word ben primarily refers to a male offspring, son, or descendant. In 1 Samuel 1:4, the plural form "sons" refers to the male children born to Peninnah, highlighting her household abundance in sharp, painful contrast to Hannah's childlessness during the annual sacrificial feast at Shiloh.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israelite culture, children were viewed as direct expressions of divine favor and essential for the survival of the family line. A family's heritage, land inheritance, and long-term security rested heavily on male descendants. Sons preserved the family name, inherited the ancestral land allotments, and provided protection and economic strength for the household.

In domestic settings, particularly polygamous families, the presence or absence of children often shaped social standing within the home. Mothering male children brought honor and security to a wife, while childlessness carried heavy social stigma and emotional sorrow. This cultural reality forms the immediate backdrop to the opening narrative of 1 Samuel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word ben fundamentally means a male child or son. It identifies a direct physical relationship between a father or mother and their male offspring.

Depending on context, the word can refer to:

  • A direct biological male child born to parents.

  • Plural descendants, including grandchildren or entire generations of offspring.

  • Young animals or offspring of livestock.

  • Members of a specific group, nation, or class.

  • An idiom describing age or personal character traits.

Here in 1 Samuel 1:4, the word carries its natural biological meaning, referring specifically to the literal male children fathered by Elkanah through Peninnah.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Scripture, ben appears in a wide variety of contexts ranging from immediate family ties to national history. Most often, it indicates direct parentage, such as a father listing his children or establishing legal heirship.

In wider applications, the plural form is frequently used to identify broader groups, such as the whole assembly of Israel ("sons of Israel") or entire clan lineages. The word can also express close relational bonding, such as an older elder addressing a younger protégé as a son. In poetic or agrarian passages, the term describes young livestock born to a herd.

4. Theological Implications

Scripture consistently presents children as gifts created and bestowed by God. Human effort alone cannot guarantee offspring; life comes through God's creative power.

The contrast between fruitfulness and barrenness highlights God's ultimate sovereignty over human history. While society associated multiple sons with power and privilege, God often worked redemptive purpose through the weak, the barren, and the overlooked. The gift of male children guaranteed the continuation of God's covenant promises from generation to generation, ultimately leading to the royal line of David.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:4, Elkanah travels with his family to worship and sacrifice to the Lord of hosts at Shiloh. When distributing the portions of the peace offerings, he gives portions to his wife Peninnah and to "all her sons and her daughters."

The explicit mention of Peninnah's "sons" establishes the painful domestic tension at the heart of the story. Peninnah enjoyed full motherly status and social security, represented by her multiple sons and daughters receiving sacrificial portions at the table. Hannah, whom Elkanah loved deeply, had no children because the Lord had closed her womb.

The annual sacrificial meal, which was meant to be a joyful celebration of God's goodness, became a moment of deep anguish for Hannah. Peninnah used her fruitfulness—evidenced by her sons—to provoke and distress Hannah. Thus, the plural "sons" in this verse serves as a crucial narrative marker: it underscores Peninnah's visible abundance and deepens the emotional grief that drove Hannah to her knees in silent, weeping prayer before the Lord.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The presence of Peninnah's sons makes the dramatic shift in 1 Samuel all the more powerful. Peninnah possessed many sons, yet none of them are named or called to lead God's people. In contrast, Hannah had no sons, yet her desperate prayer resulted in the birth of Samuel—a child dedicated entirely to God from birth who became one of Israel's greatest prophets and judges.

This contrast reminds readers that divine purpose is not measured by numerical abundance or human status. God bypassed the established status of Peninnah's household to work through Hannah's humble brokenness, demonstrating that God delights in answering the prayers of the afflicted.
