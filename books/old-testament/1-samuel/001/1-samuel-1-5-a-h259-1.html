A - (ʼechâd, Strong's H259)

Summary:

The word means "one" or "a single," serving as a fundamental numerical term that identifies an individual unit, a first item in a series, or a specific person or object set apart from a group. In 1 Samuel 1:5, the word specifies a distinct, individual allotment given to Hannah during the annual family worship at Shiloh. By marking this particular portion, the narrator emphasizes Elkanah's deliberate act of love toward his childless wife amid family tension, setting the stage for God's gracious intervention.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The historical setting of 1 Samuel begins during the period of the judges, a time marked by spiritual confusion and political instability in Israel. The story opens within the household of Elkanah, a Levite living in the hill country of Ephraim. Elkanah had two wives: Hannah, who was childless, and Peninnah, who had children. Each year, the family traveled to the sanctuary at Shiloh to sacrifice to the Lord of hosts.

In ancient Israelite worshiping practices, peace offerings involved a communal meal where portions of the sacrificial meat were distributed to family members. This annual pilgrimage highlighted the personal grief of Hannah's barrenness alongside the rivalry within the home. The term 'echad appears in this opening narrative as part of the description of how Elkanah distributed these sacrificial portions, drawing attention to the specific gift he prepared for Hannah.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of 'echad is "one." It is the standard Hebrew numerical word used to count objects, identify a single unit, or designate first place in a series.

Depending on how it functions in a sentence, the word can mean:

  • A cardinal number meaning "one" (a single entity or item).

  • An ordinal indicator meaning "first" (such as the first day of a time period).

  • An indefinite marker corresponding to English "a" or "a certain."

  • An adjective signifying "alone" or "only."

In 1 Samuel 1:5, the feminine singular form 'achat modifies the noun describing the sacrificial portion. It identifies a single, specific unit assigned to Hannah.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament narrative and poetry, 'echad serves as a foundational building block for counting and identifying items. When applied to physical objects, it distinguishes one item from a group or sets apart a single portion from a larger collection.

The word can stress complete unity when describing multiple individuals joining together into one body, or it can isolate a single person from a group. When describing sequence, it marks the beginning or initial point, such as the first day of creation. In narrative settings, it often acts as an indefinite marker, focusing the listener's attention on one particular person, place, or gift out of many possibilities. In the context of offerings and distributions, specifying "one" portion highlights the exact, deliberate nature of what is given.

4. Theological Implications

While 'echad is a simple numerical word, its use in sacrificial contexts carries theological weight regarding God's care for the individual. Sacrifices under the Old Covenant were not merely corporate rituals; they involved personal offerings distributed to real families with real sorrows.

In 1 Samuel 1, the distribution of sacrificial portions reflects both the order of worship and the personal affections of the worshipper. God sees the individual believer within the broader worshiping community. Hannah was not overlooked in the crowd at Shiloh, nor was she forgotten by God despite her inability to bear children. The attention given to a single portion reminds readers that God's providence works through the specific details of daily life and sacrificial devotion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:5, the narrative contrasts the portions given to Peninnah and her children with the portion given to Hannah. Elkanah gave multiple portions to Peninnah and her family, but to Hannah he gave a single, chosen portion. NKJV renders the broader idiom in this verse as "a double portion" (or a special portion), reflecting the idea of a prime or special selection given to demonstrate favor.

The word 'echad emphasizes that this was a distinct, individual gift set apart specifically for Hannah. Even though Peninnah taunted Hannah because the Lord had closed her womb, Elkanah's act of presenting this single, targeted gift demonstrated his deep love for her. The word functions in the verse to draw a clear contrast between the routine distribution to the larger family and the special, intentional care shown to Hannah in her sorrow.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:5 — "So the evening and the morning were the first day." Here the word is translated "first," marking the initial day of God's creation week.

  • Genesis 44:28 — "And the one went out from me, and I said, 'Surely he is torn to pieces'; and I have not seen him since." In Jacob's plea, the word appears as "one," referring specifically to his lost son Joseph.

  • Joshua 22:20 — "Did not Achan the son of Zerah commit a trespass in the accursed thing, and wrath fell on all the congregation of Israel? And that man did not perish alone in his iniquity." In this passage, the word is translated "alone," emphasizing that one man's sin affected the entire community.

  • Ecclesiastes 4:11 — "Again, if two lie down together, they will keep warm; but how can one be warm alone?" Here the word is rendered "one," contrasting the isolation of an individual with the strength found in companionship.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The target occurrence in 1 Samuel 1:5 is grammatically feminine singular, matching the feminine noun it modifies in the sentence. Hebrew adjectives regularly agree in gender and number with the nouns they qualify.

The ancient translation history of 1 Samuel 1:5 contains an interesting textual distinction. The Greek Septuagint translated the phrase with a word meaning "a single portion," emphasizing that Hannah received only one portion despite Elkanah's love, because she had no children to receive extra shares. The Hebrew text preserved in the traditional Masoretic tradition combines 'echad with another term to indicate a double or choice portion given out of love. Under either translation reading, the word 'echad grounds the expression in a concrete unit presented at the sanctuary.
