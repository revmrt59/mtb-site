CLOSED - (çâgar, Strong's H5462)

Summary:

The word sagar means to shut, close, seal, or hand over. In everyday ancient life, it described shutting a door, closing a city gate, or securing a space so that nothing could enter or leave. In 1 Samuel 1:5, the word describes God's direct oversight in withholding childbearing from Hannah. It demonstrates that her barrenness was not a random medical misfortune, but a deliberate situation controlled by God to accomplish His ultimate purpose.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Near East, the idea of shutting or closing carried strong associations with protection, restraint, and boundaries. People shut city gates at sunset to keep out enemies and wild animals. They shut house doors to secure privacy and safety. They also closed up wounds or containers to keep contents secure.

When applied to human biology, the imagery of closing or opening a womb was widely understood across the Old Testament world. Life was recognized as coming directly from God, who alone held authority over the human body. Restraining or opening a woman's womb was seen as an action within God's absolute authority over human reproduction and family lineage.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of sagar is to shut or close up. Depending on the setting, the word can refer to:

  • Physically closing an object, such as shutting a wooden door, a city gate, or a window.

  • Enclosing or stopping up physical flesh, such as healing over a wound or surrounding an area.

  • Confining or isolating a person, such as shutting up someone diagnosed with a contagious disease.

  • Handing over or delivering someone into the power of another, as when a captive is delivered into an enemy's hand.

Here in 1 Samuel 1:5, the word means to close or restrain biologically, referring to God preventing conception. The word itself describes the simple action of closing or shutting off access; the theological significance comes from God being the one who performs the action.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses sagar across a wide variety of settings. In practical everyday life, it describes routine security, such as closing the city gates of Jericho before nightfall or shutting a house door behind oneself for privacy during prayer or miracles. In legal and ceremonial contexts, the word describes isolating a person to protect the community from skin disease.

In military and narrative texts, sagar frequently carries the sense of delivering or trapping someone, where God or an army shuts a person in so they cannot escape.

When applied to childbearing, Scripture uses sagar to explain that fertility is controlled by God. Rather than attributing childlessness to fate, nature, or idols, the biblical writers use this word to highlight God's direct sovereignty over human birth and the womb.

4. Theological Implications

The use of sagar in relation to Hannah highlights the reality of God's supreme authority over human life. In ancient Israelite culture, childlessness brought intense personal grief and severe social reproach. Yet the narrative explicitly attributes Hannah's condition to the LORD.

This truth prevents several misunderstandings:

  • It refutes the idea that Hannah's suffering was a secondary accident outside of God's awareness.

  • It refutes the claim of local pagan religions that fertility was controlled by idol gods or magical rituals.

  • It shows that when God closes a door or a womb, it is not necessarily a sign of moral failure or divine wrath.

In God's broader work in Scripture, a closed womb often prepares the way for a crucial redemptive work. God restrained Hannah's womb for a time so that her eventual son, Samuel, would be dedicated entirely to God's service as a prophet and judge during a critical transition in Israel's history.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:5, the word appears in the masculine singular, perfect form, identifying the LORD as the subject who performed the action. The text presents Elkanah giving his wife Hannah a double portion at the yearly sacrifice because he loved her, "although the LORD had closed her womb."

The contrast in the passage is sharp:

  • Human love and favor could offer comfort and material gifts, but Elkanah could not resolve Hannah's deeper sorrow.

  • The rival wife, Peninnah, used Hannah's childlessness to provoke and grieve her deeply.

  • Yet the real key to the situation lay with God, who had appointed this season of restraint.

The immediate repetition of the word in verse 6 reinforces that Hannah's pain was real and continuous. Yet by identifying God as the one who closed her womb, the narrative sets up the true solution: Hannah must turn directly to the LORD in prayer, because only the One who closed her womb possesses the authority to open it.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:21 – "And the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall on Adam, and he slept; and He took one of his ribs, and closed up the flesh in its place."

  • Joshua 2:5 – "And it happened as the gate was being shut, when it was dark, that the men went out."

  • 1 Samuel 1:6 – "And her rival also provoked her severely, to make her miserable, because the LORD had closed her womb."

  • 2 Kings 4:4 – "And when you have come in, you shall shut the door behind you and your sons; then pour it into all those vessels, and set aside the full ones."

  • Isaiah 22:22 – "The key of the house of David I will lay on his shoulder; so he shall open, and no one shall shut; and he shall shut, and no one shall open."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The double mention of sagar in 1 Samuel 1:5–6 places God's sovereign hand at the center of the story before Hannah utters a single prayer. It establishes that her ultimate deliverance will not come through human strength, medical remedies, or husbandly favoritism, but through divine intervention.

Furthermore, this passage connects to a larger pattern throughout Biblical history. Major redemptive figures—such as Isaac, Jacob, Joseph, and Samuel—were born to mothers whose wombs had previously been closed by God. Divine closure in Scripture is frequently the prelude to a monumental display of divine grace and glory.
