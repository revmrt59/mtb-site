DOUBLE - (ʼaph, Strong's H639)

Summary:

clear and grounded pastoral summary text follows.

The Hebrew word 'aph literally refers to the nose or nostrils, or in its dual form, the face. Because heavy breathing through the nose accompanies intense emotion, the word is most frequently used across the Old Testament to describe anger or wrath. In 1 Samuel 1:5, however, the word appears in the dual form as part of a specific Hebrew idiom translated "double." Here, it contributes to an expression describing a generous, distinguished portion of sacrificial meat that Elkanah gives to his wife Hannah, highlighting his deep affection and honor for her despite her childlessness.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word 'aph reflects concrete physical imagery typical of ancient Hebrew vocabulary. Its primary anatomical meaning refers to the nose or nostrils through which a person breathes. In Hebrew thought, internal feelings were often described through bodily expressions. Flared nostrils and heavy breathing marked strong emotion, leading the word to become the standard biblical term for burning anger.

In 1 Samuel 1:5, the word occurs in the dual form. While the dual form often refers physically to two nostrils or the face, here it operates as part of an established Hebrew idiom. The historical setting is the sanctuary at Shiloh during the annual sacrifice. As head of the household, Elkanah distributed portions of the offered meal to his family members.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic senses of 'aph include:

  • Nostril or nose, describing the physical organ of breath.

  • Face or countenance, particularly when a person bows low to the ground.

  • Anger, wrath, or fierce displeasure, which is its predominant figurative use.

In 1 Samuel 1:5, the word functions within an idiomatic phrase rather than referring to divine anger or physical anatomy. In this expression, it denotes a double or honorable portion set aside for a favored guest or family member.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament narrative, poetry, and law, 'aph moves between physical and figurative applications:

  • Physical organ: It describes God breathing life into human nostrils or a worshipper lowering their face toward the earth.

  • Emotion of anger: It represents human or divine anger that kindles or burns in response to provocation.

  • Special portion: In 1 Samuel 1:5, the dual form works within a phrase indicating abundance and distinction in meal distribution.

The grammatical form in 1 Samuel 1:5 is common gender in the dual number. This dual form fits the idiomatic expression of a portion meant to show extra favor and honor during a sacred family feast.

4. Theological Implications

1 Samuel opens with domestic tension and personal grief. Hannah suffers from childlessness, a condition that brought significant sorrow and social vulnerability in ancient Israel. Peninnah, Elkanah's second wife, taunts Hannah because the Lord has closed Hannah's womb.

In this painful setting, Elkanah's action of giving Hannah a double portion carries real pastoral weight. Although Hannah has no children to receive individual shares of the sacrifice, Elkanah makes sure she is not dishonored at the sacred meal. His action reflects faithfulness and affection within the home, showing that Hannah's worth in his eyes is not tied strictly to childbearing. This setting of deep sorrow eventually leads Hannah to pray earnestly at the tabernacle, where God answers her and raises up Samuel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:4–5, Elkanah distributes the sacrificial meat after presenting his offering to the Lord. He gives portions to Peninnah and to all her sons and daughters. When he reaches Hannah, he gives her a double portion because he loves her, even though her womb is closed.

The contrast between Peninnah's large family receiving multiple portions and Hannah receiving one double portion underscores Elkanah's deliberate devotion. While Peninnah's share is multiplied by her children, Hannah receives a designated portion of honor that expresses Elkanah's care directly to her. The narrator explicitly attributes this extra portion to Elkanah's love, demonstrating that her childlessness does not deprive her of honor at God's house.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:7 — In the creation account, God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life," using the word for the physical organ of breath.

  • Exodus 4:14 — When Moses hesitated to obey God's command, "the anger of the LORD was kindled against Moses," illustrating the word's common figurative sense.

  • 1 Samuel 20:41 — Before parting from Jonathan, David "fell on his face to the ground," where the dual form refers to the physical face placed low in reverence.

  • Psalms 145:8 — The psalm declares that "the LORD is gracious and full of compassion, slow to anger and great in mercy," highlighting God's patient character.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The idiomatic rendering of this phrase in 1 Samuel 1:5 has been understood in various ways by ancient translations, but the Hebrew text presents a clear picture of honor and favor. Rather than forcing a literal physical connection between nostrils or faces and a piece of meat, readers should recognize how Hebrew expressions naturally adapt physical words to convey concepts of measurement, distinction, and love.
