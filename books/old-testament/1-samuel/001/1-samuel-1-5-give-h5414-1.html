GIVE - (nâthan, Strong's H5414)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb natan means to give, bestow, grant, place, or set. It is a general term used throughout the Old Testament to describe passing an object, privilege, responsibility, or land from one party to another. In 1 Samuel 1:5, the word describes Elkanah giving a double portion of sacrificial meat to his wife Hannah during their annual worship at Shiloh. In this passage, the word highlights Elkanah's active love and commitment to honor Hannah in the midst of her grief over barrenness.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb natan is one of the foundational action words in the Hebrew language. It covers a broad spectrum of everyday and sacred actions. At its simplest, it describes the physical motion of handing something over to someone else. In wider contexts, it describes setting an object in a specific location, entrusting responsibility to a leader, or granting legal inheritance.

Because natan is used so broadly, its precise force in any passage depends heavily on who is acting and what is being given. When people are the subjects, the word often touches on generosity, hospitality, or sacrifice. When God is the subject, it highlights His sovereign authority to bestow life, blessing, guidance, or land upon His creation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of natan is to give or present something to another person. Depending on the surrounding sentence, it can refer to:

  • Bestowing a gift, portion, or reward.

  • Setting or placing an object in a particular position.

  • Granting authority, permission, or favor.

  • Handing over a possession or enemy.

When a person gives something to another in Scripture, natan focuses on the outward act of transfer. Here in 1 Samuel 1:5, the word describes a husband offering a special portion of food to his wife as an intentional expression of personal care.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, natan functions as a versatile word for all forms of distribution and placement. The word itself is simple and direct; it does not carry an inherently emotional or technical meaning by itself. Instead, the context fills the act of giving with significance.

For example, giving food to a guest expresses hospitality, giving a portion of sacrifice expresses family devotion, and giving land expresses covenant promise. The word is equally at home in practical daily life, sacrificial rituals, and royal declarations. The meaning stays grounded in the physical or covenantal act of bestowing something valuable.

4. Theological Implications

In the broader story of Scripture, human giving reflects the generous nature of God. God is the primary source of every good thing, granting breath, rain, harvest, and land to humanity. When human beings give gifts or offer sacrifices, they operate within the reality of God's prior generosity.

At the same time, Scripture reveals the limitations of human giving. A person can give earthly gifts, wealth, or comfort, but only God can give life, healing, and salvation. In 1 Samuel 1, this contrast is central. Elkanah can give Hannah a choice portion of meat to demonstrate his affection, but he cannot give her a child. The narrative shows both the beauty of human kindness and its inability to solve deep spiritual and physical needs apart from God's intervention.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:5, the family of Elkanah travels to Shiloh for the annual sacrifices to the LORD. During the sacrificial meal, the head of the household would distribute portions of the peace offerings to his family members. Elkanah gives portions to his wife Peninnah and all her sons and daughters. However, when he comes to Hannah, he gives her a double portion.

The grammatical form of the verb here is masculine singular imperfect, describing a repeated or customary action in this narrative context. Year after year, whenever the family went up to Shiloh, Elkanah would give Hannah this special portion.

The context explicitly connects this act of giving to Elkanah's devotion: he gave her a double portion "for he loved Hannah, although the LORD had closed her womb." In a culture where childlessness brought social shame and personal sorrow, Elkanah used the distribution of the sacrificial meal to publicly affirm Hannah's worth and his enduring love for her. His gift was a tangible sign of honor, meant to cushion the grief caused by her barrenness and by Peninnah's ongoing provocation.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The act of giving in 1 Samuel 1 creates an important narrative thread that runs through the whole chapter. The chapter opens with Elkanah giving physical portions of food to his family. Human giving, however, leaves Hannah unsatisfied because her grief remains.

In her distress, Hannah turns to prayer and vows that if God will give her a male child, she will give him back to the LORD all the days of his life. When God answers her prayer and grants her a son, she names him Samuel and faithfully brings him to Shiloh to dedicate him to divine service. Thus, the passage moves from human giving (Elkanah's portion) to divine giving (God granting a child) to dedicated human giving in response to grace (Hannah giving Samuel back to God).
