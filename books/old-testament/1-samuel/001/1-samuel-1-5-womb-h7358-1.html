WOMB - (rechem, Strong's H7358)

Summary:

The word rechem refers to the physical womb or matrix, the place of human conception and birth. In 1 Samuel 1:5, the word highlights Hannah's painful struggle with childlessness and explicitly attribute her condition to the Lord's divine oversight. By stating that God had closed her womb, the text frames her personal sorrow as part of a larger divine plan that ultimately leads to the birth of the prophet Samuel.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, family life and lineage held immense significance. Children were viewed as a vital blessing, securing the family heritage, providing support in old age, and maintaining the covenant community. Childlessness brought intense personal grief and often public reproach.

In 1 Samuel 1, Elkanah lived with his two wives, Peninnah and Hannah. Peninnah had borne children, but Hannah had none. Despite Elkanah's genuine love and favored treatment toward Hannah, her barrenness weighed heavily upon her. The narrative sets this human suffering directly within the realm of God's overarching providence, stating that "the LORD had closed her womb."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Rechem refers directly to the female reproductive organ, the womb. It designates the place where human life is conceived, protected, and developed prior to birth.

The noun belongs to a broader root family that includes concepts of tender care and deep affection. While the physical noun rechem simply means the anatomical womb, its association with maternal care underscores the deep emotional and relational bonds tied to motherhood in biblical thought. In standard usage, it functions as a clear physical noun representing the seat of childbearing.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, rechem is consistently tied to the initiation of human life and the sovereign work of God. Scripture does not treat biological fertility as an isolated mechanical process. Instead, God is repeatedly shown as the One who opens or closes the womb according to His sovereign wisdom.

The word is also used in redemptive instructions regarding the firstborn. Israel was commanded to consecrate every male that "opens the womb," marking those children and livestock as specially dedicated to the Lord. Thus, rechem connects daily biological reality with sacred covenant obedience.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of rechem reinforces a foundational theological truth: God is the Creator and Sustainer of life. Human effort, medicine, and natural processes are subordinate to His divine order.

When Scripture describes a closed womb, it frequently sets the stage for a significant act of divine intervention. Throughout Israel's story—from Sarah, Rebekah, and Rachel to Hannah—barrenness becomes the backdrop against which God displays His power, answers prayer, and advances His redemptive plans. A closed womb is not presented as a sign that God has forgotten a person, but often as the quiet beginning of a extraordinary work of grace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:5, rechem appears in a singular common noun form to describe Hannah's specific physical situation. The passage notes that Elkanah gave Hannah a double portion because he loved her, "although the LORD had closed her womb."

This explicit statement serves several important contextual purposes:

  • It removes any doubt about the cause of Hannah's barrenness, identifying God's sovereign hand rather than pure chance or pagan superstition.

  • It highlights the limits of human comfort. Elkanah's deep devotion and double portion could not remove the sorrow of a closed womb.

  • It prepares the reader for Hannah's desperate prayer at Shiloh. Her grief drives her directly to the throne of God, recognizing that only the One who closed her womb could open it.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 29:31 – "When the LORD saw that Leah was unloved, He opened her womb; but Rachel was barren."

  • Genesis 30:22 – "Then God remembered Rachel, and God listened to her and opened her womb."

  • Exodus 13:2 – "Sanctify to Me all the firstborn, whatever opens the womb among the children of Israel, both of man and beast; it is Mine."

  • 1 Samuel 1:6 – "And her rival also provoked her severely, to make her miserable, because the LORD had closed her womb."

  • Job 31:15 – "Did not He who made me in the womb make them? Did not the same One fashion us in the womb?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The stark contrast between Peninnah's natural fertility and Hannah's closed womb emphasizes that Samuel's eventual birth was an act of sovereign divine grace. Samuel was not merely another child born into Israel; he was a prophet given in response to faithful prayer to transition Israel from the chaotic era of the judges to the establishment of the monarchy. Hannah's temporary suffering served a pivotal purpose in the history of redemption.
