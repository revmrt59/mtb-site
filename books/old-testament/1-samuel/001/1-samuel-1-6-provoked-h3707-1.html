PROVOKED - (kaʻaç, Strong's H3707)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ka'as means to provoke to anger, vex, irritate, or cause deep grief and distress. In 1 Samuel 1:6, it describes how Peninnah intentionally antagonized and wounded Hannah over her barrenness, turning Hannah's painful vulnerability into an occasion for cruel distress.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The opening chapter of 1 Samuel introduces the domestic setting of Elkanah's family. Elkanah had two wives: Hannah, whom he loved deeply, and Peninnah. In ancient Israelite culture, childbearing was tied to family heritage, personal identity, and social security. Peninnah had borne sons and daughters, while Hannah remained unable to conceive.

This imbalance created severe strain within the home. Rather than showing compassion, Peninnah used her fruitfulness to taunt Hannah. The annual family trips to worship at Shiloh intensified this pain. Instead of being a season of joyous feast and thanksgiving, these trips became periods of grief for Hannah because of Peninnah's cruel behavior.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

bound up with emotional turmoil, ka'as means to provoke, anger, vex, or cause sharp sorrow and distress. The word can describe both the act of provoking someone else and the emotional state of being vexed or filled with grief.

When people provoke God, ka'as refers to rebellious actions—such as idolatry or disobedience—that arouse His righteous anger. When used between human beings, the word describes hostile speech or behavior designed to stir up anger, frustration, or deep emotional pain. In 1 Samuel 1:6, the word highlights the painful effect of Peninnah's mockery on Hannah, driving her to sorrow and tears.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, ka'as appears in two main contexts: divine judgment and human anguish.

Most frequently, Scripture uses ka'as to describe Israel provoking the Lord to anger through idolatry, covenant unfaithfulness, and persistent sin. In these cases, the word highlights how human disobedience grieves God and summons His righteous judgment.

In wisdom literature and narrative accounts, ka'as describes human grief, sorrow, or irritation caused by difficult life circumstances or harsh treatment by others. In the story of Hannah, the focus is on interpersonal provocation: one person intentionally weaponizing another person's suffering to cause heartbreak.

4. Theological Implications

Peninnah's treatment of Hannah reveals how sin distorts human relationships. Rather than displaying mercy toward someone experiencing trial, fallen human nature often prompts people to exalt themselves over the vulnerable.

The text also demonstrates that God takes notice of emotional distress caused by cruelty. Hannah did not retaliate against her rival; instead, she brought her grief directly to the Lord in prayer. This setting shows that prayer is a proper home for deep pain. God hears the cries of those who are provoked and oppressed, moving in sovereign grace to fulfill His broader purpose for His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:6, Peninnah is identified as Hannah's "rival," who "provoked her severely" to make her miserable. Grammatically, the verb form is feminine singular, identifying Peninnah as the individual causing this distress.

The surrounding narrative makes clear that this mistreatment was not a isolated incident. The text notes that this harassment occurred year after year when they went up to the house of the LORD. Peninnah seized upon the sacred feast times to magnify Hannah's pain, reminding her that "the LORD had closed her womb."

Hannah's response highlights the depth of her grief: she wept and could not eat. Even her husband Elkanah's gentle words could not heal her sorrow. The relentless provocation stripped away every earthly source of comfort, leaving Hannah with nowhere to turn except the altar of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 1:7 — "And so it was, year by year, when she went up to the house of the LORD, that she provoked her; therefore she wept and did not eat."

  • 1 Kings 21:22 — "I will make your house like the house of Jeroboam the son of Nebat... because of the provocation with which you have provoked Me to anger, and made Israel sin."

  • Nehemiah 4:5 — "Do not cover their iniquity, and do not let their sin be blotted out from before You; for they have provoked You to anger before the builders."

  • Psalms 106:29 — "Thus they provoked Him to anger with their deeds, and the plague broke out among them."

  • Ecclesiastes 5:17 — "All his days he also eats in darkness, and he has much sorrow and sickness and anger."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between Peninnah and Hannah serves a major purpose in the narrative of 1 Samuel. Peninnah had children, yet her character produced strife and cruelty. Hannah had no children, yet her suffering drove her to humble, persistent prayer.

God used this crucible of provocation to prepare Hannah to surrender her future son fully to divine service. Samuel's eventual birth was not merely the resolution of a domestic conflict, but the answer to a nation's need for a faithful prophet and judge. Through the grief caused by Peninnah's provocation, God worked to bring about a turning point in Israel's history.
