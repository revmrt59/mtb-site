RIVAL - (tsârâh, Strong's H6869)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun tsarah basic meaning refers to distress, trouble, pressure, or tight affliction. It comes from a root that describes being squeezed into a tight or narrow place where there is no easy escape. While the word usually describes severe trial or painful hardship, in 1 Samuel 1:6 it is applied directly to a person. Here, it refers to Peninnah as Hannah’s "rival" wife, highlighting her role as an active source of constant emotional distress and painful pressure within Hannah’s home.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The opening chapter of 1 Samuel introduces the household of Elkanah, a Levite living in the hill country of Ephraim. Elkanah had two wives: Hannah, whom he loved deeply, and Peninnah. Hannah was unable to have children, whereas Peninnah had borne sons and daughters. In ancient Israelite culture, childlessness brought heavy social stigma and personal grief.

Although polygamy was practiced in the ancient Near East, Scripture repeatedly shows that departing from God's original pattern for marriage created bitter domestic friction. In this family, Peninnah used her fruitfulness to mock and humiliate Hannah year after year, especially during their annual family trips to worship at the tabernacle in Shiloh. The word tsarah captures the heavy atmosphere of hostility and emotional confinement that surrounded Hannah in her own home.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The fundamental meaning of tsarah is distress, trouble, or severe pressure. It picture a situation where a person feels hemmed in, crushed, or squeezed by difficult circumstances.

In most of its Old Testament uses, the word refers to an abstract condition—a time of deep sorrow, a period of national calamity, or an overwhelming personal crisis. However, in 1 Samuel 1:6, the word is used in a specialized concrete sense to describe a rival wife. By calling Peninnah Hannah's tsarah, the text identifies her as an adversary whose cruel behavior turned Hannah's everyday life into a constant state of affliction and distress.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, tsarah is frequently paired with themes of trouble and deliverance. It describes the intense inner pain of grief, the terror of surrounding enemies, or the sudden hardship that forces a person to look to God for help.

When applied to human relationships, the word stresses the crushing effect one person's malice can have on another. Peninnah did not merely hold a different position in the family; she acted as an intentional adversary. Her relentless teasing pressed upon Hannah’s broken heart, turning a painful physical trial—barrenness—into an overwhelming spiritual and emotional burden.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of tsarah in Hannah’s home illustrates how human sin and broken family structures generate real suffering. God does not ignore this pain. The narrative of 1 Samuel shows that God sees the affliction of the downtrodden and hears their cries.

Hannah’s acute distress became the very catalyst that drove her to extreme, heartfelt prayer before the LORD. Rather than retaliating against her rival or giving up in despair, she poured out her soul at the sanctuary. God met her in her tsarah, granted her request, and gave her a son, Samuel, whom He used to bring spiritual renewal to the entire nation. This reveals a central biblical truth: God often uses moments of intense pressure to move His people toward deep prayer and to unfold His redemptive plans.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:6, the word functions as a feminine singular noun to designate Peninnah specifically in relation to Hannah: "And her rival also provoked her severely, to make her miserable, because the LORD had closed her womb."

The text highlights a cruel irony. The yearly journey to Shiloh was meant to be a joyful celebration of God's goodness, yet Peninnah seized those sacred occasions to inflict maximum emotional pain. The word tsarah emphasizes that Peninnah's actions were designed to provoke Hannah and make her feel trapped in her misery. The verse connects Hannah’s heavy trial directly to her rival’s continuous harassment, showing why Hannah wept and could not eat.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 35:3 — "Then let us arise and go up to Bethel; and I will make an altar there to God, who answered me in the day of my distress and has been with me in the way which I have gone."

  • 1 Kings 1:29 — "And the king took an oath and said, 'As the LORD lives, who has redeemed my life out of every distress...'"

  • Psalms 37:39 — "But the salvation of the righteous is from the LORD; He is their strength in the time of trouble."

  • Proverbs 17:17 — "A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity."

  • Isaiah 33:2 — "O LORD, be gracious to us; we have waited for You. Be their arm every morning, our salvation also in the time of trouble."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding tsarah as "distress" or "trouble" reveals the depth of Hannah's victory. Peninnah had children, status, and social leverage, while Hannah had only grief and severe pressure. Yet Peninnah fades from the biblical storyline almost immediately, while Hannah’s prayerful response to her distress produced one of Israel's greatest prophets and leaders.

When believers face intense pressure or hostility from others, Hannah's example demonstrates that bringing private distress directly to God in honest prayer is the true path to peace and deliverance.
