WOMB - (rechem, Strong's H7358)

Summary:

The Hebrew word rechem refers to the womb or uterus, the organ of childbearing and human origin. Throughout the Old Testament, it identifies the place where human life begins under God's sovereign control and creative design. In 1 Samuel 1:6, the word describes Hannah's physical inability to conceive children, emphasizing that her barrenness was governed by the LORD's sovereign oversight. This condition became the occasion for her rival's cruel mockery, which ultimately drove Hannah to pour out her heart before God in persistent prayer.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israelite society, family legacy, covenant continuity, and personal honor were deeply connected to childbearing. A household without children faced not only emotional distress but also social vulnerability and the loss of family inheritance. Barrenness was often felt as a severe personal affliction and public trial.

The opening chapter of 1 Samuel introduces Elkanah's family, where two wives present a sharp contrast: Peninnah had children, but Hannah had none. In 1 Samuel 1:6, the narrative notes that the LORD had closed Hannah's womb. This reality forms the setting for the bitter conflict between Peninnah and Hannah, serving as the starting point for God's larger work in bringing forth Samuel, the prophet who would guide Israel through a critical transition in its history.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun rechem refers to the female womb or uterus as the bodily organ of gestation. Grammatically in this verse, the noun appears in a singular form with common gender. Beyond its basic anatomical sense, the word is used in Scripture to designate the very beginning of human existence.

The word can refer to:

  • The physical organ of conception and birth in women.

  • The maternal source from which a child emerges into the world.

  • Metaphorically, the place of origin or formation where God fashions a human being.

In Biblical thought, the function of the womb is never viewed as a purely mechanical or biological process detached from God. Instead, the opening or closing of the womb is consistently recognized as being under the authority of the Creator.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, rechem appears in several distinct contexts:

  • Patriarchal Narratives: God's involvement with the womb is central to the covenant promises. The opening or closing of a womb is used to direct lineage and demonstrate divine intervention, as seen in the lives of Sarah, Leah, and Rachel.

  • Mosaic Law: The phrase "whatever opens the womb" carries specific legal and worship significance. God claimed the firstborn of both people and clean livestock as holy to Him, setting them apart to commemorate Israel's deliverance from Egypt.

  • Wisdom and Prophetic Writings: Writers use the word to reflect on God's foundational work in human creation. God forms individuals from the womb, knows them before their birth, and sets them apart for His purposes.

In each of these settings, the word emphasizes human dependence on God at life's origin.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical usage of rechem reveals key truths about God's character and providence:

  • Divine Sovereignty over Creation: God maintains ultimate authority over human fertility and biological processes. Life is a gift directly governed by God rather than governed by chance.

  • Purpose in Painful Seasons: The closure of a womb in Scripture often serves a divine purpose that human participants cannot initially see. God uses seasons of waiting and hardship to prepare His servants for significant works.

  • The Power of Prayer: Human helplessness regarding the womb frequently turns God's people toward earnest prayer, demonstrating that divine sovereignty invites faithful petition rather than resignation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:6, the narrator repeats a crucial phrase first stated in verse 5: "because the LORD had closed her womb." This repetition highlights both the direct source of Hannah's grief and the catalyst for Peninnah's hostility. Peninnah exploited Hannah's barrenness to provoke her severely and make her miserable year after year.

The narrative explicitly attributes the closing of Hannah's womb to the LORD. This establishes an essential framing for the whole story. Hannah's suffering was not an accident of nature, nor was it a sign that God had abandoned her. Rather, God restrained her fertility for a season to accomplish a greater redemptive work.

Peninnah used Hannah's closed womb to bring distress, but Hannah responded by taking her pain to the tabernacle at Shiloh. The closed womb became the very instrument God used to lead Hannah into deep, desperate prayer, resulting in her vow to dedicate her child entirely to the LORD's service.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 29:31 — "When the LORD saw that Leah was unloved, He opened her womb; but Rachel was barren."

  • Exodus 13:2 — "Sanctify to Me all the firstborn, whatever opens the womb among the children of Israel, both of man and beast; it is Mine."

  • Job 31:15 — "Did not He who made me in the womb make them? Did not the same One fashion us in the womb?"

  • Jeremiah 20:17 — "Because he did not kill me from the womb, that my mother might have been my grave and her womb always enlarged with me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The noun rechem is closely related in its underlying root to the vocabulary for deep compassion, mercy, and tender care (rachamim). In Hebrew thought, the emotional depth of intense, protective compassion is mirrored in the natural devotion associated with maternal care.

Furthermore, the resolution of Hannah's closed womb in 1 Samuel 1 leads directly to her famous song of praise in 1 Samuel 2. There she exclaims that "the barren has borne seven" (1 Samuel 2:5), celebrating how God reverses human fortunes, lifts up the humble, and displays His sovereignty over life and death.
