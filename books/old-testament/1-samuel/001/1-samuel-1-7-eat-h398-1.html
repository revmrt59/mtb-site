EAT - (ʼâkal, Strong's H398)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb akal means to consume food, partake of a meal, or metaphorically to devour. In 1 Samuel 1:7, the word describes Hannah's complete loss of appetite caused by intense emotional grief and relentless provocation during her family's annual sacrificial feast at Shiloh.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb akal is the standard Hebrew word for eating or consuming food. It appears across all eras of Old Testament history and describes everything from everyday meals and sacred covenant feasts to metaphorical consuming, such as fire devouring wood or judgment consuming a land.

In 1 Samuel 1, the setting is the annual family pilgrimage to Shiloh, where Israel gathered at the tabernacle to offer sacrifices and worship God. These occasions included a fellowship meal where family members sat together to eat portions of the sacrificed peace offering. For most families, this was a time of joy, gratitude, and communal fellowship. However, for Hannah, it became an annual season of deep distress due to her barrenness and the constant taunting of her rival, Peninnah.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its simplest level, akal refers to the physical act of eating food for nourishment. Depending on who or what is performing the action, the word carries several shades of meaning:

  • To partake of everyday food or bread.

  • To participate in a ceremonial or sacrificial meal.

  • To devour or consume completely, as when used metaphorically of fire, disease, sword, or wild animals.

In 1 Samuel 1:7, the verb refers to literal eating, but in a negative context: Hannah "did not eat." Her emotional distress was so overwhelming that she was unable or unwilling to partake of the sacrificial meal.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, eating is deeply connected to spiritual and social life. Shared meals signified peace, fellowship, and divine blessing. To eat in the presence of the LORD at Shiloh was meant to express joy for God's provision and covenant faithful care.

When extreme sorrow strikes, loss of appetite often follows. Scripture frequently connects deep grief, fasting, or anguish of spirit with the inability or refusal to eat. Grammatically, the verb form used in 1 Samuel 1:7 is feminine, singular, and imperfect, reflecting an ongoing or repeated action—year after year, when the family went up to Shiloh, this painful pattern recurred: Hannah wept and would not eat.

4. Theological Implications

The use of akal in this passage highlights a striking contrast between external religious routine and internal heart pain.

  • Sacrificial meals were designed by God as festive celebrations of His covenant goodness.

  • Cruelty and provocation within a household can turn a sacred feast into a place of bitter grief.

  • God pays attention to those who cannot eat because of sorrow. Hannah's inability to participate in the joyous eating did not alienate her from God; instead, her grief drove her to direct, honest prayer in the tabernacle.

Hannah's sorrowful abstinence from food functioned as an unforced, grief-driven fast that preceded her passionate prayer for a child.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:7, the text notes that "as often as she went up to the house of the LORD, so she provoked her; therefore she wept and did not eat." Elkanah gave Hannah a double portion of the sacrificial meat because he loved her, but food could not heal her broken heart.

Peninnah used these sacred trips to mock Hannah's barrenness, turning what should have been a time of holy gladness into a painful trial. Hannah's refusal to eat was not a petulant strike, but a natural physiological reaction to severe emotional burden. Her appetite was completely taken away by her anguish. Elkanah asked her why she was crying and why she would not eat, offering his love as comfort. Yet Hannah took her burden where Elkanah could not reach—straight to God in silent, desperate prayer.

  • Psalms 78:25 — "Men ate angels' food; He sent them food to the full."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's inability to eat at Shiloh sets the stage for God's powerful work in her life. While others were eating and drinking at the feast, Hannah rose in the presence of the LORD to pour out her soul. Eli the priest initially misjudged her silent, sorrowful praying, assuming she was drunk from the feast. Hannah corrected him, explaining that she was not drunk, but deeply troubled.

God heard her sorrowful cry, answered her prayer, and gave her a son, Samuel. Later in the narrative, after God answered her prayer and she dedicated Samuel to the LORD, her weeping and sorrow were transformed into a song of praise and gladness.
