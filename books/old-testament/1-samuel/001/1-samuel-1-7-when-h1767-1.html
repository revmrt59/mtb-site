WHEN - (day, Strong's H1767)

Summary:

The Hebrew word day basic meaning refers to sufficiency, a measured amount, or what is needed for a specific requirement. When combined with prepositional prefixes, it often indicates a regular measure of time or frequency, translated as "as often as," "whenever," or "when." In 1 Samuel 1:7, the word is translated "when" to describe the painful cycle in Hannah's life. It marks every recurring pilgrimage to the tabernacle at Shiloh, highlighting how Hannah's ongoing barrenness and her rival Peninnah's cruel taunts turned expected seasons of sacred celebration into regular, severe grief.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word day comes from ancient Hebrew usage describing exact proportions, measures, or supplies. In its simplest form, it answers the question of whether a person or group has enough for a given task, sacrifice, or daily need. Rather than signifying an abstract concept, it relates directly to tangible resources, such as food, financial capability, or the required amount of materials for a project.

In Israel's worship and daily life, exact standards and appropriate measures were important. Whether assessing a family's financial capability to bring a sacrifice or calculating the building supplies needed for the tabernacle, day provided a way to express adequacy and proportion. Over time, the word also developed a temporal usage. When linked with prepositions of comparison or origin, it describes an action occurring according to the full measure of a repeated interval—meaning "as often as" or "whenever" a particular event took place.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word day can refer to a sufficient quantity, a required measure, or a adequate supply.

When a person or community possesses day, they have enough to cover their present necessity. For example, it describes having sufficient food to eat or having enough materials to complete a work of construction.

When used to describe personal financial capacity, the word refers to what a person is able to afford. In the sacrificial laws, God adjusted required offerings based on an individual's financial ability, measuring what was required by what the person could reasonably provide.

When used in time expressions, the word functions idiomatically to describe frequency or regular recurrence. In these settings, it means "as often as," "whenever," or "at the return of" a specific moment or activity. Here in 1 Samuel 1:7, the word takes on this temporal force, indicating that the action occurred reliably every time the annual event arrived.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament historical books, laws, and wisdom literature, day appears in two main ways: quantitative measure and recurring frequency.

In legal and practical contexts, the word describes physical sufficiency. In the construction of the tabernacle, the people brought gifts until the leaders announced there was enough material to finish the work. Similarly, laws concerning redemption and property rights used the word to determine whether a debtor had saved enough to buy back lost family land. In these settings, the emphasis rests on reaching a needed threshold.

In narrative passages, the word frequently forms a time phrase indicating steady repetition. When describing royal routines, military developments, or regular religious journeys, the word marks actions that occur every single time a specific trigger happens. It stresses that the action was not an isolated incident, but a predictable, unbroken pattern.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of day brings out important biblical truths about God's provision and human experience.

First, God scales His expectations to human capacity. In the Mosaic law, God allowed poorer worshipers to bring smaller sacrifices if they lacked the means for a costly animal. By anchoring worship requirements to a person's actual capability, Scripture shows that God values genuine devotion over commercial value. He makes access to fellowship with Him available to everyone, regardless of wealth.

Second, the temporal use of day reflects how suffering and spiritual trial often occur in predictable cycles. In 1 Samuel, the word underlines the painful repetition of trial within religious life. The annual feasts at Shiloh were designed to be joyful occasions of feasting and thanksgiving for God's blessings. Yet for Hannah, these fixed worship seasons became painful reminders of her ongoing struggle. Scripture does not hide the truth that spiritual duties and sacred calendar events can sometimes intersect with deeply felt personal pain.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:7, the narrative describes the domestic conflict within the household of Elkanah. Elkanah had two wives: Peninnah, who had children, and Hannah, who had none. Year after year, the family traveled up to the house of the LORD at Shiloh to offer sacrifices.

The word translated "when" marks the exact trigger for Hannah's recurring agony. Every time the family made the journey to Shiloh, Peninnah would intentionally provoke Hannah to irritate her because of her barrenness. The word emphasizes that this cruelty was not an occasional outburst; it happened with clockwork regularity. Whenever the annual pilgrimage came around, the emotional assault restarted.

This word creates a sharp contrast between the intended purpose of the trip and Hannah's reality. Shiloh was the place of divine blessing, corporate worship, and sacrificial feasting. Yet "when" she went up—at the exact point where she should have experienced holy joy—she was driven to tears and unable to eat. The word highlights the unbearable weight of a trial that repeats without relief every time a holy season arrives.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Grammatically, the word in 1 Samuel 1:7 carries singular number and common gender. This single form functions prepositionally to establish a temporal boundary: each annual journey brought the exact same tragic dynamic.

It is worth noting that Hannah's persistent sorrow took place within the place of worship. The text does not hide the fact that the sanctuary itself became the backdrop for her deep distress. The word "when" bridges her private agony at home with her public experience at the tabernacle. It was precisely this unbroken pattern of grief at Shiloh that ultimately drove Hannah to pour out her soul directly before the LORD in silent, desperate prayer, leading to God's gracious answer in the birth of Samuel.
