BETTER - (ṭôwb, Strong's H2896)

Summary:

The word tob describes what is good, pleasant, beneficial, or superior in quality or value. When used in a comparative sense, it means "better" or preferred above an alternative.

In 1 Samuel 1:8, Elkanah asks his sorrowful wife Hannah if his personal devotion to her is "better" to her than having ten sons. While spoken out of genuine affection, his question highlights the limitation of human love in resolving a deep spiritual and physical grief that only God could address.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew word tob is one of the foundational terms in Old Testament vocabulary. It touches every sphere of ancient Israelite life, describing physical beauty, material value, moral integrity, practical wisdom, and comparative worth.

In 1 Samuel 1, the narrative opens with the distress of Hannah, who was childless and subjected to ongoing provocation by Peninnah, Elkanah's other wife. During their family's annual trip to worship at Shiloh, Elkanah gave Hannah a double portion because he loved her, yet Hannah wept and could not eat. Elkanah approached her with tender concern, asking why she was weeping and why her heart was grieved. He ended his gentle inquiry with the question: "Am I not better to you than ten sons?"

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of tob is "good." It refers to that which is beneficial, pleasant, high-quality, or intrinsically right. Depending on the setting, it can refer to:

  • Physical appearance or quality (beautiful, fine, pleasing).

  • Functional usefulness or benefit (good, profitable, best).

  • Moral goodness or ethical rightness (upright, good).

  • Comparative worth or superiority (better, preferable).

In 1 Samuel 1:8, tob functions in this comparative sense. Elkanah compares the value of his devoted love as a husband to the joy and security of having ten sons, asking if his care ought to be viewed as superior or more satisfying in her eyes.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, tob appears in several distinct settings:

  • Divine Creation: God repeatedly views His created work and declares it "good," meaning it is complete, orderly, and functioning according to His holy design.

  • Material and Practical Excellence: The word describes rich land, pure gold, sweet spices, or pleasant news.

  • Moral and Relational Uprightness: It characterizes individuals and actions that align with God's commands and bring blessing to others.

  • Comparative Wisdom and Value: In Wisdom literature and narrative comparisons, tob contrasts two options to show which is preferable. For example, wisdom is declared better than earthly riches, and quiet peace is better than conflict.

In 1 Samuel 1:8, the comparative nuance is central. Elkanah uses tob to measure the value of marital affection against the gift of offspring.

4. Theological Implications

Elkanah’s question brings out important truths regarding human comfort and divine help:

  • Marital affection is a genuine blessing from God, but it cannot replace every God-given longing. Elkanah was a devoted husband, yet his love could not fulfill Hannah's natural and godly desire for motherhood.

  • Human sympathy has real limits. Elkanah attempted to console Hannah by pointing to his own goodness toward her, but genuine relief for her deep anguish required seeking God directly.

  • Hannah's response demonstrates that when human comfort falls short, believers are invited to pour out their souls before the Lord in prayer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:8, Elkanah speaks to Hannah out of sincere care, attempting to ease her emotional burden. However, his question reveals a lack of full understanding regarding the depth of her sorrow.

In ancient Israel, having children—especially sons—was associated with family continuity, protection, social standing, and divine covenant blessing. Ten sons represented an abundance of blessing and total family security. When Elkanah asked if he was "better" to her than ten sons, he was attempting to weigh his personal devotion against the full measure of family inheritance and societal security.

Authoritative grammatical details identify the target word in this verse as singular in number and common in gender, describing Elkanah's self-assessment as he speaks to Hannah.

While Elkanah hoped his love would be enough to quiet her tears, Hannah recognized that her sorrow was a matter to lay before the Almighty. She did not argue with her husband; instead, she went to the tabernacle at Shiloh to pour out her soul in prayer to the Lord of hosts.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Compassion without minimization: Elkanah's intentions were noble, but his question inadvertently minimized Hannah's grief. Effective pastoral care listens attentively to sorrow without attempting to quickly substitute one earthly comfort for another.

  • Prayer as the ultimate refuge: Hannah's quiet departure to pray shows that she did not let Elkanah's well-meaning words stop her from seeking God's divine action. Her prayer resulted in the birth of Samuel, showing that God hears the cries of the afflicted.
