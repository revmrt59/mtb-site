EAT - (ʼâkal, Strong's H398)

Summary:

Akal is the standard Hebrew verb for eating, partaking of food, or devouring. It ranges from describing daily human sustenance and sacred feasting to metaphorical descriptions of destruction and judgment. In 1 Samuel 1:8, the word describes Hannah's total loss of appetite due to intense emotional anguish over her childlessness during a communal sacrificial meal at Shiloh. Her refusal to eat signals deep grief that human comfort cannot cure, driving her ultimately to silent, desperate prayer before the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, sharing a meal was far more than a routine physical necessity. Meals were central to family life, hospitality, and religious life. Sacred feasts, particularly those held at the central sanctuary in Shiloh, were joyful occasions where families gathered to worship God, offer peace offerings, and feast together in God's presence. Partaking of the sacrificial meat was a visible sign of fellowship, gratitude, and communal joy.

When Hannah abstains from eating at Shiloh, her choice breaks the expectation of communal celebration. While others around her celebrate, her grief over her closed womb and the constant provocation from Peninnah leaves her unable to participate in the feast. Her refusal to eat marks a breakdown of personal joy at a time when the whole community was expected to rejoice.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Akal describes the act of taking in food, consuming sustenance, or devouring. When used of human beings, it refers to eating meals for physical life and strength. When used of animals, it refers to devouring prey or grazing. In broader contexts, it describes fire consuming a sacrifice or judgment sweeping away a land.

In 1 Samuel 1:8, the verb refers directly to taking part in the physical meal prepared from the family sacrificial offerings. Hannah's inability to eat is not a ritual prohibition or a formal fast, but a direct physical result of extreme sorrow taking away her appetite.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, Akal carries several clear shades of meaning:

  • Physical Sustenance: It refers to daily meals, eating bread, and gathering God's provision for life.

  • Sacred Fellowship: It describes eating sacrifices in the presence of God, where partaking of holy food signifies communion with the Lord and His priests.

  • Refusal and Mourning: Abstaining from eating frequently accompanies deep grief, fasting, or repentance, reflecting a soul too heavy to receive physical nourishment.

  • Destruction or Devouring: In judgment contexts, the word describes enemy nations, wild animals, or divine fire devouring what stands in their path.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's inability to eat during a holy feast brings out an important truth about worship and human suffering. God does not demand that His people suppress genuine sorrow to maintain a outward show of piety. Feasting at Shiloh was intended to be joyful, yet God acknowledges Hannah's honest pain in the holy place.

Furthermore, the passage highlights the limitations of human sympathy. Elkanah offers material portions and emotional reassurances, asking why she does not eat. Yet his comfort falls short because he cannot fix her underlying pain. Hannah's loss of appetite pushes her past human consolation and drives her directly to the throne of God, showing that spiritual distress often forces a believer to seek God alone for true relief.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:8, Elkanah addresses his wife with four sharp questions: "Hannah, why do you weep? Why do you not eat? And why is your heart grieved? Am I not better to you than ten sons?" The verb appears in a singular, feminine, imperfect form, describing Hannah's ongoing, present state of refusing food while sitting at the table.

Elkanah notices her empty plate and visible tears. Her rival, Peninnah, had provoked her severely year after year precisely at the time of the sacrificial trip. The feast, which should have been a high point of spiritual joy, had become an annual reminder of Hannah's barrenness. Her grief was so severe that her body physically rejected food. Elkanah's questions show genuine affection, but also a lack of understanding regarding the depth of a woman's anguish over childlessness in Israelite society. Her refusal to eat serves as the turning point that moves her from the dining table to the sanctuary door, where she pours out her soul before the Lord.

  • Jeremiah 15:3 — "'And I will appoint over them four forms of destruction,' says the LORD: 'the sword to slay, the dogs to drag, the birds of the heavens and the beasts of the earth to devour and destroy.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's empty plate sets up a powerful contrast in the first chapter of 1 Samuel. While Elkanah gives her a double portion of meat out of love, the physical food remains untouched. Immediately after this encounter, Hannah rises after they had finished eating and drinking at Shiloh (1 Samuel 1:9) to pray. While she could not consume the physical meal, her grief became the catalyst for intense spiritual fellowship with God. Her physical emptiness prepared her to be filled with divine promise and peace long before her physical womb was opened.
