GRIEVED - (yâraʻ, Strong's H3415)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yara refers to something being bad, displeasing, or troublesome, and when paired with "heart," it describes a spirit burdened by pain, grief, or distress. In 1 Samuel 1:8, Elkanah uses this word to ask Hannah why her heart is so deeply grieved during their annual worship meal, highlighting the intense emotional pain caused by her childlessness and ongoing provocation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew word yara belongs to a word group that broadly expresses badness, harm, injury, or distress. It can refer to physical hardship, moral wrong, or internal grief. In ancient Israelite thought, the inner life was often described through physical expressions, so a situation that caused emotional distress was spoken of as being "bad" or "evil" to a person's heart.

In the setting of 1 Samuel 1, the nation of Israel is living near the end of the period of the judges. Hannah, one of Elkanah's two wives, faces severe distress. She is unable to have children, and her rival Peninnah relentlessly taunts her to provoke her to frustration. This distress peaks during the family's annual trip to Shiloh to offer sacrifices and feast before the LORD. Instead of enjoying the worship feast, Hannah's sorrow overwhelms her, prompting her husband Elkanah to address her broken condition.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yara can refer to an action, situation, or condition that is displeasing, harmful, troublesome, or painful. In many contexts, it describes something that causes displeasure to human leaders or to God.

When yara is joined with the word "heart," the focus shifts to internal pain and emotional grief. Rather than pointing to moral evil, the combination describes a heart that is aching, heavy, or burdened by sorrow. In 1 Samuel 1:8, the word describes a deep emotional wound. Hannah's heart is not morally wrong; rather, it is crushed under the weight of severe grief and despair.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, yara is used in contexts where something causes offense, disturbance, or trouble. It frequently describes an event or decision that displease an authority figure or the LORD Himself. In situations involving personal conflict or national crisis, the word carries the sense of harm or trouble.

When applied to personal emotions, yara captures how deeply painful experiences affect a person's spirit. It is not a shallow sadness, but a heavy distress that disrupts normal daily life. In Hannah's case, this inner trouble takes away her desire to eat and turns what should be a joyful religious feast into a place of weeping.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's heavy heart reveals that genuine faith does not shield believers from deep sorrow or grief. She was faithful in accompanying her family to worship the LORD at Shiloh, yet her pain was real and overwhelming.

Scripture does not condemn Hannah for her grieved heart. Her response to her trouble sets an important pattern: rather than turning away from God in bitterness, she brings her sorrow directly to Him in prayer. The passage also demonstrates the limits of human comfort. Elkanah loved Hannah deeply and offered extra portions of sacrificial meat, but his devotion could not heal her inner ache. Ultimate relief for a grieved heart must come from God alone.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:8, Elkanah sees his wife weeping and refusing to eat during the sacrificial meal. He asks her four questions: "Hannah, why do you weep? Why do you not eat? And why is your heart grieved? Am I not better to you than ten sons?"

The word grieved appears in a masculine, singular, imperfect form, agreeing grammatically with the noun "heart." Elkanah recognizes that Hannah's distress is not merely external weeping or a loss of physical appetite; the trouble reaches into her very heart. His question acknowledges that her inner spirit is heavy with grief. Although Elkanah's final question shows his inability to fully grasp the unique pain of her barrenness, his initial concern highlights how visible and profound her heart's grief was. This deep affliction drives Hannah straight to the tabernacle door, where she pours out her soul to the LORD.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Later in the chapter, when Eli the priest mistakes Hannah's silent praying for drunkenness, Hannah explains her true condition by saying she is "a woman of a sorrowful spirit" who has been pouring out her soul before the LORD (1 Samuel 1:15). The description of her heart as grieved in verse 8 sets up this crucial interaction. Her inner pain was not a sign of rebellion or spiritual weakness, but the very catalyst that drove her to persistent, honest prayer before God.
