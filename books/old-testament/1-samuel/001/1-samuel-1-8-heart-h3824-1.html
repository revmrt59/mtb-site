HEART - (lêbâb, Strong's H3824)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun lebab refers to the inner core of a human being, encompassing the seat of thoughts, emotions, desires, and the will. Rather than referring strictly to the physical organ, Scripture uses this term for the invisible inner person where deep feelings are experienced and choices are made. In 1 Samuel 1:8, the word describes Hannah's inner grief and deep emotional distress over her barrenness, highlighting the profound pain she carried inside before taking her sorrow directly to the Lord in prayer.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The opening chapter of 1 Samuel takes place during the period of the judges, a time of spiritual instability in Israel. Each year, Elkanah traveled with his family to the tabernacle at Shiloh to worship and offer sacrifices. Within his household, however, sat a painful family dynamic. Elkanah had two wives: Peninnah, who had children, and Hannah, who was childless because the Lord had closed her womb.

Peninnah provoked Hannah severely year after year to make her miserable, turning what should have been a joyful sacrificial feast into a time of deep anguish. Hannah wept and could not eat. In 1 Samuel 1:8, Elkanah observes her distress and asks why her heart is grieved. The word used here for her inner self is lebab, bringing the reader past her external tears into the private sanctuary of her inner pain.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun lebab refers to the inner person. In ancient Hebrew thought, the heart was not viewed merely as an emotion-producing organ, but as the comprehensive center of human personality and consciousness.

The word can refer to several dimensions of the inner life:

  • The seat of emotions, including joy, fear, courage, or severe grief.

  • The seat of intellect and reflection, where a person thinks, plans, and remembers.

  • The seat of the will and decision-making, where moral choices and commitments are formed.

In 1 Samuel 1:8, the focus is on the emotional life. Here the word means the inner self as the place where sorrow and grief are deeply felt.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, writers use lebab to describe the entire internal reality of a person. When individuals experience profound sadness, Scripture describes that pain as reaching the lebab. Physical actions like weeping or fasting flow out from what is occurring within the inner person.

In Hannah's situation, her grief was not a superficial bad mood or temporary irritation. It was an overwhelming sorrow seated in her lebab. Her husband noticed that this inner distress affected her physical appetite and outward behavior. The passage illustrates that true sorrow resides deep within, beyond what simple external comforting words can cure.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical depiction of the lebab carries important theological truths regarding how God relates to people:

  • God sees and cares about the inner life. Humans react to outward tears, but God beholds the underlying heart.

  • Human comfort has limits. Elkanah loved Hannah and tried to comfort her by offering a double portion of meat and asking, "Am I not better to you than ten sons?" Yet his well-meaning words could not reach or heal her grieved inner self.

  • Heavy heart sorrow can drive a believer to honest worship. Hannah did not let her inner grief curdle into bitter resentment toward God; instead, she brought her grieved heart directly to the tabernacle, pouring out her soul before the Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:8, Elkanah asks four questions to uncover the reason for Hannah's sorrow: "Why do you weep? Why do you not eat? And why is your heart grieved? Am I not better to you than ten sons?" The noun lebab is grammatically singular and common in gender, focusing attention specifically on Hannah's individual inner condition.

Elkanah's final question reveals a common human limitation: he focused on his own devotion to her, hoping his love would be enough to compensate for her childlessness. But Hannah's grieved lebab pointed to a longing that only the Lord could fulfill. The context contrasts Elkanah's well-meaning yet incomplete consolation with Hannah's decision to lay her broken heart before God in persistent, silent prayer.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The same Hebrew word appears in various contexts throughout Scripture to highlight different aspects of the inner life:

  • Deuteronomy 4:29 — "But from there you will seek the LORD your God, and you will find Him if you seek Him with all your heart and with all your soul."

  • 1 Samuel 12:24 — "Only fear the LORD, and serve Him in truth with all your heart; for consider what great things He has done for you."

  • 2 Chronicles 16:9 — "For the eyes of the LORD run to and fro throughout the whole earth, to show Himself strong on behalf of those whose heart is loyal to Him."

  • Psalms 78:72 — "So he shepherded them according to the integrity of his heart, and guided them by the skillfulness of his hands."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A notable contrast exists in 1 Samuel 1 between how God understands the human heart and how human leaders misjudge it. While Hannah was praying silently from her grieved lebab, moving her lips without making a sound, Eli the priest observed her outward appearance and concluded she was drunk.

Eli judged her by external sight, but Hannah corrected him, explaining that she was a woman of a sorrowful spirit pouring out her soul before the Lord. This comparison highlights that human observers often misinterpret the deep groanings of the lebab, whereas the Lord hears and responds to the quietest cries of a heavy heart.
