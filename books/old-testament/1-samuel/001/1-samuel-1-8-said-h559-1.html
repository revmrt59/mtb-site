SAID - (ʼâmar, Strong's H559)

Summary:

In the Old Testament, 'amar is the basic verb meaning to say, speak, declare, or think. It is most frequently used to introduce direct spoken words or inner thoughts. In 1 Samuel 1:8, the word introduces Elkanah's direct appeal to his grieving wife Hannah, bringing his tender but humanly limited attempt at comfort into the narrative.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The book of 1 Samuel begins in a time of spiritual dark and personal distress in Israel. The opening chapter focuses on an ordinary family traveling annually to Shiloh to worship at the tabernacle. Within this family, deep emotional pain exists: Hannah is childless, while her husband’s second wife, Peninnah, has children and provokes Hannah ruthlessly.

In 1 Samuel 1:8, the grammatical form of the verb is masculine singular, appearing as a wayyiqtol (imperfect) form. This construction carries the narrative forward sequentially: "Then Elkanah her husband said to her..." The word sets the scene for a conversation in which a husband notices his wife's sorrow and attempts to address her broken heart directly.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

outputs plain speech. The verb can mean to say, speak, utter, declare, or intend. It is the primary Hebrew verb used throughout the Old Testament to report what someone actually spoke aloud or whispered internally.

While other Hebrew verbs focus on the act or volume of speaking (such as shouting or making a formal speech), 'amar simple serves as the doorway into the specific words of the speaker. It answers the question, "What did the speaker say?" It can describe God issuing a sovereign command, a king issuing a decree, a friend offering advice, or a person reasoning within his or her own heart.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, 'amar appears in several key contexts:

  • Direct Quotation: The standard function of the verb is to introduce exact quotes from individuals, whether kings, prophets, ordinary citizens, or angels.

  • Divine Commands: When God speaks creation into existence or issues commands to His prophets, the Bible uses this verb to show divine authority communicated in clear speech.

  • Internal Dialogue: Hebrew frequently uses 'amar paired with "in his heart" to express silent thoughts, decisions, or self-reflection.

  • Official Declarations: On occasion, the context turns spoken words into an official pledge or legal affirmation, such as when Israel or God formally declares covenant commitment.

4. Theological Implications

Speech in Scripture is powerful. God reveals Himself not through silent, abstract principles, but through spoken revelation. When God speaks, reality changes: light appears, covenants are formed, and promises are guaranteed.

Human speech, however, shows both the capacity for genuine compassion and the limitations of creaturely comfort. Human words can express real love, yet they often lack the power to fix deeper spiritual or emotional suffering. Elkanah's speech demonstrates that well-meaning human affection cannot substitute for God's divine answer. Ultimately, human speech must yield to divine speech, for only God's word carries the authority to create life out of emptiness.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:8, 'amar introduces Elkanah's sincere attempt to console Hannah: "Hannah, why do you weep? Why do you not eat? And why is your heart grieved? Am I not better to you than ten sons?"

Elkanah’s spoken words reveal both genuine affection and human misunderstanding. He notices her tears, her loss of appetite, and her deep sorrow. Yet his final question shows that he does not fully grasp the depth of her grief or her desire for a child. His words, though tenderly spoken, do not lift Hannah's burden.

The placement of 'amar here contrasts human speech with Hannah's later silent prayer before the LORD. Elkanah speaks to Hannah, but Hannah speaks to God. Elkanah's words bring no relief, but when Hannah pours out her soul to the LORD, she leaves with peace.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The narrative of 1 Samuel 1 creates a striking contrast around the act of speaking. In verse 8, Elkanah speaks aloud to comfort his wife. Later in the chapter, Eli the priest misjudges Hannah because her lips are moving while no sound is heard—she is speaking directly to God in her heart. Finally, the LORD speaks by answering her prayer, giving her a son named Samuel, whose name means "heard by God." The progression highlights that God hears the spoken and unspoken cries of the afflicted when human speech falls short.
