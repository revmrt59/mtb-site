SONS - (bên, Strong's H1121)

Summary:

The Hebrew word ben primarily refers to a male child, son, or male descendant, but it can also refer to children generally, young animals, or membership in a specific group. In 1 Samuel 1:8, Elkanah uses "sons" (ben) to comfort his wife Hannah in her grief over barrenness, asking if his personal devotion to her is not better than the social security and cultural honor provided by having ten sons.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the culture of ancient Israel, children—and male offspring in particular—were seen as vital blessings from God. A son carried forward the family name, inherited and maintained the family land, provided social and economic security for aging parents, and ensured the continuity of the covenant lineage. Barrenness was often felt as a deep personal tragedy and a source of societal shame.

In the historical setting of early 1 Samuel, Israel stood at a transitional moment before the establishment of the monarchy. Personal and familial inheritance mattered greatly for household survival. Within Elkanah’s household, the presence of children created relational tension. Peninnah had sons and daughters, while Hannah had none. Elkanah’s question to Hannah in 1 Samuel 1:8 addresses this painful contrast directly.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word ben fundamentally denotes a male offspring or son. However, the word carries a broad range of usage across the Hebrew Scriptures:

  • A direct male child or son in relation to parents.

  • A male descendant across multiple generations (grandson, great-grandson, or distant descendant).

  • Offspring or children in general, including both sons and daughters when used in the plural.

  • The young of animals, such as colts or lambs.

  • Idiomatically, a person characterized by a particular quality, age, or membership in a group (such as "son of peace" or "son of a year").

Here in 1 Samuel 1:8, the word refers specifically to male children as the ideal source of family legacy and security.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament historical books, poetry, and prophetic literature, ben describes family structures and covenant lines.

In family narratives, the word highlights inheritance and lineage, such as Jephthah’s brothers who drove him away from his inheritance or the genealogical lists of David’s household. When translated broadly as "children," the word points to whole generations carrying the weight of family history or divine instruction.

The word is also used for the young of livestock, demonstrating that the underlying concept involves offspring born to a parent. Whether describing human sons, whole generations, or young animals, the term emphasizes offspring who receive life, name, or identity from their source.

4. Theological Implications

Scripture consistently presents children as a gift and inheritance from the Lord. Sons brought strength, protection, and continuity to a household under God’s covenant promises.

At the same time, 1 Samuel 1:8 reveals that even good, god-given desires—like having sons—can become a source of overwhelming grief when unfulfilled. Elkanah attempts to offer his own covenantal love as a comfort, asking Hannah to recognize that his devotion holds real value even in the absence of children. The passage illustrates that human security and personal worth ultimately rest in God's care and the genuine relationships He provides, rather than strictly in societal status or familial abundance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:8, Elkanah addresses Hannah during their annual journey to the tabernacle at Shiloh. Distressed by Peninnah’s teasing and heartbroken by her inability to conceive, Hannah wept and refused to eat.

Elkanah asks four tender questions, ending with: "Am I not better to you than ten sons?" The number ten represents full abundance and complete family blessing. Elkanah uses "sons" in the masculine plural form to represent the maximum social ideal of a large, secure household.

While Elkanah’s question expresses deep affection, it also exposes the limit of human consolation. Elkanah’s love could not quiet Hannah’s deep longing to bear a child for the Lord. Her pain was not merely cultural; it was a deeply personal cry before God. Elkanah's reference to "sons" sets up the dramatic turn in the story where Hannah takes her sorrow directly to the Lord in prayer, leading to the birth of Samuel.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Elkanah’s phrase "ten sons" serves as a poetic expression for complete family fulfillment. A similar expression appears in Ruth 4:15, where Ruth’s devotion to Naomi is described as better than seven sons. In both cases, extreme numbers of sons represent the highest earthly security ancient Israelite society could imagine.

Elkanah’s gentle questioning demonstrates that well-meaning human comfort can fall short when a soul is carrying a spiritual burden. His question highlights the reality of human love while pointing toward Hannah’s true refuge: pouring out her soul before the Lord at the sanctuary.
