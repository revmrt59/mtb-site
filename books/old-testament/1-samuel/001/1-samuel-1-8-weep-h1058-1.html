WEEP - (bâkâh, Strong's H1058)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bakah fundamentally means to weep, shed tears, cry, or express sorrow through weeping. It describes a physical and emotional response to grief, pain, loss, or spiritual longing. In 1 Samuel 1:8, the word expresses Hannah's heartbreak over her childlessness and Peninnah's persistent provocation, capturing the deep grief that drove her to pour out her soul before the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israelite culture, weeping was an open, honest expression of grief, pain, or spiritual burden. Tears accompanied major personal and national events, including mourning the loss of loved ones, crying out under oppression, repenting before God, or expressing intense personal longing. The verb bakah is the standard Old Testament word for weeping and shedding tears.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb bakah means to weep, cry, shed tears, or mourn. It refers both to the physical act of tears falling and to the internal sorrow that causes them. Depending on who is weeping and why, the word can express:

  • Grief and bereavement over loss.

  • Tears of affection or relief during family reunions.

  • Contrite sorrow and repentance before God.

  • Anguish under distress or helplessness.

In 1 Samuel 1:8, the word refers to Hannah's weeping over her childlessness and her deep emotional distress.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, bakah describes a wide range of human experiences. Sometimes it marks sorrow over death or national crisis. At other times, it describes tears of joy when estranged family members embrace.

In spiritual settings, weeping can express genuine repentance for sin or intense pleading with God in prayer. The word itself simply denotes the act of weeping; the spiritual character of the tears depends on the condition of the worshipper's heart. Scripture shows that weeping can be an expression of bitter complaint or an act of honest faith that cries out to God.

4. Theological Implications

Scripture does not treat sorrow or tears as a lack of faith. God created human beings with the capacity to feel grief, and He pays close attention to human suffering. Throughout biblical history, tears often serve as the turning point where human weakness meets divine comfort. When believers bring their sorrow directly to God, weeping becomes a form of worship and honest prayer.

God is near to the brokenhearted. Hannah's tears did not distance her from God; instead, her grief prompted her to seek Him at the tabernacle. Her weeping opened the door to a heartfelt vow and a transformed life, demonstrating that God welcomes the raw, unfiltered burdens of His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:8, Elkanah notices his wife Hannah weeping during the annual sacrificial feast at Shiloh. Year after year, Peninnah provoked Hannah relentlessly because the Lord had closed her womb. Elkanah asks her, "Hannah, why do you weep? Why do you not eat? And why is your heart grieved?"

Grammatically, the verb appears as a feminine singular imperfect form, addressing Hannah directly in her ongoing state of distress. Elkanah's question shows how visible her grief was to those around her. Although he loved her and treated her with special favor, his words could not heal the deep ache in her heart. Her weeping was not a brief emotional outburst, but the deep overflow of a grieved spirit.

Rather than allowing her grief to turn into bitterness toward her rival, Hannah channeled her tears into prayer. Her weeping in verse 8 sets the scene for her encounter with the Lord in verse 10, where she pours out her soul and finds peace long before her physical circumstances change.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 37:35 — "And all his sons and all his daughters arose to comfort him; but he refused to be comforted, and he said, 'For I shall go down into the grave to my son in mourning.' Thus his father wept for him."

  • 1 Samuel 30:4 — "Then David and the people who were with him lifted up their voices and wept, until they had no more power to weep."

  • 2 Chronicles 34:27 — "because your heart was tender, and you humbled yourself before God when you heard His words against this place and against its inhabitants, and you humbled yourself before Me, and you tore your clothes and wept before Me, I also have heard you, says the LORD."

  • Nehemiah 8:9 — "And Nehemiah, who was the governor, Ezra the priest the scribe, and the Levites who taught the people said to all the people, 'This day is holy to the LORD your God; do not mourn nor weep.' For all the people wept, when they heard the words of the Law."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's story shows how personal heartbreak can become a meeting place with God. In 1 Samuel 1:8, her weeping seems helpless, but after bringing her burden to the Lord in prayer, "her face was no longer sad" (1 Samuel 1:18). The inner burden lifted before she ever conceived a child. This illustrates that bringing our tears to God in faith brings relief to the heart even while waiting for His answer.
