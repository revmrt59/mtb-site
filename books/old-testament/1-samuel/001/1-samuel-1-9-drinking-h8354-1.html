DRINKING - (shâthâh, Strong's H8354)

Summary:

The word shatah means to drink, consume liquid, or participate in a banquet where beverages are served. In 1 Samuel 1:9, it describes the customary consumption of drink during the sacrificial family meal at Shiloh. Its presence in the narrative highlights that the communal meal had fully concluded, showing that Hannah's subsequent silent prayer was a deliberate act of deep devotion rather than a result of feast-day excess.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israelite life, drinking was both a basic daily necessity and a central part of communal gatherings. Water, milk, and wine were the primary beverages consumed. While water provided essential hydration in an arid climate, wine and other fermented drinks were standard elements of festive meals and religious celebrations.

In Israel's worship, sacrificial peace offerings were accompanied by communal meals at the central sanctuary. Families traveled together to sacred sites like Shiloh, sacrificed animals, and ate and drank together in the presence of the Lord. These meals were meant to be joyful occasions of thanksgiving and fellowship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of shatah is to drink or consume liquid. The word can refer to a range of settings involving the swallowing of liquids:

  • Satisfying physical thirst with water or milk.

  • Participating in a banquet or fellowship meal with wine.

  • Consuming drink as an act of daily nourishment.

In metaphorical contexts elsewhere in the Old Testament, the word can describe taking in instruction, soaking up injustice, or experiencing God's judgment as a cup handed to an individual or nation. In its literal sense, however, it remains the standard verb for drinking.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses shatah across various narrative and legal settings:

  • Daily Survival: It frequently appears alongside eating to describe the basic routine of human life and physical sustenance.

  • Fellowship and Worship: Sacred feasts involved eating and drinking to mark covenant joy and gratitude before God.

  • Self-Denial and Fasting: Abstaining from drinking water or wine often accompanied intense seasons of mourning, repentance, or divine communion.

  • Warnings Against Excess: While moderate drinking during feasts was normal, Scripture warns against overindulgence that leads to drunkenness and spiritual insensibility.

4. Theological Implications

Drinking in Scripture carries several important theological themes:

  • God's Generous Provision: Water and food are gifts from God that sustain human life, demonstrating His ongoing care for Creation.

  • Covenant Fellowship: Eating and drinking in God's presence at holy sites expressed peace between God and His people, as well as unity among the worshipers.

  • Spiritual Thirst: Physical drinking highlights human dependence. Just as the body requires water to live, human souls require God's presence and Word for spiritual vitality.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:9, the word shatah appears in a historical note setting up Hannah's earnest prayer. Elkanah had brought his family to Shiloh for the annual sacrifice. After the family had finished eating and drinking, Hannah stood up to pray near the tabernacle doorpost.

Grammatically, the word appears here in an infinitive absolute form alongside "eating," functioning as a verbal noun to mark the action that had just ended ("after eating and drinking"). This time marker is crucial for several reasons:

  • Separation from the Feast: It shows that Hannah did not pray in the middle of a noisy family celebration. The meal was over, and she stepped away to seek God privately.

  • Clear Mind and Deep Sorrow: By emphasizing that the drinking was finished, the text sets up her defense in verse 15. When Eli later misjudges her trembling lips and accuses her of being drunk, the narrative has already established that her behavior stems from a heavy heart, not from overindulgence at the feast table.

  • Transition to Prayer: The conclusion of physical nourishment marks the beginning of her spiritual crying out to God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 25:34 — Then Jacob gave Esau bread and stew of lentils; then he ate and drank, arose, and went his way. Thus Esau despised his birthright.

  • Deuteronomy 9:9 — When I went up into the mountain to receive the tablets of stone... I stayed on the mountain forty days and forty nights. I neither ate bread nor drank water.

  • 1 Samuel 1:15 — But Hannah answered and said, "No, my lord, I am a woman of sorrowful spirit. I have drunk neither wine nor intoxicating drink, but have poured out my soul before the LORD."

  • Ecclesiastes 5:18 — Here is what I have seen: it is good and fitting for one to eat and drink, and to enjoy the good of all his labor in which he toils under the sun all the days of his life which God gives him; for it is his heritage.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Inward Heart vs. Outward Observance: The context highlights a contrast between external participation in religious ritual and genuine, heart-felt prayer. Elkanah and Peninnah participated in the feast's eating and drinking, but Hannah’s deep sorrow drove her past the meal into direct, personal intercession with the Lord.

  • Misunderstanding Spiritual Grief: Eli's quick assumption that Hannah was drunk illustrates how easily spiritual leaders can mistake intense emotional prayer for fleshly excess. The note about the finished meal in 1 Samuel 1:9 protects Hannah's reputation and exposes Eli's lack of spiritual discernment at this stage in Israel's history.
