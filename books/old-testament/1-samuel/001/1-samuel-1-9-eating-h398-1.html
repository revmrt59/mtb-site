EATING - (ʼâkal, Strong's H398)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'akal basic meaning is to eat, consume, or partake of food. In 1 Samuel 1:9, it describes the conclusion of the sacrificial fellowship meal at Shiloh. The mention of this completed act marks a key turning point in the story: after the family finished eating, Hannah stepped away from the dinner table to pour out her heart in silent, earnest prayer before the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

1 Samuel 1 opens in the hill country of Ephraim, focusing on the family of Elkanah. Each year, Elkanah traveled with his family to the tabernacle at Shiloh to worship and offer sacrifices to the Lord of hosts.

Under the Mosaic law, certain offerings—such as peace offerings—were shared between the altar, the priests, and the worshippers. The family would sit together near the sanctuary to share a sacred feast. This annual gathering was intended to be a time of joy and corporate thanksgiving. However, for Hannah, it was deeply painful. Peninnah, her husband's second wife, mocked her relentlessly because Hannah was childless. Though Elkanah gave Hannah a double portion of the sacrificial meat to show his love, her heart was filled with grief, causing her to weep and refuse food.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew verb 'akal is the common word for eating or consuming. Depending on the subject and context, the word can mean:

  • To eat food for basic physical nourishment.

  • To partake of a ceremonial or covenant feast.

  • To devour or destroy (when describing fire consuming an offering, disease destroying a crop, or a sword devouring enemies).

In 1 Samuel 1:9, the word refers directly to human eating—specifically, partaking in the sacrificial meal at Shiloh.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses 'akal across a wide variety of settings. It frequently describes daily human life, such as eating bread, harvesting crops, or sharing hospitality. It also plays a vital role in biblical worship, where partaking of sacred offerings symbolized fellowship with God and unity within the community.

In terms of grammatical function in 1 Samuel 1:9, 'akal appears as an infinitive construct. This structure sets a time boundary, indicating that the action of eating had come to an end. The text establishes that once the meal was completed, Hannah initiated her decisive action.

4. Theological Implications

The context surrounding 'akal in 1 Samuel 1 highlights an important spiritual truth: participating in outward religious routines does not guarantee peace of heart. The annual feast at Shiloh was meant to celebrate God's goodness, yet Hannah remained in deep sorrow. Physical food and formal ceremony could not satisfy her soul's grief.

This passage demonstrates that God cares deeply for individuals who are hurting, even during times when everyone else is celebrating. While the rest of the family engaged in the fellowship meal, Hannah turned her attention beyond the table to the Lord Himself, showing that genuine faith seeks God directly in times of distress.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:9, the finished meal serves as the backdrop for Hannah's movement. Up to this point, Hannah had endured years of silent misery and provocation during these trips. She had wept and abstained from eating.

The statement that they had finished eating and drinking signals a shift from passive sorrow to active intercession. Hannah did not allow her grief to drive her into despair or bitterness against her family. Instead, as soon as the meal ended, she stood up and walked toward the tabernacle. While the priest Eli sat comfortably by the doorpost, Hannah stood nearby, silently crying out to God from the depth of her soul.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:16 — "And the LORD God commanded the man, saying, 'Of every tree of the garden you may freely eat...'"

Here the word describes God's abundant provision for humanity in Eden.

  • Leviticus 24:9 — "And it shall be for Aaron and his sons, and they shall eat it in a holy place..."

Here the verb refers to the priests consuming the holy bread set before the Lord.

  • Joshua 24:13 — "...of the vineyards and olive groves which you did not plant, you eat."

In this covenant renewal, the word highlights God's grace in giving Israel land and fruit they did not earn.

  • Psalms 78:25 — "Men ate angels' food; He sent them food to the full."

This passage recalls God's miraculous wilderness provision of manna for His people.

  • Nehemiah 9:36 — "Here we are, servants today! And the land that You gave to our fathers, to eat its fruit and its bounty, here we are servants in it!"

The returning exiles use the word to reflect on the purpose of the Promised Land as a gift of divine goodness.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition following the meal exposes the contrast between human comfort and spiritual hunger. Elkanah tried to comfort Hannah with food and kind words, asking why her heart was grieved. Yet human affection could not solve her distress.

By waiting until the family finished eating before approaching the tabernacle, Hannah respected the order of the family gathering while prioritizing her need to bring her request before God. Her silent, persistent prayer immediately following the meal eventually led to God answering her plea with the birth of Samuel.
