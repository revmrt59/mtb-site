TABERNACLE - (hêykâl, Strong's H1964)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun hekal refers to a royal palace, a temple, or a sacred sanctuary reserved for a king or deity. In 1 Samuel 1:9, the word describes the sanctuary of the LORD at Shiloh, where Eli the priest sat by the doorpost. By using this term, the passage presents the worship site at Shiloh as the royal house of Israel's divine King, showing that Hannah brought her deep personal sorrow directly into the court of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Near East, a grand palace or temple was understood as the official residence of a monarch or deity. The Hebrew word hekal reflected this cultural background, designating a spacious building of significance, such as a royal palace or a holy temple.

During the time of the judges, before Solomon built the Jerusalem temple, the central sanctuary of Israel was located at Shiloh. The Ark of the Covenant was kept there, and families traveled to Shiloh annually to offer sacrifices to the LORD of hosts. Though the sanctuary began as the tent tabernacle built in the wilderness, by the era of Eli it had developed into a semi-permanent sanctuary complex with fixed doorposts and structures.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word hekal can refer to a human king's royal palace, a holy temple dedicated to God, or an inner sanctuary within a temple complex. When used for human rulers, it describes their royal residence. When used for God, it describes either His earthly sanctuary—where His name and presence dwell among His people—or His heavenly throne room.

In 1 Samuel 1:9, the word is translated "tabernacle" in the NKJV and refers to the sanctuary structure at Shiloh. The grammatical form here is a singular common noun. In this setting, the word highlights the sanctuary as the earthly throne room of Israel's true King.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses hekal in several distinct settings:

  • Human Palaces: The word describes royal residences belonging to earthly monarchs, such as the palace of the king of Babylon.

  • The Earthly Temple: Most frequently, the word refers to the Jerusalem temple built by Solomon and later rebuilt after the exile. It identifies the temple as a royal palace built for the LORD.

  • Early Sanctuaries: Before the construction of the Jerusalem temple, the word described the sacred worship site at Shiloh where the Ark resided.

  • The Heavenly Sanctuary: Psalms and prophetic writings use the word for God's heavenly throne room, showing that earthly sanctuaries reflect God's rule over the universe.

4. Theological Implications

The term hekal emphasizes the sovereignty and kingship of God. An earthly sanctuary was not merely a building for ritual obligations; it was the courtroom of the supreme King of Israel. When Israel gathered at the sanctuary, they were entering the presence of their divine Sovereign.

This divine context makes Hannah's approach remarkable. Though she was a grieved, childless woman with no political standing or high social status, she stepped into the royal court of the LORD of hosts. Her story demonstrates that God's royal house is not an exclusive court closed to the suffering, but a place where the brokenhearted can lay their burdens before their King.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 1:9, Eli the priest is sitting on a seat by the doorpost of the hekal (tabernacle) of the LORD. This placement sets up a powerful contrast between human priestly leadership and divine authority.

Eli sits outside by the doorpost as an official guardian of the sanctuary, yet he lacks spiritual perception. When Hannah prays silently in deep anguish, Eli misinterprets her sorrow and accuses her of drunkenness. The mention of the hekal reminds the reader that the true ruler at Shiloh is the LORD inside the sanctuary, not the aging priest sitting outside. Hannah's heart was focused on God, the King who dwells in the sanctuary, and He heard her prayer when human leadership misunderstood her.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The use of hekal at Shiloh marks an important historical transition in Israel's worship. The earlier word mishkan emphasized the portable, tent-like nature of God dwelling among a moving people in the wilderness. The use of hekal in 1 Samuel indicates that by the time of Eli, the sanctuary at Shiloh had acquired permanent features, including structural doorposts.

This detail sets the stage for the narrative developments in early Samuel. While the physical structure at Shiloh would later be destroyed due to the wickedness of Eli's sons, God's true royal sanctuary remained secure in heaven, ready to respond to humble faith like Hannah's.
