1 Samuel 1:1
1 Samuel 1:1
NKJV

1 Samuel 1:1 Now there was a certain man of Ramathaim Zophim, of the mountains of Ephraim, and his name was Elkanah the son of Jeroham, the son of Elihu, the son of Tohu, the son of Zuph, an Ephraimite.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 1:1 There was a man named Elkanah who lived in Ramah in the region of Zuph in the hill country of Ephraim. He was the son of Jeroham, son of Elihu, son of Tohu, son of Zuph, of Ephraim. (NLT)

Main Idea

The narrative opens by introducing Elkanah, a man of Ramathaim Zophim in the mountains of Ephraim, whose ancestry is traced back four generations to Zuph. God’s work in this book begins within a particular Israelite household rooted in a real place and family.

Verse Explanation

The narrative opens by introducing Elkanah, a man of Ramathaim Zophim in the mountains of Ephraim, whose ancestry is traced back four generations to Zuph. God’s work in this book begins within a particular Israelite household rooted in a real place and family.

“Now there was a certain man of Ramathaim Zophim,”

First Samuel opens the way several stories in Judges do, with “a certain man” from a particular town (compare Judges 13:2; 17:1). The book that will tell of prophets and kings begins in an ordinary household. “Ramathaim Zophim” is the full name of the town later called Ramah (1:19; 2:11). The name means ‘the two heights,’ and “Zophim” probably ties the place to the family's ancestor Zuph, named at the end of the verse. Ramah became Samuel's home base for the rest of his life; he judged Israel there and built an altar to the LORD there (7:17).

“of the mountains of Ephraim,”

The town lay in the hill country of Ephraim, the central highlands north of Jerusalem. Shiloh, where the tabernacle stood, was in the same region, a journey of perhaps a day or two on foot.

“and his name was Elkanah the son of Jeroham, the son of Elihu, the son of Tohu, the son of Zuph,”

The narrator traces “Elkanah” back four generations to “Zuph.” An ancestry this full is usually given for someone whose family matters to the story. Elkanah's name means ‘God has created’ or ‘God has acquired.’ The genealogy roots the beginning of God's work in a real family with a remembered past. Nothing here is legend; God's purposes for Israel begin in a home whose ancestors could be named.

“an Ephraimite.”

The final description places Elkanah in Ephraim. Chronicles lists Samuel's family among the Levites, descendants of Kohath, and names the same ancestors under slightly different forms (1 Chronicles 6:26–27, 33–35). The best explanation is that Elkanah belonged to a Levitical family living in the territory of Ephraim. Levites did not receive a tribal territory of their own; they lived in towns scattered among the other tribes, and the Kohathites were given towns in Ephraim (Joshua 21:20–21). Judges speaks of Levites living “in the remote mountains of Ephraim” (Judges 19:1). “Ephraimite” therefore identifies where the family lived, while Chronicles identifies its tribe.

Study Notes

Chronicles gives Samuel's ancestry twice. 1 Chronicles 6:26–27 reads Zophai, Nahath, Eliab, Jeroham, Elkanah, and 1 Chronicles 6:33–35 reads Zuph, Toah, Eliel, Jeroham, Elkanah, Samuel.

Toah and Nahath correspond to Tohu here, and Eliel and Eliab to Elihu. Such variation in name forms is common between genealogical records.

Some interpreters take “an Ephraimite” to mean that the family was Ephraimite by ancestry and that Chronicles later placed Samuel among the Levites because of his service. Chronicles presents a Levitical descent, and the Hebrew term can describe residence, so a Levitical family living in Ephraim fits both texts.

Heman the singer, one of the leaders of worship David appointed, was Samuel's grandson (1 Chronicles 6:33).

Ramathaim Zophim is commonly identified with Ramah, Samuel's later home (1:19). The exact site of the town is debated.

1 Samuel 1:2
NKJV

1 Samuel 1:2 And he had two wives: the name of one was Hannah, and the name of the other Peninnah. Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 1:2 Elkanah had two wives, Hannah and Peninnah. Peninnah had children, but Hannah did not. (NLT)

Main Idea

Elkanah has two wives, and the verse sets them in painful contrast: Peninnah has children, but Hannah has none. This difference creates the tension that the rest of the chapter will resolve.

Verse Explanation

Elkanah has two wives, and the verse sets them in painful contrast: Peninnah has children, but Hannah has none. This difference creates the tension that the rest of the chapter will resolve.

“And he had two wives: the name of one was Hannah, and the name of the other Peninnah.”

Elkanah had two wives. Scripture records polygamy among Israel's ancestors without commending it, and the households that practiced it are regularly marked by rivalry and grief, as with Sarah and Hagar (Genesis 16:4) and Rachel and Leah (Genesis 29:31; 30:1). “Hannah” is named first, which may suggest she was the first wife or the more loved. Her name means ‘grace’ or ‘favor.’ “Peninnah” may mean ‘pearl’ or ‘coral,’ though the meaning is uncertain.

“Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children.”

The verse ends with a sharp contrast. In Israel, children were a sign of God's blessing and the hope of a family's future. A woman without children often carried shame as well as sorrow. The words “Hannah had no children” set the problem the chapter will resolve. Scripture repeatedly begins God's great acts with a barren woman: Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, and Samson's mother (Genesis 25:21; Judges 13:2). The pattern shows that the child who comes is God's gift, not human achievement.

Study Notes

The Hebrew wording ‘the name of one... the name of the other’ is a common way of introducing two people. It does not by itself establish which wife Elkanah married first.

NKJV

NLT

1 Samuel 1:3 Each year Elkanah would travel to Shiloh to worship and sacrifice to the LORD of Heaven’s Armies at the Tabernacle. The priests of the LORD at that time were the two sons of Eli — Hophni and Phinehas. (NLT)

Main Idea

Elkanah faithfully goes up from his city each year to worship and sacrifice to the LORD of hosts at Shiloh, where the tabernacle stood. The note that Eli’s sons Hophni and Phinehas serve there as priests introduces the men whose conduct will soon come under the LORD’s judgment.

Verse Explanation

Elkanah faithfully goes up from his city each year to worship and sacrifice to the LORD of hosts at Shiloh, where the tabernacle stood. The note that Eli’s sons Hophni and Phinehas serve there as priests introduces the men whose conduct will soon come under the LORD’s judgment.

“This man went up from his city yearly to worship and sacrifice to the LORD of hosts in Shiloh.”

Elkanah was faithful in worship. He “went up” each year from Ramah to “Shiloh,” where the tabernacle had stood since Joshua's day (Joshua 18:1). The Law called Israelite men to appear before the LORD three times a year (Deuteronomy 16:16). Whether Elkanah's yearly visit was one of these feasts or a special family sacrifice is not stated. What matters is that he went “to worship and sacrifice.” In a period when “everyone did what was right in his own eyes” (Judges 21:25), this household kept going up to the LORD.

“The LORD of hosts” presents God as commander of the armies of heaven and ruler over every power. This is the first time in the order of the biblical books that God is called by this title. It will appear again in Hannah's prayer (1:11), in connection with the ark (4:4), and on David's lips as he faces Goliath (17:45). The household's worship was directed to the sovereign King of all.

“Also the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, the priests of the LORD, were there.”

The narrator adds a note that seems minor but will become central. “Hophni and Phinehas” were Eli's sons and served as “the priests of the LORD” at Shiloh. The title is accurate, but the following chapter will show how badly they dishonored it (2:12–17). From the opening verses, the reader sees both the faithful worshiper and the unfaithful priests at the same sanctuary.

Study Notes

“LORD of hosts” translates YHWH tseva'ot. “Hosts” means armies and is used of Israel's armies, the heavenly beings, and the stars. This is the first use of the title in the Bible's order of books.

Both Hophni and Phinehas appear to be Egyptian names; Phinehas is often understood as ‘the Nubian.’

The same name belonged to Aaron's grandson (Numbers 25:7–13), a different person from Eli's son.

Shiloh lay north of Bethel in Ephraim (Judges 21:19). By this time the sanctuary had doors and doorposts (1:9; 3:15) and is called “the house of the LORD” (1:7), suggesting a more permanent structure around or alongside the tent.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

At the sacrificial meal Elkanah gives portions to Peninnah and to all her sons and daughters. The ordinary custom of sharing the offering makes the size of Peninnah’s family plain to everyone at the table.

“And whenever the time came for Elkanah to make an offering,”

The scene is the yearly sacrifice at Shiloh. The kind of offering described here was a peace offering, in which part of the animal was burned on the altar, part given to the priest, and the rest eaten by the worshiper's family in a meal before the LORD (Leviticus 7:31–34).

“he would give portions to Peninnah his wife and to all her sons and daughters.”

At the meal Elkanah distributed portions of the sacrificial meat. Peninnah received her share, and so did “all her sons and daughters,” one by one. The ordinary custom of sharing the offering made the size of Peninnah's family plain to everyone at the table. Each portion handed out was a reminder to Hannah of what she did not have. The feast that should have been a joy became, year after year, the setting of her pain.

1 Samuel 1:5
1 Samuel 1:5
NKJV

1 Samuel 1:5 But to Hannah he would give a double portion, for he loved Hannah, although the LORD had closed her womb.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 1:5 And though he loved Hannah, he would give her only one choice portion because the LORD had given her no children. (NLT)

Main Idea

Elkanah gives Hannah a double portion because he loves her, even though the LORD had closed her womb. The verse sets a husband’s real love beside a sorrow that only the LORD can address.

Verse Explanation

Elkanah gives Hannah a double portion because he loves her, even though the LORD had closed her womb. The verse sets a husband’s real love beside a sorrow that only the LORD can address.

“But to Hannah he would give a double portion,”

Elkanah gave Hannah a special portion. “A double portion” marked her as honored and loved. Elkanah was trying to show publicly that Hannah's barrenness did not lessen her place in his heart.

“for he loved Hannah,”

The narrator explains his motive plainly: “he loved Hannah.” His love was real, and Scripture does not criticize it.

“although the LORD had closed her womb.”

The verse ends with the deeper reality. “The LORD had closed her womb.” The narrator does not attribute Hannah's barrenness to chance or to some hidden sin. It lay under the LORD's sovereign hand. That is both the hardest and the most hopeful part of the verse. If the LORD had closed her womb, then only the LORD could open it. Elkanah's love was genuine, but it could not reach the place where Hannah's sorrow lay.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase translated “a double portion” is unusual and difficult. It may mean ‘a portion of two faces,’ that is, a double or worthy portion.

The Septuagint reads ‘a single portion, because she had no child,’ taking the sense as one portion only, though Elkanah loved her. The NKJV's reading fits the context of Elkanah's love and is followed by most translations.

The firstborn's double portion of inheritance (Deuteronomy 21:17) uses a different Hebrew expression.

Genesis uses the same kind of language for the LORD opening the womb for Leah and Rachel (Genesis 29:31; 30:22) and closing the wombs of Abimelech's household (Genesis 20:18).

Main Idea

Peninnah, called Hannah’s rival, provokes her severely to make her miserable, using her barrenness against her. For the second time the narrator states that the LORD had closed Hannah’s womb, keeping her situation under His hand.

Verse Explanation

Peninnah, called Hannah’s rival, provokes her severely to make her miserable, using her barrenness against her. For the second time the narrator states that the LORD had closed Hannah’s womb, keeping her situation under His hand.

“And her rival also provoked her severely,”

Peninnah is called Hannah's “rival.” The Hebrew word describes a co-wife as an adversary. It is related to the word for distress, and the same word is used in the Law's prohibition against marrying a woman's sister as “a rival” (Leviticus 18:18). “Provoked her severely” means she deliberately irritated and taunted Hannah. The rivalry was not a misunderstanding; it was cruelty.

“to make her miserable,”

Peninnah's aim was to hurt. She wanted Hannah to feel her shame. The narrator does not explain Peninnah's motive, though Elkanah's evident love for Hannah may have fed her jealousy.

“because the LORD had closed her womb.”

For the second time the narrator states that “the LORD had closed her womb” (1:5). Peninnah used Hannah's barrenness as a weapon, but the narrator keeps placing it under the LORD's hand. What Peninnah meant as a taunt was a situation God would answer in His time.

Verse Explanation

The provocation is not a single incident but happens year after year whenever Hannah goes up to the house of the LORD. The very occasion of worship becomes the time of her deepest grief, so that she weeps and will not eat.

“So it was, year by year,”

The provocation was not a single incident. It happened “year by year.” The repetition stretched Hannah's grief over many years, with each visit renewing the wound.

“when she went up to the house of the LORD, that she provoked her;”

The cruelty came “when she went up to the house of the LORD.” The very occasion of worship became the time of Hannah's deepest grief. Shiloh, the place where Israel met God, was where Peninnah taunted her most.

“therefore she wept and did not eat.”

The result was weeping and refusal to eat. At a feast where everyone ate their portions, Hannah could not. Her grief was so heavy that she could not share the meal. The narrator lets the reader feel the weight of these years before Hannah's prayer is recorded. Her later prayer was not the outburst of a moment but the pouring out of long sorrow.

Study Notes

The phrase “the house of the LORD” usually refers to the temple in Jerusalem, but here it describes the sanctuary at Shiloh.

Elkanah asks why Hannah weeps, why she does not eat, and why her heart is grieved, and whether he is not better to her than ten sons. His love is sincere, but his words show that he cannot meet the deepest longing of her heart.

“Then Elkanah her husband said to her, ‘Hannah, why do you weep? Why do you not eat? And why is your heart grieved?’”

Elkanah saw her distress and asked four questions. They show genuine concern. He noticed her tears, her refusal to eat, and her grieved heart. “Your heart” points to her inner life; her grief went to the center of who she was.

“Am I not better to you than ten sons?”

His final question reveals both love and limitation. “Ten sons” expresses a complete and abundant family. Elkanah was saying, in effect, ‘Isn't my love enough?’ His love was sincere, but his words show that he could not meet the deepest longing of her heart. Scripture does not record Hannah's answer. Instead, she rose and went to pray. The one who could meet her need was not Elkanah but the LORD.

Main Idea

After the meal at Shiloh, Hannah rises to go and pray, while Eli the priest sits by the doorpost of the tabernacle of the LORD. The scene moves from the family’s yearly pattern to this particular visit and places Eli where he will observe her.

Verse Explanation

After the meal at Shiloh, Hannah rises to go and pray, while Eli the priest sits by the doorpost of the tabernacle of the LORD. The scene moves from the family’s yearly pattern to this particular visit and places Eli where he will observe her.

“So Hannah arose after they had finished eating and drinking in Shiloh.”

The narrative moves from the yearly pattern to one particular visit. After the meal, “Hannah arose.” The verb marks a turning point. She did not remain at the table in tears; she got up and went to pray. “Eating and drinking” describes the feast that had just ended, and the reference to drinking prepares for Eli's mistaken assumption in verse 13.

“Now Eli the priest was sitting on the seat by the doorpost of the tabernacle of the LORD.”

The narrator introduces “Eli the priest.” He was “sitting on the seat,” probably a seat of honor, “by the doorpost of the tabernacle of the LORD.” From that position he could observe all who came to the sanctuary. The word translated “tabernacle” here is the word for a temple or palace, again suggesting a substantial building. Eli's place by the entrance sets him where he will observe Hannah, and his seat will appear again on the day he dies (4:13, 18).

Study Notes

“Tabernacle” translates hekal, which means a palace or temple. It is the word later used for Solomon's temple.

The “seat” (kisse') is the ordinary word for a chair or throne and may indicate Eli's official position.

The Septuagint adds that Hannah stood before the LORD after the meal. The Hebrew text followed by the NKJV is shorter.

1 Samuel 1:10 And she was in bitterness of soul, and prayed to the LORD and wept in anguish.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 1:10 Hannah was in deep anguish, crying bitterly as she prayed to the LORD. (NLT)

Main Idea

In bitterness of soul Hannah prays to the LORD and weeps in anguish. She brings her grief directly to God rather than hiding it or turning away from Him.

Verse Explanation

In bitterness of soul Hannah prays to the LORD and weeps in anguish. She brings her grief directly to God rather than hiding it or turning away from Him.

“And she was in bitterness of soul,”

The narrator describes Hannah's inner condition. “Bitterness of soul” describes deep anguish, the bitterness of someone whose life has been emptied. Naomi used the same idea when she asked to be called Mara, ‘bitter’ (Ruth 1:20). Hannah's grief had built up over years of barrenness and provocation.

“and prayed to the LORD”

Hannah took her bitterness to the LORD. She did not hide it, deny it, or turn away from God because of it. She “prayed to the LORD,” the God who had closed her womb (1:5–6) and the only one who could open it. Her prayer did not wait until she felt better. She came in her bitterness.

“and wept in anguish.”

Her prayer came with tears. “Wept in anguish” means she wept bitterly and continuously. Scripture does not treat her tears as a lack of faith. It presents them as part of her prayer. The Psalms invite God's people to “Pour out your heart before Him” (Psalm 62:8), and Hannah did exactly that.

Verse Explanation

Hannah calls on the LORD of hosts to look on her affliction, remember her, and give her a son, and she vows to give that son to the LORD all the days of his life, with no razor coming upon his head. Her vow is understood as a response to God’s leading concerning this child rather than a bargain, although the passage does not record a message from God to her.

“Then she made a vow and said, ‘O LORD of hosts,’”

Hannah's prayer included a “vow,” a solemn promise made to God. She addressed Him as “LORD of hosts,” the title used for His worship at Shiloh (1:3). She called on the sovereign ruler of heaven's armies to act in her small, private grief.

“if You will indeed look on the affliction of Your maidservant and remember me, and not forget Your maidservant,”

Hannah asked God to “look on” her affliction, to “remember” her, and not to “forget” her. These are the words of someone who felt unseen. Leah used similar words when she said, “The LORD has surely looked on my affliction” (Genesis 29:32). To “remember” in Scripture means more than recalling; it means to act on someone's behalf, as when God “remembered Noah” (Genesis 8:1) and “remembered Rachel” (Genesis 30:22). Three times she calls herself “Your maidservant,” presenting herself humbly as God's servant.

“but will give Your maidservant a male child,”

Her request was specific: “a male child.”

“then I will give him to the LORD all the days of his life,”

Her vow was costly. She would give the son she longed for “to the LORD all the days of his life.” She asked for a son in order to give him back. That is the heart of the verse. Her longing and God's purpose came together in her prayer. Scripture does not record God speaking to Hannah, but the narrative as a whole points to more than a bargain struck in desperation. Her changed face after prayer (1:18), Elkanah's words, “Only let the LORD establish His word” (1:23), and the prophetic reach of her song (2:1–10) together suggest that her prayer became a place where God made something of His purpose known, and her vow was her response to what He was revealing. She wanted a son; God was preparing a prophet.

“and no razor shall come upon his head.”

Hannah added a sign of lifelong consecration. “No razor shall come upon his head” recalls the vow of the Nazirite, whose uncut hair marked him as set apart to the LORD (Numbers 6:5). Samson was dedicated in a similar way from birth (Judges 13:5). Normally a Nazirite vow was temporary; Hannah's son would be set apart for life.

Study Notes

The Hebrew text of 1 Samuel does not use the word “Nazirite,” but the promise about the razor resembles the Nazirite vow (Numbers 6:2–8).

The Septuagint adds that the child would drink no wine or strong drink, and a Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel (4QSam^a) at 1:22 calls him a Nazirite forever.

Numbers 30:6–15 gave a husband the right to confirm or cancel his wife's vow when he heard it. Elkanah's response in 1:23 shows his consent to Hannah's vow.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Hannah is praying in her heart, with her lips moving but her voice unheard. Seeing only these outward signs, Eli concludes that she is drunk.

“Now Hannah spoke in her heart;”

The narrator explains what Eli could not see. “Hannah spoke in her heart.” Her prayer was inward, directed to God from the depths of her being. In the ancient world prayer was usually spoken aloud, so silent prayer was unusual.

“only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard.”

Her lips moved, but no sound came. This was the prayer of someone too deeply moved to speak aloud, pouring out her heart in words only God could hear.

“Therefore Eli thought she was drunk.”

Eli drew the wrong conclusion. Seeing only the outward signs, he assumed “she was drunk.” Feasts at Shiloh could involve drinking (1:9), and Judges records dancing and festivity there (Judges 21:19–21), so drunkenness at the sanctuary may not have been unheard of. Eli's mistake was understandable on the surface, but it showed that he could not discern what was before him. The priest at Shiloh mistook the most earnest prayer in the sanctuary for drunkenness, while his own sons were defiling the sanctuary in plain sight (2:12–17, 22).

But he had misread the situation completely. The priest at Shiloh misjudged a woman in sincere prayer, showing that holding religious office does not guarantee discernment. Hannah, who had come to God in bitterness, was now accused by the priest.
