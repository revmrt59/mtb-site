1 Samuel 1:1
NKJV

1 Samuel 1:1 Now there was a certain man of Ramathaim Zophim, of the mountains of Ephraim, and his name was Elkanah the son of Jeroham, the son of Elihu, the son of Tohu, the son of Zuph, an Ephraimite.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 1:1 There was a man named Elkanah who lived in Ramah in the region of Zuph in the hill country of Ephraim. He was the son of Jeroham, son of Elihu, son of Tohu, son of Zuph, of Ephraim. (NLT)

Main Idea

The story begins with Elkanah, a man from the hill country of Ephraim whose family line is traced back several generations. These details show he was an ordinary Israelite from a real place and family, and his household would become the setting for the birth of Samuel, the prophet who would shape Israel's future.

Verse Explanation

The story begins with Elkanah, a man from the hill country of Ephraim whose family line is traced back several generations. These details show he was an ordinary Israelite from a real place and family, and his household would become the setting for the birth of Samuel, the prophet who would shape Israel's future.

"Now there was a certain man of Ramathaim Zophim,"

This is how several stories in Judges begin, including the one about Manoah, whose wife was also unable to have children until God gave them a son (Judges 13:2). The opening places Elkanah's family in the same troubled period, when Israel had no king and the judges still led the nation.

The name Ramathaim means "two heights," a fitting name for a town in the hills. Zophim connects it to the region named after Elkanah's ancestor Zuph, later called "the land of Zuph" (9:5). The shorter name Ramah is used for the same town in 1:19, when the family returns home. Ramah mattered beyond this chapter. Samuel would later make his home there, judge Israel from there, and build an altar to the LORD there (7:17). When he died, he was buried in Ramah (25:1). The prophet's story begins and ends in this hometown.

"of the mountains of Ephraim,"

This is the hill country in the middle of the land, given to the tribe of Ephraim, one of the largest and most important tribes. The region was rugged and well settled, and it lay within traveling distance of the place where Elkanah's family would go to worship each year.

"and his name was Elkanah the son of Jeroham, the son of Elihu, the son of Tohu, the son of Zuph,"

Tracing Elkanah's family back four generations shows that he came from a known family with real roots in Israel. The same family line appears in 1 Chronicles 6:33–38, where it runs through Samuel down to his grandson Heman, one of the singers David appointed for worship. Some names are spelled a little differently there, such as Eliel for Elihu and Toah for Tohu, but it is clearly the same family. Chronicles places this family in the tribe of Levi, among the descendants of Kohath. The Levites were the tribe set apart to serve at the sanctuary. So the boy who would grow up serving the LORD at the tabernacle came from a Levite family.

"an Ephraimite."

If Elkanah was a Levite, calling him an Ephraimite tells where he lived, not which tribe he was born into. The Levites received no territory of their own. Instead they were given towns scattered among the other tribes, and some of the Kohathite families received their towns from the tribe of Ephraim (Joshua 21:5). A Levite living in Ephraim's hill country could be known as an Ephraimite, much as the Levite in Judges 17:7 is described as belonging to the family of Judah. Elkanah is introduced not as a priest or a leader, but as a man from a hill town in Ephraim.

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Christ & Life] Born on the Heights of the Watchers. Samuel’s family comes from Ramathaim-zophim, a name rendered “the double heights of the watchers.” Before Israel ever heard a prophet, God was already at work in a town whose very name speaks of watching. True spiritual authority is birthed in the hidden heights of prayer and watchfulness, far from the religious machinery of the day. God loves to begin His new movements in the ordinary, unnoticed lives of those who dwell on the heights and keep watch.

[Seed] The Importance of Hidden Faithfulness. The “nobodies” of Ephraim become the incubators for national revival. God often begins His greatest works in places the world barely notices.

1 Samuel 1:2
NKJV

1 Samuel 1:2 And he had two wives: the name of one was Hannah, and the name of the other Peninnah. Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 1:2 Elkanah had two wives, Hannah and Peninnah. Peninnah had children, but Hannah did not. (NLT)

Main Idea

Elkanah had two wives, Peninnah and Hannah, and while Peninnah had children, Hannah had none. In that culture childlessness was often seen as a deep shame and sorrow, and this painful contrast between the two women sets up the tension of the whole chapter.

Verse Explanation

Elkanah had two wives, Peninnah and Hannah, and while Peninnah had children, Hannah had none. In that culture childlessness was often seen as a deep shame and sorrow, and this painful contrast between the two women sets up the tension of the whole chapter.

"And he had two wives:"

Elkanah, an Ephraimite from Ramah, was married to two women at the same time. Many men in the Old Testament had more than one wife, and the Law of Moses gave rules for such households, including protecting the inheritance rights of a firstborn son whose mother was the less-loved wife (Deuteronomy 21:15–17). But it was never God's design. From the beginning, marriage was one man and one woman becoming "one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). Scripture records polygamy without approving it, and its stories show the jealousy and pain it brought. Sarah and Hagar, and later Rachel and Leah, lived in the same kind of divided home. Elkanah's household would be no different.

"the name of one was Hannah, and the name of the other Peninnah."

Hannah is named first, and many interpreters take this to mean she was Elkanah's first wife. If so, Elkanah may have married Peninnah because Hannah had not given him children. That was a common reason for taking a second wife in the ancient world, but Scripture does not say this was Elkanah's reason. Hannah's name comes from a Hebrew word meaning "grace" or "favor."

"Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children."

Here the order is reversed. Peninnah, named second before, is now mentioned first, because she had what Hannah did not. The two women lived side by side in the same family, and the difference between them was impossible to miss.

In Israel, children were seen as a gift from God: "Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD, the fruit of the womb is a reward" (Psalm 127:3). Sons carried on the family name and inheritance, and they cared for their parents in old age. A woman without children often felt she had failed her husband. Others might even take it as a sign of God's displeasure. Rachel cried out to Jacob, "Give me children, or else I die!" (Genesis 30:1). Centuries later, when Elizabeth finally conceived, she said God had acted "to take away my reproach among people" (Luke 1:25). Hannah carried that same sorrow and shame every day, while another woman's children filled her home.

Yet Scripture has told this kind of story before. Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, and the mother of Samson were all unable to have children until God stepped in (Genesis 18:11–14; 25:21; 30:22; Judges 13:2–3). Each time, the child who was finally born played a vital part in God's plan for His people. Hannah's empty arms are where this chapter begins, but they are also where God's work begins.

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Discovery] The Flesh and the Spirit in One House. Peninnah has children; Hannah has none. The scene mirrors Paul’s allegory of Hagar and Sarah (Galatians 4:21–31). Peninnah represents the fruitfulness of the natural: easy, immediate, and abundant. Hannah represents the promise of the Spirit: barren at first, requiring a miracle, and finally bringing forth the seed of destiny. The same two women live in our own house. The flesh produces quickly what everyone can see; the Spirit forms slowly what God has promised. The question is which one we are measuring ourselves by.

[Application] Do Not Measure Favor by What Is Seen. Peninnah’s full household did not prove that she was more favored, and Hannah’s empty arms did not prove that she was forgotten. Do not measure divine favor by external, outward measures. We look not at the things which are seen, but at the things which are not seen (2 Corinthians 4:18).

[Foundational Truth] Barrenness That Creates Dependence. God intentionally uses the barrenness of the natural to create a desperate dependency on Him to see Him move in miraculous and powerful ways.

[Christ & Life] Embrace the Barren Season. The believer must embrace the “barren” seasons when human effort fails, knowing that Christ is forming a deeper, more enduring fruit within.

[Insight] When Custom Replaces God’s Order in the Home. Elkanah’s two wives reflect the customs of his day rather than God’s design for marriage. When we allow the values of the surrounding culture, or what simply seems right in our own eyes, to replace God’s revealed order in our homes, trouble is sure to follow, and the grief of this household begins right here. His Word, rather than the customs around us, must govern the life of the home.

1 Samuel 1:3
NKJV

1 Samuel 1:3 This man went up from his city yearly to worship and sacrifice to the LORD of hosts in Shiloh. Also the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, the priests of the LORD, were there.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 1:3 Each year Elkanah would travel to Shiloh to worship and sacrifice to the LORD of Heaven’s Armies at the Tabernacle. The priests of the LORD at that time were the two sons of Eli — Hophni and Phinehas. (NLT)

Main Idea

Every year Elkanah traveled to Shiloh, where the tabernacle stood, to worship and offer sacrifices to the LORD of hosts, a title meaning the LORD who commands heaven's armies. Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, served there as priests, and their mention here prepares for their later failures.

Verse Explanation

Every year Elkanah traveled to Shiloh, where the tabernacle stood, to worship and offer sacrifices to the LORD of hosts, a title meaning the LORD who commands heaven's armies. Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, served there as priests, and their mention here prepares for their later failures.

"This man went up from his city yearly"

"This man" is Elkanah, an Ephraimite from Ramah. Shiloh lay in the hill country, so travelers literally went up to reach it, but "went up" was also the usual way of describing a trip to worship the LORD. "Yearly" translates a Hebrew phrase meaning "from days to days," the regular return of an appointed time. The Law of Moses called Israel's men to appear before the LORD at the yearly feasts (Exodus 23:14–17; Deuteronomy 16:16), and Judges mentions "a yearly feast of the LORD in Shiloh" (Judges 21:19). Families could also keep their own yearly sacrifice, as David's family did in Bethlehem (20:6). Scripture does not say exactly which occasion Elkanah kept. What matters is that he kept it faithfully, year after year, during the time of the judges, when "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). He brought his whole household with him (1:4, 21).

"to worship and sacrifice to the LORD of hosts"

The Hebrew word for "worship" means to bow down, an act of humble submission before God. Worship and sacrifice belonged together, since coming before the LORD meant bringing an offering.

This is the first time the title "LORD of hosts" appears in the Bible. "Hosts" means armies. The title presents the LORD as the commander of heaven's armies, the angels and the stars, and as the One who rules over every power in heaven and on earth. Israel's armies also belonged to Him, as David told Goliath: "But I come to you in the name of the LORD of hosts, the God of the armies of Israel, whom you have defied" (17:45). The title fits this chapter. Israel was weak and disordered, and Hannah had no power to change her own situation. When she prays, she calls on God by this same name (1:11). The God Elkanah's family came to worship had all power, even over a closed womb.

"in Shiloh."

Shiloh was a town in the territory of Ephraim, north of Bethel. After Israel entered the land, "the whole congregation of the children of Israel assembled together at Shiloh, and set up the tabernacle of meeting there" (Joshua 18:1). The tabernacle was the sacred tent where God's presence dwelt among His people and where the ark of the covenant was kept (3:3). By this time it seems to have become a more permanent place of worship, since it is called "the house of the LORD" (1:7, 24) and "the tabernacle," a word that can also mean temple (1:9; 3:3), and it had doorposts (1:9). For Israel, Shiloh was the one place where sacrifices to the LORD were properly offered.

"Also the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, the priests of the LORD, were there."

Eli was the priest at Shiloh, and his two sons served alongside him. As descendants of Aaron, they held the sacred responsibility of offering the people's sacrifices and representing the people before God. Phinehas shared the name of Aaron's grandson, who had been zealous for the LORD's honor and received God's "covenant of peace" (Numbers 25:7–13). Eli's son would prove to be very different.

Their mention here prepares for what follows. In chapter 2 these "priests of the LORD" are called "corrupt; they did not know the LORD" (2:12). They took the best of the sacrifices for themselves before the fat was offered to God, treating "the offering of the LORD" with contempt (2:13–17), and they lay with the women who served at the door of the tabernacle (2:22). A man of God announced that both sons would die on the same day (2:34), and they were killed when the Philistines captured the ark (4:11). God later pointed to Shiloh as a warning: "But go now to My place which was in Shiloh, where I set My name at the first, and see what I did to it because of the wickedness of My people Israel" (Jeremiah 7:12).

From the start, then, a faithful family coming to worship stands beside the priests who should have been leading Israel in worship. In that setting the LORD would answer a godly woman's prayer and give the son through whom He would bring His word back to Israel.

Study Notes

Shiloh has been identified with Khirbet Seilun, a hilltop site about twenty miles north of Jerusalem, matching the location given in Judges 21:19. Excavations there, especially in the 1980s, uncovered a settlement from the period of the judges with large buildings and many storage jars. That settlement was destroyed by a fierce fire around the middle of the eleventh century BC. Many scholars connect this destruction with the Philistine victory in 1 Samuel 4, when the ark was captured. Samuel does not describe Shiloh's fall, but later Scripture remembers it: God "forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh" (Psalm 78:60), and Jeremiah held up Shiloh as a warning (Jeremiah 7:12–14; 26:6). The archaeological link fits well, but it is an inference, not a direct confirmation.

The sanctuary itself raises a related question. Chapters 1 and 3 call it a "temple" with doorposts (1:9; 3:3), yet 2:22 calls it the "tabernacle of meeting," and the LORD later told David He had "moved about in a tent" until then (2 Samuel 7:6). The likeliest explanation is that the tent sanctuary stood within a more permanent compound of buildings. Later Jewish tradition remembered Shiloh's sanctuary as stone walls with a roof of curtains.

Spirit & Life

[Seed] The First Revelation of “the LORD of Hosts”. This verse marks the first time in Scripture that God is called Jehovah Sabaoth, the LORD of Hosts, the LORD of Armies. It is striking that this militant, sovereign title is first spoken not on a bloody battlefield by a king or general, but in the setting of a family’s yearly worship and a barren woman’s private anguish. The LORD’s heavenly armies are engaged in the intimate, quiet battles of our personal lives. Hallelujah!

[Application] Don't Let Man's Unfaithfulness Undermine Yours. Elkanah kept going up to Shiloh to worship even though Hophni and Phinehas, the priests serving there, were deeply corrupt. We must be careful not to use the weaknesses of spiritual leaders as an excuse to withdraw from the life of the church. Our commitment to gather with God’s people is rooted in devotion to Christ, not in the worthiness of those who lead. Look past the failures of human leaders to the Head of the Church, Christ, the true High Priest.

[Foundational Truth] The LORD Still Reigns When His Representatives Fail. God remains the LORD of Hosts, reigning supreme, even when His earthly representatives fail. The corruption at the altar never touches the authority of the throne.

1 Samuel 1:4
NKJV

1 Samuel 1:4 And whenever the time came for Elkanah to make an offering, he would give portions to Peninnah his wife and to all her sons and daughters.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 1:4 On the days Elkanah presented his sacrifice, he would give portions of the meat to Peninnah and each of her children. (NLT)

Main Idea

At the yearly sacrifice, Elkanah shared portions of the offering with Peninnah and all her sons and daughters. This family meal highlighted how many children Peninnah had, which made Hannah's empty place at the table all the more obvious.

Verse Explanation

At the yearly sacrifice, Elkanah shared portions of the offering with Peninnah and all her sons and daughters. This family meal highlighted how many children Peninnah had, which made Hannah's empty place at the table all the more obvious.

"And whenever the time came for Elkanah to make an offering,"

This happened every year, on the family's trip to worship at Shiloh, where the tabernacle stood. The offering Elkanah brought was the kind of sacrifice the Law calls a peace offering (Leviticus 3; 7:11–18). Unlike a burnt offering, which was burned completely on the altar, a peace offering was shared. The fat was burned on the altar for the LORD, the priest received the breast and the right thigh (Leviticus 7:31–34), and the rest of the meat went back to the worshiper to eat. The Law told Israel to eat this meal before the LORD at the place He chose, rejoicing with their sons and daughters over the ways He had blessed them (Deuteronomy 12:7, 12). The sacrifice was an act of worship, and it was also a family feast, one of the few times in the year an ordinary family ate meat together in God's presence.

As head of the household, Elkanah brought the sacrifice. He would then divide the meat that came back to the family.

"he would give portions to Peninnah his wife and to all her sons and daughters."

Elkanah handed a share of the meat to each member of the family, one by one. Peninnah received her portion, then each of her children received theirs. "All her sons and daughters" shows how many there were. Her family was large, and the serving of the meal made that obvious to everyone at the table.

That is what made this meal so hard for Hannah. Israel understood children as a gift from God: "Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD, the fruit of the womb is a reward" (Psalm 127:3). The feast was meant to celebrate the LORD's blessings on the household, and Peninnah's children were the clearest sign of blessing at that table. Hannah received her own portion (1:5), but no child stood beside her waiting for one. Every year, in the place where the family came to rejoice before God, the dividing of the meat reminded her of what she did not have.

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] Rivalries Acted Out at the Altar. The portions were handed out on the day Elkanah sacrificed. Religious activities and church settings can become the very stage where our deepest insecurities and rivalries are acted out.

[Foundational Truth] The Economy of Natural Provision. Every son and every daughter received a portion, yet nothing on that table could fill what was empty in Hannah. The distribution of earthly portions only highlights how inadequate natural provision is to satisfy spiritual emptiness.

1 Samuel 1:5
NKJV

1 Samuel 1:5 But to Hannah he would give a double portion, for he loved Hannah, although the LORD had closed her womb.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 1:5 And though he loved Hannah, he would give her only one choice portion because the LORD had given her no children. (NLT)

Main Idea

Elkanah gave Hannah a double portion to show his special love for her, even though the LORD had not allowed her to have children. The verse makes clear that Hannah's childlessness was under God's control, not a sign that she was forgotten or unloved.

Verse Explanation

Elkanah gave Hannah a double portion to show his special love for her, even though the LORD had not allowed her to have children. The verse makes clear that Hannah's childlessness was under God's control, not a sign that she was forgotten or unloved.

"But to Hannah he would give a double portion,"

At the family's sacrificial meal, Elkanah handed out portions of the meat to Peninnah and to each of her sons and daughters (1:4). Hannah had no children to share in the meal, so by ordinary custom she would have received a single share. Instead, Elkanah gave her a double portion, a larger share that marked her with special honor. The Hebrew expression is unusual, and the King James Version renders it "worthy portion," but the point is clear either way: Hannah's share stood out. The words "would give" describe what Elkanah did year after year, not on just one occasion. In that setting, a larger serving at a meal was a public way of showing favor, much as Joseph had Benjamin served far more than his brothers (Genesis 43:34). Everyone at the table could see whom Elkanah cherished.

"for he loved Hannah,"

Scripture states Elkanah's love for Hannah plainly. Her childlessness had not lowered her in his eyes. The situation resembles Jacob's household, where Jacob loved Rachel more than Leah, yet "the LORD saw that Leah was unloved, He opened her womb; but Rachel was barren" (Genesis 29:31). In both homes, the wife who was most loved was the one without children, while another wife filled the house with sons and daughters. That kind of imbalance bred bitter rivalry in Jacob's family, and the same tension runs through Elkanah's home (1:6). Elkanah's kindness was real, but a larger portion of meat could not give Hannah what she longed for.

"although the LORD had closed her womb."

Hannah's childlessness was not an accident of nature, and nothing in the passage suggests it was a punishment for sin. The LORD Himself had withheld children from her. Scripture consistently presents the opening and closing of the womb as God's doing. He "closed up all the wombs of the house of Abimelech" (Genesis 20:18), He opened Leah's womb while Rachel remained barren (Genesis 29:31), and He later opened Rachel's womb as well (Genesis 30:22). Both Sarah and Rebekah were barren for years before God gave them sons who would carry His promises forward (Genesis 18:11–14; 25:21).

This truth cuts two ways for Hannah. It meant her sorrow came under God's control, and that could feel even harder to bear, because the One she worshiped at Shiloh was the One who had not given her a child. But it also meant her situation was not hopeless. The LORD who had closed her womb was the only One who could open it, and that is exactly where Hannah would take her grief. Her husband's love was genuine, yet the answer to her need would have to come from God.

Study Notes

The Hebrew behind "double portion" is unusual, which is why translations differ. It reads literally "one portion of two faces" or "of two nostrils." The word can mean "face," so the phrase may describe a portion of honor, the kind set before a guest someone wishes to favor. Most translators take the doubled form of the word to mean "double."

The ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, the Septuagint, reads the verse differently: Elkanah gave Hannah "one portion" because she had no child, though he loved her more than Peninnah. Some modern translations follow this, so that Elkanah gave Hannah only a single share but still loved her. Several details favor the NKJV's reading. The verse begins with "But," which sets Hannah's portion against the many portions given to Peninnah's family in verse 4. The words "for he loved Hannah" read naturally as the reason for a special gift. Elsewhere in Scripture a larger or reserved portion marks honor, as when Samuel had a special portion set aside for Saul (9:23–24). Either way, the main point stands: Elkanah's affection was real, but it could not remove Hannah's grief.

Spirit & Life

[Christ & Life] Love Exceeding Output. Elkanah gives Hannah a double portion, or a worthy, choice portion, simply because he loved her. His love was not tied to her to her fruitfulness. This is a beautiful shadow of Christ’s love for His bride: He loves us not for what we produce, but because we are His. We are invited to a table where our portion rests on the finished work of Christ, not on our natural fruitfulness. Grace provides a double portion precisely where there is no performance or fruit to earn it.

[Foundational Truth] The Sovereign Closure of the Womb. The text says plainly, and repeats it in the next verse, that “the LORD had closed her womb.” Hannah’s barrenness was not an accident of nature; it was a divine assignment. God closed her womb in the natural so that He could open her spirit to bring forth something supernatural, and so that the child, when he came, would be recognized entirely as His work. God will sometimes frustrate our natural fruitfulness to press us into a desperate dependency that births a lasting spiritual legacy. Without the barrenness, Hannah might have had another farmer instead of a prophet that changed history. Divine barrenness is often the forerunner of divine birthing.

[Christ & Life] Closings That Drive Us to Christ. God’s intentional closings in our lives are designed to drive us past earthly comforts and desires into desperate communion with Christ.

[Insight] Meat Cannot Satisfy a Longing for a Son. The double portion of meat could not satisfy a woman who wanted a son. Elkanah tried to solve a spiritual crisis with material abundance. As well intentioned as we may be, our best natural solutions can never solve or fulfill that which only God can supply.

1 Samuel 1:6
NKJV

1 Samuel 1:6 And her rival also provoked her severely, to make her miserable, because the LORD had closed her womb.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 1:6 So Peninnah would taunt Hannah and make fun of her because the LORD had kept her from having children. (NLT)

Main Idea

Peninnah, called Hannah's rival, cruelly taunted her over her inability to have children, deliberately trying to make her miserable. The repeated reminder that the LORD had closed Hannah's womb shows that her suffering was something only God could change.

Verse Explanation

Peninnah, called Hannah's rival, cruelly taunted her over her inability to have children, deliberately trying to make her miserable. The repeated reminder that the LORD had closed Hannah's womb shows that her suffering was something only God could change.

"And her rival also provoked her severely,"

The "rival" is Peninnah, Elkanah's other wife, who had children while Hannah had none (1:2). The Hebrew word comes from a root meaning distress or hostility. It describes a woman who shares a husband with another wife and becomes her opponent. Scripture shows again and again the trouble that came from homes with more than one wife. Hagar looked down on Sarah once she conceived (Genesis 16:4–5), and Rachel envied Leah's children so bitterly that she cried to Jacob, "Give me children, or else I die!" (Genesis 30:1). Hannah lived in that same kind of painful household.

The word "also" sets Peninnah's cruelty beside what comes just before. Elkanah gave Hannah a double portion because he loved her (1:5), but Peninnah worked against her. The Hebrew literally says she "provoked her with provocation," a doubled expression that makes the taunting stronger, which is why the NKJV adds "severely." The word for "provoked" means to vex, irritate, or stir someone up to grief or anger. This was not a single careless remark but steady, painful pressure. Many interpreters suggest that Peninnah resented Elkanah's affection for Hannah and used her own children to strike back. Scripture does not state her motive, but it leaves no doubt about what she did.

"to make her miserable,"

Peninnah's taunting had a purpose. She meant to upset Hannah and keep her stirred up, and she went after the one thing Hannah could not answer. Scripture records no reply from Hannah. When the taunting came again year after year, she wept and would not eat (1:7).

"because the LORD had closed her womb."

These words repeat what was said in verse 5, that the LORD had withheld children from her. The repetition shows what Peninnah's mockery focused on. In a culture where childlessness brought shame, Hannah's empty arms were an easy target. Peninnah could use Hannah's barrenness as a weapon, and Elkanah could try to comfort her, but neither of them could change it. The cause lay with the LORD, and so the answer could come only from Him. That is where Hannah would finally take her sorrow, pouring out her heart in prayer at Shiloh (1:10).

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Application] Bring the Wound to God. The place of our deepest pain can become the place of our deepest encounter with God. Peninnah meant to wound Hannah, but she could not decide what Hannah would do with that wound. We cannot always choose what others do to us, but we can choose to carry what they have done into the presence of God. There, instead of festering into bitterness, our wounds can become prayer.

[Insight] An Attack Aimed at Her Faith. Notice the reason given for the provocation: “because the LORD had closed her womb.” Peninnah likely used Hannah’s own theology against her: “If God loves you, why has He closed your womb?” The provocation was aimed at Hannah’s faith, not just her fertility. The enemy’s sharpest attacks often take what God has allowed and turn it into an accusation against God’s love.

[Christ & Life] Mocked for What They Cannot See. In our barrenness we share in the fellowship of Christ’s sufferings (Philippians 3:10), mocked by those who cannot perceive the invisible work of the Spirit.

[Application] Beware of Comparison. Peninnah had children and much to be thankful for, yet comparison with Hannah seems to have turned her blessing into rivalry. Comparison moves our eyes off God’s goodness and onto ourselves and others. Receive what God has given you with gratitude, rather than letting someone else’s portion rob you of contentment.

[Insight] An Attack Reveals the Attacker’s Heart. Sometimes another person’s attack says more about what is happening inside them than about us. Peninnah’s cruelty, sustained year after year, may have come from a heart that did not know how deeply it was loved. That does not excuse her behavior. But it can help us see those who hurt us differently and lead us to ask the Father to show us how to pray for them.

"So it was, year by year,"

This was not a single bad visit. The pattern repeated every year, and the opening of the chapter shows it had gone on long enough to become routine (1:3). Year after year Hannah stayed childless, and year after year the same painful scene played out. The repetition made it worse, because each trip brought back the old wound.

"when she went up to the house of the LORD,"

The family's yearly trip to worship at Shiloh, where the tabernacle stood, was meant to be a time of joy. Israel's feasts centered on eating and rejoicing together before God, and the Law pictured whole households celebrating: "You shall rejoice before the LORD your God, you and your son and your daughter" (Deuteronomy 16:11). For Hannah, that picture of family gladness only showed what she lacked. At the family's sacrificial meal, Peninnah received portions for all her sons and daughters (1:4), while Hannah had no children to share her portion with. The setting itself put her emptiness on display. Still, she kept going up to the house of the LORD year after year, and it was there that she would finally pour out her heart to Him (1:10).

"that she provoked her;"

The pronouns refer to two different women: Peninnah provoked Hannah. The word is the same one used for Peninnah's deliberate taunting in the previous verse (1:6). What stands out here is the timing. Peninnah's cruelty came in its sharpest form during the very visits meant for honoring God. Peninnah, Hannah's rival, did more than rub in her advantage at home. She aimed her provocation at the moment when Hannah's sorrow was already closest to the surface.

"therefore she wept and did not eat."

Hannah was so crushed that she could not bring herself to eat. This was not a planned religious fast but the loss of appetite that comes with deep grief. Eating the sacrificial meal was itself part of the celebration before the LORD, so her sorrow kept her from sharing in the joy of worship. Elkanah noticed and asked, "Hannah, why do you weep? Why do you not eat?" (1:8). Later in the chapter, after she has prayed, these same two details are reversed (1:18).

Rather than simply bargaining with God for a child, her vow appears to reflect a response to God's leading about His purpose for this son, a purpose that would unfold as Samuel grew into Israel's prophet.

"Then she made a vow and said, 'O LORD of hosts,"

A vow was a solemn promise made to God, usually to give or do something for Him. It was voluntary, but once spoken it had to be kept. The law warned that the LORD would require it, and that failing to keep it was sin (Deuteronomy 23:21). Hannah was not making a casual promise in a moment of distress. She was binding herself before God at the tabernacle.

She addresses Him as the LORD of hosts, the LORD who commands heaven's armies. This is the first recorded prayer in Scripture to use that title. Hannah had no power over her circumstances, and Peninnah had mocked her for years, so she called on the One who rules every power in heaven and on earth.

"if You will indeed look on the affliction of Your maidservant"

The Hebrew repeats the verb for emphasis, something like "if You will truly see." Hannah's "affliction" was her childlessness and the misery that came with it, including Peninnah's taunting. Leah, another wife in a household with a rival, had used similar words when God gave her a son: "Surely the LORD has looked on my affliction" (Genesis 29:32). Hannah asks God to look on her in the same way.

Three times in this one sentence Hannah calls herself "Your maidservant," a female servant. This is a humble way of speaking about herself before God. She does not come demanding her rights. She comes as a servant asking her Master for help. Centuries later Mary used the same kind of language when she answered the angel: "Behold the maidservant of the Lord!" (Luke 1:38). In her song she also praised God because "He has regarded the lowly state of His maidservant" (Luke 1:48), words that echo Hannah's prayer.

"and remember me, and not forget Your maidservant,"

When God "remembers" someone in Scripture, it does not mean He had forgotten them. It means He acts on their behalf. Hannah says it two ways, "remember me" and "not forget," and this shows how deeply she felt her need. Her words are answered directly a few verses later, when "the LORD remembered her" (1:19).

"but will give Your maidservant a male child,"

The Hebrew is literally "offspring of men," a son who would grow into a man. Hannah's request is specific. She asks for a son, and in the same breath she explains what that son would be for.

"then I will give him to the LORD all the days of his life,"

Hannah's request and her promise belong together. She is not trying to buy a child from God with an offer He could not refuse. Her vow is best understood as her response to God's leading about His purpose for this son. Scripture does not record how God made that purpose known to her, but she asked for a child she already intended to belong wholly to the LORD. She was asking God to give her a son so she could give him back.

The gift went far beyond what the law required. Samuel came from the tribe of Levi (1 Chronicles 6:33–38), and Levites normally served at the tabernacle only during their adult working years (Numbers 8:24–25). Hannah promised her son for his entire life, beginning in childhood. She kept that promise in full when she brought Samuel to Shiloh (1:28).

"and no razor shall come upon his head."

Uncut hair was the visible mark of a Nazirite, a person set apart to God by a special vow (Numbers 6:1–8). Nazirite vows were usually made by adults for a limited time. Before Samson's birth, the angel spoke these same words about him: "no razor shall come upon his head, for the child shall be a Nazirite to God from the womb" (Judges 13:5). Like Samson, Samuel would be set apart from birth and for life. Samson treated his consecration carelessly, but Samuel would serve the LORD faithfully from childhood to old age, and under his leadership Israel would finally see the Philistines subdued (7:13). Every time people saw Samuel's long hair, it would remind them that this boy belonged to God.

Study Notes

Hannah's vow mentions only the razor and never uses the word "Nazirite," but ancient evidence shows that early readers understood Samuel as one. The Septuagint adds to her vow, "and he shall drink no wine or intoxicating drink," the other main Nazirite rule (Numbers 6:3). A copy of Samuel found among the Dead Sea Scrolls includes at verse 22 the words "I will give him as a Nazirite forever, all the days of his life." The Hebrew text of the book of Sirach, written about two centuries before Christ, also calls Samuel a Nazirite of the LORD. Later Jewish teachers debated the question, and one argued from the phrase about the razor, which also appears in the account of Samson's birth (Judges 13:5).

The standard Hebrew text behind the NKJV does not include these additions. Most scholars regard them as later expansions that state openly what the razor clause already implies, though some think they preserve original wording. If Hannah's vow did include abstaining from wine for her son, Eli's accusation that she was drunk carries an added irony.

Hannah was praying silently in her heart, with her lips moving but no sound coming out, which was unusual since prayer was normally spoken aloud. Seeing this, Eli jumped to the wrong conclusion that she was drunk.

Verse Explanation

Hannah was praying silently in her heart, with her lips moving but no sound coming out, which was unusual since prayer was normally spoken aloud. Seeing this, Eli jumped to the wrong conclusion that she was drunk.

"Now Hannah spoke in her heart;"

In Scripture the heart is a person's inner life: thoughts, desires, and will. Hannah's prayer came from deep inside her and was meant for God alone. She had come to the LORD "in bitterness of soul" and "wept in anguish" (1:10), and her words stayed within, between her and the LORD. Abraham's servant once prayed in much the same way at the well, and before he "had finished speaking in my heart," God had already brought Rebekah to him (Genesis 24:45). A prayer does not need to be heard by people to be heard by God.

"only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard."

In Israel, people usually prayed out loud, even when praying privately. The psalmist could say, "I cried to the LORD with my voice, and He heard me" (Psalm 3:4). Hannah's silent prayer would have looked strange to anyone watching. Her lips formed the words, but no sound came out. She had been praying for a long time (1:12), and the sight of a woman standing near the tabernacle at Shiloh, weeping and silently mouthing words, could easily be misread.

"Therefore Eli thought she was drunk."

Eli, the priest at Shiloh, had been sitting by the doorpost of the tabernacle (1:9) and had been watching her mouth (1:12). He saw her moving lips but missed the sorrow behind them. The family had just finished "eating and drinking" at the sacrificial meal (1:9), and wine was part of such feasts, so drunkenness at a festival may not have seemed unlikely to him. Still, the priest who should have recognized a worshiper in distress judged her by what he could see and got it badly wrong. Later the LORD would tell Samuel, "man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (16:7). Eli heard nothing, but the LORD heard every word Hannah spoke in her heart.

Spirit & Life

[Discovery] The Silent Prayer God Heard. “Hannah spoke in her heart; only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard.” Eli heard nothing, but God heard everything. In a world where prayer was usually spoken aloud, Hannah’s silent prayer stood out. The thoughts of our hearts are heard as clearly in heaven as the words of our mouths. The volume of a prayer is not the measure of its depth; the depth of prayer is measured by the engagement of the heart. There are times when sorrow is too deep to say much aloud, but silence does not keep God from hearing us. Often the heart speaks loudest when the voice is silent.

[Insight] The Flesh Misreads the Spirit. Eli thought she was drunk. Deep, Spirit-led, agonizing prayer is frequently misunderstood by those accustomed only to formal or superficial religion. The religious system Eli represented lacked the discernment to recognize the deep, groaning work of the Spirit (Romans 8:26). Carnal leadership will often mislabel spiritual desperation as intoxication or disorder, just as the crowd at Pentecost mistook Spirit-filled believers for men full of new wine (Acts 2:13). The deep, silent communion of the inner life is often mistaken by the natural man for drunkenness or madness. The believer must be prepared to look foolish, or be misjudged by the religious establishment, when moving in the Spirit.

[Application] Slow to Judge What We Do Not Understand. Spiritual leadership requires more than seeing what is happening outwardly; it requires discernment. Eli saw Hannah’s moving lips and wrongly concluded she was drunk. Intense, travailing prayer has often been misread by onlookers throughout the history of the church. We should be slow to judge what we do not understand. Paul reminds us, “But the natural man does not receive the things of the Spirit of God, for they are foolishness to him; nor can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned” (1 Corinthians 2:14, NKJV).

[Insight] Our Heart Shapes What Our Eyes See. Eli was accustomed to the corrupt behavior of his own sons, Hophni and Phinehas, at the sanctuary, and he projected their fleshly conduct onto Hannah’s spiritual agony. Paul writes, “To the pure all things are pure, but to those who are defiled and unbelieving nothing is pure” (Titus 1:15). What we tolerate in our own lives can distort how we perceive others. Eli had become so familiar with corruption that he mistook genuine intercession for drunkenness.

[Foundational Truth] Knowledge of God Is Not Knowing God. Being around the things of God does not make us spiritual. Eli lived and served at the very center of Israel’s worship, yet he mistook a woman pouring out her heart to God for someone who was drunk. Familiarity with the things of God must never become a substitute for knowing God Himself.

"So Eli said to her,"

Eli was sitting on his seat by the doorpost of the tabernacle (1:9), where he could watch the worshipers. As Hannah kept praying, he studied her face. Her lips moved, but no sound came out, because "she spoke in her heart" (1:13). In that day people usually prayed out loud, so a woman standing before the LORD's house for a long time, mouthing words without a voice, looked strange to him. He drew the wrong conclusion and spoke to her before asking a single question.

"How long will you be drunk?"

Eli's question was really an accusation. "How long" suggests he thought she had been drunk for some time and was still carrying on. His mistake is understandable in one sense. The yearly visits to Shiloh centered on a sacrificial meal, and Hannah's family had just "finished eating and drinking" (1:9). In the days of the judges, festivals at Shiloh could become rowdy occasions (Judges 21:19–21), and Eli had likely seen worshipers drink too much at these meals. As priest, he was responsible for guarding the holiness of the LORD's house.

Still, Eli badly misread what was in front of him. Hannah was not drunk. She was pouring out her grief to the LORD in deep, silent prayer. The man whose life was devoted to God's house could not tell the difference between drunkenness and earnest prayer. His sharp words also stand in contrast to the way he treated his own sons, the priests at Shiloh, whose failures came later. Their sins were far worse, but his rebuke to them was mild, and he did not restrain them (2:22–25; 3:13). He was quick to correct a grieving woman and slow to correct the men who were dishonoring the LORD's offerings.

"Put your wine away from you!"

The phrase can mean "Get rid of your wine," or more broadly, "Sober up." Either way, Eli was ordering Hannah to stop disgracing herself in front of the tabernacle. For Hannah, the timing could hardly have been worse. She had come with tears and "bitterness of soul" (1:10), and she had just made a solemn vow, offering to give her son to the LORD for life. At her most honest moment before God, the priest who should have encouraged her treated her like a drunkard. Her answer shows humility and self-control. She did not lash out at him. She calmly explained that she was not drunk but deeply sorrowful (1:15–16).

Spirit & Life

[Insight] Tolerating Wolves, Attacking Sheep. Eli, who failed to restrain his own sons’ open wickedness, was quick to falsely accuse a righteous woman of drunkenness. The man who should have been interceding for the people was instead accusing the only true intercessor in Shiloh. His inability to tell the groaning of the Spirit from the effects of wine exposes the spiritual bankruptcy of the place. When spiritual leaders lose their discernment, they tolerate wolves but attack the sheep. [Application] Let the Lord Search Us First. Eli was quick to correct Hannah for what he thought was wrong, while later chapters reveal that he failed to deal firmly with the serious sins of his own sons (1 Samuel 2:12–17, 22–25; 3:13). It is easier to see what we think is wrong in someone else than to face what God wants to correct in us. Before we rush to correct someone else, we should let the Lord search our own hearts.

[Application] The Wounds of the Watchman. “Put your wine away from you!” Eli’s rebuke was harsh, inaccurate, and based on superficial observation. In Song of Solomon 5:7, the bride searching for her beloved encounters the watchmen: “They struck me, they wounded me.” We must be prepared for the wounds of the watchman—those painful moments when the very people who should help us find the Lord misunderstand, criticize, or even falsely accuse us. Hannah did not allow Eli’s rebuke to turn her heart away from God. She refused to become offended or abandon her pursuit of the Lord. We must never allow the wounds of the watchman to keep us from the One we are seeking.

[Foundational Truth] Discernment Needs More Than Eyesight. The natural mind cannot understand the things of the Spirit and often mislabels spiritual intensity as fleshly excess. Spiritual discernment requires more than physical eyesight; it requires the illumination of the Holy Spirit.

[Christ & Life] The True Judge Knows. When falsely accused, the believer can rest in the reality that Christ, the true Judge, knows the purity of their motives.

[Seed] Seek to Understand. Eli rebuked Hannah before understanding what was happening in her heart. Leaders must learn to seek to understand before speaking, correcting, or acting. Discernment begins with listening, not assuming.

The Hebrew expression literally means "hard of spirit," describing a spirit weighed down and burdened. Hannah had come to the tabernacle "in bitterness of soul" (1:10), carrying years of grief over her childlessness and Peninnah's taunting. What Eli took for drunkenness was the outward sign of a heavy heart. Hannah did not hide her sorrow or pretend to be fine. She named it honestly to the priest.

"I have drunk neither wine nor intoxicating drink,"

"Intoxicating drink" refers to strong fermented drinks other than wine, often made from grain or fruit. Hannah denies having touched anything that could make her drunk. Wine was a normal part of the family's sacrificial meal, and she had just come from that meal (1:9). That may help explain why Eli jumped to his conclusion. The accusation carries a painful irony. Priests were forbidden to drink "wine or intoxicating drink" when they went into the tabernacle (Leviticus 10:9), because they served in God's presence. Yet the real disgrace at Shiloh was not a praying woman but Eli's own sons, the priests whose failures would soon be exposed (2:12–17). Eli was quick to rebuke a woman praying in sorrow while he failed to deal with the serious sin in his own household.

"but have poured out my soul before the LORD."

Eli had told her to "put your wine away," as though she had been pouring out drink. Hannah answers that what she had poured out was her soul. The picture is of a vessel emptied completely, holding nothing back. She had brought her whole grief to God. The psalmist calls God's people to pray the same way: "Pour out your heart before Him; God is a refuge for us" (Psalm 62:8).

"Before the LORD" shows where her prayer was directed. She was not speaking for Eli or anyone else to hear. She was speaking to the LORD Himself, and that is why her lips moved without sound. The priest misunderstood her, but the One she was speaking to did not.

Spirit & Life

[Application] Honor Without Taking Offense. Being wrongly judged does not give us permission to respond wrongly. Hannah did not accept Eli’s false accusation, but neither did she answer with anger or turn his own household’s failures against him. She spoke the truth with humility and courage, honoring the very priest who had misjudged her. We cannot always prevent others from wounding us, but we can refuse to let offense take root in our hearts. We can stand for the truth without retaliating, becoming defensive, or dishonoring those who have wronged us.

True prayer is not merely the recitation of words. It is emptying our anxieties, desires, and griefs into the hands of God, emptying the vessel of self so it can be filled with divine purpose. That emptying creates room for the Holy Spirit to fill. To pour out the soul is to hold nothing back, edit nothing, and hide nothing from God.

"Go in peace,"

This was a common way of saying goodbye in Israel, but it carried real meaning. The Hebrew word for "peace," shalom, means wholeness and well-being, not simply the absence of trouble. Hannah had come to the tabernacle "in bitterness of soul" (1:10), and now she is sent away with a word of peace. Eli spoke as the LORD's priest, and blessing the people was part of a priest's work. The blessing God gave Aaron and his sons to speak over Israel ends with these words: "The LORD lift up His countenance upon you, and give you peace" (Numbers 6:26). Centuries later Jesus sent away a suffering woman He had healed with the same words: "Daughter, your faith has made you well. Go in peace" (Mark 5:34).

"and the God of Israel grant your petition which you have asked of Him."

Eli did not know what Hannah had asked for. She had told him only that she was praying out of deep grief, not that she wanted a son or had made a vow. Eli simply placed her request in the hands of "the God of Israel," the God who had made Israel His own people and heard their cries. The Hebrew can be read either as a prayer, "may the God of Israel grant," or as a statement, "the God of Israel will grant." Either way, Hannah received these words as more than polite comfort. She went away, ate, and "her face was no longer sad" (1:18), even though nothing had changed in her circumstances yet.

In Hebrew, "your petition which you have asked" is literally "your asking which you asked." The word for "asked" comes from the same root Hannah would later use to explain Samuel's name, "Because I have asked for him from the LORD" (1:20), and when she returned with the boy she would point back to this very blessing (1:27). God used the words of an aging priest, who had misjudged her only moments before, to give Hannah assurance that her prayer had been heard.

"As your soul lives, my lord,"

This is a solemn way of affirming that what follows is true, meaning "as surely as you are alive." The same expression appears elsewhere when someone wants to assure another person of his sincerity (1 Samuel 17:55; 2 Kings 2:2). Hannah may have used it because a good deal of time had passed since Eli last saw her. Samuel had been born and weaned in the meantime. Eli would have seen countless worshipers come and go at Shiloh, and he may not have recognized the woman standing before him or understood why she was bringing him a small child.

"I am the woman who stood by you here, praying to the LORD."

Hannah takes Eli back to that earlier day at Shiloh. Eli had been sitting by the doorpost of the tabernacle (1:9) while she stood nearby, praying in deep distress. She prayed silently, with only her lips moving, and Eli concluded she was drunk: "How long will you be drunk? Put your wine away from you!" (1:14). When she explained that she had been pouring out her soul before the LORD, he sent her away with a blessing (1:17).

The word "here" shows that Hannah was standing in the very place where she had once prayed. Eli had misread her prayer as drunkenness. Now the priest who once rebuked her is looking at the answer to that prayer, a child standing right in front of him. By saying she was "praying to the LORD," Hannah also makes clear where the answer came from. Eli had blessed her, but it was the LORD she had asked, and the LORD who had given her this son.

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] The Secret Place Revealed. Hannah introduced herself not by her family line but by her posture of prayer: “I am the woman who stood by you here, praying to the LORD.” She pointed Eli back to her moment of desperate, silent prayer. The results of secret prayer are eventually seen in public. The prayer that was hidden in her heart, and misunderstood by the priest, was now vindicated by the child at her side.

[Christ & Life] Sorrow Does Not Have the Final Word. Hannah stood again where she had once wept, but now Samuel stood beside her as the living answer to that very prayer. This finds its fullest expression in Jesus. The cross, the place of deepest sorrow, became through His resurrection the great testimony of God’s saving victory. In Christ, sorrow does not have the final word.

[Application] Known as One Who Prayed. The most glorious identity a person can carry is “the one who stood here praying.” Our truest identity in the kingdom of God is forged in our secret history of intercession. God transformed Hannah’s identity from her affliction, the barren woman, to her victory, the praying woman. In Christ our past shame is erased, and we are known by our communion with the Father. Let your defining characteristic be that you are a person of prayer. If you came back to this altar years from now, could you say, “I am the one who stood here praying”?

Verse Explanation

Hannah declared that she had prayed for this very child and that the LORD had given her exactly what she asked. Her testimony gives God full credit for Samuel's birth.

"For this child I prayed,"

Hannah is standing before Eli at Shiloh, with young Samuel beside her, now that he has been weaned. Years earlier she had stood in this same place "in bitterness of soul" and prayed while she "wept in anguish" (1:10), so quietly that only her lips moved (1:13). Now she can point to the boy and tell the priest that he is the very child she asked for. Her request had been specific: "give Your maidservant a male child" (1:11). This was not a general wish for better days. She asked God for a son, and the son was standing in front of them.

These words also repeat the reason she gave for his name. When Samuel was born, Hannah named him, "Because I have asked for him from the LORD" (1:20). Every time she spoke his name, she was reminded where he came from. Now, years later, she tells Eli the same thing. Her gratitude had not faded once the child grew out of infancy.

"and the LORD has granted me my petition which I asked of Him."

These are nearly the same words Eli spoke when he sent her away in peace: "the God of Israel grant your petition which you have asked of Him" (1:17). At the time, Eli's words were a blessing and a hope. Hannah now repeats them as a report of what God actually did. The priest who once mistook her for a drunken woman hears from her own mouth that his blessing came true.

In Hebrew, "petition" and "asked" come from the same root word, which means to ask or request. It is the same idea behind Samuel's name in verse 20. Hannah's language keeps returning to asking, and that makes the answer stand out even more. She had asked, and the LORD had given.

Hannah does not credit her own persistence, Eli's position, or her husband's love for the child's birth. She names the LORD as the One who granted her request. The same God who had withheld children from her is the One who gave her this son. Her prayer came from her deep sorrow, but it was also tied to God's purpose for the child, since she had already promised to give him to the LORD for life. The apostle John later wrote, "if we ask anything according to His will, He hears us. And if we know that He hears us, whatever we ask, we know that we have the petitions that we have asked of Him" (1 John 5:14–15). Hannah is a living example of that confidence. Because she receives Samuel as God's gift, she is ready to give him back to God, and her testimony soon turns into a song: "My heart rejoices in the LORD" (2:1).

None.

Spirit & Life

[Application] Radical Acknowledgment of the Source. “For this child I prayed, and the LORD has granted me my petition.” Hannah’s testimony was simple. She took no credit for her fruitfulness. She did not boast to Peninnah; she pointed entirely to the LORD, who heard and answered. The life she held was a direct gift from heaven. Testify boldly to what God has done, and connect the answered prayer directly to the God who gave it.

[Discovery] The Invisible Petition Made Visible. Hannah held up the boy as the tangible answer to invisible groans. The prayer no one could hear had become a child everyone could see. Samuel was, in a sense, the word made flesh in her own life: a spiritual petition that took on physical form. Answered prayer is the visible fruit of the hidden union between the believer’s asking and God’s giving.

[Insight] Confirming Eli’s Blessing. Hannah used the same language Eli had used in verse 17, “grant your petition.” She was confirming to the priest that his blessing had come true.

[Application] Pray Specifically. Specific prayer can become unmistakable testimony. Hannah could say, “For this child I prayed,” and stand before Eli with the very answer beside her. There is value in bringing our desires before God specifically, so that we can recognize His answer when it comes.

[Application] Tell the One Who Saw Your Sorrow. Hannah deliberately returned to name what God had done rather than let it pass uncredited. There is life in telling plainly, to someone who once saw our sorrow, exactly what the Lord has done.

[Seed] For This I Prayed, and This God Did. God is a prayer-answering God who delights to grant the petitions of His people. Our testimony can be just this simple: “For this I prayed, and this God did.”

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Hannah fulfilled her vow by giving Samuel to the LORD for his entire life, and the family worshiped God there at Shiloh. By keeping her promise, Hannah showed that true thankfulness means giving back to God what He has given, and Samuel's lifelong service would bless all of Israel.

"Therefore I also have lent him to the LORD;"

"Therefore" ties this sentence to what Hannah has just said: "For this child I prayed, and the LORD has granted me my petition which I asked of Him" (1:27). Because the LORD gave her the son she asked for, she now gives that son back to Him. The word "also" carries that sense: the LORD gave, and she gives in return.

The Hebrew verb translated "lent" comes from the same root as "asked" in the previous verse. Hannah asked the LORD for Samuel, and now she hands him over to the LORD as one asked for. Samuel's name already reminded her that she had asked for him from the LORD (1:20). The child who came through her asking is now given back in answer to God. The English "lent" can sound like a temporary arrangement, but the rest of the sentence shows that nothing temporary is meant.

"as long as he lives he shall be lent to the LORD."

This is Hannah's vow kept in full. In her prayer at Shiloh she had promised, "then I will give him to the LORD all the days of his life, and no razor shall come upon his head" (1:11). She made that vow in response to God's leading, and now she keeps every part of it. She does not offer a few years of service or plan to bring Samuel home when he is older. He belongs to the LORD for as long as he lives.

That went well beyond what was normally required. Samuel came from the tribe of Levi (1 Chronicles 6:33–38), and Levites served at the tabernacle during a set period of adult life, beginning at twenty-five and retiring at fifty (Numbers 8:24–25). Samuel's service began while he was still a small child, once he was weaned, and it would never end. This was costly for Hannah. After this visit Elkanah returned home to Ramah while "the child ministered to the LORD before Eli the priest" (2:11). From then on Hannah saw her son only on the family's yearly trips, when she brought him a little robe she had made (2:19).

The LORD used that lifelong gift to bless the whole nation. Samuel grew up at Shiloh, and the LORD spoke to him at a time when "the word of the LORD was rare" (3:1). Before long "all Israel from Dan to Beersheba knew that Samuel had been established as a prophet of the LORD" (3:20). He called Israel back from idols, prayed for them, and led them as their judge, and "Samuel judged Israel all the days of his life" (7:15). That phrase echoes Hannah's vow. The son she gave "all the days of his life" spent all the days of his life serving God's people. He anointed Israel's first two kings, Saul (10:1) and David (16:13), and he refused to stop praying for the people even after they demanded a king (12:23). Scripture later ranks him alongside Moses as one of the great intercessors of Israel (Psalm 99:6; Jeremiah 15:1). Peter also names him as the first of the prophets who foretold the coming of Christ (Acts 3:24).

"So they worshiped the LORD there."

"Worshiped" means to bow down before the LORD in reverence. It took place "there," at Shiloh, where the tabernacle stood, the same place where Hannah had once wept and refused to eat during the yearly trip to worship (1:7). Now, at the very moment she leaves her young son behind, the family bows before the LORD. Hannah's response to giving up what she loved most was not bitterness but worship, and her prayer of praise that immediately follows begins, "My heart rejoices in the LORD" (2:1).

Study Notes

The verb Hannah uses here builds on the explanation of Samuel's name in verse 20, and it carries a significance for the rest of the book. The words "he shall be lent" translate a single Hebrew word, sha'ul, meaning "asked for" or "given in answer to asking." That is exactly the Hebrew form of the name Saul. The name Samuel itself is not built from this verb. It most likely means "name of God" or is connected with "heard by God." Hannah's explanation in verse 20 is a play on the sound of the name, not a strict definition.

Some scholars have suggested the birth story originally explained Saul's name, but no ancient manuscript supports this. A likelier view is that the wordplay deliberately anticipates what comes later. The same verb returns when Israel "asked" Samuel for a king (8:10; 12:13, 17). Many interpreters see an intended contrast. Hannah's asked-for son was given back to the LORD for life, while the asked-for king Israel demanded for itself would ultimately fail. The echo is plausible and fits the book's story, though Scripture does not spell it out.

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] The Irrevocable Yield. “Therefore I also have lent him to the LORD.” The word for “lent” or “dedicated” comes from the same root as “asked” (sha’al), the word Hannah tied to Samuel’s name. She asked for him, and now she gives him back. What we receive through prayer must be yielded back to the Giver. If we clutch the blessing, it becomes an idol; if we dedicate it to God, it becomes a kingdom legacy.

[Christ & Life] Loving the Giver More Than the Gift. Faith does not clutch the answer once it comes. Hannah had desperately sought Samuel, but when God gave him to her, she gave him back. Her surrender showed that she loved the Giver more than the gift she had so deeply desired. It is the same pattern the Father would one day complete in reverse: He so loved the world that He gave His only Son (John 3:16), so that everything we now receive from Him, including our very lives, might be freely given back.

[Seed] Holding the Gift Without It Holding Us. Like Abraham with Isaac, Hannah shows the pattern: God gives the gift, we offer it back, and then we can hold the gift without the gift holding us.

[Application] Never Wasted in His Hands. What is placed wholly in God’s hands is never wasted. Samuel was never Hannah’s to keep in the first place; surrendered fully to God, his life became far more than she could ever have made it by holding on.

[Christ & Life] We Are Not Our Own. Total consecration is the fitting response to divine grace. Just as Hannah lent Samuel to the LORD for a lifetime, we yield our lives entirely to Christ, for we are not our own; we were bought with a price (1 Corinthians 6:19–20).
