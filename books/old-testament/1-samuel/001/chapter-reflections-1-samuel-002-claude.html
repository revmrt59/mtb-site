Chapter Reflections

1 Samuel 2

Hannah’s Song, Eli’s Sons, and the Faithful Priest

Foundational Truths · Chapter Insights · Christ & Life · Dwelling in the Word
Scripture quotations are from the New King James Version (NKJV).

Foundational Truths

1. The LORD is holy, incomparable, and fully knowing. Hannah’s song begins with who God is before it turns to what He has done: “No one is holy like the LORD … nor is there any rock like our God” (2:2), and “the LORD is the God of knowledge; and by Him actions are weighed” (2:3). The rest of the chapter shows that holiness from two sides. The same God who stoops to lift a weeping woman refuses to be treated lightly at His altar. Mercy to Hannah and judgment on Eli’s house are not competing moods in God but one holy character meeting two different hearts.

2. God is sovereign over the circumstances people assume are fixed. Hannah moves through strength and weakness, hunger and plenty, barrenness and fruitfulness, life and death, poverty and honor (2:4–8). None of these conditions lies outside God’s rule, and none is His final verdict on a person’s worth. The song is not a formula that every barren woman will bear children or every poor person will prosper; it declares that what appears settled to us is not settled to God.

3. Nearness to holy things is not the same as knowing God. Hophni and Phinehas lived at the altar, wore the priestly garments, and handled the sacrifices daily, yet “they did not know the LORD” (2:12). Samuel, a child with no standing, “ministered to the LORD” and grew “before the LORD” in the same sanctuary (2:11, 21, 26). Religious position, activity, and heritage can exist without relationship, and the chapter calls everyone raised near sacred things to move from proximity to knowing Him.

4. Those entrusted with God’s things are stewards, not owners. God speaks of “My priest,” “My altar,” “My sacrifice and My offering,” “My dwelling place,” and “Israel My people” (2:28–29). Eli’s sons treated the offerings and the worshipers as if they existed for their own benefit, and the damage spread to the people who came to worship (2:17, 24). Greater privilege brings greater accountability, and God takes seriously both how His worship is handled and how His people are treated.

5. God honors those who honor Him. Verse 30 states the principle that governs the whole chapter: “those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed.” To honor God is to give Him weight when His will collides with other loyalties. Eli knew what was right and said so, yet when the choice came he gave greater weight to his sons (2:29). The chapter’s outcomes, a child growing in favor and a priestly house losing its place, show that principle already at work.

Chapter Insights

Hannah’s song interprets the chapter

Hannah’s prayer is not a detached poem placed before the story of Eli’s sons; it is the lens through which that story should be read. She sings that “those who were full have hired themselves out for bread” (2:5), and the chapter closes with Eli’s descendants begging “for a piece of silver and a morsel of bread” (2:36). She ends by speaking of God’s “anointed” (2:10), and the oracle ends with a faithful priest who will walk “before My anointed forever” (2:35). The chapter that begins with a barren woman lifted up ends with a proud house brought low. It is also striking that the chapter’s deepest theology comes from a grieving woman with no official standing, while the high priest, who once mistook her prayer for drunkenness, cannot discern or discipline his own household.

Two households in one sanctuary

The narrator cuts back and forth deliberately: Samuel ministering (2:11), the sons’ corruption (2:12–17), Samuel in his linen ephod (2:18–21), Eli’s failed rebuke (2:22–25), Samuel growing in favor (2:26), and the oracle of judgment (2:27–36). Light and darkness grow up side by side in the same house, under the same watching God. Two objects capture the contrast. The priests’ three-pronged fork reaches into worshipers’ pots to take what is not theirs (2:13–14); Hannah’s little robe is made at home and carried to Shiloh each year to clothe the son she has given away (2:19). One household comes to the sanctuary to take; one mother comes bringing something she has made and given. The difference between them is not age, title, or religious activity, but the orientation of the heart toward the LORD.

The escalation of self-serving ministry

The sins of Eli’s sons do not stay in one place. They take whatever the fork brings up (2:13–14), then demand their portion before God’s fat is burned (2:15), then threaten force when a worshiper objects (2:16), then commit sexual sin at the tabernacle door (2:22), until the worship of God itself is despised (2:17). Set beside them are two other postures. Eli knows what is right and speaks it but will not act (2:23–25, 29). The faithful priest God promises “shall do according to what is in My heart and in My mind” (2:35). The chapter sets three responses side by side: “what I want,” “what I know but will not do,” and “what God wants.”

What a person gives weight to

The Hebrew idea of honor is weight, and this thread runs through the story. Eli honors (gives weight to) his sons above God (2:29); God will honor those who honor Him and make light of those who despise Him (2:30). Two chapters later Eli, described as “heavy,” falls and dies (4:18), and his grandson is named Ichabod, “the glory has departed” (4:21), using the same word family. Eli gave weight to the wrong things, and the house that made God light lost its glory. The story finally asks what each of us treats as weighty.

Christ & Life

The Anointed King and the Faithful Priest

Hannah speaks of God’s “anointed” king before Israel has any king (2:10), and the oracle promises a faithful priest who will do what is in God’s heart, with a “sure house,” serving “before My anointed forever” (2:35). In their immediate setting these words anticipate Israel’s monarchy and a faithful priesthood after Eli’s house falls. Samuel is the faithful servant God raises up at once, and Zadok’s line serving David and Solomon is the clearest historical fulfillment of the priestly promise (1 Kings 2:27, 35). The same language of a “sure” house reappears in God’s covenant with David (2 Samuel 7:16).

Read in the light of the whole Bible, the two promises converge. The word anointed (mashiach) becomes “Messiah,” and Zechariah praises God for raising “a horn of salvation … in the house of His servant David” (Luke 1:69). In 1 Samuel the faithful priest and the anointed king are two different persons, one serving before the other. Hebrews brings the two offices together in Jesus, the “merciful and faithful High Priest” (Hebrews 2:17) who is also the royal priest after the order of Melchizedek (Hebrews 7). The chapter’s two hopes, a king God strengthens and a priest who does what is in God’s heart, meet in one Person. Where the priests at Shiloh used God’s house to serve themselves, believers now approach God through a Priest-King wholly given to the Father’s will.

The God Who Brings Low and Lifts Up

Hannah’s song celebrates a God who breaks the mighty, raises the poor from the ash heap, and holds even the grave in His hand (2:4–8). That pattern does not end with Hannah. Mary deliberately echoes her when she sings that God “has put down the mighty from their thrones, and exalted the lowly” (Luke 1:52), and the pattern reaches its deepest expression in Christ, who humbled Himself to the cross and was highly exalted (Philippians 2:6–11). Hannah does not speak of resurrection directly, yet the God who “brings down to the grave and brings up” (2:6) is the God who raised Jesus from the dead (Acts 2:24–32). For those who belong to Him, this is the ground for humility in strength and hope in weakness.

The Priests Who Took and the Priest Who Gave

Eli asks the question that hangs over his sons: “If a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?” (2:25). The men meant to stand between the people and God had become the ones who needed someone to stand for them. Eli is not pointing to Christ, but the gospel answers his question: “there is one God and one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5), who “always lives to make intercession” for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). The chapter’s two failures, priests who seize the offering and a priesthood with no one to plead for it, are answered together in the Good Shepherd who does not take from the sheep but lays down His life for them (John 10:11). Life with Christ therefore begins not with religious access or position but with knowing Him and resting on His intercession, and it shapes every form of leadership among His people into giving rather than taking.

Dwelling in the Word

1. Hannah’s song was sung as she left her son at Shiloh, and it is filled with God rather than with Samuel (2:1–2, 11). What has God given me that I find hard to release, and where is my joy actually resting?

2. “The LORD is the God of knowledge; and by Him actions are weighed” (2:3). What in my words or motives would I be uneasy to see placed on His scales?

3. Hannah describes strength broken, the hungry filled, and the poor raised from the dust (2:4–8). What situation in my life or someone else’s have I begun to treat as fixed, and what would it mean to place it again under God’s authority?

4. Hophni and Phinehas served at the altar but “did not know the LORD” (2:12). Has familiarity with spiritual things, church, ministry, or a Christian upbringing, taken the place of knowing Him?

5. The priests claimed their portion before God received His (2:15–16). Where am I taking first what belongs to God, or using what He has entrusted to me for my own advantage?

6. Eli heard everything and spoke rightly, yet God said he honored his sons more than Him (2:22–25, 29). Where has a relationship I value kept me from the obedience I know God requires?

7. Samuel ministered before the LORD in a household that was failing around him (2:18, 26). Have I allowed the failures or hypocrisy of others to weaken my own faithfulness to God?

8. God says, “those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed” (2:30). When my loyalties collide, what does my life show that I give the most weight to?
