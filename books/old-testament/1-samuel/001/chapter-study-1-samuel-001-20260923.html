1 Samuel 1 — Chapter Study

1 Samuel 1:1

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:1

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:1

Main Idea

This verse introduces Samuel. His father was Elkanah was a Levite living in the hill country of Ephraim.

Verse Explanation

“There was a certain man of Ramathaim Zophim” identifies Elkanah’s hometown. Ramathaim Zophim is generally associated with Ramah, the place later connected closely with Samuel (1 Samuel 1:19; 7:17). “Zophim” probably reflects the family or district associated with Zuph, the ancestor named later in the verse.

“of the mountains of Ephraim” means that Elkanah lived in the hill country belonging to Ephraim. This helps explain the description at the end of the verse.

“Elkanah the son of Jeroham, the son of Elihu, the son of Tohu, the son of Zuph” traces Elkanah through four generations. This grounds Samuel’s story in a particular family rather than simply introducing Samuel as an unexplained prophetic figure. Zuph also appears to have given his name to the surrounding territory, which is later called “the land of Zuph” (1 Samuel 9:5).

“An Ephraimite” describes Elkanah according to where his family lived. Other biblical genealogies trace his ancestry to Levi, which means he was geographically associated with Ephraim while belonging tribally to the Levitical line.

Study Notes

Ramathaim Zophim likely means “the two heights of the Zophites” or refers to the region of Zuph. It appears to be another designation for Ramah, Samuel’s later home and center of ministry.

Elkanah’s genealogy in 1 Chronicles 6:33–38 traces his family through Kohath to Levi. Some names appear in slightly different forms—Elihu may correspond to Eliel, and Tohu to Toah—but the family line is recognizably the same. This kind of variation was normal in the ancient world, where names were sometimes recorded with different spellings, shortened forms, or alternate family names across different books and generations of copying. It reflects the ordinary habits of ancient record-keeping, not a contradiction in the biblical text.

The detailed introduction prepares for Samuel’s significance. As a Levite, Samuel would be especially connected with Israel’s worship, while his prophetic ministry would guide the nation from the disorder of the judges toward the establishment of the monarchy.

Spirit & Life

  • God works within the real history of His people. Elkanah belongs to a family whose generations are named and to a place that can be identified. Our lives also stand within a history God sees completely. We may see only the present moment, but God sees where we have come from and what He intends to do through our lives.

  • Even Elkanah’s own name quietly points to the chapter’s theme: it means “God has purchased” or “God has possessed.” Before Samuel is ever born, this family already belongs to God. Our identity, like theirs, is meant to be defined by whose we are, not merely by what the world happens to notice about us.

1 Samuel 1:2

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:2

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:2

Main Idea

Elkanah’s divided household and Hannah’s childlessness establish the painful circumstances through which God will bring Samuel into Israel’s history.

Verse Explanation

“He had two wives” introduces a troubled family arrangement. Although polygamy appears in several Old Testament accounts, Scripture is clear that it is not God’s original design for marriage. Wherever Scripture records multiple wives, such households repeatedly produced rivalry, jealousy, trouble, and sorrow.

“The name of one was Hannah, and the name of the other Peninnah” names Elkanah’s two wives. Hannah’s name means “grace” or “favor,” while Peninnah’s name is commonly understood to mean “pearl” or “coral.”

“Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children” states the source of the household’s tension. In ancient Israel, children were regarded as a great blessing, while childlessness often brought deep personal grief and social shame. The contrast is deliberately sharp: Peninnah possessed what Hannah desperately desired. Hannah’s barrenness will become the setting for earnest prayer and God’s remarkable intervention.

Study Notes

No additional notes.

Spirit & Life

  • When we allow the values of the surrounding culture, or what simply seems right in our own eyes, to replace God’s revealed order in our homes, trouble is sure to follow. His Word, rather than the customs around us, must govern the life of the home.

  • There is a striking contrast in these two names. Hannah means “grace,” yet at this point she has nothing outwardly visible to suggest God’s favor. Peninnah means “pearl,” yet she is the one who possesses children and standing. We must not measure God’s favor by outward appearance or present circumstances.

  • Hannah’s barrenness places her among Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, and later Elizabeth — women whose empty arms were never incidental in Scripture. God repeatedly chooses barrenness as the backdrop for showing that the child of promise comes by His grace, not by human ability.

1 Samuel 1:3

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:3

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:3

Main Idea

Elkanah faithfully traveled to Shiloh each year to worship and sacrifice to the Lord, even though Israel’s priesthood was already marked by serious corruption.

Verse Explanation

“This man went up from his city yearly” describes Elkanah’s regular journey from his home region to Shiloh for worship. The chapter does not identify which annual festival occasioned this particular journey.

“To worship and sacrifice to the LORD of hosts” identifies the purpose of the journey. Elkanah went to honor God and present the sacrifices required for Israel’s worship. “LORD of hosts” presents God as the sovereign Commander of heaven’s armies and the ruler over every earthly power. This title is especially meaningful in a book filled with religious corruption, political instability, warfare, and human weakness.

“In Shiloh” points to Israel’s principal place of worship during this period. The tabernacle and the ark of the covenant were located there, making Shiloh the center of the nation’s sacrificial life before the temple was built in Jerusalem.

“Also the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, the priests of the LORD, were there” quietly introduces men who will soon become central to the story. They held sacred offices but did not honor the God they supposedly served. Their presence foreshadows the spiritual corruption that made Samuel’s birth and future ministry so important.

Study Notes

This is the Bible’s first occurrence of the title “LORD of hosts,” from the Hebrew Yahweh tseva’ot. “Hosts” can refer to armies, including the heavenly forces under God’s command. The title emphasizes that the Lord possesses unlimited authority and power.

“In Shiloh” points to Israel’s principal place of worship during this period. After Israel entered Canaan, the tabernacle was established at Shiloh (Joshua 18:1), and it remained an important center of Israel’s worship through the period of the judges. The ark of the covenant was there in Samuel’s early years (1 Samuel 3:3; 4:3–4). Elkanah therefore traveled to Shiloh because this was where the sanctuary of the LORD stood and where Israel came to offer sacrifices before Him.

Shiloh was located in the hill country of Ephraim, north of Jerusalem, approximately twenty miles from Jerusalem in a straight line and farther by the ancient routes through the hill country. Jerusalem had not yet become Israel’s central place of worship. That would occur later, after David established Jerusalem as his capital and brought the ark there, followed by Solomon’s construction of the temple.

The name Shiloh is commonly associated with the idea of “rest” or “tranquility,” although the precise meaning of the place-name is uncertain. This should be distinguished from the difficult use of “Shiloh” in Genesis 49:10, whose meaning and translation are debated.

Shiloh is especially important to the opening of 1 Samuel. The story begins at the place where the LORD had caused His name to dwell and where Israel came to worship, yet the priesthood serving there was already deeply corrupt. Samuel will be brought to this very sanctuary, and there the LORD will call him and begin speaking through him at a time when “the word of the LORD was rare” (1 Samuel 3:1).

Spirit & Life

  • Elkanah continued to go up to Shiloh to worship even though Hophni and Phinehas, the priests serving there, were deeply flawed. We should be careful not to use the weaknesses of spiritual leaders as an excuse for withdrawing from the life of the church. Our commitment to gather with God’s people is rooted in devotion to Christ, not in the worthiness of those who lead.

  • Elkanah’s yearly journey was quiet, unglamorous faithfulness kept in a nation that had largely abandoned it. Spiritual formation is often built on rhythms we keep year after year, even when the culture around us has stopped noticing or caring.

1 Samuel 1:4

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:4

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:4

Main Idea

During the annual family worship at Shiloh, Elkanah distributed portions of the sacrificial meal to his wife Peninnah and all her children so they could participate in the celebration.

Verse Explanation

“Whenever the time came for Elkanah to make an offering” refers to the family’s recurring worship at Shiloh. The offering appears to have included a peace offering, sometimes called a fellowship offering. With this kind of sacrifice, part was offered to the LORD, part belonged to the priest, and the remaining portion was eaten by the worshiper and his household as a sacred meal before the LORD (Leviticus 7:11–18; Deuteronomy 12:5–7). The sacrifice, therefore, was not simply presented at the sanctuary and left there. Worship included eating together from what had been offered to God. This explains why Elkanah distributed portions to Peninnah and her children and, in the following verse, gave a special portion to Hannah. The family meal was part of their worship and fellowship in the LORD’s presence.

“He would give portions to Peninnah his wife” means Elkanah distributed to Peninnah her share of the meat from the sacrificial meal. In a peace or fellowship offering, part of the sacrifice was eaten by the worshiper and his household before the LORD. The “portion” was therefore food for the sacred family meal associated with their sacrifice at Shiloh. The mention of Peninnah’s portion prepares for the contrast in the next verse, where special attention is given to the portion Elkanah gave Hannah.

“And to all her sons and daughters” shows that Peninnah had several children, though the text does not tell us how many. At the sacrificial meal, each son and daughter received a portion, making the contrast with Hannah especially visible. Surrounded by Peninnah’s children, Hannah was confronted again with the one thing she deeply desired but did not have. What should have been a joyful occasion of worship and fellowship before the LORD thus became, year after year, a painful reminder of her barrenness.

Study Notes

The offering was likely a peace or fellowship offering. In this kind of sacrifice, one portion was offered to God, another belonged to the priests, and the worshiper’s household ate the remaining portion as a communal meal. The meal expressed fellowship with God and shared celebration among His people.

The plural words “sons and daughters” indicate that Peninnah had at least four children.

Spirit & Life

Peninnah’s abundance and Hannah’s barrenness warn us against measuring God’s favor by outward circumstances. Material prosperity, visible fruitfulness, and natural abundance do not necessarily indicate that a person is walking closely with God, and their absence does not mean He has withheld His favor.

1 Samuel 1:5

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:5

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:5

Main Idea

Elkanah gave Hannah a special portion because he loved her, but his affection could not remove the sorrow of the childlessness God had permitted.

Verse Explanation

“But to Hannah he would give a double portion” distinguishes Hannah from Peninnah and her children at the sacrificial meal. The Hebrew expression translated “a double portion” is difficult and has been understood in several ways, including a double, special, or choice portion. Whatever the precise amount, the point is clear: Elkanah deliberately singled Hannah out for special treatment. At a meal where Peninnah sat surrounded by her sons and daughters, Elkanah made his affection for Hannah publicly visible by giving her an honored portion.

“For he loved Hannah” gives the reason for this special treatment. Elkanah’s love for Hannah was not dependent upon her ability to give him children. In a world where motherhood carried enormous social importance and barrenness could bring reproach and deep personal sorrow, Hannah remained deeply loved by her husband. He loved her not for what she could give him, but for who she was.

“Although the LORD had closed her womb” explicitly places Hannah’s barrenness under the sovereignty of God. Nothing here suggests that Hannah’s childlessness resulted from sin or was a punishment from God.

Because the LORD is identified as the One who had closed Hannah’s womb, Samuel’s eventual conception is more than a fortunate change in circumstances. When “the LORD remembered her” and Hannah conceived (v. 19), God Himself had intervened in the condition only He could change. Her closed womb makes His hand in Samuel’s birth unmistakable. Out of Hannah’s long sorrow and human inability, the Lord would bring forth the son she had prayed for and, through Samuel, begin something that would reach far beyond Hannah’s personal desire to His purposes for all Israel.

Study Notes

The Hebrew expression translated “a double portion” is difficult and has also been understood as a special or choice portion. Elsewhere in Scripture, a double portion can signify distinguished status or honor. The firstborn son received a double share of the inheritance (Deuteronomy 21:17), and Elisha’s request for a “double portion” of Elijah’s spirit likely draws upon the language of the principal heir (2 Kings 2:9).

Here, however, the setting is a sacrificial meal rather than an inheritance. Elkanah was distributing portions of the meal among his household. If “double portion” is the intended sense, Hannah received a conspicuously generous and honoring share. Peninnah received portions for herself and for her sons and daughters, while childless Hannah received a distinguished portion of her own because Elkanah “loved Hannah.” The expression therefore emphasizes not merely the quantity of food, but the special honor and affection Elkanah showed her..

The wording “the LORD had closed her womb” is part of the Old Testament understanding that the giving of life ultimately rests with God. Similar language appears in the accounts of Sarah, Rachel, and Abimelech’s household.

Spirit & Life

  • Hannah was loved, yet she was still barren. Elkanah’s special affection for her did not remove the pain of her longing. In the same way, God’s love should not be measured by the absence of difficulty. We can be deeply loved and still carry an unanswered longing or walk through circumstances we do not understand.

  • A closed womb is not the same thing as rejection. What felt like absence was not abandonment; what Hannah could not understand was still under God’s sovereign care. The same LORD who closes is the LORD who opens (v.19). Naming His sovereignty over our hardest “not yet” is often the first step away from despair and toward believing prayer.

1 Samuel 1:6

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:6

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:6

Main Idea

Peninnah deliberately used Hannah’s childlessness to humiliate her and rub salt into her wound.

Verse Explanation

“And her rival also provoked her severely” identifies Peninnah as Hannah’s rival within Elkanah’s divided household. Two wives sharing one husband created jealousy and competition within the home. Peninnah had the children Hannah desperately wanted, while Hannah had Elkanah’s special love. Although Scripture does not tell us exactly what drove Peninnah, jealousy likely stood behind her treatment of Hannah. She repeatedly used Hannah’s barrenness to hurt her. This was not an occasional cutting remark, but persistent and deliberate provocation.

“To make her miserable” shows that Peninnah wanted Hannah to suffer. The Hebrew word carries the sense of troubling or agitating someone, even causing them to become deeply distressed. Peninnah knew that Hannah’s inability to have children was already a source of deep grief and deliberately kept stirring that grief. Hurting Hannah may have given Peninnah a sense of satisfaction in their rivalry, particularly because having children could not give her the special place Hannah held in Elkanah’s heart.

“Because the LORD had closed her womb” makes Peninnah’s treatment of Hannah especially cruel. Hannah had no control over her barrenness and could do nothing to change it, yet Peninnah repeatedly tormented her because of it.

Study Notes

The Hebrew description of Peninnah’s provocation is unusually strong. The wording combines related terms from the same Hebrew root, giving the sense that her rival provoked her with severe or repeated provocation. The construction intensifies the action and supports the picture of deliberate, sustained harassment rather than a single insult.

The NKJV “to make her miserable” translates a verb that can mean to trouble, disturb, or agitate. The idea is that Peninnah sought to unsettle Hannah deeply by repeatedly pressing upon the grief of her barrenness.

The phrase “because the LORD had closed her womb” repeats the statement already made in verse 5. The repetition is significant. Hannah’s barrenness explains Peninnah’s point of attack, but it also keeps the LORD’s sovereignty over the situation in view

Spirit & Life

  • The place of our deepest pain can become a place of deeper encounter with God. Peninnah meant to wound Hannah, but she could not determine what Hannah would do with that pain. We cannot always choose what others do to us, but we can bring what they have done into the presence of God, where our wounds can become prayer.

  • Beware of comparison. Peninnah had children and much for which to be thankful, yet comparison with Hannah seems to have turned blessing into rivalry. Comparison moves our eyes from God’s goodness to ourselves and others; receive what God has given you with gratitude rather than letting someone else’s portion rob you of contentment.

  • Sometimes another person’s attack says more about what is happening within them than about us. Peninnah’s repeated cruelty, sustained year after year, may have arisen from a heart that did not know how deeply it was loved. This does not excuse her behavior, but it can help us see those who hurt us differently, and to ask the Father to help us see the need that may lie behind theirs.

1 Samuel 1:7

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:7

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:7

Main Idea

Peninnah’s harassment continued year after year, leaving Hannah so overwhelmed with grief that she wept and could not participate in the sacrificial meal.

Verse Explanation

“So it was, year by year” shows that Hannah’s pain continued for a long time. Year after year she remained without children, and year after year Peninnah continued to provoke her. Nothing seemed to change. Hannah carried the same unanswered longing from one year to the next while facing the same hurtful treatment at home.

“When she went up to the house of the LORD” makes Hannah’s situation even more painful. The family was going to Shiloh to worship, offer sacrifices, and rejoice before the LORD. Yet for Hannah, these visits also became times of deep sorrow. She came to worship God while carrying a grief He had not yet answered. Going into the presence of the Lord did not mean that she came without pain.

“That she provoked her” shows that Peninnah’s treatment was repeated and deliberate. She continued to stir up Hannah’s grief over being unable to have children. What should have been a time of worship and fellowship became painful because of the way Peninnah treated her. Hannah was not only grieving her childlessness; she was also being repeatedly hurt by someone within her own family because of it.

“Therefore she wept and did not eat” shows how deeply Hannah was hurting. Peninnah’s repeated attacks brought her to tears and took away her desire to eat. She was too distressed even to share in the sacrificial meal. Her sorrow had become more than she could quietly carry. Soon, however, Hannah would take that sorrow before the LORD and pour out her heart to Him.

Study Notes

“The house of the LORD” refers to the sanctuary at Shiloh, where the tabernacle was located. It does not refer to Solomon’s temple, which would not be built until much later.

Refusing food was not necessarily a formal fast. In this context, it most naturally reflects the physical effect of Hannah’s intense sorrow. The verbs suggest a repeated pattern: Peninnah provoked, Hannah wept, and Hannah did not eat.

Spirit & Life

  • Hannah came to the house of the LORD carrying deep pain; worship did not mean pretending that everything was well. There are times when we also come before God hurting or worn down by something that has continued for a long time, and we do not have to hide that from Him.

  • Notice when the provocation came: “year by year, when she went up to the house of the LORD.” Opposition often intensifies precisely when we draw near to worship. We should not be surprised, or deterred, when the hardest resistance meets us right at the moments meant to strengthen our faith.

  • People, conflict, and painful experience among God’s own people can make worship difficult. But we should not let what someone else has done to us keep us from the Lord. Hannah’s answer was not found in Peninnah changing; it was found as Hannah turned toward God and poured out her heart before Him.

1 Samuel 1:8

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:8

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:8

Main Idea

Elkanah lovingly tried to comfort Hannah, but his questions reveal that he did not fully understand the particular depth of her grief.

Verse Explanation

“Hannah, why do you weep?” Elkanah saw Hannah’s tears and was concerned for her. He loved her and wanted to comfort her, though he did not fully understand the depth of her sorrow.

“Why do you not eat?” Hannah’s grief was so great that she could not share in the sacrificial meal. Her sorrow affected her physically and took away her desire to eat.

“And why is your heart grieved?” Elkanah could see that Hannah was deeply troubled, but his question also suggests that he did not fully understand her sorrow. He loved Hannah, yet he could not fully enter into the pain of her longing for a child.

“Am I not better to you than ten sons?” Elkanah was assuring Hannah of his love, but his love could not replace the child she longed for. His words were sincere, yet they could not remove her pain. Some needs cannot be met even by those who love us most. Hannah’s unmet longing would soon lead her to pour out her heart before the LORD.

Study Notes

Elkanah asks four closely connected questions. The first three concern Hannah’s visible distress, while the fourth offers his love as the reason she should be comforted.

“Ten sons” was a way of describing a complete or exceptionally large family rather than specifying an exact number. Elkanah was emphasizing the strength of his love for Hannah, not claiming that the relationship between a husband and wife was identical to that between a mother and her children.

Spirit & Life

  • Even the love of those closest to us cannot meet every need of the heart. Elkanah truly loved Hannah, but his love could not give her the child she desired or take away the sorrow she carried. There are places within us that no husband, wife, or friend can fully satisfy — places meant to teach us to look more deeply to the Lord.

  • Those who love us will not always be able to understand what we are carrying, and their inability to understand does not mean they do not love us. When others cannot grasp what we are carrying, we can bring it to the Lord, who sees what others cannot see.

  • There is also a caution here for those who comfort others. Elkanah’s question was sincere but could not touch Hannah’s specific ache. Well-meant reassurance that minimizes another person’s grief, however lovingly intended, rarely heals it; sometimes the more faithful comfort is simply to sit with someone’s sorrow rather than talk them out of it.

1 Samuel 1:9

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:9

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:9

Main Idea

After the family meal, Hannah rose from her grief and goes to seek the LORD in prayer at the tabernacle, where Eli the priest is sitting nearby.

Verse Explanation

“So Hannah arose after they had finished eating and drinking in Shiloh” Hannah had been weeping and unable to eat, but now she got up to seek the LORD and take her pain, complaint and prayer to Him.

“Now Eli the priest was sitting on the seat” introduces Eli, the high priest at Shiloh and a descendant of Aaron through the priestly line of Ithamar. He was the leading priest at the sanctuary and would become an important figure in Samuel’s early life. Eli was sitting near the entrance to the sanctuary, where he could see Hannah as she came before the LORD.

“By the doorpost of the tabernacle of the LORD” places Eli near the entrance to the sanctuary. Hannah had come to the place where Israel worshiped the LORD, and Eli was positioned close enough to observe her as she prayed.

Study Notes

“After they had finished eating and drinking” refers to the family meal. Verse 7 says Hannah did not eat when Peninnah provoked her; the plural “they” need not include Hannah among those who ate. There is no conflict between the accounts.

The tabernacle itself was not a gathering place where ordinary Israelites entered to worship. Its inner areas were reserved for the priests, with the Most Holy Place entered only by the high priest on the Day of Atonement. Worshipers brought their sacrifices to the sanctuary and worshiped in the surrounding court or near its entrance. Hannah therefore would have been praying in the sanctuary area outside the tabernacle itself, close enough for Eli, who was sitting near the entrance, to see her praying.

Spirit & Life

  • There are times when nothing changes until we rise to pray. Hannah had wept, stopped eating, and received Elkanah’s attempts to comfort her, but nothing changed until she “arose” to seek the Lord.

  • Hannah shows us what to do with burdens we cannot resolve ourselves. We may not be able to change our situation or the people around us, but we can turn toward the Lord and pour out our hearts to Him. The first change was not in her circumstances but in where she took her pain.

  • Notice the picture the verse sets before us: Eli sitting in his seat of institutional authority, and Hannah standing in urgent, personal prayer. The renewal Israel needed would not come from Eli’s seat but from Hannah’s prayer — a reminder that God’s answer for a whole generation can begin in one obscure person’s desperate turning to Him.

1 Samuel 1:10

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:10

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:10

Main Idea

Overwhelmed by deep anguish, Hannah poured out her sorrow directly to the Lord through prayer and tears.

Verse Explanation

“And she was in bitterness of soul” describes Hannah’s deep inner pain. “Bitterness” does not mean that she was bitter against God. She was deeply distressed by her childlessness and by Peninnah’s repeated attacks.

“And prayed to the LORD” shows what Hannah did with her pain. She could not change her circumstances, and those closest to her could not fully understand or answer her sorrow. So Hannah brought it to the LORD. Her pain did not drive her away from God; it drove her to Him in prayer.

“And wept in anguish” shows that Hannah prayed through her tears. She did not hide her grief or try to appear stronger than she was. Her tears became part of her prayer as she poured out the pain of her heart before the LORD. Hannah came to God just as she was, with prayer on her lips and tears in her eyes.

Study Notes

No additional notes.

Spirit & Life

  • Hannah did not allow her grief to turn into resentment toward God, nor did she strike back at Peninnah. She took her pain to the LORD. This is the heart of biblical lament: bringing our sorrow honestly to God rather than turning away from Him because of it.

  • Her prayer was not polished or composed; Scripture describes it as bitterness of soul and anguish. God does not ask us to hide our pain before Him or to tidy it up before we bring it. Prayer gives our grief somewhere to go, so that instead of becoming bitterness or retaliation, it is poured out before the Lord who hears us.

1 Samuel 1:11

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:11

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:11

Main Idea

Hannah vowed that if the Lord gave her a son, she would consecrate and return him completely to God for lifelong service.

Verse Explanation

“Then she made a vow” Hannah joined a solemn promise to her prayer. If the LORD gave her the son she desired, she promised to give that son back to Him for lifelong service.

“O LORD of hosts” is the first recorded use of this title for God in Scripture. It presents the LORD as the ruler over the hosts of heaven and earth, with all power under His command. Hannah was bringing an impossible situation to the God for whom nothing was beyond His authority.

“If You will indeed look on the affliction of Your maidservant” Hannah asked God to see her suffering and act on her behalf. Three times in this prayer she calls herself the LORD’s “maidservant,” expressing both humility and her belonging to Him.

“And remember me, and not forget Your maidservant” does not mean that God had actually forgotten Hannah. In Scripture, when God “remembers” someone, He turns toward that person to act on their behalf. Hannah was asking the LORD not only to see her suffering but to answer her need.

“But will give Your maidservant a male child” Hannah told God plainly what she desired. She wanted a son, and she brought that specific longing directly to the LORD.

“Then I will give him to the LORD all the days of his life” Hannah was asking God for a son while promising to give that son back to Him. She was not offering Samuel for a short period of service but for his entire life. The son she most deeply desired would belong wholly to the LORD.

“And no razor shall come upon his head” was an outward sign that the son God gave her would be specially set apart to Him. Uncut hair was part of the Nazirite dedication described in Numbers 6:1–5. Hannah’s vow therefore points to a lifelong consecration resembling the Nazirite commitment, although only the uncut hair is specifically mentioned here.

Study Notes

A vow was a solemn and binding promise made before God. Once made, it was to be faithfully fulfilled. Hannah’s vow should not be understood as bargaining with God or offering Him something in order to persuade Him to give her a son. The movement of the chapter shows Hannah responding to God in prayer and committing herself to what He was revealing concerning Samuel.

Verse 23 helps interpret what happened in verse 11. Elkanah later tells Hannah, “Only let the LORD establish His word.” He does not say merely, “May you fulfill your vow.” He speaks of “His word,” something originating with the LORD that the LORD Himself must establish. Hannah had evidently shared with Elkanah what God had spoken to her concerning Samuel, and Elkanah believed that she had heard from God. His concern was now that the LORD would bring His own word to fulfillment.

This also helps explain Hannah’s change after prayer. In verse 18, before she had conceived and before anything outward had changed, Hannah went away, ate, and “her face was no longer sad.” Her confidence was not simply psychological relief from having expressed her emotions. She had met with God and heard from Him concerning the matter she had brought before Him. She could therefore rise from prayer with confidence before the answer appeared.

A vow was a voluntary but binding promise made to God. Once spoken, it was expected to be fulfilled faithfully. Hannah’s vow should not be understood as an attempt to purchase God’s favor; it expressed what she would do if He graciously answered her prayer.

The prohibition against cutting Samuel’s hair points toward a Nazirite-type dedication. Numbers 6 also mentions abstaining from grape products and avoiding ceremonial defilement from a dead body, but this verse mentions only the razor. Samuel is therefore closely associated with Nazirite devotion, though the Hebrew text does not explicitly call him a Nazirite.

Spirit & Life

  • Prayer is a place where God speaks into what we bring before Him. Hannah came carrying her own desperate desire for a son, but she did not leave prayer with only her request before God; she heard from Him concerning the purpose He had for the son He would give her, and her vow was her response of agreement, not a bargain.

  • Her surrender was total and permanent from the very beginning. She asked for the gift precisely so that she could give it away, refusing to hoard God’s blessing for herself. True consecration is willing to release what it has asked for.

  • Hannah brought her impossibility to the “LORD of hosts,” the God who commands the hosts of heaven and whose resources are without limit. We do not pray because we have the power to produce the answer. We pray because He does.

1 Samuel 1:12

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:12

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:12

Main Idea

Hannah continued praying at length while Eli watched her closely, setting the stage for his misunderstanding of her behavior.

Verse Explanation

“And it happened, as she continued praying before the LORD” Hannah did not offer a quick prayer and leave. She remained before the LORD, continuing to pour out the sorrow and desire she had carried for so long. Her prayer took time because she had much in her heart to bring before God.

“That Eli watched her mouth” Eli noticed Hannah as she prayed and watched her lips moving. He could see that she was speaking, but he could not hear her words. Seeing only what was happening outwardly, would soon lead him to a mistaken conclusion.

Study Notes

Prayer in ancient Israel was often spoken aloud, even when a person was praying privately. Hannah’s prayer was different: her lips moved, but her voice could not be heard (v. 13). This helps explain why Eli watched her mouth and then misunderstood what he was seeing. He could see the outward signs of speech without hearing any words and wrongly concluded that she was drunk.

Spirit & Life

  • Hannah’s prayer was “before the LORD.” She came to meet with God and pour out her heart to Him, not to be heard, noticed, or approved by others — even when Eli misunderstood her.

  • Concern about the opinions of others can hinder honest prayer. We can become too aware of how we sound or appear. But prayer is not a performance for people; it is fellowship with God. When we are truly “before the LORD,” our attention moves from those around us to the One we came to meet.

1 Samuel 1:13

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:13

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:13

Main Idea

Hannah’s prayer was silent but deeply sincere, and Eli misjudged her because he could see her lips moving but could not hear her words.

Verse Explanation

“Hannah spoke in her heart” means Hannah was praying silently within herself. Her prayer was directed to God alone. No one around her could hear what she was saying, but the LORD heard every word of her heart.

“Only her lips moved, but her voice was not heard” Hannah was silently forming the words of her prayer. Her lips were moving, but no sound could be heard. She was pouring out her heart to God in a way that was deeply personal and known only to Him.

“Therefore Eli thought she was drunk” Eli saw Hannah’s lips moving without hearing her voice and misunderstood what was happening. He saw her outward behavior but could not see the sorrow, prayer, and faith within her heart. His mistake reminds us that what we see outwardly does not always tell us what is happening within another person.

Study Notes

Hannah’s prayer is an important biblical example of silent prayer. Although her voice could not be heard, Scripture still describes what she was doing as prayer to the LORD (vv. 12–13). Prayer does not depend upon an audible voice; words spoken only within the heart can be directed to God.

Because prayer was commonly spoken aloud, Hannah’s moving lips without an audible voice would have been unusual enough to draw Eli’s attention. This helps explain his misunderstanding without excusing his mistaken judgment.

Spirit & Life

  • The thoughts of our hearts are as clearly heard in heaven as the words of our mouths. Hannah “spoke in her heart”; Eli heard nothing, but God heard everything. There are times when sorrow is too deep for us to say much aloud, but silence does not keep God from hearing us.

  • Spiritual leadership requires more than seeing what is happening outwardly; it requires discernment. Eli saw Hannah’s moving lips and wrongly concluded she was drunk — and intense, travailing prayer has often been misread by onlookers throughout the church’s history (compare Acts 2:13). We should be slow to judge what we do not understand.

  • Being around the things of God does not make us spiritual. Eli lived and served at the very center of Israel’s worship, yet he mistook a woman pouring out her heart to God for someone who was drunk. Familiarity with the things of God must never become a substitute for knowing God Himself.

1 Samuel 1:14

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:14

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:14

Main Idea

Eli wrongly accused Hannah of drunkenness, judging what he saw without understanding what was happening in her heart.

Verse Explanation

“How long will you be drunk?” Eli had already decided that Hannah was drunk. He did not ask what was wrong or why she was behaving this way; he spoke as though her guilt were certain. Hannah had come to the LORD carrying deep sorrow, only to be wrongly judged by the priest who saw her praying.

“Put your wine away from you!” Eli commanded Hannah to stop drinking and become sober. He believed he was correcting sinful behavior at the sanctuary, but he had completely misunderstood her. Hannah was not dishonoring God; she was pouring out her heart before Him. Eli saw the outward appearance of her prayer but failed to recognize what was actually happening within her.

Study Notes

No additional notes.

Spirit & Life

Eli was quick to correct Hannah for what he thought was wrong, while later chapters reveal that he failed to deal firmly with the serious sins of his own sons (1 Samuel 2:12–17, 22–25; 3:13). It can be easier to see what we think is wrong in someone else than to face what God wants to correct in us. Before we rush to correct someone else, we should let the Lord search our own hearts first.

1 Samuel 1:15

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:15

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:15

Main Idea

Hannah respectfully corrected Eli and explained that she was not drunk but deeply sorrowful, pouring out her heart before the LORD.

Verse Explanation

“No, my lord” Hannah corrected Eli firmly but respectfully. Although he had judged her wrongly, she did not answer with anger or disrespect. She spoke the truth while still showing honor to Eli as priest.

“I am a woman of sorrowful spirit” explains what Eli had misunderstood. Hannah’s unusual behavior came from deep sorrow, not drunkenness. Her barrenness and Peninnah’s repeated provocation had left her carrying great pain within her.

“I have drunk neither wine nor intoxicating drink” directly answers Eli’s accusation. Hannah had not been drinking. What Eli had mistaken for drunkenness was the outward expression of a woman deeply burdened and earnestly seeking God.

“But have poured out my soul before the LORD” describes the depth and honesty of Hannah’s prayer. She did not hide her sorrow or hold back what was in her heart. She brought her grief, longing, tears, and desire before the LORD and gave them expression in His presence.

Study Notes

The expression “poured out my soul” pictures emptying the contents of one’s heart before God. Similar language appears in Psalm 42:4 and Lamentations 2:19, where overwhelming emotion is openly brought into God’s presence.

Hannah’s respectful address does not mean that Eli’s accusation was justified. It shows her self-control and humility under pressure. She defended the truth without allowing his error to determine the spirit of her response.

Spirit & Life

  • Being wrongly judged does not give us permission to respond wrongly. Hannah did not accept Eli’s false accusation, but neither did she answer with anger or turn his own household’s failures against him. Her response joins honor with courage: we can speak the truth clearly without retaliating or dishonoring the one who wronged us.

  • Notice the completeness of what she brought to God: nothing hidden, nothing held back. She had “poured out” her soul before the LORD. This is what it looks like to actually pray, rather than merely to perform a request — laying the whole of our grief and longing bare before a God who is safe to receive it.

1 Samuel 1:16

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:16

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:16

Main Idea

Hannah asked Eli not to regard her as a wicked woman, explaining that her prolonged prayer came from the depth of her grief, not from drunkenness.

Verse Explanation

“Do not consider your maidservant a wicked woman” continues Hannah’s respectful defense. Eli had not only mistaken her for being drunk; his accusation also treated her as someone behaving wickedly in the presence of God. Hannah wanted him to understand that she had come seeking the LORD, not dishonoring Him.

“Out of the abundance of my complaint and grief” explains what had been pouring out of Hannah’s heart. “Complaint” does not mean that she was faithlessly accusing God. It refers to the deep trouble and distress she was bringing before Him. Years of barrenness and Peninnah’s repeated provocation had left Hannah with an abundance of sorrow to pour out before the LORD.

“I have spoken until now” refers to the prayer Eli had been watching. Hannah had continued speaking before the LORD because there was much within her heart to bring before Him. What Eli had mistaken for drunkenness was a grieving woman remaining before God and pouring out the burden she had carried for so long.

Study Notes

“Wicked woman” is literally “daughter of Belial,” a Hebrew expression describing a morally worthless, wicked, or rebellious person. At this point in Scripture, “Belial” is not being used as a personal name for Satan but as a term associated with wickedness and worthlessness.

The wording becomes especially significant in the next chapter. Eli had wrongly suspected Hannah of being a “daughter of Belial,” yet 1 Samuel 2:12 describes Eli’s own sons with the corresponding expression, “sons of Belial” (“corrupt” in the NKJV). Hannah was innocent of the wickedness Eli suspected, while serious wickedness was present within his own household.

Spirit & Life

An abundance of grief can become an abundance of prayer. Hannah did not allow her complaint and grief to turn her away from God; she brought it all before Him and continued praying until she had poured out what filled her heart. We do not have to wait until our hearts are settled before we pray — the greater the burden, the more reason to bring it before the Lord.

1 Samuel 1:17

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:17

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:17

Main Idea

After hearing Hannah’s explanation, Eli withdrew his accusation and sent her away in peace, expressing his desire that God would grant the request she had brought before Him.

Verse Explanation

“Go in peace” marks a complete change in Eli’s response. Once he understood that Hannah was praying out of deep sorrow rather than drunkenness, he no longer condemned her. He sent her away with words of peace rather than accusation.

“And the God of Israel” directs Hannah’s attention to the One who could answer her prayer. Eli could offer encouragement, but Hannah’s hope rested in the God of Israel, to whom she had poured out her soul.

“Grant your petition which you have asked of Him” expresses Eli’s desire that God would give Hannah what she had requested. Scripture does not indicate that Eli knew the details of her prayer. He did not possess the power to give Hannah what she desired; he simply directed her hope toward the God who did. The answer, if it came, would come from the LORD.

Study Notes

“Go in peace” was more than a casual farewell. The Hebrew word shalom carries the sense of peace, well-being, and being at rest. After first accusing Hannah, Eli now sent her away with words of peace.

“May the God of Israel grant your petition” is best understood as a prayerful blessing rather than necessarily a prophetic declaration that Hannah would conceive. Scripture does not say that Eli knew what Hannah had requested or that God had revealed the outcome to him. His words nevertheless encouraged Hannah to leave her request with God.

Spirit & Life

  • God can minister to us through imperfect people. Eli had misjudged Hannah and had serious failures of his own, yet God still used his words to encourage her: “Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition.” Our confidence is not in the vessel, but in the God who can speak and work through imperfect people.

  • Eli’s earlier mistake did not disqualify him from speaking a needed word once the truth became clear. Faithful ministry does not require perfect discernment at every moment — only a willingness to bless when we see we were wrong.

1 Samuel 1:18

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:18

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:18

Main Idea

Hannah received Eli’s blessing with gratitude and left with renewed peace, even though her circumstances had not yet changed.

Verse Explanation

“Let your maidservant find favor in your sight” expresses Hannah’s gratitude for Eli’s changed response and encouraging words. She hoped he would now regard her with kindness rather than the suspicion he had shown earlier.

“So the woman went her way and ate” reveals a noticeable change in Hannah. Earlier, her grief had been so great that she wept and could not eat. After pouring out her heart before the LORD and receiving Eli’s blessing, she returned to the meal.

“Her face was no longer sad” shows the outward evidence of an inward change. Hannah had not yet conceived, and nothing in her circumstances had visibly changed. Yet she was no longer carrying her sorrow in the same way. She had poured out her soul before the LORD and entrusted her request to Him.

Study Notes

To “find favor” in someone’s sight is a common biblical expression meaning to receive kindness, acceptance, or gracious treatment. Hannah was respectfully acknowledging Eli’s changed response toward her.

The contrast between Hannah refusing food in verse 7 and eating in verse 18 is significant. Her change came before Samuel was conceived. Her circumstances had not yet changed, but something had changed within Hannah after she poured out her soul before the LORD.

Spirit & Life

  • We can leave our burden at the altar. Hannah went away, ate, and “her face was no longer sad,” even though nothing in her circumstances had yet changed. She had met with God, poured out her soul before Him, and left what she could not carry in His hands (1 Peter 5:7).

  • This is the shape faith takes before the answer arrives: peace received by trust, not by sight. Hannah’s face changed while her womb was still closed. Faith has something to rest on before it has anything to see.

1 Samuel 1:19

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:19

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:19

Main Idea

After worshiping the Lord, Hannah and Elkanah returned home and resumed married life, and God began to act in response to Hannah’s prayer.

Verse Explanation

“They rose early in the morning and worshiped before the LORD” shows that Hannah and her family concluded their time at Shiloh by honoring God. Hannah worshiped before she had received the child she requested. Her prayer had not replaced worship, and her unresolved need did not prevent her from giving God reverence.

“Returned and came to their house at Ramah” brings the family back into ordinary life. They left the place of worship and returned home, carrying on without yet seeing any outward evidence that Hannah’s circumstances would change.

“Elkanah knew Hannah his wife” is a respectful biblical expression for marital relations. Hannah’s conception would involve the natural union of husband and wife, while the narrative makes clear that the Lord was the One who enabled her to conceive.

“The LORD remembered her” does not mean that God had forgotten Hannah and suddenly recalled her. In Scripture, God’s “remembering” often means that He begins to act on behalf of someone according to His purpose and faithfulness. The God who had heard Hannah’s silent prayer now moved toward answering it.

Study Notes

No additional notes.

Spirit & Life

  • Faith worships before circumstances change. Hannah rose and “worshiped before the LORD” while she was still barren. Worship is not a reward we give God once our prayer is answered; it is a posture we keep regardless of the outcome.

  • God often works through the ordinary means He has established. Hannah had asked God to do what she could not do, yet Scripture says “Elkanah knew Hannah his wife, and the LORD remembered her.” Trusting God does not mean becoming passive. We pray for what only He can do while faithfully doing what He has given us to do.

1 Samuel 1:20

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:20

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:20

Main Idea

Hannah gave birth to the son she had asked from the LORD and named him Samuel, making his name a lasting reminder of answered prayer.

Verse Explanation

“It came to pass in the process of time” indicates that Hannah’s answer did not appear immediately. After she and Elkanah returned home, Hannah conceived, completed the normal period of pregnancy, and eventually gave birth.

“Hannah conceived and bore a son” records the fulfillment of what began when “the LORD remembered her” (v. 19). The son for whom Hannah had wept and prayed was now placed in her care as a gift from the LORD.

“Called his name Samuel” gave the child a name connected with Hannah’s answered prayer. Hannah associates Samuel’s name with her asking the LORD for him: “Because I have asked for him from the LORD.” His name therefore carried for Hannah the significance “asked of God,” continually reminding her that Samuel was the son she had sought from the LORD.

Study Notes

Samuel’s name is traditionally understood as “asked of God,” which reflects Hannah’s own explanation in this verse. The precise Hebrew etymology of Shemu’el is debated because the name itself is not formed directly from the Hebrew verb sha’al, “to ask.” Other explanations have been proposed, including “name of God” and an association with God hearing. Whatever the precise etymology, Scripture itself deliberately connects Samuel’s name with Hannah’s asking him from the LORD.

Spirit & Life

Do not forget the prayers that stand behind the gifts in your life. Hannah named her son Samuel because “I have asked for him from the LORD.” Every time she said his name, it became a living memorial to God’s faithfulness, more lasting than any monument. We should remember the places where we once cried out to God and recognize His hand in what now seems ordinary to us.

1 Samuel 1:21

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:21

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:21

Main Idea

Elkanah continued leading his household in worship, returning to Shiloh to offer the yearly sacrifice and fulfill his vow to the LORD.

Verse Explanation

“The man Elkanah and all his house went up” describes the family’s next journey from Ramah to the tabernacle at Shiloh. As he had done in previous years, Elkanah brought his household to worship the LORD.

“To offer to the LORD the yearly sacrifice” shows that Samuel’s birth did not end the family’s regular devotion. Elkanah continued returning to Shiloh to worship and offer the family’s yearly sacrifice before the LORD.

“And his vow” refers to a vow Elkanah had made to God. Scripture does not tell us what he had promised, but he returned to Shiloh to fulfill it. This is distinguished from Hannah’s vow concerning Samuel, while the following verses explain why Hannah and Samuel remained at home.

Study Notes

“All his house” refers generally to Elkanah’s household and does not require every member to have made the journey. Verse 22 immediately clarifies that Hannah remained home with Samuel.

Scripture does not explain the nature of Elkanah’s vow. Vows were voluntary promises made to God, but once made they were binding and were to be faithfully fulfilled (Numbers 30:2; Deuteronomy 23:21–23).

Spirit & Life

  • The answer to prayer can test our hearts as surely as the waiting did. Need drove Hannah toward God; the question now was whether gratitude would become lasting devotion, and whether a commitment made in need would be remembered once the pressure had lifted.

  • A personal answer to prayer is meant to renew a whole household’s devotion, not remain privately held. Elkanah returned to Shiloh with his whole house, showing that God’s grace to one of us is meant to overflow into the worship of those around us.

1 Samuel 1:22

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:22

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:22

Main Idea

Hannah remained home to nurse Samuel until he was weaned, intending afterward to bring him permanently into the Lord’s service at Shiloh.

Verse Explanation

“But Hannah did not go up” identifies her as the exception to the household journey described in the previous verse. She did not abandon worship or disregard her vow; she remained home because the infant Samuel still depended upon her care.

“Not until the child is weaned” explains the temporary delay. Hannah intended to nurse and care for Samuel through his earliest stage of life before taking him to Shiloh. Once weaned, he would be able to live apart from her immediate physical care.

“Then I will take him” shows Hannah’s continuing determination to fulfill her promise. Samuel’s birth had not caused her to reconsider the vow she made while praying for him.

“That he may appear before the LORD and remain there forever” points to Samuel’s lifelong dedication. Hannah intended to bring him to the Lord’s sanctuary, where he would grow up in sacred service rather than return home after each annual visit.

Study Notes

In the ancient world, weaning commonly occurred later than it often does today, possibly when a child was two or three years old or even older. The text does not state Samuel’s exact age when Hannah eventually brought him to Shiloh.

“Forever” should be understood in light of Hannah’s vow in verse 11: Samuel would be given to the Lord “all the days of his life.” It refers to lifelong service, not to endless existence at the tabernacle. Samuel would later travel and minister in other locations, but his entire life remained devoted to God.

Spirit & Life

  • Giving something to God does not mean neglecting the responsibility He has placed in our hands for it. Hannah had promised Samuel to the LORD, but before she could bring him to Shiloh, she had to nurse and care for him. Surrender does not release us from responsibility; it changes how we understand it.

  • Faithful obedience does not always mean immediate action. Hannah’s timing was wise, not reluctant — she negotiated when the vow would be fulfilled without abandoning its substance. God’s call on our lives can include patient, Spirit-led planning rather than rushing to prove our sincerity.

1 Samuel 1:23

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:23

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:23

Main Idea

Elkanah supported Hannah’s decision to remain with Samuel until he was weaned, believing what she had heard from the LORD concerning Samuel and trusting God to establish His word.

Verse Explanation

“Do what seems best to you” expresses Elkanah’s agreement with Hannah’s decision. He did not pressure her to make the annual journey or to bring Samuel to Shiloh before he was ready to remain there. Elkanah accepted both Hannah’s plan for Samuel’s immediate care and the commitment she had made concerning his future.

“Wait until you have weaned him” confirms that Hannah would continue caring for Samuel during his earliest years. Her decision to remain home was not a retreat from what God had made known or from her commitment to give Samuel to Him. Caring for Samuel was the responsibility before her until the time came to present him to the LORD.

“Only let the LORD establish His word” gives important insight into what had happened when Hannah prayed in verse 11. Elkanah does not speak merely of Hannah fulfilling her vow; he speaks of the LORD establishing “His word.” Hannah had heard from God concerning the son He would give her and His purpose for that child, and she had responded by promising to give Samuel wholly to the LORD. Hannah had evidently shared with Elkanah what she had received from God, and Elkanah believed that she had heard from Him. He now looked to the LORD to establish what He had spoken concerning Samuel.

“Then the woman stayed and nursed her son” shows Hannah faithfully caring for the child whose future God had already spoken about. Knowing God’s purpose for Samuel did not cause her to rush ahead of God’s timing. She cared for him in the present while trusting the LORD with what He had revealed concerning his future.

“Until she had weaned him” marks the temporary nature of Hannah’s decision. She knew Samuel belonged to the LORD, but there was a season of preparation before the time of presentation. Hannah would faithfully care for him until the time came to bring him to Shiloh and give him to the LORD as she had promised.

Study Notes

Numbers 30:6–8 allowed a husband to annul his wife’s vow when he learned of it. If he allowed it to stand, however, the vow remained binding. Elkanah’s response therefore carries particular significance. He had heard Hannah’s account of what had happened in prayer and accepted her commitment concerning Samuel rather than annulling it.

“Only let the LORD establish His word” is an important interpretive key to the chapter. In verse 11 Hannah made her vow, but in verse 23 Elkanah speaks of the LORD establishing His word. This distinction points back to Hannah’s prayer and indicates that her dedication of Samuel was a response to something she had received from the LORD. She had not attempted to bargain with God by offering Him a son in exchange for opening her womb. As she prayed, God made known His purpose concerning the son He would give her, and Hannah responded by surrendering Samuel to that purpose.

Verse 23 also helps explain Hannah’s remarkable change in verse 18. She rose from prayer, ate, and “her face was no longer sad” before she had conceived Samuel. Her confidence did not rest upon changed circumstances. She had heard from God. What she received in prayer gave her something upon which faith could rest while she waited for what God had spoken to become visible.

Spirit & Life

  • When God speaks, we do not have to make His word happen; we must be faithful while He establishes it. Hannah cared for the responsibility God had placed immediately before her rather than rushing Samuel to Shiloh. We are responsible to believe what God has spoken and faithfully do what He places before us today, trusting Him with what only He can establish tomorrow.

  • There is power in a marriage that comes into agreement with what God is doing. Elkanah had the right to annul Hannah’s vow (Numbers 30:6–8); instead he used his leadership to support and encourage it, becoming her partner in it rather than an obstacle to it.

  • Those quiet nursing years were not wasted time before Samuel’s ministry began — they were its first, most intimate stage. Unseen, ordinary faithfulness in the present is itself sacred service, not a delay of what God has called us to.

1 Samuel 1:24

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:24

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:24

Main Idea

After Samuel was weaned, Hannah faithfully brought him to the Lord’s house at Shiloh, accompanied by generous offerings for worship.

Verse Explanation

“When she had weaned him” marks the moment Hannah had anticipated. Samuel no longer depended upon nursing, so she was ready to carry out the vow made before his birth.

“She took him up with her” records Hannah personally bringing Samuel to Shiloh. This journey was not merely another annual visit; she was preparing to leave her young son there in lifelong service to the Lord.

“With three bulls, one ephah of flour, and a skin of wine” lists the provisions brought for sacrifice and worship. Hannah did not arrive casually or empty-handed. The animals, flour, and wine provided the elements associated with burnt offerings, grain offerings, and drink offerings.

“Brought him to the house of the LORD in Shiloh” identifies the tabernacle as Samuel’s new home and place of service. It is called the Lord’s “house” even though the permanent temple in Jerusalem had not yet been built.

“And the child was young” emphasizes the tender age at which Samuel was presented. He had been weaned, but he was still a small child who would require care and instruction at the sanctuary.

Study Notes

The Hebrew text behind the NKJV reads “three bulls.” Some ancient textual witnesses read “a three-year-old bull,” which explains why translations differ. Verse 25 specifically mentions the sacrifice of “a bull,” but the remaining animals may have been intended for additional offerings connected with the family’s worship.

An ephah was a substantial dry measure, probably around twenty-two liters, or roughly five to six gallons. The flour and wine accompanied sacrificial offerings according to Israel’s worship practices.

The “house of the LORD” refers to the tabernacle complex at Shiloh. The language suggests an established sanctuary with structures surrounding the sacred tent, not Solomon’s later temple.

Spirit & Life

  • Wholehearted surrender is accompanied by wholehearted worship. Hannah did not come to Shiloh reluctantly or empty-handed; the size of her offering matched the seriousness of her vow. True consecration does not ask, “What is the least I must give?”

  • She did not wait until Samuel was old enough to understand or old enough to seem useful. She surrendered him at the earliest moment her vow allowed. Obedience does not have to wait for a more convenient time.

1 Samuel 1:25

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:25

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:25

Main Idea

After sacrificing a bull, the family brought Samuel to Eli to fulfill Hannah’s vow.

Verse Explanation

“They slaughtered a bull” Samuel’s dedication takes place in the setting of worship and sacrifice. Hannah does not simply leave her son at the tabernacle; she approaches God according to the worship He had established for Israel. The sacrifice acknowledges that Samuel belongs to the Lord who graciously gave him.

“And brought the child to Eli” Eli was the priest responsible for the tabernacle at Shiloh. Bringing Samuel to him formally places the child under his supervision for service in the Lord’s house. This is the decisive moment when Hannah begins fulfilling the vow she made in her anguish: the son she had asked from the Lord will now be given back to Him.

Study Notes

The bull was part of the sacrificial provisions brought to Shiloh in verse 24. Although verse 24 mentions multiple animals in the traditional Hebrew text, verse 25 focuses on the particular bull slaughtered in connection with Samuel’s presentation.

Samuel was likely a recently weaned child, perhaps around three years old, though Scripture does not specify his exact age. “Brought the child to Eli” does not mean Samuel was offered as a sacrifice. He was dedicated to lifelong service at the sanctuary under Eli’s priestly oversight.

Spirit & Life

  • Surrender must become action. It is one thing to tell God that something belongs to Him while the moment of giving still lies ahead; it is another to follow through once what we are surrendering has become precious to us. True surrender remains surrender when we actually let go.

  • The sacrifice came before the presentation of the child, pointing us toward a greater truth fulfilled in Christ. We do not give ourselves to God in order to make ourselves acceptable to Him. We belong to Him and offer ourselves to Him because Christ has first given Himself for us.

1 Samuel 1:26

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:26

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:26

Main Idea

Hannah respectfully reminds Eli that she is the woman he previously saw praying in deep anguish at the tabernacle.

Verse Explanation

“O my lord!” Hannah addresses Eli with the respect appropriate for his position as Israel’s priest. The title refers to Eli’s authority and is not an act of worship.

“As your soul lives, my lord” This was a customary way of emphasizing the truthfulness and seriousness of what followed. Hannah wants Eli to recognize that she is speaking about their earlier encounter.

“I am the woman who stood by you here” Hannah identifies herself as the distressed woman Eli had mistakenly thought was drunk. The word “here” draws attention to the same location where her sorrow had previously poured out before God.

“Praying to the LORD” Hannah defines that earlier moment by what was truly happening: she had been earnestly seeking the Lord. She now stands before Eli with visible evidence that God heard the prayer he witnessed.

“As your soul lives” is a respectful Hebrew expression affirming the certainty of one’s words. It does not suggest that Hannah was worshiping Eli.

Hannah’s words deliberately connect this visit with 1 Samuel 1:12–18. Eli may not immediately have remembered her, so she identifies herself by the intense prayer that had marked their first meeting.

Spirit & Life

  • God can turn places of painful prayer into places of testimony. Hannah stood again where she had once wept before the LORD, but now Samuel stood beside her as the living answer to that very prayer.

  • This finds its fullest expression in Jesus. The cross, the place of deepest sorrow, became through His resurrection the great testimony of God’s saving victory. In Christ, sorrow does not have the final word.

1 Samuel 1:27

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:27

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:27

Main Idea

Hannah identifies Samuel as the specific answer to the prayer she had poured out before the Lord.

Verse Explanation

“For this child” Hannah points directly to Samuel as the reason for her return. The child standing before Eli is the visible answer to the sorrow and longing Eli had witnessed.

“I prayed” Samuel’s birth was not merely the result of changing circumstances. Hannah had deliberately brought her need before the Lord and entrusted it to Him.

“The LORD has granted me my petition” Hannah gives the Lord full credit for Samuel. Eli had blessed her departure, but it was God who heard her and graciously answered.

“Which I asked of Him” Hannah had requested this particular gift from the Lord. These words prepare for the wordplay in the following verse: because Samuel was given in response to her asking, she will now give him back to the Lord’s service.

  • Specific prayer can become unmistakable testimony. Hannah could say, “For this child I prayed,” and now stood before Eli with the very answer beside her. There is value in bringing our desires before God specifically, so that we can recognize His answer when it comes.

  • She deliberately returned to name what God had done rather than let it pass uncredited. There is life in telling plainly, to someone who once saw our sorrow, exactly what the Lord has done.

1 Samuel 1:28

NKJV: 1 Samuel 1:28

NLT: 1 Samuel 1:28

Main Idea

Hannah fulfills her vow by dedicating Samuel to lifelong service before the Lord, and the moment concludes in worship.

Verse Explanation

“Therefore I also have lent him to the LORD” Hannah had asked the LORD for Samuel, and now she gives the son she received back to Him. “Lent” does not mean temporarily; Hannah is fulfilling her vow by giving Samuel wholly to the LORD for life.

“As long as he lives” Hannah fulfills the full extent of her vow from verse 11. Samuel’s dedication is not limited to childhood; his entire life is set apart for the Lord’s service.

“He shall be lent to the LORD” emphasizes again that the child Hannah received from God is now placed completely at His disposal. Hannah releases her daily care of Samuel so that he can remain at Shiloh and serve the LORD under Eli’s supervision. She received Samuel as God’s gift, but she never ceased to recognize that he ultimately belonged to God.

“So they worshiped the LORD there” Like Abraham, Elkanah and Hannah worship at the place of sacrifice. The son for whom she had wept, prayed, waited, and received was now given back to the LORD, and the response was worship before the God who had heard her.

Study Notes

The NKJV’s “lent” can sound temporary in English, but Hannah immediately explains the meaning: “as long as he lives.” Samuel is not temporarily loaned to God. He is yielded to the LORD for lifelong service.

Spirit & Life

  • Faith does not clutch the answer once it comes. Hannah had desperately sought Samuel, but when God gave him to her, she gave him back. Her surrender showed that she loved the Giver more than the gift she had so deeply desired.

  • What is placed wholly in God’s hands is never wasted. Samuel was never Hannah’s to keep in the first place; surrendered fully to God, his life became far more than she could ever have made it by holding on.

  • The chapter that opened with a divided, grieving household closes in unified worship — and this is not the end of Hannah’s story. Surrendered, released prayer matures into praise that outlives the need that first drove her to her knees, as her own song in the following chapter will show.
