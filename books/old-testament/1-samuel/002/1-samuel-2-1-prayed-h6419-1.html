PRAYED - (pâlal, Strong's H6419)

Summary:

The word palal means to pray, appeal to a higher authority, make supplication, or intercede on behalf of another. In certain contexts, it can also carry the sense of arbitrating or making a judgment. In 1 Samuel 2:1, palal introduces Hannah's song of thanksgiving and praise after God granted her a son, showing that biblical prayer includes joyous adoration and thanksgiving alongside earnest petition.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Hannah had lived for years under the pain of barrenness and the taunts of her rival, Peninnah. In 1 Samuel 1, she traveled to the tabernacle at Shiloh and poured out her heart in silent, weeping petition before the Lord. God answered her cry, and she gave birth to Samuel. After weaning the child, Hannah returned to Shiloh to present Samuel to the Lord for a lifetime of service.

When Hannah steps forward in 1 Samuel 2:1, her spirit is completely transformed. The sorrow that once weighed down her speech gives way to a poem of victory, praise, and prophetic insight. The opening verse signals this transition, marking her words not as a bitter plea, but as an exalted act of worship offered to the God who saves.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb palal primarily refers to addressing God in prayer, seeking His favor, or interceding for others. When someone uses this word, it describes coming before a higher authority—most often the Lord Himself—to present a matter, seek help, or offer praise.

In a smaller number of contexts, palal carries the sense of mediating, arbitrating, or coming to a judgment. This judgment sense helps explain the depth of the word when applied to prayer: to pray (palal) is to bring one's situation before the ultimate Judge of the universe, asking Him to act according to His justice and mercy.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses palal across a wide variety of circumstances:

  • Intercession: God commands prophets or leaders to pray for others, as when Abraham prays for Abimelech or when Moses intercedes for Israel in the wilderness.

  • National Crisis: Kings like Hezekiah pray to God when facing terrifying military threats, placing the destiny of the nation in God's hands.

  • Corporate Worship: Solomon uses the word repeatedly during the dedication of the temple, establishing the temple as a house where God's people bring their prayers in times of drought, defeat, or exile.

  • Judgment and Arbitration: In rare cases, the word describes evaluating a situation or stepping in to settle a grievance, as seen in the story of Phinehas stopping a plague or Eli discussing human disputes.

These varied uses demonstrate that palal is not limited to asking for personal needs. It covers every form of communication in which a worshiper acknowledges God's supreme authority over human affairs.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of palal reveals that God is personally accessible to His people. Prayer in Israel was not an attempt to bribe a distant deity, but a confident appeal to the living God who hears and responds.

Furthermore, Hannah's prayer shows that God cares for the lowly and oppressed. Her experience demonstrates that God reverses human expectations—bringing down the proud and lifting up the helpless. When believers pray, they align themselves with God's sovereign rule, trusting Him to enact His righteous justice in His timing.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:1, palal introduces a passage that reads more like a psalm or a hymn than a standard request. In 1 Samuel 1:10, Hannah prayed (palal) out of deep anguish and tears, desperate for a child. Here in chapter 2, she prays (palal) again, but the tone is radically different. Her heart exalts in the Lord, her horn is lifted high, and she speaks boldly against her enemies.

This contrast shows that answered prayer leads naturally to worship. Hannah does not merely thank God for giving her a boy; she sees her personal blessing as a demonstration of God's universal power. Her prayer sets the spiritual baseline for the rest of the books of Samuel, announcing that God will exalt those who humbly trust Him while humbling those who rely on worldly strength.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 1:10 — "And she was in bitterness of soul, and prayed to the LORD and wept anguish."

  • 2 Kings 19:15 — "Then Hezekiah prayed before the LORD, and said: 'O LORD God of Israel, the One who dwells between the cherubim, You are God, You alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth.'"

  • Psalms 106:30 — "Then Phinehas stood up and intervened, and the plague was stopped."

  • Job 42:10 — "And the LORD restored Job’s losses when he prayed for his friends."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's prayer in 1 Samuel 2:1 provides the literary and theological framework for Mary’s song of praise, the Magnificat, in Luke 1. Both women were brought low by circumstances, both experienced God’s gracious intervention, and both burst into prophetic song celebrating God's power to turn human status upside down. Hannah's act of prayer marks a vital turning point in Israel's history, transitioning the narrative from the dark, chaotic era of the Judges toward the prophetic ministry of Samuel and the establishment of God's kingdom line.
