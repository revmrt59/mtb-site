ANOINTED - (mâshîyach, Strong's H4899)

Summary:

The Hebrew word mashiach refers to a person set apart for a special task or sacred office through the ceremonial pouring of oil. In ancient Israel, this act designated individuals chosen by God for leadership, primarily priests and kings. In 1 Samuel 2:10, the word refers to the king whom God will empower and exalt. Spoken by Hannah at a time when Israel had no earthly king, the word introduces the royal title of God's chosen ruler, pointing forward to King David and ultimately to Jesus the Messiah.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In 1 Samuel 2, Hannah travels to the tabernacle at Shiloh to present her young son Samuel to the LORD. Filled with gratitude because God answered her prayer for a child, she sings a song of praise. At this point in Israel's history, the nation was governed by judges and suffering under corrupt priestly leadership. Israel had not yet instituted a monarchy.

Despite these chaotic conditions, Hannah closes her song with a dynamic prophetic declaration about a king and an anointed ruler. Anointing with oil was already a recognized ceremony used to consecrate priests for holy service. By connecting this ritual with royal leadership, Hannah's prayer anticipates the coming of Israel's God-given monarchy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun mashiach comes from a Hebrew verb meaning to smear or anoint with liquid, typically olive oil. It describes a person who has received this official consecration. The physical act of anointing symbolized that the individual was set apart from common life and endowed with authority from God.

In the Old Testament, the word applies to high priests and Israelite kings who were solemnly dedicated to their office. In 1 Samuel 2:10, mashiach appears in direct parallel with "His king." This pairing shows that the ruler being described is not a self-appointed monarch or a tyrant ruling by personal ambition, but a ruler chosen, set apart, and authorized by God.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, the word mashiach reflects key offices in Israel's civic and spiritual life:

  • Priestly Office: In the books of the Law, the term describes the consecrated high priest who was set apart to minister in the sanctuary and present offerings on behalf of the nation.

  • Royal Office: In the historical books, especially 1 and 2 Samuel, the word becomes the primary title for Israel's king. Saul and David were both anointed by God's prophets, marking them as rulers designated by the LORD.

  • Prophetic Hope: As Israel's human kings repeatedly failed to rule with righteousness, the word grew to describe a future, ideal King. God promised to send a ultimate descendant of David who would execute perfect justice and reign over an everlasting kingdom.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's use of mashiach in 1 Samuel 2:10 sets forth fundamental truths about divine sovereignty and human authority:

  • God Authorizes Leadership: Human rulers in Israel did not hold absolute authority in their own right. True governance was delegated by God, as indicated by the ritual of anointing.

  • Moral Accountability: Because the king was God's anointed, he was bound to rule according to God's law and standards of righteousness rather than personal whim.

  • Respect for Divine Appointment: The title carried deep sanctity. David consistently refused to harm King Saul, calling him "the LORD's anointed," demonstrating that God's appointment must be honored even when the officer acts unworthily.

  • Divine Triumph: Hannah's song reveals that God's plan to overcome evil and judge the earth is linked to the exaltation of His appointed leader.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Hannah concludes her song in 1 Samuel 2:10 by expanding her focus from her personal experience to God's cosmic victory over all opposing powers. She declares that those who contend with the LORD will be broken, that God will thunder against them from heaven, and that He will execute judgment across the ends of the earth.

Immediately alongside these acts of divine judgment, Hannah proclaims that God will give strength to His king and exalt the horn of His anointed. In Hebrew poetry, the "horn" represents physical power, dignity, and victorious strength. Hannah connects the triumph of God's justice with the strengthening of His chosen ruler. The passage demonstrates that salvation and order do not come through human military might, but through the power God grants to His anointed representative.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 4:3 — "if the anointed priest sins, bringing guilt on the people, then let him offer to the LORD for his sin which he has committed a young bullock without blemish as a sin offering." Here the word refers to the high priest set apart for holy service in the tabernacle.

  • 1 Samuel 2:35 — "Then I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest who shall do according to what is in My heart and in My mind. I will build him a sure house, and he shall walk before My anointed forever." God promises to establish a faithful priest who will serve in the presence of His chosen king.

  • 1 Samuel 12:3 — "Here I am. Witness against me before the LORD and before His anointed: Whose ox have I taken, or whose donkey have I taken...?" Samuel calls upon both God and the king as witnesses to his righteous leadership.

  • 1 Samuel 24:6 — "And he said to his men, 'The LORD forbid that I should do this thing to my master, the LORD's anointed, to stretch out my hand against him, seeing he is the anointed of the LORD.'" David recognizes Saul's sacred office and refuses to take the king's life.

  • 2 Samuel 22:51 — "He is the tower of salvation to His king, and shows mercy to His anointed, to David and his descendants forevermore." David uses the word at the end of his life to praise God for His faithful promises to the royal line.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Literary Bookends: Hannah's prayer in 1 Samuel 2:10 and David's song in 2 Samuel 22:51 frame the entire story of Samuel and Kings. Both songs conclude with praise for God's victory through "His anointed."

  • Translation to the New Testament: When the Hebrew Scriptures were translated into Greek, mashiach was translated as Christos. This Greek term is the source of the English title "Christ," directly connecting Hannah's ancient prayer to Jesus.

  • Echoed by Mary: Hannah's song provided the pattern for Mary's song of praise in Luke 1. Mary echoes Hannah's themes of God humbling the proud, lifting up the poor, and bringing salvation through His ultimate Anointed One.
