EXALT - (rûwm, Strong's H7311)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb rum fundamentally means to be high, elevated, or lifted up, whether physically or metaphorically. In 1 Samuel 2:10, the word describes God actively elevating the strength, dignity, and victorious authority ("the horn") of His anointed king.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

1 Samuel 2 opens with Hannah's hymn of praise after presenting her young son Samuel to the LORD at Shiloh. Hannah had suffered years of barrenness and social reproach, accompanied by constant mocking from her rival, Peninnah. When God answered her desperate prayer, Hannah responded not merely with relief, but with a prophetic song about God's character and righteous rule.

Hannah's song sets the thematic foundation for the entire book of 1 Samuel. She celebrates how God regularly reverses human circumstances—bringing down the proud and powerful while lifting up the weak and needy. Near the conclusion of her song in verse 10, she looks beyond her personal situation to declare how God will judge the earth, grant victory to His king, and elevate the authority of His anointed leader.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb rum refers to physical height, elevation, or raising something upward. It describes objects or actions that are tall, raised, or set on high.

Beyond physical height, the word frequently carries figurative meanings depending on the context:

  • To raise or lift up an object physically (such as hands, banners, or offerings).

  • To exalt or praise God, lifting up His name above all others.

  • To elevate a person in status, position, power, or honor.

  • To act arrogantly or proudly (literally having a "lifted up" heart or eyes).

In 1 Samuel 2:10, the word appears in an active sense where God is the subject doing the lifting. God Himself takes the action of elevating the "horn" (representing strength and dignity) of His anointed one.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, rum serves both concrete and highly symbolic functions. In narrative texts, it describes literal physical rising, such as floodwaters rising above the earth or sacrificial portions being lifted up as heave offerings.

In the Psalms and prophetic books, the word shifts heavily toward worship and royal theology. When directed toward God, worshippers use rum to praise His supreme majesty—declaring that God is far higher than earthly idols or kings, or resolving to "extol" Him for His salvation.

When used of human beings, the nuance depends entirely on who is doing the lifting. When people try to lift themselves up in pride, Scripture condemns their haughtiness as dangerous arrogance. But when God lifts a person up, rum represents divine favor, vindication, and the bestowing of genuine authority.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's use of rum highlights a core biblical truth: true exaltation belongs to God alone, and only He has the right to grant honor to human beings. Human self-exaltation leads to ruin, whereas divine elevation brings humble security.

Furthermore, 1 Samuel 2:10 introduces a key messianic theme early in the Old Testament story. At the time Hannah uttered this prayer, Israel had no earthly king. Her statement that God would give strength to His king and lift up the horn of His anointed prophetically points forward. It anticipates the rise of David, but ultimately looks ahead to Jesus Christ, the true Anointed King whose ultimate exaltation comes through God's power.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:10, Hannah combines two vivid expressions to describe divine deliverance: God gives "strength to His king" and will "exalt the horn of His anointed." The "horn" (qeren) in ancient Israelite imagery derived from strong animals like oxen, symbolizing power, defense, and triumph. A lowered horn signaled defeat or shame, while a raised horn signified victory, honor, and strength.

The verb form used here is masculine singular imperfect. In this poetic context, it expresses a confident declaration about what God will surely do as His purpose unfolds. Hannah proclaims that God will defeat those who oppose Him and raise His appointed ruler to a place of dignity and victory. This assurance contrasts sharply with the corrupt leadership of Eli's sons described in the surrounding chapter, proving that lasting power comes only from God's hands.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 15:2 – "The LORD is my strength and song, and He has become my salvation; He is my God, and I will prepare Him a habitation; My father’s God, and I will exalt Him." Moses and Israel use the word to lift up God in praise following their rescue at the Red Sea.

  • Psalms 30:1 – "I will extol You, O LORD, for You have lifted me up, and have not let my foes rejoice over me." David uses the word to offer elevated praise to God for rescuing him from trouble.

  • Psalms 89:17 – "For You are the glory of their strength, and in Your favor our horn is exalted." The psalm reflects Hannah's imagery, showing that the strength and honor of God's people come entirely through His unmerited favor.

  • Psalms 107:32 – "Let them exalt Him also in the assembly of the people, and praise Him in the company of the elders." Public worship calls God's redeemed people to lift up His name among the congregation.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's song in 1 Samuel 2 forms the direct literary model for Mary's song (the Magnificat) in Luke 1:46–55. Just as Hannah praised God for lifting up the needy and exalting the horn of His anointed, Mary praised God for scattering the proud and exalting the lowly through the coming birth of Jesus. The theme of divine elevation runs continuously from Hannah's prayer to the gospel story.
