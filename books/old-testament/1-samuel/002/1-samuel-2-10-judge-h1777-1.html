JUDGE - (dîyn, Strong's H1777)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb din refers to judging, contending, ruling, or advocating a cause. It carries the sense of rendering an authoritative decision, defending the oppressed, or executing justice over opposing forces. In 1 Samuel 2:10, Hannah declares that the Lord will evaluate and bring righteous order over the entire world, establishing His ultimate reign and upholding His anointed king.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Hannah’s prayer in 1 Samuel 2 marks a major turning point in Israel's history. Up to this point, Israel had experienced a turbulent period governed by local judges and threatened by neighboring enemies. In her song of praise, Hannah moves beyond her personal gratitude for the birth of Samuel to proclaim a broad prophetic vision for Israel and the world.

When Hannah speaks of God judging, she uses a word that describes a sovereign ruler stepping in to set things right. This statement sets the stage for the book of 1 Samuel, which charts Israel's transition from regional tribal leaders to a unified kingdom under God's chosen king.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb din means to judge, govern, contend, or plead a cause. In ancient Hebrew thought, a judge was not simply a neutral observer sitting in a legal hall. A judge actively intervened to uphold what was right, protect the defenseless, settle grievances, and bring punishment upon wrongdoers.

When applied to human rulers, the word often describes defending the rights of the poor and vulnerable against oppression. When applied to God, din describes His divine prerogative to evaluate human actions, enforce His holy standards, bring down the proud, and deliver those who trust in Him.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, din appears in several distinct settings:

  • Legal Advocacy: The word is frequently used for defending the helpless in court or advocating for those who cannot defend themselves.

  • Governing Authority: It describes the active exercise of leadership and administrative rule, where a leader maintains order and justice.

  • Divine Retribution and Assessment: It expresses God’s global authority as He evaluates nations, confronts evil, and executes sentence against those who oppose His righteousness.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s declaration reveals fundamental truths about God's reign. First, divine judgment is universal. God’s authority is not limited to Israel; His judgment reaches the ends of the earth. No nation, ruler, or individual is exempt from His evaluation.

Second, God’s judgment works hand in hand with His plan for salvation and governance. In 1 Samuel 2:10, God's act of judging the earth directly connects to giving strength to His King and exalting His Anointed. God's judgment clears away wicked opposition so that His righteous kingdom can be established in peace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:10, the action of judging appears alongside dramatic imagery: adversaries being broken in pieces and the Lord thundering from heaven against His foes. The verb form presents this divine judgment as an event that God will surely carry out.

While human powers rely on weapons and status, Hannah emphasizes that true power belongs to the Lord. God will judge the ends of the earth by assessing every human power structure and executing His righteous decree. This universal judgment provides the ultimate defense for the humble and establishes the foundation for the kingdom He appoints.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 15:14 — "And also that nation whom they serve I will judge; afterward they shall come out with great possessions."

  • Psalms 7:8 — "The LORD shall judge the peoples; judge me, O LORD, according to my righteousness, and according to my integrity within me."

  • Psalms 54:1 — "Save me, O God, by Your name, and vindicate me by Your strength."

  • Jeremiah 22:16 — "He judged the cause of the poor and needy; then it was well. Was not this knowing Me?” says the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah’s song provides the thematic framework for the rest of 1 Samuel and serves as a primary backdrop for Mary’s song of praise in Luke 1. By declaring that God will judge the world and exalt His anointed, Hannah introduces the grand biblical theme of God humbling the proud and lifting up the lowly. The word din assures the reader that God's ultimate rule over the world is grounded in perfect justice, assuring His people that evil will not have the final word.
