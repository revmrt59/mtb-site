CORRUPT - (bᵉlîyaʻal, Strong's H1100)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun beliya'al describes moral corruptness, worthlessness, or lawlessness. In 1 Samuel 2:12, the author uses this word to characterize Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, who served as priests at Shiloh. The expression highlights that despite holding a sacred office, their lives and ministry were completely destitute of moral value and reverence for God. Their corrupt behavior subverted divine worship and brought dishonor upon the sanctuary.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

During the period of the judges, Shiloh served as the religious center of Israel where the tabernacle and the ark of the covenant were located. Eli served as high priest, and his two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, carried out priestly duties. The second chapter of 1 Samuel introduces a sharp narrative contrast between young Samuel, who was dedicated to God's service, and Eli's sons, whose greed and immorality corrupted the sanctuary. In 1 Samuel 2:12, the author opens the description of these priests by assigning them a label that defines their true character in God's eyes.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word beliya'al is formed from two Hebrew components: beliy (meaning "without") and ya'al (meaning "profit," "value," or "usefulness"). At its basic level, the noun means worthlessness, uselessness, or lack of value. In moral and spiritual contexts, it refers to active evil, lawlessness, and moral ruin.

In Hebrew idiom, the word often appears in expressions such as "son of beliya'al" or "man of beliya'al." In Hebrew thought, calling someone a "son" or "child" of a quality means that individual is fundamentally characterized or driven by that trait. Therefore, a "son of beliya'al" is a person whose conduct is thoroughly corrupt and base. In 1 Samuel 2:12, the word identifies Eli's sons as morally vile men who acted without regard for God's holy law.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, Scripture uses beliya'al to describe individuals, attitudes, and destructive forces that exhibit deep moral decay:

  • Base or Lawless Scoundrels: The word describes lawless individuals who commit violent crimes, give false testimony, or despise God-appointed leaders.

  • Evil Schemes and Thoughts: It applies to wicked internal motives or plots that seek to harm others or subvert justice.

  • Ruin and Destruction: In poetic passages, the word describes overwhelming floods of ungodliness or lethal threats that surround a sufferer.

While later Jewish tradition and the New Testament developed Belial into a proper name for Satan, in Old Testament usage it functions as a common noun denoting moral worthlessness and lawless corruption.

4. Theological Implications

  • Office Without Character: Holding a sacred religious title does not guarantee personal godliness. Hophni and Phinehas inherited the priesthood, yet God judged them as corrupt and worthless.

  • Knowledge of God as the Foundation of Ethics: 1 Samuel 2:12 connects moral corruption directly with a lack of spiritual knowledge: "they did not know the LORD." Where true reverence for God is absent, moral boundaries collapse.

  • Leadership Contamination: When those who lead God's people act with corrupt self-interest, they cause others to stumble and dishonor the sanctuary of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:12, the author places the statement "the sons of Eli were corrupt" immediately after Hannah's song of praise and the mention of young Samuel ministering before the LORD. This placement highlights the contrast between faithful worship and corrupt leadership.

The surrounding narrative explains what made these priests "corrupt." Instead of following the Mosaic law for peace offerings, they used three-pronged forks to claim whatever meat they wanted from the boiling pots. Worse yet, they demanded raw meat with fat still on it before the burnt offering was made to the LORD, enforcing their demands with threats of violence. The word beliya'al exposes the reality behind their actions: they viewed God's worship as a means for personal gain and treated holy things with contempt.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 15:9 — "Beware lest there be a wicked thought in your heart..."

  • 1 Samuel 1:16 — "Do not consider your maidservant a wicked woman..."

  • 1 Samuel 25:25 — "Let not my lord... regard this scoundrel, Nabal..."

  • 1 Kings 21:10 — "...and seat two men, scoundrels, before him..."

  • Psalms 18:4 — "The pangs of death surrounded me, and the floods of ungodliness made me afraid."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A notable narrative irony occurs earlier in 1 Samuel. When Hannah prayed silently in deep anguish, Eli mistakenly assumed she was drunk and rebuked her. Hannah responded, "Do not consider your maidservant a wicked woman" (literally, a daughter of beliya'al, 1 Sam 1:16). Eli proved slow to recognize Hannah's true piety while failing to confront the actual corruption (beliya'al) of his own sons. This contrast underscores Eli's spiritual failure as high priest and father.
