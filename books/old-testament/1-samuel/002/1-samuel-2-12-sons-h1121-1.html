SONS - (bên, Strong's H1121)

ben ( ben , Strong’s [H1121] )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ben most basically means son, child, or descendant, derived from a root associated with building or continuing a family line. In 1 Samuel 2:12, it introduces Eli’s rebellious offspring, specifically translated as "sons," marking them as descendants who should have carried forward a faithful legacy but instead chose a destructive path.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word ben serves as one of the most common foundational building blocks in biblical Hebrew for describing family relationships and lineage. The term traces back to a root meaning to build, reflecting the ancient understanding that children represent the building and continuation of a household. Beyond immediate parent-child relationships, the language frequently uses the term in a broader sense to indicate membership in a group, tribe, or guild, or to describe someone possessing a specific characteristic.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to a biological son, a male descendant across generations, or children generally within a family. Beyond physical parentage, the term frequently describes character or belonging, such as "sons of the prophets" indicating members of a prophetic guild, or descriptive phrases like "sons of valor" indicating brave men. In this passage, the term identifies direct biological offspring who share in a family line.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses the term across many contexts to trace genealogies, establish legal inheritance, and express close relational bonds. The word often carries significant weight regarding discipleship and mentorship, where a teacher or spiritual leader refers to a follower as a son. The term also highlights moral alignment, where individuals are described not merely by physical birth, but by the spiritual or behavioral family they resemble through their actions.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of the term in historical narratives frequently underscores the weighty responsibility of generational faithfulness. Family lineage alone does not guarantee spiritual standing or godly character. The recurring biblical theme of sons who fail to walk in their fathers' righteousness demonstrates that covenant relationship with God requires individual obedience, while also showing the tragic ripple effects of parental failure within a household.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In this verse, the word introduces a sharp moral contrast within the leadership of Israel. The narrative uses the plural form to link Eli’s two offspring together in their corruption. By explicitly identifying them as the "sons" of Eli at the opening of this dark chapter, the text sets up a tragic tension between their privileged priestly heritage and their actual godless behavior. Their identity as descendants of a recognized spiritual leader makes their rebellion against God all the more glaring.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 3:16 — The word appears in reference to childbearing, illustrating the pain and relational strain introduced into human families after the fall.

  • Judges 11:2 — The term describes offspring within a family dispute, highlighting the domestic friction and social displacement surrounding Jephthah's early life.

  • 1 Chronicles 3:9 — The word is used in genealogical lists to record royal descendants, maintaining the historical record of David's royal line.

  • Nehemiah 7:43 — The term designates returning exiles by their ancestral family groups to re-establish proper temple administration in Jerusalem.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The repetition of the term throughout historical and legal literature demonstrates how deeply ancient Hebrew culture was shaped by clan and household structures. A person's identity was rarely viewed in isolation from their family origin. Yet, as the narrative of Eli's household shows, the Scriptures consistently challenge mere generational pedigree by demanding personal accountability before God.
