CUSTOM - (mishpâṭ, Strong's H4941)

mishpat ( mishpat , Strong's H4941 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun mishpat refers broadly to a legal decision, standard, justice, rightful claim, or established practice. When describing God's standards, it refers to His holy ordinances and righteous judgments. In 1 Samuel 2:13, the word refers to the official habit or claimed entitlement of the priests at Shiloh. The author uses mishpat here to show how Eli's corrupt sons had turned an unauthorized, self-serving practice into a settled routine, treating their selfish greed as if it were a legitimate priesthood right.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The noun mishpat comes from the root verb shaphat, which means to judge, govern, or render a decision. In ancient Israel, mishpat formed the core of communal life, worship, and government. It described both the legal decisions handed down by judges and the permanent laws God gave His people through Moses.

In 1 Samuel 2, the historical setting centers on the sanctuary at Shiloh during the priesthood of Eli. Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, served as priests, but the text calls them corrupt men who did not know the Lord. Rather than administering God's holy tabernacle according to His revealed law, they established their own self-serving system for handling sacrifices. The word mishpat appears here to describe the customary procedure these priests enforced upon worshippers coming to present offerings to God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Mishpat has a wide range of uses in the Old Testament:

  • It refers to divine justice, uprightness, and fairness in contrast to wickedness or oppression.

  • It refers to God's legal decrees, statutes, and revealed rules for Israel's worship and civil life.

  • It refers to a legal right, privilege, or proper entitlement belonging to a person or group.

  • It refers to a regular custom, established procedure, or standard mode of operation.

In 1 Samuel 2:13, mishpat carries the sense of established procedure or claimed privilege. It describes the settled policy that the priests enforced when worshippers came to offer sacrifices at the tabernacle.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture frequently contrasts God's righteous mishpat with corrupted human judgments. When God is the author of mishpat, it represents absolute truth, fairness, and holiness. God's judgments protect the vulnerable and order the worship of His sanctuary so that He is honored.

However, when human beings invent their own mishpat apart from God's word, it becomes a tool of exploitation. In 1 Samuel 2:13, mishpat is used descriptively rather than normatively. It does not mean God approved of their custom. Instead, it describes what had become the standard operating procedure for Eli's sons. They had institutionalized their abuse of power until public expectation treat it as the priest's regular "custom."

4. Theological Implications

The tragic misuse of mishpat by Eli's sons reveals a profound spiritual lesson about religious corruption. God had given clear instructions in the Law of Moses regarding which portions of sacrificial animals belonged to the priests and when those portions could be taken. The sacred fat belonged to the Lord first, burned on the altar as an offering.

When spiritual leaders replace God's revealed standards with their own manufactured practices, worship is severely damaged. Hophni and Phinehas treated sacred worship as personal gain. By insisting on their unholy custom, they forced worshippers to yield to human greed rather than God's law. This passage demonstrates that an established religious practice or institutional custom is not holy simply because leaders have performed it for a long time. Any human practice that contradicts God's word corrupts worship and invites divine judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:13–17, the narrator details exactly what the "priests' custom" involved. When a worshipper brought a sacrifice and began boiling the meat, the priest's servant would arrive with a three-pronged fork, plunge it into the pot, and take whatever meat the fork brought up. Furthermore, as verse 15 explains, they demanded raw meat before the fat was even burned for God on the altar.

This custom violated God's commands on two fronts:

1. It replaced God's designated portions (the breast and thigh given by law) with a random, greedy grab.

2. It put the priests' desire for roasted meat ahead of God's right to receive the fat sacrifice first.

The word mishpat in verse 13 carries deep contextual irony. The priests claimed this procedure was their legitimate "right" or "custom," yet verse 17 concludes that "the sin of the young men was very great before the Lord, for men abhorred the offering of the Lord." What the priests called an established right, God viewed as contempt for His holy altar.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The corruption of mishpat at Shiloh serves as a dramatic backdrop for the calling of Samuel. While Eli's sons abused their position and demanded their self-made custom, young Samuel was growing up in the tabernacle, serving before the Lord. God eventually broke His silence in Israel not through the corrupt priests who enforced greedy routines, but through the child who listened to His word.

True ministry never treats God's holy gifts as personal rights. Whenever spiritual leaders act out of entitlement, they distort divine justice into selfish custom. Faithful ministry submits human practice to God's written word, honoring His standards above human tradition.
