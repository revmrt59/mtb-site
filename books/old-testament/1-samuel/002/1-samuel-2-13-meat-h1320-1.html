MEAT - (bâsâr, Strong's H1320)

BAsar ( basar , Strong’s [H1320] )

Summary:

The Hebrew term basar refers primarily to physical flesh, the bodily tissue of humans or animals, and by extension can describe living creatures or humanity as a whole. In 1 Samuel 2:13, the NKJV translates this term as "meat," referring specifically to the portion of the sacrificial animal brought by the worshipper. Here, the word highlights the tangible, physical provision of the sacrifice that corrupt priests were unlawfully demanding before the fat could be burned on the altar.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the Old Testament world, physical meat from animal sacrifices served not only as food for sustenance but also as a central element of fellowship offerings shared between God, the priests, and the worshippers. The material reality of flesh anchored ancient worship in concrete daily life rather than abstract ritual. In the historical setting of early Israel, animal sacrifices were regulated by strict divine law to ensure proper reverence and to prevent greed or sacrilege from entering the tabernacle service.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Basar most fundamentally denotes physical flesh or bodily tissue. When used regarding animals, it refers to meat. Beyond its physical sense, the word frequently describes humankind in its frailty, weakness, or earthly existence. The term carries no inherent moral quality by itself; it simply points to the material substance of bodies. In 1 Samuel 2:13, the word designates the boiled meat of the sacrificial offering that was rightfully reserved in part for the worshipper and the Lord, but which Eli's wicked sons sought to seize by force.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses basar across several distinct contexts while retaining its core focus on physical reality. It often describes the physical body of a person or animal. Elsewhere, it refers broadly to all mortal creatures or human beings in general, emphasizing human frailty in contrast to divine strength. When applied to dietary laws and sacrificial procedures, the word denotes consumable animal flesh. The noun is singular in form.

4. Theological Implications

The use of basar in sacrificial contexts underscores how God meets His people in their physical, bodily existence. Worship in ancient Israel involved real food, real animals, and tangible acts of obedience. When holy things were treated with contempt—as seen in the greed of the sons of Eli regarding the sacrificial meat—the corruption violated the physical ordinances God established to teach reverence and holiness.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:13, the term describes the raw or boiled meat brought by ordinary Israelites offering sacrifices at Shiloh. The immediate narrative exposes the severe spiritual corruption of Hophni and Phinehas, who sent their servant with a three-pronged fleshhook to demand whatever meat he thrust into the pot. This physical greed directly defied God's regulations and demonstrated a profound disregard for the holiness of the Lord, turning sacred worship into an instrument of personal gluttony.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:21 — And the Lord God caused a deep sleep to fall on Adam, and he slept; and He took one of his ribs, and closed up the flesh in its place.

  • Leviticus 22:6 — The person who has touched any such thing shall be unclean until evening, and shall not eat the set meat unless he has bathed his body in water.

  • Numbers 18:15 — Everything that first opens the matrix of all flesh, which they bring to the Lord, whether man or beast, shall be yours: nevertheless the firstborn of man you shall surely redeem, and the firstborn of unclean animals you shall redeem.

  • Job 19:20 — My bone clings to my skin and to my flesh, and I have escaped by the skin of my teeth.

  • Isaiah 58:7 — Is it not to share your bread with the hungry, and that you bring to your house the poor who are cast out; when you see the naked, that you cover him, and not hide yourself from your own flesh?

7. Other Relevant Insights

The physical nature of the sacrificial system emphasized that sin and fellowship with God involved real-life actions. The abuse of the sacrificial meat at Shiloh was not merely a breach of kitchen etiquette, but a direct rebellion against the divine order of worship. Scripture consistently uses physical terminology like flesh to remind readers of humanity's complete dependence on God for daily sustenance and life.
