PRIEST - (kôhên, Strong's H3548)

Summary:

The word kohen refers to a priest, a minister consecrated to act as a mediator between God and human worshippers by managing sanctuary services, presenting sacrifices, and teaching God's law. In 1 Samuel 2:14, the word refers to the officiating minister serving at the tabernacle in Shiloh under the house of Eli. In this setting, it highlights the tragic contrast between a sacred office ordained for God's glory and the greedy, self-serving actions of Eli's sons, who unlawfully seized sacrificial meat for themselves.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Under the Law of Moses, God established the priesthood to maintain holy order and facilitate worship among Israel. The priests came from the tribe of Levi and the family line of Aaron. They were tasked with tending the tabernacle, offering sacrifices upon the altar, keeping the lamps burning, burning incense, declaring ceremonial purity, and teaching God's statutes to the nation.

During the era of the judges, the central sanctuary stood at Shiloh. The high priest Eli presided over the worship, but his sons, Hophni and Phinehas, served corruptly. Rather than guiding Israel in reverent devotion, they used their priestly authority to extort the worshippers and desecrate the sanctuary offerings.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word kohen primarily means a priest—an official who performs religious rites and stands as a representative between a deity and people.

When applied to Israel, the word refers to an anointed servant from the line of Aaron who stands before the Lord on behalf of the congregation. The main responsibilities of a kohen include:

  • Presenting animal sacrifices and grain offerings on the altar.

  • Maintaining the sacred furniture, lamps, and veil inside the sanctuary.

  • Making atonement for the sins and impurities of the people.

  • Pronouncing divine blessings upon the congregation.

The word can also describe non-Israelite priests, such as Melchizedek, king of Salem, or priest-figures of foreign nations. On rare occasions, it describes chief ministers or royal advisors in a king's court. In 1 Samuel 2:14, the word identifies the priest acting at the Shiloh altar.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the books of Moses, kohen carries a sense of holy separation. A priest was required to meet specific physical and moral standards, wear consecrated garments, and follow precise instructions for every sacrifice. The priest was not a free agent; he was bound to God's exact instructions because the sanctuary represented the holy presence of God dwelling among His people.

Scripture shows that the effectiveness and integrity of the priesthood depended on faithful obedience to God's commandments. A priest who honored God preserved the spiritual health of the entire nation. Conversely, when a priest ignored God's directions, the people were led into sin, and God's holy name was profaned.

4. Theological Implications

The office of kohen reveals key truths about God's holiness and man's need for mediation:

  • God is holy, and sinful humanity cannot approach Him carelessly. God appointed priests as mediators so that people could bring offerings and draw near safely.

  • Priestly service is a trust granted by divine appointment. Human ministers do not own the sanctuary or the sacrifices; they are guardians serving God and His worshippers.

  • Unfaithful priests bring severe judgment. When those ordained to represent God use their position for selfish gain, they distort the character of God in the eyes of the people.

Ultimately, the limitations and failures of human Levitical priests highlight the need for a perfect, unblemished mediator. Jesus Christ fulfills and surpasses the Old Testament priesthood, serving as the flawless High Priest who offered Himself once for all.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:14, kohen is used to describe the practice of Eli's wicked sons and their servants at Shiloh. According to the Law, specific portions of peace offerings—namely the breast and the thigh—were designated for the priests after the fat was burned on the altar for the Lord.

Instead of waiting for God's portion to be offered first, Eli's priests sent a servant with a three-pronged fork while the meat was boiling. Whatever the fork brought up out of the pot, "the priest took for himself."

The word priest in this verse underscores the tragedy of spiritual abuse. The sacred title kohen should have guaranteed holiness, fairness, and reverence. Instead, it became associated with violence, greed, and contempt for God's altar. By taking "for himself" what belonged to God and the worshipper, the priest turned a sacred act of fellowship into an act of robbery.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The violation described in 1 Samuel 2:14 was particularly destructive because peace offerings were designed to be joyful communal meals. In a peace offering, God received the fat on the altar, the priest received his commanded portion, and the worshipper ate the remainder with family and friends before the Lord.

By demanding raw meat before the fat was burned, or by thrusting a fork into the pot to grab whatever they wanted, Eli's sons ruined the peace meal. Worshippers who came to celebrate God's goodness were instead intimidated and robbed. This is why 1 Samuel 2 notes that the sin of the young men was very great before the Lord, for men abhorred the offering of the Lord.
