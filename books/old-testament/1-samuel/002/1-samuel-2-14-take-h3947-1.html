TAKE - (lâqach, Strong's H3947)

take - ( laqach, Strong’s [H3947] )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb laqach means to take, grasp, seize, capture, or receive. In 1 Samuel 2:14, the word describes the brazen action of the corrupt priests' servant who would use a three-pronged fleshhook to "take" whatever meat the fork brought up from the boiling pot for himself, forcefully claiming what belonged by right to God and the worshipper.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word laqach is a common Hebrew verb used throughout the Old Testament for physical movement, grabbing, and acquiring objects or persons. It appears in everyday transactions, such as taking a wife, acquiring land, or gathering supplies, as well as in solemn religious contexts, such as God taking a person in death or judgment. The physical action is direct and intentional, involving a person reaching out to grasp or secure something for possession.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to picking up an object, seizing property by force or authority, accepting a gift, or receiving instruction. When used in everyday contexts, it simply means to lay hold of an item. When used in official or legal contexts, it can describe acquiring ownership or taking someone into custody. In this verse, the term denotes the physical act of grasping and pulling meat out of the boiling pot for personal consumption.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses laqach across a wide range of literal and metaphorical settings. It frequently describes physical acquisition, such as gathering materials, capturing cities in warfare, or receiving offerings. The word also carries profound spiritual weight when God is the subject, as when He takes a person into fellowship or takes away life. The nuance depends heavily on who is acting and what is being acquired, shifting easily from mundane borrowing to forceful appropriation.

4. Theological Implications

The context of 1 Samuel 2 surrounds the wicked sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, who abused their priestly office. Their servants used the fleshhook to take whatever came up, violating the proper order of sacrifices established in the law. While the verb itself simply means to take, its use here exposes a greedy heart that appropriates holy things for selfish stomachs. The theological weight in this passage lies not in the definition of the verb, but in the blatant disregard for God's prescribed boundaries regarding sacrifices.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:14, the text describes the exact method by which the corrupt priests abused the sacrificial system. Before the fat was burned up to God as required, the servant would thrust the fleshhook into the pan, kettle, caldron, or pot, and whatever the hook brought up the priest would take for himself. The verb highlights the aggressive, entitled manner in which these men laid hands on portions that rightfully belonged to the Lord or the worshipper, treating holy offerings as common food for their own greed.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The actions of Eli’s sons reveal how outward religious service can become corrupted by internal greed. The physical act of reaching into the pot with a fleshhook symbolizes a deeper spiritual reality: taking what belongs to God and using it to satisfy fleshly appetites. This abuse of the sacrificial meal ultimately brought severe divine judgment upon Eli's house.
