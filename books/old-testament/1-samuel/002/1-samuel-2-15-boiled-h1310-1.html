BOILED - (bâshal, Strong's H1310)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bashal means to cook, boil, or seethe food in liquid. It describes the standard process of preparing meat or grain through boiling, particularly during religious sacrifices and communal meals in ancient Israel.

In 1 Samuel 2:15, bashal describes meat that had been cooked in water according to established sacrificial procedures. In this passage, the corrupt sons of Eli explicitly rejected this standard "boiled" meat, demanding raw meat instead so they could roast it with the choice fat still attached before the Lord's portion was offered on the altar.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, cooking meat usually involved boiling it in liquid within pottery jars, kettles, or metal pans. When Israel gathered at the tabernacle in Shiloh to offer peace offerings and fellowship sacrifices, the prescribed procedure was for the worshiper and priest to boil the sacrificial meat after the fat portions had been burned on the altar for the Lord.

During the period of the judges, Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, served as priests at Shiloh. Instead of honoring the sacrifice as a holy meal dedicated to God, they used their priestly authority to satisfy their own selfish desires. The word bashal appears in 1 Samuel 2:15 within a description of how Eli's sons violated God's instructions regarding the sacrifices brought by Israelite worshipers.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of bashal is to cook food by boiling or seething it in liquid. In a few agricultural contexts, it can also describe fruit ripening or coming to maturity through natural warmth.

When applied to food preparation, bashal specifically designates cooking in liquid, distinguishing it from roasting directly over an open fire. In 1 Samuel 2:15, the word appears as a passive participle describing meat that has undergone the process of boiling. Here it refers to meat that has been properly cooked in water as part of the sacred worship routine.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, bashal is used for both sacred and ordinary culinary contexts. In everyday settings, people boiled meat or grains to prepare meals for guests or large gatherings.

In the sacrificial system, bashal plays an important role:

  • Priestly Consecration: Moses was commanded to boil the flesh of the consecration ram at the entrance of the tabernacle for Aaron and his sons to eat (Exodus 29:31; Leviticus 8:31).

  • Peace Offerings: After presenting the fat on the altar, worshipers boiled the remaining meat at the sanctuary to share in a communal meal before God.

  • Passover Restrictions: The original Passover instructions specifically prohibited boiling (bashal) the lamb in water, commanding that it be roasted over fire instead (Exodus 12:9). Later under King Josiah, Passover sacrifices were roasted, while other holy offerings brought alongside Passover were boiled in pots and kettles (2 Chronicles 35:13).

4. Theological Implications

The mention of "boiled" meat in 1 Samuel 2:15 highlights the contrast between God's ordained order for worship and human self-exaltation. God's law specified that the fat of an animal belonged to Him alone and was to be burned on the altar first as a pleasing aroma. Only after God received His portion was the remaining meat to be boiled for the priests and worshipers.

By refusing "boiled" meat and demanding raw meat beforehand, Eli's sons committed a severe theological offense:

  • They robbed God of His rightful portion by taking the meat before the fat was offered on the altar.

  • They despised the fellowship meal provided by God, preferring tender raw meat for personal roasting over the cooked portion appointed for priests.

  • They treated sacred worship as a means of personal gain, showing that they preferred satisfying their own physical appetites over honoring Yahweh.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:15, the priest's servant approached worshipers before they could burn the fat on the altar, declaring: "Give meat for roasting to the priest, for he will not take boiled meat from you, but raw."

The passive form of bashal in this verse identifies the meat prepared according to God's standard rule. Under normal practice, the meat was boiled only after the fat was presented to God. Eli's sons refused to wait for this process. They rejected the customary "boiled" meat because boiling rendered the fat into the broth, leaving leaner portions. They demanded raw meat with the fat intact so they could roast it for themselves.

This detail reveals that the sin of Eli's sons was not merely a matter of bad manners or administrative greed. It was an intentional act of rebellion against God's sacred instructions, prioritizing personal luxury over holy obedience.

  • 2 Chronicles 35:13 – "Also they roasted the Passover offerings with fire according to the ordinance; but the other holy offerings they boiled in pots, in kettles, and in pans, and divided them quickly among all the lay people."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The distinction between boiling and roasting in ancient Israel carried practical and symbolic weight. Boiling in water was the standard, frugal method for feeding many people thoroughly and evenly from communal offerings. Roasting over open flame was often reserved for special occasions or specific ritual requirements like the Passover lamb.

By turning up their noses at "boiled" meat, Hophni and Phinehas showed how far they had drifted from true priestly service. Their refusal of the boiled sacrificial portions exposed a heart attitude that viewed sacred duties as an opportunity for self-indulgence rather than a privilege of serving God and His people.
