BURN - (qâṭar, Strong's H6999)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qatar means to burn an offering on an altar so that sacrificial smoke rises upward to God. Unlike general words for burning that describe destruction by fire, qatar refers specifically to a holy act of worship where gifts such as fat, incense, or burnt offerings are turned into a soothing aroma for the Lord. In 1 Samuel 2:16, the word highlights the sacred boundary between divine honor and human selfishness. An ordinary worshiper pleads with the priest's servant to burn the fat to God first before taking meat for himself, exposing how Eli's corrupt sons were despising God's altar by snatching their share before the Lord received His.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the worship system given to Israel at Mount Sinai, sacrifices served as a vital means of fellowship, atonement, and tribute between God and His people. When an animal was sacrificed on the altar, specific portions—most notably the fat covering the internal organs—were set apart exclusively for the Lord.

The verb qatar describes the physical and spiritual act of burning these designated holy portions on the altar. As the fire consumed the offering, the rising smoke signified that the sacrifice was ascending to God as an acceptable gift. The setting of 1 Samuel 2 takes place at the Tabernacle in Shiloh during the time of the judge Eli. The priesthood had fallen into deep spiritual decay under Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, who viewed the Lord's sacrifices as a tool for personal gain rather than a sacred duty.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word qatar refers to burning an offering, incense, or fat on an altar to produce sacrificial smoke. It describes transforming a physical sacrifice into smoke that ascends toward heaven as a sweet-smelling aroma to the Lord.

The word differs from general Hebrew terms for burning. Standard words for burning often refer to destroying something with fire, such as consuming trash, burning down a city, or destroying evidence. In contrast, qatar carries a specialized worship meaning. It is not about destruction, but about presentation. When qatar is used, the focus is on the altar, the smoke, and the ritual offering of a sacrifice to a deity. Depending on the context, the word can refer to burning fat on the altar of burnt offering, burning fragrant incense on the golden altar, or improperly offering smoke to false gods.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the books of Moses, qatar appears frequently to outline the official duties of the priesthood. Priests were commanded to burn the fat of peace offerings and sin offerings on the altar, ensuring that God received His designated share first. The word is also central to the daily burning of incense in the Holy Place, where the fragrant smoke represented the prayers and worship of God's people.

In the historical and prophetic books, qatar takes on a tragic shift. When Israel fell into apostasy, the people used this same sacred action to worship pagan deities. Instead of burning incense and sacrifices to the Lord at His sanctuary, they burned incense to Baal and other idols on high places and rooftops.

In 1 Samuel 2, the word serves as a benchmark for true worship. It highlights the proper procedure that every Israelite understood: God's portion must go up in smoke on the altar before any human participant enjoys the sacrificial meal.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical mandate to burn the fat first reveals fundamental truths about God's holiness and worship:

  • God claims priority in all worship. Under the Old Covenant, the fat belonged to the Lord. Burning it on the altar acknowledged His absolute ownership and sovereignty before anyone else satisfied their physical hunger.

  • Sacred worship must not be bent to human appetite. The altar was designed to honor God, not to cater to the convenience or greed of human ministers.

  • Robbing God of His portion is an act of contempt. When priests or worshipers bypassed the altar burning, they were declaring that their own desires mattered more than God's command.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:16, the word appears in a dramatic confrontation between a faithful worshiper and a corrupt priestly servant. According to the Law, when a peace offering was brought, the fat was burned on the altar for the Lord, a portion of the meat was given to the priest, and the rest was eaten by the worshiper.

However, Eli's sons sent their servants to demand raw meat with the fat still attached, so they could roast it for themselves. When an ordinary worshiper objected, saying, "They should really burn the fat first, then you may take as much as your heart desires," he was pleading for God's honor to be respected.

The worshiper understood that the burning of the fat (qatar) was the necessary first step that made the entire meal holy. The servant's reply—threatening to take the meat by force—showed that the priests no longer cared about God's altar. By refusing to let the fat be burned to the Lord first, Eli's sons were treating the Lord's offering with outright disdain.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between raw meat for roasting and fat burned for God highlights the spiritual blind spot of Eli's sons. Boiling the meat after burning the fat was the prescribed biblical pattern. But Eli's sons preferred raw, uncooked meat so they could roast it with the fat still on it, indulging their own palates.

This detail shows that the sin of Hophni and Phinehas was not merely bad etiquette; it was a brazen violation of God's sanctuary rules. The ordinary Israelite in verse 16 showed greater reverence for God's holiness than the very priests who were appointed to serve at the Tabernacle.
