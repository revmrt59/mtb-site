FAT - (cheleb, Strong's H2459)

Summary:

The Hebrew word cheleb refers to animal fat, specifically the choice fat portions surrounding the internal organs, and metaphorically denotes the finest, richest, or best part of something. In Israelite sacrificial worship, the fat belonged exclusively to God and was burned on the altar as a pleasing aroma. In 1 Samuel 2:16, the word refers to the fat of the sacrificial offering that was required to be burned on the altar before anyone else received a share. The priests' refusal to let the fat be burned first showed open contempt for God's law and exposed their willingness to put their physical appetites ahead of God's honor.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

During the period of the judges, Israel’s sanctuary was located at Shiloh, where the tabernacle housed the Ark of the Covenant. Eli served as high priest, but his sons, Hophni and Phinehas, served as priests alongside him. The book of 1 Samuel opens by contrasting the quiet piety of faithful worshipers like Elkanah and Hannah with the corruption occurring at the central sanctuary.

Under the Mosaic law, specific offerings—such as the peace offering or fellowship offering—allowed the worshiper, the priest, and the LORD to share in a communal meal. However, God established strict instructions regarding the order and distribution of the animal. Before any meat could be taken by the priest or returned to the worshiper, the fat had to be separated and burned on the bronze altar. By demanding raw meat before the fat was burned, Eli’s sons disrupted the sacred rhythm of worship and claimed for themselves what belonged solely to God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun cheleb refers literally to the fat of animals. In ancient Near Eastern husbandry and sacrifice, fat was not viewed as waste or unwanted byproduct; rather, it was considered the richest, most flavorful, and most valuable part of the animal.

In the Old Testament, the term is used in two primary ways:

  • Literal animal fat: The hard fat covering the internal organs (such as the kidneys, liver, and entrails) of cattle, sheep, and goats offered in sacrifice.

  • Metaphorical best or choice part: By extension, the word describes the finest or highest quality of produce, such as the best of the oil, wine, or grain.

In sacrificial contexts, cheleb identifies the portion that was strictly set apart for the LORD. It was never to be eaten by the people or claimed by the priests for personal consumption.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, cheleb appears most frequently in the books of Exodus, Leviticus, and Numbers, where the altar instructions for sacrifices are laid out in detail.

In the sacrificial system, burning the fat on the altar represented giving the very best of the animal to God. Leviticus explicitly declares that "all the fat is the LORD's." Because the fat represented the richness and vitality of the life offered, burning it as a sweet aroma signified that God has absolute priority over the worshipers' gifts.

Beyond sacrificial contexts, cheleb expresses the concept of abundance, richness, or supreme quality. When used to describe agricultural yield, it highlights the finest portion of the harvest. Thus, the word carries a consistent nuance of highest value and divine ownership.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical instructions regarding cheleb highlight several important theological truths:

First, God demands the first and finest portion. Worship is not giving God leftovers; it is presenting Him with the best of what He has provided. Reserving the fat for the altar acknowledged God as the source and owner of all blessings.

Second, taking what belongs to God is sacrilege. The fat belonged to the LORD as an altar offering. To divert the fat—or to ignore the requirement to burn it first—was an act of robbery against God. It replaced divine authority with human self-interest.

Third, reverence in worship reflects heart attitude. External rituals were never meant to be empty mechanical steps. Honoring the instructions for the fat demonstrated genuine fear of God and respect for His holiness. Disregarding those steps revealed an unregenerate or hardened heart that treated holy things as common.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:16, the spiritual collapse of Eli’s sons is put on full display. When a conscientious worshiper came to offer a sacrifice, he recognized the command of Scripture and pleaded with the priest's servant: "Let them burn the fat first, then take as much as your heart desires."

This ordinary worshiper understood the priority of God's right. He was willing to give the priests whatever meat they wanted, provided God received His altar portion first. But the servant of the priests replied with a threat of violence, insisting on taking raw meat immediately so it could be roasted rather than boiled.

In this context, cheleb serves as the key line of demarcation between true worship and profane arrogance:

  • The worshiper respected the cheleb as the LORD's holy portion.

  • The priests despised the cheleb because burning it meant waiting on God rather than gratifying their own immediate desires.

By demanding their share before the fat was offered, Eli's sons treated the offering of the LORD with contempt. Their sin was not merely greed for food; it was practical atheism within the holy place, putting personal luxury above divine glory.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 4:4 — "Abel also brought of the firstborn of his flock and of their fat."

  • Leviticus 3:17 — "This shall be a perpetual statute throughout your generations in all your dwellings: you shall eat neither fat nor blood."

  • Numbers 18:32 — "And you shall bear no sin because of it, when you have offered the best of it..."

  • 1 Samuel 15:22 — "Has the LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, as in obeying the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to heed than the fat of rams."

  • 2 Chronicles 7:7 — "Furthermore Solomon consecrated the middle of the court... because the bronze altar which Solomon had made was not able to receive the burnt offerings, the grain offerings, and the fat."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The conflict in 1 Samuel 2 illustrates how religious leaders can abuse spiritual authority for personal gain. Hophni and Phinehas used their holy office to satisfy physical appetites, treating God's altar as secondary to their own kitchen.

Later in the book, Samuel echoes this same principle when confronting King Saul in 1 Samuel 15:22, noting that to heed God is better than offering the "fat of rams." Sacrifices and ritual offerings mean nothing to God when accompanied by willful disobedience. The judgment that eventually fell upon Eli's house stands as a perpetual warning that God will honor those who honor Him, but those who despise Him will be lightly esteemed.
