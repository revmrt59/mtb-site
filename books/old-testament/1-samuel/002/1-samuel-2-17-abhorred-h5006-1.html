ABHORRED - (nâʼats, Strong's H5006)

Summary:

The Hebrew word na'ats means to spurn, despise, reject, or treat with disdainful contempt. It describes an attitude or action that treats something holy, valuable, or authoritative as worthless or reviled. In 1 Samuel 2:17, the word describes how the people of Israel came to abhor and revile the Lord's sacrificial offerings because of the corrupt, greedy, and abusive conduct of Eli's sons at the tabernacle.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

1 Samuel 2 presents a sharp contrast between young Samuel, who served before the Lord in purity, and the sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, who served as priests in Shiloh. The tabernacle was Israel's central place of worship, where worshippers brought peace offerings to express fellowship with God. According to God's law, the fat belonged to the Lord and was burned on the altar first, after which the priests received a designated portion of meat.

However, Eli's sons treated this divine order with total disregard. They demanded raw meat before the fat was offered, and if a worshipper hesitated, their servants took it by force. This public abuse of sacred duties created a spiritual scandal in Israel, causing the people to look upon the sacred offerings with revulsion and contempt.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word na'ats means to despise, spurn, treat with disdain, or show active contempt toward someone or something. It goes beyond mild disrespect; it expresses a sharp rejection or severe disdain that treats a sacred object, a divine commandment, or God Himself as worthless or distasteful.

When directed toward God, na'ats describes spurning His authority or blaspheming His holy name. When directed toward God's Word or instruction, it means despising His counsel. In 1 Samuel 2:17, the word describes the public reaction to spoiled worship: the people spurned and abhorred the sacrificial offerings of the Lord.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, the word na'ats is used when people show open contempt for divine authority or sacred things.

  • When people reject God's word or promise, Scripture uses this word to show how their disbelief acts as a direct insult to God, as seen when Israel refused to enter the Promised Land (Numbers 14:11).

  • When people mock or blaspheme the Lord, na'ats describes the severe offense of holding the Creator in contempt (Psalms 74:18).

  • When people reject wisdom and instruction, the word highlights the foolish decision to treat God's guidance as useless (Proverbs 1:30).

In all these contexts, na'ats carries the heavy nuance of treating with contempt what ought to be held in highest honor.

4. Theological Implications

The tragic use of na'ats in 1 Samuel 2:17 reveals critical truths about leadership, worship, and God's holiness.

First, spiritual leadership directly impacts how people view God. When leaders act with greed, arrogance, or immorality while handling sacred duties, they drive people away from God and make holy worship seem distasteful.

Second, God's holiness is offended when His worship is corrupted. Sacrifices were appointed as the gracious means for sinful people to draw near to God. Corrupting these gifts led to public revulsion toward the very altar God established for grace and atonement.

Third, God holds leaders accountable for causing others to stumble. Later in 1 Samuel 2, God directly confronts Eli and his household for honoring themselves above Him, demonstrating that God will guard the honor of His own name when human leaders profane it.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:17, the statement that "men abhorred the offering of the LORD" explains why the sin of Eli's sons was called "very great before the LORD."

The verb appears in a plural common perfect form to express a widespread reality among the worshippers. The scandal was not an isolated incident or a minor private grievance; it became a widespread public consensus. Israelite worshippers, coming to Shiloh out of devotion to God, were met with intimidation and robbery by the priests. Consequently, the people developed a deep revulsion toward the sacrifice itself.

The sorrowful irony of the passage is that the priests were supposed to lead Israel in honoring God, but instead, their wickedness led the entire congregation to treat God's sacrifices with abhorrence.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 14:11 — "How long will these people reject Me?" (Here the word describes Israel's spurning of the Lord despite His miraculous signs.)

  • Psalms 107:11 — "Because they rebelled against the words of God, and despised the counsel of the Most High." (Here the word shows the folly of treating divine wisdom with contempt.)

  • Proverbs 1:30 — "They would have none of my counsel and despised every rebuke of mine." (Here the word depicts the foolish rejecting wisdom's loving correction.)

  • Isaiah 1:4 — "They have forsaken the LORD, they have provoked the Holy One of Israel to anger." (Here the word captures how Israel's sin spurned God's holy presence.)

  • Jeremiah 14:21 — "Do not abhor us, for Your name's sake; do not disgrace the throne of Your glory." (In this prayer, the prophet begs God not to spurn His covenant people.)

7. Other Relevant Insights

The sin at Shiloh serves as a timeless warning against handling holy things with indifference or self-serving motives. The Old Testament sacrifices at Shiloh pointed forward to the ultimate sacrifice of Jesus Christ. When leaders or believers treat worship as a means for personal gain, they distract from the gospel and cause observers to despise what is meant to bring life. True worship honors God's holiness and treats His appointed means of grace with reverent awe.
