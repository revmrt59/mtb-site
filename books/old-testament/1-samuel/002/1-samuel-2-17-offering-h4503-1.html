OFFERING - (minchâh, Strong's H4503)

OFFERING - ( minchah , Strong’s [H4503] )

Summary:

Minchah refers to a gift, tribute, or sacrifice brought to a superior, a ruler, or to God. In 1 Samuel 2:17, the word describes the grain or meal portion of the sacrifices brought to the tabernacle at Shiloh, which the wicked sons of Eli treated with contempt.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew word minchah is used throughout the Old Testament to describe various forms of tribute and offering. In ancient Near Eastern culture, a lesser party would bring a minchah—such as agricultural produce, valuable goods, or a tribute payment—to express respect, submission, or loyalty to a king or dignitary. Within Israel's worship, the term frequently designates grain and drink offerings presented to God, often accompanying animal sacrifices. It represents a portion of the provision God gave, returned to Him as an act of worship and acknowledgment of His lordship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Minchah can refer to a gift given voluntarily, a tribute exacted by a conqueror, or a sacred offering presented to God. When directed toward people, it functions as a diplomatic present or tribute to secure favor or recognize authority. When directed toward God, it identifies a sacrificial offering, most commonly a cereal or grain tribute presented on the altar. The word carries the core idea of rendering what is due, offering homage, or presenting a gift to express devotion and dependence.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses minchah in both secular and sacred contexts. In secular settings, it often describes tribute paid by subject nations or gifts brought to appease an offended person. In religious settings, it frequently points to the cereal or grain offerings prescribed in the Mosaic law, though it is also used broadly for other sacrificial offerings brought before the Lord. The specific nuance depends on the recipient and the setting, shifting from diplomatic tribute to devotional worship.

4. Theological Implications

When brought to God, a minchah is an acknowledgment that all resources originate from Him. The act of bringing an offering recognizes God's holiness, sovereignty, and right to receive humanity's best. Conversely, when offerings are mishandled, despised, or offered with corrupt hearts, the act ceases to be true worship and becomes rebellion. Scripture consistently shows that God looks beyond the outward gift to the moral and spiritual condition of the offerer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:17, the word describes the sacrificial offerings brought by the people of Israel to the tabernacle. The immediate context contrasts the faithful worship of the people with the greed and wickedness of Hophni and Phinehas, the sons of Eli. These corrupt priests treated the Lord's offering with utter contempt by seizing the best portions for themselves before the fat could be burned on the altar. The tragedy of the passage is that the sacred gift meant for God was violated by the very men appointed to guard His holiness, causing the people to abhor the worship of the Lord.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 4:3 — "In the process of time it came to pass that Cain brought an offering of the fruit of the ground to the Lord."

  • Leviticus 2:5 — "If your offering is a grain offering baked in a pan, it shall be of fine flour, unleavened, mixed with oil."

  • Judges 3:17 — "So he brought the offering to Eglon king of Moab. Now Eglon was a very fat man."

  • Psalms 96:8 — "Give to the Lord the glory due His name; Bring an offering, and come into His courts."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The violation of the sacrificial system by Eli's sons highlights a major theme in early Samuel: the severe consequences of religious corruption within the priesthood. Because the priests despised the offerings of the Lord, God pronounced judgment on Eli's house, demonstrating that religious position does not exempt individuals from divine accountability.
