SIN - (chaṭṭâʼâh, Strong's H2403)

Summary:

The word chattat basic meaning is wrongdoing, moral failure, missing the divine mark, or the sacrificial "sin offering" provided for ritual atonement. In 1 Samuel 2:17, it describes the grave moral offense and high-handed contempt of Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, whose selfish greed corrupted the tabernacle service and turned the worship of God into a public disgrace.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, worship at the central sanctuary was guided by clear instructions regarding priestly service and animal sacrifices. The priests served as mediators between God and the people, ensuring that offerings were made with reverence according to the Law of Moses.

The narrative of 1 Samuel opens during a period of spiritual low point in Israel's history. Eli served as priest at Shiloh, but his sons, Hophni and Phinehas, used their priestly position for personal gain. Instead of facilitating true worship, they demanded raw meat before the Lord's share (the fat) was burned on the altar, enforcing their demands with threats of violence. In this setting, the text uses chattat to summarize the gravity of their spiritual treason.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew word chattat comes from a root that means "to miss the mark" or "to step off the right path." It can refer to:

  • An act of moral failure or rebellion against God's commands.

  • The state of guilt or culpability resulting from wrong conduct.

  • A sacrificial "sin offering" brought to make ritual atonement and purify sacred space.

In 1 Samuel 2:17, the word is used in its feminine singular form to designate the specific, overwhelming guilt and moral offense committed by Eli's sons.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, chattat is one of the primary terms used for human moral failure. It ranges in nuance from unthinking mistakes to deliberate, high-handed rebellion against God.

A notable aspect of this word is its dual usage: it describes both the breach of God's covenant and the sacrifice appointed to deal with that breach. This connection demonstrates that sin creates a serious spiritual debt and defilement that requires ritual cleansing and divine forgiveness.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical concept of chattat shows that sin is never merely a horizontal human disagreement or a harmless flaw. It is an offense committed directly against God's holiness.

When spiritual leaders commit chattat, the consequences spread beyond their own lives. By mistreating the sanctuary sacrifices, Eli's sons damaged the nation's spiritual life, causing ordinary Israelites to shrink back from bringing their offerings to the Lord. Scripture reveals that God holds spiritual leaders to a high standard because their misconduct misrepresents His character and corrupts the worship of His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:17, the writer emphasizes that "the sin of the young men was very great before the LORD." The phrase "before the LORD" highlights that while Eli's sons were offending the people, their primary offense was directly against God Himself in the very place where His glory dwelt.

The text links their chattat to a specific cause: "for men abhorred the offering of the LORD." By taking their choice cuts of meat before burning the fat to God, Hophni and Phinehas showed that they valued their own physical appetites above God's explicit commands. Their misconduct changed what should have been a sacred time of joy and reconciliation into a scene of extortion.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 4:7 — "And if you do not do well, sin lies at the door."

  • Leviticus 4:3 — "if the anointed priest sins... then let him offer to the LORD for his sin which he has sinned a young bullock without blemish for a offering."

  • 1 Samuel 15:25 — "Now therefore, please pardon my sin, and return with me, that I may worship the LORD."

  • Psalms 79:9 — "Help us, O God of our salvation, for the glory of Your name; and deliver us, and provide atonement for our sins, for Your name's sake!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a striking literary irony in 1 Samuel 2:17. The primary duty of priests was to offer the chattat (the sin offering) on behalf of Israel to remove guilt from the community. Yet Hophni and Phinehas became the primary source of chattat in the sanctuary. Instead of removing sin, their actions generated severe moral corruption right at the altar.

This passage forms a strong contrast with the growth of young Samuel, who is mentioned nearby in the text as ministering before the Lord in simple faithfulness.
