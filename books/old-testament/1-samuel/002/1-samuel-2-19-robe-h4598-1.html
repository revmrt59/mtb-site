ROBE - (mᵉʻîyl, Strong's H4598)

Summary:

Me'il refers to an outer garment, tunic, or robe worn over basic clothing, often signaling honor, official position, or royal and priestly standing. In 1 Samuel 2:19, the word describes the small outer robe that Hannah faithfully made and brought to young Samuel year after year when she came with her husband to offer the annual sacrifice at Shiloh.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, everyday clothing typically consisted of a basic inner tunic, while an outer garment provided warmth, distinction, and social representation. The word me'il describes a high-grade outer garment or robe, often sleeveless, worn over the inner clothing by individuals holding respected or sacred positions in society.

In the historical setting of 1 Samuel 2, Israel was entering a time of spiritual transition. The priesthood at Shiloh was marred by the corruption of Eli's sons, yet God was preparing a faithful leader in the young child Samuel. Dedicated to God by his mother Hannah in fulfillment of her vow, Samuel was left at the tabernacle to serve under Eli. Hannah's practice of crafting and bringing Samuel a new outer garment each year illustrates her continuing love and covenant devotion during this critical period in Israel's history.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word me'il refers to an outer robe, cloak, or tunic worn over an inner garment. It was typically woven from fine materials such as wool or linen and was designed to cover the body down to the knees or ankles.

Depending on who wore it, the garment served various purposes:

  • High Priestly Attire: A specialized holy garment, such as the blue robe of the ephod worn beneath the high priest's breastplate.

  • Royal and Noble Apparel: Clothing worn by kings, royal daughters, and persons of high social status.

  • Prophetic and Distinguished Dress: A dignified mantle marking a recognized prophet or leader.

In 1 Samuel 2:19, the word is modified by the Hebrew word for "little" (qaton), describing a youth-sized outer robe crafted specifically for a growing child serving in the sanctuary.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Scripture, me'il appears in contexts where outward apparel reflects an individual's spiritual office, civil rank, or personal state of heart.

When used of the High Priest in Exodus, the robe represents sacred setting and consecration, trimmed at its hem with pomegranates and golden bells. When worn by royal figures like David or Jonathan, it symbolizes royal dignity and princehood. Giving away one's robe, as Jonathan did for David, signified a surrender of royal preeminence and an acknowledgment of God's chosen king.

The garment also features prominently in moments of deep grief or intense spiritual crisis. Patriarchs and leaders tore their outer robes to express brokenness, mourning, or repentance before God. Thus, the robe is never merely fabric in biblical narrative; it reflects the honor, grief, or calling of the person wearing it.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's gift of a small robe to young Samuel carries rich theological significance within the narrative of 1 Samuel:

  • Divine Calling and Preparation: Though Samuel was only a boy, wearing a me'il placed him visually in the company of priests and prophets. God was quietly preparing him to serve as Israel's judge, prophet, and priest long before his public ministry began.

  • Parental Faithfulness: Hannah did not abandon her maternal responsibility after handing Samuel over to the sanctuary. Her yearly gift demonstrated that dedication to God does not destroy godly family love; rather, it sanctifies it.

  • Contrast of Righteousness and Corruption: In 1 Samuel 2, the faithful, humble care of Hannah and the quiet service of Samuel stand in sharp contrast to the greedy, self-serving actions of Eli's sons. While Eli's family desecrated the priesthood, God was raising up a boy clothed in a mother's faithful prayers.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:19, the narrator notes that Hannah made Samuel a "little robe" and brought it to him from year to year when she came up with her husband to offer the yearly sacrifice.

The noun is singular, referring to the specific single garment brought during each annual trip. This yearly ritual underscores Samuel's ongoing growth. As the boy grew physically, he required a larger robe each year. Crucially, the passage directly connects Samuel's physical growth with his spiritual development before the Lord. The robe served as a visible marker of time passing and of God's faithful hand upon the young servant in the tabernacle.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The image of the me'il in 1 Samuel forms an important literary thread through the book. Later in Samuel's life, King Saul tore the hem of Samuel's me'il in despair, symbolizing the kingdom being torn from Saul (1 Samuel 15:27). Still later, David cut off the corner of Saul's me'il in a cave, demonstrating that God had delivered the king into David's hands (1 Samuel 24:4). Even after Samuel's death, when King Saul sought a medium at En-dor, Samuel was recognized by his characteristic mantle or robe (1 Samuel 28:14). The simple "little robe" given by Hannah in chapter 2 was thus the first of several robes that marked Samuel's lifelong prophetic identity.
