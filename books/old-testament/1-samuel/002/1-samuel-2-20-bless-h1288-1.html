BLESS - (bârak, Strong's H1288)

bless - ( barak , Strong’s [H1288] )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb barak primarily means to bless, invoke divine favor, or pronounce a benison upon another person, and it can also mean to praise or kneel. In 1 Samuel 2:20, Eli pronounces a blessing over Elkanah and Hannah, asking the Lord to grant them children to replace the one Hannah dedicated to the service of the tabernacle. The word here highlights a pastoral and priestly act of intercession, where God's representative invokes His goodness and favor upon faithful parents.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the opening chapters of First Samuel, the narrative transitions from the sorrowful barrenness of Hannah to the birth and dedication of Samuel. Eli serves as the high priest at the sanctuary in Shiloh. When Elkanah and Hannah come to worship year by year, Eli observes their faithfulness and pronounces a blessing over them. The cultural and religious setting is one of priestly intercession, where those who minister before God mediate divine favor and speak words of encouragement and spiritual well-being into the lives of God's people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb barak covers a range of meanings depending on who is speaking and who is addressed. When God is the subject, it means to bestow material or spiritual good, prosperity, and life upon people. When humans are the subject toward God, it means to praise, worship, and kneel in adoration. When humans are the subject toward other people, as Eli is here, it means to invoke God's favor, speak a blessing, or wish well upon someone. In this verse, Eli acts as a priest pronouncing a formal wish and prayer for God's continued goodness toward Elkanah and Hannah.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses barak in three main directions: downward from God to humanity, upward from humanity to God, and outward between people. In patriarchal narratives and priestly scenes, blessing is more than a polite greeting; it is an active appeal to God to pour out His tangible grace and protection. When Eli blesses Elkanah and Hannah, he acknowledges their sacrifice of dedication and asks the Lord to repay their devotion with offspring and family restoration.

4. Theological Implications

The practice of blessing reminds believers that every good gift originates with God. A human pronouncement of blessing does not possess inherent magical power; rather, it expresses faith that God alone is the source of fruitfulness and life. Eli's blessing points forward to the reality that human devotion is never forgotten by God. By asking the Lord to give Elkanah children from Hannah, Eli anchors his prayer in God's character as a rewarder of those who seek Him and honor their vows.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the immediate context of 1 Samuel 2:20, Eli responds to Elkanah and Hannah's ongoing worship and sacrifice. After Hannah leaves Samuel to serve in the house of the Lord under Eli's care, Eli blesses the parents. The surrounding text emphasizes the reward of their faithfulness, noting that the Lord visited Hannah so that she bore more children. Eli's blessing serves as both a pastoral comfort for a grieving mother's heart and a prophetic anticipation of the family's future restoration.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:22 — "And God blessed them, saying, 'Be fruitful and multiply...'"

  • Numbers 6:23 — "Speak to Aaron and his sons, saying, 'This is the way you shall bless the children of Israel...'"

  • Psalms 100:4 — "Enter into His gates with thanksgiving, and into His courts with praise. Be thankful to Him, and bless His name."

  • Psalms 135:20 — "Bless the House of Levi! You who fear the Lord, bless the Lord!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Israelite thought, words spoken in blessing carried immense weight, reflecting the speaker's desire aligned with God's covenant promises. Eli's role as high priest gave weight to his pronouncement, assuring the parents that their sacrifice at Shiloh was recognized and valued within the community of faith.
