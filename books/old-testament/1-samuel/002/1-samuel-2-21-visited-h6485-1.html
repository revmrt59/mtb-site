VISITED - (pâqad, Strong's H6485)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb paqad carries the basic meaning of attending to, inspecting, or stepping in to take specific action. When God is the subject, His "visiting" is never a mere physical appearance or passive observation; it is a decisive divine intervention. In 1 Samuel 2:21, the word describes God turning His favorable attention toward Hannah, fulfilling His gracious purpose by giving her three sons and two daughters after she dedicated her firstborn, Samuel, to His service.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The book of 1 Samuel opens during a spiritually dark era in Israel's history. The sanctuary at Shiloh was led by the aging priest Eli and his corrupt sons, Hophni and Phinehas, who abused their religious authority. In contrast to this spiritual failure, the text introduces Hannah, a godfearing woman who suffered deep distress because of her barrenness.

Hannah prayed fervently at the tabernacle, asking God for a son and promising to dedicate him entirely to divine service. God answered her prayer, giving her Samuel. After weaning the child, Hannah kept her vow and brought Samuel to Shiloh to serve the Lord for life. Chapter 2 records Hannah’s prayer of praise alongside a sobering account of Eli’s household. Right in the middle of this narrative contrast, 1 Samuel 2:21 records that the Lord "visited" Hannah, showing that God pays close attention to those who honor Him.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

processing details, the word paqad describes a superior taking notice of a person or situation in order to bring about a deliberate outcome. It carries a broad range of related meanings depending on the context:

  • To attend to or care for someone.

  • To inspect, oversee, or conduct an official accounting (such as taking a census or numbering troops).

  • To intervene actively, either to grant relief or to execute judgment.

When a person is the subject, paqad often means counting, appointing, or checking on the welfare of others. When God is the subject, the verb means He brings His presence and authority to bear on a human situation. If He visits in grace, He provides help, grants children, or delivers from oppression. If He visits in judgment, He calls people to account and punishes wrongdoing. In 1 Samuel 2:21, the word is used in its gracious sense: God attended to Hannah's need and brought life to her home.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, paqad functions in administrative, judicial, and redemptive contexts.

In military and governmental settings, the word describes counting soldiers or organizing officials. In these cases, English versions often translate it as "numbered," "counted," or "appointed." The core concept remains inspection and care: an leader counts the people to ensure everyone is accounted for and assigned a proper role.

In judicial settings, paqad describes God holding nations or individuals accountable for sin. When God "visits" wickedness, He uncovers hidden corruption and enforces righteous justice. In these passages, the word is frequently rendered as "punish" or "avenge."

In covenantal and personal settings, paqad signifies God remembering His promises to His people. Divine visitation brings dramatic transformation to human helplessness. When God visits the destitute, the captive, or the barren, He restores joy and accomplishes His redemption.

4. Theological Implications

The narrative use of paqad reveals several key truths about God's character and actions:

  • God's Active Providence: God is not an distant observer of human suffering. When Scripture says God visits someone, it highlights His active involvement in earthly affairs.

  • God's Sovereignty Over Life: Human limitations do not restrict divine power. Just as God opened Hannah's womb to give her Samuel, His gracious visitation brought further fruitfulness, demonstrating that life is entirely in His hands.

  • Divine Generosity: Hannah gave up her only son to serve God at the sanctuary. God's response—visiting her with five additional children—demonstrates that God is never in debt to His servants. He repays sacrificial devotion with abundant grace.

  • Moral Contrast: The same God who visits the faithful with mercy also visits the unrepentant with judgment. 1 Samuel juxtaposes God's gracious visit to Hannah with His impending judgment on Eli's house, proving that God evaluates human hearts impartially.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:21, the statement "the LORD visited Hannah" serves as a vital turning point in her story. Humanly speaking, Hannah had made an immense sacrifice. She had released her long-awaited firstborn son, leaving him at Shiloh under the care of Eli. To an outside observer, her home was left empty once again.

However, the text uses paqad to show that God did not leave her empty-handed. God attended to Hannah personally. As a direct result of this divine visitation, she conceived and bore three sons and two daughters. Meanwhile, the young Samuel grew in the presence of the Lord.

The immediate context highlights a quiet double growth: Hannah’s family expanded at home through God's gracious visitation, while Samuel grew spiritually in the sanctuary. This passage confirms the main theme of Hannah's prayer in 1 Samuel 2:1–10: the Lord lifts up the humble, fills the hungry with good things, and rewards those who put their trust in Him.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 21:1 — "And the LORD visited Sarah as He had said, and the LORD did for Sarah as He had spoken."

  • Exodus 20:5 — "...visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children to the third and fourth generations of those who hate Me,"

  • Job 5:24 — "You shall know that your tent is in peace; you shall visit your dwelling and find nothing amiss."

  • Jeremiah 14:10 — "...He will now remember their iniquity and punish their sins."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The connection between Genesis 21:1 and 1 Samuel 2:21 is rich in historical and canonical significance. In both passages, the verb paqad describes God intervening in the life of an otherwise barren woman to fulfill a crucial step in His redemptive plan. Sarah’s divine visitation produced Isaac, the child of promise; Hannah’s divine visitation produced Samuel, the prophet who would anoint King David.

Furthermore, this passage illustrates that God's visitation is perfectly timed. Hannah waited through years of severe provocation and grief before God acted. When God finally "visited" her, it was not a sudden impulse, but the exact moment ordained to advance His kingdom purpose for Israel.
