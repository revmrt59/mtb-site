OLD - (zâqên, Strong's H2204)

zaqen ( zā·qên , Strong’s [H2204] )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb can mean to grow old, to age, or to become advanced in years. In 1 Samuel 2:22, the verb describes Eli’s advanced physical age as he receives alarming reports regarding the grievous sins of his sons. The word highlights his elderly status in a narrative that contrasts his passive neglect with the active corruption spreading through the tabernacle.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root underlying this term frequently connects to the beard or chin in Hebrew vocabulary, drawing a natural imagery from the physical markers of maturity and advanced age in the ancient Near East. In biblical culture, gray hair and physical aging carried strong associations with status, wisdom, and responsibility within the community and family structure. The term appears across historical and narrative books to describe figures who have lived through decades of service, leadership, or family life, marking a distinct stage of human life characterized by declining physical strength and accumulated years.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb describes the process of growing old or reaching an advanced age. When applied to human life, it points directly to the natural progression of years that leads to physical seniority. The term does not inherently carry a moral evaluation; a person can grow old in righteousness or grow old while failing in vital duties. Its primary function in biblical Hebrew is simply to designate the physical reality of aging and the status of being an elder or an aged individual.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses the term across various contexts to mark the passage of time in the lives of patriarchs, leaders, and ordinary individuals. It often serves as a temporal anchor in narrative literature, signaling a transition in leadership, the approach of the end of a lifetime, or a shifting generational dynamic within a family or nation. While physical aging often brought respect and authority, biblical narratives sometimes pair advanced age with human failure or a loss of effective domestic and spiritual oversight, demonstrating that growing old does not automatically secure spiritual vigilance.

4. Theological Implications

The process of aging is placed firmly within the sovereign span of God's design for human life. While human strength wanes as years advance, Scripture consistently portrays old age as a reality witnessed and sustained by God. At the same time, the Bible does not romanticize age as a guarantee of spiritual faithfulness. The frailties and failures that sometimes accompany advanced years serve as a sobering reminder that every stage of human life depends entirely upon ongoing grace, watchfulness, and obedience to God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:22, Eli is very old, a detail that frames his tragic inability to properly restrain his wicked sons, Hophni and Phinehas. The narrative notes his advanced age just before detailing how he heard about the evil deeds his sons were committing against the people and at the doorway of the tent of meeting. His elderly status underscores the heartbreaking breakdown of leadership and parental discipline within the priestly house of Shiloh. Rather than actively removing his corrupt sons from sacred service, the aged priest offers only a weak verbal reprimand, illustrating how physical and moral exhaustion can cripple spiritual authority when it matters most.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 18:13 — "And the LORD said to Abraham, 'Why did Sarah laugh, saying, "Shall I surely bear a child, since I am old?"'"

  • Joshua 13:1 — "Now Joshua was old, advanced in years. And the LORD said to him: 'You are old, advanced in years, and there remains very much land yet to be possessed.'"

  • 1 Samuel 12:2 — "And now here is the king walking before you. And I am old and grayheaded, and indeed my sons are with you. I have walked before you from my childhood to this day."

  • Psalms 37:25 — "I have been young, and now am old; yet I have not seen the righteous forsaken, nor his descendants begging bread."

  • Proverbs 22:6 — "Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The occurrence of the verb in 1 Samuel 2:22 forms part of a broader structural contrast in the early chapters of Samuel. While Eli grows old and physically dim, the young boy Samuel grows up before the Lord in faithfulness. The contrast emphasizes a changing of the guard in Israel's leadership, where failing generational stewardship gives way to a new instrument of divine service.
