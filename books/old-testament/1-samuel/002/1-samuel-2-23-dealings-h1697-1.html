DEALINGS - (dâbâr, Strong's H1697)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun dabar broadly denotes a spoken word, a matter, a report, or an action. Depending on context, it can refer to a physical thing, a verbal message, a royal command, or a line of conduct. In 1 Samuel 2:23, the word is translated as "dealings" and refers to the widespread reports of evil deeds committed by Eli's sons at the tabernacle. Eli confronts his sons because public reports of their corrupt actions have reached his ears, showing how their misconduct had become a widespread scandal.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew noun dabar comes from a root associated with speaking and communicating. In ancient Hebrew usage, speech and action were closely connected. A word was not viewed merely as an abstract idea, but as an expression that carried reality and substance. Consequently, dabar bridges the gap between what is said and what is done. It can refer to a single spoken word, an entire message, a matter under discussion, an event, or a concrete act.

In ancient Israel, community reports and public reputation held significant weight. When news spread among the tribes regarding events at the central sanctuary in Shiloh, those reports were referred to as dabarim (the plural form of dabar). During the time of the judges, worshippers traveled from across the land to offer sacrifices. When the priests in charge of the tabernacle abused their position, reports of their corrupt behavior quickly spread throughout the nation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Dabar possesses a wide range of meaning in Old Testament usage. The word can mean:

  • A spoken word, statement, or speech.

  • A topic, matter, issue, or business under consideration.

  • An action, deed, or practice.

  • A report, message, or account brought from one person to another.

  • A decree, commandment, or divine message.

In 1 Samuel 2:23, the masculine plural form refers specifically to reported actions or conduct, translated in the NKJV as "dealings." Eli asks his sons why they commit such things, noting that he hears of their evil practices from "all the people." Here, dabar carries the sense of bad behavior that has been observed and reported.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, dabar moves fluidly between speech and action. When God speaks, His dabar brings creation into order and establishes His promises through the prophets. When used of human speech, dabar can denote honest communication, empty words, or official testimony.

When applied to conduct, dabar refers to the actual deeds of a person's life. In historical accounts, the "acts" or "matters" of a king's reign are summarized using this term. When joined with a moral description—such as "evil"—it highlights specific bad deeds that affect others. The plural form in 1 Samuel 2:23 emphasizes that Eli was responding not to an isolated mistake, but to multiple ongoing actions that had created a public pattern of sin.

4. Theological Implications

The use of dabar for priestly corruption highlights important truths about ministry, public accountability, and God's holiness:

First, public conduct carries spiritual consequences. The sinful actions of Hophni and Phinehas were not hidden; they discredited the service of the Lord and discouraged Israel from worship.

Second, news of injustice and sin reaches those in authority. God ensures that hidden corruption eventually comes to light, making leadership accountable for addressing evil.

Third, speech and behavior must align. Those appointed to handle God's word must reflect God's holiness in their actions, as corrupt deeds contradict holy claims.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:23, Eli addresses his sons Hophni and Phinehas after learning of their greedy and immoral practices at the tabernacle. Eli asks, "Why do you do such things? For I hear of your evil dealings from all the people."

The context highlights three key elements:

  • Public Extent: The phrase "from all the people" shows that the news was widely known. Israelite pilgrims who came to worship at Shiloh witnessed the corruption and carried reports back to their home tribes.

  • Parental Knowledge: Eli was fully informed of their bad behavior. His mention of their "dealings" indicates that detailed accounts of their sins had reached him repeatedly.

  • Weak Confrontation: Although Eli verbally recognized their actions as evil, he failed to remove them from their priestly office. He acknowledged their corrupt "dealings," but he did not take decisive action to stop them, leading ultimately to divine judgment on his house.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The overlap between "word" and "deed" in dabar reinforces the principle that actions speak as clearly as verbal statements. In 1 Samuel 2, Eli's sons were tasked with serving at the sacred altar where God's words were honored. Yet their corrupt "dealings" sent a clear message of disrespect toward the sanctuary. The passage shows that recognizing bad conduct is not enough; godly leadership requires acting decisively to uphold righteousness.
