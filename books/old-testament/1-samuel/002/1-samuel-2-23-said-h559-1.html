SAID - (ʼâmar, Strong's H559)

said - ( amar, Strong’s [H0559] )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb amar is the foundational and most frequent word in the Old Testament for speaking, uttering, or declaring. It covers everything from spoken dialogue and outward commands to internal reflection and silent resolve. In 1 Samuel 2:23, Eli speaks to his wayward sons, confronting their grievous sins. The word introduces Eli’s parental rebuke, marking the moment an aging father addresses his sons’ corruption with sobering words.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Amar is used across the Hebrew Old Testament to express the spoken word, functioning as the primary vehicle for human communication, divine revelation, and personal thought. In ancient Hebrew culture, speech carried real weight and moral responsibility, serving to convey commands, express intentions, and expose character. When used in narrative literature, the verb often advances the plot by revealing the inner motives and direct words of historical actors.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb can refer to speaking, saying, commanding, answering, or thinking. When a person is the subject, it frequently introduces direct speech or a spoken report. When used in reflective contexts, it can describe what someone says within their own heart or mind. The word is broad and flexible, taking its specific color from the immediate context and the relationship between the speaker and the hearer.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture employs amar in various settings, ranging from intimate conversations to formal royal decrees and divine pronouncements. It frequently bridges the gap between inner thought and outward expression, showing how human words reveal the moral condition of the heart. The word does not carry inherent theological or emotional force by itself; rather, the weight of the statement depends entirely on who is speaking, to whom they are speaking, and the nature of the message delivered.

4. Theological Implications

The frequent use of amar throughout the Old Testament highlights the importance of divine and human speech. God creates, guides, and judges through His spoken word, while human speech exposes righteousness or wickedness. In narrative passages involving correction or judgment, spoken words often serve as a moral turning point, confronting sin and calling individuals back to obedience before judgment falls.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:23, Eli addresses Hophni and Phinehas regarding their corrupt and profane actions at the tabernacle. Eli’s words represent an overdue parental and priestly intervention. The narrative uses this verb to set the scene for his direct confrontation, highlighting the tragic failure of Eli’s earlier passivity and the solemnity of the warning he now delivers to his sons.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 27:35 — "And he said, 'Your brother came with deceit and has taken away your blessing.'"

  • Psalms 27:8 — "When You said, 'Seek My face,' my heart said to You, 'Your face, Lord, I will seek.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In narrative texts, the transition from internal thought to spoken declaration is often marked by this verb, inviting the reader to observe how private intentions translate into public action. In Eli’s case, his spoken rebuke underscores the tragic reality of authority that spoke too late and too weakly against unbridled sin.
