INTERCEDE - (pâlal, Strong's H6419)

Summary:

Palal is the primary Hebrew verb for praying, appealing, or making intercession before God. In 1 Samuel 2:25, it refers to making an entreaty to God on behalf of someone who has committed an offense. In this passage, Eli uses the word to highlight the grave spiritual situation of his sons: when people sin directly against the LORD in His house, finding an acceptable mediator to intercede for them becomes uniquely difficult.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The historical setting of 1 Samuel 2 involves a time of deep spiritual decline in Israel. Eli served as high priest at Shiloh, but his sons, Hophni and Phinehas, abused their priestly office. They treated the sacrifices of the LORD with contempt and engaged in blatant immorality at the tabernacle.

Eli confronted his sons with a solemn warning in 1 Samuel 2:25, contrasting offenses between human beings with offenses committed directly against the LORD. When one person wrongs another, civil or judicial arbitration is possible. But when leaders openly corrupt God's worship and sin against the LORD Himself, Eli asks who can step forward to intercede for them.

Depending on the context, palal can refer to:

  • Making intercession on behalf of another person or group.

  • Entreating God for relief, mercy, or guidance.

In 1 Samuel 2:25, the word means to intercede or plead before God on behalf of an offender. It describes a mediator presenting a prayer of entreaty so that divine wrath might be turned away or mercy granted.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, palal is the standard word for prayer. It describes individuals turning their hearts toward God in moments of earnest need. The word appears in a wide variety of settings, including personal laments, national prayers of repentance, royal petitions, and prophetic intercessions.

In intercessory contexts, the verb highlights the role of a mediator. A prophet, priest, or leader stands before God on behalf of those who are weak, suffering, or guilty. The act of intercession assumes that God hears the entreaties of His faithful servants and that prayer is a real means through which He works.

4. Theological Implications

The use of palal in 1 Samuel 2:25 brings out crucial truths about divine holiness, sin, and mediation:

  • The Severity of Sin Against God: Eli distinguishes between human-to-human conflicts and direct high-handed rebellion against the Holy God. While human society provides mechanisms for resolving disputes between neighbors, sinning directly against God's holy name and appointed worship creates a far deeper spiritual crisis.

  • The Necessity of Mediation: Intercession requires a recognized mediator who can approach God with acceptance. If the priests themselves—the very ones appointed to offer sacrifices and intercede for the people—are despising God's altar, they destroy the earthly means of intercession available to them.

  • The Need for a Perfect Intercessor: Eli's question ("who will intercede for him?") reveals the limitation of sinful human priests. A flawed mediator cannot force God to overlook deliberate contempt for His holiness. This tension in the Old Testament priesthood points canonically toward the necessity of a truly righteous advocate who can effectively intercede for sinners before God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:25, Eli's question is rhetorical and weighty. He is not teaching that God refuses to forgive true penitents, but rather warning his sons about the danger of their ongoing, defiant conduct. The verb form is masculine singular and imperfect, presenting the act as a prospective appeal that someone might make on their behalf.

The tragedy of Hophni and Phinehas is that they were abusing the sanctuary—the very place designed for atonement and intercession. By treating God's sacrifices with contempt, they cut themselves off from the mercy offered through those sacrifices. Eli warns them that if they corrupt the sanctuary itself, no human intercessor remains to plead their case. The narrative immediately notes that they refused to listen to their father's voice, highlighting the hardness of their hearts.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 20:7 — God tells Abimelech that Abraham is a prophet who will "pray" for him so that he may live, showing the power of prophetic intercession.

  • 1 Samuel 1:10 — In her deep anguish, Hannah "prayed" to the LORD for a child, demonstrating earnest personal entreaty at the tabernacle.

  • 1 Samuel 12:23 — Samuel assures the nation that far be it from him to sin against the LORD by ceasing to "pray" for them, establishing intercession as a core duty of his ministry.

  • 2 Chronicles 7:14 — God promises that if His people humble themselves and "pray" and seek His face, He will hear from heaven and heal their land.

  • Nehemiah 2:4 — Before giving his request to King Artaxerxes, Nehemiah briefly "prayed" to the God of heaven, seeking divine favor and direction.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The book of 1 Samuel places significant emphasis on the theme of prayer and intercession. The entire narrative opens with Hannah's prayer (palal) at Shiloh, which God answers by giving her Samuel. Samuel's very name is tied to asking God, and his life is marked by faithful intercession for Israel.

Against this backdrop, the failure of Eli's sons stands out in sharp relief. While Hannah prays in humble faith and Samuel grows up as a man of prayer, Hophni and Phinehas treat God's house with contempt. The contrast reinforces a central theme of 1 Samuel: God honors those who honor Him, but those who despise Him will be lightly esteemed.
