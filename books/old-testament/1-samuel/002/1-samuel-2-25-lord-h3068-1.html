LORD - (YHWH, Strong's H3068)

Summary:

The word refers to the personal divine name of the God of Israel, traditionally rendered as "LORD" in English Bibles. In 1 Samuel 2:25, the name identifies the holy and supreme God who brings judicial judgment upon Eli's unrepentant sons for treating His sanctuary and sacrifices with contempt.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In 1 Samuel 2, the priesthood at Shiloh had fallen into grave corruption under Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas. Instead of serving God and guiding the people in holiness, they abused their priestly power, treated the sacred sacrifices with contempt, and engaged in immoral conduct at the tabernacle. Eli warned his sons of the spiritual danger of their actions, pointing out that while human disputes can be mediated, sin committed directly against the Lord invites divine reckoning. The sons refused to listen to their father's rebuke. The text explains their hardness of heart by noting that the Lord had determined to execute judgment upon them for their persistent rebellion.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word is the personal proper name of the God of Israel, commonly known as the Tetragrammaton because of its four Hebrew consonants. In ancient Hebrew tradition, the name is closely associated with the verb meaning "to be" or "to exist," expressing God's self-existence and His identity as the living God who reveals Himself to His people. Unlike general titles for God that can also refer to earthly rulers or false idols, this proper name exclusively identifies the one true God who made Himself known to Abraham, Moses, and the nation of Israel. In English translations, it is traditionally printed in small capital letters as "LORD" to distinguish it from the general master or ruler title.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, the name distinguishes the living God from the powerless idols of the surrounding nations. It identifies God in His personal relationship with Israel, His righteous standard, and His divine rule over creation. When the word appears in narrative accounts of judgment, it emphasizes that divine punishment is not a chance event or an impersonal fate. Instead, judgment comes directly from the personal exercise of God's holy justice against willful transgression.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of the divine name in warnings and judgments shows that God holds human leaders accountable, especially those who serve in His sanctuary. God's holiness demands that those who minister in His name treat His worship with reverence. When Eli's sons repeatedly desecrated the holy offerings and ignored righteous warnings, they proved their defiance. The resolve of the Lord to judge them reveals that persistent, high-handed sin eventually brings divine judicial abandonment, where God leaves unrepentant sinners to the consequences of their rebellion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:25, Eli draws a clear contrast between earthly misdeeds and direct offenses against the Lord. Human judges can intervene when people wrong one another, but when priests knowingly desecrate the altar of God, they turn directly against their only possible defender. The statement that they did not listen "because the LORD desired to kill them" shows that their refusal to repent was the evidence of God's incoming judgment. After long-standing, unrepentant wickedness, the Lord determined to remove them from office and end their lives. The name Lord emphasizes that the supreme authority executing this judgment is the very God whose holy presence and worship they had despised.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:4 — "This is the history of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens."

  • Joshua 14:12 — "Now therefore, give me this mountain of which the LORD spoke in that day..."

  • Psalms 25:1 — "To You, O LORD, I lift up my soul."

  • Proverbs 16:6 — "In mercy and truth atonement is provided for iniquity; and by the fear of the LORD one departs from evil."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The English rendering of this proper name in small capital letters ("LORD") follows an ancient translation practice that reflects

reverent handling of the sacred divine name. In the book of 1 Samuel, the spiritual failure of Eli's household serves as a sharp contrast to the young boy Samuel, who ministered before the Lord and grew in favor with God and men. This contrast illustrates that true leadership before God depends on sincere reverence for His holy name.
