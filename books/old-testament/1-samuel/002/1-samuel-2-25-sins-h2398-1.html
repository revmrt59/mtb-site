SINS - (châṭâʼ, Strong's H2398)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb chata' basic definition means to miss a target, wander from a designated path, or fail to meet an established standard. In moral and biblical contexts, it refers to violating God's law or committing a fault against a fellow human being. In 1 Samuel 2:25, the word describes an offense committed by one person against another, contrasting human grievances that can be mediated with direct rebellion against God where no human mediator can step in to standardly remove the liability.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root of chata' carries the primary concrete picture of missing a mark or straying off the right road. In ancient Israelite life, the word could describe an archer missing a target or a traveler taking a wrong turn off a path.

When applied to human relationships and religion, the concrete idea of missing the mark becomes a moral and spiritual concept. God's moral law and revealed will define the correct path for human behavior. To commit chata' is to turn aside from that standard, whether by active transgression, neglect, or wrongdoing against God or neighbor.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to committing a moral failure, committing a crime or offense against a person, or violating God's explicit commands. Depending on who is offended and the nature of the action, chata' can describe an intentional act of rebellion, an unintentional mistake, or a civil wrong between two people.

When a person acts wrongfully toward another human being, chata' describes an offense that disturbs human harmony and demands resolution or compensation. When a person acts against God, the word describes an offense against the ultimate Judge and Sovereign of the universe.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament historical books, wisdom literature, and prophetic writings, chata' appears in several distinct settings:

  • Human Interpersonal Offenses: It describes acts of wronging another person, such as breaking an agreement, committing a crime, or treating a neighbor unfairly.

  • Covenantal Disobedience: It refers to violating God's statutes, worshipping idols, or breaking covenant promises made to the Lord.

  • Ceremonial Cleansing: In certain specialized grammatical forms (primarily in priestly instruction), the word carries a secondary ceremonial meaning, referring to purifying a person, sanctuary item, or vessel from ritual defilement.

In moral contexts, the word emphasizes both the action itself and the real guilt produced by that action.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical concept of chata' shows that sin is never a neutral human error or a harmless weakness. It is a failure to align with God's absolute moral character.

First, sin creates liability. Because God is just, violating His standards brings judgment and requires mediation or sacrifice. Second, Scripture distinguishes between offenses against human beings and direct offenses against God. While wronging a human neighbor is serious, every offense against a person is also ultimately an offense against God, who established moral order and created human beings in His image.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:25, Eli speaks to his sons, Hophni and Phinehas, who were priests acting with gross corruption at the tabernacle in Shiloh. They were stealing sacrificial portions and committing sexual immorality, treating God's altar with contempt.

Eli warns them: "If one man sins against another, God will judge him. But if a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?" Here, the word sins in its first appearance in the verse highlights civil or interpersonal wrong. When a person wrongs another human being, civil judges, appointed officials, or priests can step in to hear the case, assess restitution, and facilitate reconciliation.

However, when Eli's sons directly desecrated God's sanctuary and defied His holy worship, they committed a direct, high-handed offense against the Almighty. Eli points out the absolute peril of their position: if the primary line of offense is directly against God Himself, human arbitration reaches its boundary. There is no human advocate capable of overriding divine judgment when a person willfully despises God's own sacrificial system.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 20:6 — "And God said to him in a dream, 'Yes, I know that you did this in the integrity of your heart. For I also withheld you from sinning against Me; therefore I did not let you touch her.'"

  • 1 Samuel 12:23 — "Moreover, as for me, far be it from me that I should sin against the LORD in ceasing to pray for you; but I will teach you the good and the right way."

  • Job 35:6 — "If you sin, what do you accomplish against Him? Or, if your transgressions are multiplied, what do you do to Him?"

  • Proverbs 8:36 — "But he who sins against me wrongs his own soul; all those who hate me love death."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Eli’s warning in 1 Samuel 2:25 exposes a terrifying reality for his sons, but it also points forward canonically to the necessity of a mediator superior to any earthly priest. Eli asked, "Who will intercede for him?" under the Old Covenant levitical system, human priests could not intercede for those who defiantly rejected the sacrificial system itself.

This question finds its eventual resolution in the person and work of Jesus Christ. Where human mediation fails because of human weakness and direct rebellion against God, Jesus serves as both the perfect High Priest and the sinless sacrifice, making intercession for those who turn to Him in genuine repentance.
