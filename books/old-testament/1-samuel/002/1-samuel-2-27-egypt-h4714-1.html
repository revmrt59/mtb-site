EGYPT - (Mitsrayim, Strong's H4714)

Egypt - (Mitsrayim, Strong’s [H4714])

Summary:

The Hebrew proper name translated as Egypt designates both the geographical nation of the Nile Valley and its historical identity as a place of bondage and redemption. In 1 Samuel 2:27, the term anchors a divine reminder of Israel’s foundational deliverance from the house of slavery, establishing the authority of God's covenant grace over the house of Eli.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew name Mitsrayim identifies the land of Egypt and its people, appearing frequently throughout the Old Testament. Etymologically, the name refers to the double strait or twin boundaries of the land, reflecting the natural divisions of Upper and Lower Egypt along the Nile River. In biblical history, Egypt represents a powerful regional empire, a place of refuge during famine, and above all, the crucible of oppression from which God rescued His people. When the man of God comes to Eli in 1 Samuel, the mention of Egypt immediately recalls the historic moment when Yahweh intervened to bring the Israelites out of physical servitude and into covenant relationship with Himself.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Mitsrayim is a proper noun used primarily to name the nation of Egypt, its rulers, and its inhabitants. It describes a specific geographic territory centered on the Nile River delta and valley, bounded by deserts and seas. In Scripture, the name carries deep historical weight, frequently associated with both the hardships of slavery and the displays of divine power during the Exodus. While the term fundamentally identifies a foreign nation, its primary biblical significance is theological, marking the boundary between slavery under human empire and freedom under divine rule.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses Egypt in multiple ways depending on the historical context. It functions literally as a destination for travelers, a political ally sought by kings, and a geopolitical adversary to Israel. Spiritually and theologically, however, Egypt serves as the primary biblical archetype of bondage, idolatry, and human might opposed to God. Yet Scripture also records promises concerning Egypt's eventual inclusion in the worship of Yahweh, showing that even this historic oppressor remains subject to the sovereign grace and ultimate redemption of God.

4. Theological Implications

The mention of Egypt in prophetic and historical texts constantly reminds Israel of the source of their salvation. God identifies Himself as the One who brought His people out of Egypt, grounding His ongoing moral and covenant authority in His past redemptive acts. This history teaches that deliverance belongs entirely to God, not to human strength. Furthermore, the memory of Egypt guards against spiritual complacency, warning God's people never to return to the bondage from which they were freed.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:27, the man of God confronts Eli with a sharp divine inquiry concerning his ancestral house. The reference to Egypt serves as an anchor for divine memory and authority. God reminds Eli's family that He revealed Himself to their forefathers when they were servants in Pharaoh's house in Egypt. By recalling this specific history, the text contrasts God's gracious choice of Aaron's household with the wicked abuse of priestly privilege currently practiced by Eli's sons. The memory of Egypt establishes that the priesthood exists entirely by divine grace and deliverance, making the current corruption of the sanctuary an offense against the God who redeemed them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 1:18 — The king of Egypt commands the Hebrew midwives, illustrating the hostile imperial power that oppressed Israel before their deliverance.

  • Deuteronomy 10:19 — Moses reminds Israel to love the stranger, recalling that they themselves were strangers in the land of Egypt, linking historical memory to present ethical responsibility.

  • 1 Samuel 12:8 — Samuel recounts how the Lord brought their fathers out of Egypt, using the same historical landmark to call the nation to faithfulness during the transition to the monarchy.

  • Nehemiah 9:9 — The Levites praise God in prayer, remembering the affliction of their fathers in Egypt and God's hearing of their cry by the Red Sea.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The biblical narrative consistently uses the Exodus from Egypt as the defining baseline for understanding God's relationship with His people. Whenever prophets or messengers invoke Egypt, they are not merely citing a date in history; they are appealing to the foundational event that proved God's power to save and His right to command obedience. In 1 Samuel, this appeal highlights the tragic irony that those rescued from the house of bondage are now fostering corruption within the very house of God.
