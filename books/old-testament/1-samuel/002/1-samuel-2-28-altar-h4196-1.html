ALTAR - (mizbêach, Strong's H4196)

mizbeach - ( miz-bay'-akh, Strong’s [H4196] )

Summary:

The Hebrew term refers to a place of sacrifice or slaughter, specifically an altar where offerings are presented to God. In 1 Samuel 2:28, the word describes the designated place of worship where the priesthood offered sacrifices on behalf of Israel, highlighting the sacred privileges and solemn responsibilities entrusted to the house of Eli.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word comes from a root meaning to slaughter or sacrifice. In ancient Israelite culture, the altar was the central physical focal point for interaction between a holy God and a sinful people. It was the place where blood was shed and offerings were burned, bridging the immense gap between humanity and the Divine. In the early days of Israel’s monarchy, the tabernacle and its furnishings—including the altar—remained the institutional heart of the nation’s religious life, representing God's dwelling place among His people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word refers to a raised structure or place built for offering sacrifices, burnt offerings, and incense to a deity. When used in the Old Testament, it primarily designates the altar of burnt offering or the altar of incense. It is a place of meeting, where gifts are rendered, atonement is sought, and worship is visibly enacted. The structure itself derives its significance entirely from the God who commanded it and the sacrifices permitted upon it.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses the term to mark places of encounter, covenant renewal, and solemn worship. Altars could be modest structures of earth or unhewn stone built in the patriarchal era, or elaborate bronze-clad structures later incorporated into the tabernacle and temple service. The word carries the heavy weight of substitutionary life: something must die upon the altar so that the worshipper may draw near. It signifies both the grace of divine provision—providing a way for sinful people to approach—and the strict demand for holiness.

4. Theological Implications

The altar stands as a perpetual reminder of human sin and divine mercy. Because humanity cannot approach a holy God on its own terms, the altar provides the divinely appointed channel for atonement. It points forward to the ultimate sacrifice, where Christ offered Himself once for all. In the Old Testament system, approaching the altar was a high and terrifying privilege restricted to those whom God had consecrated, emphasizing that approaching God requires cleansing and authorized mediation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:28, a man of God confronts Eli, reminding him of the high priestly privileges granted to his family from among all the tribes of Israel. Specifically, God chose Eli's lineage to go up to His altar, to burn incense, and to wear an ephod before Him. The mention of the altar underscores the immense dignity and sacred trust once given to Eli’s house. Yet, the surrounding context turns this privilege into a sharp indictment, as Eli's sons have abused their holy office at that very altar, treating the offerings of God with contempt and forsaking the responsibilities attached to the sanctuary.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 8:20 — Noah built an altar to the LORD, taking of every clean animal and every clean bird, and offered burnt offerings on the altar, marking a fresh start for humanity under divine mercy after the flood.

  • Exodus 30:1 — God instructed Moses to make an altar to burn incense upon, establishing the specific provision for daily prayer and communion within the holy place.

  • Joshua 8:31 — Joshua built an altar to the LORD God of Israel on Mount Ebal, as Moses commanded, publicly anchoring the newly entered land in covenant obedience and worship.

  • 1 Kings 18:30 — Elijah repaired the broken altar of the LORD on Mount Carmel, calling the wandering nation back to true worship and undivided loyalty before the consuming fire of God fell.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The physical altar was never meant to be an end in itself; its entire purpose directed the worshipper's gaze toward the God who commanded the sacrifice. When the priesthood grew corrupt, as seen in the days of Eli, the rituals performed at the altar became empty or rebellious rather than honoring. The severe judgment announced against Eli’s family demonstrates that proximity to holy things like the altar does not insulate anyone from the consequences of persistent sin.
