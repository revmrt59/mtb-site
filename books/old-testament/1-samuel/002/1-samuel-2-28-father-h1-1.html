FATHER - (ʼâb, Strong's H1)

AB — ( ab, Strong’s [H0001] )

Summary:

The Hebrew word for "father" carries a range of basic meanings, referring primarily to a biological male parent, but extending to grandfathers, ancestors, and figures of authority, guidance, or protection. In 1 Samuel 2:28, the term is used in the context of divine election and priestly heritage, pointing back to the ancestral lineage of the Levitical priesthood. Here, the word underscores the historical origin and spiritual responsibility handed down through the generations of Israel's priestly service, reminding Eli of his family's privileged place within the covenant.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew term for father is one of the foundational familial words in Scripture. Beyond the immediate biological parent, ancient Hebrew society used the word broadly to describe any male ancestor, a clan head, or a respected leader who exercised authority, guidance, or mentorship. In the setting of 1 Samuel, lineage and ancestral heritage carried profound legal, social, and spiritual weight. Families were tied not only by bloodlines, but by shared stewardship of duties, inheritances, and covenant responsibilities before God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to a biological father who begets children. It also expands easily to mean a grandfather, a remote ancestor, or the founder of a tribe or clan. Beyond the family unit, the term describes a person who acts in a protective, instructional, or authoritative role, such as a king's counselor, a prophet, or a priest providing spiritual guidance. In this passage, the word identifies the ancestral forebears from whom the Levitical priestly line descended.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses the term across many layers of meaning. It describes natural parent-child relationships, traces tribal and national roots through generations of ancestors, and honors figures of spiritual or institutional authority. When used of ancestors, the word frequently links present generations to past divine promises, commands, or failures. The actions and legacy of the fathers often cast a long shadow over their descendants, shaping how later generations walk in covenant faithfulness or reap the consequences of rebellion.

4. Theological Implications

The use of the term in connection with the priesthood highlights the weight of spiritual inheritance. God deals with families and generations, holding leaders accountable for the stewardship entrusted to their ancestral line. While biological descent grants unique historical privileges and responsibilities, it does not permanently secure God's favor if those privileges are abused. The passage demonstrates that sacred offices received from the fathers carry strict moral and spiritual demands that each generation must personally uphold.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:28, a man of God delivers a severe rebuke to Eli, reminding him that the Lord chose his family out of all the tribes of Israel to serve at the altar, burn incense, and wear the ephod. By using the word in this setting, the text recalls the historical grace shown to Eli’s ancestors when God established their priestly service. The reminder highlights the great privilege Eli's house received from their forebears, making their current corruption and abuse of the sacrifices all the more inexcusable before God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:24 — "Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and they shall become one flesh."

  • Numbers 27:7 — "The daughters of Zelophehad speak what is right; you shall surely give them a possession of an inheritance among their father’s brothers, and cause the inheritance of their father to pass to them."

  • Jeremiah 35:16 — "Because the sons of Jonadab the son of Rechab have performed the commandment of their father, which he commanded them, but this people has not obeyed Me..."

  • Psalms 78:12 — "Marvelous things He did in the sight of their fathers, in the land of Egypt, in the field of Zoan."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The broader biblical use of the term reveals that heritage alone cannot sustain a spiritual calling. Time and again, Scripture records instances where descendants failed to walk in the righteous paths of their fathers, or conversely, where individuals broke away from the ungodly patterns of their ancestors to follow the Lord. The generational continuity implied by the word serves both as an anchor of historical identity and a sobering call to personal accountability.
