OFFER - (ʻâlâh, Strong's H5927)

`alah ( alah , H5927 )`

Summary:

The Hebrew verb alah fundamentally means to go up, ascend, or climb. In 1 Samuel 2:28, where it appears in an infinitive construct form translated as "offer," it describes the priestly act of causing sacrifices or offerings to ascend on the altar before God.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root behind this word is one of the most common verbs of motion in biblical Hebrew, appearing across many Old Testament books to describe literal upward movement, such as climbing a mountain, going up to Jerusalem, or watching smoke rise toward the sky. In the setting of Israel's sanctuary worship, the physical action of ascending took on deep ritual significance. Priests stood at the tabernacle or temple, taking the offerings provided by the people and lifting them upward onto the altar where the fire consumed them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, the word means to go up or ascend. When used in connection with sacrifices, it describes the movement of lifting something upward. The physical trajectory is always from a lower place to a higher place. In sacrificial contexts, the term highlights the upward movement of the offering from the ground or the hands of the priest toward the altar, or the rising of the smoke toward heaven as an acceptable pleasing aroma to God.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses this verb in a wide range of settings. It describes people traveling upward to high elevations, armies marching up to battle, smoke rising from a burning city, or vapor ascending from the earth. When applied to religious service, the verb often connects directly to the offerings placed upon the altar, signifying that the sacrifice is lifted up to God's dwelling place. The focus remains on the literal or ceremonial direction of movement—upward.

4. Theological Implications

The upward movement described by this word pictures the bridge between earth and heaven in Israel's worship. When sacrifices were lifted up, the action symbolized devotion, atonement, and the submission of human life to God. While the verb itself simply denotes going up, its application within the sacrificial system underscores that worship requires approaching God on His terms, moving from human need toward divine holiness.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:28, God is confronting Eli through a prophet regarding the corruption of Eli's sons. The Lord reminds Eli that out of all the tribes of Israel, He chose Eli’s family to be priests, to wear the ephod, and to offer sacrifices upon His altar. Here, the infinitive describes the core duty entrusted to the priesthood. Instead of honoring this sacred responsibility of lifting offerings up to God, Eli's sons treated the offerings with contempt, robbing God and the people. The word highlights the exact holy privilege that the corrupt priests were abusing.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 8:2: "When you light the lamps, the seven lamps shall face toward the face of the lampstand." (Using the rendering "arrange" in the NKJV text where the underlying Hebrew verb directs the setup of the lamps).

  • 1 Samuel 9:14: "So they went up to the city." (Describing the literal ascent of Saul and the servant as they climbed toward the town).

  • 2 Samuel 2:2: "So David went up there." (Recording David's physical journey upward to Hebron).

  • Psalms 68:18: "You have ascended on high." (Using the translation "ascended" to portray God's triumphant rising in victory).

7. Other Relevant Insights

The physical act of going up is often paired in Scripture with spiritual approaches to God. Whenever people came to worship at the central sanctuary, they physically went up because the tabernacle or temple was situated on elevated ground or viewed as the dwelling place of the Most High. The language of ascent thus frames worship as a deliberate movement away from ordinary life toward the presence of God.
