PRIEST - (kôhên, Strong's H3548)

Summary:

The word kohen refers to a sacred minister or priest who represents people before God, offers sacrifices, and performs holy service at the sanctuary. In 1 Samuel 2:28, the word highlights the high privilege and solemn responsibility given to Aaron and his descendants to serve at God's altar, wear the sacred ephod, and minister in the divine presence—a grace that Eli and his sons treated with disregard.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel, a priest (kohen) served as an ordained mediator between God and the nation. While the entire house of Israel was called to be a kingdom of priests, God set apart Aaron and his sons from the tribe of Levi to hold the formal office of priesthood.

The setting of 1 Samuel 2 takes place during the transition from the period of the judges to the establishment of the monarchy. The tabernacle was stationed at Shiloh, where Eli served as high priest alongside his sons, Hophni and Phinehas. Despite their sacred position, Eli's sons abused their authority by stealing the best portions of the sacrifices and treating God's altar with contempt. In response, God sent a prophet, identified as a "man of God," to announce divine judgment upon Eli’s household.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word kohen denotes a minister or priest who performs official religious duties in a sacred space. A priest presented offerings, offered incense, sought divine guidance, and instructed the people in God's laws.

In Israelite life, a kohen was not self-appointed; the office required divine calling and specific lineage through Aaron. In 1 Samuel 2:28, the word appears in the singular form as a common noun, pointing to the collective line or representative office established in Aaron when God chose his family out of all Israel's tribes to serve at the altar.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses kohen primarily for the Levitical priests who ministered at the tabernacle and temple. However, the term is broad enough in Old Testament usage to describe other types of religious leaders:

  • Priests of the True God: Figures like Melchizedek, Aaron, Phinehas, and Zadok who served the LORD according to His word.

  • Foreign or Pagan Priests: Sacred ministers of other nations, such as Potiphera in Egypt, the priests of Dagon in Philistia, or the worship leaders of Baal.

  • Chief Royal Ministers: On a few occasions in early Israelite history, the title was applied to close advisors or chief ministers in the king's court.

When applied to Israel’s sacred order, the primary role of the kohen centered on maintaining holy worship, making atonement for sins through sacrifices, and keeping the sanctuary pure for God’s presence.

4. Theological Implications

The office of the priest emphasizes three vital biblical truths:

  • Divine Initiative: No man made himself a priest. God explicitly chose Aaron’s family to draw near to Him. Human access to God is established on God's terms, not human ambition.

  • Sacred Mediation: Sinful human beings cannot approach a holy God without an appointed mediator and an acceptable sacrifice. The priest stood in the gap to keep the fellowship between God and His people unbroken.

  • Accountability for Leadership: Privileged access to God brings intense accountability. To hold the title of kohen while desecrating God's altar brought swift divine judgment, as Eli's house discovered.

These Old Testament principles lay the groundwork for understanding Jesus Christ, who fulfills and surpasses the Aaron-led priesthood as the sinless High Priest who offers Himself once for all to bring His people to God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:28, God speaks directly to Eli through His messenger, reminding him of the immense honor given to his family line: "Did I not choose him out of all the tribes of Israel to be My priest, to offer upon My altar, to burn incense, and to wear an ephod before Me?"

The context creates a stark contrast between divine generosity and human dishonor. God details three specific duties belonging to the priest:

1. Offering sacrifices upon the altar.

2. Burning fragrant incense in the sanctuary.

3. Wearing the ephod to represent the tribes before the LORD and seek His guidance.

In addition, God granted the priest a legitimate share of the sacrificial food for his family's survival. The word kohen here serves as a reminder of an unearned privilege. God gave Eli’s ancestors the honor of divine fellowship and ministry, making the greed and irreverence of Eli’s sons all the more offensive. They treated as common profit what God had given as a holy trust.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The failure of the priesthood in 1 Samuel 2 highlights a major turning point in Israel's history. Eli’s sons brought the office of kohen into disrepute, causing the people to abhor the offerings of the LORD.

However, God's purpose cannot be thwarted by human corruption. Later in the same chapter, God promises to replace the fallen house of Eli: "Then I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest who shall do according to what is in My heart and in My mind" (1 Samuel 2:35). While this prophecy found initial fulfillment in faithful leaders like Samuel and Zadok, its full reality is realized in Jesus, the ultimate faithful Priest who perfectly honors the Father and secures everlasting salvation for His people.
