HONOR - (kâbad, Strong's H3513)

Summary:

The word kabad fundamentally means to be heavy, weighty, or glorious, and when used relationally, it means to treat someone with high honor, respect, and priority. In 1 Samuel 2:29, God confronts the high priest Eli for giving greater weight and deference to his corrupt sons than to God Himself, putting family approval above the holiness of divine worship.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

During the period of the judges in Israel, Shiloh served as the center of worship where the tabernacle and the Ark of the Covenant were located. Eli served as high priest, and his sons, Hophni and Phinehas, served as priests under him. However, his sons treated the sacrifices of the LORD with utter contempt, taking choice portions of the offering by force and committing gross wickedness at the door of the tabernacle.

Though Eli knew of his sons' wicked actions, his reprimands were weak and ineffective. He allowed them to remain in office and continued to share in the meats brought by the worshippers. In response, God sent an unnamed prophet to deliver a severe message of judgment against Eli and his priestly house. In this message, God directly accuses Eli of honoring his sons above the LORD.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root meaning of kabad is to be heavy or weighty. From this physical sense of weight, the word develops several distinct biblical usages:

  • Physical weight or difficulty: describing something heavy, hard to bear, or burdensome.

  • Wealth and importance: describing someone who has great abundance or social influence.

  • Honor and glory: treating someone as heavy, significant, worthy of honor, deference, or supreme reverence.

When applied to God, kabad means recognizing His supreme weight and authority by giving Him the reverence, obedience, and worship due to His name. When applied to human relationships, it means giving respect, weight, or proper standing to parents, rulers, or distinguished individuals.

In 1 Samuel 2:29, the word describes Eli giving ultimate weight, deference, and priority to his sons' desires rather than to God's holy commands.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In ancient Hebrew thought, honor is connected to weightiness. To honor someone is to view them as heavy, serious, and worthy of respect. Conversely, to dishonor someone is to treat them as light, insignificant, or trivial.

Because God is the supreme Creator and Redeemer, He possesses absolute weightiness. Worship is the act of ascribing to God the full weight of His glory. When a person values human approval, personal comfort, or family loyalty more than obedience to God, they treat God as light and the human object as heavy.

Scripture uses kabad in both positive and negative settings. God demands that His people honor Him, their parents, and proper authority. However, when human honor is misplaced—giving people or earthly desires the weight that belongs exclusively to God—it becomes a form of idolatry.

4. Theological Implications

The account of Eli highlights key spiritual truths about divine honor and stewardship:

  • God demands supreme allegiance: No human relationship, even the bond between a parent and child, can take precedence over allegiance to God and His holy word.

  • Passive tolerance is active complicity: Eli did not personally commit the sanctuary abuses his sons committed, yet God held him responsible because he failed to restrain them and continued to benefit from their stolen meat.

  • The principle of divine reciprocity: In the immediate context of 1 Samuel 2:30, God declares, "Those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed." God honors those who treat Him as weighty, but those who disregard Him forfeit His blessing.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:29, God asks Eli a piercing question: "Why do you kick at My sacrifice and My offering... and honor your sons above Me, to make yourselves fat with the best of all the offerings of Israel My people?"

The word appears as a masculine singular form describing Eli's past and ongoing posture toward his sons. Eli chose family peace and soft indulgence over the hard duty of exercising priestly discipline. By allowing his sons to profane the altar and by eating the stolen portions himself, Eli actively chose to weigh his sons' desires higher than God's law.

This misuse of honor inverted the divine order. Instead of making God central and weighty, Eli made his sons central. The ultimate tragedy is that by seeking to preserve his sons' standing, Eli brought destruction upon his entire house.

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a deliberate literary and spiritual irony in the story of Eli regarding the word kabad.

While Eli refused to give spiritual weight (kabad) to God, he and his sons grew physically heavy by fattening themselves on the stolen sacrifical portions. Later, in 1 Samuel 4:18, when Eli receives the catastrophic news that the Ark of God was captured and his sons were killed in battle, he falls backward off his seat, breaks his neck, and dies, with the text noting that "he was an old man and heavy (kabad)."

The physical heaviness that brought about Eli's fatal fall stands as a vivid sign of his spiritual failure to give proper weight to the honor of God.
