KICK - (bâʻaṭ, Strong's H1163)

KICK - ( ba'at , H1163 )

Summary:

The word ba'at means to kick out, spurn, or treat with disdain, like an unruly farm animal kicking against its master or trampling its food. In 1 Samuel 2:29, God uses this vivid image to rebuke Eli and his sons for treating sacred sacrifices with arrogant contempt and self-serving disrespect.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient agricultural life, domestic animals such as oxen and cattle were expected to accept their yokes and yield to their master's guidance. An ox or donkey that turned unruly would kick out against its driver or trample the feeding trough. This physical action of kicking out became a vivid picture for stubborn rebellion and brazen insolence.

In the historical setting of 1 Samuel 2, Israel's priesthood under Eli had fallen into severe corruption. Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, used force to seize the best portions of sacrificial meat for themselves before God received His designated portion. Eli knew of their wickedness but failed to restrain them. God sent a messenger to confront Eli, using this word to describe how Eli and his sons treated the Lord's holy sanctuary.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew verb ba'at fundamentally refers to striking out with the foot or kicking. When used figuratively, the word means to spurn, trample upon, or treat with contemptuous disdain.

It describes an attitude of arrogant disregard that rejects what ought to be honored. Rather than receiving God's commands and sacred provisions with gratitude and reverence, a person who "kicks" strikes out against divine authority and despises holy things.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Scripture, ba'at carries a consistent tone of willful, ungrateful rebellion. It does not describe an accidental mistake or an act of ignorance, but deliberate insolence.

The term highlights two distinct aspects of human rebellion:

First, it conveys ungratefulness in the midst of prosperity. When people are well-fed and comfortable, they often grow proud and kick against the very God who provided for them.

Second, it conveys sacrilege. To kick at God's sacrifices means to treat holy offerings as common items to be grabbed, controlled, or discarded at human whim.

4. Theological Implications

The use of ba'at reveals how God views human arrogance toward His worship. Sacred worship is never neutral; to treat God's appointed offerings with selfish neglect is an act of direct defiance against Him.

This word also illustrates the danger of spiritual compromise among leaders. Eli did not directly abuse the sacrifices in the same violent manner as his sons, yet by honoring his sons above God and benefiting from their stolen fat portions, God held Eli accountable for kicking at the sacrifices as well. Tolerating sacrilege in God's house makes a leader complicit in the contempt shown toward God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:29, ba'at sits at the center of God's indictment against Eli's priestly household. The Lord asks, "Why do you kick at My sacrifice and My offering which I have commanded in My dwelling place...?" The verb appears in a masculine plural imperfect form, addressing the priestly family in their ongoing, established pattern of behavior.

The surrounding sentence highlights a sharp contrast. While God commanded sacred offerings to be brought to His sanctuary for holy worship, Eli's household treated those offerings as personal property to satisfy their own physical appetites. By taking the choicest parts of every offering, they made themselves fat while dishonoring God. The word shows that their greedy behavior was not merely a breach of ceremonial procedure, but a hostile, defiant kick against God's sovereign rule.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 32:15 — "But Jeshurun grew fat and kicked; you grew fat, you grew thick, you are obese! Then he forsook God who made him, and scornfully esteemed the Rock of his salvation."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The imagery of an animal kicking against its master appears elsewhere in biblical thought to describe human resistance to God's direction. In the New Testament, Jesus uses a similar agricultural metaphor when He tells Saul of Tarsus, "It is hard for you to kick against the goads" (Acts 26:14). Both images picture the folly and self-destruction of human rebellion against divine authority.

Furthermore, the rebuke in 1 Samuel 2:29 establishes that God values heartfelt reverence above formal priestly lineage. Even those chosen for high spiritual privileges forfeit God's favor when they despise His holy altar.
