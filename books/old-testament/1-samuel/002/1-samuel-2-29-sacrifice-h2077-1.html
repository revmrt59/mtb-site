SACRIFICE - (zebach, Strong's H2077)

Summary:

Zevach refers to an animal sacrifice slaughtered as a sacred gift to God, often associated with a peace offering or shared fellowship meal. In 1 Samuel 2:29, God uses this word to rebuke Eli and his sons for despising and misusing the sacred offerings brought by the people of Israel to the sanctuary at Shiloh.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word zevach originates in the sacrificial system that structured ancient Israel's worship. God established sacrifices to provide a way for His people to approach Him, seek forgiveness, offer thanksgiving, and celebrate communion with Him. Unlike offerings that were consumed entirely by fire on the altar, a zevach frequently involved a shared sacred meal. After the required parts—such as the fat—were burned for God on the altar and designated portions were given to the officiating priest, the worshiper and their family ate the remaining meat in a holy setting.

In 1 Samuel 2, the sanctuary at Shiloh served as the central place of Israelite worship before the construction of the temple in Jerusalem. Eli served as high priest, and his sons, Hophni and Phinehas, served as priests under him. Instead of honoring God and serving the worshippers, Eli's sons treated the altar gifts with contempt. They demanded raw meat before the fat was offered to God and took choice portions by force. When Eli failed to restrain his sons, God sent a prophet to deliver a divine rebuke, confronting Eli for despising the sanctuary sacrifices.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Zevach identifies a slaughtered animal offered in divine worship. The word can refer to individual peace offerings, general animal sacrifices, or covenant-affirming meals.

When a person brought a zevach to the tabernacle, the act involved several distinct elements:

  • The slaughter of an approved animal.

  • The presentation of the blood and fat to God at the altar.

  • The distribution of priest portions according to God's law.

  • The eating of the remaining meat in joyful fellowship before the LORD.

Because zevach emphasizes the communion meal and the sharing of the worship gift, it highlights both the holiness of God's altar and the joy of fellowship among His people. In 1 Samuel 2:29, the word describes the total system of animal offerings brought by Israel to God's tabernacle.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, zevach appears in several distinct contexts of worship and community life:

  • Covenant Meals: Early in Israel's history, a zevach marked solemn covenants and family solemnities, where eating together pledged mutual peace under God's witness.

  • Peace and Thanksgiving Offerings: In the Mosaic law, the word frequently describes voluntary peace offerings brought to express gratitude, fulfill a vow, or enjoy fellowship in God's presence.

  • National Dedications: Israel offered widespread sacrifices during major spiritual milestones, such as temple dedications or national renewals.

  • Warnings Against Empty Ritual: The Old Testament repeatedly warns that a zevach offered without obedience, humility, or sincerity is unacceptable to God. External ritual cannot substitute for personal righteousness.

4. Theological Implications

The use of zevach in Scripture carries deep theological truths about worship, holiness, and leadership:

  • Sacrifices Belong to God: In 1 Samuel 2:29, God calls the offering "My sacrifice." Worshippers do not give to priests; they give to God. Priests are merely stewards of what belongs to the LORD. Misappropriating holy things is theft from God Himself.

  • Heart Attitude Matters Most: Sacrifices were never meant to function as mechanical rituals. God values genuine faith and obedience above animal offerings.

  • The Gravity of Spiritual Leadership: Spiritual leaders hold a solemn duty to protect the purity of worship. Using sacred ministry positions for personal gain, luxury, or family favoritism brings severe divine judgment.

  • Anticipation of Christ: The entire sacrificial system pointed forward to the complete, once-for-all sacrifice of Jesus Christ, who offered Himself to reconcile sinners to God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:29, God asks Eli: "Why do you kick at My sacrifice and My offering which I have commanded in My dwelling place, and honor your sons above Me, to make yourselves fat with the best of all the offerings of Israel My people?"

The phrasing "kick at" pictures an animal stubbornly trampling on something valuable. By allowing his sons to seize choice portions of the zevach before the fat was offered on the altar, Eli treated God's sacred instructions as worthless. The priests were enriching themselves off the sacrifices brought by humble worshippers.

The word zevach in this verse emphasizes that what Hophni and Phinehas were corrupting was not merely institutional routine, but the very fellowship meal that brought God's people into communion with Him. Eli's primary sin was passive indulgence—he loved and honored his sons more than he honored God, permitting them to dishonor the LORD's altar.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The failure of Eli's house stands as a perpetual warning against taking sacred responsibilities lightly. When ministers treat holy things as common, or view ministry as a tool for personal profit, comfort, or family advancement, they corrupt the worship of God's people.

God's priority remains unchanged across redemptive history: He demands priority in the human heart. True worship requires honoring God on His terms, reverencing His holiness, and bringing Him sincere devotion rather than hollow external activity.
