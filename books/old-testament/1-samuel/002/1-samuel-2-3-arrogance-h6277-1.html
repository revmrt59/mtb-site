ARROGANCE - (ʻâthâq, Strong's H6277)

Summary:

The word ataq refers to insolent, arrogant, or brazen speech that expresses defiance and self-exaltation. In 1 Samuel 2:3, Hannah uses ataq in her song of praise to warn against haughty words, reminding her listeners that the Lord knows all things and weighs human actions.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In 1 Samuel 2, Hannah stands at the tabernacle in Shiloh to present her young son Samuel to the Lord. After years of painful barrenness and enduring cruel taunts from her rival Peninnah, God answered Hannah's desperate prayer. Rather than boasting in her victory, Hannah presents a song of worship celebrating God's power and holiness.

Hannah's song sets the thematic foundation for the entire book of 1 Samuel. It contrasts those who boast in human strength with the sovereign God who lowers the proud and exalts the humble. In verse 3, she confronts human pride directly, commanding her hearers to cease their arrogant boasting because God alone is the ultimate judge of human deeds.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Ataq is a noun that refers to insolent, defiant, or arrogant speech. It describes bold, brazen words spoken with self-confidence, contempt, or rebellion toward God and others. The term conveys speech that oversteps proper boundaries, expressing a haughty spirit through shameless words.

In Scripture, ataq does not describe mere confidence or strong speech; it specifically identifies proud, disrespectful speech that ignores God's authority or insults His people.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses ataq primarily in poetic passages and prayers to describe the boastful words of the arrogant. It is frequently associated with the mouth, lips, or speech, highlighting how heart-level pride inevitably overflows into spoken words.

The word often appears alongside images of rebellion, such as a "stiff neck," or descriptions of wicked people boasting about their own power. When people speak ataq, they act as though they are accountable to no one, forgetting that God hears every word they utter.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s use of ataq highlights several important biblical truths:

First, God is fully aware of human speech and motives. Hannah declares that the Lord is "a God of knowledge," meaning nothing is hidden from Him. Human boasting is foolish because God sees past outward bravado to the heart.

Second, God actively opposes human pride. Throughout Scripture, arrogant speech represents a creature attempting to elevate itself above the Creator. God routinely reverses human circumstances, silencing the boastful and defending the helpless.

Third, true security is found in God, not in self-exaltation. Those who speak with arrogance trust in their own strength, status, or power, but Hannah’s prayer demonstrates that God alone lifts up and brings low.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:3, Hannah addresses those who speak with excessive pride: "Talk no more so very proudly; let no arrogance come from your mouth." The word ataq is translated here as "arrogance," describing the insolent language used by the proud.

The immediate parallelism of the verse reinforces this meaning. The first command, "Talk no more so very proudly," points to the act of boastful speaking. The second command warns against letting ataq proceed from the mouth. Hannah grounds this double warning in God's nature: because the Lord weighs every action, human arrogance is completely out of place.

This warning applies immediately to Peninnah's past mocking, but it extends far beyond Hannah's personal story. It serves as a prophetic warning to Israel's leaders and kings—such as King Saul later in the narrative—that God brings down those who exalt themselves.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalms 31:18 — "Let the lying lips be put to silence, which speak insolent things proudly and contemptuously against the righteous." Here the word is rendered as "things" within the phrase "insolent things," describing malicious, arrogant speech directed against God's faithful servant.

  • Psalms 75:5 — "Do not lift up your horn on high; do not speak with a stiff neck." In this passage, the word is rendered as "stiff," forming the expression "stiff neck" to depict stubborn, rebellious defiance against God's supreme authority.

  • Psalms 94:4 — "They utter speech, and speak insolent things; all the workers of iniquity boast in themselves." The word is translated as "things" in "insolent things," highlighting the careless, boastful words of evil men who assume God will not hold them accountable.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's prayer establishes a key theological pattern that echoes throughout the rest of God's Word. Her emphasis on God humbling the proud and exalting the lowly reappears in Mary's song of praise (the Magnificat) in Luke 1:46–55. Both women recognize that human arrogance is silenced in the presence of God's holiness and salvation.
