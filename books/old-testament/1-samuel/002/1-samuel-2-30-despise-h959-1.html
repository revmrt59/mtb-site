DESPISE - (bâzâh, Strong's H959)

Summary:

The Hebrew word bazah means to treat someone or something with contempt, to regard as worthless or trivial, or to hold in low esteem. In 1 Samuel 2:30, bazah describes those who treat God and His holy worship with disrespectful indifference. God declares that while He honors those who honor Him, those who despise Him will be held in contempt and brought low.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The book of 1 Samuel opens during a dark moral and spiritual period in Israel's history. The tabernacle was stationed at Shiloh, and Eli served as high priest. However, Eli’s sons, Hophni and Phinehas, abused their priestly office. They forcibly took choice portions of sacrifices for themselves and committed sexual immorality at the entrance of the tabernacle. Although Eli knew of their wickedness, he offered only weak verbal rebukes and failed to remove them from office.

In response, God sent a prophet to deliver a message of divine judgment against the house of Eli. The prophecy made clear that Eli honored his sons above God by allowing them to profit from corrupted sacrifices. In 1 Samuel 2:30, God contrasts His divine favor toward those who revere Him with the judgment awaiting those who treat Him with contempt. The word bazah captures the underlying failure of Eli's family: they treated the living God and His worship as something insignificant.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of bazah is to look down on something, to view it as cheap, or to treat it as having little to no value. It is the opposite of giving weight, honor, or high esteem to a person or object.

When people are the subject and God or His commands are the object, bazah describes an attitude of heart that expresses itself in contemptuous actions. It is not merely a quiet feeling of dislike. Rather, it is demonstrated when a person treats sacred duties, God's word, or His divine authority as trivial, unnecessary, or inferior to personal desires.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, bazah appears in several distinct settings:

  • Personal Choice and Value: It describes treating valuable inheritance or covenant privilege as insignificant, such as when Esau traded his birthright for a single meal.

  • Relational Contempt: It refers to looking down on someone with disgust or disdain, such as when Michal viewed David with contempt as he worshipped before the Lord.

  • Spiritual Rebellion: It describes treating God's law or His holy name as cheap and disregardable, such as when Israel's rulers or priests corrupted true worship.

In every case, bazah highlights a distorted valuation. The person using bazah fails to recognize true worth, choosing temporary selfish gain or pride over what is truly sacred.

4. Theological Implications

Scripture reveals that God is holy and worthy of supreme honor. The Hebrew concept of honor (kabad) literally means to recognize someone's weight or glory. God deserves the heaviest weight in human devotion, worship, and obedience.

To despise God (bazah) means to treat Him as light or weightless. It assumes that God's presence, authority, and laws do not matter enough to govern daily life. 1 Samuel 2:30 reveals a foundational biblical principle of divine response: God honors those who honor Him, but those who treat Him with contempt will be lightly esteemed. God does not lose His intrinsic glory when people despise Him, but those who diminish God in their conduct will ultimately lose their own honor and standing before Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:30, bazah functions as a masculine plural participle, describing a ongoing group or category of people: "those who despise Me." This grammatical form highlights an established character or pattern of behavior rather than a single accidental misstep.

Eli’s sons continually dishonored the sacrifice of the Lord, treating holy worship as a means for personal enrichment. Eli himself participated in this dynamic by tolerating their wickedness, placing family peace above God's holiness. Through the prophet, God confronted this compromise directly. The contrast in the passage is sharp: those who value God will experience His lasting favor, but those whose lives demonstrate contempt for Him will be rendered insignificant. The destruction of Eli’s priestly lineage later in the narrative stands as the direct fulfillment of this solemn principle.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 25:34 – Esau ate, drank, and arose to leave, showing how he "despised" his birthright by trading an enduring covenant blessing for immediate physical satisfaction.

  • 2 Samuel 6:16 – As

the ark of the Lord entered Jerusalem, Michal looked through a window, saw King David dancing with all his might, and "despised" him in her heart because she viewed his enthusiastic worship as undignified.

  • 2 Samuel 12:10 – The prophet Nathan confronted King David after his sin regarding Bathsheba and Uriah, stating that David had "despised" God by breaking His commandments and acting as though God's law could be set aside for personal desire.

  • Psalms 22:24 – The psalmist offers praise because God has not "despised" nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted, reassuring believers that God does not look upon the suffering with cold contempt.

  • Malachi 1:6 – God rebukes the priests who brought blind and lame animals for sacrifice, asking where His honor was when the religious leaders themselves "despised" His name by offering defiled worship.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The argument of 1 Samuel 2:30 relies on a deliberate contrast of Hebrew terms. The verse contrasts "honor" (kabad, meaning heavy or weighty) with "despise" (bazah, meaning cheap or worthless) and "lightly esteemed" (qalal, meaning light or swift to be brought low).

This language shows that worship is ultimately a matter of spiritual weight. Those who assign supreme weight to God are recognized by Him. Those who treat God as light and insignificant (bazah) will themselves be reduced to insignificance (qalal). Eli tried to honor God with pious words, but His actions gave more weight to his sons. The passage demonstrates that God measures worship not by outward religious status, but by whether His word and holiness carry governing weight in practical choices.
