LORD - (YHWH, Strong's H3068)

YHWH ( yah-weh , Strong’s [H3068] )

Summary:

The proper, personal, and covenant name of the God of Israel, most frequently rendered in English Bibles as "Lord." In 1 Samuel 2:30, the name identifies the divine speaker who confronts Eli the high priest through an unnamed prophet, anchoring the solemn declaration of judgment and the promise of a faithful priesthood in the unchanging authority, holiness, and personal commitment of Israel's covenant God.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew name translated as "Lord" in this passage is the sacred four-letter designation for the God of Israel, traditionally referred to as the tetragrammaton. In English translations, it is typically set in capital letters to distinguish it from common nouns or titles of authority like master or ruler. This name was revealed to Israel as the personal and covenantal identity of the Almighty, signifying the one who is self-existent, eternal, and unfailingly faithful to His promises. In the historical setting of early Israel, as the nation transitions from the era of the judges to the establishment of the monarchy, this name constantly reminds the people that their society, worship, and leadership operate under the direct oversight of the living God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term functions as the proper personal name of God rather than a descriptive title.

  • When used throughout the Old Testament, it identifies the specific God who brought Israel out of Egypt, established a covenant at Sinai, and revealed His moral and spiritual character to His people.

  • It emphasizes personal presence, steadfast covenant loyalty, and absolute authority over history and human actions.

  • The name carries no abstract philosophical definition within the text itself; rather, its meaning is continually filled and demonstrated by what God says and does throughout biblical history.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses this name to distinguish the true God of Israel from the lifeless idols of surrounding nations. It frequently appears in contexts involving divine revelation, commands, warnings, and promises, underscoring that the One speaking is the ultimate source of moral law and historical consequence. When God speaks or acts under this name, His word carries absolute weight and inescapable truth. The usage highlights a personal relationship with His people—one marked by deep grace toward the obedient, but also by uncompromising holiness and justice toward those who persist in rebellion or spiritual compromise.

4. Theological Implications

The use of the personal name of God in divine pronouncements establishes that moral accountability is rooted directly in personal relationship with the Creator. God is not an impersonal force or a distant deity indifferent to human behavior, but the living Sovereign who evaluates human actions and holds leaders strictly accountable for their stewardship. The passage demonstrates that divine judgment and divine grace proceed from the same unchanging character. God’s commitment to His own holiness means He cannot permanently tolerate the corruption of sacred responsibilities, yet His commitment to His redemptive purposes ensures that He will always raise up faithful servants to accomplish His will.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:30, the appearance of the divine name marks a dramatic turning point in the chapter. Eli and his household have treated the offerings of God with contempt, and the priesthood has fallen into severe moral decay. By beginning the divine message with this personal name, God asserts His supreme authority directly against the corruption of the house of Eli. The statement contrasts human failure with divine constancy. Because God is who He is, His previously spoken promises regarding the priesthood remain completely valid, yet they will be executed through a severe realignment that strips the unfaithful descendants of Eli of their honored place and establishes a faithful priest who shall walk before God forever.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:4 — "This is the history of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the Lord God made the earth and the heavens."

  • Exodus 13:5 — "And it shall be, when the Lord brings you into the land of the Canaanites and the Hittites and the Amorites and the Hivites and the Jebusites, which He swore to your fathers to give you, a land flowing with milk and honey, that you shall keep this service in this month."

  • Psalms 25:1 — "To You, O Lord, I lift up my soul."

  • Jeremiah 33:1 — "Moreover the word of the Lord came to Jeremiah a second time, while he was still shut up in the court of the prison..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The deliberate choice of the personal covenant name in 1 Samuel 2:30 grounds the prophet's sobering warning not in human opinion or priestly politics, but in the immutable character of God Himself. The solemn principle that those who honor God will be honored, while those who despise Him shall be lightly esteemed, flows directly from who God is in His holiness and justice. This sobering reality calls every generation of spiritual leaders to examine their motives and actions in the immediate presence of the living God.
