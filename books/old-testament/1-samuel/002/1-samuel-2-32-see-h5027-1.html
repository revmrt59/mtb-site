SEE - (nâbaṭ, Strong's H5027)

nabat ( nabat , Strong’s [H5027] )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nabat means to look, gaze, regard, or observe with focused attention. In 1 Samuel 2:32, the word is used in a solemn divine warning delivered to Eli, where God announces that His adversary will witness distress in His dwelling place while Israel experiences prosperity. Here, the action of seeing highlights an inescapable, distressing reality that Eli’s house will be forced to behold, underscoring the certainty of divine judgment upon unfaithful leadership.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Nabat is a common biblical Hebrew verb used to describe physical sight, but it frequently carries a deeper mental and emotional dimension than ordinary seeing. It is not merely the passive reception of light by the eye, but an intentional turning of attention toward someone or something. In the Old Testament, when God is the subject of the verb, it often conveys looking down in grace or judgment; when human beings are the subject, it describes gazing with wonder, seeking help, or observing a situation intently. In the historical narrative of 1 Samuel, the word appears at a critical turning point as God confronts the corruption of Eli's priestly household through an unnamed prophet.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word can refer to the physical act of turning the eyes to look at an object, person, or distant scene. Beyond physical sight, it frequently means to regard, consider, or pay close attention to something with the mind. When used in contexts of judgment or distress, the term can mean to witness or gaze upon a scene with a sense of impact or realization. Here in 1 Samuel, the verb describes beholding a future calamity with unavoidable clarity.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses nabat across various literary genres, including narrative, prophetic literature, and the Psalms. The verb often emphasizes the quality of observation. For example, looking to God in prayer involves turning one's spiritual gaze away from earthly distress and toward divine mercy. Conversely, when God looks upon humanity, the action reveals His omniscience and active involvement in human affairs. In prophetic and historical texts, observing a divine judgment with nabat signifies that the witnesses cannot look away from the unfolding consequences of sin.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of this verb in contexts of divine visitation reminds readers that human actions do not escape notice. God sees human behavior, and in turn, He brings about circumstances that humanity must ultimately behold. In 1 Samuel, the pronouncement against Eli’s house reveals that God’s sovereign evaluation of leadership is active and decisive. Privileged spiritual position does not shield individuals from accountability; those who dishonor God will eventually witness the dismantling of their own security.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:32, the divine message confronts Eli concerning the greed and rebellion of his sons. The Lord declares that an enemy will occupy His dwelling place, bringing distress, while Israel enjoys divine blessing. The use of nabat here emphasizes that Eli’s descendants will look upon this reversal of fortune with their own eyes. The word stresses the inescapable tragedy of watching the glory depart from their house because of their persistent failure to honor God above family loyalty.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 15:5 — "Then He brought him outside and said, 'Look now toward heaven, and tell the stars, if you are able to number them.'" Here the verb invites Abraham to raise his eyes and gaze at the vast night sky to comprehend the scale of God's promise.

  • Psalms 33:13 — "The Lord looks from heaven; He beholds all the sons of men." The word describes God's attentive, comprehensive observation of humanity from His heavenly dwelling.

  • Isaiah 51:2 — "Look to Abraham your father, and to Sarah who bore you; for I called him alone, and blessed him and increased him." The prophet uses the term to direct the attention of the exiles backward in history to remember God's faithfulness in their origins.

  • Lamentations 1:11 — "Consider, O Lord, and look; for I am despised." The suffering petitioner calls upon God to turn His gaze toward the misery of Jerusalem and take note of her affliction.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The act of looking in biblical Hebrew often carries relational or moral weight. To look upon something in Scripture frequently leads to action, whether of mercy or retribution. In the narrative of 1 Samuel, the prophecy forces Eli and his house to confront a future they would rather ignore. The stark reality of what they will see serves as a sobering reminder that God’s word concerning righteousness and judgment always comes to pass.
