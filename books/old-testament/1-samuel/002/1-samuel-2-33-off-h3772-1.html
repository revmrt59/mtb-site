OFF - (kârath, Strong's H3772)

Summary:

Karath primarily means to cut, sever, or chop down. Scripture uses it literally for chopping wood or clearing trees and figuratively for removing individuals, cutting off a family line, or solemnizing a covenant by cutting sacrifice animals. In 1 Samuel 2:33, the word expresses God's direct act of judgment in severing Eli's priestly lineage, leaving behind only a remnant whose tragic lives would serve as a visible reminder of divine discipline.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

1 Samuel 2 records God's solemn message of judgment delivered by an unnamed man of God to Eli, the high priest at Shiloh. Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, treated the LORD's offerings with contempt and engaged in sexual immorality at the tabernacle. Although Eli gently rebuked them, he failed to restrain them, effectively honoring his sons above God. In response, God announced that the priestly privilege granted to the house of Eli would be stripped away. In 1 Samuel 2:33, God declares that while He will cut off most of Eli's descendants from serving at His altar, any survivor left behind will endure sorrow and early death, showing that persistent disobedience brings severing from divine favor.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Karath means to cut, sever, or cut down. Physical uses include chopping down trees, cutting off branches, or destroying physical objects. When applied to people or lines of descent, the word means to eliminate, destroy, or remove someone from a community, office, or family lineage.

Karath also features prominently in biblical covenant-making, where ancient parties cut animals in two to solemnize an agreement, creating the idiomatic expression "to cut a covenant." When God acts as the subject causing the action, as in 1 Samuel 2:33, the word indicates divine intervention that removes an individual or family from their former place of blessing or sacred ministry.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, karath functions in three primary settings:

  • Physical cutting or clearing, such as chopping wood or cutting down idolatrous groves.

  • Legal and judgment contexts, where God or human leaders cut off wicked individuals from among the covenant people or destroy enemies.

  • Covenantal contexts, where making a binding agreement is described as cutting a covenant.

In judicial contexts, being cut off carries the weight of divine discipline, exile, or the absolute termination of a family's legacy. It highlights a decisive break: what was once attached to God's community or service is severed.

4. Theological Implications

The use of karath in God's speech reveals important truths about divine justice and holiness:

  • Divine sovereignty over ministry privileges: God gives sacred responsibilities, such as the priesthood, but unfaithfulness leads to the forfeiture of those duties.

  • The reality of divine judgment: God's holiness demands that persistent, unrepentant contempt for holy things results in separation and removal.

  • Integrity in covenant life: While God keeps His promises, leaders who abuse their sacred trust cannot expect to enjoy the ongoing fruit of covenant blessings without obedience.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:33, God speaks directly, declaring that He Himself determines who will be removed from sacred service. The sentence presents a sobering contrast: God will cut off Eli's house from the sanctuary altar, yet any descendant who is not immediately cut off will suffer severe grief and die in youth.

This judgment is not an arbitrary act, but a righteous response to Eli's compromise. The lingering survivors do not escape judgment; rather, their continuous sorrow serves as an ongoing testimony to the reality of God's word. The word karath here underscores that serving at God's altar is a holy gift that God Himself guards, removes, or bestows according to His righteous standards.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 9:11 — "Never again shall all flesh be cut off by the waters of the flood..." Here the word describes God's promise never again to destroy all earthly life through a global flood.

  • Numbers 4:18 — "Do not cut off the tribe of the families of the Kohathites from among the Levites..." This verse uses the word as an admonition to safeguard the Kohathite clan from holy danger during tabernacle transport.

  • 1 Samuel 23:18 — "So the two of them made a covenant before the LORD..." Here the word is translated "made" as part of the formal Hebrew expression for entering into a covenant between David and Jonathan.

  • Psalms 37:34 — "When the wicked are cut off, you shall see it." In this psalm, the word describes God's eventual judgment and removal of the ungodly from the land.

  • Isaiah 56:5 — "I will give them an everlasting name that shall not be cut off." God promises faithful outsiders an enduring legacy in His house that can never be destroyed.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The judgment declared in 1 Samuel 2:33 finds its tragic fulfillment later in Israel's history. During Saul's reign, the priests of Nob—Eli's descendants—were slaughtered at Doeg the Edomite's hand. Later, King Solomon deposed Abiathar, the last high priest from Eli's line, fulfilling the prophecy spoken at Shiloh. The history of Eli's house demonstrates that when God speaks of cutting off a lineage, His word stands sure across generations.
