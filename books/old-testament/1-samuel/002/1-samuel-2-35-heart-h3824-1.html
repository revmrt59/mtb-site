HEART - (lêbâb, Strong's H3824)

Summary:

Lebab refers to the inner self, encompassing the mind, will, desires, and motives of a person. In 1 Samuel 2:35, the word describes God's divine will, holy desires, and inner purpose, highlighting that the faithful priest raised up by God will act in complete harmony with God's own heart and soul.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Hebrew thought, the heart was understood as the inner center of human life. Rather than referring primarily to romantic or physical emotions as modern expressions often suggest, the heart represented the seat of intellect, decision-making, choice, moral character, and spiritual commitment. It was seen as the inner command center from which all thoughts, choices, and actions originate.

In the historical setting of 1 Samuel 2, Israel's priesthood faced a profound leadership crisis under Eli and his sons, Hophni and Phinehas. Eli's sons treated the worship of God with contempt, dishonored the tabernacle sacrifices for personal gain, and engaged in widespread wickedness. Eli failed to restrain his sons, allowing corrupt family loyalty to compromise the sanctuary. God sent a messenger to announce judgment upon Eli's house, declaring that his priests would be cut off. In contrast to this corrupt priesthood, God promised to raise up a faithful priest who would act according to His holy character and divine purposes.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lebab refers to the inner person or inner life. Depending on the context, the word can refer to the mind, intellect, intentions, decisions, moral inclination, or spiritual devotion of an individual.

When used of human beings, the word describes the source of thoughts, intentions, and obedience or rebellion. When used anthropomorphically of God, as in 1 Samuel 2:35, it speaks of God's holy intent, desires, wise counsel, and sovereign purpose. In this verse, God's "heart" signifies His holy will and righteous desires regarding worship, sanctuary service, and leadership over His people.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, lebab serves as a comprehensive term for complete commitment and internal motivation. It is where human plans are formed before they express themselves in outward behavior.

Scripture frequently focuses on the condition of the heart to evaluate spiritual life. A person may seek God with all their heart, hide His commandments within the heart, or allow the heart to turn away toward idols and pride. When the word is applied to God's own heart, it highlights the divine standards and purposes that govern His actions toward His people.

4. Theological Implications

The reference to God's heart in 1 Samuel 2:35 establishes that acceptable spiritual leadership must align with God's revealed character and desires. Eli's sons lived to satisfy their own appetites and personal ambitions, desecrating sacred duties for immediate self-gratification. They prioritized their personal desires over the honoring of God.

God's promise to raise up a priest who acts according to His heart shows that true ministry arises from alignment with God's holy character. Historically, this prophecy found immediate partial expression in Samuel's faithful ministry, later in the priesthood of Zadok during Solomon's reign, and ultimately points forward to Jesus Christ, the true High Priest who performed the Father's will in perfect obedience.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:35, the phrase "according to what is in My heart and in My mind" creates a sharp contrast between human selfishness and divine holiness. Eli's sons acted according to their corrupt human desires, despising God's offerings and dishonoring His sanctuary.

God answers human corruption by establishing a priest whose service is shaped by God's own inner purposes. The word lebab pairs with God's "mind" (or soul) to emphasize complete, wholehearted harmony between the leader's service and God's holy character. The passage demonstrates that divine service is not merely a matter of inherited status or outward tradition, but requires personal fidelity to God's holy purposes.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 20:5 — Abimelech defended his moral integrity before God, explaining that he acted in the "integrity of my heart and innocence of my hands."

  • 1 Samuel 12:24 — Samuel instructed the people of Israel to "fear the LORD, and serve Him in truth with all your heart," calling them back to wholehearted devotion.

  • 1 Kings 8:17 — Solomon recalled that "it was in the heart of my father David to build a temple for the name of the LORD God of Israel."

  • 2 Chronicles 16:9 — The prophet Hanani declared that the eyes of the Lord look throughout the earth to strengthen those "whose heart is loyal to Him."

  • Psalms 78:72 — Summarizing David's faithful rule over Israel, the Scripture notes that "he shepherded them according to the integrity of his heart."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The emphasis on God's heart in 1 Samuel 2:35 anticipates a broader theme in the books of Samuel. Later, when God rejects King Saul, He seeks out "a man after His own heart" to lead His people (1 Samuel 13:14). Together, these passages demonstrate that God evaluates human leaders by their inner loyalty and obedience rather than by external privileges, lineage, or public appearance.
