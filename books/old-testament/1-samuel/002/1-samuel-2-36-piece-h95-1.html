PIECE - (ʼăgôwrâh, Strong's H95)

Summary:

The word agorah refers to a small piece of silver, a small coin, or a minimal payment. In 1 Samuel 2:36, it describes the tragic future of Eli’s family line, who would be reduced from corrupt luxury to begging for a small piece of silver just to buy daily food.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In 1 Samuel 2, an unnamed man of God arrives to pronounce divine judgment on the house of Eli, the high priest at Shiloh. Eli’s sons, Hophni and Phinehas, had severely abused their sacred responsibilities. They took the best portions of the sacrifices for themselves by force and dishonored the sanctuary of God. Eli failed to restrain his sons, honoring his family above the Lord.

Because of this systemic corruption, God declared that Eli’s house would lose its priestly prominence and strength. God promised to raise up a faithful priest who would do according to what was in His heart and mind. The climax of this announcement appears in 1 Samuel 2:36, where the remaining members of Eli’s family are pictured coming to the newly established priest to beg for the smallest pittance of silver and a morsel of bread.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word agorah refers to a small piece of money, a small coin, or a small payment of silver. It is related to a root that means to gather or collect, suggesting a small collection of metal or a minor wage.

In 1 Samuel 2:36, the word appears in connection with silver to form the phrase "a piece of silver." It does not refer to bread or food. Later in the same verse, separate Hebrew words are used to speak of a "morsel of bread" and a "piece of bread." The word agorah strictly identifies the small silver payment or piece of money that Eli's descendants would beg for.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In early biblical times, standardized minted coins were not yet used in Israel. Instead, silver was weighed out to conduct financial transactions. A piece of silver in this context refers to a small scrap, token, or minor fraction of silver weighed out for small daily purchases.

The word carries a clear nuance of poverty and humility. It does not speak of wealth, regular compensation, or an honorable wage. Instead, it describes the smallest possible financial payment—a mere pittance requested by someone who has no other means of support.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of this word in God's judgment highlights the holiness and justice of God. Eli’s sons treated the offerings of the Lord with contempt, greedily seizing the rich, choice cuts of sacrificial meat for themselves. They sought luxury through sacrilege and self-exaltation.

God's response demonstrates a fundamental spiritual truth: those who honor God will be honored, but those who despise Him will be lightly esteemed. The tragic reversal of Eli’s house illustrates this principle. Those who formerly gorged themselves on sacred sacrifices would one day beg for a tiny piece of silver just to avoid starvation. God's judgment strips away dishonorably gained abundance and humbles the proud.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:36, agorah emphasizes the deep humiliation of Eli's descendants. Rather than serving as honored leaders at God's altar, they would be reduced to suppliants. They are described as bowing down before the faithful priest, asking for a small piece of silver and a bite of bread.

They even plead to be assigned to some minor priestly duty simply to get something to eat. The word highlights how far the house of Eli was destined to fall—from enjoying the holy revenues of the tabernacle to begging for scraps of silver from the very priesthood that replaced them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • This term is unique to this context and does not appear elsewhere in the biblical corpus.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The downfall of Eli's house stands in stark contrast to the rise of Samuel and later Zadok, the faithful priest whose line served in Jerusalem. While Eli's family lost their position through self-indulgence and compromise, God preserved a priesthood that would carry out His sovereign purpose.

Ultimately, this shift points toward the perfect priesthood fulfilled in Jesus Christ. Unlike the corrupt priests of Eli's line who sought personal gain, Christ offered Himself selflessly for His people, establishing a kingdom characterized by true righteousness rather than selfish ambition.
