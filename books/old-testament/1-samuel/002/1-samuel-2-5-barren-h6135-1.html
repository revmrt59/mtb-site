BARREN - (ʻâqâr, Strong's H6135)

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'aqar describes a state of childlessness or infertility, referring to an inability to conceive or bear offspring. In 1 Samuel 2:5, Hannah uses this word to celebrate how God overturns human expectations, transforming total weakness and societal shame into complete joy and fruitfulness.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Near East, family lineage and childbearing were vital to personal identity, inheritance, and social security. Infertility was not merely a private grief; it often brought public reproach and financial vulnerability. A childless woman carried a heavy emotional and cultural burden, as children were viewed as a clear sign of family continuity and divine favor.

Hannah's personal story in 1 Samuel 1 provides the direct background for this term. For years, Hannah endured severe distress because she was unable to bear children, while her rival, Peninnah, had many sons and daughters. When God heard Hannah's heartfelt prayer and gave her a son, Samuel, her sorrow turned into praise. In 1 Samuel 2:1–10, Hannah sings a prophetic song celebrating God's power to lift up the needy and reverse human fortunes.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word 'aqar is derived from a root associated with plucking up or severing. As an adjective, it describes a condition of being childless, barren, or infertile. It refers to the physical inability of a person or animal to yield offspring.

When applied to individuals, 'aqar describes a woman who cannot conceive children naturally. In 1 Samuel 2:5, the word functions as a noun ("the barren"), identifying a woman who was previously marked by childlessness but who now experiences God's supernatural provision.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses 'aqar across several major narrative and prophetic contexts:

  • Patriarchal Narratives: Key matrons in Israel's history—such as Sarah, Rebekah, and Rachel—were initially described as childless. In each case, their physical inability to conceive highlighted that the covenant promise would move forward through divine power rather than human self-sufficiency.

  • Covenant Promises: In the law given to Israel, God promised that under His covenant blessing, neither male nor female would suffer childlessness without cause among His obedient people.

  • Poetic and Prophetic Writings: Psalms and prophetic literature use the image of the childless woman to illustrate God's mercy to the outcast and His future restoration of His people.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of 'aqar throughout the biblical storyline emphasizes the truth that life originates with God alone. Human weakness and physical impossibility become the backdrop against which God displays His creative power and grace.

The word reveals that God notices the suffering of the forgotten and vulnerable. Rather than measuring a person's worth by cultural standards of success or status, God delights in lifting up the humble and giving hope to those who feel helpless.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:5, Hannah sets up a series of dramatic contrasts:

  • The full must hire themselves out for bread, while the hungry no longer go without.

  • The barren woman bears seven, while she who has many children grows feeble.

The number seven symbolizes full completion and perfect abundance. Hannah does not necessarily mean she personally bore seven individual children, but rather that God’s gracious intervention brings complete and overflowing blessing.

Through this contrast, Hannah shows that earthly advantages are temporary, whereas God's sovereign intervention is absolute. The childless woman who relies on God receives ideal fullness, while those who trust in their own strength or earthly status find that their power fades away.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 11:30 — "But Sarai was barren; she had no child."

  • Genesis 25:21 — "Now Isaac pleaded with the LORD for his wife, because she was barren; and the LORD granted his plea, and Rebekah his wife conceived."

  • Judges 13:2 — "Now there was a certain man from Zorah, of the family of the Danites, whose name was Manoah; and his wife was barren and had no children."

  • Psalms 113:9 — "He grants the barren woman a home, like a joyful mother of children."

  • Isaiah 54:1 — "'Sing, O barren, you who have not borne! Break forth into singing, and cry aloud, you who have not labored with child!'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's song in 1 Samuel 2 serves as a literary and theological foundation for the rest of the Books of Samuel. It introduces themes that reappear throughout Israel's monarchy: God humbles the proud (such as King Saul) and exalts the humble (such as David).

Furthermore, Hannah's praise strongly echoes in the New Testament in Mary's song, the Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55). Just as Hannah rejoiced over God's kindness in giving a child to "the barren," Mary rejoiced that God looked upon her humble estate to bring forth the Savior of the world.
