BORNE - (yâlad, Strong's H3205)

YALAD ( yalad , Strong’s [H3205] )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yalad primarily means to bear, bring forth, give birth to, or beget. In 1 Samuel 2:5, translated in the NKJV as "borne," the word describes the physical reality of childbearing within Hannah’s poetic prayer of praise, where she marvels at how the barren woman has borne seven children while the one who had many children has grown feeble.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient biblical culture, family lineage and childbearing carried immense social, economic, and theological weight. To bring forth children was viewed as a primary blessing and a sign of divine favor, while childlessness often brought deep social grief and public humiliation. The verb yalad stands at the center of this cultural landscape, frequently used throughout the Hebrew Scriptures to record generations, trace family lines, and describe the physical birth of children. In Hannah’s song, the word captures the dramatic reversal of human fortunes orchestrated by God, who steps into ordinary family struggles to reshape the future.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yalad can refer to the physical act of giving birth when spoken of a mother, or siring and begetting offspring when spoken of a father. In a broader sense, it can also describe the formation or bringing forth of a people, a nation, or even inanimate features of creation by divine action. When used of human parents, it points directly to the biological origin of life and the generational continuity of families. Here, it describes a woman giving birth to children, moving from the state of barrenness into the joy of motherhood.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses yalad across various historical and poetic contexts to emphasize the origin of life. It appears frequently in genealogical lists to anchor biblical history in real families and historical time. In poetic and prophetic literature, the word often gains added resonance, describing not only physical birth but also the spiritual awakening or renewal brought about by God. In 1Samuel 2:5, the word carries a stark contrast between human expectation and divine intervention, highlighting how God numbers the days of the barren and grants families to the lonely.

4. Theological Implications

The use of yalad in Hannah's song underscores God’s sovereign control over life, fertility, and human destiny. In the ancient world, barrenness was often treated as a permanent and unalterable tragedy. Yet Hannah’s song proclaims that human limitations do not restrict God's power. By reversing the circumstances of the barren and the fertile, the verse points to a God who humbles the proud and lifts up the lowly. The ability to bring forth life is ultimately traced back to the Creator's generous and unexpected grace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:5, Hannah rejoices after the birth of Samuel and her dedication of him to the Lord. Her personal story of suffering through childlessness and receiving a miraculous answer to prayer broadens into a universal statement about God’s rule over the world. The specific use of "borne" marks the tangible, physical evidence of God's reversing grace. The woman who once wept in bitterness now rejoices over a full household, illustrating that God delights in turning human weakness into a platform for His praise.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 3:16 — God tells the woman that He will greatly multiply her sorrow and her conception, and in pain she shall bring "forth" children.

  • Genesis 21:5 — Abraham was one hundred years old when his son Isaac was "born" to him.

  • Genesis 29:35 — Leah conceived again and bore a son, declaring that now she would praise the Lord, naming him Judah.

  • Leviticus 12:2 — The law addresses a woman who has conceived and "borne" a male child, outlining the ceremonial provisions for ritual purification.

  • 1 Samuel 2:21 — The Lord visited Hannah, and she conceived and "bore" three sons and two daughters while the young boy Samuel grew up before the Lord.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's song serves as a theological bridge in the book of Samuel, setting the tone for the entire narrative. The sudden shift from barrenness to abundance prefigures the larger political and spiritual turnaround in Israel's history, moving from the dark days of the judges toward the establishment of the monarchy. The physical act of childbearing described by yalad thus becomes a vivid symbol of new beginnings and divine faithfulness in dark times.
