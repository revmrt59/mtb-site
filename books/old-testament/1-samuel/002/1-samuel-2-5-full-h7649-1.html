FULL - (sâbêaʻ, Strong's H7649)

Summary:

The Hebrew word sabea means satisfied, filled, or sated, describing a condition of having abundance with no remaining lack. In 1 Samuel 2:5, it refers to prosperous people who were well-fed and comfortable, but whose self-sufficiency is overturned by God as He humbles the proud and provides for the needy.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In 1 Samuel 2, Hannah offers a song of praise after God answers her prayer for a child and she presents young Samuel to the Lord at Shiloh. Her prayer moves from personal thanksgiving to a poetic declaration of God's character and government over human society.

The ancient Near Eastern world was marked by sharp social and economic divisions. The wealthy enjoyed abundant harvests and physical security, while the poor frequently suffered from scarcity and hunger. In 1 Samuel 2:5, Hannah describes a complete reversal of these worldly fortunes. Those who were once filled and comfortable are brought to a state where they must work as day laborers for a basic loaf of bread.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Sabea describes a state of being satisfied, full, or completely sated. It conveys the idea of having enough to meet physical or personal desires so that no hunger or lack remains.

The word can refer to physical fullness after eating food, a deep sense of personal contentment, or being thoroughly filled with days at the end of a long life. In some contexts, it describes being filled to overflowing with hardship or distress. In 1 Samuel 2:5, the word identifies those who possess physical abundance and material security.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses sabea in a few distinct ways:

  • Physical repletion: Having eaten enough food to be satisfied and well-fed.

  • Fulfillment in life: Describing an older person who has lived a complete life, frequently rendered as being "full of days" or "full of years."

  • Saturation with hardship: Describing a person thoroughly filled with trouble, grief, or emotional pain.

  • Contentment: Describing a soul that is satisfied through reverence for God.

In each case, the word highlights the state of being filled to capacity, whether that capacity is filled with food, years, trouble, or contentment.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s song emphasizes that human status, wealth, and physical satisfaction are neither permanent nor self-sustaining. When people rely on their own abundance and ignore God, they discover how fragile earthly fullness truly is.

Scripture reveals that God lowers those who trust in earthly security while

raising up those who depend on Him. Earthly fullness does not guarantee lasting peace. True contentment and ultimate security come from God alone, who controls the fortunes of the proud and the humble alike.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:5, sabea describes those who enjoyed wealth, comfortable living, and an abundance of food. Hannah contrasts them directly with the hungry who had nothing.

In the poetic structure of the verse, God reverses their conditions. Those who were once satisfied with food are reduced to hiring themselves out as day laborers just to obtain bread. The masculine plural form identifies these individuals as a group of prosperous people whose self-reliance is exposed as temporary. God's judgment strips away their pride, demonstrating that human prosperity depends entirely on the Lord's divine sovereign will.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 25:8 — "Then Abraham breathed his last and died in a good old age, an old man and full of years, and was gathered to his people." Here the word describes a long life completed in divine blessing and satisfaction.

  • Job 14:1 — "Man who is born of woman is of few days and full of trouble." In this passage, the word describes human life saturated with hardship and sorrow.

  • Proverbs 19:23 — "The fear of the LORD leads to life, and he who has it will abide in satisfaction; he will not be visited with evil." Here the word expresses the deep, abiding security granted to those who honor God.

  • Proverbs 27:7 — "A satisfied soul loathes the honeycomb, but to a hungry soul every bitter thing is sweet." Here the word refers to someone who is physically full, showing how abundance alters a person's evaluation of basic needs.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's song in 1 Samuel 2 forms a major thematic foundation for the historical books that follow. Her declaration that God humbles the proud and exalts the lowly foreshadows the rise of David over Saul and anticipates the great reversal in God's redemptive plan. This theme reemerges in the New Testament in Mary's song of praise, where God fills the hungry with good things and sends the rich away empty.
