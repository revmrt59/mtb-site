ALIVE - (châyâh, Strong's H2421)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb chayah means to live, remain alive, recover health, or be restored to life. In its foundational sense, it describes physical existence and vitality as opposed to death and destruction. When used of God's action toward human beings, it highlights His power to grant life, preserve individuals through danger, and revive those who are weak or dying. In 1 Samuel 2:6, chayah describes God's sovereign authority over physical existence, declaring that the LORD alone possesses the power to give life and bring life out of death.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

1 Samuel 2 opens with Hannah's prayer of thanksgiving after the birth of her son, Samuel. For years, Hannah suffered the pain of barrenness and the ridicule of her rival. When God remembered her and granted her a child, Hannah did not merely celebrate her personal joy; she praised God for His sovereign character and supreme authority over all creation.

Her song of praise sets the spiritual tone for the entire book of 1 Samuel. It presents a world where human status, physical strength, and worldly advantages mean nothing apart from God. Into this song of contrasts—where the hungry are filled, the mighty are broken, and the barren bear children—Hannah declares that the LORD holds absolute dominion over life and death itself.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

centered around physical vitality and its preservation, the word chayah refers to the state of being alive or the process of coming to life. Its meanings fall into several related areas:

  • To possess physical life and breath, standing in contrast to death or non-existence.

  • To survive mortal danger, battle, famine, or illness, continuing in physical existence.

  • To recover health or regain strength after weakness, sickness, or injury.

  • To be revived, restored, or given life by an external power.

When a human being is the subject, the word describes living out one's days or surviving through trials. When God is the subject acting upon human beings, the word describes His divine activity in bestowing vitality, sustaining life, or bringing a person back from extreme weakness or death.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, chayah functions as the primary word for physical and spiritual vitality. The word is used in ordinary historical contexts to describe how long patriarchs lived or how survivors escaped dangerous events. It also appears in legal and wisdom passages to describe the ongoing life that flows from walking in God's ways.

In prophetic and poetic literature, chayah takes on deep theological depth. Prophets use it to describe God breathing life into a fallen nation or renewing a broken people. Poets use it in prayers asking God to spare them from destruction or to grant fresh spiritual strength. Throughout these varied contexts, the word consistently highlights that human life is not self-sustaining; it depends entirely on the word and power of God.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of chayah in Scripture establishes that God is the sole author and sustainer of life. Human beings do not possess independent vitality; every breath is maintained by divine providence. Life and death are not governed by fate, chance, or pagan deities, but by the conscious will of the one true God.

This word also lays the groundwork for the biblical hope of divine restoration. Because God is the Master of life, He is not limited by human helplessness, physical decay, or extreme suffering. His ability to grant life extends into situations that appear completely hopeless. What looks like final defeat to human eyes remains subject to His life-giving command.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:6, Hannah uses chayah within a poetic line built on direct parallelism: "The LORD kills and makes alive; He brings down to the grave and brings up." The word functions as a masculine singular participle, presenting this life-giving action as a defining, ongoing attribute of God's character and rule.

Hannah's choice of words directly reflects her personal experience. In ancient Israel, barrenness was often experienced as a form of living death and social isolation. By giving her a child, God brought life where there was no natural expectation of life. Yet Hannah expands her praise beyond her own situation to declare a general truth: God is the one who determines who lives, who dies, who falls into the grave, and who is brought back up. The word highlights that God's authority over physical existence is total and absolute.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 1:17 – "But the midwives feared God, and did not do as the king of Egypt commanded them, but saved the male children alive." Here the word describes the physical preservation of infant boys in defiance of Pharaoh's decree.

  • Deuteronomy 8:3 – "So He humbled you, allowed you to hunger, and fed you with manna... that He might make you know that man shall not live by bread alone; but man lives by every word that proceeds from the mouth of the LORD." In this passage, the word emphasizes that true physical and spiritual survival depends on God's sustaining word.

  • Joshua 14:10 – "And now, behold, the LORD has kept me alive, as He said, these forty-five years..." Caleb uses the word to acknowledge God's faithful preservation of his physical strength through decades in the wilderness and warfare.

  • Psalms 119:154 – "Plead my cause and redeem me; revive me according to Your word." The psalmist uses the word as a prayer for God to grant renewed strength and vitality according to His promise.

  • Ezekiel 37:6 – "I will put sinews on you and bring flesh upon you, cover you with skin and put breath in you; and you shall live." God uses the word to promise the restoration of life to dry bones, symbolizing the future revival of His people.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's song in 1 Samuel 2 shares strong thematic links with the song of Mary in Luke 1. Both women praise God for overturning human expectations, bringing down the proud, and elevating the low. Hannah's declaration that God makes alive stands as an early Old Testament witness to God's ultimate power over death, pointing forward to the full revelation of God's life-giving power through Jesus Christ.
