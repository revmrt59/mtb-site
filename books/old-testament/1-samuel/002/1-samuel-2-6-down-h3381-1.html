DOWN - (yârad, Strong's H3381)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yarad fundamentally means to move downward, descend, or bring down. It is used throughout the Old Testament for physical movement down a hill, road, or river, as well as for divine action when God comes down to earth or brings people down into lowliness and the grave. In 1 Samuel 2:6, the word forms part of the expression "brings down," describing God's absolute power to lower individuals into the depths of death and Sheol, paired directly with His power to raise them up again.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

1 Samuel 2 opens with the prayer of Hannah, a formerly barren woman whom God blessed with a son, Samuel. Delivered at the sanctuary in Shiloh, Hannah’s prayer moves far beyond her personal gratitude. She offers a prophetic song celebrating the holy character and supreme sovereignty of God over human history.

In this song, Hannah contrasts human arrogance with divine authority. Earthly strength, wealth, and status are shown to be fragile and fleeting, while God alone directs human destiny. In verse 6, she declares that the Lord controls the ultimate boundaries of human existence—life and death, humiliation and exaltation. Within this poetic setting, yarad highlights God's authority over the lowest realm of human experience.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of yarad is to move from a higher place to a lower place—to descend, step down, go down, or bring down.

Depending on the context, the word can refer to:

  • Physical movement down a slope, mountain, stair, or body of water.

  • Travel from a higher elevation to a lower region, such as traveling from Canaan down to Egypt.

  • A change in state or position, such as going down to the grave, falling in defeat, or being brought low by God.

In 1 Samuel 2:6, the verb is used in a causative sense, describing God as the One who causes a person to descend into the grave or Sheol.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses yarad across a wide variety of everyday and theological settings:

  • Physical Descent: It routinely describes ordinary travel, such as walking down a hill, stepping down into a pool, or descending to a valley.

  • Geographic Travel: Because the land of Israel is topographically elevated, traveling away to neighboring regions like Egypt is consistently described as going "down."

  • Divine Descent: The word is used when God manifests His presence on earth, such as coming down to investigate human activity or to reveal Himself on Mount Sinai.

  • Sovereign Humiliation: When God acts in judgment or providence, He brings down the proud, reduces powerful cities, or causes individuals to enter suffering and death.

The verb itself expresses directional downward motion. The surrounding context determines whether that motion is a simple physical step, a geographic journey, or a profound work of divine providence.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s use of yarad in 1 Samuel 2:6 reinforces a central biblical truth: God possesses total authority over life and death. Human beings do not hold ultimate power over their own lives, nor do life’s circumstances occur outside divine oversight.

Scripture does not present death or affliction as forces beyond God's control. By describing God as the One who brings down to the grave and brings up, Hannah establishes that even the deepest places of human helplessness remain subject to His command. This truth grounds the believer's hope. If God is sovereign over the downward journey to death, He is equally able to reverse that movement and bring life out of hopeless circumstances.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:6, yarad appears in a dynamic parallel pair. Hannah combines two contrasting statements to describe God's absolute lordship:

  • "The LORD kills and makes alive;"

  • "He brings down to the grave and brings up."

The target word is expressed as a masculine singular participle, functioning as an active description of God's ongoing character and power. He is continuously the One who brings low and lifts up.

Hannah’s own life illustrated this truth. She had experienced the deep grief of barrenness and social reproach—a status of being brought low—before God lifted her up into joy and fruitfulness. In her song, however, she expands her personal story into a universal principle: God routinely turns human expectations upside down. He brings down the self-reliant and exalts the humble.

  • Genesis 46:3 — "So He said, 'I am God, the God of your father; do not fear to go down to Egypt, for I will make of you a great nation there.'"

  • 2 Kings 5:14 — "So he went down and dipped seven times in the Jordan, according to the saying of the man of God; and his flesh was restored like the flesh of a little child, and he was clean."

  • 2 Chronicles 7:3 — "When all the children of Israel saw how the fire came down, and the glory of the LORD on the temple, they bowed their faces to the ground on the pavement, and worshipped and praised the LORD..."

  • Psalms 49:17 — "For when he dies he shall carry nothing away; His glory shall not descend after him."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Throughout the Old Testament, yarad (to go down) is frequently paired with alah (to go up). Together, these two directional verbs form a literary pair used to summarize the full range of human experience and divine authority.

This pairing plays a structural role in the narrative of 1 Samuel. The book opens with Hannah’s song proclaiming that God brings down the proud and exalts the low. The subsequent narrative demonstrates this truth in action: the proud house of Eli is brought down while young Samuel is raised up; King Saul is eventually brought down in judgment while the humble shepherd David is lifted up to the throne.
