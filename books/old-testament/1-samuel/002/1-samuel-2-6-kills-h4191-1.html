KILLS - (mûwth, Strong's H4191)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb mut fundamentally refers to dying, ceasing to live, or being put to death. When used of direct divine action, it refers to God executing judgment or exercising His sovereign right to end mortal life. In 1 Samuel 2:6, Hannah uses the word to declare the absolute authority of the LORD over human existence: He alone holds ultimate power over both life and death.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

First Samuel 2:6 is located within Hannah’s prayer of thanksgiving after presenting her young son Samuel to the LORD at Shiloh. For years, Hannah had suffered the pain of barrenness and the taunts of her rival. When the LORD answered her persistent prayer, her gratitude expanded into a hymn of praise that looks far beyond her personal story.

Hannah’s song celebrates God's holiness, justice, and supreme sovereignty over human society. She contrasts human pride, military strength, and earthly position with the irresistible power of the LORD. Within this series of contrasts, she declares that human destinies are not governed by blind fate, luck, or human rulers. The LORD Himself holds total jurisdiction over the ultimate boundaries of human existence—life and death.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb mut is the standard Hebrew word for death and dying. In its simplest form, it describes the cessation of biological life. In different grammatical contexts, its primary senses include:

  • To die naturally or as the result of disease, old age, or injury.

  • To be executed or put to death under legal or judicial authority.

  • To kill, slay, or cause the death of another person or creature.

In 1 Samuel 2:6, the word is expressed as a participle (functioning like a descriptive title or ongoing action) and means "causes to die" or "kills." It does not describe unlawful murder or arbitrary violence, but rather God's sovereign prerogative as the Creator who gives life and claims it according to His righteous will.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, mut spans every category of physical death. It describes patriarchs finishing their earthly journeys, soldiers falling in battle, and individuals facing judicial penalties established by the Law.

When the verb refers to human actions, it often highlights either legal execution or sinful violence. However, when God is the subject, the word takes on a distinct theological weight. God’s act of taking life is never presented as unrighteous or chaotic. Because God is the Author of life, His authority to end life is absolute and holy. Scripture consistently distinguishes between human murder—which violates God's law—and divine judgment, in which the Lord of all the earth exercises His righteous rule.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah’s declaration that the LORD kills and makes alive establishes foundational truths about God’s identity and authority:

  • God’s Absolute Sovereignty: Death is not an independent kingdom or an uncontrolled force. It remains completely subject to the Creator. Earthly powers cannot preserve life apart from God’s permit, nor can they thwart His decrees.

  • Human Frailty and Accountability: Mortals do not own their lives; human existence is a given trust. Every person lives by God's sustaining breath and remains accountable to Him.

  • Hope Beyond the Grave: By pairing the power to kill with the power to make alive and bring up from the grave, the passage reveals that God's authority is not merely destructive. His sovereignty encompasses restoration, renewal, and ultimate victory over death.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:6, "kills" stands in direct poetic balance with "makes alive," followed immediately by "He brings down to the grave and brings up." This paired structure emphasizes that the LORD rules over both ends of the human spectrum.

This truth had intense personal meaning for Hannah. Barrenness in the ancient world was often viewed as a social death—a state of helplessness and shame. By granting her a son, God had brought life out of barrenness. Hannah recognized that her personal miracle was part of a much larger reality: God routinely humbles the proud and lifts up the lowly. By stating that the LORD "kills and makes alive," Hannah affirms that no circumstance is too severe for God to transform, and no human pride is too strong for Him to humble.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 1:6 — "And Joseph died, all his brothers, and all that generation." Here the word describes the natural end of an earthly generation, marking a historical transition in Egypt.

  • Exodus 21:29 — "But if the ox tended to thrust with its horn in times past, and it has been made known to its owner, and he has not kept it confined, so that it has killed a man or a woman, the ox shall be stoned and its owner also shall be put to death." Here the word is used in a civil statute regarding liability for fatal bodily harm.

  • 1 Samuel 22:17 — "Then the king said to the guards who stood about him, 'Turn and kill the priests of the LORD, because their hand also is with David.'" Here the verb describes King Saul’s wicked command to murder innocent priests, contrasting human tyranny with God's righteous rule.

  • Proverbs 19:18 — "Chasten your son while there is hope, and do not set your heart on his destruction." Here the word is rendered "destruction" to describe the ultimate fatal consequence of leaving a child without moral discipline.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's song in 1 Samuel 2 sets a major theological theme that echoes through the rest of 1 and 2 Samuel. Kings like Saul attempted to secure their thrones through political violence and human force, yet Saul ultimately perished under God's judgment. Conversely, David was repeatedly rescued from death because his trust was in the LORD who controls life and death.

Furthermore, Hannah's praise provides the structural and thematic model for Mary's hymn of praise (the Magnificat) in Luke 1. Both songs rejoice in the God who overturns human power structures, brings down the mighty, exalts the humble, and demonstrates supreme power over mortality itself.
