UP - (ʻâlâh, Strong's H5927)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'alah basic root meaning is to go up, ascend, or cause to rise up from a lower place to a higher one. In 1 Samuel 2:6, the word describes God's supreme power to raise a person up out of the depths of Sheol (the grave), demonstrating His absolute authority to reverse human hopelessness and restore life.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the geography of ancient Israel, land elevations played a central role in everyday life and thought. Traveling to Jerusalem, climbing to a hilltop sanctuary, or leaving the low river valleys required physical upward movement. Consequently, the standard Hebrew verb for going up or ascending was used constantly in daily speech to describe physical movement from lower to higher terrain.

Hannah speaks this word in her hymn of praise at the tabernacle in Shiloh (1 Samuel 2:1–10). After enduring years of childlessness, social reproach, and deep sorrow, God answered her prayer by giving her a son, Samuel. When she brought Samuel to the house of the LORD, she broke into a song that celebrated God's power to turn human circumstances upside down. In this setting, physical concepts like going down into a pit or coming up out of a valley provided a vivid picture of God's power over human life and destiny.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

clear primary sense of the word is to move from a lower position to a higher one, translated as "to go up," "to ascend," or "to climb." When used of objects, it can mean to rise, grow, or spread upwards. When used of people, it describes ascending a hill, going up to a city, or entering a sacred space.

When the verb functions in an active, causative sense, it means "to bring up," "to cause to ascend," or "to lift up" something or someone from a lower location. In concrete historical contexts, it refers to bringing people up out of a pit, leading a nation up out of captivity, or presenting a sacrifice on an altar. In non-physical settings, it describes God restoring a person from extreme distress, weakness, or the threat of death. In 1 Samuel 2:6, the word carries this causative meaning: God is the one who brings people up from the depths of the grave.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

across the Old Testament, the word appears in several key settings:

  • Spatial and geographical movement: It describes individuals climbing mountains, entering cities built on hills, or traveling upward toward a sanctuary.

  • Corporate restoration: It describes God leading His covenant people out of bondage or exile and bringing them up into a place of freedom and safety.

  • Worship and sacrifice: It describes ascending to God's presence on a holy mountain or bringing up offerings that rise toward heaven.

  • Life and death contrast: Paired with words for going down, it describes the movement between destruction and rescue.

In 1 Samuel 2:6, the word is directly paired with its natural opposite, "brings down." By placing these two actions side by side, the passage establishes the total boundary of divine authority. God brings down to the grave, and He brings up.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's song uses this word to articulate key truths about God's character and sovereignty:

First, God possesses exclusive authority over life and death. Death is not an independent power operating outside of God's control. Sheol (the grave) cannot hold anyone whom God determines to lift out of it.

Second, God routinely works through divine reversals. Human society often values power, fertility, wealth, and status, while viewing weakness and death as final. Hannah declares that God routinely reverses these outcomes: He humbles the proud and lifts up the helpless.

Third, the pattern of bringing someone up out of the grave prefigures the broader biblical hope of resurrection and deliverance. While full physical resurrection is fully revealed in the New Testament, the Old Testament repeatedly emphasizes that God's power reaches down into the lowest depths to pull His people back into life.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:6, Hannah says, "The LORD kills and makes alive; He brings down to the grave and brings up." The grammatical form presents a narrative sequence describing God's characteristic, ongoing actions.

Hannah is not offering a cold philosophical speech on divine power. Her words spring from her lived experience. Humanly speaking, her barrenness was a form of social and relational death. Her opponent taunted her, and her heart was grieved. Yet God intervened, giving her a child and lifting her out of her misery.

When Hannah sings that God "brings up," she looks beyond her own situation to state a general truth about how God governs the world. The grave represents the absolute end of human strength. Once a person goes down into the grave, no human power can reverse the transition. By declaring that God "brings up" from the grave, Hannah highlights that God's power begins precisely where human ability completely ends.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 24:18 — "So Moses went into the midst of the cloud and went up into the mountain. And Moses was on the mountain forty days and forty nights." Here the word describes Moses physically ascending Mount Sinai into the glorious presence of God to receive the law.

  • Psalms 68:18 — "You have ascended on high, You have led captivity captive; You have received gifts among men." The word describes a triumphant upward ascent, picturing God's victorious enthronement over His enemies.

  • Jeremiah 16:15 — "'but, "The LORD lives who brought up the children of Israel from the land of the north and from all the lands where He had driven them."' Here the word describes God's powerful work of gathering His scattered people from exile and restoring them to their land.

  • Ezra 1:11 — "All the articles of gold and silver were five thousand four hundred. All these Sheshbazzar took up with those of the captivity who were brought from Babylon to Jerusalem." The rendering "took up" (or brought up) describes bringing the holy vessels and exiled people back up to Jerusalem to rebuild the temple.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The theme of divine reversal in Hannah's song directly shaped later biblical worship. Centuries later, Mary, the mother of Jesus, offered a remarkably similar song of praise known as the Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55). Mary echoed Hannah's language, praising God for taking down the mighty from their thrones and exalting the lowly.

Furthermore, the pairing of bringing down to the grave and bringing up provides a foundation for understanding God's salvation. In human experience, the ultimate low point is death and the grave. By establishing that God has the authority to bring people up out of that final place, the Old Testament prepares believers to recognize God's supreme power displayed in the resurrection of Jesus Christ.
