LOW - (shâphêl, Strong's H8213)

Summary:

The word shaphel means to bring down, abate, make low, or humble. It describes the reduction of height, status, or self-exaltation. In 1 Samuel 2:7, the word highlights God's absolute power to lower those who rely on worldly position or pride, contrasting His action of bringing someone low with His divine power to lift up the needy.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The song of Hannah in 1 Samuel 2 forms the structural and theological foundation for the books of Samuel. Set during a time of moral decay and leadership transition in Israel, the narrative opens with Hannah, a woman grieving her barrenness and enduring the provocation of her rival. When God answers her prayer and gives her a son, Samuel, Hannah responds not merely with personal gratitude, but with a prophetic hymn celebrating God's holy character and sovereign rule.

Hannah's song reflects a grand spiritual pattern: human status is unstable, human power is limited, and God regularly reverses human expectations. Within this prayer, shaphel appears as part of a series of contrasting divine actions that show God's ultimate authority over wealth, poverty, life, death, weakness, and honor.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shaphel fundamentally means to make lower, to cast down, or to bring to a low condition. In physical descriptions, it refers to lowering high mountains, leveling raised structures, or reducing elevation. In moral, social, and spiritual contexts, it refers to bringing down human pride, stripping away self-sufficiency, or lowering a person's social standing.

When God is the subject performing the action, the word describes His divine intervention against pride and human confidence. God acts to bring low those who elevate themselves above His authority, while simultaneously showing favor to the humble. The term does not imply arbitrary destruction; rather, it describes God's righteous authority setting right human self-exaltation.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament poetry, prophecy, and wisdom literature, shaphel is frequently paired in direct contrast with words meaning to exalt, raise up, or lift high. This structural pairing creates a clear rhetorical contrast between divine humbling and divine elevation.

The word is frequently applied to:

  • High places, city walls, and lofty trees that are brought down by divine judgment.

  • Proud rulers, oppressive nations, and arrogant individuals who trust in military might or wealth.

  • The inner posture of the heart, where God humbles human haughtiness so that He alone is exalted.

The word emphasizes that human position is neither self-made nor self-sustained. Earthly power and high status exist only under God's sovereign oversight, and He can reduce them in an instant.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of shaphel reveals foundational truths about God's governance of the world. First, God alone holds ultimate authority over human circumstances. No person achieves high standing apart from His allowance, and no elevated status can resist His sovereign decree.

Second, God actively opposes human pride. Human self-exaltation attempts to claim the glory that belongs exclusively to God. By bringing low what is proud, God demonstrates His holiness and exposes the frailty of human arrogance.

Third, God's action of bringing low serves a constructive, righteous purpose within redemptive history. It makes room for divine grace. By humbling the proud and elevating the needy, God establishes a moral order where trust is placed in Him rather than in earthly power, wisdom, or wealth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:7, Hannah proclaims: "The LORD makes poor and makes rich; He brings low and lifts up." Here, shaphel works alongside the phrase "makes poor" and directly opposes "lifts up."

Grammatically, the word appears as a masculine singular participle, presenting God as the active, ongoing subject whose characteristic work includes humbling human pride. The immediate context reflects Hannah's personal experience. Her rival Peninnah had treated her with contempt because of her barrenness, relying on her own fertile status. Yet God reversed their conditions, giving the barren woman children and silencing the boastful.

This statement establishes a major theme for the entire book of 1 Samuel. Throughout the narrative that follows, God repeatedly brings low those who exalt themselves in pride—such as Eli's corrupt sons, the Philistine idol Dagon, and King Saul—while lifting up those who walk in humility, such as young Samuel and David.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Samuel 22:28 — "You will save the humble people; But Your eyes are on the haughty, that You may bring them down."

David acknowledges in his song of deliverance that God actively observes the proud to bring them down, while extending salvation to the afflicted.

  • Psalms 75:7 — "But God is the Judge: He puts one down, And exalts another."

In the context of divine judgment, God is described as the righteous Judge who humbles the wicked and elevates those He chooses.

  • Psalms 113:6 — "Who humbles Himself to behold The things that are in the heavens and in the earth!"

This psalm uses the word to highlight God's incredible condescension; though supreme over all creation, He stoops down to care for the earth and raise the poor from the dust.

  • Proverbs 29:23 — "A man’s pride will bring him low, But the humble in spirit will retain honor."

Wisdom literature uses the word to illustrate a spiritual law: self-exaltation leads directly to ruin and humiliation.

  • Isaiah 2:12 — "For the day of the LORD of hosts Shall come upon everything proud and lofty, Upon everything lifted up— And it shall be brought low;"

Isaiah uses the term prophetically to describe the coming judgment of God, where every exalted human achievement and arrogant heart will be leveled before God's glory.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Hannah's song in 1 Samuel 2 provides the direct thematic backdrop for Mary's song of praise in Luke 1 (the Magnificat). Mary echoes Hannah's language when she declares that God "has put down the mighty from their thrones, and exalted the lowly."

This theme of divine reversal finds its ultimate fulfillment in Jesus Christ. As Philippians 2 explains, Christ willingly humbled Himself by taking the form of a bondservant and enduring the cross. Because He lowered Himself in perfect obedience, God highly exalted Him. For believers, understanding that God brings low the proud provides both a sobering warning against self-reliance and a comforting assurance that God delights in lifting up the humble.
