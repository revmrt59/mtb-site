UP - (rûwm, Strong's H7311)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb rum fundamentally means to be high, elevated, or raised up, whether physically, metaphorically, or spiritually. Across Scripture, it describes physical height, the lifting up of hands or banners, the act of praising God, the sovereign elevation of individuals to honor, or the sinful arrogance of self-exaltation. In 1 Samuel 2:7, the word refers to God's sovereign act of elevating the humble and poor from lowliness to honor, demonstrating that earthly status and success are ultimately granted by the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

1 Samuel 2 records Hannah's song of praise following the birth and dedication of her son Samuel. For years, Hannah suffered barrenness and bitter provocation from her rival Peninnah. In her deep distress, Hannah prayed silently at the tabernacle in Shiloh, asking God for a male child and promising to dedicate him to the Lord's service. God answered her prayer, and after Samuel was weaned, Hannah brought him to the house of the Lord.

Hannah's song is not merely a personal expression of relief; it is a prophetic hymn celebrating God's character and providence. At the heart of her song is the declaration that God overturns human expectations and earthly power structures. In 1 Samuel 2:7, Hannah declares that the Lord makes poor and makes rich, bringing low and lifting up. This statement introduces the central theme of the entire book of 1 Samuel, where God repeatedly humbles the self-reliant (such as Eli's sons and King Saul) while exalting the humble and faithful (such as Hannah, Samuel, and David).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Rum is a primary Hebrew verb meaning to be high, to rise, or to lift up. The word can refer to physical elevation, such as high mountains, raised hands, or tall structures. When used figuratively, the verb carries several distinct senses depending on who is performing the action and who receives it:

  • When God is the subject lifting up a person, the word means to elevate, promote, rescue, or restore. God raises someone out of suffering, danger, or low social standing into a place of security and honor.

  • When people lift up God, the word means to praise, magnify, or extol His name, acknowledging His supreme majesty.

  • When people lift up themselves, their hearts, or their horns, the word often describes pride, arrogance, or rebellion against God.

In 1 Samuel 2:7, the word is used in the sense of divine promotion and restoration. God is the one who actively raises up those who are downcast or impoverished.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Scripture uses rum across a broad range of contexts:

  • Physical Elevation and Setting Apart: The word describes things raised high in space, such as towering trees, elevated city walls, or offerings lifted up before God at the altar.

  • Praise and Exaltation: In worship, believers call upon one another to exalt the Lord. To lift God up means to proclaim His high position above all creation and earthly powers.

  • Human Pride: A frequent negative usage occurs when human beings lift up their own hearts or eyes in pride. Scripture warns that when a person's heart is lifted up in self-sufficiency, downfall inevitably follows.

  • Sovereign Deliverance and Promotion: God repeatedly presents Himself as the One who raises the fallen. He lifts the needy out of the ash heap and sets them among princes, showing that human elevation is a gift of divine grace rather than a human achievement.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's use of rum in 1 Samuel 2:7 reinforces several crucial biblical truths regarding God's sovereignty and righteousness:

  • Absolute Divine Sovereignty: Wealth, poverty, lowliness, and exaltation are not governed by fate, blind chance, or ultimate human self-determination. God exercises ultimate authority over human circumstances.

  • Divine Reversal: Human society tends to favor the powerful, wealthy, and self-assertive. God's kingdom operates on a different principle. God resists the proud and lifts up those who recognize their need for Him.

  • Protection of the Lowly: God's act of lifting up the poor and humble highlights His justice and compassion. He does not abandon those who suffer under oppression or weakness.

  • Foundation for Redemptive History: This theme of divine reversal runs throughout the Old Testament and finds its ultimate fulfillment in Jesus Christ. Hannah's song serves as a direct prototype for Mary's song of praise in the Gospel of Luke, where Mary celebrates God pulling down rulers from their thrones and exalting the humble.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:7, the word functions as a participle (masculine singular), presenting God as the One who continually performs the work of lifting up. The verse pairs two balanced contrasts: "The LORD makes poor and makes rich; He brings low and lifts up."

This dynamic of bringing low and lifting up explains Hannah's personal experience. She was low—mocked, grieving, and childless—yet God lifted her up by giving her a son and placing her among the honored mothers in Israel.

Furthermore, this line sets up the narrative arch of 1 Samuel. Eli's house enjoyed high priestly honor but was brought low because of irreverence and greed. Saul was physically tall and lifted up to kingly stature, yet his pride led to his humbling. David, the youngest shepherd boy whom his own family overlooked, was lifted up by God to become king over Israel. The verse establishes that true promotion comes neither from the east nor from the west, but from God alone.

  • Psalms 89:17 — "For You are the glory of their strength, and in Your favor our horn is exalted."

  • Nehemiah 9:5 — "Stand up and bless the LORD your God forever and ever! 'Blessed be Your glorious name, which is exalted above all blessing and praise!'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Image of the Horn: In 1 Samuel 2:1 and 2:10, Hannah speaks of her "horn" being exalted in the Lord. In ancient Near Eastern imagery, an animal's horn represented power, dignity, and strength. To lift up someone's horn means to grant them victory, dignity, and confidence after a period of humiliation.

  • Messianic Foreshadowing: Hannah closes her song in 1 Samuel 2:10 by declaring that God will give strength to His king and exalt the horn of His anointed. This is the first explicit mention of the "Anointed One" (Messiah) as king in the Old Testament, connecting the divine act of lifting up directly to God's messianic promise.
