LIFTS - (rûwm, Strong's H7311)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb rum fundamentally means to be high, to rise, or to elevate. When used in an active sense, it describes lifting something or someone upward—whether physically raising an object, exalting God in worship, or elevating a person to a position of dignity and honor. In 1 Samuel 2:8, the word describes God's sovereign action of raising the impoverished and destitute out of absolute lowliness and restoring them to a place of distinction and honor.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Hannah offers her prayer of praise in 1 Samuel 2 after God grants her request for a child and she presents young Samuel for service at the tabernacle in Shiloh. Her prayer moves quickly from her personal experience of answered prayer to a broader celebration of God's character and rule over human history. In Hannah's culture, childlessness brought intense social stigma and economic vulnerability. Having suffered bitter mockery and helplessness, Hannah recognizes that human strength cannot secure true victory or social standing.

Against this backdrop, Hannah speaks of God as the One who reverses human fortunes. Society often treats the poor and needy as forgotten or fixed in their hardship. Hannah's song declares that earthly rank is subject to divine authority. God brings down the proud and lifts up the lowly. The verb rum anchors this theme of divine elevation, showing that true exaltation comes not from human ambition or social standing, but from God's personal intervention.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of rum is to be high, elevated, or exalted. Depending on how it is used in a sentence, the word can describe physical height, a person's social standing, or an act of worship.

When applied to objects or physical creation, the word refers to spatial height, such as floodwaters rising or a signal flag set upon a hill. When applied to people, it can have either a negative or positive meaning. Negatively, it can describe human pride or a haughty heart that exalts itself above others. Positively, when God is the subject, the word describes His action of rescuing, honoring, or raising people out of degradation. When people are the subject in relation to God, it means praising or extolling His supreme name.

In 1 Samuel 2:8, the word expresses God's active work of elevating the helpless. The verse pairs rum with the image of the ash heap—the public dump outside a town where outcasts gathered—to show a dramatic change of position from total poverty to honor.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Scripture, rum appears in several distinct settings:

  • Physical Elevation: The word describes physical height or rising, such as the floodwaters lifting Noah's ark high above the earth in Genesis 7:17.

  • Divine Exaltation in Worship: Worshippers use the word to praise God, recognizing His supreme authority and lifting up His name in songs of thanksgiving (Exodus 15:2; Psalm 30:1).

  • Rescue and Spiritual Deliverance: God lifts up those who are trapped in distress or near death, pulling them out of danger into safety (Psalm 9:13).

  • Social Reversal: God exalts rulers or deposes them according to His sovereign will, lifting the lowly to seats of authority (1 Samuel 2:8).

The word highlights that elevation is not merely a change in physical position, but a complete transformation of condition and dignity.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's song introduces a major theme that runs through the entire Bible: God actively opposes human pride and exalts the humble. The word rum in 1 Samuel 2:8 highlights three key biblical truths:

First, God exercises complete authority over human hierarchies. Human society frequently ranks individuals by wealth, power, and status. Hannah's praise demonstrates that these earthly structures are not ultimate. God can easily demote the self-sufficient and raise up the forgotten.

Second, God's mercy focuses on the helpless. The needy person on the ash heap has no social leverage, influence, or power to change their situation. When God lifts such a person, the deliverance is visibly an act of pure divine grace rather than human merit.

Third, this theme of divine reversal points forward to the gospel. Hannah's song provides the theological pattern for Mary's song in Luke 1, where Mary celebrates God filling the hungry and exalting the humble. Ultimately, Jesus Christ embodies this reversal by humbling Himself to death on a cross so that sinners might be raised to glory with Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:8, rum works alongside parallel poetic lines to create a vivid picture of divine transformation. The verse pairs two actions: God "raises" the poor from the dust and "lifts" the beggar from the ash heap. In the ancient Near East, the ash heap was where discarded waste was dumped and where the poorest outcasts sat to beg. By placing rum in contrast with the ash heap, Hannah highlights the vast distance between human degradation and divine exaltation.

God does not merely grant slight relief to the poor; He lifts them all the way to sit among princes and inherit a throne of glory. The action reflects a characteristic pattern of God's sovereign rule. The reversal is complete and purposeful. Hannah herself experienced a personal version of this lifting when God remembered her affliction and granted her a son, but she recognizes that her story reflects how God governs the world.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The imagery of lifting someone from the dust or ash heap carries deep cultural meaning in the ancient world. Dust and ashes symbolized mourning, poverty, disease, and social isolation. To be seated on an ash heap meant a person was entirely dependent on charity and excluded from community life.

When God lifts a person from that state, He restores both their life and their rightful place in the community. This poetic imagery reassures believers that no situation is beyond God's power to transform. His grace reaches to the lowest places of human suffering to bring about restoration and honor.
