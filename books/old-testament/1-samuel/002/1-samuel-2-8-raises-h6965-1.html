RAISES - (qûwm, Strong's H6965)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qum basic meaning is to stand up, arise, or cause something to rise. In its causative sense, it describes lifting up someone who is low, setting something upright, or establishing a person or decree in a position of stability. In 1 Samuel 2:8, the word describes God actively lifting the poor and needy out of complete degradation and placing them in a position of honor among leaders.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Hannah speaks these words in her song of praise after bringing her young son Samuel to the tabernacle at Shiloh. For years, Hannah suffered the pain of childlessness and the public mockery of her rival. When the Lord remembered her prayer and gave her a son, her heart overflowed with gratitude. Rather than focusing only on her personal joy, Hannah recognized that her experience reflected a larger truth about who God is. Her song celebrates God's sovereign control over human circumstances. In 1 Samuel 2:8, she uses the word qum to describe how God routinely turns human expectations upside down by elevating the forgotten and weak.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Qum is a common Hebrew verb that means to arise, stand up, or get up. In its simplest sense, it describes a basic physical movement, such as a person standing up from a seated or reclining position.

When the verb describes an action performed toward another, it shifts from standing up oneself to lifting up or establishing someone or something else. In this sense, the word can mean:

  • To raise or lift up a person who is low or falling.

  • To set up or erect a structure, pillar, or altar.

  • To establish, confirm, or fulfill a covenant, oath, or promise.

  • To bring a leader or nation onto the public stage.

In 1 Samuel 2:8, the word describes God as the one who lifts up or raises the poor out of dust and degradation.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, qum appears across many different settings. In narrative, it describes ordinary human actions, such as getting up in the morning or standing up to travel. In judicial and civic settings, the word describes standing firm in judgment or rising up to give testimony.

When used of God's actions toward people, qum highlights divine initiative and authority. People often attempt to exalt themselves through pride, wealth, or political power, but Scripture emphasizes that true exaltation comes from God. Whether God is establishing His covenant, fulfilling a prophetic word, or raising up a deliverer for His people, the verb stresses that God makes firm what was previously low, weak, or unrealized. The helpless do not lift themselves out of the dust; God actively intervenes to raise them.

4. Theological Implications

Hannah's use of qum highlights several key theological truths about God's character and governance:

  • Divine sovereignty over human status: Human power and social hierarchy are not ultimate. God possesses the absolute right to bring down the proud and lift up the humble.

  • God's compassion for the lowly: God actively attends to those in extreme poverty and distress. He is not indifferent to those trapped in the lowest conditions of life.

  • Salvation as divine action: Just as the poor cannot raise themselves to sit among princes, humanity cannot rescue itself from spiritual helplessness. Deliverance requires the active, initiating power of God.

This pattern of divine reversal runs throughout the biblical storyline, pointing forward to the ultimate lifting up of the lowly through the work of God in redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:8, the word functions grammatically as a participle describing an ongoing, characteristic action of God: He is "the one who raises." This indicates that lifting up the needy is not merely an isolated miracle, but part of God's enduring character.

The contrast in the verse underscores the magnitude of God's action. The "poor" and "needy" are pictured in the "dust" and the "ash heap"—the lowest possible places in ancient society, representing extreme poverty, grief, and social exclusion. God does not merely offer them minor relief; He raises them all the way from the ash heap to "sit among princes" and inherit a seat of honor.

Hannah grounds this astounding power in God's creation. The end of verse 8 notes that "the pillars of the earth are the Lord’s, and He has set the world upon them." Because God created and sustains the physical foundations of the earth, He has complete authority to order and reorder human society according to His righteous will.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 27:43 — "arise"

Rebekah instructs Jacob to "arise" and flee to Haran, illustrating the standard use of the word for getting up to take action.

  • 1 Kings 11:14 — "up"

The text states that the Lord raised "up" an adversary against Solomon, demonstrating how God brings historical actors onto the public stage.

  • Psalms 1:5 — "stand"

The psalm declares that "the ungodly shall not stand in the judgment," using the word to describe remaining upright and vindicated before God.

  • Psalms 78:5 — "established"

The psalmist records that God "established a testimony in Jacob," showing how the word describes setting up a covenant witness that remains firm.

  • Jeremiah 28:6 — "perform"

Jeremiah prays that the Lord would "perform" the words of prophecy, using the verb for bringing spoken promises into actual fulfillment.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Hannah's celebration of God raising the poor provides the thematic blueprint for the entire book of 1 Samuel. Throughout the book, God repeatedly lowers those who rely on earthly power (such as Eli's household and King Saul) and raises up the humble (such as Samuel and David).

  • This passage forms a direct parallel with Mary's song of praise in Luke 1:46–55. Mary draws directly on Hannah's imagery to rejoice that God has brought down the mighty from their thrones and exalted the lowly.
