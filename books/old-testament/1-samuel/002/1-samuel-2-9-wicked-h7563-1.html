WICKED - (râshâʻ, Strong's H7563)

Summary:

The Hebrew word rasha refers to a person who is guilty, morally corrupt, or actively living in rebellion against God's law. In judicial contexts, it describes someone who stands condemned before a judge, while in poetic and wisdom writings, it describes those whose lives display hostility toward God and His standards. In 1 Samuel 2:9, the word describes those who oppose God's righteous rule, emphasizing that despite their temporary strength or confidence, God will ultimately silence them in judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word rasha is rooted in Old Testament legal and moral life. In the legal setting of ancient Israel, a judge used this term to declare a person guilty of a crime or covenant violation. Outside the courtroom, Israel's prophets and poets used the word to describe people whose character and choices were marked by self-reliance, injustice, and disregard for God.

In 1 Samuel 2, Hannah speaks this word as part of her famous prayer of thanksgiving. After years of barrenness and mistreatment, God answered her prayer by giving her a son, Samuel. As she presents Samuel to the LORD at Shiloh, her heart erupts in praise. Her song is not merely about her personal relief; it is a prophetic celebration of how God operates in history. She declares that God humbles the proud and lifts up the needy. Within this setting, rasha represents all who rely on human power and defy God's authority.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word rasha identifies a person who is wrong, morally guilty, or living in defiance of God's revealed will. It stands in direct contrast to the Hebrew word for righteous (tzaddik).

The word can refer to several related concepts:

  • A person who is legally guilty in a judicial setting.

  • An ungodly person whose life is marked by evil deeds and oppression of others.

  • Anyone who consciously rejects God's commands and acts as though God does not rule.

Rather than describing an occasional mistake, the term usually points to a moral condition or standing. It marks a person who is out of alignment with God's justice and truth.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, rasha is used frequently in wisdom literature, the Psalms, and the prophets to contrast two basic trajectories of human life: the way of the righteous and the way of the wicked.

In the Law, God forbids judges from justifying the rasha or taking bribes to clear the guilty. In the Psalms, the word describes those who plot against the innocent, trust in their wealth, and forget God. In the Proverbs, the term highlights the destructive consequences of evil living, showing that the pathway of the wicked leads to ruin.

Scripture does not present the wicked as merely weak or mistaken; they are viewed as actively pursuing a life opposed to God's kingdom. Yet Scripture also consistently reveals that their apparent success is temporary and unstable.

4. Theological Implications

The biblically defined rasha reveals important truths about God's character and human responsibility:

  • God is the supreme Judge: Moral standards are defined by God's character, not human opinion. Those who violate His standards stand accountable before Him.

  • Human power is insufficient: Human strength, political influence, or personal confidence cannot protect a person from divine judgment.

  • God's ultimate justice: Though the wicked often seem to prosper for a time, God promises that justice will prevail. His kingdom will endure, while rebellion will be brought to an end.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In 1 Samuel 2:9, Hannah places two groups side by side: God's faithful holy ones ("saints") and the "wicked." She declares, "He will guard the feet of His saints, but the wicked shall be silent in darkness."

The contrast is striking. While God protects the path of those who trust Him, those who live in rebellion will find their voice and confidence taken away. To be "silent in darkness" pictures absolute defeat, confusion, and judgment. The wicked often speak arrogantly and act with high-handed confidence, but before God's judgment, their proud speeches cease.

Hannah summarizes the reason for this outcome at the end of the verse: "For by strength no man shall prevail." The plural form of the word in this verse highlights that this truth applies to all who rely on human power rather than God. Within the broader story of 1 Samuel, this sets up the downfall of corrupt leaders, such as the sons of Eli, and prepares the reader for how God raises up the humble.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 18:23 — Abraham appeals to God's justice, asking, "Would You also destroy the righteous with the wicked?"

  • Job 36:6 — Elihu describes God's sovereign justice, saying, "He does not preserve the life of the wicked, but gives justice to the oppressed."

  • Psalms 9:16 — David highlights divine judgment, noting, "The LORD is known by the judgment He executes; the wicked is snared in the work of his own hands."

  • Proverbs 10:16 — Solomon contrasts the outcomes of two ways of life: "The labor of the righteous leads to life, the wages of the wicked to sin."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In 1 Samuel 2:9, the word appears in the masculine plural form, addressing the wicked as a collective group. Hannah is not focusing merely on one specific enemy, but on the general principle that all who align themselves against God will face the same outcome.

Her prayer provides a foundational theme for the rest of the Books of Samuel and echoes throughout the rest of the Bible. It anticipates the teaching of the Psalms and the prophets, demonstrating that true security is found only in God's protective care, not in human might.
