EG Culture - 1 Samuel 2

HONOR || Through His Eyes

Culture Statement

Relationships are protected when we ascribe heaven’s value to the people around us. Like Jesus, we choose to interact with people, not as they deserve, but according to their God given value.

Living It in 1 Samuel 2

Honor is a central issue in this chapter, and it appears in more than one way.

Giving God His rightful place. God’s verdict on Eli’s household is stated in terms of honor: “you … honor your sons more than Me” (2:29), and then, “those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed” (2:30). The Hebrew sets kabed (heavy, weighty) against qalal (light). To honor God is to give Him weight when other loyalties compete. Hophni and Phinehas gave God no weight at all: they took His portion before it was offered (2:15–16) and treated His offering with contempt (2:29). In this chapter, honor for people cannot be separated from honor for God, because the first thing that goes wrong is that God is made light.

Treating people as if they existed for our benefit. Eli’s sons treated the people who came to worship as a source of meat. They took from their pots, demanded their portion before God’s, and threatened force when a worshiper objected (2:13–16). God calls those worshipers “Israel My people” (2:29), and Eli himself says they are “the LORD’S people” (2:24). They were not the priests’ to use. The text also names sexual immorality with the women who served at the tabernacle (2:22). It does not tell us whether those women were willing or were pressured, but where sexual misconduct joins a large difference in power, honor means protecting the vulnerable rather than the reputation of the powerful.

Misplaced honor. Eli was not a cruel man. He rebuked his sons, but he did not restrain them, and God says the reason lay in whom he honored. The text does not state Eli’s inner motive, but it appears that his attachment to his sons made it hard to act against them when obedience required it. Love for family, loyalty to a position, or the wish to keep the peace can become reasons to leave God and His people unprotected. Honor that will not act when someone is being harmed is not the honor of heaven.

Honor through God’s eyes. Hannah’s song shows what heaven’s value looks like. God “raises the poor from the dust and lifts the beggar from the ash heap, to set them among princes” (2:8). The world’s ranking of who matters is not His. The chapter therefore gives us a picture of Honor at East Gate: people are valued according to what God says about them, whether they sit on a refuse heap, hold a priestly office, or are a child in a linen ephod, and God Himself honors those who honor Him.

HEALTH || Trickle Down

Culture Statement

We are healthy when our thoughts, feelings, and desires reflect God’s. When we care for our spiritual, physical, financial, emotional, and interpersonal health, we are empowered to influence our communities for the better. Health flows from the top down and from the inside out.

Living It in 1 Samuel 2

The condition of Eli’s household shows how unhealthiness can flow outward from the top. The chapter is specific about what kind of health was missing.

Spiritual health, from the inside out. Verse 12 gives the diagnosis: Hophni and Phinehas “did not know the LORD.” Verses 13–17 and 22 show the symptoms. Their desires were governed by appetite rather than by reverence, and it showed in how they handled sacrifice, authority, and sexuality. Samuel, by contrast, “ministered to the LORD” and “grew before the LORD” (2:11, 18, 21). The text distinguishes the root from the fruit, which is exactly the order of “from the inside out.”

Leadership health, from the top down. Eli’s household is a picture of a leader who knows what is wrong and does not act. He confronted his sons but did not restrain them (2:22–25; 3:13), and the damage reached their own family, the priesthood, and the worshiping community, where people’s relationship to the offering was harmed (2:17, 24). The consequences of his failure would reach later generations of his house, though each person remains responsible for himself. Health that flows from the top down also means that what a leader tolerates shapes the people below him.

Relational and emotional health. Samuel grew “in stature, and in favor both with the LORD and men” (2:26). His relationship with God and his relationships with people were growing together, and the order matters. Healthy influence begins in a life that knows God and increasingly reflects His ways. The chapter does not say Samuel’s growth was easy or that a corrupt environment is harmless, and it does not treat unhealthy environments as something to accept. It shows that a life oriented toward the LORD can keep growing even when the people above it are failing.

GENEROSITY || Its Not Ours Anyway

Culture Statement

We recognize that every resource we’ve been given comes from our generous Father. We choose to be just like Dad, and give as He leads.

Living It in 1 Samuel 2

The chapter sets two attitudes toward what belongs to God side by side.

Entitlement. Eli’s sons treated the Lord’s offerings as though they belonged to them. God had provided for the priests: “Did I not give to the house of your father all the offerings of the children of Israel made by fire?” (2:28). Instead of receiving that provision, they took portions they wanted, demanded them before the fat was burned to the LORD, and threatened force (2:13–16). Their sin was not need but dissatisfaction with what God had given. What God provides is never ours to seize, manipulate, or use without regard for Him. The repeated “My” in verses 28–29 (My priest, My altar, My sacrifice, My offering, My people) is God’s reminder that these things were never theirs.

Open-handed giving. Hannah gave Samuel to the LORD because she had promised to, and she kept her vow (1:11, 28). She sang on the day she left him (2:1), and she kept bringing a robe each year (2:19). The text does not present her giving as an investment. God gave her more children (2:21), but that was His grace and not payment, and the passage gives no formula in which giving to God obligates Him to give back. First Samuel 2 reminds us that generosity begins by recognizing that what we have comes from God and remains under His authority, and that we give as He leads, not to obtain a return.

EMPOWERMENT || Model and Invite

Culture Statement

Humility is the key that unlocks empowerment at East Gate. Feedback is the doorway through which we mature, as we intentionally submit and embrace opportunities for growth. When we choose to be teachable and correctable, we are empowered to fulfill every good and perfect work.

Living It in 1 Samuel 2

First Samuel 2 shows both what is lost when correction is refused and what is possible when a young person is allowed to serve and grow.

Refusing correction. Eli’s warning to his sons was direct: “No, my sons! … You make the LORD’S people transgress” (2:24). The narrator says, “they did not heed the voice of their father” (2:25). The word for “heed” (shama‘) is hearing that leads to obedience. Hophni and Phinehas had heard, but they would not be corrected. Their story ends in judgment, and it does so after a long refusal. The chapter does not describe a leader who was corrected and grew. It describes a household in which correction was ignored, and the result was the loss of the office they held (2:30–36).

Correction reaches everyone. When Eli’s own correction did not go far enough, God sent “a man of God” to Eli himself (2:27). No position in God’s house, including that of the senior priest, places a person beyond correction. Empowerment that is safe rests on leaders who remain correctable.

Invited to serve, and growing. Samuel is a picture of the other side. He was a child, yet he “ministered before the LORD” in a linen ephod (2:18), and he grew steadily “in stature, and in favor both with the LORD and men” (2:26). He was given real responsibility at his stage of life, and he grew into more. Hannah’s yearly visits and Eli’s blessing (2:19–20) show the adults around him also playing their part. The chapter does not present Samuel’s teachability in so many words, so this connection should be held as an illustration of a young person being invited into real service and growing, and not as a claim about his inner attitude.

We are People of the Word

Culture Statement

The Holy Bible is the only infallible and final authority for Christian faith and life. The Holy Scriptures are timeless, inerrant and are to be read personally, discerned corporately, and walked out in community. We interpret all Scripture through the life and teachings of Jesus, trusting Holy Spirit to reveal truth and transform us as we submit ourselves fully to God’s revealed Word.

Living It in 1 Samuel 2

The chapter shows what happens when custom replaces commandment, and what happens when a person stays anchored in what God has said.

Custom against commandment. The priests’ “custom” (mishpat) with the people was to send a servant with a three-pronged fork to take whatever came up (2:13–14). God’s law had already said which portions belonged to the priests, and that the fat belonged to the LORD and must be burned first (Leviticus 3:16; 7:28–34). Eli’s sons replaced God’s instructions with a procedure that served themselves. “The way we have always done it” does not make something right. The standard remains what God has said.

A worshiper who held to the Word. When the servant demanded meat, an ordinary Israelite answered, “They should really burn the fat first” (2:16). He knew what God required and said so, even under threat of force. The narrator sets his clarity against the priest’s. Being people of the Word means that God’s instructions are not the property of those in office, and that faithfulness to what God has said can come from anyone.

Hannah’s song is steeped in Scripture. Her prayer draws on the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32), for example in “The LORD kills and makes alive” (2:6; Deuteronomy 32:39) and in the title “Rock” (2:2). Her worship is shaped by what God has already said about Himself, and it is one of the reasons her song carries such weight in the rest of the Bible.

God’s word comes to pass. The man of God speaks, “Thus says the LORD” (2:27), and the sign he gives, that both sons will die on the same day (2:34), is fulfilled in chapter 4. God’s warnings are as sure as His promises.

EXCELLENCE || Your Best, His Glory

Culture Statement

We choose to do the best we can, with what we have, even when no one is watching. Our aim is progress, not perfection. We pursue excellence in all things because the Lord is always worthy.

Living It in 1 Samuel 2

The chapter contrasts who receives “the best.”

Eli’s household took “the best of all the offerings of Israel” for themselves (2:29). They wanted the preferred meat, prepared their way, before God’s portion was burned (2:15–16). By contrast, the offering God asked for was first His: the fat had to be burned before anyone ate. Excellence in this chapter is not about performance for its own sake. It is about doing what we do for God and not for ourselves, with God’s portion coming first.

Samuel shows the other side in quiet ways. He was a child “wearing a linen ephod” (2:18), doing whatever service came to him before the LORD, in years when nobody knew who he would become. His growth is described as steady and gradual (2:26). This is progress, not perfection, and the chapter shows it happening “before the LORD,” where he was faithful whether or not others noticed. The connection to excellence is a modest one: the chapter does not describe the quality of Samuel’s work, only the faithfulness of his service, and so it should not be pressed further than that.

We are a Tabernacle of David

Culture Statement

We were created to delight the Father and host His presence. As we embrace His ways, our lives become continual offerings of praise—both personally and together. We live with unhindered access to God, ministering to Him first and carrying His presence wherever we go. As His dwelling is restored among us, we become a people through whom the nations are drawn to seek the Lord.

Living It in 1 Samuel 2

The sanctuary at Shiloh was meant to be a place where God’s people approached Him, and the chapter shows two ways of living in it.

Ministering to Him first. The narrator says Samuel “ministered to the LORD” (2:11) and “ministered before the LORD” (2:18). Ministry began as service to God before it was service to people. Hophni and Phinehas served the sanctuary in a very different way: they “did not know the LORD” (2:12), and their ministry was directed at themselves. The priests had unhindered access to God’s altar and used it to satisfy their appetites.

A path to God that was blocked. Instead of making it easy for people to draw near, the priests made obedience harder (2:15–17, 24). God’s people came to Shiloh to worship, and the people responsible for helping them approach God were making it more difficult. The chapter shows how serious this is: “the sin of the young men was very great before the LORD” (2:17). A people who host God’s presence must protect the way to Him for others.

Lives as offerings of praise. Hannah’s song is a picture of worship offered from a whole life. She sings on the day she surrenders her son, rejoicing “in the LORD” rather than in the gift (2:1). Her song does not come from ease, but from the God who lifted her, and it is offered in the sanctuary itself. God’s presence, the chapter warns, is His gift and not the guaranteed property of a corrupt priesthood; within a few chapters the ark is captured and Shiloh ceases to be the center of Israel’s worship.
