Chapter Study

1 Samuel 2

Verse-by-Verse
1 Samuel 2:1

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:1 And Hannah prayed and said: "My heart rejoices in the LORD; My horn is exalted in the LORD. I smile at my enemies, Because I rejoice in Your salvation.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:1 Then Hannah prayed: “My heart rejoices in the LORD!The LORD has made me strong.Now I have an answer for my enemies;I rejoice because you rescued me. (NLT)

Main Idea

Having just given Samuel back to the LORD, Hannah rejoices not mainly in the son she received but in the God who saw her, heard her, and lifted her from humiliation. Her deepest joy has moved from the gift to the Giver.

Verse Explanation

This is Hannah’s prayer after bringing Samuel to Shiloh and giving him to the LORD, just as she had promised. What makes the verse striking is its timing. Hannah has just surrendered the son for whom she had prayed so desperately, and yet her dominant response is not grief over what she has given up. It is joy in the God who answered her.

Hannah says, “My heart rejoices in the LORD.” She certainly rejoices that God gave her a son, but her joy is not finally centered on Samuel. Her heart rejoices “in the LORD.” Her experience has moved her attention from the gift to the Giver. In chapter 1 we watched Hannah pour out her anguished soul before God. Now the same heart that was so deeply grieved is rejoicing in Him.

“My horn is exalted in the LORD” pictures strength and dignity restored. An animal raises its horn when it stands strong rather than defeated. Hannah had been humiliated and provoked by Peninnah because she was barren; now she can lift her head again. But the repeated phrase matters: “in the LORD.” Hannah is not boasting in herself, or even simply in the fact that she now has a child. God has lifted her from humiliation and given her renewed strength and standing.

“I smile at my enemies” means that Hannah can now speak with confidence in the face of those who had mocked or opposed her. The line almost certainly brings Peninnah to mind, since 1 Samuel 1:6 says her rival repeatedly provoked her over her barrenness. Hannah had once wept and could not eat. Now God’s intervention has reversed her situation, and she no longer stands silenced and humiliated before her adversary.

Yet Hannah is not celebrating a personal victory over Peninnah. She immediately explains why she can speak confidently: “Because I rejoice in Your salvation.” Her attention stays on what God has done. “Salvation” here carries the broad Old Testament sense of deliverance or rescue. Hannah has experienced God as the One who saw her affliction, heard her prayer, remembered her, and changed her circumstances.

Already, though, the prayer is becoming larger than Hannah’s personal story. The verses that follow move from her individual experience to a sweeping declaration about the character and government of God: He brings down the proud and raises the lowly, He makes poor and makes rich, He guards His saints and judges His adversaries (2:3–10). In her own life Hannah has recognized something much larger about who God is and how He works.

Study Notes

  • Prayed (Hebrew palal, H6419): the same verb used for Hannah’s anguished prayer in 1:10, 12, 26–27. What follows is poetry, a song of praise, but the narrator still calls it prayer. Worship and prayer flow from the same relationship.

  • Rejoices (Hebrew ‘alats, H5970): to exult or rejoice triumphantly. The object of the verb is the LORD, not Samuel.

  • Horn (Hebrew qeren, H7161): drawn from the wild ox or bull lifting its head in strength. “To exalt the horn” is a common Hebrew idiom for being strengthened, vindicated, or restored to honor (see Psalm 75:10; 89:17, 24; 92:10). The image returns at the end of the song, “exalt the horn of His anointed” (2:10), forming a frame around the whole prayer.

  • I smile at my enemies: the Hebrew reads more literally, “my mouth is enlarged (or opened wide) over my enemies” (rachab, H7337). The idea is not a smirk but freedom to speak. The woman who once could only move her lips silently (1:13) can now open her mouth boldly.

  • Salvation (Hebrew yeshu‘ah, H3444), from the root yasha‘: deliverance, rescue, victory. The Old Testament often pictures such deliverance as being brought into a “broad place” (for example Psalm 18:19; 118:5), but that imagery is a picture of what deliverance feels like, not the strict dictionary meaning of the root. The same root forms the names Joshua and Yeshua (Jesus), “the LORD saves.”

  • Hannah’s song is closely echoed in Mary’s Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55). Both women rejoice in God, celebrate His salvation, and speak of God bringing down the powerful and lifting up the lowly. Compare “My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit has rejoiced in God my Savior” (Luke 1:46–47).

Spirit & Life

  • Joy anchored in the Giver survives the surrender of the gift. Hannah received what she had desperately wanted, gave it back to God, and discovered that her deepest joy was not in what God had given her but in God Himself. The woman who “poured out her soul” in bitterness (1:15) now pours out praise in the same sanctuary, on the very day she leaves her little boy behind. That is the test of our joy: if God asked us to hold His answer with open hands, would our song survive?

  • Let God lift your horn rather than lifting it yourself. Hannah endured years of provocation, yet her song names no rival and settles no score. Her mouth is opened over her enemies because her vindication came from God, not from her own retaliation. The believer who waits for God’s vindication is set free from the exhausting work of defending himself.

  • Salvation makes room. Barrenness had been Hannah’s narrow place, and God’s deliverance brought her out into breathing space. Every rescue God gives His people is a small picture of the great salvation He would one day give in His Son, whose very name, Yeshua, means “the LORD saves.”

  • Hannah’s personal deliverance becomes part of a larger biblical pattern. Her song anticipates Mary’s, and both point toward God’s saving work through His Messiah. The God who lifted one grieving woman in Shiloh is the God who would one day lift the lowly through Jesus Christ.

1 Samuel 2:2

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:2 "No one is holy like the LORD, For there is none besides You, Nor is there any rock like our God.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:2 No one is holy like the LORD!There is no one besides you;there is no Rock like our God. (NLT)

Main Idea

Hannah moves from celebrating what God has done to declaring who He is: utterly holy, without rival, and as unchanging as a rock. Her circumstances changed dramatically, but the God she trusted did not.

Verse Explanation

Hannah moves from celebrating what God has done for her in verse 1 to declaring who God is in verse 2. Her answered prayer has led her into a deeper recognition of God’s character, and she emphasizes three realities about Him: His holiness, His uniqueness, and His stability.

“No one is holy like the LORD.” Holiness certainly includes God’s absolute moral purity, but here it means more than that God never sins. To be holy is to be utterly set apart and distinct. God belongs in no category with anyone else. He is not merely better than others; He is unlike all others.

That is especially meaningful coming from Hannah. She has discovered that the LORD cannot be measured by what people see at a particular moment. In chapter 1 Peninnah had children while Hannah was barren. From outward appearances Peninnah looked favored and Hannah looked forgotten. But Hannah learned that circumstances do not define God. He remains holy, sovereign, faithful, and completely unlike anyone else.

“For there is none besides You” explains the uniqueness behind God’s holiness. Hannah is not saying that God holds first place among many competing gods. She is confessing that there is no one else like the LORD. No rival possesses His character, authority, or power. This begins to widen Hannah’s prayer beyond her own situation. Her God is not simply the God who happened to answer one woman’s prayer; He is the incomparable LORD who rules over everything.

“Nor is there any rock like our God.” A rock pictures strength, stability, security, and dependability. It does not move when everything around it changes. Hannah’s life had been anything but stable: barrenness, Peninnah’s provocation, tears, unanswered prayer, and then the birth and surrender of Samuel. Her circumstances changed dramatically, but she discovered that God did not change with them.

Notice also the movement from “You” to “our God.” Hannah’s testimony is personal, but the truth she has discovered is not private. The God who proved faithful to Hannah is the God on whom all His people can depend.

Verses 1 and 2 belong together. Verse 1 says, “I rejoice in the LORD.” Verse 2 explains why the LORD is worthy of that joy. Hannah’s confession carries special weight because she learned it while passing through circumstances she could not understand. God proved to be her Rock before He changed her circumstances, while she waited, and after He answered.

Study Notes

  • Holy (Hebrew qadosh, H6918): set apart, distinct, other. The word includes moral purity but centers on God’s absolute distinctness from all created things.

  • None besides You: a strong confession of the LORD’s uniqueness, in line with Deuteronomy 4:35, 39 and Isaiah 45:5–6. Hannah is not ranking the LORD above other gods; she denies that any true rival exists.

  • Rock (Hebrew tsur, H6697): a favorite title for God in Moses’ song, “He is the Rock, His work is perfect” (Deuteronomy 32:4; see also 32:15, 18, 30–31), where it speaks of His changeless faithfulness. David’s song near the end of 2 Samuel returns to the same title (“The LORD is my rock and my fortress,” 2 Samuel 22:2–3, 32), so that the books of Samuel are framed by two songs that celebrate the LORD as Rock.

  • The shift from second person (“You”) to first person plural (“our God”) is common in Hebrew poetry and moves the song from private thanksgiving toward shared confession.

Spirit & Life

  • Praise who God is before you praise what God gave. Hannah’s first great confession is not “He gave me a son” but “No one is holy like the LORD.” She has a child in her arms, yet her song is filled with God. Worship that only celebrates benefits quietly turns God into a means rather than the end. God’s best gift is God Himself.

  • Our circumstances can change dramatically while the character of God remains unchanged. The God Hannah cried to when she was barren is the same God she worships after Samuel is born. Her confidence rests not finally in the answer she received but in the unchanging God who gave it. That is solid ground for anyone who is still waiting: God is already the Rock before the answer comes.

  • Hannah’s private discovery becomes a public confession: “our God.” What God teaches us in hidden seasons is rarely meant to stay private. Testimony of His faithfulness strengthens the whole people of God.

1 Samuel 2:3

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:3 "Talk no more so very proudly; (H1364) Let no arrogance come from your mouth, For the LORD is the God of knowledge; And by Him actions are weighed.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:3 “Stop acting so proud and haughty!Don’t speak with such arrogance!For the LORD is a God who knows what you have done;he will judge your actions. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because the LORD alone is exalted and knows everything, Hannah warns against proud, boastful speech. God sees what we cannot see and weighs every action accurately, so present circumstances are never His final verdict about a person.

Verse Explanation

Hannah now moves from praising God to giving a warning. Verse 2 declared that the LORD is incomparable. Verse 3 draws out one implication: if God alone is exalted, human pride has no legitimate place before Him.

“Talk no more so very proudly.” Hannah has personal experience with proud speech. Peninnah had repeatedly provoked her because of her barrenness (1:6–7), so it is natural to hear an echo of Peninnah here. But Hannah does not name her, and the statement is broader than one woman. Her personal experience has taught her a universal truth about pride before God.

The wording is emphatic. Hannah is confronting speech that exalts ourselves because we think our present position proves our superiority. Peninnah had children and Hannah did not; from outward appearances Peninnah had reason to boast. Then God reversed Hannah’s circumstances. That reversal exposes the foolishness of pride. We see the present; God knows the whole story.

“Let no arrogance come from your mouth.” Arrogance is pride expressed through speech. What is in the heart eventually comes out through the mouth. Hannah had experienced words designed to make her feel small, and now she warns against speaking as though our strength, success, position, or circumstances make us superior to someone else.

This becomes a major theme throughout 1 Samuel. People repeatedly misjudge situations because they evaluate by outward appearances. Eli first misjudged Hannah. Later Samuel will misjudge which of Jesse’s sons God has chosen, and God will tell him, “for man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart” (16:7).

“For the LORD is the God of knowledge” explains why arrogant speech is so foolish. God knows the heart behind our words. He knows the circumstances we cannot see. He knows the person being mocked as completely as He knows the person doing the mocking. Someone looking at Hannah’s barrenness might have concluded that God had rejected or forgotten her. But God knew Hannah completely, heard the prayer others could not hear, and was already working in ways neither Hannah nor Peninnah could see.

“And by Him actions are weighed.” The picture is of a scale or balance. God does not merely observe human actions; He evaluates them accurately. He sees beneath the action to its true character, motive, and weight. That makes the line both a warning and a comfort. It warns the proud, because God’s evaluation is not controlled by reputation, success, words, or present circumstances. It comforts the humble, because God knows what other people do not know. Hannah did not have to prove herself to Peninnah. God knew her.

This prepares for the great reversals Hannah describes in verses 4–8. God can overturn human circumstances so completely because He sees accurately and judges rightly.

Study Notes

  • So very proudly: the Hebrew doubles the word for “high” or “lofty” (gebohah gebohah, from gaboah, H1364), an intensive construction: “lofty, lofty talk.”

  • Arrogance (Hebrew ‘athaq, H6277): speech that is bold, insolent, or overbearing (compare Psalm 31:18; 75:5; 94:4).

  • God of knowledge (Hebrew El de‘ot): the noun “knowledge” (de‘ah, H1844) is plural. The plural is unusual and may emphasize the fullness or variety of God’s knowledge, a “God of all knowledges,” though a plural of this kind should not be pressed mechanically into a technical claim about exhaustive knowing. The context (“by Him actions are weighed”) makes the point clear: nothing escapes His accurate knowledge.

  • Weighed (Hebrew takan, H8505): to measure, estimate, or weigh. The same idea appears in “the LORD weighs the spirits” (Proverbs 16:2; compare 21:2; 24:12).

  • Textual note: the Hebrew consonantal text reads “and not (lo’) are actions weighed,” while the Masoretic scribal note (the qere) reads “and by Him (lo) actions are weighed.” The two words sound alike in Hebrew. Most translations, including the NKJV, follow the qere. Either way the central thought is clear: God possesses perfect knowledge and therefore judges human conduct rightly.

Spirit & Life

  • Every proud word is placed on God’s scales. God knows what people are, not merely what they say, and He deals with them accordingly. Arrogant speech, whether Peninnah’s or ours, never escapes His final evaluation. That is worth pondering whenever we are tempted to speak as if our present position makes us better than someone else.

  • Never mistake someone’s present circumstances for God’s final judgment about that person. God knows what we cannot see, weighs what we cannot measure, and is fully able to reverse what now looks permanent. This frees us from despising those who seem to be losing, and it frees the humble from needing to prove themselves to anyone. Hannah did not have to answer Peninnah; she was already fully known by God.

  • There is deep rest in being known by the God of knowledge. The same God who weighs the proud also sees the silent prayer of the grieving. Hannah’s lips moved without sound in 1:13, and the God of knowledge heard every word.

1 Samuel 2:4

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:4 "The bows of the mighty men are broken, And those who stumbled are girded with strength.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:4 The bow of the mighty is now broken,and those who stumbled are now strong. (NLT)

Main Idea

Hannah begins to describe God’s power to reverse human circumstances: the strong can be broken and the stumbling can be clothed with strength. Human strength is never beyond God’s power to break, and human weakness is never beyond His power to strengthen.

Verse Explanation

Hannah now introduces one of the major themes of her prayer: God is able to reverse human circumstances. Verse 3 warned against pride because the LORD knows everything and weighs human actions. Verse 4 begins to show why no one should place confidence in present strength or assume that another person’s weakness will last forever. This is the first of a series of contrasts that runs through verse 8, and it establishes the pattern the chapter will follow: what appears fixed to us is not fixed to God.

“The bows of the mighty men are broken.” The bow was a key weapon of war and a natural picture of military strength and human power. A mighty warrior with a bow was equipped to fight, defend himself, and overcome an enemy. A broken bow was useless. Hannah’s point is that strength which appears formidable can be brought to nothing when God acts.

The problem is not physical strength, military ability, or human capability in themselves. The problem is confidence in human strength as though it were ultimate. The person who appears powerful today is not beyond God’s authority, and what seems secure from a human perspective can be broken very quickly.

Hannah’s own experience gives these words force. Peninnah had held the stronger position. She had children while Hannah was barren, and she used that advantage to provoke Hannah. Hannah had no power to change her condition. Yet God changed what Hannah could not change.

“And those who stumbled are girded with strength.” To stumble pictures someone who is weak, faltering, or unable to stand securely. The contrast is deliberate: the first person begins strong and is brought low; the second begins weak and is given strength. To be “girded with strength” uses the picture of fastening something around oneself like a belt or garment. The one who had been staggering is now clothed and equipped with strength. Importantly, Hannah does not present the weak as discovering enough strength within themselves. The whole prayer makes clear that God is the One who brings about the reversal.

The same principle runs throughout 1 Samuel. Israel will be tempted to trust visible strength. Saul will look like a king because of his impressive appearance. David will seem insignificant beside Goliath. Again and again the book shows that human strength is not decisive when God is involved.

The verse does not teach that every strong person will become weak, or that every weak person will automatically become powerful in this life. Hannah is describing God’s sovereign ability to overturn human conditions and exposing the mistake of treating present circumstances as permanent.

Study Notes

  • Bows (Hebrew qesheth, H7198) and mighty men (gibborim, H1368, “warriors, heroes”). The bow is often a symbol of military power in the Old Testament (compare Psalm 37:15; 46:9; Jeremiah 49:35; Hosea 1:5).

  • Broken (Hebrew chath, H2844): shattered, dismayed. Grammatically the adjective agrees with “mighty men” rather than “bows,” so the line can be read as “the mighty men of the bow are shattered.” Either way, the warrior and his weapon fall together.

  • Stumbled (Hebrew kashal, H3782): to totter, stagger, or falter, often from weakness or exhaustion.

  • Girded (Hebrew ’azar, H247): to bind on, as a belt or armor. The same picture appears in David’s song: “It is God who arms me with strength” (2 Samuel 22:33, 40; Psalm 18:32, 39).

  • The reversals of verses 4–8 anticipate the story of the whole book: Eli’s house falls while Samuel rises; Saul falls while David rises.

Spirit & Life

  • Hannah had experienced both sides of this truth. She knew what it was to stumble under grief and humiliation, and she now knew what it was for God to strengthen her. Her testimony is not that the weak eventually become strong through determination. It is that the LORD girds the stumbling with His own strength. That is good news for the believer who feels unable to stand: the strength we need is something God fastens around us, not something we must produce from within. No one’s present weakness is the last word when God is at work.

  • The same truth is a warning to the strong. Whatever we lean on in place of God, whether ability, resources, reputation, or position, is a bow that can be broken in a moment. Paul would later learn this from the other side: “My strength is made perfect in weakness” (2 Corinthians 12:9).

1 Samuel 2:5

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:5 Those who were fullhave hired themselves out for bread, And the hungry have ceased to hunger. Even the barren has borne seven, And she who has many children has become feeble.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:5 Those who were well fed are now starving,and those who were starving are now full.The childless woman now has seven children,and the woman with many children wastes away. (NLT)

Main Idea

Hannah applies God’s power of reversal to provision and children: the full become needy, the hungry are satisfied, and the barren becomes fruitful. What we possess does not make us secure apart from God, and what we lack does not place us beyond His ability to act.

Verse Explanation

Hannah continues the theme she began in verse 4, now turning to two areas where people often feel either secure or helpless: material provision and children.

“Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread.” To be “full” describes people who once had plenty to eat and were secure enough that hunger was not a concern. Yet their circumstances have changed so completely that they must now hire themselves out simply to get food. Hannah is showing how fragile human security can be. Abundance today does not guarantee abundance tomorrow. Wealth, possessions, position, and resources can create the impression of security, but none of them is permanent or independent of God.

“And the hungry have ceased to hunger.” The reversal works in the other direction as well. Those who once lacked food now have enough. They no longer need to labor desperately for food because their hunger has been satisfied. Hannah is not merely commenting on changing economic fortunes. She is describing God’s sovereignty over conditions people often assume will remain as they are.

Then Hannah moves to something very close to her own experience: “Even the barren has borne seven.” Barrenness had defined much of her suffering in chapter 1. She could not change it, and Peninnah continually used it to provoke her. Now Hannah describes the barren woman as becoming abundantly fruitful. “Seven” here is poetic language for abundant fruitfulness rather than an exact count of children. What had looked like the permanent definition of her life was not permanent at all.

“And she who has many children has become feeble” is the opposite side of the reversal. A woman with many children possessed something highly valued in the ancient world and could appear secure and honored. Yet even that position could change. Peninnah naturally comes to mind, since she had sons and daughters while Hannah had none. But the text never says that Peninnah later lost her children or became feeble, so we should not turn Hannah’s poetic statement into a specific historical claim about her. Hannah’s personal circumstances lie behind the prayer, but the principle she expresses has grown broader than her individual conflict.

The verse helps explain what Hannah learned through her years of barrenness: present circumstances do not tell the whole story. The one who has abundance should not become proud, and the one who suffers lack should not conclude that God is unable to change the situation. At the same time, Hannah is describing God’s sovereign power, not offering a formula by which every barren woman will bear children or every poor person will become wealthy.

Study Notes

  • Full (Hebrew sabea‘, H7649): satisfied, sated. Hired themselves out (sakar, H7936): to work for wages, here as day laborers working only for food.

  • Ceased to hunger: the Hebrew is unusually compressed, literally “and the hungry have ceased” (chadal, H2308). The most natural sense is that they have ceased from hunger, or ceased from hard labor for food. Some scholars read the final word of the line with the next clause (“they have ceased … until the barren has borne seven”), but the NKJV rendering captures the main contrast.

  • Seven: often a number of fullness in Scripture (compare Ruth 4:15, “better to you than seven sons”; Jeremiah 15:9), though it should not be treated as a rigid numerological rule. Hannah later bore three more sons and two daughters (2:21), for six children in all including Samuel, which supports reading “seven” here as poetic rather than numerical.

  • Feeble (Hebrew ’umlal, H535): to languish, droop, or wither. Jeremiah 15:9 uses the same verb for “She languishes who has borne seven.”

  • Jesus’ words “Blessed are you who hunger now, for you shall be filled … Woe to you who are full, for you shall hunger” (Luke 6:21, 25) stand in the same tradition of reversal.

Spirit & Life

  • What we presently possess does not make us secure apart from God, and what we presently lack does not place us beyond His ability to act. The comfortable are called to humility, and the needy are called to hope. Both stand before the same God.

  • Hannah’s own experience became a window into God’s ways. The years that seemed wasted in barrenness became the very place where she learned who God is. Seasons of lack are not outside His purposes; they are often the school in which His people learn that He alone is their security.

1 Samuel 2:6

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:6 "The LORD kills and makes alive; He brings down to the grave and brings up.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:6 The LORD gives both death and life;he brings some down to the grave but raises others up. (NLT)

Main Idea

Hannah carries her series of reversals to its farthest limit: the LORD rules over life and death itself. Even the grave is not beyond His authority.

Verse Explanation

Hannah continues the series of reversals, but now she carries the thought to its greatest possible extreme. She has moved from strength and weakness, to fullness and hunger, to barrenness and fruitfulness, and now to life and death themselves. Her point is that God’s sovereign authority reaches over the entire range of human existence.

“The LORD kills and makes alive.” Hannah is declaring God’s ultimate authority over life and death. Life is not independent of God, and death is not outside His rule. The LORD who gives life also sets the boundaries of human life. This is Hebrew poetry describing the extent of God’s sovereignty, not a claim that every individual death is a direct act of divine judgment. Nothing, not even death, places human existence outside His authority.

There may be an intentional connection with Hannah’s own experience. Barrenness was the inability to bring forth life, and Hannah had been powerless to create life in her own womb. When Samuel was born, she understood his life as something God had given. Her personal experience has widened into a much larger confession: the LORD is the giver and ruler of life itself.

“He brings down to the grave and brings up.” Hannah pictures someone descending toward death and someone being brought upward again. “Brings up” may describe God rescuing someone who has come close to death and restoring that person to life and health; similar language appears elsewhere in the Old Testament when people describe deliverance from circumstances that seemed certain to end in death. At the same time, the language is strong enough to fit the Bible’s unfolding revelation that God’s authority extends beyond death itself. Hannah is not explaining the full New Testament doctrine of bodily resurrection here, but she does establish something resurrection depends upon: the grave is not beyond God’s authority. The God who gives life has power even over the realm of the dead.

This prepares directly for verse 7 and for the larger story of 1 Samuel, which is full of dramatic reversals. Hannah’s prayer provides a theological framework for understanding those events before they happen. The woman who once could not produce life now praises the God who holds life and death in His hand.

Study Notes

  • Kills (Hebrew muth, H4191, causative form) and makes alive (chayah, H2421, causative form). The line closely echoes Deuteronomy 32:39: “I kill and I make alive; I wound and I heal; nor is there any who can deliver from My hand.” Hannah’s song repeatedly draws on the Song of Moses.

  • Grave (Hebrew She’ol, H7585): in the Old Testament, the realm of the dead or the condition of death, often pictured as “down.” The pairing “brings down … brings up” is a merism, two opposites that together express total control.

  • Compare other Old Testament uses of “brought up from Sheol” for rescue from near death (Psalm 30:3; 86:13; Jonah 2:6), and the stronger hope in Psalm 16:10 and 49:15.

  • Interpretive balance: the verse is poetry about the scope of God’s rule. It should not be pressed into a statement that every death is a direct judgment, nor should the full doctrine of resurrection be read into it as though Hannah were teaching it explicitly. It lays a foundation that later revelation builds upon.

Spirit & Life

  • The LORD holds the keys of death. Life and death are finally in God’s hand. For the believer walking through a “down” season, the comfort is precise: the descent is within His hand too, and the God who brings down is the God who brings up.

  • Hannah confesses God’s authority over the grave; the resurrection of Jesus shows its full force. Peter quotes Psalm 16, “For You will not leave my soul in Hades,” as fulfilled when God raised Christ (Acts 2:27–32). The risen Christ says, “I am He who lives, and was dead, and behold, I am alive forevermore … And I have the keys of Hades and of Death” (Revelation 1:18). The God of Hannah’s song is the God of the empty tomb.

  • Human limitations are not God’s limitations. Hannah could not create life in her own womb, yet God did. Even the greatest boundary we know, death itself, does not lie beyond His rule. That is the ground of every believer’s hope in the face of loss.

1 Samuel 2:7

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:7 The LORD makes poor and makes rich; He brings low and lifts up.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:7 The LORD makes some poor and others rich;he brings some down and lifts others up. (NLT)

Main Idea

Hannah declares that wealth and poverty, humiliation and honor all stand under God’s sovereign rule. Being low is a condition, not necessarily God’s final word over a person’s life.

Verse Explanation

Hannah continues describing the sovereignty of God through a series of striking reversals. Verse 6 spoke of life and death; verse 7 turns to poverty and wealth, humiliation and exaltation. Her central point is that the circumstances and status that seem to define a person’s life are not beyond God’s authority.

“The LORD makes poor and makes rich.” Hannah recognizes that material circumstances ultimately exist under God’s sovereign rule. Wealth is not proof of someone’s superiority, and poverty is not proof that someone is inferior or forgotten by God. This should not be turned into a simple formula that traces every person’s poverty or wealth to an immediate, identifiable act of God. Scripture recognizes many causes of poverty, including injustice, oppression, foolish choices, calamity, and circumstances beyond a person’s control. Hannah’s concern is broader: neither poverty nor wealth places anyone outside God’s sovereignty.

“He brings low and lifts up.” Hannah now widens the idea beyond money. To be brought low can mean being humbled, reduced in status, weakened, or placed in humiliating circumstances. To be lifted up means being raised from that low position and given strength, dignity, standing, or honor.

Coming from Hannah, this language carries special weight. She knew what it meant to be brought low. Her barrenness had become a source of humiliation, and Peninnah’s constant provocation deepened her suffering. In 1 Samuel 1:10 Hannah was “in bitterness of soul” and wept in anguish before the LORD. Yet by 2:1 she could say, “My horn is exalted in the LORD.” The woman who had been brought low had experienced God lifting her up.

But Hannah is no longer describing only herself. Her experience has taught her something about the way God rules. Human society naturally notices the strong, prosperous, influential, and honored. God is not bound by those measurements. He can humble the person who appears secure and raise the person whom others have overlooked. This becomes a major pattern in 1 Samuel. Eli’s priestly house has status but will be brought down. Saul will be raised from relative obscurity to kingship and later lose it. David, the youngest son whom his own family did not at first present to Samuel, will be raised to the throne.

There is also an important distinction between God humbling someone and someone simply experiencing hardship. We should not assume that everyone who has been brought low is being punished by God. Hannah herself shows why that conclusion is wrong. Her suffering was not evidence that she was wicked or rejected; chapter 1 presents her as a woman who earnestly sought the LORD. That makes the second half of the verse especially important. Being low is a condition, not necessarily God’s final word over someone’s life.

Study Notes

  • Makes poor (Hebrew yarash, H3423, causative form, “to dispossess, impoverish”) and makes rich (‘ashar, H6238, causative form). Brings low (shaphel, H8213) and lifts up (rum, H7311, the same verb used in verse 1, “my horn is exalted”).

  • The paired participles again form a merism: every condition between poverty and riches, humiliation and honor, falls under God’s rule.

  • Compare Psalm 75:6–7, “For exaltation comes neither from the east nor from the west nor from the south. But God is the Judge: He puts down one, and exalts another,” and Job 1:21.

  • Theological clarification: God’s sovereignty over circumstances does not erase human responsibility or make injustice acceptable. The prophets consistently condemn those who create poverty by oppression (for example Amos 2:6–7; Isaiah 3:14–15). Hannah’s point is that no condition lies outside God’s rule, not that every condition is His direct verdict.

Spirit & Life

  • This verse overturns two opposite mindsets: the prosperity mindset that says wealth proves God’s favor, and the self-made mindset that says wealth proves my effort. Neither poverty nor plenty is a verdict on a person’s worth, and neither lies outside God’s rule. That does not make every hardship an assignment from His hand; injustice, oppression, and foolish choices still stand under His judgment. What it does mean is that a low place is never beyond His reach, and a high place is never a place to stop needing Him.

  • Hannah learned not to judge God’s purposes only by what she could see at a given moment. The LORD is not limited by where a person begins, how powerless that person appears, or how permanent the circumstances seem. For anyone living in a low place, this is an invitation to hope in God rather than in a change of circumstances.

  • Paul learned the same secret: “I know how to be abased, and I know how to abound … I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me” (Philippians 4:12–13). Contentment in every condition grows from knowing that every condition is held by the same faithful God.

1 Samuel 2:8

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:8 He raises the poor from the dust And lifts the beggar from the ash heap, To set them among princes And make them inherit the throne of glory. "For the pillars of the earth are the LORD's, And He has set the world upon them.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:8 He lifts the poor from the dustand the needy from the garbage dump.He sets them among princes,placing them in seats of honor.For all the earth is the LORD’s,and he has set the world in order. (NLT)

Main Idea

Hannah gives a vivid picture of how far God can lift someone, from the refuse heap to a seat among princes. He can do this because the whole world belongs to Him and rests on the foundations He set.

Verse Explanation

Hannah continues the theme of God reversing human circumstances, but verse 8 takes the thought further than verse 7. She has just said, “He brings low and lifts up.” Now she paints a vivid picture of how dramatic that lifting can be. God can take someone from the lowest imaginable position and place that person in a position of extraordinary honor.

“He raises the poor from the dust.” In the ancient world, sitting in the dust could picture poverty, humiliation, grief, or social insignificance. Hannah describes someone who has almost nothing and occupies a place others would easily overlook. The key word is “raises.” The poor person does not have the resources or status to raise himself. God is able to intervene in circumstances that seem humanly irreversible.

“And lifts the beggar from the ash heap.” The ash heap was the place where refuse and ashes piled up around a town. The image is deliberately extreme: someone sitting at the very bottom of society, surrounded by what others have thrown away. That makes the next statement astonishing: “To set them among princes.” God does not merely improve the poor person’s situation slightly. In Hannah’s picture He takes someone from the refuse heap and seats that person among rulers.

“And make them inherit the throne of glory” intensifies the reversal again. The person who had virtually nothing is given a place of honor and authority. The word “inherit” stresses receiving something the person did not possess or create for himself.

In the immediate context, Hannah is describing God’s sovereign ability to exalt the lowly. But her words also fit remarkably well with what will happen later in 1 Samuel. David will begin as the youngest son tending sheep, apparently so insignificant that Jesse does not at first bring him before Samuel. God will eventually place him on Israel’s throne. Hannah’s prayer sets out the principle before David’s story unfolds: God is able to raise an overlooked person to a place no human observer would have predicted.

The final part of the verse explains why God has authority to produce such enormous reversals: “For the pillars of the earth are the LORD’S, and He has set the world upon them.” “Pillars of the earth” is poetic language for the stability and foundations of the world. Hannah is not giving a scientific description of the earth’s structure. She is declaring that the created order belongs to God and depends on Him. The One who established the world certainly has authority over the positions people occupy within it. Human social structures, wealth, influence, and power do not restrict Him. There is also something deeply personal behind Hannah’s words. She knew what it meant to occupy the lower place, and her own experience became evidence of a much larger truth.

At the same time, this verse is not a promise that every poor believer will become wealthy or politically powerful. Hannah is declaring God’s freedom and power to overturn human circumstances and His refusal to measure people by the world’s ranking. Verses 4–8 have been building to this point. The prayer begins with broken bows and strengthened stumblers and ends with someone lifted from a refuse heap to a throne. Behind every one of these reversals stands the same truth: what appears fixed to us is not fixed to God.

Study Notes

  • Dust (Hebrew ‘aphar, H6083) and ash heap (’ashpoth, H830): the town refuse heap, where garbage, ashes, and dung were dumped and where beggars and outcasts often sat. Job sat “in the midst of the ashes” (Job 2:8), and Lamentations 4:5 describes the once-wealthy who now “embrace ash heaps.”

  • Beggar (Hebrew ’ebyon, H34): the needy, destitute person.

  • Princes (Hebrew nedibim, from nadib, H5081): nobles, people of high rank.

  • Throne of glory (Hebrew kisse’ kabod): literally “seat of honor.” The word kabod (H3519), “glory, weight, honor,” becomes a key word later in the chapter (2:29–30) and in the naming of Ichabod (4:21).

  • Pillars (Hebrew metsuqe, H4690): supports or foundations, a poetic picture of the world’s stability under God (compare Psalm 75:3; Job 38:4–6).

  • Psalm 113:7–8 repeats Hannah’s words almost exactly: “He raises the poor out of the dust, and lifts the needy out of the ash heap, that He may seat him with princes.” Psalm 113:9 then adds, “He grants the barren woman a home, like a joyful mother of children,” likely a deliberate echo of Hannah.

  • Mary echoes the same reversal: “He has put down the mighty from their thrones, and exalted the lowly” (Luke 1:52).

Spirit & Life

  • From the ash heap to the seat of honor. Scripture marvels at the distance God stoops to lift the lowly and seat them with princes. The person whom others regard as insignificant is not insignificant to Him, and no position is so low that it lies beyond His ability to lift someone from it.

  • This reversal finds its fullest expression in the gospel. The Son of God took the lowest place, coming in humility and crucified outside the city, so that He could lift us to share His throne (Philippians 2:5–11; Revelation 3:21). Believers, once dead in sin, are raised up and made to “sit together in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus” (Ephesians 2:6). What we “inherit” we never earned; it is given by grace.

  • God’s ownership of the world is the believer’s security. The One who holds up the pillars of the earth is not intimidated by the circumstances that seem impossible to us. When our world feels unstable, its foundations are still His.

1 Samuel 2:9

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:9 He will guard the feet of His saints, But the wicked shall be silent in darkness. "For by strength no man shall prevail.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:9 “He will protect his faithful ones,but the wicked will disappear in darkness.No one will succeed by strength alone. (NLT)

Main Idea

God guards the steps of His faithful ones, while the proud and wicked are finally silenced. No one prevails by human strength; the security of God’s people rests in God’s strength, not their own.

Verse Explanation

Hannah now moves from describing God’s reversals in verses 4–8 to showing how God deals with two different groups of people: those who belong to Him and the wicked who oppose Him. The verse emphasizes God’s protection, God’s judgment, and the inadequacy of human strength apart from Him.

“He will guard the feet of His saints.” The saints are God’s faithful or godly ones, those who belong to Him and live in faithful relationship with Him. Hannah is not describing people who never fail or sin, but people whose lives are oriented toward the LORD and who trust Him. Feet represent a person’s walk or path through life. To guard someone’s feet is to preserve that person along the road ahead, keeping him from being finally overcome or destroyed.

This does not mean God’s people will never suffer. Hannah herself proves otherwise. She was one of God’s faithful people, yet she endured years of barrenness, humiliation, provocation, grief, and unanswered prayer. God’s guarding did not mean she avoided every painful experience. It meant that her suffering did not place her outside God’s care or keep His purposes for her from being fulfilled. There may also be a deliberate contrast with verse 4, where Hannah spoke of “those who stumbled.” People can stumble because they are weak, but God is able both to strengthen them and to guard their steps.

“But the wicked shall be silent in darkness.” Hannah now describes the opposite outcome. The wicked may speak proudly for a time, as verse 3 describes, but they will not have the final word. Darkness often represents judgment, defeat, distress, or death, so Hannah’s picture is severe. Those who reject God and rely on themselves will discover that their apparent power cannot protect them from His judgment. Proud speech may be loud for a while, but it never has the last word before God.

“For by strength no man shall prevail” gives the reason for the contrast between the two groups. The faithful are preserved because God guards them. The wicked fall because human strength is insufficient apart from God. Hannah is not saying that strength itself is evil, but that no one finally succeeds simply because he is stronger than someone else. People naturally assume that the strongest person has the greatest security. Hannah has learned otherwise. The decisive issue is not how strong a person is, but that person’s relationship to the LORD.

This truth becomes crucial for the rest of 1 Samuel. Israel will repeatedly be tempted to judge by visible strength. Saul will look impressive because he is physically imposing. Goliath will seem unbeatable because of his size, weapons, and experience. David will face Goliath without the conventional strength of a warrior. It also explains Hannah’s own story. She did not overcome her barrenness through strength. She could not force God to give her a child. She came before Him weeping, praying, and admitting her dependence. Verse 9 prepares for verse 10, where Hannah widens this principle to God’s enemies in general and, remarkably, speaks of God’s king and anointed.

Study Notes

  • Saints (Hebrew chasidim, from chasid, H2623): the godly or faithful ones, those bound to God in chesed (H2617), His steadfast covenant love and loyalty. The word describes people in covenant relationship with God, not a spiritual elite. (The Hebrew text has a written form that could be read as singular, “His faithful one,” and a reading tradition that gives the plural; the NKJV follows the plural.)

  • Guard (Hebrew shamar, H8104): to keep, watch over, or protect, as a watchman guards a city or a shepherd his flock. Compare “He will not allow your foot to be moved; He who keeps you will not slumber” (Psalm 121:3).

  • Silent (Hebrew damam, H1826): to be still, silenced, or brought to an end. Darkness (choshek, H2822).

  • Strength (Hebrew koach, H3581): human power or ability. Prevail (gabar, H1396): to be strong, to win, related to gibbor (“mighty man”) in verse 4.

  • The line anticipates “Not by might nor by power, but by My Spirit,” says the LORD of hosts (Zechariah 4:6) and David’s words to Goliath: “the LORD does not save with sword and spear; for the battle is the LORD’s” (1 Samuel 17:47).

Spirit & Life

  • Safe footing comes from the Keeper, not the climber. The believer’s safety lies not in the firmness of his own step but in the faithfulness of the One who keeps it. Peter says believers “are kept by the power of God through faith” (1 Peter 1:5), and Jude closes with praise “to Him who is able to keep you from stumbling” (Jude 24).

  • Being guarded is not the same as being spared all pain. Hannah was one of God’s faithful ones and still walked through years of sorrow. God’s keeping means that suffering never removes us from His care or defeats His purpose for us. That is a truer and deeper comfort than a promise of an easy road.

  • Hannah began her story with almost no power to change her circumstances. What she learned was not how to become powerful enough to control her life, but something far more important: God guards those who belong to Him, and human strength, however impressive, can never take the place of dependence on Him.

1 Samuel 2:10

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:10 The adversaries of the LORD shall be broken in pieces; From heaven He will thunder against them. The LORD will judge the ends of the earth. "He will give strengthto His king, And exalt the horn of His anointed."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:10 Those who fight against the LORD will be shattered.He thunders against them from heaven;the LORD judges throughout the earth.He gives power to his king;he increases the strength of his anointed one.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Hannah’s prayer reaches its climax as she looks beyond her own story to God’s victory over His enemies, His judgment of the whole earth, and His strengthening of the king He will anoint. Her song begins with God’s salvation in her own life and ends with God’s Anointed ruling in strength.

Verse Explanation

Hannah’s prayer reaches its climax in verse 10. What began with her personal rejoicing has expanded steadily outward. She has spoken of God’s holiness, His knowledge, His power to reverse human circumstances, and His guarding of His faithful people. Now she looks beyond her own situation to God’s rule over His enemies, over the whole earth, and ultimately over His king and anointed one.

“The adversaries of the LORD shall be broken in pieces.” In verse 1 Hannah spoke of her own enemies: “I smile at my enemies.” Now the language has changed. These are “the adversaries of the LORD.” Hannah’s prayer has moved beyond Peninnah or anyone who personally opposed her. Their defeat continues the reversal theme running through the whole prayer. People may seem strong enough to resist God, but their strength is temporary. Verse 9 has just said, “by strength no man shall prevail.” Verse 10 shows the final result when human strength sets itself against God.

“From heaven He will thunder against them.” Thunder is a vivid picture of God’s overwhelming power and judgment. Against all the human boasting and strength Hannah has described stands the voice of God from heaven. This image becomes especially meaningful later in the book. In 1 Samuel 7:10, when the Philistines attack Israel, “the LORD thundered with a loud thunder upon the Philistines that day, and so confused them.” Hannah’s song provides the framework for understanding that later victory. Israel’s deliverance does not depend on superior human strength; the LORD Himself fights for His people.

“The LORD will judge the ends of the earth.” Hannah’s vision now becomes universal. The God she prayed to in her personal anguish is not a local deity concerned with one woman, one family, or even one nation. He is Judge of the entire earth. This is a remarkable expansion. Hannah entered the story as a grieving woman praying quietly in the tabernacle at Shiloh; her prayer now reaches to God’s government over the whole world.

Then comes the most remarkable statement in the verse: “He will give strength to His king.” At this point Israel did not yet have a human king. The period of the judges was drawing to a close, but Saul would not become king until later in 1 Samuel. Hannah is therefore speaking prophetically about what God will do. Kingship becomes one of the major themes of the book, which tells the story of Saul’s rise and fall and David’s rise toward the throne. Hannah introduces God’s king before either man appears.

Notice where the king’s strength comes from: God gives it. This connects directly with verse 9. God’s king will not succeed because of natural ability, impressive appearance, military resources, or human power. His strength must come from the LORD. That distinction will become crucial when Saul and David are compared. Saul will often act from human calculation and visible strength. David, despite his failures, will be marked by dependence on the LORD.

“And exalt the horn of His anointed.” As in verse 1, the horn represents strength, dignity, and a victorious position. Hannah began by saying, “My horn is exalted in the LORD.” She ends by saying that God will “exalt the horn of His anointed.” Her personal experience of being lifted up becomes a picture of something much larger that God will do through His chosen king. At its basic Old Testament level, an “anointed one” is someone specially appointed and consecrated by God, particularly a king, and David will become the LORD’s anointed. We should first hear Hannah’s words in that setting: they anticipate Israel’s monarchy and especially the kind of king God Himself chooses and strengthens.

At the same time, this language begins a biblical path that reaches beyond David. God’s promise of a lasting Davidic kingdom will later develop into the hope of the ultimate Anointed One, the Messiah. Hannah is not giving a detailed prediction of Jesus here, but her prayer introduces language and themes that become central to the Bible’s messianic hope. The God who lifted one suffering woman is the God who overturns human pride, governs life and death, raises the lowly, judges the whole earth, and establishes His chosen King.

Study Notes

  • Adversaries (Hebrew meribav, from rib, H7378, “to contend, strive”): those who contend with the LORD, His opponents at law or in battle. Broken in pieces (chathath, H2865): shattered, terrified.

  • Thunder (Hebrew ra‘am, H7481): God’s thunder is often associated with His intervention in battle and His voice in judgment (Psalm 18:13; 29:3; 2 Samuel 22:14). Its fulfillment within the book comes at Mizpah (1 Samuel 7:10).

  • Judge (Hebrew din, H1777): to govern, render judgment, vindicate. Ends of the earth (’aphse ’arets, from ’ephes, H657): the farthest limits of the world.

  • Anointed (Hebrew mashiach, H4899): one consecrated with oil for a God-given office. This appears to be the first time in the Bible that mashiach is used for a king. It is the source of the English word “Messiah”; its Greek equivalent is Christos, “Christ.”

  • Structure: the song forms a bracket, or inclusio. It opens with “My horn is exalted in the LORD” (v. 1) and closes with “exalt the horn of His anointed” (v. 10). The books of Samuel are likewise framed by Hannah’s song here and David’s song near the end (2 Samuel 22), which closes with “He is the tower of salvation to His king, and shows mercy to His anointed, to David and his descendants forevermore” (22:51).

Spirit & Life

  • A mother’s song sees a King who is not yet born. Israel had no king when Hannah sang, yet her song, preserved as Scripture, reaches past Samuel to David, and past David toward Christ. One woman’s answered prayer becomes a window into God’s plan for the world, and it was given to a woman who came to God with nothing but her need.

  • Hannah’s horn is exalted in Samuel; the world’s horn of salvation is raised in Jesus. Zechariah, father of John the Baptist, sings that God “has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of His servant David” (Luke 1:69). Mary’s Magnificat deliberately echoes Hannah, and both mothers sing because of a son given by God. Christ, God’s final and eternal Anointed King, is where Hannah’s song finally lands.

  • The true King draws His strength from the LORD. Even the Son of God said, “I can of Myself do nothing” (John 5:30). If God’s anointed King lived in dependence on the Father, how much more should all who follow Him. The kingdom advances not by human might but by strength that God gives.

1 Samuel 2:11

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:11 Then Elkanah went to his house at Ramah. But the child ministered to the LORD before Eli the priest.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:11 Then Elkanah returned home to Ramah without Samuel. And the boy served the LORD by assisting Eli the priest. (NLT)

Main Idea

Elkanah and Hannah keep their vow and go home without Samuel, while the young boy begins serving the LORD at Shiloh. Hannah’s private prayer for a son has now become part of God’s larger work in Israel.

Verse Explanation

Verse 11 closes the account of Hannah’s prayer and moves the story forward. On the surface it is a simple statement that Elkanah returned home while Samuel stayed at Shiloh. But it marks a real turning point: Hannah and Elkanah have followed through on their promise and left Samuel there to serve the LORD.

“Then Elkanah went to his house at Ramah.” Elkanah returns to the family home in Ramah after their time of worship at Shiloh. Hannah is not named in this sentence, but the context indicates that she returned with him. Samuel did not. Hannah had prayed, “if You will … give Your maidservant a male child, then I will give him to the LORD all the days of his life” (1:11). God answered her prayer, and Hannah kept her word. In 1:28 she said, “Therefore I also have lent him to the LORD; as long as he lives he shall be lent to the LORD.” Verse 11 quietly records the reality of that surrender. They went home without Samuel.

That gives added weight to Hannah’s prayer in verses 1–10. Her rejoicing was not spoken while she was taking Samuel home with her. She had just entrusted the child she had so desperately wanted to the service of the LORD, and she was able to worship God even as she released that blessing back to Him.

“But the child ministered to the LORD.” Samuel was still very young, though the text does not give his exact age. He would not yet have been carrying out the full responsibilities of an adult priest; his service would have fit his age and grown as he grew. The important point is that Samuel’s life is now directed toward service to the LORD. Hannah had not merely brought him to Shiloh for a ceremony. He stayed and began the life of service for which she had dedicated him.

The phrase “to the LORD” is especially important because of what comes next. Samuel is serving in a religious setting that is about to be exposed as deeply corrupt. Verses 12–17 describe the wickedness of Eli’s sons, Hophni and Phinehas, priests who “did not know the LORD” (v. 12). Samuel is a young child who “ministered to the LORD.” Eli’s sons are adult priests who did not know Him. The difference is not age, position, title, or religious activity.

“Before Eli the priest.” Eli is responsible for Samuel’s supervision and instruction at Shiloh. Hannah has entrusted Samuel not only to the LORD but, in practical terms, to the setting where Eli serves as priest. That becomes uncomfortable as the chapter continues, because Eli’s household is seriously unhealthy. This does not excuse Eli’s failures or suggest that harmful spiritual environments should simply be tolerated. Within this story, it emphasizes that Samuel’s calling depends on the LORD, not on the perfection of the people around him. This verse begins an alternation that will run through the chapter, setting Samuel beside Eli’s sons, and the child Hannah surrendered will become the prophet through whom God guides the nation and anoints its first kings.

Study Notes

  • Ramah: Elkanah’s home, called Ramathaim Zophim in 1:1, in the hill country of Ephraim. Its exact identification is debated, so the distance and direction from Shiloh cannot be stated precisely. Hannah saw Samuel on the family’s yearly trip (2:19).

  • Ministered (Hebrew sharat, H8334): to serve, attend, or minister. The word is regularly used for priestly and Levitical service at the sanctuary (Exodus 28:43; Numbers 3:6; Deuteronomy 10:8), not for simple chores. It is used of Samuel again in 2:18 and 3:1.

  • Before Eli: literally “in the presence of” or “under the oversight of” Eli, describing Samuel’s apprenticeship.

  • Structure: this verse begins a deliberate alternation that runs through the chapter, setting Samuel (vv. 11, 18–21, 26) beside Eli’s sons (vv. 12–17, 22–25).

Spirit & Life

  • Worship can rise at the very moment of release. Hannah’s great song was sung on the day she walked away from her little boy. Holding God’s gifts with open hands is costly, but it is the posture in which the heart learns to rejoice in God Himself.

  • Small hands doing real ministry. Samuel ministered “to the LORD” before he ever ministered to the people, and that order shaped his whole life. Ministry begins as service to God before it is service to others. Churches should also treat children as real participants in worship; God can do genuine work in a young life.

1 Samuel 2:12

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:12 Now the sons of Eli were corrupt; they did not know the LORD.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:12 Now the sons of Eli were scoundrels who had no respect for the LORD (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli’s sons held the office of priest, but they were worthless men who did not truly know the LORD. Their corrupt ministry flowed from the fact that they had religious position without a real relationship with God.

Verse Explanation

Verse 12 begins a major contrast in the chapter. Verse 11 ended by telling us that young Samuel “ministered to the LORD before Eli the priest.” Immediately afterward we meet Eli’s own sons, Hophni and Phinehas. They held the office of priest, but spiritually they were the opposite of Samuel.

“Now the sons of Eli were corrupt.” The Hebrew expression is stronger than the English word “corrupt” might suggest. Literally, Hophni and Phinehas are called “sons of worthlessness,” thoroughly wicked and lawless men. That is especially serious because they were not outsiders attacking Israel’s worship. They were priests serving within the sanctuary itself (1:3). They had the position, the garments, the responsibilities, and the privileges of the priesthood, but their lives contradicted the God they claimed to represent.

The verses that follow explain why the narrator describes them so severely. They abused the sacrificial system for their own benefit, took portions of sacrifices that did not belong to them, threatened worshipers who resisted, and treated the LORD’s offering with contempt. Verse 22 adds sexual immorality at the tabernacle. “Corrupt” does not describe minor flaws or occasional failures. These men had settled into a pattern of abusing sacred authority for personal gratification.

“They did not know the LORD.” This is the most serious statement in the verse. Hophni and Phinehas knew about the LORD. They knew Israel’s religious system, the sacrifices, and the priestly procedures. They had grown up in the household of Israel’s high priest. Yet Scripture says they did not know the LORD. In the Bible, knowing God means much more than having information about Him. It describes real recognition of God, relationship with Him, submission to Him, and a life that responds rightly to who He is. Their behavior revealed that they lacked that knowledge. Had they truly recognized the holiness and authority of the God they served, they could not have casually treated His sacrifices as opportunities for personal gain.

This is one of the strongest warnings in the passage: religious position is not the same as knowing God. A person can know religious language, take part in worship, hold spiritual authority, and perform religious duties while remaining personally estranged from the God those activities are supposed to honor. Samuel had no priestly experience and no important position. He was simply “the child.” Hophni and Phinehas had the title and authority. Yet Samuel was ministering “to the LORD,” while they “did not know the LORD.” The narrator will keep setting Samuel’s quiet spiritual growth beside the growing corruption of Eli’s sons.

There is another sobering element. Hophni and Phinehas grew up surrounded by the things of God, yet familiarity with sacred things did not produce reverence for God. The problem was deeper than bad priestly behavior. Their corrupt ministry flowed from the fact that they did not truly know the LORD. Verse 12 gives the diagnosis; verses 13–17 show the symptoms.

Study Notes

  • Corrupt: literally “sons of Belial” (Hebrew bene beliyya‘al, from beliyya‘al, H1100), usually explained as “without worth” or “good for nothing.” The phrase describes the worst kind of lawless people (Deuteronomy 13:13; Judges 19:22; 1 Samuel 25:17). The irony is sharp: in 1:16 Hannah pleaded with Eli not to consider her a “wicked woman,” literally a “daughter of Belial,” while his own sons were sons of Belial. By New Testament times “Belial” had become a name for Satan (2 Corinthians 6:15).

  • Did not know (Hebrew yada‘, H3045): the common verb for personal, experiential, relational knowing, not merely factual awareness (compare Jeremiah 22:16; Hosea 4:1, 6). The same phrase is used of Pharaoh, “I do not know the LORD” (Exodus 5:2), and of the generation after Joshua (Judges 2:10).

  • Contrast 1 Samuel 3:7: “Now Samuel did not yet know the LORD, nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him.” Samuel’s “not yet” is the innocence of a child whom God is about to meet; the sons’ “did not know” is settled rejection.

  • Hophni and Phinehas are named in 1:3 as priests at Shiloh. Phinehas shares the name of Aaron’s zealous grandson (Numbers 25:7–13), a painful contrast.

Spirit & Life

  • Raised at the altar, strangers to God. Hophni and Phinehas knew all about the LORD and handled holy things daily, yet had no personal knowledge of Him. Nearness to the sanctuary is not intimacy with God. Every generation raised in church must move from proximity to real relationship. Jesus warned of those who will say, “Lord, Lord, have we not prophesied in Your name …?” and hear, “I never knew you” (Matthew 7:22–23).

  • God is not impressed by spiritual titles or outward closeness to sacred things. What matters is whether a person actually knows Him, and whether that relationship shows in how His presence, His Word, His worship, and His people are treated. It is worth asking honestly whether we have mistaken church attendance, ministry roles, or a Christian upbringing for knowing the LORD.

  • Familiarity can breed contempt, even with holy things. The danger for those who live close to spiritual things is not ignorance but numbness. Reverence must be renewed, not assumed.

1 Samuel 2:13 And the priests' custom with the people was that when any man offered a sacrifice, the priest's servant would come with a three-pronged fleshhook in his hand while the meat was boiling.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:13 or for their duties as priests. Whenever anyone offered a sacrifice, Eli’s sons would send over a servant with a three-pronged fork. While the meat of the sacrificed animal was still boiling, (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli’s sons had replaced God’s instructions for priestly portions with their own “custom” of taking what they wanted from worshipers’ sacrifices. Their failure to know God showed in the way they handled what belonged to Him.

Verse Explanation

Verse 13 begins to explain what made the behavior of Eli’s sons so corrupt. The problem was not that priests received meat from the sacrifices; God had specifically provided portions of certain offerings for the priests. The problem was that Hophni and Phinehas had developed their own custom for taking what they wanted rather than receiving what God had assigned to them.

“And the priests’ custom with the people was” describes an established practice, not an isolated incident or a momentary lapse. It had become the normal way Eli’s sons handled the sacrifices worshipers brought. The word “custom” matters because their practice was not the same as God’s law. The law already specified what belonged to the priests. Eli’s sons were replacing God’s instructions with a procedure that benefited themselves.

“When any man offered a sacrifice” shows that their behavior directly affected ordinary Israelites who came to worship. These people brought animals from their own flocks and herds in obedience to the LORD. Their offerings were acts of worship, thanksgiving, fellowship, and devotion, and Hophni and Phinehas inserted their selfishness into that worship. They were using their spiritual position to take advantage of people at the very moment those people were approaching God.

“The priest’s servant would come with a three-pronged fleshhook in his hand” describes how the practice worked. The priests sent servants to collect meat for them, using a large fork-like utensil to pull meat from the cooking pot. The fact that a servant did the work does not remove responsibility from Hophni and Phinehas. The servants acted according to the priests’ practice, and the abuse originated with those who held authority.

“While the meat was boiling” places this during the preparation of the sacrificial meal. Verse 14 explains how the fork was used, and verse 15 will reveal an even greater abuse.

Verse 13 connects directly with verse 12. The narrator has told us that Eli’s sons “did not know the LORD.” Now we begin to see what that looked like in practice. Their corruption was especially dangerous because they had turned abuse into routine. Something contrary to God’s command had become “the priests’ custom,” and once repeated often enough, a sinful practice begins to feel normal simply because everyone is used to it.

Study Notes

  • Custom (Hebrew mishpat, H4941): normally “judgment,” “ordinance,” or “just decree.” The irony is pointed: the priests had made their own “ordinance.” The same word appears in 1 Samuel 8:9–11, where Samuel warns of “the behavior of the king” (the mishpat of the king), who “will take” from the people again and again.

  • The priests’ lawful portion: in the peace offering the priest received the breast (waved before the LORD) and the right thigh, after the fat had been burned on the altar (Leviticus 7:28–34; compare Deuteronomy 18:3, where priests also receive the shoulder, cheeks, and stomach of certain sacrifices).

  • Fleshhook (Hebrew mazleg, H4207): a fork used in sacrificial cooking. Such forks were among the bronze utensils of the altar (Exodus 27:3; 38:3). “Three-pronged” is literally “three teeth.”

  • Boiling: in the peace offering, most of the meat went back to the worshiper, who boiled it for a family meal eaten before the LORD (compare 1:4, 9). The servant’s fork entered the worshiper’s own cooking pot.

Spirit & Life

  • When custom replaces commandment. Eli’s sons wrote their own law. Religious tradition, established practice, or “the way we have always done it” does not make something right. Religious systems drift when leaders turn personal preferences into sacred “policy” that serves themselves. The standard remains what God has said.

  • Leaders who take instead of give are a recurring danger among the people of God. Ezekiel would later indict Israel’s shepherds in words that sound like Hophni and Phinehas: “You eat the fat and clothe yourselves with the wool … but you do not feed the flock” (Ezekiel 34:3). Jesus is the opposite: the Good Shepherd who “gives His life for the sheep” (John 10:11). Where Eli’s sons took the offering by force, Jesus became the offering.

  • It is worth asking where our own preferences have quietly become “the way we’ve always done it,” serving us more than they honor God.

1 Samuel 2:14

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:14 Then he would thrust it into the pan, or kettle, or caldron, or pot; and the priest would take for himself all that the fleshhook brought up. So they did in Shiloh to all the Israelites who came there.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:14 the servant would stick the fork into the pot and demand that whatever it brought up be given to Eli’s sons. All the Israelites who came to worship at Shiloh were treated this way. (NLT)

Main Idea

Whatever the servant’s fork pulled from the pot became the priest’s, and this abuse had become routine for every Israelite who came to Shiloh. Eli’s sons had taken what God established for His worship and altered it for their own benefit.

Verse Explanation

Verse 14 continues describing the corrupt practice introduced in verse 13, showing both how it worked and how widespread it had become.

“And he would thrust it into the pan, or kettle, or caldron, or pot.” The servant plunged the three-pronged fork into whatever vessel worshipers were using to prepare their sacrificial meal. The list shows how indiscriminate the practice was. Wherever the sacrifice was being cooked, the priest’s servant could reach in and take meat.

“And the priest would take for himself all that the fleshhook brought up.” Whatever the hook caught became the priest’s portion. The priest was not receiving the portion assigned to him by God’s law; he was taking whatever happened to come up on the fork. Their sin was not receiving provision from the sacrifices. Their sin was refusing to stay within the boundaries God had set and creating a system that let them take what they wanted. The words “take for himself” expose the self-centered character of it all. The sacrifice was being offered to the LORD, but the priests were treating it as an opportunity to satisfy themselves.

“So they did in Shiloh to all the Israelites who came there.” This makes the seriousness of the situation much clearer. It was not something Hophni and Phinehas did occasionally to a few worshipers. It had become their normal practice. Shiloh was where the tabernacle stood and therefore a central place of Israel’s worship. People came there because they wanted to worship the LORD according to His commands. When they arrived, they met priests abusing the very sacrificial system they were supposed to guard. The problem was institutional rather than merely personal; a repeated abuse had become part of the ordinary experience of worship at Shiloh.

This helps explain the unusually severe judgment later in the chapter. Hophni and Phinehas were not merely committing private sins. Their position allowed their sin to interfere with other people’s worship of God.

Study Notes

  • Pan, kettle, caldron, pot: four different Hebrew words for cooking vessels (kiyyor, dud, qallachath, parur). The exact difference between them is uncertain; the list simply means “every kind of pot.”

  • Shiloh: the site of the tabernacle from the days of Joshua (Joshua 18:1) until the events of 1 Samuel 4. It was the central place where Israel came for the yearly feasts (1 Samuel 1:3). Its later destruction became a warning remembered for centuries (Psalm 78:60; Jeremiah 7:12–14).

  • The worshipers’ meal was part of the peace offering, a fellowship meal eaten in God’s presence (Deuteronomy 12:7, 17–18). By invading the family’s portion, the priests disrupted the very meal that expressed communion between God and His people.

Spirit & Life

  • Spiritual authority becomes corrupt when those entrusted with it begin treating what belongs to God and His people as something available for their own advantage. Every gift of leadership, whether in a church, a ministry, or a home, is held in trust. It is given for the sake of others and for the honor of God, never for self-enrichment.

  • The fork and the robe. Two objects capture this chapter: the priests’ three-pronged fork, stabbing into worshipers’ offerings to take what was not theirs, and Hannah’s little robe, carried each year to clothe the son she had given away (2:19). One pictures religion that takes; the other pictures love that gives. The contrast is a quiet sermon on the difference between consuming ministry and self-giving devotion.

  • Sin that is repeated often enough begins to feel normal. What was unthinkable the first time can become routine. The only safe standard is God’s Word, not what everyone around us has grown used to.

1 Samuel 2:15

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:15 Also, before they burned the fat, the priest's servant would come and say to the man who sacrificed, "Give meat for roasting to the priest, for he will not take boiled meat from you, but raw."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:15 Sometimes the servant would come even before the animal’s fat had been burned on the altar. He would demand raw meat before it had been boiled so that it could be used for roasting. (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli’s sons went further still, demanding raw meat for roasting before God’s portion, the fat, had even been burned on the altar. What was supposed to be offered first to God was being claimed first by the priest.

Verse Explanation

Verse 15 reveals that the corruption of Eli’s sons went even further than the practice described in verses 13–14. They were not satisfied with whatever their fork brought out of the cooking pot. They demanded their preferred portion before the sacrifice had been properly offered to God.

“Also, before they burned the fat” is the crucial detail. According to the law, the fat of certain sacrifices belonged especially to the LORD and was to be burned on the altar. In the peace offering, the fat was removed and burned to the LORD first, and only then were the appropriate portions available for the priests and the worshiper. The order mattered because it expressed priority: God received what belonged to Him first. Eli’s sons were overturning that order. Their appetite was taking precedence over God’s portion of the sacrifice. That makes their conduct more serious than simple greed. They were treating as common something God had declared holy, and as priests they should have understood the meaning of the sacrifice better than almost anyone in Israel.

“The priest’s servant would come and say to the man who sacrificed.” Once again servants carried out the demands, but they acted for the priest. The worshiper had come to Shiloh to offer something to God, yet the priest’s representative approached him with a demand before the sacrifice was complete. This placed worshipers in a painful position. To resist the servant was to resist someone acting with priestly authority, yet to comply was to take part in something contrary to the proper handling of the sacrifice.

“Give meat for roasting to the priest.” The meat taken from the boiling pot in verses 13–14 apparently did not satisfy them. They wanted raw meat that could be roasted according to their preference. They were not merely making sure they had enough food; they were demanding the kind of meat and preparation they preferred.

“For he will not take boiled meat from you, but raw.” The language is demanding rather than grateful. The priest refused what came through the normal preparation of the sacrificial meal and wanted the meat before it was cooked. Verse 16 will show that ignorance was not the issue. The proper procedure was known; the priests were deliberately overriding it. Their behavior revealed a deeper attitude toward God: they acted as though their desires had a greater claim on the sacrifice than God’s commands did.

Study Notes

  • Fat (Hebrew cheleb, H2459): the fat covering the internal organs, kidneys, and liver, which was always burned on the altar as the LORD’s portion (Leviticus 3:3–5, 14–16; 7:23–25, 30–31). “All the fat is the LORD’s” (Leviticus 3:16). Israelites were forbidden to eat it (Leviticus 3:17; 7:25). The fat was regarded as the richest part, and the legislation makes plain that it belonged to the LORD.

  • Burned (Hebrew qatar, H6999): to make smoke, to burn as an offering so that it rises as “a sweet aroma to the LORD” (Leviticus 3:5).

  • Proper order in the peace offering: the animal was killed, its blood applied to the altar, the fat burned, and only then were the priest’s portions (the breast and right thigh) given and the rest eaten by the worshiper (Leviticus 3; 7:29–34).

  • Roasting versus boiled: roasted meat was generally considered finer fare. Demanding raw meat meant taking it before any portion reached the altar or the worshiper’s pot.

Spirit & Life

  • Don’t take your portion before God gets His. The fat belonged to the LORD alone and had to be burned before anyone ate. Eli’s sons would have God wait on them rather than waiting on God. The heart question is timeless: does God receive our firstfruits, our first hours, first resources, and first affections, or only whatever is left over?

  • Appetite reveals what we worship. The priests’ appetites had become more important to them than obedience, and their position gave them power to impose those appetites on the people they were supposed to serve. What we insist on having first shows what we actually treasure most.

  • God’s order protects worship. Putting God first is not an arbitrary rule; it keeps the whole act of worship pointed toward Him. When anything else moves to the front of the line, even something legitimate, worship begins to be about us.

1 Samuel 2:16

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:16 And if the man said to him, "They should really burn the fat first; then you may take as much as your heart desires," he would then answer him, "No, but you must give it now; and if not, I will take it by force."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:16 The man offering the sacrifice might reply, “Take as much as you want, but the fat must be burned first.” Then the servant would demand, “No, give it to me now, or I’ll take it by force.” (NLT)

Main Idea

When faithful worshipers reminded the priest’s servant that God’s portion must come first, the priests refused and threatened to take the meat by force. Their sin had grown from improper practice into deliberate defiance of God and intimidation of His people.

Verse Explanation

Verse 16 makes the corruption of Eli’s sons unmistakable. Verse 15 showed that they demanded raw meat before the fat had been offered to the LORD. Verse 16 shows that the worshipers themselves recognized this was wrong, but the priests insisted on getting what they wanted anyway.

“And if the man said to him, ‘They should really burn the fat first.’” The worshiper is trying to preserve the proper order of the sacrifice. According to the law, the fat belonged to the LORD and was to be burned on the altar first. The worshiper was not refusing to provide for the priest. He was insisting that God receive what belonged to Him first. This removes any suggestion that Hophni and Phinehas simply misunderstood the procedure. Someone was explicitly reminding their servant what should happen, and the demand did not change.

“Then you may take as much as your heart desires” shows how accommodating the worshiper was willing to be. His concern was not mainly about losing some of his meat but about the order of worship. He was saying, in effect, “Honor the LORD first, and then take your portion.” There is a painful irony here. The worshiper was willing to be generous toward the priest, but even generosity was not enough, because the priest wanted control over the sacrifice.

“No, but you must give it now.” The word “now” exposes the heart of the abuse. The servant, acting for the priest, would not wait until God’s portion had been offered. The priest’s desire had become more urgent to him than God’s command.

“And if not, I will take it by force.” Corruption now becomes coercion. The priest’s servant threatens the worshiper with physical force if he refuses. This is a profound abuse of spiritual authority. Priests were given their position to serve God and minister on behalf of His people. Instead, they were using that authority to intimidate the very people they were supposed to serve. The obstacle to faithful worship was coming from inside the priesthood. Priests were meant to help Israel approach God according to His instructions; Hophni and Phinehas were making obedience to God harder.

The progression through verses 13–16 is deliberate: the priests take whatever their fork pulls from the pot, then demand raw meat before the fat is burned, and finally, when someone objects on the basis of what God requires, threaten to take the meat by force. This prepares directly for God’s verdict in verse 17.

Study Notes

  • They should really burn the fat first: the Hebrew uses an emphatic construction (infinitive absolute with the verb), literally “let them surely burn, burn the fat,” stressing the worshiper’s insistence on the LORD’s portion.

  • As much as your heart desires: literally “as your soul (nephesh, H5315) desires.” The worshiper offers the priest whatever he wants, only after God has been honored.

  • By force (Hebrew chozqah, H2394): with strength, violently. The same idea of taking by force appears in Ezekiel 34:4, where God condemns shepherds who ruled “with force and cruelty.”

  • The phrase “he would answer” (along with the other verb forms in vv. 13–16) describes repeated, habitual action. This happened again and again.

Spirit & Life

  • Ministry that bullies is not ministry. When a worshiper asked simply that God be honored first, the answer was, “Give it now, or I will take it by force.” Coercion had entered the house of God. Scripture does not soften this: a sacred office never excuses abuse. God’s people should recognize spiritual bullying for what it is, and leaders should examine whether they are using their position to take rather than to give.

  • The humble worshiper saw more clearly than the priest. An ordinary Israelite understood what honoring God required, while the man in the sacred office did not.

  • Jesus described true leadership in the opposite direction: “whoever desires to become great among you, let him be your servant … just as the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life a ransom for many” (Matthew 20:26–28).

1 Samuel 2:17

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:17 Therefore the sin of the young men was very great before the LORD, for men abhorred the offering of the LORD.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:17 So the sin of these young men was very serious in the LORD’s sight, for they treated the LORD’s offerings with contempt. (NLT)

Main Idea

God judged the sin of Eli’s sons as very great because their abuse of the sacrifices dishonored the LORD’s offering and damaged the worship of the very people they were supposed to serve.

Verse Explanation

Verse 17 gives God’s evaluation of the behavior described in verses 12–16. The narrator now explains why it was so serious: their conduct was affecting not only themselves but the way the LORD’s offering was regarded.

“Therefore the sin of the young men was very great before the LORD.” God did not regard their behavior as a minor abuse or technical violation of procedure. Their sin was “very great.” The phrase “before the LORD” is also significant. Hophni and Phinehas may have had enough authority to intimidate worshipers and continue without immediate human consequences, but everything they did was taking place before God. Their position did not shield them from His evaluation. Their sin involved several things at once: greed, disregard for God’s instructions, abuse of priestly authority, and threats against people who wanted to honor God rightly. Most seriously, they treated what belonged to God as though it existed for their own benefit.

“For men abhorred the offering of the LORD” explains one of the most destructive consequences of their behavior, and here the Hebrew is difficult (see the Study Note). Two things can be said, and they do not compete. First, the surrounding narrative already establishes that Hophni and Phinehas themselves treated the LORD’s offering with contempt (vv. 13–16, 29). Second, the NKJV rendering adds that their conduct caused the worshipers to abhor the offering. Both are true to the passage: familiar, self-serving handling of holy things made the priests numb to what was holy, and their irreverence spread to the people who came to worship. In either reading, the sacrificial system itself was not the problem; God had given it as part of Israel’s worship. The corruption of those administering it was causing something holy to be treated as contemptible. Israelites coming to Shiloh encountered greed, intimidation, and abuse from the very priests who were supposed to help them worship faithfully.

There is an important distinction here. The corruption of the priests did not make the offering itself corrupt. It was still “the offering of the LORD.” God’s institution remained holy even though the men administering it were acting wickedly. The failure belonged to the priests, not to God or to what God had commanded. Their misconduct could seriously damage people’s perception of worship, but it did not change God’s character. Hophni and Phinehas misrepresented the LORD; they did not reveal what He is really like.

Verse 17 also explains why God will deal so severely with Eli’s household later in the chapter. These priests had been entrusted with something that belonged to God and was meant to draw people into worship. Instead of protecting it, they exploited it. The contrast with Samuel is again deliberate. Verse 18 immediately turns back to him: “But Samuel ministered before the LORD, even as a child.”

Study Notes

  • Young men (Hebrew ne‘arim, H5288): here refers to Hophni and Phinehas (and possibly their servants, who are also called na‘ar in vv. 13, 15). The same word is used for Samuel in 2:11, 18, 21, 26, sharpening the contrast between two kinds of “young men” at Shiloh.

  • Abhorred (Hebrew na’ats, H5006): to spurn, despise, or treat with contempt (compare Numbers 14:11, 23; 16:30).

  • Textual note: the Hebrew is difficult. It can be read as “the men (that is, Hophni and Phinehas) treated the LORD’s offering with contempt” or, as the NKJV takes it, “men (the people) abhorred the offering of the LORD” because of the priests’ conduct. Some manuscripts and ancient versions lack “the men.” Either way, the central point stands: the priests’ behavior brought contempt on what God had made holy.

  • Offering (Hebrew minchah, H4503): here used broadly of the sacrificial gifts brought to the LORD.

  • Compare Romans 2:24: “For ‘the name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you,’” where Paul applies the same principle to those who dishonor God while claiming to represent Him.

Spirit & Life

  • The priests became numb to what was holy, and their irreverence then made the people despise what was holy. Both movements are in the passage: leaders who handle sacred things for their own advantage lose their reverence for them, and people who watch that happen can come to despise what God intended for their worship. Leaders answer not only for their own sin but for the effect of their example. It is a sobering question for anyone with influence: has anything in my leadership, parenting, or example made it harder for someone else to love worshiping God?

  • “Before the LORD.” What worshipers endured in the courtyard, God saw from His throne. No abuse in His house is hidden from Him, and no victim of it goes unseen. That is a warning to those who misuse authority and a comfort to those who have been wounded by it.

  • The failure of leaders does not change the character of God. Many people have been hurt by those who claimed to represent Him. Hophni and Phinehas misrepresented the LORD; they did not reveal Him. The offering was still “the offering of the LORD,” holy and good, even when handled by corrupt hands. God is not what His worst representatives made Him look like.

  • Appetite that is not governed by reverence does not stay in one place. The priests demanded meat before the fat was offered, and verse 22 will show sexual sin as well. The text does not say they ate the LORD’s fat, only that they claimed their portion ahead of His. Paul describes such people as those “whose god is their belly” (Philippians 3:19). The cure for disordered desire is not mere restraint but a greater delight in the LORD.

1 Samuel 2:18

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:18 But Samuel ministered before the LORD, even as a child, wearing a linen ephod.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:18 But Samuel, though he was only a boy, served the LORD. He wore a linen garment like that of a priest. (NLT)

Main Idea

In the same sanctuary where corrupt priests were dishonoring God, the young Samuel was quietly and faithfully serving before the LORD. God’s most significant work is not always the most visible thing happening at the moment.

Verse Explanation

Verse 18 deliberately turns our attention away from the corruption of Hophni and Phinehas and back to Samuel. The contrast is striking. Verse 17 has just said that the sin of Eli’s sons was “very great before the LORD.” Verse 18 begins, “But Samuel.” In the same place where corrupt priests were dishonoring God, a young boy was quietly growing in faithful service to Him.

“But Samuel ministered before the LORD” repeats the description from verse 11. Samuel had been dedicated to the LORD by Hannah and was now actively serving at the sanctuary. The phrase “before the LORD” is especially important. Samuel worked under Eli’s supervision, but Eli was not the ultimate object of his service. Samuel was serving the LORD. That becomes all the more significant because of the environment in which he was growing up. Hophni and Phinehas were corrupt, and Eli had serious failures as both father and priest. Yet Samuel’s relationship with God was developing in the middle of that environment, and the corruption around him did not determine what he would become. The text is not suggesting that unhealthy spiritual environments are harmless; the chapter strongly condemns what was happening at Shiloh. Rather, it shows God’s ability to preserve and develop Samuel even while serious corruption surrounded him. The same sanctuary held two opposite spiritual directions: Samuel was moving toward God while Eli’s sons were moving away from Him.

“Even as a child” emphasizes Samuel’s youth. His exact age is not given, but he was young enough for the narrator to call him a child. Hophni and Phinehas were grown men who held priestly office, yet they did not know the LORD. Samuel was only a child, yet he was already serving before Him. Spiritual maturity cannot be measured simply by age, title, position, or years spent around religious things. Hannah had not merely dedicated Samuel’s future to the LORD; his life of service had already begun. What she promised in 1:11, that her son would be given to the LORD “all the days of his life,” was becoming visible in everyday life.

“Wearing a linen ephod.” An ephod was a garment associated with priestly and sanctuary service. The high priest wore an elaborate ephod, but Samuel’s is described as a simple linen one. This does not necessarily mean that he was functioning as an ordained priest or performing every priestly duty. The garment visibly identified him with service at the sanctuary. There is also a quiet contrast between Samuel’s clothing and the conduct of Eli’s sons. The linen ephod represents Samuel’s participation in sacred service, while Hophni and Phinehas were exploiting that same sacred setting for themselves. Samuel’s outward role and his actual conduct were moving together; theirs had come apart.

The verse also shows what God is doing quietly in the background. Eli’s house is moving toward judgment, but God is already preparing Samuel. The corruption at Shiloh may seem dominant in verses 12–17, but it is not the only thing happening there.

Study Notes

  • Ministered (Hebrew sharat, H8334): see the note on verse 11. The participle describes ongoing, continuing service.

  • Ephod (Hebrew ’ephod, H646) and linen (bad, H906). The high priest’s ephod was an elaborate garment of gold, blue, purple, and scarlet with the breastpiece attached (Exodus 28:4–14). The simple linen ephod was worn more widely by those serving at the sanctuary: the eighty-five priests of Nob (1 Samuel 22:18) and David when he brought the ark to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6:14).

  • Samuel was a Levite by descent (1 Chronicles 6:26–28, 33–34) but, so far as Scripture shows, not a descendant of Aaron, and so not a priest by birth. His later offering of sacrifices (for example 1 Samuel 7:9–10) reflects his unique role as prophet and judge in a time of transition.

  • Child (Hebrew na‘ar, H5288): a boy or young man; the same word used for Eli’s sons in verse 17 (“the young men”), heightening the contrast between the two kinds of ne‘arim at Shiloh.

Spirit & Life

  • Never underestimate God’s “but.” Against the backdrop of greed, the story turns on a single word: “But Samuel.” While the old order was rotting, God was already clothing His answer. In dark seasons, God is often quietly at work where we are not yet looking.

  • Samuel’s story shows that a corrupt environment did not determine his own response to the LORD. That is a word of hope for believers living amid compromise, while the chapter itself also makes clear that unhealthy spiritual environments are serious and should not be treated as harmless.

  • Samuel’s importance was not yet visible. He was simply a child in a linen ephod doing whatever small tasks he had been given. Yet this hidden faithfulness was the beginning of a life God would use profoundly. Small, unseen service offered “before the LORD” is never small to Him.

1 Samuel 2:19

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:19 Moreover his mother used to make him a little robe, and bring it to him year by year when she came up with her husband to offer the yearly sacrifice.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:19 Each year his mother made a small coat for him and brought it to him when she came with her husband for the sacrifice. (NLT)

Main Idea

Hannah kept her vow and left Samuel at Shiloh, but she never stopped loving him, lovingly making and bringing him a new robe every year. Surrender to God did not end her love or responsibility; it changed the way she expressed them.

Verse Explanation

Verse 19 gives a brief but deeply personal picture of Hannah’s continuing relationship with Samuel after she dedicated him to the LORD. Samuel now lived and served at Shiloh, but Hannah’s surrender of her son did not mean she stopped being his mother or disappeared from his life.

“Moreover his mother used to make him a little robe” shows Hannah continuing to care for Samuel in a practical and personal way. Verse 18 has just told us that Samuel wore a linen ephod while ministering before the LORD; the robe appears to have been an additional outer garment his mother made for him. The word “little” stresses Samuel’s youth and gives the scene great tenderness. Samuel was not yet the great prophet who would lead Israel, confront kings, and anoint David. He was still a little boy whose mother made his clothes. The kind of garment may also be significant. The same word is used elsewhere for important outer garments, and later for the robe Samuel wears as an adult. Hannah was not making Samuel a high priest’s garment, but the robe fits the larger picture of a boy growing into a life devoted to service before the LORD.

“And bring it to him year by year” shows that she did this repeatedly. Each year she made another robe and brought it to Samuel. Because he was growing, each new robe would need to be larger. This small detail lets us see Samuel’s growth almost visually. Hannah had surrendered control of his life to God, but she continued to take part in his life in the ways still open to her.

“When she came up with her husband” shows that Hannah kept going with Elkanah to Shiloh, following the family pattern described in chapter 1. For Hannah, those yearly journeys now carried added meaning. Shiloh was where she had once wept bitterly because she had no child, where she poured out her soul before the LORD, where God answered her prayer, and where she later brought Samuel and dedicated him to God. Now each return allowed her to see the son for whom she had prayed.

“To offer the yearly sacrifice.” Hannah and Elkanah continued their regular worship of God. Samuel had been a tremendous answer to prayer, but their relationship with God did not end once the prayer was answered. This also shows that Hannah’s surrender was real. She did not bring Samuel home after a short time because the separation became too hard. Year after year she returned, saw him, provided for him, and then left him there to continue serving the LORD.

There is a meaningful balance in this verse between surrender and continuing love. Hannah had said, “Therefore I also have lent him to the LORD; as long as he lives he shall be lent to the LORD” (1:28). She kept that commitment. But giving Samuel to God did not mean becoming emotionally detached from him. She remained his mother and expressed her love through continued care. The verse also stands in deliberate contrast with Eli’s family. Eli’s sons used their position at Shiloh to take for themselves. Hannah came to Shiloh bringing something she had made for someone else. Their relationship to the sanctuary was marked by taking; hers was marked by giving.

Study Notes

  • Robe (Hebrew me‘il, H4598): an outer robe or mantle, worn by people of standing. The word is used for the high priest’s blue robe beneath the ephod (Exodus 28:31) and for the robes of kings and nobles (1 Samuel 18:4; 24:4). It reappears at key moments in Samuel’s life: Saul tears the hem of Samuel’s robe when Samuel announces that the kingdom has been torn from him (15:27–28), and the medium at Endor describes Samuel as “an old man … covered with a mantle” (28:14).

  • Year by year and the yearly sacrifice: the family’s annual pilgrimage to Shiloh (1:3, 7, 21), probably connected with one of the major feasts.

  • The verb forms describe customary, repeated action: this was Hannah’s regular practice.

Spirit & Life

  • The robe that grew with the boy. Hannah could not keep Samuel, but she could clothe him, and each year the robe grew larger as he did. She could no longer oversee his daily life, but she faithfully did what remained hers to do: make the robe, bring it, see how he was growing, and keep entrusting the rest of his life to the LORD. Here is a picture of releasing parenting: letting go of control while never ceasing to invest, visit, and pray.

  • Surrender is not detachment. Giving someone or something to God does not mean ceasing to love or care. It means loving within God’s purposes rather than against them. Hannah’s love did not shrink when she let go; it found a new and faithful shape.

  • The yearly robe can be seen as love woven in secret and carried to the house of God. (This is devotional reflection built on the story, not something the text states directly.) It invites a question: what faithful, hidden act of love can we keep carrying to God on behalf of someone we have entrusted to Him?

1 Samuel 2:20

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:20 And Eli would bless Elkanah and his wife, and say, "The LORD give you descendants from this woman for the loan that was given to the LORD." Then they would go to their own home.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:20 Before they returned home, Eli would bless Elkanah and his wife and say, “May the LORD give you other children to take the place of this one she gave to the LORD. ” (NLT)

Main Idea

Each year Eli blessed Hannah and Elkanah, asking the LORD to give them more children in place of the son they had given to Him. The God who had opened Hannah’s womb was not finished showing her kindness.

Verse Explanation

Verse 20 continues the account of Hannah and Elkanah’s yearly visits to Shiloh. Each year they returned to offer their sacrifice, Hannah brought Samuel the robe she had made, and Eli spoke a blessing over them. The blessing recognizes both what Hannah had given to God and the hope that God would give back to her beyond what she had surrendered.

“And Eli would bless Elkanah and his wife.” Despite the serious failures about to be exposed in his household, Eli still served as Israel’s priest and here spoke a priestly blessing over Samuel’s parents. There is a meaningful connection with chapter 1. When Eli first saw Hannah, he misunderstood her anguished prayer and thought she was drunk. After she explained, Eli told her, “Go in peace, and the God of Israel grant your petition which you have asked of Him” (1:17). God answered that prayer by giving her Samuel. Now Eli again speaks a blessing concerning children.

“The LORD give you descendants from this woman” expresses Eli’s desire that God give Hannah and Elkanah more children. Because Hannah had dedicated Samuel to lifelong service at Shiloh, Eli prayed that God would give them other children who would remain with them at home. The wording keeps the source of the blessing clear. Eli does not claim he can give them children; he says, “The LORD give you.” Hannah’s story began with the recognition that only God could open her womb, and that same dependence on God continues here.

“For the loan that was given to the LORD.” The “loan” refers to Samuel. The language reaches back to Hannah’s words in 1:27–28. The English word “loan” can give the wrong impression if we think of something given temporarily with the expectation of taking it back. Hannah was not lending Samuel to God for a few years and planning to reclaim him. Her vow was to give him to the LORD “all the days of his life” (1:11). The emphasis is on Samuel being received from God as an answer to prayer and then devoted to God.

There is a beautiful movement in the story. Hannah once asked God for one son. God gave her Samuel. She then surrendered Samuel to the LORD rather than holding tightly to the answer she had received. Now Eli asks God to give her more children in place of the son she had dedicated. This does not establish a formula in which God is obligated to return more whenever someone gives something to Him. The passage describes what God did specifically for Hannah; verse 21 will show that He did indeed answer Eli’s blessing.

“Then they would go to their own home.” This simple statement again underlines the reality of Hannah’s surrender. After worshiping, visiting Samuel, bringing his robe, and receiving Eli’s blessing, Hannah and Elkanah returned home while Samuel stayed at Shiloh. Year after year she kept living by what she had promised.

Study Notes

  • Descendants (Hebrew zera‘, H2233): literally “seed,” meaning offspring.

  • The loan that was given (Hebrew hashsha’elah ’asher sha’al): built on the verb sha’al (H7592), “to ask,” which in some forms can also mean “to lend” or “to give on request.” This word runs throughout Hannah’s story: she asked (sha’al) the LORD for a son (1:17, 20, 27), and she has given him to the LORD, literally “made him asked for” (1:28). The same root sounds in the name Saul (Sha’ul, “asked for”) and is echoed in the explanation of Samuel’s name (1:20). A smoother rendering might be “in place of the one she asked for and dedicated to the LORD.”

  • The Hebrew text of this phrase is difficult; the Greek Old Testament (Septuagint) reads “for the loan which you lent to the LORD.” The overall meaning is clear either way.

  • Bless (Hebrew barak, H1288): priests were charged with blessing the people in the LORD’s name (Numbers 6:22–27; Deuteronomy 10:8).

Spirit & Life

  • Give with an open hand, not as an investment. Hannah did not surrender Samuel in order to obtain more children. She gave him because she had promised to. God’s later gifts were grace, not payment. Giving to God as a transaction subtly makes God our debtor; giving to Him in trust makes Him our joy.

  • Hannah gave without any promise of more, and God delighted to add to her. That is a revelation of His generous character, not a formula for getting a return. The LORD receives what is given to Him, and He answers in His own wisdom and kindness, sometimes in ways that look nothing like what we gave.

  • God’s blessing can come even through imperfect vessels. Eli was a flawed priest with a failing household, yet the LORD answered the blessing he spoke. God is not limited by the weakness of His servants, though He will hold them accountable for their faithfulness.

1 Samuel 2:21

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:21 And the LORD visited Hannah, so that she conceived and bore three sons and two daughters. Meanwhile the child Samuel grew before the LORD.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:21 And the LORD blessed Hannah, and she conceived and gave birth to three sons and two daughters. Meanwhile, Samuel grew up in the presence of the LORD. (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD graciously visited Hannah and gave her five more children, while Samuel continued growing in God’s presence at Shiloh. God was at work both in the family Hannah returned to and in the son she had placed in His hands.

Verse Explanation

Verse 21 completes the account of God’s response to Hannah’s faithfulness while continuing the chapter’s contrast between Samuel and Eli’s household. Hannah had given Samuel to lifelong service at Shiloh, and Eli had prayed that the LORD would give her more children. Now the narrator tells us that God did exactly that.

“And the LORD visited Hannah” does not mean that God appeared to her physically. In biblical language, when God visits someone, He turns His attention toward that person in order to act. Depending on the context, divine visitation can bring judgment or blessing; here it clearly describes God acting graciously toward Hannah. The expression also connects with her original prayer. In 1:11 she pleaded, “O LORD of hosts, if You will indeed look on the affliction of Your maidservant and remember me.” Then 1:19 says, “and the LORD remembered her.” Now 2:21 says, “the LORD visited Hannah.” This does not mean God had forgotten Hannah and suddenly remembered she existed. Such language emphasizes the moment when God acts on someone’s behalf according to His purposes. Hannah had lived through years when nothing seemed to be happening. Looking back, the narrator consistently identifies God as the One at work.

“So that she conceived and bore three sons and two daughters.” God gave Hannah five more children after Samuel, making her the mother of six. This sheds light on her words in verse 5, “Even the barren has borne seven.” Hannah did not have exactly seven children, at least according to the biblical record, which supports reading “seven” in her song as poetic language for fullness. In chapter 1 she was identified by what she lacked. By chapter 2 the woman who once could not conceive has become the mother of a growing family. The verse also fulfills Eli’s blessing in verse 20.

It is important not to turn Hannah’s experience into a universal transaction with God. The passage does not teach that if someone gives something valuable to God, He is obligated to repay with more. Nor does it teach that every woman who experiences infertility will eventually conceive if she has enough faith. The text records a particular act of God’s grace in Hannah’s life within His larger purposes for Israel. When Hannah fulfilled her vow, she had no recorded promise that more children would follow. She gave Samuel because she had promised to. The additional children were God’s gracious provision, not payment she had earned.

“Meanwhile the child Samuel grew before the LORD.” The verse now turns from Hannah’s growing family at home to Samuel’s development at Shiloh. “Meanwhile” shows that two things were happening at once: God was blessing Hannah with more children while Samuel was growing in the place where she had dedicated him. “Grew” certainly includes physical growth, but “before the LORD” gives it a larger meaning. He was growing up in God’s presence and in service connected with the sanctuary. There is also a fitting resolution to Hannah’s surrender. She could no longer oversee Samuel’s daily life, but the text assures us that he was growing “before the LORD.” She had released him from her immediate care, but not into emptiness. She had entrusted him to the God who had given him to her in the first place.

Study Notes

  • Visited (Hebrew paqad, H6485): to attend to, take note of, or act toward someone with purpose. It is a broad word for attention that issues in action. In this context it describes gracious divine attention, as when the LORD “visited Sarah as He had said” (Genesis 21:1) and when “the LORD had visited the children of Israel” (Exodus 4:31). It can also mean to visit in judgment (Exodus 20:5).

  • Remembered (Hebrew zakar, H2142) in 1:19: in Scripture God’s “remembering” means acting on behalf of someone in faithfulness (compare Genesis 8:1; 30:22; Exodus 2:24).

  • Hannah’s six children: Samuel plus three sons and two daughters. Her song’s “seven” (2:5) is poetic fullness.

  • Grew before the LORD: literally “with the LORD” (‘im YHWH), suggesting growth in the LORD’s company and presence.

  • Samuel’s growth is noted at intervals (2:21, 26; 3:19), a pattern Luke echoes for John the Baptist (Luke 1:80) and for Jesus (Luke 2:40, 52).

Spirit & Life

  • When the LORD visits. God’s “visit” is gracious attention that moves into action. The years when nothing seemed to be happening were not years when God had forgotten Hannah. His timing is not our timing, but His attention never lapses. Every believer who is waiting can take heart that God’s eyes are already on them.

  • Grow up in the presence. Samuel grew “with the LORD.” Growth in His presence is the kind that lasts, and it is the only kind that does not depend on the surroundings.

  • Entrusting is not abandoning. Hannah could not control what happened at home or at Shiloh, but God was at work in both. Whatever we place in His hands, a child, a calling, a future, is held by the One who gave it in the first place.

1 Samuel 2:22

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:22 Now Eli was very old; and he heard everything his sons did to all Israel, and how they lay with the women who assembled at the door of the tabernacle of meeting.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:22 Now Eli was very old, but he was aware of what his sons were doing to the people of Israel. He knew, for instance, that his sons were seducing the young women who assisted at the entrance of the Tabernacle. (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli, now very old, heard the full report of his sons’ abuses, including sexual immorality with the women who served at the tabernacle. His knowledge removed any excuse and made him responsible for how he would respond.

Verse Explanation

Verse 22 returns from Samuel’s quiet growth to the corruption of Eli’s sons. The chapter has already exposed their abuse of the sacrifices. Now we learn that their sin extended beyond greed and misuse of priestly authority to sexual immorality. The verse also begins to focus more directly on Eli’s responsibility, because he knew what his sons were doing. In this verse, the point is that Eli knows.

“Now Eli was very old.” Eli’s advanced age may help explain why his sons were carrying much of the active priestly work at Shiloh, but it does not remove his responsibility as senior priest and as their father. His age also contributes to the larger transition in the story. Eli belongs to an older priestly household approaching judgment, while Samuel is still a child growing before the LORD.

“And he heard everything his sons did to all Israel.” Their behavior was no longer hidden; reports had reached Eli. The words “to all Israel” do not necessarily mean they personally mistreated every Israelite. They emphasize the broad and public character of their misconduct. Their actions affected the worshiping community that came to Shiloh, and their reputation had spread among the people. The fact that Eli heard these things is important because ignorance can no longer explain his failure to act.

“And how they lay with the women” introduces another dimension of their wickedness. The expression plainly describes sexual relations. The priests who were supposed to represent the holiness of God were engaging in sexual misconduct with women connected to service at the sanctuary. The verse does not give enough information to determine every circumstance, whether the women were willing, whether Hophni and Phinehas used their authority to pressure them, or how long the conduct had continued. Because of the great difference in power between priests and those serving at the sanctuary, abuse of authority is a serious possibility, but the text does not say so with certainty. What it does make clear is that their behavior was sinful and scandalous. The men entrusted with sacred responsibility were using the setting around the tabernacle for sexual immorality.

“Who assembled at the door of the tabernacle of meeting.” These women apparently performed some kind of recognized service at the entrance to the sanctuary. Their corruption had reached into the community of people serving around the house of God. The place meant for worship and service had become the setting in which the priests pursued their own appetites.

Verse 12 gave the underlying diagnosis: “they did not know the LORD.” Verses 13–17 showed the symptoms in their handling of the sacrifices, and verse 22 shows that their corruption was not confined to one area of life. The verse also prepares us to evaluate Eli. From here the question becomes what Eli will do now that he knows. Eli did something, but not enough, as the verses ahead will show. His knowledge created responsibility.

Study Notes

  • Eli’s age: he was ninety-eight when he died (4:15), so here he is likely nearing ninety.

  • Assembled (Hebrew tsaba’, H6633): to serve, to muster, or to perform organized service. The word is related to tsaba’ (“host, army”) and is used of Levitical service in Numbers 4:23; 8:24. Exodus 38:8 refers to “the serving women who assembled at the door of the tabernacle of meeting,” whose bronze mirrors were used to make the laver. The women here appear to have had a similar recognized role.

  • Tabernacle of meeting (Hebrew ’ohel mo‘ed): the tent-sanctuary where God met with His people. At Shiloh it seems to have had more permanent structures around it, since 1:9 and 3:3, 15 mention a doorpost and doors and call it “the temple of the LORD.”

  • Textual note: the clause about the women is absent from the main Greek manuscript tradition (Septuagint) and apparently from the Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel (4QSam a) at this point, leading some scholars to consider it a later addition. It is present in the Masoretic Hebrew text followed by the NKJV and most English translations.

Spirit & Life

  • Knowing is not the same as acting. Eli was not ignorant; he heard everything. He is a portrait of a passive father and a permissive priest. Information without action only increases accountability. It is worth asking what we already know about sin in our own lives, families, or ministries that we have addressed only with words.

  • Sexual misconduct at the door of the sanctuary. These were women who had come to serve God, and the priests engaged in sexual sin with them. The text does not tell us whether the women were willing or whether the priests used their position to pressure them. Yet where sexual misconduct joins a large difference in power, the church must take seriously the possibility of abuse, protect those under spiritual care, and guard the vulnerable rather than the reputation of the powerful. God saw everything that happened at the door of His house, and He did not overlook it.

1 Samuel 2:23

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:23 So he said to them, "Why do you do such things? For I hear of your evil dealings from all the people.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:23 Eli said to them, “I have been hearing reports from all the people about the wicked things you are doing. Why do you keep sinning? (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli confronts his sons and rightly calls their conduct evil, but his response stops at words. Knowing that something is wrong and speaking against it does not fulfill a leader’s responsibility when he has the authority to stop it.

Verse Explanation

Verse 23 records Eli’s response after hearing about the conduct of Hophni and Phinehas. He does not ignore the reports. He confronts his sons and names their behavior as evil. The problem, as the larger passage will show, is that his response stops largely at words.

“So he said to them, ‘Why do you do such things?’” Eli confronts his sons directly. He does not defend them, excuse them, or dismiss the reports. His question expresses disapproval and calls them to account. At the same time, the question sounds surprisingly restrained given the seriousness of their conduct. Hophni and Phinehas were not children who had made an isolated mistake. They were priests who had established a pattern of abusing sacrifices, threatening worshipers, and committing sexual immorality. Their sin had already been described as “very great before the LORD” (v. 17).

Eli recognized that his sons were doing wrong, but recognition and verbal correction were not enough. As senior priest he was responsible for what was happening at the sanctuary. First Samuel 3:13 will say that Eli knew his sons were making themselves vile, “and he did not restrain them.” Eli’s failure was not that he said nothing; verse 23 shows that he did speak. His failure was that he did not take the action needed to stop what he knew was happening.

“For I hear of your evil dealings” shows that Eli had received reports and understood their moral character. The word “evil” shows that he was not merely worried about controversy or damage to the family’s reputation. He knew their actions were wrong. There is also a contrast between what Eli “heard” and what God knew. Hannah had declared that the LORD is “the God of knowledge; and by Him actions are weighed” (2:3). Their conduct was never hidden from God. Now it had become known to Eli and the wider community as well.

“From all the people” emphasizes how widespread the reports had become. It does not necessarily mean that every Israelite complained to Eli personally, but their reputation was broadly known. That was especially damaging because Hophni and Phinehas were public representatives of Israel’s worship.

An important leadership issue is developing here. Eli is both their father and the priest with authority over them, and those two relationships pull against each other. As a father, confronting his sons would have been painful. As a spiritual leader, he was responsible to protect the worship of God and the people who came to worship. Verse 23 therefore introduces an important distinction between confronting wrongdoing and actually dealing with it. The final diagnosis will come from God Himself in verse 29.

Study Notes

  • Evil dealings (Hebrew dibrekem ra‘im): literally “your evil words” or “evil matters,” from dabar (H1697, “word, matter, thing”) and ra‘ (H7451, “evil, bad”).

  • God’s later verdict gives the interpretive key: “For I have told him that I will judge his house forever for the iniquity which he knows, because his sons made themselves vile, and he did not restrain them” (1 Samuel 3:13). Restrain (Hebrew kahah, H3543) means to rebuke with force, to check or curb. The law took priestly defilement and rebellion seriously (for example Leviticus 22:1–9), but Scripture does not spell out the precise procedure by which Eli could have removed his sons. The secure biblical point is that he was responsible to restrain them and did not.

Spirit & Life

  • Words without action. Eli’s words were true, but his response did not match the seriousness of what he knew, and it did not fulfill his responsibility to restrain his sons. Correction that stops at words can leave the wrong impression about how seriously the wrong is taken.

  • Love and leadership must not be set against each other. Eli’s affection for his sons was not wrong. The failure came when loyalty to them outweighed loyalty to God and to the people God had entrusted to him. Parents and leaders alike are called to love in a way that is willing to act, even at personal cost.

  • Nothing is hidden from the God of knowledge. What Eli heard late, God had seen all along. That is a solemn truth for anyone tempted to think that private sin can remain private, and a comfort to anyone who has suffered under leaders who were never held to account.

1 Samuel 2:24

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:24 No, my sons! For it is not a good report that I hear. You make the LORD's people transgress.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:24 You must stop, my sons! The reports I hear among the LORD’s people are not good. (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli tells his sons to stop, recognizing that their sin is public and is harming the LORD’s people, not only themselves. Their authority was meant to help God’s people worship, but their conduct had become a spiritual danger to them.

Verse Explanation

Verse 24 continues Eli’s confrontation with Hophni and Phinehas. Here he makes the seriousness of their behavior clearer. Because they were priests, their sin was affecting God’s people as well as themselves.

“No, my sons!” expresses strong disapproval. Eli is not uncertain about whether their conduct is acceptable; he knows it is wrong and tells them to stop. Calling them “my sons” reminds us that Eli speaks in two roles: father and senior priest. That combination is the heart of the tragedy. Eli loves his sons, but God will later say that he has honored his sons more than Him (2:29).

“For it is not a good report that I hear.” Eli again refers to reports reaching him from the people. His statement is an understatement compared with the narrator’s verdict in verse 17, where their sin is “very great before the LORD.” Still, Eli clearly recognizes that what he is hearing is serious and unacceptable. The word “report” also stresses the public nature of their conduct. Hophni and Phinehas were not privately struggling with hidden sins. Their behavior was widely known, and the reputation of the priesthood and of worship at Shiloh was being damaged.

“You make the LORD’S people transgress” is the most important statement in the verse, though the Hebrew is difficult (see the Study Note). The phrase “the LORD’S people” is especially significant. The Israelites coming to Shiloh did not belong to Hophni and Phinehas. They belonged to God, and the priests had been entrusted with serving God’s own possession. Their authority was supposed to help God’s people worship faithfully. Instead, their behavior had become an obstacle to worship and was drawing others into the consequences of their sin. We have already seen this in verses 13–17: worshipers who wanted to offer sacrifices properly were met by servants demanding meat before the fat had been offered to God, and those who tried to follow God’s order were threatened with force. Personal sin is always serious, but sin committed through spiritual authority has a spreading effect.

Eli therefore understands something important. His sons’ actions cannot be treated as a private family matter. That makes his failure to restrain them all the more serious. Once he knew their behavior was harming the people under their spiritual care, simply expressing disapproval was not enough.

Study Notes

  • Not a good report (Hebrew lo’ tobah hashshemu‘ah): shemu‘ah (H8052) means a report, rumor, or news. The phrase is a restrained understatement.

  • Textual and translation note: the Hebrew of the last clause is difficult. It can be read as “the report that I hear the LORD’s people spreading” (so several modern translations) or, as the NKJV takes it, “you make the LORD’s people transgress” (from ‘abar, H5674, “to pass over, transgress”). Either way, the surrounding passage makes clear that the priests’ conduct was having a destructive effect on the worshiping community.

  • The LORD’S people (Hebrew ‘am YHWH): a covenant phrase marking Israel as God’s own possession (compare Deuteronomy 7:6; 32:9).

  • Jesus warned with similar seriousness about those who cause others to stumble: “it would be better for him if a millstone were hung around his neck, and he were thrown into the sea” (Luke 17:2).

Spirit & Life

  • Sin through spiritual authority has a spreading effect. The priests had been given authority to help people approach God, but their conduct was doing the opposite. Anyone entrusted with influence, whether in a church, a small group, a classroom, or a home, carries a weight of responsibility for the people entrusted to them. Those people are not ours; they are the LORD’s. James writes, “My brethren, let not many of you become teachers, knowing that we shall receive a stricter judgment” (James 3:1). The call to lead God’s people is a call to guard them, never to use them.

  • The people of God belong to God. This is both a warning to leaders and a comfort to those who have been harmed. God does not treat wounds inflicted on His people lightly. He is their true Shepherd, and He will call to account those who scatter His flock (Ezekiel 34:10).

1 Samuel 2:25

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:25 If one man sins against another, God will judge him. But if a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?" Nevertheless they did not heed the voice of their father, because the LORD desired to kill them.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:25 If someone sins against another person, God can mediate for the guilty party. But if someone sins against the LORD, who can intercede?” But Eli’s sons wouldn’t listen to their father, for the LORD was already planning to put them to death. (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli warns that sin directly against the LORD leaves no higher judge to appeal to, but his sons refuse to listen. Their persistent rebellion has reached the point where God’s judgment is settled, and the text holds their guilt and God’s judgment together.

Verse Explanation

Verse 25 is the climax of Eli’s warning. In this verse the sons refuse correction and judicial judgment is settled. Eli has told his sons that their behavior is evil and is harming the LORD’s people. Now he explains why their position is especially dangerous. Their offenses are not merely against other people. By corrupting the sacrifices and abusing their priestly office, they are treating God and His holy things with contempt.

“If one man sins against another, God will judge him.” Eli begins with an ordinary offense between human beings. When one person wrongs another, there is a recognized way to bring the matter before God or before those appointed to judge under His authority. The point is not that sin against another person is unimportant. Eli is setting up a comparison: a human offense can be brought before a higher authority for judgment and resolution.

“But if a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?” Eli now describes the far more dangerous position of his sons. The One who would normally be appealed to as the highest Judge is Himself the One offended. Who is left to take up the offender’s case when the offense is directly against the supreme Judge? This applies especially well to Hophni and Phinehas because their sins were committed in connection with the LORD’s sacrifices. There is tragic irony here. Priests were supposed to serve in a mediating role for Israel. Hophni and Phinehas held an office meant to help people approach God, yet their own contempt for God had left them in need of someone to intercede for them.

Eli’s question does not teach that sins committed against God can never be forgiven. The Old Testament itself provides for sacrifice, priestly intercession, repentance, and divine mercy, and the larger witness of Scripture makes clear that God forgives those who truly repent and ultimately provides the true Mediator between God and humanity. Eli’s point is the terrifying position of persistent rebels who are directly despising the God to whom they must appeal for mercy.

“Nevertheless they did not heed the voice of their father.” Eli’s warning produced no repentance. Hophni and Phinehas heard what he said, but they refused to listen in the biblical sense of responding with obedience. They knew the sacrificial requirements. Worshipers had objected. Reports had spread through Israel. Their father had confronted them directly. Yet they continued in the same direction. This is no longer a picture of ignorance; it is persistent resistance.

“Because the LORD desired to kill them” is the most difficult statement in the verse. At first it can sound as though Hophni and Phinehas wanted to repent but God prevented them because He had already decided to kill them. The context points in a different direction. The chapter has already described a settled pattern of deliberate wickedness. They did not know the LORD (2:12). They treated His offerings with contempt (2:17). They abused worshipers and refused correction. By verse 25, their resistance has hardened and judgment has been determined.

The statement therefore describes divine judgment on persistent rebellion. God’s decision to judge them does not excuse their refusal to listen; their choices are consistently presented as wicked and fully their own. At the same time, the text tells us that their continued refusal now stands within God’s judicial purpose against them. The word “desired” should be understood in that judicial setting. It does not mean God took cruel pleasure in killing them. It means He had determined that their conduct would no longer be tolerated and that judgment was now His settled purpose. Verse 34 will announce that both will die on the same day. The tragedy is not that two innocent men wanted God and were prevented from coming to Him. It is that men who repeatedly rejected God’s authority reached the point where the judgment they had invited was going to fall.

Study Notes

  • Judge and intercede: both translate forms of the Hebrew verb palal (H6419). In the first clause it means to arbitrate or mediate (“God,” or possibly “the judges,” ’elohim, will arbitrate for him); in the second, the reflexive form means to intercede or pray on someone’s behalf. The same verb is used for Hannah’s prayer (1:10, 26; 2:1), and from it comes tefillah, the common Hebrew word for prayer.

  • God will judge him: the Hebrew ’elohim can refer to God or, in some legal contexts, to human judges acting as God’s representatives (compare Exodus 21:6; 22:8–9, where some translations render ’elohim as “the judges”). Either way, a higher authority exists to settle human disputes.

  • Heed (Hebrew shama‘, H8085): to hear, with the sense of hearing that leads to obedience.

  • Desired (Hebrew chaphets, H2654): to delight in, will, or be pleased to. Compare Ezekiel 18:23, 32 and 33:11, where God says He has no pleasure (chaphets) in the death of the wicked. The two statements are not in conflict: God does not delight in death as such, yet He can will judgment on those who persistently reject Him.

  • Parallel pattern: in Exodus, Pharaoh hardens his own heart (Exodus 8:15, 32; 9:34) and God also hardens Pharaoh’s heart (Exodus 9:12; 10:1). Paul describes the same judicial “giving over” in Romans 1:24, 26, 28: “Therefore God also gave them up.”

Spirit & Life

  • The question the New Testament answers. Eli’s question exposes the terror of standing against God with no one to appeal to: when the offense is against God Himself, who can stand between? The Old Testament story leaves that question hanging over these men. The New Testament answers it at its deepest level: “For there is one God and one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5). Sinners who come to Christ have an Advocate: “we have an Advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous” (1 John 2:1), who “always lives to make intercession” for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). To live daily in that relief is one of the great gifts of the gospel.

  • There is a line where God gives people over. After long, persistent rebellion, God confirms the direction the heart has chosen. Scripture holds both truths together: they would not listen, and God had determined judgment. God can will hardening without being the author of evil. This is a sobering warning not to presume on endless opportunity. God’s patience is real, but it is patience meant to lead to repentance (Romans 2:4), not permission to continue in sin.

  • God did not tolerate their abuse because He was indifferent. He had already determined its end. For those who have suffered under abusive leaders and wondered why God seemed silent, this verse speaks: God saw, God weighed, and God acted. His timing is His own, but His justice is certain.

1 Samuel 2:26

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:26 And the child Samuel grew in stature, and in favor both with the LORD and men.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:26 Meanwhile, the boy Samuel grew taller and grew in favor with the LORD and with the people. (NLT)

Main Idea

While Eli’s sons hardened toward judgment, Samuel kept growing physically, spiritually, and relationally, gaining favor with God and people. God was quietly forming Samuel’s future usefulness in the ordinary years before anyone recognized him.

Verse Explanation

Verse 26 turns our attention back to Samuel after the sobering description of Hophni and Phinehas in verse 25. The placement is deliberate. Eli’s sons are growing more hardened in sin and moving toward God’s judgment, while Samuel is moving in exactly the opposite direction. He is growing physically, spiritually, and relationally.

“And the child Samuel grew in stature” first describes Samuel’s normal physical development. We saw this growth pictured indirectly in verse 19, where Hannah brought him a new robe each year; now the narrator states it outright. Samuel was not remaining the little child introduced earlier in the chapter. Time was passing, and he was steadily becoming the man God would use to lead Israel. God’s preparation of him was gradual. He did not suddenly appear as Israel’s mature prophet. At this stage he was simply growing and serving faithfully in responsibilities fitting for his age.

“And in favor both with the LORD” moves beyond physical growth. Samuel was developing in a way that pleased God. “Favor” carries the idea of acceptance, approval, or gracious regard. It does not mean Samuel was earning God’s love through good behavior. It describes the positive character of his growing relationship with God and the way his life was received before Him. The contrast with Eli’s sons could hardly be stronger. Verse 17 said their sin was “very great before the LORD,” and verse 25 said that judgment had been determined against them. Samuel, meanwhile, was growing in favor with the LORD. All three served around the same sanctuary, yet they were moving in completely different spiritual directions. Being near the things of God is not the same as knowing God.

“And men.” Samuel was also gaining favor with people. As he grew, his character and conduct earned the respect and goodwill of those around him. Here again is a contrast with Hophni and Phinehas, whose reputation had become disgraceful; Eli was hearing a bad report about them from the people (v. 24). The two dimensions belong together. Samuel’s spiritual growth was not confined to private religious experience. The character being formed in his relationship with God was becoming visible in his relationships with others.

Samuel probably could not see the significance of what God was doing during these years. He was simply growing, serving, learning, and carrying out his duties at Shiloh. Yet those ordinary years were part of God’s preparation for what would follow. His future usefulness did not begin when he became publicly recognized as a prophet. It was already being formed while he was still called “the child Samuel.” Eli’s sons already hold priestly position but are losing everything through their rebellion. Samuel has almost no position yet, but God is quietly preparing him for greater responsibility.

Study Notes

  • Grew in stature: literally “was going on and growing great” (Hebrew holek wegadel), an idiom for continual, progressive growth (compare Genesis 26:13; 2 Samuel 3:1; 5:10).

  • Favor: literally “and good” (Hebrew tob, H2896), meaning pleasing, approved, well-regarded.

  • Luke deliberately echoes this verse in describing Jesus: “And Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men” (Luke 2:52; compare 2:40). A similar phrase describes John the Baptist (Luke 1:80). The parallel does not make Samuel equal to Jesus, but it places both within a recognizable biblical pattern of a child growing physically while also growing in favor before God and people.

  • Compare Proverbs 3:3–4: “Let not mercy and truth forsake you … And so find favor and high esteem in the sight of God and man.”

Spirit & Life

  • Grow in favor with God first. This growth is balanced, Godward and manward, but in that order. Favor with people that is not rooted in favor with God is fragile; favor with God will eventually shape a life that others recognize as good. What is formed in secret with God becomes visible in time.

  • Luke’s echo invites us to see Samuel as a faithful servant growing up in a corrupt setting, and to hear in Luke’s description of Jesus (Luke 2:52) a deliberate reminder of him. Samuel takes his place in a biblical pattern that is fulfilled supremely in Jesus, the Son who grew in wisdom and stature and in favor with God and men.

  • God does His deepest work in ordinary years. Before God entrusts greater influence, He forms character: maturity, relationship with Him, and integrity others can see. Hidden seasons of faithful growth are not wasted time. They are the soil in which future usefulness is rooted.

1 Samuel 2:27

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:27 Then a man of God came to Eli and said to him, "Thus says the LORD: "˜Did I not clearly reveal Myself to the house of your father when they were in Egypt in Pharaoh's house?(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:27 One day a man of God came to Eli and gave him this message from the LORD: “I revealed myself to your ancestors when they were Pharaoh’s slaves in Egypt. (NLT)

Main Idea

God sends an unnamed prophet to confront Eli, beginning His judgment by reminding him of the grace He had shown his priestly family since Egypt. Eli’s family was accountable precisely because it had received so much from God.

Verse Explanation

Verse 27 begins God’s formal judgment against Eli’s household. Eli had confronted his sons, but he had not restrained them, and their corruption continued. God now sends an unnamed prophet, called simply “a man of God,” to confront Eli himself. The focus shifts from the sins of Hophni and Phinehas to Eli’s responsibility for what had been happening under his leadership.

“Then a man of God came to Eli.” “Man of God” is a title often used in the Old Testament for someone who speaks with God’s authority. The fact that he is unnamed keeps attention on the message rather than the messenger. This is significant because Eli was Israel’s priest and held considerable spiritual authority. Yet God sends someone else to speak His word to him. Eli’s position does not place him beyond correction or accountability. The prophet’s arrival also shows that God does not bring judgment on Eli’s house without explanation. Eli receives a direct word identifying what God had given his family, how they had violated that trust, and what the consequences would be.

“And said to him, ‘Thus says the LORD.’” This establishes the authority behind what follows. The prophet is not offering personal advice or an opinion about Eli’s parenting or leadership. He is delivering God’s judgment. Eli had heard reports from the people; now he hears from the LORD Himself.

“Did I not clearly reveal Myself to the house of your father” begins by reminding Eli of the grace and privilege his family had received. Before God addresses their failure, He reminds Eli of His own faithfulness to the priestly house. “The house of your father” refers to Eli’s priestly ancestry, the family of Aaron whom God had chosen for priestly service. Their position was not something Eli’s ancestors achieved. God had graciously revealed Himself to them and called them into His service.

“When they were in Egypt in Pharaoh’s house?” takes Eli back to the beginning of Israel’s deliverance. Aaron stood with Moses during God’s confrontation with Pharaoh and the exodus from slavery. God revealed Himself to Israel in that period and then appointed Aaron and his sons to the priesthood. The reference to Egypt reminds Eli that his family’s privileged position came entirely from God. They had once belonged to an enslaved people under Pharaoh. God delivered them, revealed Himself to them, and chose Aaron’s family for the extraordinary privilege of ministering before Him.

A pattern begins here that continues through verses 28–30. God first reminds Eli of what He had given his family. Only afterward does He confront Eli about what his family had done with those gifts. Verse 27 therefore begins judgment by remembering grace. The tragedy was that Hophni and Phinehas had inherited the position without honoring the God who gave it, and verse 12 had already given the devastating verdict: “they did not know the LORD.”

Study Notes

  • Man of God (Hebrew ’ish ha’elohim): a common title for a prophet (used of Moses, Deuteronomy 33:1; of Samuel, 1 Samuel 9:6–10; and of Elijah and Elisha). This prophet is never named.

  • Clearly reveal Myself: the Hebrew uses an emphatic construction (infinitive absolute with the verb galah, H1540, “to uncover, reveal”), literally “Did I not surely reveal Myself?” The question expects the answer “Yes.”

  • The house of your father: Aaron’s house, and more broadly the tribe of Levi. The Chronicler’s genealogies indicate that Eli’s line descended from Aaron’s younger son Ithamar (compare 1 Chronicles 24:3, where Ahimelech, a descendant of Eli, is “of the sons of Ithamar”), whereas Zadok, who later replaced Eli’s line, descended from Eleazar (1 Chronicles 6:4–8, 50–53).

  • In Egypt in Pharaoh’s house: God first revealed Himself to Aaron in Egypt (Exodus 4:27; 12:1). The phrase “in Pharaoh’s house” means under Pharaoh’s rule. Some ancient versions read “as slaves to Pharaoh’s house.”

Spirit & Life

  • God sends a voice when leaders will not correct themselves. When leadership fails to deal with its own corruption, God raises other voices to speak. No position in God’s house, however high, is beyond His correction. That is both a warning to leaders and a reminder that God has not abandoned His people to their leaders’ failures.

  • Divine rebuke begins in remembered kindness. God starts with grace: “Did I not reveal Myself to your father’s house?” Before He names Eli’s failure, He names His own faithfulness. In this passage the accountability rests on the grace that came first, and the same pattern of a Father who disciplines those He loves appears in Hebrews 12:5–6.

  • Spiritual heritage is a privilege, never a substitute. Eli’s sons came from a family to whom God had revealed Himself, yet they themselves did not know Him. A godly heritage, a Christian upbringing, or a family history of ministry is a gift, but each person must personally know and honor the God who gave it.

1 Samuel 2:28

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:28 Did I not choose him out of all the tribes of Israel to be My priest, to offer upon My altar, to burn incense, and to wear an ephod before Me? And did I not give to the house of your father all the offerings of the children of Israel made by fire?(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:28 I chose your ancestor Aaron from among all the tribes of Israel to be my priest, to offer sacrifices on my altar, to burn incense, and to wear the priestly vest as he served me. And I assigned the sacrificial offerings to you priests. (NLT)

Main Idea

God reminds Eli how much He had given Aaron’s house: calling, access to His altar and presence, and generous provision from Israel’s offerings. The corruption of Eli’s household did not come from any lack on God’s part, but from discontent with what He had given.

Verse Explanation

Verse 28 continues the message of the man of God to Eli. Verse 27 reminded Eli that God had revealed Himself to his priestly ancestors. Now God reminds him of the specific privileges He had given Aaron’s house.

“Did I not choose him out of all the tribes of Israel to be My priest” refers primarily to Aaron, whom God chose from the tribe of Levi to serve as Israel’s high priest, with his sons serving in the priesthood after him. The emphasis falls on God’s choice. Aaron did not appoint himself, and the priesthood was not a position his descendants created or earned. Their priestly authority belonged to God before it belonged to them; they were stewards of something God had entrusted to their family.

“To offer upon My altar” names one of the priesthood’s central responsibilities: presenting Israel’s sacrifices according to God’s instructions. Notice the repeated word “My”: “My priest,” “My altar.” God is emphasizing ownership. Hophni and Phinehas had behaved as though the sacrificial system existed for their benefit, manipulating sacrifices offered on God’s altar to get the meat they wanted. They treated God’s altar as a source of personal provision rather than something holy entrusted to their care.

“To burn incense” refers to another sacred priestly duty. Priests were allowed to enter a kind of service that ordinary Israelites could not perform. God had brought Aaron’s family unusually near to His presence and entrusted them with holy responsibilities.

“And to wear an ephod before Me?” The ephod was the priestly garment associated with service before the LORD. The phrase “before Me” is significant. Priestly ministry was performed in God’s presence and for God. Hophni and Phinehas may have grown used to the sanctuary and its rituals, but familiarity did not make those things ordinary. There is also a quiet contrast with Samuel. Verse 18 told us that Samuel, though still a child, ministered before the LORD “wearing a linen ephod.” Eli’s sons had the hereditary priesthood and all its privileges, yet they dishonored it, while the child with little status served faithfully.

“And did I not give to the house of your father all the offerings of the children of Israel made by fire?” God now reminds Eli that He had also provided materially for the priests. Certain portions of Israel’s sacrifices were specifically assigned to Aaron’s descendants for their support. This directly exposes the greed of Hophni and Phinehas. They could not claim that God had failed to provide for them. Their sin was not driven by real need. They were not satisfied with what God had assigned, and they wanted to decide for themselves which portions they would take. When their servants plunged the fork into the pot or demanded raw meat before the fat was burned, they were not trying to obtain something God had forgotten to provide. They were taking beyond what God had provided. Their corruption arose because what God had given was no longer enough for them.

Study Notes

  • Choose (Hebrew bachar, H977): God’s sovereign selection. Aaron’s appointment is recorded in Exodus 28:1, and God confirmed it when Aaron’s rod budded (Numbers 17:1–11).

  • Offer upon My altar: literally “to go up upon My altar,” the ramp leading up to the altar of burnt offering.

  • Burn incense (Hebrew qatar, H6999, with qetoreth, “incense”): offered each morning and evening on the golden altar before the veil (Exodus 30:1–10). Incense often pictures prayer rising before God (Psalm 141:2; Revelation 5:8; 8:3–4).

  • Ephod: the high priest’s ephod carried the breastpiece with the Urim and Thummim, used to seek God’s guidance (Exodus 28:6–30). The priestly calling therefore included access (the altar), intercession (incense), and seeking God’s will (the ephod).

  • Offerings made by fire (Hebrew ’ishshe, from ’ishsheh, H801): the portions of the sacrifices assigned to the priests as their provision (Leviticus 6:16–18; 7:31–36; Numbers 18:8–20; Deuteronomy 18:1–3). God called Himself the priests’ inheritance: “I am your portion and your inheritance among the children of Israel” (Numbers 18:20).

Spirit & Life

  • They had plenty and still grabbed for more. God recounts the priestly calling, access to His altar, intercession before Him, and seeking His guidance, and then the provision He gave. Jesus’ principle applies: “For everyone to whom much is given, from him much will be required” (Luke 12:48).

  • Dissatisfaction with God’s provision is at work in this sin. The passage shows greed, appetite, and discontent with the portion God assigned, and it points to a heart that has stopped believing that what God gives is enough. Contentment is not passivity; it is trust that what God gives is enough. “Now godliness with contentment is great gain” (1 Timothy 6:6).

  • Everything we have in ministry is “His.” The repeated “My” in this verse (My priest, My altar, before Me) is a healthy reminder for anyone who serves. Our calling, our gifts, our place of service, and the people we serve all belong to God. We are stewards, never owners.

1 Samuel 2:29

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:29 Why do you kick at My sacrifice and My offering which I have commanded in My dwelling place, and honor your sons more than Me, to make yourselves fat with the best of all the offerings of Israel My people?'(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:29 So why do you scorn my sacrifices and offerings? Why do you give your sons more honor than you give me — for you and they have become fat from the best offerings of my people Israel! (NLT)

Main Idea

God charges Eli directly: his household has treated God’s sacrifices with contempt, and Eli has honored his sons more than God while benefiting from what they took. When loyalty to his sons conflicted with loyalty to God, Eli protected his sons.

Verse Explanation

Verse 29 brings God’s accusation directly against Eli. Until now much of the chapter has focused on the wickedness of Hophni and Phinehas. Eli had rebuked them, but God now makes clear that Eli himself bears responsibility. God names the deeper issue as misplaced honor, and this verse is the interpretive center of the chapter.

“Why do you kick at My sacrifice and My offering.” To kick at something pictures contemptuous resistance, like an animal kicking against what it refuses to submit to. God is accusing Eli’s household of treating His sacrifices with contempt rather than reverence. The repeated word “My” matters again. These were not the priests’ sacrifices to handle however they pleased. Verse 28 emphasized what God had given the priesthood; verse 29 emphasizes what still belonged only to Him. Yet God addresses Eli as part of the “you.” Eli is not described as threatening worshipers or wielding the fork himself, but he knew what his sons were doing and allowed them to keep serving as priests. His failure to restrain them made him accountable for what he permitted under his authority.

“Which I have commanded in My dwelling place” emphasizes that God established the sacrificial system. The priests were not free to redesign it to suit themselves. “My dwelling place” refers to the sanctuary at Shiloh, the very place associated with God dwelling among His people. Their conduct was not mere administrative misconduct. They were treating God’s commands casually in the place dedicated to His presence.

“And honor your sons more than Me” identifies the heart of Eli’s failure and is one of the most important statements in the whole account. This is God’s own explicit judgment, and it is certain. To honor someone is to treat that person as weighty and significant. Eli had confronted his sons, told them their behavior was wrong, and warned them about sinning against the LORD. His problem was not that he could not tell right from wrong. When obedience to God came into conflict with his relationship with his sons, God says, Eli honored his sons more than Him.

Scripture does not state why Eli repeatedly confronted his sons with words but did not restrain them, and so what follows is inference. It appears that Eli’s attachment to his sons, a reluctance to act decisively against them, or a desire to preserve the relationship may have contributed to his failure. The narrative suggests that Eli gave greater practical weight to keeping his sons in their positions than to protecting the holiness of God’s worship. That may be why his rebuke in verses 23–25 was not enough. Words can express disapproval while actions reveal what we actually value most. This does not mean Eli should have stopped loving his sons. The problem was allowing that affection to override obedience to God and his responsibility to God’s people.

“To make yourselves fat with the best of all the offerings of Israel My people?” God now connects Eli to the benefits of his sons’ corruption. The plural “yourselves” is significant: it clearly implicates Eli, along with his sons, in benefiting from the system. The text does not tell us exactly what Eli ate or how, but the charge is that the household as a whole was prospering from offerings handled corruptly. The description is especially striking because 1 Samuel 4:18 later says Eli was “old and heavy.” The text does not say that Eli’s physical condition came entirely from eating stolen sacrificial meat, but the verbal connection is hard to miss. “The best” refers to the choicest portions. Hophni and Phinehas were not taking only what they needed to live; they wanted the preferred cuts and better preparation. And again God says, “Israel My people.” Just as the sacrifices belonged to God, so did the people. Eli’s household had been entrusted with both and was abusing both.

Verse 29 therefore reveals that Eli’s sin was deeper than weak parenting. He was a spiritual leader who knew serious wrongdoing was happening under his authority, was implicated in the benefit of the corrupt system, spoke against it, but did not stop it. His actions revealed a priority his words could not correct.

Study Notes

  • Kick (Hebrew ba‘at, H1163): to kick, used only here and in Deuteronomy 32:15, “But Jeshurun grew fat and kicked … Then he forsook God who made him.” The echo is pointed: in both places prosperity leads to fatness and fatness to rebellion.

  • Dwelling place (Hebrew ma‘on, H4583): a habitation or dwelling. The same word is used in verse 32.

  • Honor (Hebrew kabed, H3513): the root k-b-d means “to be heavy, weighty,” and so “to honor, glorify.” This root runs through these chapters: Eli honors (kabed) his sons (2:29); God will honor (kabed) those who honor Him (2:30); Eli is “heavy” (kabed) when he falls and dies (4:18); and his grandson is named Ichabod, “the glory (kabod) has departed” (4:21). Eli gave weight to the wrong things, and he became weighty in the wrong way.

  • Make yourselves fat (Hebrew bara’, H1254 in a rare form meaning “to fatten”): the plural “yourselves” includes Eli with his sons.

  • God’s later word to Samuel confirms the charge: “he did not restrain them” (3:13).

Spirit & Life

  • Eli was implicated in the plate. The pronoun is plural: “yourselves.” Whatever the exact mechanics of what Eli ate, the charge is that he shared in the benefit of a system he knew was wrong. Passivity toward sin is rarely neutral; it often means quietly benefiting from what we refuse to confront. It is worth asking whether we are enjoying the fruit of something we know God does not approve.

  • Whom or what have I honored above God? Eli’s failure was not that his sons mattered to him, but that they mattered more to him than obedience when he was forced to choose. We can truly love people and still refuse to let that love take the place that belongs to God. Jesus said, “He who loves son or daughter more than Me is not worthy of Me” (Matthew 10:37). The question for each of us is what we give the most weight to when loyalties collide: a child, a relationship, a career, our comfort, or God.

  • Prosperity can make the heart kick. Like Israel in Deuteronomy 32:15, Eli’s house grew fat and kicked. Blessing that is not received with humility and gratitude can breed contempt for the One who gave it. The more God gives, the more tenderly we should hold both the gift and the Giver.

1 Samuel 2:30

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:30 Therefore the LORD God of Israel says: "˜I said indeed that your house and the house of your father would walk before Me forever.' But now the LORD says: "˜Far be it from Me; for those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:30 “Therefore, the LORD, the God of Israel, says: I promised that your branch of the tribe of Levi would always be my priests. But I will honor those who honor me, and I will despise those who think lightly of me. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because Eli’s house had despised Him, God declares that its privileged priestly position will not protect it. God honors those who honor Him, while those who treat Him as light will themselves be treated as light.

Verse Explanation

Verse 30 announces the consequence of the accusation in verse 29 and states one of the central principles of this entire section. Eli had honored his sons more than God, and his household had treated God’s sacrifices with contempt. God now declares that the priestly privilege Eli’s family had enjoyed would not protect them from judgment.

“Therefore the LORD God of Israel says.” “Therefore” connects the judgment directly to what has just been described. God is not acting arbitrarily. The judgment follows their persistent abuse of the priesthood, their contempt for the sacrifices, and Eli’s failure to restrain his sons. Calling Him “the LORD God of Israel” also reminds Eli that the priesthood exists within God’s relationship with His people. Eli’s family does not own the priesthood; they serve the God of Israel and are accountable to Him.

“I said indeed that your house and the house of your father would walk before Me forever.” God refers to His earlier commitment to Aaron’s priestly family. The priesthood had been given to Aaron and his descendants as an enduring privilege. To “walk before Me” describes serving in God’s presence and carrying out priestly ministry before Him. God is not admitting that He broke an unconditional promise. The priesthood itself continues. What changes is the privileged position of Eli’s particular house within that priesthood. Participation in the privileges of Aaron’s line did not give a corrupt branch of the family immunity from judgment.

“But now the LORD says: ‘Far be it from Me.’” This marks the reversal. “Far be it from Me” is a strong rejection. God will not allow His past generosity toward Eli’s house to become protection for continued rebellion. In light of verses 27–28, their judgment comes not because they lacked God’s goodness, but because they answered His goodness with contempt.

“For those who honor Me I will honor” states the governing principle behind God’s dealings with Eli’s household. To honor God is to treat Him as worthy of the highest weight, value, reverence, and obedience. Honor is not merely what someone says about God. It becomes visible in the choices made when God’s will collides with competing loyalties or desires. God says He responds to those who honor Him by honoring them. This does not mean everyone who honors God will receive wealth, recognition, an important position, or an easy life; Samuel himself will know difficulty, rejection, and grief. The promise concerns God’s approval and His commitment ultimately to vindicate and uphold those who give Him first place. The surrounding story gives a living example. Samuel is still a child with little visible authority, but he serves faithfully before the LORD. Hophni and Phinehas hold the prestigious priestly position but dishonor God.

“And those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed.” To despise God does not necessarily mean openly declaring hatred for Him. Hophni and Phinehas despised God by treating what belonged to Him as though it carried little weight. They knew His instructions but treated their own desires as more important. The contrast is striking: to honor is to treat something as weighty, while to be lightly esteemed is to be treated as light or insignificant. Those who treat God as though He has little weight will discover that their own status carries little weight before Him. This directly overturns Eli’s priorities. He gave too much weight to his sons and too little to God, and now the honor and position his family valued will be taken from them.

The verse does not teach salvation by good behavior or offer a formula for earthly success. It speaks about how seriously God regards the response people make to Him, especially those entrusted with spiritual responsibility. The position someone holds today does not determine the honor God ultimately gives. What matters is the place God Himself occupies in that person’s life.

Study Notes

  • Walk before Me: a Hebrew idiom for living and serving in God’s presence and favor (compare Genesis 17:1; 24:40). Here it refers to priestly ministry.

  • Forever (Hebrew ‘ad ‘olam): in covenant contexts this often describes an enduring, ongoing arrangement. The Aaronic priesthood was called a “perpetual statute” (Exodus 29:9; compare Numbers 25:13, where God gives Phinehas son of Eleazar “a covenant of an everlasting priesthood”). The point here is narrower than a general theory of “forever” promises: God does not abolish the Aaronic priesthood, but He judges Eli’s particular branch of it, and later Scripture explicitly associates Abiathar’s removal with the fulfillment of this word (1 Kings 2:27).

  • Far be it from Me (Hebrew chalilah li): an exclamation of strong rejection, “Heaven forbid!”

  • Honor (kabed, H3513, “to treat as heavy, weighty”) and lightly esteemed (qalal, H7043, “to be light, trivial, contemptible”). Despise (bazah, H959): to regard with contempt, to treat as worthless. The wordplay between “heavy” and “light” is the key to the verse.

  • Fulfillment: Eli appears to have descended from Aaron’s son Ithamar. Solomon’s removal of Abiathar, Eli’s descendant, and the establishment of Zadok, a descendant of Eleazar, as leading priest (1 Kings 2:26–35) completed an important part of this judgment. “So Solomon removed Abiathar from being priest to the LORD, that he might fulfill the word of the LORD which He spoke concerning the house of Eli at Shiloh” (1 Kings 2:27).

Spirit & Life

  • The principle of divine honor. This verse is the moral center of the chapter. To honor God is to give Him weight: first place, first portion, true reverence. To despise Him is to treat Him as light. God does not merely observe this; He responds to it, honoring those who honor Him and making light of those who make light of Him. The question for every believer is practical: what does it look like for me to give God weight this week?

  • Privilege is never a license for irreverence. “I said indeed … but now the LORD says.” Eli’s house presumed that its heritage guaranteed its future. God’s gifts are real, but they are not a shield for persistent contempt. No ministry, heritage, or position is so secure that it can treat God lightly without consequence.

  • God’s honor is worth more than people’s. Samuel had little status and much favor with God; Hophni and Phinehas had high status and none. Jesus said, “If anyone serves Me, him My Father will honor” (John 12:26). The honor that lasts is the honor God gives, and He gives it to those who live for His glory rather than their own.

  • Mercy and judgment flow from one holy character. The holiness that stooped to lift a weeping woman in verse 1 is the same holiness that refuses to be treated lightly at His altar. They are not competing moods in God; they are one holy character meeting two different hearts.

1 Samuel 2:31 The time is coming when I will put an end to your family, so it will no longer serve as my priests. All the members of your family will die before their time. None will reach old age. (NLT)

Main Idea

God announces that He will break the strength of Eli’s priestly house, so that its members will die before reaching old age. The household that used its position to make itself secure would lose the very strength and continuity it relied on.

Verse Explanation

Verse 31 begins to describe the specific judgment God is bringing on Eli’s household. Verse 30 stated the principle: “those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed.” Now God explains what that will mean for Eli’s family. The priestly house that had enjoyed authority, privilege, and provision would lose its strength and experience repeated early death.

“Behold, the days are coming” introduces something God has determined will happen. The judgment will not necessarily happen all at once; its fulfillment unfolds over a long period. Some of it will begin very soon, with the death of Hophni and Phinehas on the same day (4:11) and of Eli himself (4:18). Other parts will unfold through later generations, including the killing of the priests at Nob (22:18–19) and the removal of Abiathar by Solomon (1 Kings 2:26–27), which 1 Kings 2:27 explicitly connects with the word of the LORD concerning Eli’s house.

“That I will cut off your arm and the arm of your father’s house.” The arm is a picture of strength, power, ability, and continuing influence. To cut off someone’s arm, figuratively, is to break his strength and ability to keep exercising power. God is not predicting a literal amputation; He is describing the breaking of the strength of Eli’s priestly house. This is significant because Eli’s family possessed considerable religious authority. They belonged to the priesthood, served at Israel’s central sanctuary, and enjoyed the privileges of that office. Their position may have seemed secure because it rested on generations of priestly heritage. God declares that He is able to remove the very strength on which that household depended. The phrase “your arm and the arm of your father’s house” widens the judgment beyond Eli personally.

“So that there will not be an old man in your house” explains one way the strength of Eli’s family will be broken: his descendants will repeatedly die before reaching old age. In the Old Testament world, reaching old age was normally seen as a blessing. It allowed a family line to be established across generations and let elders provide continuity, wisdom, and leadership. The absence of old men means more than individual deaths. It represents the weakening and instability of the whole household. The judgment is especially striking because verse 22 introduced Eli as “very old.” Eli himself enjoyed a long life, but the generations after him would not normally share that blessing.

This statement should not be taken to mean that every early death is evidence of divine judgment. This is a specific prophetic judgment on a particular household for clearly named sins. The text itself tells us why it is happening, and it should not become a general rule for interpreting anyone else’s lifespan.

There is also a striking reversal here. Eli’s sons used the priesthood to make themselves secure and prosperous, yet the very household they were trying to enrich would lose its strength and continuity. What Hannah celebrated in song is now beginning to unfold in history: “The bows of the mighty men are broken” (2:4), and “by strength no man shall prevail” (2:9).

Study Notes

  • Behold, the days are coming: a prophetic formula for certain future judgment or salvation (compare 2 Kings 20:17; Amos 4:2; Jeremiah 7:32; 31:31).

  • Arm (Hebrew zeroa‘, H2220): a common symbol of strength and power (Job 22:9; Psalm 10:15; 37:17, “For the arms of the wicked shall be broken”). Cut off (gada‘, H1438): to hew down or cut in pieces.

  • Fulfillment in stages: Hophni and Phinehas die in one day (4:11); Eli dies at ninety-eight (4:15, 18); eighty-five priests of Eli’s line are killed at Nob, with only Abiathar escaping (22:18–20); Abiathar is later removed by Solomon (1 Kings 2:26–27).

  • Theological clarification: this is not guilt transferred to innocent descendants (compare Ezekiel 18:20), but the loss of a forfeited office and the breaking of a household’s standing as a priestly dynasty.

Spirit & Life

  • The long shadow of unchecked sin. The fulfillment of this word unfolds over generations. A father’s passivity and his sons’ corruption had real consequences for the household that came after them, though each descendant remains responsible for his own life before God. Our faithfulness, or unfaithfulness, rarely affects only ourselves. What we tolerate today can shape the legacy we leave.

  • What we grasp to make ourselves secure can be the very thing we lose. Eli’s sons enriched their house by taking, and their house was emptied of strength. The security worth having is found only in God: “The name of the LORD is a strong tower; the righteous run to it and are safe” (Proverbs 18:10).

1 Samuel 2:32

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:32 And you will see an enemy in My dwelling place, despite all the good which God does for Israel. And there shall not be an old man in your house forever.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:32 You will watch with envy as I pour out prosperity on the people of Israel. But no members of your family will ever live out their days. (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli’s house would see distress and loss at the very sanctuary that had been the center of its privilege, even while God continued doing good for Israel. The failure of one priestly family could not stop God’s purposes for His people.

Verse Explanation

Verse 32 continues the judgment against Eli’s priestly house. Verse 31 declared that God would cut off the strength of Eli’s family. Verse 32 adds another painful dimension: Eli’s family would experience distress connected with the sanctuary itself, even while God continued to do good for Israel as a whole.

“And you will see an enemy in My dwelling place.” “My dwelling place” refers to God’s sanctuary, which was then at Shiloh, where Eli’s family served as priests and where the tabernacle and ark were located. The place that had been the center of their privilege would become associated with grief and loss. The most immediate fulfillment comes in 1 Samuel 4, when Israel is defeated by the Philistines, Hophni and Phinehas are killed, and the ark of God is captured. Shiloh itself later ceases to be Israel’s central sanctuary, and Jeremiah looks back on what God did to Shiloh as an example of judgment. It is wise not to tie the phrase too narrowly to one event, but the larger point is clear. There is painful irony here. Hophni and Phinehas had treated God’s dwelling place as though it existed for their benefit. Now the sanctuary that had been the source of their status and provision would become the place where they witnessed judgment. This warns that sacred structures do not guarantee divine approval.

“Despite all the good which God does for Israel” creates an important contrast between what happens to Eli’s house and what God continues to do for His people. God’s judgment on Eli’s family does not mean He has abandoned Israel. He can remove an unfaithful priestly household while continuing His good purposes for the nation. What is good for God’s people is not necessarily the preservation of a particular person, family, or institution in its current position. That fits verse 29, where God called Israel “My people.” God’s commitment was ultimately to His own purposes and His people, not to preserving Eli’s family in office regardless of its conduct.

“And there shall not be an old man in your house forever.” This repeats and strengthens the judgment of verse 31. “Forever” emphasizes the lasting effect of the judgment on Eli’s particular priestly house. It is not a claim that every biological descendant of Eli throughout all history must die young; the focus is the priestly house and its loss of enduring strength and prominence.

Verse 32 therefore draws an important distinction between the failure of spiritual leaders and the faithfulness of God. Eli’s house is going down, but God’s purposes for Israel continue. Verse 35 will state most fully what God is doing in its place.

Study Notes

  • Enemy (Hebrew tsar, H6862): this word can mean “adversary, enemy” or “distress, affliction” (from the root meaning “narrow, tight”). The NKJV takes it as “an enemy”; other translations render it “distress in My dwelling place.” The Hebrew of the whole first clause is difficult, and the Greek Old Testament and the Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel differ from the Masoretic text here and in verse 33.

  • Dwelling place (Hebrew ma‘on, H4583): the same word used in verse 29.

  • The fall of Shiloh: after the ark was captured (4:10–11), it never returned to Shiloh. Psalm 78:60–61 says God “forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh … and delivered His strength into captivity, and His glory into the enemy’s hand.” Jeremiah warned Jerusalem: “But go now to My place which was in Shiloh, where I set My name at the first, and see what I did to it because of the wickedness of My people Israel” (Jeremiah 7:12; compare 7:14; 26:6, 9). The biblical evidence for Shiloh’s later destruction is clearer than the precision available from archaeology, and excavators have proposed a destruction of the site around the eleventh century BC, though the dating and its connection to 1 Samuel 4 are debated.

  • When Phinehas’s wife gave birth after hearing the news, she named the child Ichabod, “The glory has departed from Israel!” because the ark had been captured (4:21–22).

Spirit & Life

  • Sacred structures do not guarantee divine approval. Within a few chapters the ark is captured and Shiloh ceases to be the center of Israel’s worship. God’s presence is His gift, not the guaranteed property of a corrupt priesthood. A religious institution can keep its building and its schedule while its leaders treat holy things lightly. The question for every church and every heart is not only whether the structures remain, but whether God is honored there.

  • “All the good which God does for Israel.” Even in a chapter full of judgment, God’s goodness toward His people is never withdrawn. He removes what harms them precisely because He is committed to their good.

1 Samuel 2:33

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:33 But any of your men whom I do not cut off from My altar shall consume your eyes and grieve your heart. And all the descendants of your house shall die in the flower of their age.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:33 The few not cut off from serving at my altar will survive, but only so their eyes can go blind and their hearts break, and their children will die a violent death. (NLT)

Main Idea

Some of Eli’s descendants would remain at God’s altar for a time, but only to live in grief as they watched their house decline and its members die in their prime. Survival would not mean restoration.

Verse Explanation

Verse 33 continues the judgment against Eli’s household but adds a painful qualification. God will not immediately remove every descendant of Eli from priestly service. Some will remain for a time, but their survival will not mean the judgment has been cancelled. They will live to witness the decline and suffering of their own family.

“But any of your men whom I do not cut off from My altar” refers to descendants of Eli who will stay connected with priestly service after the first judgments fall. “My altar” again stresses that the altar belongs to God. The priesthood was a stewardship He granted, and He had authority both to appoint people to it and to remove them. The judgment would progressively weaken the family’s priestly position rather than eliminate every member at one moment. The family survives, but its former security and prominence do not.

“Shall consume your eyes and grieve your heart.” The language describes intense sorrow from watching what happens to the family. Eyes that are “consumed” waste away through grief, longing, or anguish over what they see. The judgment has an emotional dimension as well as a physical and institutional one. Some descendants will live long enough to see its consequences unfolding around them. There is a tragic connection with Eli himself. In chapter 4 he will live long enough to hear that Israel has been defeated, his two sons have died, and the ark of God has been captured. The news about the ark finally overwhelms him, and he falls backward from his seat and dies. Eli personally begins to experience the grief described here.

“And all the descendants of your house shall die in the flower of their age.” Members of Eli’s household will die in the strength and prime of life rather than reaching old age. This develops what God said in verses 31–32. “All the descendants” is strong prophetic language describing what will characterize the condemned priestly house; the emphasis is on the destruction of its continuing strength as a priestly dynasty, not a biological prediction about every possible descendant in all later history. Survival itself does not necessarily signal restoration. A descendant such as Abiathar may escape one judgment and still live under the larger sentence on the family.

The verse also deepens the consequences of Eli’s failure. He honored his sons more than God (v. 29) and apparently left them in their positions because he would not act decisively against his own family. But shielding his sons from discipline did not protect them. Both would die, and the consequences would reach into later generations. His unwillingness to restrain them may have seemed like preserving them, but it did not save them.

Study Notes

  • Consume your eyes (Hebrew kalah, H3615, “to finish, waste away,” with ‘ayin, “eye”): a Hebrew idiom for the failing of the eyes through grief or longing (compare Leviticus 26:16; Deuteronomy 28:32, 65; Psalm 69:3). Grieve your heart (’adab, H109): to cause pain or sorrow; literally “to make your soul (nephesh) languish.”

  • In the flower of their age: literally “they shall die as men” (’anashim), that is, as grown men in their prime rather than as old men. The Greek Old Testament and the Dead Sea Scroll of Samuel read “shall fall by the sword of men,” which fits the massacre at Nob (1 Samuel 22:18–19).

  • Eli’s line after Hophni and Phinehas is difficult to trace with certainty. First Samuel 14:3 names Ahijah son of Ahitub, the son of Phinehas, the son of Eli, as priest in Saul’s day, and 22:9, 11, 20 names Ahimelech son of Ahitub as the priest at Nob, whose son Abiathar escaped. Whether Ahijah and Ahimelech are one man under two names or two brothers is uncertain, and other passages (for example 2 Samuel 8:17; 1 Chronicles 24:6) present the names of Abiathar and Ahimelech in a different order. The genealogy is probably compressed, and the certain point is that Abiathar belonged to Eli’s line.

Spirit & Life

  • What Eli would not surrender in obedience, he lost in judgment. Eli would not act against his sons in order to honor God, and in the end he lost them anyway. Protecting people from the consequences of their sin is not the same as loving them. True love for others is willing to risk the relationship in order to honor God and seek their good.

  • A father’s passivity can cost his household for generations. The pain of this judgment is felt most by those who come after. Our choices about how to respond to known sin, especially in our families and ministries, shape more than our own lives, though God does not hold descendants guilty for sins they did not commit.

1 Samuel 2:34

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:34 Now this shall be a sign to you that will come upon your two sons, on Hophni and Phinehas: in one day they shall die, both of them.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:34 And to prove that what I have said will come true, I will cause your two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, to die on the same day! (NLT)

Main Idea

God gives Eli a specific sign to confirm the whole prophecy: Hophni and Phinehas will both die on the same day. Their priestly position, their closeness to the ark, and their descent from Aaron cannot protect them from the God whose holiness they treated lightly.

Verse Explanation

Verse 34 gives Eli a specific, near-term sign that the larger judgment against his house will truly happen. Much of the prophecy in verses 31–33 concerns future generations and will unfold over many years. So God gives Eli one event he will be able to recognize as confirmation of the whole message: both his sons will die on the same day.

“Now this shall be a sign to you” means that the death of Hophni and Phinehas will authenticate what the man of God has announced. A sign in Scripture often serves as visible confirmation that God’s word is true and that what He has declared will come to pass. Eli would not live to see every stage of his house’s decline, but he would see its beginning. When the specified sign happened exactly as predicted, it would confirm that the rest of the judgment was just as certain.

“That will come upon your two sons, on Hophni and Phinehas” brings the prophecy back to the two men whose conduct began this section. They are now named individually. Their deaths are not presented as random casualties. The chapter has carefully established the reason: they were “corrupt” and “did not know the LORD” (v. 12); their sin was “very great before the LORD” (v. 17); they ignored their father’s warning, and “the LORD desired to kill them” (v. 25). Their deaths come at the end of a long account of deliberate and persistent rebellion.

“In one day they shall die, both of them.” The precision makes the prophecy unmistakable. This is fulfilled in 1 Samuel 4. Israel goes into battle against the Philistines, and Hophni and Phinehas accompany the ark of the covenant into the fighting. Israel is defeated, the ark is captured, and “the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, died” (4:11). The fulfillment is especially significant because Hophni and Phinehas seem to treat the ark as though its presence can guarantee victory. They have spent years dishonoring the God whose presence the ark represents, yet when Israel is threatened they carry it into battle as though the sacred object itself can secure divine help. Their priestly position cannot protect them. Their nearness to the ark cannot protect them. Their descent from Aaron cannot protect them.

For Eli the sign is especially painful. God had named the center of his failure: “you … honor your sons more than Me” (v. 29). The two sons Eli would not restrain are the very sons he will lose together. This does not mean Eli caused their sins, or that they were judged merely because of his weakness; they are repeatedly held responsible for their own conduct. But Eli’s refusal to use the authority entrusted to him allowed their corruption to continue, and he shared responsibility for permitting it.

The verse also prepares for verse 35. After declaring that Hophni and Phinehas will die, God promises a faithful priest. The problem is not priesthood itself but unfaithful priests.

Study Notes

  • Sign (Hebrew ’oth, H226): a token or confirming event that authenticates a prophetic word (compare 1 Samuel 10:1–9; 1 Kings 13:3; Isaiah 7:14; 38:7).

  • Fulfillment: 1 Samuel 4:1–11. After an initial defeat, the elders ask, “Why has the LORD defeated us today …? Let us bring the ark of the covenant of the LORD from Shiloh to us, that when it comes among us it may save us” (4:3). Hophni and Phinehas come with it (4:4). About thirty thousand Israelites fall, the ark is taken, and both sons die (4:10–11).

  • The news reaches Eli, who is sitting by the road watching, “for his heart trembled for the ark of God” (4:13). When he hears the ark is captured, he falls backward and dies (4:18). His daughter-in-law names her son Ichabod (4:21).

Spirit & Life

  • God’s warnings are as sure as His promises. Hophni and Phinehas died on the same day in battle, exactly as announced. The God who kept His promise to Hannah kept His warning to Eli. His faithfulness cuts both ways.

  • Sacred objects cannot substitute for a surrendered heart. Israel carried the ark into battle as if it were a charm, and the men carrying it had long despised the God it represented. This lesson develops most fully in chapter 4, but this verse already announces the end of men who thought their office would protect them. It is always possible to treat religious things, a church, a ritual, a symbol, as a guarantee of God’s help while ignoring God Himself. God cannot be used; He must be honored.

  • Warning before judgment can be understood as mercy. God told Eli in advance what would happen, and later Eli received the word with humility: “It is the LORD. Let Him do what seems good to Him” (3:18). The text does not itself call the sign a mercy, but it does show that God speaks and explains before He acts, and gives opportunity to bow before Him.

1 Samuel 2:35

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:35 Then I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest who shall do according to what is in My heart and in My mind. I will build him a sure house, and he shall walk before My anointed forever.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:35 “Then I will raise up a faithful priest who will serve me and do what I desire. I will establish his family, and they will be priests to my anointed kings forever. (NLT)

Main Idea

God promises to raise up a faithful priest who will serve according to God’s own heart, and to give him an enduring house that serves alongside His anointed king. Eli’s house was failing, but God’s purpose was not.

Verse Explanation

Verse 35 turns from judgment to replacement, and it gives the chapter’s fullest statement of what God does when leaders fail: He raises up a faithful servant of His own. God has announced the decline of Eli’s priestly house and the coming death of Hophni and Phinehas. Now He explains that the failure of Eli’s family will not bring His purposes to an end.

“Then I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest.” God Himself provides the replacement. Eli’s house is being brought down, but God will “raise up” another servant. The words “for Myself” are especially important in light of the sins of Hophni and Phinehas. They treated the priesthood as though it existed for them, using the sacrifices to satisfy their appetites and their position to serve themselves. The priest God raises up will be “for Myself.” The priesthood exists first for God and His purposes. “Faithful” describes someone dependable, trustworthy, and reliable, the opposite of Eli’s sons, who were entrusted with holy responsibilities and proved unfaithful.

Identifying this “faithful priest” historically requires care. Samuel certainly fits the description of a faithful servant whom God is raising up, and his rise is set against the decline of Eli’s sons throughout the chapter. However, Samuel was not a descendant of Aaron serving in the normal hereditary priesthood, and the later details of this promise point beyond him. The strongest historical fulfillment of the dynastic promise is generally associated with Zadok. Abiathar, a later descendant of Eli, was removed from the priesthood by Solomon, and Solomon then put Zadok in his place (1 Kings 2:27, 35). Samuel takes part in the immediate movement away from Eli’s corrupt leadership, while Zadok and his line more directly fit the continuing priestly-house dimension of the promise.

“Who shall do according to what is in My heart and in My mind.” This is what will distinguish the faithful priest from Eli’s household: he will care about what God wants. “Heart” and “mind” should not be pressed into two separate parts of God; together they express God’s will, purpose, and desire. The faithful priest will carry out his ministry according to God’s purposes rather than his own appetites. This is the exact opposite of Hophni and Phinehas, whose behavior kept asking, in effect, “What do I want?” The faithful priest asks a different question: “What does God want?” There is also a contrast with Eli. Eli knew what God wanted and condemned his sons’ behavior in words, but when obedience required decisive action against his own sons, he did not follow through. God wants more than someone who knows His will or speaks correctly about it. The faithful priest “shall do” according to what is in God’s heart. Faithfulness is shown in action.

“I will build him a sure house.” “House” here means an enduring family line. God had just announced that Eli’s house would lose its strength and continuity. Now He promises stability to the faithful priestly line He will establish. The security comes from God: “I will build.” Eli’s sons tried to secure themselves by misusing their position, and their house would fall. The faithful priest receives stability because God Himself establishes him. This fits Zadok’s line especially well, which became the leading priestly line after Abiathar’s removal and held a significant place in Israel’s later history.

“And he shall walk before My anointed forever.” “My anointed” refers to the LORD’s anointed king. Israel still has no king at this point, yet Hannah’s prayer had already ended with “He will give strength to His king, and exalt the horn of His anointed” (2:10). Now the prophecy about the priesthood again anticipates the coming monarchy. The faithful priest will serve alongside God’s anointed king, which becomes clear when Zadok serves under David and Solomon and his line becomes associated with the Davidic throne.

The word “forever” gives the statement a forward-looking quality. At the immediate historical level, the promise concerns a faithful priestly line serving alongside God’s anointed kings, with Zadok as the strongest dynastic fulfillment. As Scripture unfolds, both priesthood and anointed kingship find their fullest expression in Jesus Christ, not as one more equivalent historical referent beside Samuel and Zadok but as the canonical culmination of the pattern. That fuller connection does not erase the historical meaning. But within the larger biblical story, the picture of a faithful priest serving before God’s anointed feeds the growing expectation that God will establish righteous priesthood and righteous kingship. Verse 35 therefore holds both judgment and hope. Eli’s house is failing, but God’s purpose is not.

Study Notes

  • Faithful (Hebrew ne’eman, from ’aman, H539): reliable, trustworthy, established. Sure in “a sure house” is the same word: bayit ne’eman, “a faithful, established house.” The word plays on the idea of firmness.

  • The same vocabulary is used of Samuel, “established (ne’eman) as a prophet of the LORD” (3:20), and in the Davidic covenant, “your house and your kingdom shall be established (ne’eman) forever before you” (2 Samuel 7:16). Abigail uses the same phrase of David: “For the LORD will certainly make for my lord an enduring (ne’eman) house” (1 Samuel 25:28).

  • My anointed (Hebrew meshichi, from mashiach, H4899): the LORD’s anointed king, the source of the term “Messiah.” See the note on 2:10.

  • Zadok’s line: Zadok descended from Aaron’s son Eleazar (1 Chronicles 6:3–8, 50–53). He served with Abiathar under David (2 Samuel 8:17; 15:24–29) and became sole chief priest under Solomon (1 Kings 2:35). Ezekiel’s vision of the restored temple gives the sons of Zadok a special place because they remained faithful (Ezekiel 44:15; 48:11).

  • Interpretive note: interpreters have identified the faithful priest as Samuel, Zadok, or ultimately Christ. These are best held as layers rather than rivals: Samuel as the immediate faithful servant raised up as Eli’s house declines, Zadok as the strongest dynastic and historical fulfillment, and Christ as the canonical culmination of faithful priesthood and kingship, not simply another historical referent.

Spirit & Life

  • The faithful Priest after God’s own heart. Samuel and Zadok were partial fulfillments, but only Jesus perfectly does all that is in the Father’s heart: “I always do those things that please Him” (John 8:29). He is “a merciful and faithful High Priest in things pertaining to God” (Hebrews 2:17), “faithful to Him who appointed Him” (Hebrews 3:2). Remarkably, in Christ the faithful Priest and the Anointed King become one person, priest and king together, after the order of Melchizedek (Hebrews 7). The priest who was to walk before God’s anointed is, in Jesus, the Anointed Himself.

  • God looks for servants who ask, “What does God want?” The most important description of the faithful priest is not his title, ancestry, or position, but that he does what is in God’s heart. That is precisely what was missing in Eli’s house. It is the question every servant of God must keep asking: am I using God’s work to satisfy myself, or can He trust me to carry out what matters to Him?

  • God builds what lasts. Eli’s sons tried to secure their house by taking, and it collapsed. The faithful priest receives a sure house because God builds it. “Unless the LORD builds the house, they labor in vain who build it” (Psalm 127:1). Security in ministry, family, and life comes from faithfulness to God, not from grasping.

  • God’s purposes never depend on failing people. In this chapter God raises Samuel and promises a faithful priest while Eli’s house collapses, and the last word over the failure of leaders is not collapse but God’s own provision. When His house is corrupted, He is already preparing what comes next, and ultimately that provision is His Son.

1 Samuel 2:36

NKJV

1 Samuel 2:36 And it shall come to pass that everyone who is left in your house will come and bow down to him for a piece of silver and a morsel of bread, and say, "Please, put me in one of the priestly positions, that I may eat a piece of bread." ' "(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:36 Then all of your surviving family will bow before him, begging for money and food. ‘Please,’ they will say, ‘give us jobs among the priests so we will have enough to eat.’” (NLT)

Main Idea

The family that abused the priesthood to seize the best of Israel’s sacrifices would be reduced to begging for a minor priestly position just to have bread to eat. The judgment reverses the very sin that marked Eli’s sons, fulfilling the great reversal of Hannah’s song.

Verse Explanation

Verse 36 completes the prophetic judgment against Eli’s house. The family that had abused the priesthood to obtain the best portions of Israel’s sacrifices would eventually be reduced to asking for the smallest priestly position just to have enough to eat. The judgment directly reverses the sin that had characterized Hophni and Phinehas.

“And it shall come to pass that everyone who is left in your house” shows that Eli’s family will not immediately disappear. As verse 33 indicated, some descendants will remain after judgment falls, but they will no longer have the strength, security, and privileged position the family once enjoyed. Those who survive will live with the consequences of the judgment pronounced against their house.

“Will come and bow down to him” describes a dramatic reversal of status. Eli’s family had held honored positions in Israel’s priesthood. Now its descendants will have to humble themselves before the faithful priestly line God establishes. Bowing down expresses submission and dependence. This connects with verse 30: Eli’s house had been honored, but because they dishonored God, they lose that honor.

“For a piece of silver and a morsel of bread” shows how completely their circumstances will change. They will be seeking basic provision. This is especially striking because God had already provided generously for Eli’s priestly family. Verse 28 reminded Eli that God had given the priests portions from Israel’s offerings. Their problem was not lack of provision but dissatisfaction with what God had provided. Now the judgment fits the offense. Those who would not be satisfied with their God-given portion will be reduced to asking for a morsel of bread. This also recalls Hannah’s song: “Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread, and the hungry have ceased to hunger” (2:5). What Hannah described as a principle of God’s rule is now worked out in Eli’s house.

“And say, ‘Please, put me in one of the priestly positions.’” Eli’s descendants will no longer assume that priestly office belongs to them. They will have to ask for a place. The request is modest. They are not asking to regain leadership over the priesthood, only for some minor assignment that would let them take part in priestly service. Ancestry had given them a prominent place in Israel’s worship, but ancestry alone could not preserve that place when they persistently dishonored God. Historically, Abiathar, after supporting Adonijah rather than Solomon, was removed from office, and 1 Kings 2:27 says this happened “that he might fulfill the word of the LORD which He spoke concerning the house of Eli at Shiloh.” Zadok was placed in his position. Scripture does not record exactly how every element of verse 36 was experienced by Eli’s later descendants, so we should not invent a specific scene of someone begging Zadok for bread. The text gives the prophetic picture, and later Scripture identifies Abiathar’s removal as part of its fulfillment.

“That I may eat a piece of bread” exposes the irony of the whole account. Hophni and Phinehas turned priestly ministry into a way of satisfying their appetites. At the beginning of the story, Eli’s sons are saying, in effect, “Give me the meat I want.” At the end of the prophecy, Eli’s descendants are pictured saying, “Please give me a position so that I can eat a piece of bread.” That reversal is deliberate and severe.

Chapter 2 ends by showing the final result: the family that tried to use spiritual position to satisfy itself loses the position it abused. Meanwhile God is already raising up Samuel and has promised a faithful priest who will do according to what is in His heart and mind.

Study Notes

  • Piece of silver (Hebrew ’agorah, H95): a small coin or payment, used only here in the Old Testament. Morsel of bread (kikkar lechem): literally “a round of bread,” a small loaf. Piece of bread (path lechem, from path, H6595): a fragment or crumb.

  • Put me (Hebrew sephach, H5596): to attach or join, “attach me to one of the priestly offices.”

  • Priestly positions (Hebrew kehunnoth, from kehunnah, H3550): the offices or duties of priesthood.

  • Historical fulfillment: Abiathar escaped Nob and served David faithfully for years (22:20–23; 2 Samuel 15:24–29), but he supported Adonijah’s attempt to take the throne (1 Kings 1:7). Solomon spared his life because of his past service to David but banished him to his estate at Anathoth (1 Kings 2:26–27). Jeremiah later came from a priestly family at Anathoth (Jeremiah 1:1), though Scripture does not say whether it was descended from Abiathar.

  • Compare Ezekiel 44:10–16, where Levites who went astray are reduced to lesser service while the faithful sons of Zadok minister at the altar, a later example of the same principle.

Spirit & Life

  • From stolen fat to begging bread. The family that seized the best meat by force will one day beg for a scrap of bread and a coin. This closes the circle opened in Hannah’s song: “Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread” (2:5). The chapter that began with a barren woman lifted up ends with a proud house brought low. The great reversal has come full circle.

  • Two kinds of ministry. Eli’s house shows what happens when ministry becomes centered on what the servant wants; the faithful priest represents ministry centered on what God wants. Every generation of God’s people must choose which of these will shape its leaders, its churches, and its own heart.

  • Where Eli’s sons took the offering by force, Jesus became the offering. This is a canonical meditation rather than the immediate meaning of “bread” in the judgment oracle. The true Priest did not grasp at bread; He gave Himself as “the bread of life” (John 6:35), and those who come to Him never need to beg for a place. In Christ, the lowly are given a seat at the table, and the hungry are filled with good things (Luke 1:53).
