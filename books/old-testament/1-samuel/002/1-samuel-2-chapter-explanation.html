Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Hannah prays in a song of rejoicing, declaring that her heart rejoices in the LORD and that her horn, a picture of strength and dignity, is exalted in Him. She can smile at her enemies because her joy rests in the LORD’s salvation, not merely in her changed circumstances.

“And Hannah prayed and said:”

Hannah's song follows directly on the dedication of Samuel (1:28). The narrator calls it a prayer, though it is filled with praise. The woman who once prayed silently in bitterness (1:10–13) now prays aloud in joy. The setting is still Shiloh, and the son she has just given to the LORD is with her.

“My heart rejoices in the LORD;”

Her first words center on the LORD. “My heart,” the seat of her inner life that had been grieved (1:8), now “rejoices.” Her joy is “in the LORD,” not first in the child. That order matters. She has just handed Samuel over; her joy rests in the God who answered her.

“My horn is exalted in the LORD.”

“Horn” is a picture of strength and dignity, drawn from the horns of a strong animal such as the wild ox (Deuteronomy 33:17). To have one's horn “exalted” meant to be raised from humiliation to honor and strength (Psalm 75:10). For years Hannah's head had been bowed under Peninnah's taunts; now God had lifted it. The image will return in the last line of the song, where the LORD exalts “the horn of His anointed” (2:10).

“I smile at my enemies,”

“I smile” is literally ‘my mouth is wide over,’ a picture of open-mouthed triumph. Hannah's “enemies” include Peninnah, who provoked her (1:6), but the plural widens the scope. She can face every adversary without fear.

“Because I rejoice in Your salvation.”

The reason is God's “salvation,” His deliverance. Hannah speaks directly to God now (“Your salvation”). Her joy rests in the LORD's saving act, not merely in her changed circumstances. What God had done for one barren woman she understood as salvation, the same word Israel used for the deliverance at the Red Sea (Exodus 15:2).

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Genre. Hannah's song is Hebrew poetry, built on parallel lines that restate or develop one another. Its themes and vocabulary resemble the Psalms, especially Psalm 113, and David's song in 2 Samuel 22, which together with Hannah's song frames the books of Samuel.

Mary's song. Mary's song in Luke 1:46–55 echoes Hannah's closely: rejoicing in God's salvation, the regard for a lowly maidservant, the scattering of the proud, the lifting of the humble, and the filling of the hungry. Mary draws on Hannah's words to praise God for the coming of the Messiah.

Vocabulary. “Horn” (qeren) is used as a figure of strength here and in 2:10. The literal horn of oil used to anoint David (16:1, 13) uses the same word, though the figurative and literal uses should be distinguished.

Verse Explanation

Hannah confesses that no one is holy like the LORD, that there is no God besides Him, and that there is no rock like our God. Her praise moves from what God has done for her to who He is.

“No one is holy like the LORD,”

Hannah's praise moves from what God has done for her to who He is. “Holy” means set apart, utterly distinct, morally pure, and majestic. “No one is holy like the LORD” echoes Moses' song after the Red Sea: “Who is like You, glorious in holiness” (Exodus 15:11). God's holiness is the first quality Hannah names. It will become a theme of the following chapters, when the men of Beth Shemesh cry, “Who is able to stand before this holy LORD God?” (6:20).

“For there is none besides You,”

“There is none besides You” declares that the LORD alone is God. No other god compares with Him because no other god truly exists (compare Deuteronomy 32:39; Isaiah 45:5). Hannah speaks this in the sanctuary at Shiloh, where Eli's sons treated the LORD with contempt.

“Nor is there any rock like our God.”

“Rock” is a common biblical image for God's strength, stability, and protection. Moses sang, “He is the Rock, His work is perfect” (Deuteronomy 32:4). A rock does not move or fail; those who take refuge in it are safe. Hannah calls Him “our God,” widening her praise from her own experience to the whole people.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Cross-reference. David's song uses the same pairing of God and rock: “For who is God, except the LORD? And who is a rock, except our God?” (Psalm 18:31; compare 2 Samuel 22:32). The parallel is one of several links between Hannah's song and David's.

Verse Explanation

Hannah warns the proud to stop their arrogant talk, because the LORD is the God of knowledge and He weighs every action. Boasting is foolish before a God who sees and evaluates all that people do.

“Talk no more so very proudly;”

Hannah now addresses the proud directly. “Talk no more so very proudly” warns those who speak arrogantly. The Hebrew repeats the word for ‘proud’ for emphasis, ‘proudly, proudly.’ Peninnah's taunts may be in view, but the warning reaches anyone who boasts against God or His people.

“Let no arrogance come from your mouth,”

“Arrogance” describes insolent, presumptuous speech. The proud speak as though they answer to no one.

“For the LORD is the God of knowledge;”

The reason boasting is foolish is that “the LORD is the God of knowledge.” He knows everything: every word, every motive, every hidden act. Nothing escapes Him.

“And by Him actions are weighed.”

God “weighs” actions, measuring them as on a scale. Proverbs says, “the LORD weighs the spirits” (Proverbs 16:2). He evaluates not only what people do but why they do it. Boasting is foolish before a God who sees and evaluates all that people do. The warning applies within Shiloh itself, where Eli's sons acted as though no one were watching.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Textual note. The written Hebrew text reads ‘and not by Him actions are weighed,’ using a negative particle, while the traditional reading marked for pronunciation reads ‘by Him,’ which the NKJV follows. The two words sound alike in Hebrew. The traditional reading fits the context and is followed by most translations.

Verse Explanation

Those who were full must hire themselves out for bread while the hungry are satisfied, and the barren woman bears seven while the mother of many grows feeble. Hannah sets her own story within a wider pattern of God reversing human conditions, with seven expressing complete blessing.

“Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread,”

The reversals continue in daily life. Those who once had plenty must now work for food. “Hired themselves out” describes the humiliation of the formerly well-off who must labor for a meal.

“And the hungry have ceased to hunger.”

Meanwhile, “the hungry,” the poor and needy, are satisfied. Mary echoes this line: “He has filled the hungry with good things, And the rich He has sent away empty” (Luke 1:53).

“Even the barren has borne seven,”

Now Hannah's own story appears in the pattern. “The barren has borne seven.” “Seven” expresses complete and abundant blessing. Hannah would eventually bear six children (Samuel and five more, 2:21), so the number speaks of fullness rather than an exact count.

“And she who has many children has become feeble.”

The mother of many “has become feeble,” grown weak and forlorn. The line recalls Peninnah, though the song does not name her. Hannah sets her own story within a wider pattern of God reversing human conditions. Her personal experience becomes a testimony to how God works with all people.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Number. Seven is used in Scripture as a number of completeness (compare Ruth 4:15, where Ruth is better to Naomi “than seven sons”). The song's “seven” is poetic and should not be counted against 2:21.

Canonical echo. Isaiah 54:1 calls the barren woman to sing because her children will outnumber those of the married wife, and Paul applies that word to the gospel in Galatians 4:27. The pattern of the barren made fruitful by God runs through Scripture.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

The LORD kills and makes alive; He brings down to the grave and brings up. Life and death themselves lie under His authority.

“The LORD kills and makes alive;”

Hannah's song now reaches the most basic reversal: life and death. “The LORD kills and makes alive.” He holds authority over both. Moses sang the same truth: “I kill and I make alive; I wound and I heal” (Deuteronomy 32:39). No power in heaven or earth shares that authority.

“He brings down to the grave and brings up.”

“The grave” translates Sheol, the realm of the dead. God “brings down” to it and “brings up” from it. Life and death themselves lie under His authority. The line may describe God's power to restore those near death, as He had brought new life out of Hannah's barren womb. In the light of the whole Bible, it also anticipates God's power to raise the dead.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Sheol. In the Old Testament, Sheol is the place or state of the dead. To be “brought up” from Sheol can describe rescue from mortal danger (Psalm 30:3) as well as God's ultimate power over death. The line does not by itself present a full doctrine of resurrection, but it affirms that death is under God's control.

Verse Explanation

The LORD makes poor and makes rich; He brings low and lifts up. Wealth and status are not finally in human hands but under His rule.

“The LORD makes poor and makes rich;”

The reversals extend to wealth. “The LORD makes poor and makes rich.” Riches and poverty are not finally determined by human effort or luck, but by God's hand.

“He brings low and lifts up.”

The summary line states the pattern of the whole song and of the whole book: “He brings low and lifts up.” Wealth and status are not finally in human hands but under His rule. In 1 Samuel this pattern will unfold in history: Eli's house brought low and Samuel raised up, Saul brought low and David raised up. The line also anticipates the larger biblical pattern seen supremely in Christ, who humbled Himself and was highly exalted (Philippians 2:8–9).

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Theme of the book. The reversal stated here organizes much of 1 Samuel. The proud house of Eli falls (2:30–34; 4:11), the boy who serves rises (3:19–21), the tall king is rejected (15:23), and the youngest son is chosen (16:11–13).

Canonical comparison. Psalm 75:7 says, “He puts down one, And exalts another.” Jesus taught, “whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted” (Matthew 23:12). The comparison with Philippians 2 is canonical rather than a direct prophecy in Hannah's words.

Verse Explanation

God raises the poor from the dust and the beggar from the ash heap to seat them among princes and give them a throne of glory. He has every right to do this, because the pillars of the earth belong to Him and He has set the world upon them.

“He raises the poor from the dust And lifts the beggar from the ash heap,”

God lifts the lowest. “The dust” and “the ash heap” picture the place of the most destitute, the garbage mound outside a town where outcasts sat. Psalm 113 uses almost the same words (Psalm 113:7–8).

“To set them among princes And make them inherit the throne of glory.”

God raises the poor not merely to survival but to honor. He seats them “among princes” and gives them “the throne of glory,” a seat of honor. The reversal is complete: from the ash heap to the throne.

“For the pillars of the earth are the LORD's, And He has set the world upon them.”

The reason God can do this is that He owns and governs the world. “The pillars of the earth” is a poetic picture of the earth's foundations (compare Psalm 75:3; Job 38:4–6). The Creator who set the world on its foundations has every right to rearrange human fortunes as He wills.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Cross-reference. Psalm 113:7–8 reads, “He raises the poor out of the dust, And lifts the needy out of the ash heap, That He may seat him with princes.” The close parallel shows how Hannah's praise belongs to Israel's wider worship of the God who lifts the lowly.

“Throne of glory.” “Glory” translates kavod, the same root that becomes significant later in the book: Eli honors his sons above God (2:29–30), and Ichabod's name declares that the glory has departed (4:21–22). Here, God gives glory to the poor.

Main Idea

The LORD will break His adversaries, thunder against them from heaven, and judge the ends of the earth, and He will give strength to His king and exalt the horn of His anointed. Verse Explanation

The LORD will break His adversaries, thunder against them from heaven, and judge the ends of the earth, and He will give strength to His king and exalt the horn of His anointed. Spoken before Israel had a king, these closing words are prophetic, anticipating the kings Samuel will anoint and, in the wider canon, the Messiah.

“The adversaries of the LORD shall be broken in pieces;”

The song moves toward its climax. “The adversaries of the LORD” are those who oppose Him, and they will be shattered.

“From heaven He will thunder against them.”

God will “thunder” from heaven against His enemies. The image of God's thunder appears in David's song (Psalm 18:13) and will be fulfilled literally later in 1 Samuel, when “the LORD thundered with a loud thunder upon the Philistines” at Mizpah (7:10).

“The LORD will judge the ends of the earth.”

God's judgment reaches “the ends of the earth.” Hannah's vision has grown from her own household to the whole world.

“He will give strength to His king,”

Now comes a remarkable line. God “will give strength to His king.” When Hannah spoke these words, Israel had no king. The people were still governed by judges, and the demand for a king lay years in the future (8:5). Kingship was anticipated in the Law (Deuteronomy 17:14–15), but Hannah speaks of “His king,” the king God Himself would provide.

“And exalt the horn of His anointed.”

The song ends with the LORD exalting “the horn of His anointed.” “His anointed” refers to the one God sets apart by anointing for His service, here the king. The Hebrew word is mashiach, from which comes ‘Messiah.’ The horn image returns from 2:1: as God exalted Hannah's horn, He will exalt the horn of His anointed king. Spoken before Israel had a king, these closing words are prophetic. Hannah's son Samuel would later anoint both Saul and David. In the wider canon, the line looks beyond them to the Messiah. Zacharias praised God for raising up “a horn of salvation for us In the house of His servant David” (Luke 1:69).

Hannah's song reaches far beyond her personal circumstances. The woman who prayed for a son now speaks of God's king and His anointed. This is consistent with the understanding that her prayer at Shiloh became an encounter with God's purposes for Israel, and that she perceived something of what He was doing through the son she had given Him.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

“His anointed.” This is the first occurrence in Scripture of mashiach applied to a king. It anticipates God's reference to “My anointed” in 2:35. The noun describes a person anointed for God's service; in later Scripture it becomes the title of the promised Messiah (compare Psalm 2:2, 6; Acts 4:26).

Interpretive note. The prophetic character of this line is supported by its setting before Israel had a king. Scripture does not say how fully Hannah understood the future fulfillment, and her words should not be expanded into a detailed prediction of Saul, David, or Christ. They stand as genuine prophetic anticipation of God's king, fulfilled in stages in the canon.

Distinction. Hannah's “His king” (God's chosen king) should be distinguished from Israel's later demand for a king “like all the nations” (8:5, 20). The first is God's purpose; the second is the people's distrust of His rule.

Literary frame. The books of Samuel open with Hannah's song about God's king and anointed and close with David's song about God's deliverance of His king and anointed (2 Samuel 22:51).

Structure. Chapter 2 alternates between Eli's sons (2:12–17, 22–25) and Samuel (2:11, 18–21, 26). The contrast is deliberate: the young servant grows before the LORD while the priests despise Him.

Main Idea

The narrator states plainly that Eli’s sons were corrupt and did not know the LORD. Though they held priestly office, they had no true knowledge of the God they were appointed to serve.

Verse Explanation

The narrator states plainly that Eli’s sons were corrupt and did not know the LORD. Though they held priestly office, they had no true knowledge of the God they were appointed to serve.

“Now the sons of Eli were corrupt;”

The narrator states plainly what Eli's sons were. “Corrupt” translates the Hebrew ‘sons of Belial,’ meaning worthless, wicked men. The same expression appeared when Hannah begged Eli not to regard her as ‘a daughter of Belial’ (1:16). Eli had suspected the godly woman of worthlessness, while his own sons were the worthless ones.

“they did not know the LORD.”

The deeper problem is stated next: “they did not know the LORD.” They held priestly office, wore the priestly garments, and handled the sacrifices, but they had no true knowledge of the God they were appointed to serve. To “know the LORD” in Scripture means more than knowing facts about Him; it means a relationship of loyalty, reverence, and obedience. After Joshua's generation died, Judges says, “another generation arose after them who did not know the LORD” (Judges 2:10). Eli's sons belonged to that kind of generation, even at the altar.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Hebrew expression. ‘Sons of Belial’ (bene beliyya'al) describes the lawless and worthless (Deuteronomy 13:13; Judges 19:22). The NKJV's “corrupt” captures the sense.

Contrast with Samuel. Samuel also “did not yet know the LORD” in 3:7, but the reason is different. Samuel had not yet received the LORD's word revealed to him; Eli's sons rejected the LORD in their conduct. The two statements should not be equated.

Priestly knowledge. The prophets later charged priests with failing to know the LORD (Jeremiah 2:8; Hosea 4:6) and taught that “the lips of a priest should keep knowledge” (Malachi 2:7).

Verse Explanation

The priests had made their own custom: while a worshiper’s sacrifice was boiling, the priest’s servant came with a three-pronged fleshhook. Their practice replaced the portion God had prescribed with a system that served their own appetite.

“And the priests' custom with the people was that when any man offered a sacrifice,”

The narrator describes the priests' practice. “Custom” translates a word that often means ‘judgment’ or ‘ordinance.’ The priests had established their own rule for dealing with the people's sacrifices, replacing what God had commanded. The sacrifices in view were peace offerings, shared between the altar, the priest, and the worshiper's family.

“the priest's servant would come with a three-pronged fleshhook in his hand while the meat was boiling.”

While the worshiper's meat was boiling, “the priest's servant would come with a three-pronged fleshhook,” a large fork for lifting meat from a pot. The Law assigned the priest specific portions, the breast and the right thigh (Leviticus 7:31–34). These priests ignored that portion and sent their servants to grab whatever they could. Their practice replaced the portion God had prescribed with a system that served their own appetite.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Priestly portions. Leviticus 7:31–34 gave the priests the breast and the right thigh of peace offerings. Deuteronomy 18:3 lists the shoulder, cheeks, and stomach. Either way, the priests' due was defined by God. Eli's sons replaced that defined portion with whatever the fork brought up.

Translation. “Custom” (mishpat) may also be read with the end of verse 12: ‘they did not know the LORD, nor the priests' due from the people.’ Some translations adopt that division. The point remains that the priests disregarded what God had ordained.

The LORD asks why Eli’s house kicks at His sacrifice and offering, and He charges Eli with honoring his sons more than Him and with sharing in the best of the offerings they took from Israel. Eli has given his sons the honor that belongs to God and has grown fat on what they seized.

“Why do you kick at My sacrifice and My offering which I have commanded in My dwelling place,”

Now comes the charge. “Why do you kick at My sacrifice and My offering?” “Kick” pictures an animal kicking back in defiance. Moses used the same image for Israel: “Jeshurun grew fat and kicked” (Deuteronomy 32:15). The offerings were ones God had “commanded in My dwelling place.” By treating them with contempt, the priests were kicking against God Himself.

“and honor your sons more than Me,”

The charge falls on Eli personally: God accuses him of choosing to “honor your sons more than Me.” “Honor” comes from the root meaning ‘weight’ or ‘glory.’ Eli gave his sons more weight than God by allowing them to continue their abuses rather than removing them from office. His fatherly attachment outweighed his duty to God.

“to make yourselves fat with the best of all the offerings of Israel My people?”

The plural “yourselves” includes Eli. He had shared in the best of the offerings that his sons seized. Eli had given his sons the honor that belonged to God and had grown fat on what they took. Later the narrator notes that Eli was “heavy” when he fell and died (4:18), using the same root as “honor.”

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Wordplay. “Honor” (kibbed), “heavy” (kaved, 4:18), and “glory” (kavod, 4:21–22) share the root kbd. The repetition traces Eli's failure from honoring his sons above God to his own death and to the departure of the glory from Israel.

Translation. The Hebrew of “kick at” is unusual here, and the Septuagint reads differently (‘look with an evil eye’). The NKJV's “kick” follows the Hebrew and fits Deuteronomy 32:15.

“and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed.”

The reverse is also true: “those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed.” “Lightly esteemed” is the opposite of ‘heavy’ or ‘honored,’ meaning treated as of little weight. God's principle governs the outcome, so the earlier word did not secure an unfaithful family's privileged place. God's promise concerning priestly service did not guarantee that an unfaithful branch would keep its office regardless of its conduct. God remained faithful to His purposes for the priesthood as a whole, but He would not honor those who despised Him.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Interpretive note. The announced judgment concerns Eli's family and its privileged position, not the abolition of the Aaronic priesthood. God's priestly purposes continued through another branch of Aaron's family (2:35; 1 Kings 2:35).

Wordplay. “Honor” (kabed) and “lightly esteemed” (qalal, ‘be light’) are opposites in Hebrew: weight and lightness. The principle is stated in the language of weight that runs through these chapters.

Canonical echo. Jesus said, “If anyone serves Me, him My Father will honor” (John 12:26). The principle of God honoring those who honor Him runs throughout Scripture.

The LORD promises to raise up for Himself a faithful priest who will act according to what is in His heart and mind, and to build him a sure house that will serve before His anointed king forever. Historically the promise is fulfilled through Zadok’s line (1 Kings 2:27, 35), and in the wider canon it reaches its fullest realization in Christ, the faithful High Priest.

“Then I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest”

God promises a new priest. “I will raise up for Myself” emphasizes that God will choose and establish him. He will be “a faithful priest,” reliable and trustworthy, in contrast to Eli's sons.

“who shall do according to what is in My heart and in My mind.”

This priest “shall do according to what is in My heart and in My mind.” He would serve according to God's desires and purposes, not his own appetite.

“I will build him a sure house,”

God would “build him a sure house,” an enduring priestly family. “Sure” means firm, established, reliable. Eli's house would fall; the faithful priest's house would stand.

“and he shall walk before My anointed forever.”

The faithful priest would “walk before My anointed forever,” serving before God's anointed king. “My anointed” connects with Hannah's song: “the horn of His anointed” (2:10). Israel still had no king, yet God speaks of the king He would anoint.

Historically, the promise is fulfilled through Zadok's line. When Solomon removed Abiathar, the last prominent priest of Eli's house, “the king put Zadok the priest in the place of Abiathar” (1 Kings 2:35). Zadok's descendants served as priests in the temple for centuries. Samuel's own faithful service also anticipates the promise in contrast to Eli's sons, but Samuel was not the exclusive fulfillment. In the wider canon, the promise finds its fullest realization in Jesus Christ, who is “a merciful and faithful High Priest” (Hebrews 2:17) and whose priesthood continues forever (Hebrews 7:24–25).

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

“Sure house.” “Sure” translates ne'eman, ‘established, faithful.’ The same expression is used when Abigail tells David that the LORD will make him “an enduring house” (25:28), and the verb describes the establishment of David's house and kingdom in 2 Samuel 7:16. The faithful priest and the anointed king are both promised enduring houses.

Zadok's line. Ezekiel later identifies “the sons of Zadok” as the priests who remained faithful (Ezekiel 44:15).

Christ connection. The connection to Christ is a canonical development. The New Testament does not cite this verse, so the historical fulfillment in Zadok should come first, and the fuller realization in Christ is presented as the larger biblical pattern of the faithful priest.
