Main Idea

Hannah praises God for answering her prayer, and her joy is rooted in the LORD Himself, not just in the son He gave her. The "horn" was a picture of strength, so she is saying that God has lifted her up and given her a confident answer to those who had mocked her.

Verse Explanation

Hannah praises God for answering her prayer, and her joy is rooted in the LORD Himself, not just in the son He gave her. The "horn" was a picture of strength, so she is saying that God has lifted her up and given her a confident answer to those who had mocked her.

"And Hannah prayed and said:"

Hannah had been unable to have children, and year after year at Shiloh she had poured out her grief to the LORD. Her earlier prayer came "in bitterness of soul," as she "prayed to the LORD and wept in anguish" (1:10). She vowed that if God gave her a son, she would give him back to the LORD for his whole life (1:11). God answered, and she named the boy Samuel, "Because I have asked for him from the LORD" (1:20). After he was weaned, she brought him to Eli and said, "For this child I prayed, and the LORD has granted me my petition which I asked of Him" (1:27).

Now Hannah prays again. This prayer is a song of praise, and she offers it at the very moment she leaves her young son behind at the tabernacle. The same woman who once prayed with tears now prays with joy, and the joy comes while she is giving her son away, not only on the day he was born.

"My heart rejoices in the LORD;"

The "heart" is Hannah's inner self, her thoughts and emotions together. The Hebrew word for "rejoices" means to exult or be glad with strong feeling. Only a short time before, her husband had asked her, "Why is your heart grieved?" (1:8). Now that same heart is full of gladness.

Her joy is "in the LORD." She is thankful for Samuel, but she does not say her heart rejoices in her son. God Himself is the source of her joy, which is why she can rejoice even while handing her child over to His service. Centuries later Mary, the mother of Jesus, praised God with words that echo Hannah's: "My soul magnifies the Lord, And my spirit has rejoiced in God my Savior" (Luke 1:46–47).

"My horn is exalted in the LORD."

In the Old Testament, the "horn" is a picture of strength, taken from animals like the wild ox, which carry their power in their horns and lift their heads high. A raised horn stands for strength, honor, and victory. A lowered or broken horn stands for weakness and defeat. The psalmist uses the same picture when God says, "All the horns of the wicked I will also cut off, But the horns of the righteous shall be exalted" (Psalm 75:10).

For years Hannah had been brought low by her shame and sorrow. Now God has lifted her head. She does not say she raised her own horn. It is exalted "in the LORD," since He is the one who gave her strength and honor. She returns to this picture at the end of her song, where it takes on a larger meaning (2:10).

"I smile at my enemies,"

The Hebrew says literally that her mouth is opened wide against her enemies. She is no longer silenced. Now she has something to say to those who mocked her. The main enemy in Hannah's story was Peninnah, Elkanah's other wife, who had children. "Her rival also provoked her severely, to make her miserable, because the LORD had closed her womb" (1:6). This happened year after year when the family went up to Shiloh, until Hannah wept and would not eat (1:7).

Hannah does not describe any revenge she took on Peninnah. Her answer to those who taunted her is what God has done for her. She is not boasting in her own success. The LORD's answer to her prayer has given her the right to speak.

"Because I rejoice in Your salvation."

Here Hannah speaks directly to God: "Your salvation." In this setting, "salvation" means deliverance, God's rescue of someone from trouble. The LORD had delivered Hannah from her barrenness, her shame, and the mockery she endured, and He did it by answering her prayer for a son. Her confidence before her enemies rests on this. She can open her mouth against them because God has acted for her. The deliverance belongs to Him, and so does her joy.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] She Praised the Giver, Not the Gift. “My heart rejoices in the LORD; my horn is exalted in the LORD.” Hannah sang this on the day she left her only son at Shiloh (1:28), yet the song never mentions Samuel. She does not say, “My heart rejoices in Samuel”; she overlooks the gift and praises the Giver. The verb for “rejoices” appears only eight times in the Old Testament, usually for the exultant joy of those God has made triumphant (Psalm 5:11; 9:2; 68:3). When God grants a long-awaited breakthrough, mature joy passes through the gift and rests in the Giver. To rejoice in the blessing alone is to quietly idolize it; to rejoice in the Lord is to keep the blessing in its proper place. Joy that rests in a gift leaves when the gift is given away. Joy that rests in the Lord can sing on the walk home. If one of His gifts were placed back in His hands, would we still be able to sing?

[Insight] The Horn of Power Rooted in Surrender. “My horn is exalted.” The horn pictures a wild ox lifting its head in strength; to have one’s horn raised is to be publicly vindicated, strength restored and honor visible (Deuteronomy 33:17; Psalm 92:10). Hannah’s dignity, trampled for years by Peninnah, was restored entirely by God. Grace is the great vindicator of the oppressed. And notice when her horn was lifted: not while she was striving with her rival, not while she was grasping for a child, but after she had surrendered her long-awaited son back to God. Here is a kingdom paradox: spiritual authority and unassailable strength are not secured by getting what we want, but by offering our most treasured desires back to heaven.

[Application] Let God Lift Your Horn. “My mouth is enlarged over my enemies, because I rejoice in Your salvation.” Literally, her mouth is opened wide, a gesture of vindication. Yet Hannah names no rival and settles no score; she never mentions Peninnah. Her answer to her antagonist is not petty retaliation but a magnificent declaration of God’s saving power. Real victory over human opposition is not found in defending ourselves but in magnifying God so loudly that the enemy’s taunts are drowned out by worship. The believer who waits for God’s vindication is freed from the exhausting work of defending himself. Where are we still trying to lift our own horn, defending our reputation or settling scores, instead of trusting God to vindicate us in His time?

[Christ & Life] Rejoice in a Salvation That Makes Room. “I rejoice in Your salvation.” The word is yeshu’ah, from a root whose core idea is to be wide or spacious, to bring someone out of a tight place into room. Barrenness had been Hannah’s narrow place; God’s deliverance gave her breathing space. The same root forms the names Joshua and Yeshua, Jesus. Every rescue God gives His people is a small picture of the great, spacious salvation He would one day give in His Son. And Christ Himself is our exalted horn: when we are weak, union with Him gives an unbreakable authority against the enemy.

[Seed] Back to the Spot Where She Wept. “And Hannah prayed.” It is the same verb used of her prayer in 1:10, but everything else has changed. Then her voice was not heard and only her lips moved (1:13); now she speaks aloud for anyone to hear. Then she prayed in bitterness of soul; now her mouth is opened wide. The woman who poured out her soul in bitterness now pours out praise in the same sanctuary. “Some day you will come to the spot where you wept and prayed,” wrote one old devotional writer, bringing praise instead. God often brings His people back to the place of their tears so that the same place can hear a new song. Is there such a place God may be bringing us back to?

[Insight] Prayer and Praise, One Language. Hannah “prayed,” yet what follows is a song. In the Spirit, deep intercession and high praise are the same language spoken from one heart. The same God who heard her sighs now receives her songs.

Main Idea

There is no one holy like the LORD and no one else who is God at all, so He alone is a "rock," a solid and unshakable place of safety. Hannah's own experience of answered prayer leads her to celebrate who God is for everyone.

Verse Explanation

There is no one holy like the LORD and no one else who is God at all, so He alone is a "rock," a solid and unshakable place of safety. Hannah's own experience of answered prayer leads her to celebrate who God is for everyone.

"No one is holy like the LORD,"

To be "holy" means to be set apart and different from everything else, especially in moral purity. The LORD is not just a greater version of anything in creation. He stands apart from it entirely, perfect in goodness and free from all evil. Hannah's words echo the song Israel sang after God brought them through the Red Sea: "Who is like You, O LORD, among the gods? Who is like You, glorious in holiness, fearful in praises, doing wonders?" (Exodus 15:11). Hannah praises God the way Israel did at the Red Sea. The God who rescued His people then had just shown Himself the same to one grieving woman through her answered prayer.

"For there is none besides You,"

"For" gives the reason no one can match the LORD's holiness: no one else is God at all. Hannah now speaks directly to Him, and her words repeat what Moses taught Israel: "the LORD Himself is God; there is none other besides Him" (Deuteronomy 4:35). This mattered in Hannah's day. During the time of the judges, Israel repeatedly "forsook the LORD and served Baal and the Ashtoreths" (Judges 2:13), and those idols were still among the people years later when Samuel called Israel to put them away (1 Samuel 7:3–4). Hannah did not turn to any of those gods for help. She brought her request to the only true God, and He answered.

"Nor is there any rock like our God."

In the rugged land of Israel, a great rock or rocky height offered shelter, a firm place to stand, and protection from enemies. Calling God a "rock" means He is steady, dependable, and safe to lean on when everything else gives way. Moses used the same picture: "He is the Rock, His work is perfect" (Deuteronomy 32:4). He also said that other nations' gods could not compare: "For their rock is not like our Rock" (Deuteronomy 32:31). Hannah had lived through years of sorrow and Peninnah's taunts, and she had found the LORD to be the One who held firm when she brought Him her grief.

Hannah says "our God," not just "my God." She moves from her own experience to what is true for all of God's people. Years later David, the king her son would anoint, praised God in almost the same words: "For who is God, except the LORD? And who is a rock, except our God?" (2 Samuel 22:32).

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] No Rock Like Ours. “No one is holy like the LORD, for there is none besides You, nor is there any rock like our God.” “Rock” (tsur) is first used as a name for God in the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32:4, 15, 18, 30–31), where it speaks of His changeless faithfulness, and Israelites carried it in their names. Hannah had leaned on a loving husband’s double portion and his “Am I not better to you than ten sons?” (1:5, 8). Now, holding the son she had begged for, she does not call Samuel her rock either. Even God’s best gifts cannot carry the weight of our lives. Build your emotional and spiritual stability on the unchangeable character of God, not on shifting circumstances. Paul says of the rock that followed Israel, “that Rock was Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4), the Rock struck for us in the wilderness, and He is the only ground that does not move. What are we leaning on as a rock that is really a gift and not the Giver?

[Foundational Truth] Holiness That Makes Him a Foundation. Hannah joins two attributes: His holiness, utterly separate and unlike any created thing, and His solidity, a Rock. Holiness is not one attribute among many; it is the overarching reality of His being, entirely “other.” And here is the profound truth: God’s utter separateness from us is precisely what makes Him a dependable foundation for us. Because He is not like anything else, He does not shift like anything else.

[Application] Praise Who God Is Before What God Gave. Hannah’s first confession is not “He gave me a son” but “There is none holy like the LORD.” God’s best gift is God Himself. Worship that only celebrates benefits quietly makes God a means rather than the end. Hannah had a child in her arms, yet her song is filled with God.

[Insight] A Rock That Is Also a Spring. An old rabbinic reading took “rock” applied to God as “source” or “spring,” a rock that is also a fountain, like the rock that gave water in the wilderness (Exodus 17:6). The Rock that holds us up is the Rock that pours life out.

NLT

1 Samuel 2:3 “Stop acting so proud and haughty!Don’t speak with such arrogance!For the LORD is a God who knows what you have done;he will judge your actions. (NLT)

Main Idea

Proud and boastful talk is foolish because the LORD knows everything and weighs every action, including the attitudes behind it. Those who look down on others, as Peninnah had looked down on Hannah, will be judged by a God who sees the truth.

Verse Explanation

Proud and boastful talk is foolish because the LORD knows everything and weighs every action, including the attitudes behind it. Those who look down on others, as Peninnah had looked down on Hannah, will be judged by a God who sees the truth.

"Talk no more so very proudly;"

Hannah has been speaking to the LORD, and now she turns to address the proud. In Hebrew the word for "proudly" is doubled, something like "lofty, lofty." The repetition stresses how swollen and boastful such talk is. Hannah knew this kind of speech from experience. Year after year Peninnah had provoked her "severely, to make her miserable" because she had no children (1:6–7). But Hannah is not simply answering one rival. She speaks to anyone who exalts himself and looks down on others, and "no more" tells them their boasting has gone on long enough.

"Let no arrogance come from your mouth,"

The word translated "arrogance" describes insolent, defiant speech, words spoken with no fear of being called to account. Proud people often reveal themselves by what they say, especially in how they speak about others. Peninnah's taunts were exactly this kind of talk: she judged Hannah's empty womb and treated her with contempt. Scripture warns where such pride leads: "Pride goes before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall" (Proverbs 16:18).

"For the LORD is the God of knowledge;"

Here is why proud talk is foolish. In Hebrew, "knowledge" is plural, which emphasizes its fullness: the LORD knows completely. The arrogant speak as if their opinion of others settles the matter and no one sees what lies behind their words. But God sees everything, including the heart. People had misjudged Hannah. Peninnah mocked her, and even Eli mistook her silent, heartbroken prayer for drunkenness (1:13–14). The LORD knew her heart all along and answered her. Samuel would later hear the same truth: "for man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart" (16:7).

"And by Him actions are weighed."

The picture is of scales. God does not judge by appearances or by how loudly someone boasts. He weighs every deed, along with the motives behind it, and His verdict is true. Proverbs says something similar: "All the ways of a man are pure in his own eyes, but the LORD weighs the spirits" (Proverbs 16:2). The proud may approve of themselves, but they do not have the final word.

This warning carries weight in this chapter. While Hannah praised God for seeing the lowly, Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, were serving as priests at Shiloh and treating the LORD's offerings with contempt (2:12–17). The God who weighs actions saw their conduct as well. Their sin was "very great before the LORD" (2:17), and His judgment on their house would follow (2:27–36). The God who had lifted up a humble woman would also bring down the proud, even inside His own sanctuary.

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] The Proud Count; God Weighs. “For the LORD is the God of knowledge, and by Him actions are weighed.” “God of knowledge” is plural in Hebrew, “God of all knowings,” an intensive form for complete, exhaustive knowledge. “Weighed” means to measure against a standard, the word Proverbs uses when “the LORD weighs the hearts” (Proverbs 16:2; 21:2; 24:12). In Elkanah’s house everything had been counted: Peninnah’s children, the portions on the plate, the years of barrenness (1:4–7). God does not count; He weighs. He does not measure what we bring; He weighs it. Two actions can look identical on the outside and carry completely different spiritual weight. Human beings weigh the outcome of actions; God weighs the motives behind them. Loud words can be very light, and quiet faithfulness can be very heavy, on the scales that matter. Where do we count what is visible while God is weighing what is hidden?

[Insight] Busy and Weightless. A massive volume of religious activity can weigh nothing at all on the scales of heaven if it lacks the substance of love and reverence. Eli’s sons were performing the outward mechanics of the priesthood, yet their actions had no spiritual mass; they are called “worthless,” men of Belial, “without profit,” without weight (v. 12). True spiritual substance is determined by the hidden motive, the reverence, and the alignment with God’s heart that fill the action.

[Application] “Talk No More”. “Talk no more so very proudly; let no arrogance come from your mouth.” Hannah had lived for years under a rival’s tongue (1:6–7). Now that God has answered, she does not answer Peninnah by name, mock her, or recount her cruelty. She simply tells pride to stop talking, because the God of knowledge has already heard everything. Peninnah’s taunts are silenced not by Hannah’s retaliation but by the scales of divine justice. Vindication from God does not need to be followed by revenge from us; God’s scales are perfectly calibrated. When God has spoken for us, we can stop speaking against others. Is there proud or careless speech in our lives that we would be ashamed to have placed on those scales?

[Seed] Pride Is a Failure to Know Who Knows. Hannah ties human arrogance directly to human ignorance. Pride is at root a failure to realize that God sees and knows everything. When the reality of His knowledge dawns on a soul, the mouth naturally shuts. Every proud word, Peninnah’s or ours, is placed on God’s scales, and none escapes the final audit.

[Insight] Old Sayings Silenced. The word for “arrogance” may mean hard or worn-out sayings, perhaps the stock insults heaped on barren women. Through the books of Samuel, God humbles the proud one after another: Peninnah, Eli’s sons, the Philistines, Goliath, Saul, Nabal, Absalom. Hannah’s line is the first notice of a pattern the whole story will keep.

1 Samuel 2:4
NKJV

1 Samuel 2:4 "The bows of the mighty men are broken, And those who stumbled are girded with strength.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 2:4 The bow of the mighty is now broken,and those who stumbled are now strong. (NLT)

Main Idea

God turns the world's expectations upside down: the weapons of the strong are broken, while those who were stumbling in weakness are given strength. Human power is no match for the LORD, who decides who stands and who falls.

Verse Explanation

God turns the world's expectations upside down: the weapons of the strong are broken, while those who were stumbling in weakness are given strength. Human power is no match for the LORD, who decides who stands and who falls.

"The bows of the mighty men are broken,"

The "mighty men" are warriors, the kind of fighters an army depends on to win its battles. In Hannah's day the bow was one of the most feared weapons on the battlefield. It let a soldier strike from a distance, and it stood for military power. To have one's bow broken meant being disarmed and defeated. Psalm 37:15 uses the same picture for the wicked: "Their sword shall enter their own heart, And their bows shall be broken." Psalm 46:9 says of God, "He breaks the bow and cuts the spear in two."

Hannah does not name who breaks the bows, but the surrounding lines make it clear. The LORD is "the God of knowledge" who weighs people's actions (2:3), and He is the one who "brings low and lifts up" (2:7). Hannah was not a soldier, and she had not been in a battle. She uses the language of war because the contrast is so sharp. The things people trust to make them secure can be snapped in a moment when God acts. First Samuel later shows this on a real battlefield. Before facing Goliath, David declared that "the LORD does not save with sword and spear; for the battle is the LORD's" (17:47).

"And those who stumbled are girded with strength."

The Hebrew word for "stumbled" describes someone staggering or tottering, too weak or worn out to stay on their feet. To be "girded" meant tying up one's long robe with a belt so it would not get in the way. People did this to get ready for work, travel, or battle. Here the belt is strength itself. God wraps it around people who could barely stand and makes them ready to act. Isaiah describes the same work of God: "He gives power to the weak, And to those who have no might He increases strength" (Isaiah 40:29).

The two halves of the verse belong together. The strong are disarmed and the weak are equipped, so the outcome depends on God, not on who seemed stronger at the start. Hannah had been the one stumbling. She had wept in "bitterness of soul" (1:10) under Peninnah's taunts. Now she sings as someone God has made strong. The weak are not strengthened because weakness has value in itself. They are strengthened because the LORD gives strength, and He is the one who decides who will stand.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] Strength You Put On. “The bows of the mighty men are broken, and those who stumbled are girded with strength.” “Girded” means to tie on, like a belt: strength here is not grown from within but fastened on from outside. The verb “broken” is used for the breaking of people, not just objects; the warriors are shattered inwardly, not merely disarmed. Every power determined to be something in itself is broken, and every weakness that despairs of itself is turned into power. “My strength is made perfect in weakness” (2 Corinthians 12:9). The stumbling believer is not disqualified; he is the one God dresses for battle.

[Insight] The Great Reversal of Human Strength. Here begins one of Scripture’s central motifs: God loves to dismantle human self-sufficiency while empowering human weakness. He breaks the bow of the strong because they rely on it; He girds the stumbler because the stumbler relies on Him. Natural strength must be broken before spiritual strength can be bound on, for God cannot use our self-sufficiency. Hannah moves here from a domestic dispute to military imagery, and the reversal she sings will run through Eli’s fall and Samuel’s rise, Saul’s fall and David’s rise. God delights to use the overlooked and the sidelined. Verses 4–7 hold seven such contrasts: mighty and stumbling, full and hungry, barren and fruitful, killing and making alive, bringing down and bringing up, poor and rich, low and lifted.

[Christ & Life] The Bow Broken at the Cross. At the cross the ultimate “bow of the mighty,” the power of Satan, was broken, and feeble humanity, through faith, was girded with resurrection life (Colossians 2:15).

Main Idea

People who once had plenty end up working for scraps, while the hungry are satisfied, and the childless woman has a full family while the mother of many grows weak. Hannah is describing her own reversal from barrenness, but she sees in it a pattern of how God works for the humble.

Verse Explanation

People who once had plenty end up working for scraps, while the hungry are satisfied, and the childless woman has a full family while the mother of many grows weak. Hannah is describing her own reversal from barrenness, but she sees in it a pattern of how God works for the humble.

"Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread,"

People who once had more food than they needed now have to work for someone else just to get something to eat. The Hebrew verb means to hire out, and here it describes people forced to sell their labor for a day's bread. In a farming society, hiring yourself out for food was a sign that you had lost your land, your harvest, or your savings. Hannah is not naming particular people. She is describing how quickly God can take security away from those who rely on their abundance.

"And the hungry have ceased to hunger."

The opposite happens to the hungry. Their hunger stops because they now have what they need. Hannah places the two groups side by side because the same God turns things around for both. Centuries later Mary echoed this line in her own song of praise: "He has filled the hungry with good things, And the rich He has sent away empty" (Luke 1:53). Like Hannah, Mary was a woman God had unexpectedly given a son, and she praised Him in the same way.

"Even the barren has borne seven,"

This is where Hannah's song becomes most personal. For years she had no children, because "the LORD had closed her womb" (1:5). In Israel, childlessness was a deep grief and often a source of shame. A woman's hope for the future, and her family's name, depended on children. Rachel once cried out, "Give me children, or else I die!" (Genesis 30:1). Hannah felt that same pain. Year after year she wept and would not eat (1:7). Her husband's love could not take it away (1:8), and Peninnah's taunts made it worse.

"Seven" is the number of completeness, so a woman with seven children has a full and blessed family. Ruth's devotion to Naomi was praised as "better to you than seven sons" (Ruth 4:15). Hannah was not counting her children. At this point she had only Samuel, and she would later have five more (2:21). She means that God had given the barren woman a complete family, the very thing that had seemed impossible. The psalmist praises God in the same way: "He grants the barren woman a home, Like a joyful mother of children" (Psalm 113:9). Hannah's reversal shows what kind of God she is praising. He sees the humble and the hopeless, and He gives what they cannot obtain for themselves.

"And she who has many children has become feeble."

The Hebrew word for "feeble" describes something that withers or wastes away, like a plant drying up. The mother who seemed to have everything grows weak. Many readers naturally think of Peninnah, who had sons and daughters (1:4) and used them to provoke Hannah. Yet Hannah does not name her, and Scripture does not say what became of Peninnah. Hannah is describing how God works in general. Children are His gift, and the woman with many has no more reason to boast than the woman with none. The one who gives life can also take it away, as the next verse says plainly (2:6).

Spirit & Life

[Seed] The Barren Has Borne Seven. “Even the barren has borne seven, and she who has many children has become feeble.” When Hannah sang this she had one son; she would eventually have six children in all (v. 21). Seven is the number of full blessing and divine completion (Ruth 4:15), so she is not counting; she is confessing what God does. Her faith spoke of God’s fullness before she could see it in her own home, and in God’s eyes she was already complete. Her song reached beyond her own life to the fruitfulness of every spiritually barren soul who trusts God, a hope sung again in Psalm 113:9 and Isaiah 54:1, applied to the church in Galatians 4:27, and echoed in Mary’s Magnificat. The same God who fills the empty can empty the full; Jeremiah later turns the picture to judgment: “She who has borne seven has languished” (Jeremiah 15:9). Faith learns to speak of His fullness before the house is full.

[Application] Never Trust in Abundance. “Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread, and the hungry have ceased to hunger.” The well-fed are reduced to day labor. God delights in overturning human systems of security. Never place ultimate trust in material abundance; it is entirely subject to divine sovereignty. This very line will be fulfilled before the chapter ends, when Eli’s well-fed house begs for bread (v. 36). (An old Jewish legend said that for every child Hannah bore, Peninnah buried two; that is legend, not text.)

NLT

Main Idea

The LORD holds the power of life and death; He alone decides who dies and who lives. Nothing, not even the grave, is outside His control.

Verse Explanation

The LORD holds the power of life and death; He alone decides who dies and who lives. Nothing, not even the grave, is outside His control.

"The LORD kills and makes alive;"

Hannah has just sung that the barren woman bears children (2:5), and now she traces that change back to its source. The LORD is the one who holds life and death in His hands. The reversal she experienced in her own life, from a closed womb to a son in her arms, reaches all the way to the most basic line in human existence: whether a person lives or dies.

Hannah's words echo what God said of Himself in the Song of Moses: "I kill and I make alive; I wound and I heal; nor is there any who can deliver from My hand" (Deuteronomy 32:39). In that passage God is setting Himself apart from every false god. No idol, power, or person shares this authority. Israel understood this. Centuries later, when a foreign commander came asking to be healed of leprosy, the king of Israel tore his clothes and said, "Am I God, to kill and make alive?" (2 Kings 5:7). Hannah is not saying that God acts carelessly with human life. She is saying that no death happens outside His rule, and no life begins without Him.

The order of the words also matters. Death comes first, then life. Hannah does not end with the grave. She ends with the God who gives life, which fits what she had seen in her own story.

"He brings down to the grave and brings up."

The Hebrew word for "grave" is Sheol, the Old Testament's name for the realm of the dead, the place every person goes at death. To be "brought down" there means to die. To be "brought up" means to be brought back from it. In the Psalms this language often describes God rescuing someone from the very edge of death: "O LORD, You brought my soul up from the grave; You have kept me alive, that I should not go down to the pit" (Psalm 30:3). Hannah may have that kind of deliverance in mind, God restoring someone who seemed already lost.

Either way, the grave is not beyond the LORD's reach. Even the place of the dead is under His control, and He can bring out of it whomever He chooses. That truth gave Old Testament believers real hope, and it was shown most fully when God raised Jesus from the dead and broke the grave's hold for everyone who belongs to Him.

The apostle Peter turns this truth into instruction for believers: "Humble yourselves under the mighty hand of God, that He may exalt you in due time" (1 Peter 5:6).

[Christ & Life] From the Ash Heap to the Throne of Glory. “He raises the poor from the dust and lifts the beggar from the ash heap, to set them among princes and make them inherit the throne of glory.” The ash heap was the town’s refuse pile, where outcasts sat and begged, the place Job ended up (Job 2:8; Lamentations 4:5). “Throne of glory” is kisse’ kabod, literally a “seat of weight.” Eli sat on his seat (kisse’) and would fall from it because he was “heavy” (kabed, 4:18); Hannah sings that God hands the seat of weight to beggars. God does not merely improve a beggar’s condition in the ash heap; He changes his citizenship and seats him with royalty. God bypasses the self-righteous and goes to the ash heap of humanity to find His kings and priests. That is the exact path of a sinner saved by grace: raised from the dirt of spiritual death and made to “sit together in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus” (Ephesians 2:6). [Seed] God Does Not Reform the Ash Heap. A powerful evangelistic text: God does not reform the ash heap; He removes the beggar from it and seats him with royalty. His grace reaches the lowest point of society to lift to the highest point of heaven.

An inheritance belongs to children and comes as a father’s gift. Every verb in the verse is God’s: He raises, lifts, sets, and makes them inherit. The honor of God’s people is not a promotion we work toward but an inheritance we receive as sons and daughters (Romans 8:17; Revelation 3:21).

[Seed] The Pillars of the Earth. “For the pillars of the earth are the LORD’s, and He has set the world upon them.” The line grounds God’s right to reverse human fortunes in His ownership of the world itself. The God who answered a barren woman's quiet prayer is the same God who rules over the storm.

"The ends of the earth" means the whole world, out to its farthest edges. God's judgment is not limited to Israel or to Hannah's household in Ramah. To "judge" here means more than to condemn. Hannah speaks of that coming king as already belonging to God: "His king." Her words are prophetic. Through her prayer God gave her insight into His purposes that reached far beyond her own family, though Scripture does not explain how much of the future she understood.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Eli's sons, who served as priests, are described as corrupt men who did not know the LORD. Though they held sacred positions, they had no real relationship with the God they were supposed to serve.

"Now the sons of Eli were corrupt;"

Eli's sons were Hophni and Phinehas, who served as priests of the LORD at Shiloh alongside their aged father (1:3). As priests, they were responsible for offering the sacrifices Israelites brought to the tabernacle and for representing the people before God. Their position was a privilege handed down within Eli's family, part of the priestly line that traced back to Aaron.

The word "corrupt" translates a Hebrew phrase that literally reads "sons of Belial." "Belial" means worthlessness or wickedness, and the expression describes men who are good for nothing and lawless. The same expression appears earlier in the story. When Eli saw Hannah's lips moving in silent prayer, he assumed she was drunk, and she pleaded with him not to consider her a wicked woman, literally a "daughter of Belial" (1:16). Eli mistook a godly woman pouring out her heart to the LORD for a worthless person, while his own sons, serving at the altar, truly were worthless men. The verse comes directly after Samuel is described as ministering to the LORD before Eli (2:11). The boy dedicated by his mother served faithfully, while the priest's own grown sons were corrupt.

"they did not know the LORD."

Hophni and Phinehas certainly knew about the LORD. They had grown up as priests' sons, handled the sacrifices, and knew the law that governed worship. In the Bible, though, "knowing" the LORD means more than having information about Him. It describes a real relationship with Him, one that acknowledges who He is and responds with reverence and obedience. These men worked at God's house every day, yet they had no regard for Him at all.

This describes the spiritual condition of Israel during the time of the judges. After Joshua's generation died, "another generation arose after them who did not know the LORD nor the work which He had done for Israel" (Judges 2:10). Now the same could be said of the very priests responsible for teaching the people God's ways. Centuries later the LORD warned Israel's priests through Hosea: "Because you have rejected knowledge, I also will reject you from being priest for Me" (Hosea 4:6). Men who served at the altar without knowing the God of the altar could not continue there, and the rest of this chapter shows how their ignorance of God showed itself in their treatment of His offerings and how He judged them for it.

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] The Tragedy of Proximity Without Intimacy. “Now the sons of Eli were worthless men; they did not know the LORD.” Hophni and Phinehas grew up in the tabernacle. They were born into the priesthood, handled the holy vessels daily, breathed the incense, and ate the sacrifices. They knew all about the LORD, yet they did not know Him. “Know” (yada’) is the word of relationship and experience, not information; the Targum renders it, “did not know to fear before the LORD.” Proximity to the things of God does not equal intimacy with the Person of God. Religious familiarity without personal surrender produces the most dangerous kind of spiritual blindness. It is entirely possible to make a career of the holy while remaining spiritually dead, and spiritual pedigree and religious vocation do not equal a relationship with God. Here is a terrifying warning to all raised in the faith or serving in ministry: unregenerate people can hold professional positions in God’s house.

[Discovery] “Not” and “Not Yet”. In the next chapter the narrator says, “Now Samuel did not yet know the LORD” (3:7). The difference is one small word. The priests’ sons had handled the altar, the meat, and the incense for years and did not know Him; the boy who was only beginning to serve did not know Him yet. A man who holds a sacred office and is presumed to know the Lord, but does not, stands where blessing hardly reaches him. Nearness to holy things is not the same as knowing the Holy One, and the person who still says “not yet” is closer than the one who has stopped asking. Are we closer to “not” or to “not yet”?

[Insight] A Sentence That Implicates the Father. The Hebrew is a verbless clause: literally, “The sons of Eli, sons of Belial.” The wording can be heard as setting Eli beside Belial, a quiet hint that the father shares the guilt, which the man of God will make plain in verse 29. “Belial” means “without profit,” worthless, good for nothing, and by Paul’s day it had become a name for Satan (2 Corinthians 6:15). The Greek Old Testament calls them “pests” or “plagues,” men dangerous to everyone around them. Hannah had once begged Eli not to count her “a daughter of Belial” (1:16); the true sons of Belial were his own. Being related to a high priest is no substitute for a relationship with God. A godly family name can sit on top of a worthless life, and a father’s position cannot cover a son’s heart. “Because you have rejected knowledge, I also will reject you from being priest for Me” (Hosea 4:6).

Eli's sons had created their own system for taking meat from the people's sacrifices: a servant would come with a large fork while the meat was cooking. God's law gave priests a specific portion, but these men had replaced God's instructions with their own greedy custom.

"And the priests’ custom with the people was"

The men behind this "custom" are Eli's sons, Hophni and Phinehas, who served as priests at Shiloh but "did not know the LORD" (2:12). The Hebrew word translated "custom" is mishpat, a word that often means a rightful claim or what is lawfully due. Moses used the same word when he set out what the priests could rightly receive: "And this shall be the priest's due from the people, from those who offer a sacrifice" (Deuteronomy 18:3). God had already decided what the priests' share would be. Here the priests had made up their own "due" and treated it as their right. A practice born of greed had taken the place of what God's law required, and by now it was simply how things were done at Shiloh.

"that when any man offered a sacrifice,"

This was a sacrifice the worshiper shared in eating, usually called a peace offering. Part of the animal was burned on the altar for the LORD. The priest received a set portion, the breast and the right thigh (Leviticus 7:31–34). The rest went back to the worshiper, who cooked it and ate it with his family as a meal before the LORD. This is the kind of sacrifice Elkanah offered when he gave portions to Peninnah, her children, and Hannah (1:4–5). The priests' portion was given to them by God as their support, so they had no need to take more. Yet this custom fell on "any man" who came. No family bringing a sacrifice was spared.

"the priest’s servant would come"

The priests did not even come for the meat themselves. They sent their servants to collect it, and those servants carried out their masters' greed among the worshipers.

"with a three-pronged fleshhook in his hand while the meat was boiling."

A fleshhook was a large fork with three prongs, used to handle meat in a cooking pot. Boiling was a normal way to prepare sacrificial meat (Leviticus 8:31). The timing shows what was happening. While the meat was boiling, the priest's lawful share had already been set aside, so the pot held the portion that belonged to the worshiper and his family for their meal before the LORD. The servant came with a tool in his hand to take from that pot. What God had given the worshiper to eat with joy in His presence, the priests were taking for themselves.

Study Notes

The NKJV starts a new sentence here: "And the priests' custom with the people was..." The Hebrew can also be divided so that these words finish verse 12. Eli's sons "did not know the LORD, nor the priests' rightful due from the people." Several modern translations take it this way, and both divisions are grammatically possible. The second reading makes the connection with God's law even more direct. The word mishpat would then name the portion God had assigned, and Eli's sons would be men who ignored both the LORD and His rules for the priesthood.

The law named specific cuts for the priest: the breast and right thigh of a peace offering (Leviticus 7:31–34), and in Deuteronomy "the shoulder, the cheeks, and the stomach" (Deuteronomy 18:3). The fork method set all of that aside and let chance decide the priest's share.

Some interpreters have suggested that verses 13–14 describe an accepted local practice and that the real wrongdoing begins in verse 15, which opens with "Also." Yet verse 17 judges the whole account as very great sin, and verse 29 charges the family with taking "the best of all the offerings." That supports treating both practices as abuses, with verse 15 marking a further step.

God's law had already set the priests' lawful portion, so the priests had no need to grab anything. Instead, they took whatever the prongs happened to catch, as much as the fork could lift and from whichever part of the animal it struck. This took meat from the family's own share, the food God had given them to enjoy before Him. A meal meant for worship and rejoicing became an occasion for the priests to help themselves. "So they did in Shiloh to all the Israelites who came there."

Shiloh was the center of Israel's worship. After the conquest of Canaan, the whole congregation gathered there and set up the tabernacle (Joshua 18:1), and the ark of God was kept there (3:3). God had told His people to bring their sacrifices to the place He chose and to eat there before Him with joy (Deuteronomy 12:5–7). Families like Elkanah's made the trip year after year (1:3). Shiloh was where an Israelite went to meet with God.

That is why this sentence is so serious. The greed was not a one-time lapse by a single priest. It was how things were done, and it happened "to all the Israelites who came there." Every family that traveled to Shiloh to worship faced the priest's servant and his fork. Hannah's own family, who came every year, would likely have known this practice well. The men who were supposed to help people draw near to God were making worship at His house a burden. Generations later, God pointed back to what became of Shiloh as a warning to His people: "But go now to My place which was in Shiloh, where I set My name at the first, and see what I did to it because of the wickedness of My people Israel" (Jeremiah 7:12).

"Therefore the sin of the young men was very great before the LORD,"

"Therefore" points back to what Eli's sons had been doing at the tabernacle at Shiloh. When worshipers came to offer sacrifices, the priest's servant would jab a three-pronged fork into the boiling pot and keep whatever it brought up (2:13–14). Worse, he demanded raw meat before the fat was even burned on the altar, and if a worshiper objected, he threatened to take it by force (2:15–16). The "young men" are Hophni and Phinehas. Their servants did the grabbing, but they did it for their masters and with their approval.

Their sin was "very great," and it was great "before the LORD," meaning in His sight. This was not a private failing hidden away somewhere. It happened in the courts of God's house, during the very acts of worship He had commanded. These men were priests, appointed to stand between the people and God and to handle holy things with care. God had already provided for them through the priests' lawful portion, and He had made clear that the fat belonged to Him: "all the fat is the LORD's" (Leviticus 3:16). Burning it first showed that God received the best before anyone else ate. By seizing meat before the fat was burned, Eli's sons put their own appetites ahead of God at His own altar. Eli himself later warned them how serious sin directly against the LORD is: "If a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?" (2:25).

"for men abhorred the offering of the LORD."

The Hebrew word translated "abhorred" means to despise, spurn, or treat with contempt. The "offering of the LORD" here refers broadly to the gifts and sacrifices people brought to God. These offerings were how an Israelite drew near to Him, whether to seek forgiveness or to celebrate fellowship with Him in a shared meal. Treating them as cheap was treating God Himself as cheap.

The priests themselves are chiefly in view. They handled the sacrifices as if they were simply food for the taking, not gifts offered to God. Centuries later, Malachi condemned priests with the same attitude, who in effect said, "The table of the LORD is contemptible" (Malachi 1:7). The Hebrew wording can also be read as saying the priests' behavior led others to despise the offering, and that was true as well. A worshiper who came to Shiloh with his sacrifice and was bullied out of the LORD's portion would leave disheartened, perhaps reluctant to come again. Eli later told his sons, "You make the LORD's people transgress" (2:24). Their greed did not just dishonor God. It corrupted the worship of the people they were supposed to serve.

An ephod was a priestly garment worn over the other clothing, something like a vest or apron. The high priest wore a special ephod made of gold, blue, purple, and scarlet thread with fine linen, and the breastplate with the names of Israel's tribes was attached to it (Exodus 28:6–14). Study Notes

The clause "and how they lay with the women who assembled at the door of the tabernacle of meeting" is part of a real textual question. It appears in the Masoretic Text, the standard Hebrew text that the NKJV and most English Bibles follow. It is missing from the oldest manuscripts of the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament). It is also missing from a scroll of Samuel found among the Dead Sea Scrolls.

Some scholars think a scribe added the clause, borrowing the wording of Exodus 38:8. They point out that elsewhere in these chapters the sanctuary at Shiloh is called in Hebrew "the temple of the LORD" (1:9; 3:3, rendered "tabernacle" in the NKJV), and this is the only place in Samuel's account of Shiloh that uses the phrase "tabernacle of meeting." Others think the clause was dropped, either by accident or because it was so offensive. They note that Scripture elsewhere calls the sanctuary at Shiloh a tent (Joshua 18:1; Psalm 78:60).

Most modern translations keep the clause, often with a footnote. Either way, the charge against Eli's sons stands. Their contempt for the offerings alone is called "very great" sin (2:17), and no doctrine depends on this clause.

Eli is reasoning with his sons. When one person wrongs another, there is a higher authority who can step in and settle the matter. The word "God" here can also mean "judges," and in the Law it sometimes refers to the leaders who decided disputes on God's behalf (Exodus 22:8–9). The verb translated "judge" carries the idea of arbitrating, standing between two parties to bring a case to a just resolution. An offense between people can be brought before God, and He can rule on it.

"But if a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?"

"Intercede" comes from the same Hebrew root as "judge" in the first line. It is the ordinary word for prayer, the same word used when "Hannah prayed" (2:1). When the LORD Himself is the one offended, there is no higher court and no one above Him who can hear the case. Eli is warning his sons that their sin falls into this second kind. Earlier the sin of these young men was described as "very great before the LORD" (2:17). They had not just mistreated worshipers. They had treated with contempt the offerings that belonged to God, at His own altar.

That made their situation especially serious. Priests were supposed to stand between sinful people and God, offering the sacrifices He provided for forgiveness. Hophni and Phinehas were the ones who should have interceded for others, and they were the ones despising the sacrifices. When a person scorns the very means God has given for dealing with sin, no other help is left to turn to. The LORD later confirmed this to Samuel: "the iniquity of Eli's house shall not be atoned for by sacrifice or offering forever" (3:14).

Eli's question does not mean no one can ever plead with God. Moses interceded for Israel after the golden calf (Exodus 32:11–14), and the New Testament presents Jesus as the one who "always lives to make intercession" for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). Eli's point is that his sons had sinned directly against the LORD and showed no desire to turn back. They had no one left to plead their case.

"Nevertheless they did not heed the voice of their father,"

These were grown men serving as priests, and they rejected their father's warning. The Law treated refusing to heed a father's voice as serious rebellion (Deuteronomy 21:18). Their refusal was the latest step in a long pattern. They "did not know the LORD" (2:12), they abused the sacrifices, and they lay with the women who served at the tabernacle door (2:22). Eli's warning was true, but it came late, and he only spoke. He did not remove his sons from the priesthood (3:13). Proverbs describes where such stubbornness leads: "He who is often rebuked, and hardens his neck, will suddenly be destroyed, and that without remedy" (Proverbs 29:1).

"because the LORD desired to kill them."

This explains why the sons did not listen. The LORD had determined to put them to death as judgment for their sin. The word "desired" means to be pleased or to will something, and it states plainly that their death was God's decision. That decision was not arbitrary, and it did not keep willing men from repenting. Hophni and Phinehas had already rebelled for a long time, and God's judgment confirmed the path they had chosen. Their deafness to their father's warning was part of that judgment. Pharaoh's story shows a similar pattern: he hardened his own heart (Exodus 8:15), and later the LORD hardened it (Exodus 9:12).

Ezekiel uses the same verb when God declares, "I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live" (Ezekiel 33:11). God does not delight in death for its own sake, and He calls sinners to repent. But when men persistently despise Him, His justice is just as real as His patience. Both truths stand together here. The sons were fully responsible for refusing to listen, and the LORD had decided to judge them. That decision was confirmed later when both men died on the same day (2:34; 4:11).

[Christ & Life] Who Will Intercede?. “If one man sins against another, God will judge him. But if a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?” There is a wordplay here: “judge” and “intercede” both come from palal, the verb used when Hannah “prayed” (1:10; 2:1), the root of the common Hebrew word for prayer. When people wrong each other, God can stand between them as judge; when a man wrongs God, who can stand between? This is one of the most terrifying questions in the Old Testament, and Eli asked it as if there were no answer. He identified the fatal problem of the old order: no human third party has the standing to mediate between God and a man who has offended God, and the Law had no remedy for high-handed rebellion against Yahweh Himself but death. This verse cries out for the incarnation. What was a rhetorical dead end in the Old Testament becomes the bedrock of the New. Here the lid comes off the Tupperware: Jesus Christ, the God-Man, holding the hand of the offended God and the hand of the offending man, is the very Intercessor Eli thought impossible to find. “There is one God and one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5). “If anyone sins, we have an Advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous” (1 John 2:1–2), and “He always lives to make intercession” (Hebrews 7:25). The most hopeless question in the chapter has its answer in Christ. Do we live daily in the relief that Jesus answers Eli’s question?

[Foundational Truth] When God Decides. “Nevertheless they did not heed the voice of their father, because the LORD desired to kill them.” “Desired” is the word God uses in Ezekiel: “I have no pleasure in the death of one who dies” (Ezekiel 18:32; 33:11). The two statements are not in conflict. God takes no pleasure in the death of sinners, yet after long, persistent, high-handed rebellion He may reach a point of judicial decision, confirming the heart’s chosen direction (Romans 1:24), as with Pharaoh, whose heart was hardened both by himself and by God. Their sins were capital offenses under the law (Leviticus 7:25; 22:9). Because they had continually rejected Him, God withdrew the grace of repentance and sealed their rebellion toward judgment; their refusal to listen was the seal of what was coming. God can will a hardening without being the author of evil. There is a “too late” no one can locate in advance, and so the call is to turn while God’s voice still pleads (Hebrews 3:15). God’s goodness gives long warning; it does not give endless permission. Are we presuming on His patience as if opportunities to repent will never run out?

[Insight] Sovereignty and Responsibility in One Verse. Verse 25 declares both that the sons “did not heed” and that “the LORD desired to kill them.” Scripture never asks us to choose between human guilt and divine sovereignty: their rebellion was fully their own; God’s judgment was fully His. The verse also answers an unspoken question. God did not tolerate their abuse because He was indifferent; He had already determined its end.

Eli knew his family's history, and God is reminding him where his position came from. Israel had twelve tribes, but God set apart Aaron and his sons to serve as priests (Exodus 28:1). "to offer upon My altar, to burn incense,"

These were the central duties of the priesthood. The altar was the bronze altar in the tabernacle courtyard, where the animals Israel brought were offered. Incense was burned on a separate golden altar inside the holy place, in front of the veil, every morning and evening (Exodus 30:7–8). The rising smoke of incense came to picture prayer going up to God: "Let my prayer be set before You as incense" (Psalm 141:2). Only priests could perform these duties. When others tried to offer incense, they were judged severely (Numbers 16:35; 2 Chronicles 26:16–21). Aaron's family had been given access to the most sacred work in Israel.

"and to wear an ephod before Me?"

Samuel wore a simple linen ephod (2:18), but the ephod of the high priest was far more elaborate. It was woven with gold, blue, purple, and scarlet thread and fine linen. Two stones on its shoulders were engraved with the names of the twelve tribes, so that Aaron carried Israel's names before the LORD as a memorial (Exodus 28:6–12). Attached to it was the breastplate holding the Urim and Thummim, which were used to seek God's decisions (Exodus 28:30). Wearing the ephod meant representing all Israel before God and seeking His direction for the nation. Eli, as the chief priest at Shiloh, carried this honor himself.

God gave Aaron's family more than the right to serve. He also provided for their needs. The man of God speaks in the name of "the LORD God of Israel," the God who had chosen Aaron's family out of all the tribes to serve as His priests (2:28). The One who gave the priesthood is the One now ruling on how it has been used.

"'I said indeed that your house and the house of your father would walk before Me forever.'"

God first quotes His own earlier promise. "Your house" is Eli's family line, and "the house of your father" is Aaron's priestly house, the family God set apart when He told Moses, "The priesthood shall be theirs for a perpetual statute" (Exodus 29:9). For a priest, to "walk before" God meant to serve in His presence, coming near His altar and ministering in His sanctuary. But walking before God was never only a matter of holding a position. When God told Abraham, "walk before Me and be blameless" (Genesis 17:1), the phrase meant living all of life under His eye. God does not deny that He made this promise. He states it plainly, and He does not take it back as though He had been mistaken.

"But now the LORD says: 'Far be it from Me;"

"Far be it from Me" is a strong expression of refusal. It means something like "That is unthinkable!" Abraham used the same expression when he said God would never "slay the righteous with the wicked" (Genesis 18:25). God will not keep Eli's family in His presence while they treat Him with contempt.

This does not mean God broke His word. The promise of a lasting priesthood was given to a family called to be holy, and it never gave an unfaithful branch the right to keep its office no matter how it behaved. Aaron's own sons Nadab and Abihu died for treating worship carelessly, and God declared then, "By those who come near Me I must be regarded as holy" (Leviticus 10:3). Yet the priesthood continued through Aaron's other sons. In the same way, the judgment here falls on Eli's line, not on the priesthood as a whole. God would still have priests serving before Him, and He would raise up a faithful priest to take the place Eli's house had lost (2:35).

"for those who honor Me I will honor,"

The Hebrew word for "honor" comes from a root that means to be heavy or weighty. To honor someone is to treat him as important. Eli had given that weight to his sons rather than to God (2:29). God answers with a simple principle: He gives honor to those who give Him honor. Jesus said much the same about those who follow Him: "If anyone serves Me, him My Father will honor" (John 12:26).

The same root appears again when Eli's house falls. On the day Eli dies, his daughter-in-law names her newborn son Ichabod, saying, "The glory has departed from Israel!" (4:21). "Glory" there comes from the same root as "honor" here. The family that refused to give God His proper weight would lose its own.

"and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed."

To "despise" is to treat as worthless. Eli's sons despised the LORD by treating His offerings with contempt (2:17). Despising what was offered to God was despising God Himself. "Lightly esteemed" translates a word that means to be light or trivial, the opposite of "heavy" or honored. Those who treated God as unimportant would themselves become unimportant. This is the same reversal Hannah sang about, when she said the LORD "brings low and lifts up" (2:7). Now it falls on the priestly family who served at the very tabernacle where she prayed.

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] Honor Has Weight; Contempt Has None. “For those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed.” This is the moral center of the chapter. “Honor” is kabed, to make heavy, give weight; “lightly esteemed” is qalal, to be light, trifling. To honor God is to give Him weight: first place, first portion, true reverence. To despise Him is to treat Him as light, and God will make us light. God does not merely observe this; He responds to it. People who give God weight are given weight; people who treat Him as light become light. If Eli had honored God in his household, God would have honored his family. This is not a reward formula but a description of how the God of glory relates to people, an unshakable law of the spiritual universe. While salvation is by unconditional grace, divine honor and usefulness in ministry are bound up with our reverence for God. He will strip the influence from anyone who handles His glory lightly. What would it look like for us to give God weight this week?

[Application] The Runner Who Gave God Weight. Eric Liddell famously received this verse on a slip of paper at the 1924 Paris Olympics after refusing to run on the Lord’s Day, and then won the 400 meters. [Foundational Truth] A Promise Is Not a Permit. “I said indeed that your house and the house of your father would walk before Me forever.’ But now the LORD says: ‘Far be it from Me.’” God had promised the priesthood to Aaron’s line “by a perpetual statute” (Exodus 29:9; Numbers 25:13). “Far be it from Me” comes from a root meaning “to profane”: it would be a profane thing to Me to keep such men walking before Me. God’s promise to Aaron was enduring, but the participation of Eli’s particular line depended on faithfulness. The promise of priesthood was never a license for irreverence. No inherited privilege can shield anyone from God’s holiness (Matthew 3:9). Are we treating a privilege, position, or spiritual heritage as a permanent pass rather than a stewardship?

Study Notes

Excavations at ancient Shiloh shed light on the disaster this verse anticipates. Shiloh is the site called Khirbet Seilun today, about twenty miles north of Jerusalem. It was dug by Danish teams in the 1920s and 1930s and by Israeli archaeologists in the 1980s. They found that the settlement from Eli's era ended in a violent fire. Buildings collapsed, and storerooms still held burned jars. This destruction is usually dated to around the middle of the eleventh century BC. That fits well with the Philistine victory in 1 Samuel 4, when the ark was captured and Hophni and Phinehas died.

First Samuel never actually describes Shiloh being destroyed, but the narrative points that way. When the ark is returned, it never goes back to Shiloh (6:21–7:2), and later the priests of Eli's line are found serving at Nob instead (21:1; 22:11). Psalm 78:60 and Jeremiah 7:12–14 speak of God forsaking Shiloh as a well-known event.

The archaeological dating is still discussed, and no inscription links the fire to a particular battle. Shiloh was also not entirely abandoned afterward, since Ahijah the prophet later came from there (1 Kings 11:29). The connection is a strong and widely held conclusion, not a proven fact.

Study Notes

Two details in this verse differ between ancient witnesses, and modern translations do not all agree. First, the Masoretic Text, which the NKJV follows, says the survivor would "consume your eyes and grieve your heart," so the grief belongs to Eli as head of the family. The Septuagint and the Samuel scroll from the Dead Sea Scrolls read "his eyes" and "his heart," so the spared man himself would weep out his eyes in sorrow.

Second, where the NKJV has "die in the flower of their age," the Hebrew says literally "die as men," meaning as grown men before old age. The Greek and the Qumran scroll read "fall by the sword of men." Some modern translations, such as the ESV and NRSV, follow that reading. The NKJV and NIV keep the Hebrew.

Behind the NKJV's "any of your men" is a singular Hebrew phrase, literally "a man of yours." Many interpreters see this as a pointer to Abiathar. Both readings of each detail fit that history, so the overall meaning of the judgment is not affected.

The key word is "faithful," meaning trustworthy and dependable, someone who can be relied on. The young Samuel, serving faithfully while Eli's sons grew worse, already gave Israel a glimpse of such a man. Later in the story Samuel "had been established as a prophet of the LORD" (3:20), and "established" there is the same Hebrew word. "Mind" translates the Hebrew word often rendered "soul," so the phrase means God's deepest desires and purposes. Eli's sons did what was in their own hearts, and Eli honored his sons more than God (2:29). The faithful priest would take his direction from God's heart. That privilege, lost through dishonor, now passes to the faithful priest, whose house would walk "forever" before God's anointed.

"My anointed" refers first to Israel's king. When these words were spoken, Israel had no king, but Hannah had already sung that the LORD would "exalt the horn of His anointed" (2:10). The faithful priest would serve alongside the king God would choose. That came to pass when Zadok himself anointed Solomon king (1 Kings 1:39) and then served before him.

The promise reaches its fullest meaning in Jesus Christ. He is the "merciful and faithful High Priest" (Hebrews 2:17) who always does what pleases His Father. He is also the Anointed King, the Christ, so in Him the faithful priest and God's anointed become one Person. Psalm 110 speaks of the King who is "a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek" (Psalm 110:4). Every human priest eventually died, but Jesus, "because He continues forever, has an unchangeable priesthood" (Hebrews 7:24). In Him the word "forever" in this verse finds its complete fulfillment.

Spirit & Life

[Christ & Life] The Faithful Priest. “Then I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest who shall do according to what is in My heart and in My mind. I will build him a sure house, and he shall walk before My anointed forever.” The prophecy found partial, temporary fulfillment in Samuel, “established as a prophet” (3:20), who replaced Eli as Israel’s leader, and in Zadok, who replaced Eli’s descendant Abiathar under Solomon (1 Kings 2:27, 35). But its ultimate trajectory is Jesus Christ. Unlike the Levitical priests who served by outward regulation and often failed, Jesus ministers entirely from the inner life, doing exactly what is in the Father’s heart and mind (John 8:29). He is the “merciful and faithful High Priest” (Hebrews 2:17), “faithful to Him who appointed Him” (Hebrews 3:2), whose house is eternal, and who works in perfect union with God forever. Here the container is opened: the hope sealed in this promise is Christ. We find our eternal security not in our own faithfulness but in the faithfulness of the Priest God raised up for Himself. His priesthood is our sure house.

[Discovery] Faithful Priest, Sure House: One Word Twice. “Faithful” and “sure” translate the same Hebrew word, ne’eman, “established, reliable.” It returns at key points: “Samuel had been established (ne’eman) as a prophet” (3:20); Abigail tells David the LORD will make him “an enduring (ne’eman) house” (25:28); God promises David, “your house … shall be established (ne’eman) forever” (2 Samuel 7:16), so that “sure house” is the very language of the Davidic covenant. God says of Moses, “He is faithful (ne’eman) in all My house” (Numbers 12:7), which Hebrews applies to Christ, “faithful … as a Son over His own house, whose house we are” (Hebrews 3:2–6). God builds sure houses for faithful servants, and the house He builds outlasts any house we inherit.

[Insight] According to What Is in My Heart. “Who shall do according to what is in My heart and in My mind.” “Mind” here is nephesh, “soul.” In verse 16 the priest’s servant was offered “as much as your heart (nephesh) desires”; the old priesthood served its own appetite. The faithful priest will serve God’s desire. The same measure is used of the king: “The LORD has sought for Himself a man after His own heart” (13:14). Faithfulness is measured by whose desire governs the servant: his own appetite, or God’s heart.

"And it shall come to pass that everyone who is left in your house"

God had already said that not every man in Eli's family line would be cut off. Some would remain to serve at the altar, but they would bring Eli grief rather than comfort (2:33). This verse describes what life would look like for those survivors. They would not lose their lives, but they would lose their standing.

"will come and bow down to him for a piece of silver and a morsel of bread,"

"Him" is the faithful priest God promised to raise up in place of Eli's house (2:35). Eli's family had held the highest priestly position in Israel, and men came to Shiloh to bring their sacrifices to them. Now Eli's own descendants would come to another priest and bow low before him. They would not come seeking honor or office, but only a small payment and a little food.

This is the reversal Hannah sang about at the beginning of the chapter: "Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread" (2:5). She was praising the LORD for lifting her up after years of humiliation. Here the same pattern falls on the priestly family that had grown fat at Shiloh. Eli's sons had taken the best meat from the people's sacrifices, even before the fat was burned to the LORD (2:15–16), and God charged the family with making themselves fat on the choicest offerings (2:29). Their descendants would be glad to receive a scrap of bread. The LORD had warned that "those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed" (2:30), and this is what that would mean for Eli's line.

"and say, “Please, put me in one of the priestly positions, that I may eat a piece of bread.”"

The priests lived from the portions of the offerings that God's law assigned to them. Eli's descendants would beg for any priestly post at all, however low, simply so they could share in that food and survive. They would not be asking to lead the priesthood or to stand in the place of honor. They would be asking only for permission to eat. The men who had treated the LORD's offerings as their own to grab would now depend on someone else's kindness for a share of them.

The judgment fell on Eli's family line, not on the priesthood itself. God's priestly service would continue through the faithful priest, and Eli's descendants would stand outside the place of honor, pleading to be let in. History records the fulfillment. Abiathar, a descendant of Eli, served as priest under David, but Solomon removed him from the priesthood. Scripture says this fulfilled the word the LORD had spoken concerning Eli's house (1 Kings 2:27), and Zadok took his place (1 Kings 2:35). With this verse, the man of God's message to Eli ends.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] From Fork to Begging Bowl. “Everyone who is left in your house will come and bow down to him for a piece of silver and a morsel of bread.” The family that took meat by force (v. 16) will beg for a crust. The “piece of silver” was the smallest coin, and “a morsel of bread” is a mouthful. They wasted the Lord’s heritage, and now they beg their bread. “Bow down” is the posture of worship, now given to a man for food. Hannah had already sung it: “Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread” (v. 5). The chapter that began with a barren woman lifted up ends with a proud house brought low, the great reversal fulfilled; God’s poetic justice is perfectly symmetrical. What we grab from God’s table, we eventually lose at our own.

[Application] Serving God for Bread. “Please, put me in one of the priestly positions, that I may eat a piece of bread.” Eli’s descendants will ask for priestly work, not to serve God, but to eat. It is the same motive that ruined their fathers, now reduced to begging. When the priesthood is pursued for what it gives rather than for Whom it serves, God’s judgment finally reduces the ministers to beggars. A line meant to feast in the presence of God is reduced to begging for a crust because it traded the sacred for the secular. Paul warns of those who “do not serve our Lord Jesus Christ, but their own belly” (Romans 16:18). Whoever serves God for bread will end up begging for it. Those who serve Him for Himself find that He is their portion. Are we serving God for Himself, or for the bread His service brings us: approval, position, security?

[Insight] A Descendant of Eli at the Ruins of Shiloh?. Abiathar was sent to his fields at Anathoth (1 Kings 2:26). Jeremiah was “of the priests who were in Anathoth” (Jeremiah 1:1), and many suggest he descended from Abiathar’s line. If so, a descendant of Eli’s house later preached, “Go now to My place which was in Shiloh” (Jeremiah 7:12). This is possible, not certain, but it would mean God turned the heir of a failed house into the voice that told its story.
