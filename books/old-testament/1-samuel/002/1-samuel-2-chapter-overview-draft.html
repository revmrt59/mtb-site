Morning Chapter Explanation

1 Samuel Chapter 2

Chapter 2

Central Message

The LORD honors those who honor Him and brings low those who treat Him as light. Hannah sings that God overturns whatever people trust in, and the chapter proves her right: Samuel, a child with no standing, grows before the LORD, while Eli’s house, secure in its office, is judged. What separates them is not position but whether God is given His place.

What This Chapter Is About

Hannah has just left her son at Shiloh, and she answers with a song rather than a lament. She does not thank God only for a child. She praises Him as the holy Rock who breaks the bow of the mighty, feeds the hungry, opens the barren womb, and lifts the poor from the ash heap. The song is theology before the story, and the story that follows tests every line of it.

Samuel now serves in a sanctuary whose priests do not know the LORD. Hophni and Phinehas are priests by birth, but they “did not know the LORD.” They seize the best of the sacrifice before the fat is burned, threaten worshipers who object, and use their position at the tabernacle door to satisfy their appetites. The narrator lets Samuel and these men share the same ground and keeps cutting from one to the other. Samuel ministers, wears the little robe his mother brings each year, and grows. Eli’s sons take, ignore their father’s rebuke, and move toward death. Eli himself stands between them, a man who speaks the right words and does not act on them.

Then God speaks directly. A man of God reminds Eli of everything his family was given and charges him with honoring his sons above the LORD. The privilege that should have bound Eli’s house to God becomes the ground of its judgment. Yet the chapter does not end in ruin alone. God promises a faithful priest who will act according to His heart and mind, and the bread the hungry sang about in verse 5 is what Eli’s descendants will beg for in verse 36.

Shiloh, in the hill country of Ephraim, holds the tabernacle in the days of the judges, before Israel has a king. Eli is the senior priest, and his sons serve with him. Samuel’s home is Ramah, and his parents come up each year to sacrifice. Religious life is centered on this one place, which is why the corruption of its priests reaches so far.

Chapter 1 ended with a barren woman’s vow kept. Chapter 3 opens with the word of the LORD “rare in those days,” and chapter 4 carries out what the man of God announced: Hophni and Phinehas die on one day, the ark is captured, and Eli dies at the news. Chapter 2 is the hinge between Hannah’s answered prayer and the collapse of the old order. It also opens a frame around the whole book. Hannah’s song and David’s song near the end (2 Samuel 22) both call the LORD a Rock, and both close with His king and His anointed.

Hannah’s song is itself framed by a horn. “My horn is exalted in the LORD” opens it in verse 1, and God exalting “the horn of His anointed” closes it in verse 10, so that a woman’s private vindication widens into the hope of a king. The narrative moves in a matching rhythm, with Samuel’s growth (vv. 11, 18–21, 26) set between the scenes of Eli’s sons (vv. 12–17, 22–25), and the reader is left to weigh the two lives side by side. Bread supplies the last reversal: those “full” who hire themselves out for bread in verse 5 become Eli’s house begging for a morsel of it in verse 36.

The Heart of the Chapter

Every figure in this chapter answers the same question: who stands first? Hannah rejoices in the LORD before she rejoices in the son she has given back. Samuel ministers to Him in a linen ephod, long before anyone knows what he will become. Hophni and Phinehas treat His offering as their own, and Eli, by failing to restrain them, honors his sons above Him. The LORD answers each in kind.

Chapter Sections

1 Samuel 2:1–10 — Hannah’s Song

Hannah turns a personal deliverance into a declaration about how God governs the world. The holy God who knows and weighs actions overturns the proud and the strong, raises the weak, and guards His faithful ones. The song ends by looking past herself to a king Israel does not yet have.

1 Samuel 2:11–17 — Samuel Ministers; Eli’s Sons Take

Samuel is introduced as a servant of the LORD before Eli, and at once the narrator sets beside him men who do not know the LORD. Their “custom” overrides God’s instructions, and their conduct brings the LORD’s offering into contempt. The verdict is stated flatly: their sin was “very great before the LORD.”

1 Samuel 2:18–21 — Samuel Grows; Hannah Is Blessed

The quiet counterpart follows: a boy in a linen ephod, a mother’s yearly robe, Eli’s blessing on Elkanah and Hannah. God visits Hannah with three more sons and two daughters. The scene is small and warm, and it gives the chapter its one picture of what a sanctuary should look like.

1 Samuel 2:22–26 — Eli’s Rebuke and Its Failure

Eli hears of his sons’ conduct, including immorality at the sanctuary door, and confronts them with words that are true and come too late. They will not listen, and the narrator says the LORD desired to put them to death. Samuel’s growth in favor with God and people closes the scene, set against that darkening backdrop.

1 Samuel 2:27–36 — The Man of God’s Word to Eli

God’s own voice answers the failure. Eli’s house is reminded of what it received, charged with contempt for the offering, and sentenced: its strength will be cut off, its men will die young, and the deaths of Hophni and Phinehas on one day will be the sign. In the midst of judgment, God promises to build a faithful priest a sure house.

Why This Chapter Matters

Samuel’s rise is not accidental. The book is built on the pattern Hannah sings, where God brings down established strength and raises what looks insignificant. Eli’s house falls and Samuel rises here; Saul falls and David rises later. The song teaches the reader how to read the rest of the book.

Office does not guarantee knowing God. Hophni and Phinehas hold every credential Israel’s worship could give, yet they do not know the LORD. Samuel holds none of them and ministers before Him. The chapter also marks a shift in leadership: authority moves away from a priestly house that has failed and toward a man whom God is forming in quiet. That shift prepares the way for the prophet who will anoint Israel’s first two kings.

God takes seriously how His name, His offering, and His people are treated. The chapter’s judgment is not a burst of anger. It comes after privilege, after warning, and after long tolerance of what Eli knew was wrong.

Key Themes to Watch For

Reversal

Hannah’s song is a catalog of turnings: the strong are broken, the hungry are filled, the barren bears, the poor sit with princes. The chapter enacts them. The song is not a formula that guarantees every outcome, but it declares that no condition lies outside God’s rule.

Knowing God and Holding Office

Priests who do not know the LORD stand beside a child who ministers to Him. The chapter separates position from relationship, and it shows what fills the gap: appetite, custom, and self-interest.

Honor and Contempt

God’s verdict on Eli’s house is spoken in terms of honor. Eli honors his sons more than God, and Hophni and Phinehas make God’s offering light. The chapter presents this as the root failure, one that Eli’s rebuke fails to reach.

Leaders Who Take

The sons of Eli grab what belongs to God and to the worshipers, and the text sees that as a leadership failure, not merely a private sin. Eli sees it too, and does not restrain them. The pattern of leaders who take rather than serve returns in Samuel’s warning about a king in chapter 8.

Quiet Preparation

While a priesthood collapses, God is growing a boy. Samuel’s advance is described in small steps, year by year, in a place where nobody would have expected a leader to form. The chapter shows that God can begin preparing what comes next even while a failing structure remains in place.

Key Words & Concepts

Horn

A horn stands for strength and dignity, and it frames the song. Hannah’s horn is exalted in verse 1; God exalts the horn of His anointed in verse 10.

Know

In this context, to know the LORD means more than possessing information about Him. The lives of Hophni and Phinehas show no genuine recognition, reverence, or submission, while Samuel “ministered to the LORD.” The chapter sets the two side by side.

Honor and Light

The Hebrew kabed means heavy, weighty; qalal means light. God tells Eli that those who honor Him will be honored and those who despise Him will be lightly esteemed. The play on words governs the oracle and returns in Ichabod’s name (4:21), where “glory” is from the same root as “honor.”

Anointed

Mashiach means “anointed one.” Here it refers to the king God will establish, and the word carries the trajectory of the book toward David and, in the larger story of Scripture, toward the Messiah.
