Chapter Reflections

1 Samuel 2

Reflect and Respond

Foundational Truths

1. God rules over the reversal of human fortunes

Hannah’s song (2:1–10) is the lens through which the rest of the chapter is read. She sings that the mighty are broken and the stumbling are strengthened, the full hire themselves out for bread and the hungry are satisfied, the barren bears and the mother of many languishes, and the poor are lifted from the ash heap to sit among princes (vv. 4–8). None of this stays in the song. Within the chapter, the woman who was low is lifted, a boy with no position grows in favor, and the priestly house that held every advantage is cut down (vv. 31–36). The same pattern carries through the whole book: Eli’s house falls while Samuel rises, and later Saul falls while David rises. History in Samuel is not driven by strength, status, or institutions. It is driven by the God who “brings low and lifts up” (v. 7).

The chapter is careful about what this does and does not mean. Hannah is not offering a formula in which every barren woman conceives or every poor person becomes rich, and she is not saying that every hardship is God’s direct verdict on a person. The Study is explicit that poverty has many causes, including injustice and oppression, and that being low is “a condition, not necessarily God’s final word” over a life. What the song establishes is that no condition lies outside God’s rule, so that no one has grounds to boast in present strength and no one has grounds to despair in present weakness.

2. God’s holiness grounds both His mercy and His judgment

“No one is holy like the LORD” (v. 2) is the truth that lifts Hannah, and it is the same truth that sentences Eli’s house. The holiness that stoops to a weeping woman at Shiloh is the holiness that will not be treated lightly at His altar. The chapter does not present mercy and judgment as two moods in God, one soft and one severe. They are one holy character meeting two different hearts: a heart that comes empty-handed and pours itself out before Him, and hearts that come to His altar to take.

This is why Hannah’s song and the oracle of judgment belong together. The God who is “a rock” for the humble (v. 2) is the God whose weighing of actions (v. 3) falls on the proud. A reader who takes only the first half of the chapter meets a God who is only gentle; a reader who takes only the second half meets a God who is only severe. The chapter gives both, and they come from the same holiness.

3. Nearness to holy things is not knowing the holy God

Hophni and Phinehas lived at the altar, wore the priestly garments, handled the sacrifices, and “did not know the LORD” (v. 12). Samuel, a child in a linen ephod, “ministered before the LORD” and “grew before the LORD” (vv. 18, 21). The difference was not age, title, family, or religious activity. It was whether a person actually knew God. The chapter also keeps the difference between Samuel’s “not yet” (3:7) and the sons’ settled rejection, so that a young person still being introduced to God is not confused with a person who has hardened against Him.

This is one of the chapter’s sharpest warnings, and it is aimed at people who are close to the things of God: those raised in the church, those in ministry, those who have learned the language and the routines. Position, access, and familiarity are gifts, but they are not the relationship. The chapter calls every generation that grows up near the sanctuary to move from proximity to knowing the LORD.

4. Leadership in God’s house is stewardship, not ownership

Throughout the oracle of judgment God speaks in the language of ownership: “My priest,” “My altar,” “My sacrifice and My offering,” “My dwelling place,” “Israel My people” (vv. 28–29). The priests were given a calling, access, and provision, but they owned none of it. Their sin began when they began to treat what belonged to God and His people as something available for their own advantage. The fork in the pot, the demand for meat before God’s portion, and the threat of force all come from that single mistake.

Because leaders answer for what is not theirs, the chapter also insists that no position places anyone beyond correction. God sends a “man of God” to Eli, the senior priest, and holds him accountable (v. 27). The greater the privilege, the heavier the accountability, and the God who gave the privilege is the One who reviews how it was used.

5. God honors those who honor Him

Verse 30 is the moral center of the chapter: “for those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed.” The Hebrew sets kabed (heavy, weighty, honored) against qalal (light, trivial). To honor God is to give Him weight, to let Him decide what comes first when loyalties collide. Eli’s failure is stated in exactly those terms: he “honor[ed]” his sons more than God (v. 29). The chapter does not say that honoring God brings wealth, ease, or recognition; Samuel himself will know difficulty and rejection. What it says is that God takes seriously the weight people give Him, and that a position of honor in the world does not settle the honor God gives.

6. God’s purposes do not depend on failing leaders

While Shiloh’s priesthood is being cut down, God is already raising Samuel (vv. 18, 21, 26) and promising a faithful priest who will “do according to what is in My heart and in My mind” (v. 35). The failure of Hophni and Phinehas is real and is condemned, and God does not let it decide what He will do. This is a comfort for those who grieve the failures of leaders they trusted, but it needs to be held as the chapter holds it: God raised up Samuel and promised a faithful priest. The chapter does not promise that every corrupt season will visibly have an immediate replacement. It shows that God’s purposes rest on Himself.

Chapter Insights

1. The author’s split-screen

The chapter is built as an alternation. Samuel ministers (v. 11); the sons abuse the sacrifices (vv. 12–17); Samuel wears his ephod (v. 18) and Hannah brings his robe (vv. 19–21); the sons sleep with the women at the door and refuse their father (vv. 22–25); Samuel grows in favor (v. 26); a man of God pronounces judgment (vv. 27–36). The camera cuts back and forth so that we watch two households growing up in the same sanctuary, in the same weeks, in opposite directions. The repetition is not filler. It is the way the narrator makes the reader feel the difference between position and direction, and it means that every notice about Samuel is a comment on the sons, and every notice about the sons is a comment on Samuel.

2. The fork and the robe

Two objects carry the chapter. One is the priests’ three-pronged fork, plunged into worshipers’ pots to take whatever came up (vv. 13–14). The other is Hannah’s little robe, made each year and carried to Shiloh for the son she had given away (v. 19). One household comes to Shiloh to take; one woman comes bringing something she has made and given. The story never states the comparison, and it does not need to. Ministry that consumes and devotion that gives appear in the same courtyard, and the reader sees which of them God is watching.

3. The bread that comes full circle

Hannah’s song says, “Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread” (v. 5). The chapter closes with the descendants of the men who seized the best of every sacrifice begging, “Please, put me in one of the priestly positions, that I may eat a piece of bread” (v. 36). The people who would not be satisfied with the portion God assigned end up asking for a morsel. This is the chapter’s reversal made concrete, and it shows that Hannah’s song was not a general sentiment: it was a prediction that the chapter then carries out in detail.

4. Priests who take, kings who take

The priests’ “custom” (mishpat) of taking (vv. 13–16) is the same word Samuel will use for “the behavior of the king” in 8:9–17, where the verb “he will take” runs through the passage. Corruption of leadership in Israel appears first at the altar and later at the throne. The book of Samuel is not only about which king God will choose. It is about what kind of leader Israel has, and whether that leader takes from the people or gives to them.

5. The weight of glory

The root k-b-d (heavy, honor, glory) is a thread through these chapters. Eli honors (kabed) his sons (2:29); God honors (kabed) those who honor Him (2:30); Eli is “heavy” (kabed) when he falls (4:18); and his grandson is named Ichabod, “the glory (kabod) has departed” (4:21). Even the “throne of glory” in Hannah’s song (2:8) belongs to the same word family. Eli gave weight to the wrong things, and he became weighty in the wrong way. The chapter is ultimately about what a person gives weight to, and what happens when God is made light.

6. Sound words and a soft will

Eli is not a cartoon villain. He blesses Hannah graciously (v. 20), speaks true words to his sons (vv. 23–25), and will later receive judgment with humility (3:18). Yet God says his fault was that he honored his sons more than God (v. 29), and later that “he did not restrain them” (3:13). The text does not tell us why he did not act; the Study notes that his attachment to his sons, a reluctance to act decisively, or a desire to preserve the relationship may have contributed. The chapter’s point does not depend on knowing his motive. Correct words, personal kindness, and even real distress over sin are not the same as acting on what one knows, especially where the sin lives at home.

7. Guilt and judgment held together

Verse 25 says both that the sons “did not heed the voice of their father” and that “the LORD desired to kill them.” Scripture does not ask us to pick one. Their rebellion was fully their own and consistently presented as wicked, and God’s judgment was fully His. The verse also answers a question the reader might silently ask: God did not tolerate their abuse because He was indifferent. He had determined its end. The chapter therefore teaches that divine judgment is not arbitrary and that persistent rebellion can reach a point where God confirms the direction it has chosen, without making God the author of evil.

8. What leaders do to worship

Hophni and Phinehas were not only wicked in themselves. Their conduct affected how people related to the offering (v. 17). The passage supports two movements at once: priests grew numb to what was holy through repeated, self-serving familiarity, and their irreverence then made the people despise what was holy. Yet the offering was still “the offering of the LORD.” The failure of leaders can damage people’s view of worship, but it does not change what God is like. This is a word both for those who lead and for those who have been hurt by leaders.

9. Surrender that keeps loving

Hannah sings on the day she leaves Samuel (v. 1), and she returns every year with a robe (v. 19). The chapter does not describe a woman who gave her son away and stopped caring. It describes a woman who gave him to God and kept loving him within that gift. Her surrender is real, and it is not detachment. God’s later gifts to her (v. 21) are grace and not payment; the Study is careful that the passage gives no formula in which giving to God obligates Him to give back.

10. A barren prayer and a prophet

Samuel arises in a day when “the word of the LORD was rare” (3:1), and he arises from a barren woman’s anguish and prayer. This is not a rule for how God always works, but it is what this story shows: Hannah’s tears in chapter 1 stand behind a voice for a whole nation in a spiritually barren generation. God was doing more through her prayer than she could have seen at Shiloh.

11. Consequences that reach forward without transferred guilt

Eli’s failure and his sons’ corruption shape his house for generations: the deaths of Hophni and Phinehas, the massacre at Nob, and Abiathar’s removal by Solomon (vv. 31–36; 1 Kings 2:27). The Study is careful that this is the loss of a forfeited office and the breaking of a household’s standing as a priestly dynasty, and not guilt passed to innocent descendants (compare Ezekiel 18:20). A leader’s decisions do reach forward, and each person remains responsible for himself before God.

Christ & Life

Hannah’s anointed and the hope of the Messiah

Hannah’s song ends by looking beyond her own story: “He will give strength to His king, and exalt the horn of His anointed” (v. 10). Israel had no king when she sang. At the historical level she is speaking of the monarchy God will give, and of the kind of king God Himself strengthens; the “anointed” in this verse is first of all a king, and David is the one who will fill the description. This is also the first time in the Bible that mashiach is used of a king, and the word is the source of “Messiah” and “Christ.” Hannah is not giving a detailed prediction of Jesus, but her song introduces language that becomes central to the Bible’s messianic hope. The song forms a frame: it begins with “my horn is exalted in the LORD” (v. 1) and ends with the horn of God’s anointed exalted (v. 10), and the books of Samuel are framed by Hannah’s song and David’s song near the end (2 Samuel 22).

The New Testament hears this. Mary’s Magnificat (Luke 1:46–55) deliberately echoes Hannah: both women rejoice in God their Savior and sing of a God who brings down the powerful and lifts the lowly, and both sing because of a son given by God. Zechariah, in Luke 1:69, says God “has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of His servant David.” Hannah’s horn is exalted in Samuel; the horn of salvation is raised in Jesus. This does not make Samuel a type of Christ in every detail. It places Hannah’s song at the beginning of a path that leads to the One in whom the reversal she sings about is fulfilled.

God’s authority over the grave

“He brings down to the grave and brings up” (v. 6) is Hebrew poetry about the scope of God’s rule, and in its immediate setting it may describe rescue from the brink of death. Hannah is not teaching the full New Testament doctrine of bodily resurrection. But she confesses something that resurrection depends upon: the grave is not beyond God’s authority. Peter sees the same God at work when he quotes Psalm 16 as fulfilled in God’s raising of Christ (Acts 2:27–32), and the risen Lord says, “I have the keys of Hades and of Death” (Revelation 1:18). The God who holds life and death in Hannah’s song is the God of the empty tomb.

From the ash heap to the throne

The lifting of the poor “from the ash heap … to set them among princes” (v. 8) finds its fullest expression in the gospel. Christ Jesus, though in the form of God, took the lowest place, humbling Himself to death on a cross (Philippians 2:5–11), and He raises those who believe to sit “together in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus” (Ephesians 2:6) and to share His throne (Revelation 3:21). What we inherit we never earned; it is given by grace. Hannah’s song already says this in its own terms: the poor do not lift themselves, and the throne is inherited, not achieved.

The Shepherd who gives Himself

Hophni and Phinehas are the first in a line of shepherds who take from the flock. Ezekiel later indicts Israel’s leaders in words that sound like them: “You eat the fat and clothe yourselves with the wool … but you do not feed the flock” (Ezekiel 34:3). God’s answer there is that He will shepherd His people Himself. In Jesus this is fulfilled: He is the Good Shepherd who “gives His life for the sheep” (John 10:11). Where Eli’s sons took the offering by force, Jesus became the offering. The fork and the robe find their final contrast here, in one Person who does not take from the people He serves but gives Himself for them.

The question Eli could not answer

“If a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?” (v. 25). The Old Testament itself knows sacrifice, priestly intercession, repentance, and divine mercy, so Eli’s question is not a claim that forgiveness was categorically closed. It describes the terrifying position of persistent rebels who despise the very God to whom they must appeal. The New Testament answers the question at its deepest level. There is “one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5), an Advocate with the Father (1 John 2:1), who “always lives to make intercession” for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). Those who take refuge in Him are not left with Eli’s question.

The faithful priest and the anointed King

God’s promise, “I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest … and he shall walk before My anointed forever” (v. 35), is best read in layers. Samuel is the immediate faithful servant raised up as Eli’s house declines. Zadok and his line are the strongest dynastic and historical fulfillment, as Solomon puts Zadok in Abiathar’s place (1 Kings 2:26–35) and Zadok serves alongside David and Solomon. Christ is the canonical culmination of the pattern, not one more equivalent historical referent beside them. He alone does all that is in the Father’s heart (John 8:29). He is the “merciful and faithful High Priest” (Hebrews 2:17; 3:2), and in Him the faithful Priest and the Anointed King become one Person, after the order of Melchizedek (Hebrews 7). The priest who was to walk before God’s anointed is, in Jesus, the Anointed Himself.

The growing child

Luke’s description of Jesus, that He “increased in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men” (Luke 2:52), deliberately echoes Samuel (v. 26). The parallel does not make Samuel equal to Jesus, and it does not need a developed typology to be meaningful. It places a faithful boy growing up in a corrupt setting within a pattern that is fulfilled supremely in the Son who grew in favor before God and men.

The bread of life

The chapter ends with descendants of men who seized the best meat begging for bread. Scripture later presents Jesus as the One who gives Himself as “the bread of life” (John 6:35) and says of the hungry that God fills them “with good things” (Luke 1:53). This is a canonical meditation rather than the immediate meaning of “bread” in the oracle, but it is a fitting close to the chapter’s reversal: those who grasp end up begging, and those who come to Christ are given a place at the table.

Life with Christ today

The chapter asks whether our joy rests in the Giver or the gift, whether we know Him or are only near holy things, and what we give weight to when loyalties collide. It teaches that God is not impressed by titles and that He sees what is done at His altar. It also gives good news to those who stand where Hannah stood: those who are low, waiting, or hurt by leaders who did not represent Him. The One who lifted a grieving woman at Shiloh has lifted His people through the Anointed One, and the Priest who is faithful now lives to intercede for them.

Dwelling in the Word

1. Hannah sang on the day she left her little boy at Shiloh. If God asked me to hold His answer to my longest prayer with open hands, would my joy be found in the gift or in the Giver? What would my song sound like?

2. Hannah’s song names no rival and settles no score, though Peninnah had provoked her for years. Where am I still lifting my own horn, and what would it look like to leave my vindication to the LORD?

3. Where might I be more concerned about how something appears to others than about what the God of knowledge sees in my motives? Verse 3 says the LORD is the “God of knowledge” and that “by Him actions are weighed.” If my recent words were placed on those scales, which of them would I most want to take back?

4. Hophni and Phinehas handled holy things every day and did not know the LORD. What have I mistaken for knowing Him: my church background, my role, my familiarity with Scripture? What would it mean to come to Him again as a child?

5. The priests’ “custom” replaced God’s commandment (v. 13). Where have my own preferences quietly become “the way we’ve always done it,” serving me more than they honor Him?

6. God’s portion, the fat, was to be burned first (v. 15). What are my first hours, first resources, and first affections, and does God receive them or what is left?

7. The chapter says the priests became numb to what was holy and their irreverence made the people despise what was holy (v. 17). Has familiarity dulled my own reverence? Has my example made it easier or harder for someone else to love worshiping God?

8. Eli heard everything, said the right things, and did not restrain his sons. What do I already know that I have addressed only with words?

9. God says Eli honored his sons more than Him (v. 29). Who or what do I honor above God when loyalties collide, and what would it look like to give God weight this week?

10. Hannah’s robe grew larger every year. What hidden, faithful act of love can I keep carrying to God on behalf of someone I have entrusted to Him?

11. When something I care about seems to be decaying, am I looking only at the corruption, or also asking where God may be quietly raising a “But Samuel”?

12. “Who will intercede for him?” Eli asked. Do I live daily in the relief that Jesus has answered that question for me? Where do I still stand as though no one were speaking for me?

13. The faithful priest does “according to what is in My heart and in My mind.” In my service, am I asking what I want or what God wants? What might He be asking of me now?

14. Hannah describes God reversing strength, weakness, hunger, barrenness, poverty, and position (vv. 4–8). What situation have I begun to treat as fixed that I need to place again under God’s authority?

15. Samuel continued serving and growing while serious failure surrounded him. Am I allowing another person’s failure, disappointment, or hypocrisy to affect my own faithfulness to God?

16. Verse 36 ends with a house asking for a morsel of bread. Where am I tempted to seize what God has not assigned, and what would it mean to be satisfied with the portion He has given?

17. After dwelling in this chapter, ask: what does my life reveal about whom I truly honor?
