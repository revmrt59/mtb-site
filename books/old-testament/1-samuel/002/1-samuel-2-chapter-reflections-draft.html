Chapter Reflections

1 Samuel 2

Reflect and Respond
1 Samuel 2:1–11 - Hannah’s Prayer of Thanksgiving

Insights

1. She came asking for a son and left seeing a King.
Hannah originally came to God carrying the anguish of barrenness and asking for a child. But her prayer in chapter 2 reaches far beyond Samuel. She begins speaking about how God governs the world, brings down the proud, raises the lowly, judges His adversaries, and finally gives strength to His king and exalts His Anointed (2:10). Prayer brought Hannah more than an answer to her need; it brought her into something of God's heart and purposes. We often come to God wanting Him to speak into our concerns, but as we remain before Him, He reveals to us a King. For us, that King is Jesus.

2. Prayer can move from pouring out our heart to receiving God's heart.
There is nothing wrong with coming to God because we need something. Hannah did. But prayer is not complete when we have finished telling God what is on our heart. As we remain before Him, He can enlarge our vision and begin showing us what is on His.

3. Our tests can become our testimony.
The broken bows, strengthened weak, hungry made full, and barren woman bearing children were not abstract theology to Hannah (2:4–5). She had personally experienced the God who overturns what looked hopeless. What had once been the place of her greatest pain had now become part of her testimony about who God is and what He has done.

4. The rival gets smaller as God gets larger.
Hannah begins with her enemies in view (2:1), but Peninnah soon disappears from the prayer. Hannah does not spend the song rehearsing what was done to her. Her attention becomes consumed with who God is. Healing often becomes visible when the offender no longer occupies the center of our story because God has become larger than our enemies and circumstances.

5. Hannah's prayer ultimately turns our eyes toward Jesus.
Her song ends with God's King and His Anointed. What was still future in Hannah's day ultimately finds its fullest expression in Christ, the Anointed King whose kingdom does not depend upon human strength and whose exaltation comes from God Himself.

Dwelling in the Word

  1. When I come to God with my needs, do I leave room for Him to show me what is on His heart to encounter my king, Jesus?

  2. Where has God turned tests into testimonies in my life?

  3. Is there a person, offense, or disappointment that has become larger in my thoughts than God Himself?

1 Samuel 2:12–17 - Eli’s Wicked Sons

Insights

1. Near the altar does not mean near to God.
Hophni and Phinehas handled sacrifices every day, yet Scripture says, “they did not know the LORD” (2:12). They lived around holy things without knowing the Holy One. Church, ministry, Scripture, worship, and spiritual activity can place us near the things of God without resulting in a relationship with God. In this we are reminded of the words of Jesus, “I never knew you; depart from Me, you who practice lawlessness! (Mat 7:23)

2. Custom becomes dangerous when it displaces obedience to His Word.
Verse 13 calls their practice “the priests’ custom,” but God had already prescribed the priests’ portion. Their custom had become a replacement for what God had written in His word. The danger is not that customs exist, but that “this is how we do it” quietly becomes more authoritative than “this is what God has said.”

3. Their fork revealed what was in their hearts.
The three-pronged fork was meant to take the priests’ permitted portion from the sacrifice, but Eli’s sons used it to take what they wanted on their own terms (2:13–14). The problem was larger than meat. Their hands exposed their hearts. They approached worship asking, in effect, “What can I get from this?” The same question can quietly enter our service to God. We can attend, serve, give, lead, or worship while still reaching for recognition, comfort, influence, blessing, or something for ourselves.

4. God gets the first and the best.
Eli’s sons demanded their portion before the fat had been offered to the LORD (2:15–16). They put their appetite ahead of God’s honor. God is not meant to receive what remains after we have satisfied ourselves. Putting Him first reaches into our money, our time, our worship, our weekly priorities, and the ordering of our lives.

5. The way we live affects how others see God.
Verse 17 says the people came to despise the offering of the LORD because of the priests’ conduct. Their sin distorted how others viewed something that belonged to God. The same is true for us. People often form impressions of God through those who claim to know Him. Our lives can make Him more beautiful and credible to others, or make Him harder for them to honor.

Dwelling in the Word

  1. Am I genuinely seeking to know the LORD, or am I mislead in thinking being around the things of God is the same as knowing Him.

  2. Is there any tradition, habit, or church custom I have allowed to carry more authority than what God has actually said?

  3. The fork exposes a question worth asking ourselves: when I come to worship, serve, give, or lead, am I coming primarily to honor God, or am I quietly reaching for what I want from Him?

  4. Does the ordering of my time, money, and priorities show that God receives first place?

  5. How does the way I live affect how others see God?

1 Samuel 2:18–21 - Samuel’s Childhood Ministry

Insights

1. What marks a servant of God is the One he serves.
Samuel ministered “before the LORD” even as a child (2:18). The contrast with Eli’s sons is sharp: they used ministry for themselves, while Samuel’s service was directed toward the LORD. What marks a servant of God is not the robe, title, platform, or size of the assignment, but the One before whom he serves.

2. Some surrender has to be lived again year after year.
Each year Hannah returned with a new robe for the son she had given to God (2:19). She remained his mother, loved him, and cared for him, but she did not take back what she had surrendered. Some offerings are placed on the altar once and then honored repeatedly afterward.

3. Samuel grew before the LORD before he grew before people.
The emphasis is not merely that Samuel got older. He “grew before the LORD” (2:21). His development was happening in God’s presence. Before God would use Samuel publicly, He was forming him privately.

4. Our Faithfulness to God does not depend on the faithfulness of people.
Hannah and Elkanah continued returning to Shiloh to worship even while the priesthood there was corrupt. Their worship was aimed at the LORD, not at the men wearing the robes. The failures of people should never be allowed to become an excuse for withdrawing our own heart from God.

5. God is able to do abundantly above what we ask or think.
Hannah had asked God for one son. After she gave Samuel back to Him, the LORD gave her three more sons and two daughters (2:21). God was not limited to the answer Hannah first imagined. Her story illustrates a larger truth seen throughout Scripture: God is able to exceed what we ask or think (Ephesians 3:20).

6. Surrender is proved by what we do after the emotional moment has passed.
It is one thing to place something in God’s hands. It is another to keep it there year after year. Hannah’s robe shows a surrender that remained intact.

7. Private growth before God matters more than early visibility.
Samuel’s significance did not begin when the nation recognized him. It began while he was still a child quietly ministering before the LORD.

Dwelling in the Word

  1. Am I growing before God in my private life? Do I minister to the Lord in my private life?

  2. Am I allowing the failures around me to determine my own spiritual condition?

  3. Have I allowed the failures of other believers or leaders to affect my own faithfulness to God?

  4. Where do I need to believe again that God is able to do beyond what I have asked or imagined?

1 Samuel 2:22–36 - Eli’s Sons Condemned and the House of Eli Judged

Insights

1. Talking is not the same as doing.
Eli confronted his sons and plainly told them that their behavior was evil (2:23–25), yet God still held him responsible because he did not restrain them. Knowing what is wrong, saying what is wrong, and actually dealing with what is wrong are not the same thing. Words can become a substitute for the obedience that truth requires.

2. Ignored warnings can become a hardened heart.
Eli’s sons had been confronted, yet “they did not heed the voice of their father” (2:25). Their refusal was no longer an isolated decision.. One of the dangers of repeatedly resisting correction is that what once convicted us can eventually stop moving us. If God is still speaking, the right time to respond is now. Now is the day of Salvation.

3. God warns before He judges.
Before the man of God announces judgment, God reminds Eli of His goodness to his family: “Did I not clearly reveal Myself...?” (2:27–28). The warning itself is mercy. God confronts because there is something that must be faced, and His warnings should never be treated lightly.

4. What we protect above God can become an idol.
God identifies Eli’s deepest failure: “You honor your sons more than Me” (2:29). His loves and loyalties were out of order. Anything we protect, excuse, or preserve at the expense of obedience has begun to compete with God’s rightful place.

5. We can condemn what we still benefit from.
Eli disapproved of his sons’ behavior, yet God says that he had made himself fat with the best of Israel’s offerings (2:29). It is possible to speak against something while remaining comfortable with what it gives us. God looks not only at what we say about wrong, but at what we continue to receive from it.

6. Man’s sin cannot thwart God’s purposes.
Eli’s house was corrupt and moving toward judgment, but God’s purposes were not in jeopardy. Samuel was already growing before the LORD, and God promised to raise up a faithful priest (2:26, 35). Human sin can cause terrible damage and real consequences, but it cannot overturn what God has determined to accomplish.

7. The faithful Priest carries the heart of God.
God promised to raise up a faithful priest who would “do according to what is in My heart and in My mind” (2:35). That promise ultimately points us to Jesus, the perfectly faithful High Priest who came and did all that was in the Father’s heart. In Jesus we see what the heart of God looks like in flesh: faithful, obedient, holy, merciful, and completely aligned with the Father.

8. Loving someone does not mean protecting them from what obedience requires.
Eli’s attachment to his sons kept him from dealing fully with what God required. Love can require confrontation, boundaries, correction, or allowing consequences rather than shielding someone from them. Love always seeks to act in someone’s eternal best interests. Love does not rejoice in iniquity, but rejoices in the truth.

9. The failed priesthood points us toward the faithful Priest.
Eli asks, “If a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?” (2:25). Scripture ultimately answers that question in Jesus, who “always lives to make intercession” for His people (Hebrews 7:25). Human priests fail, but God provides the faithful High Priest we need. Jesus stands making intercession for us right now.

Dwelling in the Word

  1. Is there anything I know is wrong but have stopped short of dealing with because obedience would be costly?

  2. Is there a warning or correction from God that I have heard so often that I am beginning to stop hearing it?

  3. Is there anyone or anything I am protecting, pleasing, or preserving above obedience to God?

  4. Am I benefiting from anything I say I disagree with or know is unhealthy?

  5. When I serve God, am I primarily trying to carry out my own preferences or to know and carry what is in His heart?
