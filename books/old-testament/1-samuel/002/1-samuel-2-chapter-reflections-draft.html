Chapter Reflections

1 Samuel 2

Reflect and Respond
1 Samuel 2:1–11 — Hannah’s Prayer of Thanksgiving

Reflections

[Insight] One Answered Prayer Taught Hannah How God Runs the World (1 Samuel 2:1–10). The song begins with “my heart,” “my horn,” “my mouth” (v. 1) and ends with “the ends of the earth,” “His king,” and “His anointed” (v. 10). Hannah starts with her personal deliverance (v. 1), turns to the character of God (vv. 2–3), widens to His dealings with all humanity, the proud and the poor, the living and the dead (vv. 4–8), and crescendos in prophetic revelation about the ends of the earth and the coming Messiah (vv. 9–10). Very quickly the song loses sight of Samuel altogether. She discerned in her own experience the general laws of God’s dealings with the world. Her prayer in 1:11 asked for one son; her praise in 2:1–10 sees a kingdom. This is the anatomy of mature worship: it uses the personal miracle as a launchpad to declare the universal majesty and eternal purposes of God. When a believer’s personal agony is resolved in God’s presence, the praise that follows breaks the boundaries of that individual life; true gratitude catapults the soul into prophetic alignment. God’s private kindness to one person is a window on how He governs history and where He is taking it. When God answers us, He is not only meeting a need; He is showing us Himself.

[Foundational Truth] The Principle of the Great Reversal (1 Samuel 2:1–11). The dominant theme of these verses is the great reversal. Bows are broken, stumblers are strengthened, the full go hungry, the barren bears seven, the poor are seated with princes. God consistently opposes the proud and exalts the humble. His kingdom runs on an inverted economy: He bypasses the proud, the resourced, and the powerful to accomplish His purposes through the broken, the empty, and the desperate. This section is the theological overture to the books of Samuel, and indeed to the whole Bible, and it introduces for the first time the “anointed” King, tying God’s final victory over darkness to a coming Messiah. The pattern plays out at once: God bypasses Eli’s powerful house for a little boy named Samuel, and later bypasses tall King Saul for a shepherd boy named David. Eli’s house falls, Samuel rises; Saul falls, David rises. History is not driven by strength, status, or institutions, but by the God who “brings low and lifts up.” We should expect His great reversals.

[Application] First the Sacrifice, Then the Song (1 Samuel 1:28; 2:1, 11). Hannah sang on the day she gave her son away (1:28; 2:11), not on the day she received him. First the sacrifice, then the song. So it was when Hezekiah restored temple worship: “when the burnt offering began, the song of the LORD also began” (2 Chronicles 29:27). Mary’s song came after her surrender: “Let it be to me according to your word” (Luke 1:38). The deepest worship in this chapter comes not from receiving but from releasing. Families who dedicate a child, ministries that hand off something precious, believers who place a dream back in God’s hands, often find that the song comes on the far side of the sacrifice, not before it.

[Discovery] A Mother’s Song and a King’s Song Frame the Books of Samuel (1 Samuel 2:1–10; 2 Samuel 22:1–51). The books of Samuel open with Hannah’s song and close, before the appendices, with David’s song, and the two share their vocabulary: horn (2:1, 10; 2 Samuel 22:3), rock (2:2; 2 Samuel 22:2–3, 32), salvation (2:1; 2 Samuel 22:3), the grave (2:6; 2 Samuel 22:6), thunder from heaven (2:10; 2 Samuel 22:14), king and anointed (2:10; 2 Samuel 22:51). Both celebrate the LORD as Rock and speak of His anointed king. David’s last line, “He is the tower of salvation to His king, and shows mercy to His anointed” (2 Samuel 22:51), answers Hannah’s last line. What God begins in a woman’s prayer He completes in a king’s praise, and everything in between, Samuel’s call, Saul’s fall, David’s rise, is the outworking of one song. It has rightly been called a golden key to the whole book.

[Christ & Life] Two Mothers’ Songs and One Horn of Salvation (1 Samuel 2:1–10; Luke 1:46–55, 68–69). Mary’s Magnificat follows Hannah’s song throughout: “He has put down the mighty from their thrones, and exalted the lowly. He has filled the hungry with good things, and the rich He has sent away empty” (Luke 1:52–53; compare 2:4–8). Both mothers celebrate a God who scatters the proud and lifts the humble, and both sing because of a miraculously given son. Zacharias takes Hannah’s last line and names it: God “has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of His servant David” (Luke 1:69). Hannah rejoiced in “Your salvation,” yeshu’ah (v. 1), and the child Mary carried was named Yeshua. Hannah’s song describes the reversal; Mary’s song names the Person who brings it. Hannah’s horn is exalted in Samuel; the world’s horn of salvation is raised in Jesus. The lowly mother lifted by God in Shiloh points to the lowly Son lifted by God from the grave (v. 6; Philippians 2:8–9), and everything Hannah praised finds its center in Him.

1 Samuel 2:12–26 — Eli’s Wicked Sons

Reflections

[Insight] The Narrator’s Alternating Camera (1 Samuel 2:11–26). The section cuts back and forth like a cinematic montage: the sons’ greed (vv. 12–17); “But Samuel” (v. 18); Hannah’s robe and Eli’s blessing (vv. 19–21); the sons’ immorality and Eli’s weak rebuke (vv. 22–25); “And the child Samuel grew” (v. 26). The two families are woven together so that each sets off the other. The sons grew on in sin while the child grew on in favor; God loves to watch His lilies grow among thorns. On one side the established religious machinery is rotting from the inside, marked by greed, sexual immorality, and cynical profiteering from the things of God; on the other a quiet, obscure boy in a linen ephod is growing in favor with God and people. Light and darkness grow up side by side in the same house, and God is watching both. This juxtaposition shows that spiritual environment does not dictate spiritual destiny. Hophni and Phinehas had every advantage and became corrupt; Samuel was placed in a toxic, backslidden environment and grew in holiness. That is a word of hope for parents, and for believers in compromised churches and cultures: the atmosphere does not have to decide the outcome. Whenever there are men like Eli’s sons, God raises up someone like Samuel. God is never left without a witness. He does not wait for corruption to end before He raises its replacement; He grows the answer in the same courtyard, often in a child no one is watching.

[Insight] The Rotting Institution and the Hidden Seed (1 Samuel 2:12–26). God does not always tear down a corrupt religious structure with a lightning bolt. Often His judgment on a rotting institution is simply to let it die under the weight of its own compromise, while He quietly, almost unnoticed, grows a pure alternative seed right in its shadow. Extortion at the altar and immorality at the door are followed again and again by the quiet refrain: “But Samuel ministered before the LORD.” God’s answer to deep systemic corruption is a devoted child wearing a linen ephod.

[Insight] Two Kinds of Young Men at Shiloh (1 Samuel 2:11, 13, 15, 17, 18, 21, 26). The same Hebrew word, na’ar, “boy, young man, servant,” describes the priest’s servant with the fork (vv. 13, 15), the “young men” whose sin was very great (v. 17), and Samuel, “the child” who ministered (vv. 11, 18, 21, 26). Same age group, same courtyard, same sanctuary, opposite service. One young servant came to take from worshipers; the other came to serve the LORD. Young people around ministry are always being formed by what they see. Leaders should ask which kind of na’ar their example is producing, and young believers should know that their surroundings do not decide which one they become.

[Application] Eli Raised Another Woman’s Son to Faithfulness and Lost His Own (1 Samuel 2:11–26; 3:9–10). Eli supervised Samuel’s service (v. 11), blessed his parents (v. 20), and later taught him how to answer God’s call (3:9). He could not have been a wholly bad father, since he helped raise Samuel to godliness. Yet his own sons “did not know the LORD” (v. 12). Eli’s failure was not an inability to teach; it was an unwillingness to confront those closest to him (v. 29). Grace does not run in the blood: God gave it to the child of an obscure country Levite and not to the sons of the high priest. Leaders can be effective with other people’s children and blind at home. The test of a spiritual father is not only whom he mentors but whom he is willing to correct.

[Application] Correction That Came Too Late and Too Soft (1 Samuel 1:14; 2:22–25; 3:13). Eli was quick and severe with Hannah, accusing a praying woman of drunkenness (1:14), and slow and mild with his own sons. He seems to have spoken only after the immorality became public (v. 22), not when the greed began (vv. 13–17); had he rebuked them for their greed, the rest might have been prevented. His words were a question (v. 23), an appeal to reputation (v. 24), and a theological observation (v. 25), with no action. God later names the root: “his sons made themselves vile, and he did not restrain them” (3:13). The Lord’s rebuke of Eli was as hard as Eli’s rebuke of his sons had been soft. Parents and pastors who correct early, clearly, and with action are not being unkind; they are being merciful before mercy runs out.

[Christ & Life] The Table Where God Shared a Meal With His People (1 Samuel 2:13–17; Leviticus 7:11–21; Deuteronomy 12:7). The peace offering was the one sacrifice that became a meal. The fat went up to God, the priest received his assigned share, and the worshiper’s family ate the rest “before the LORD your God, and you shall rejoice” (Deuteronomy 12:7). The worshiper’s portion was a token of communion and fellowship with God. Hophni and Phinehas plunged their fork into that fellowship meal and seized God’s portion before Him. The result was that “men abhorred the offering of the LORD” (v. 17): people lost the joy of eating in God’s presence. God has always wanted to dwell and dine with His people; Jesus still says, “I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me” (Revelation 3:20). The gravest damage of corrupt ministry is not only stolen meat but a spoiled table, where people stop expecting to meet God.

[Application] Appetite Tells You Who Your God Is (1 Samuel 2:12–17, 22). The priests wanted meat, then the fat, then women (v. 22), a steady escalation of appetite. Paul describes such people as those “whose god is their belly” (Philippians 3:19). Sin is preferring lesser pleasures to God, and Hophni and Phinehas are a portrait of desire gone wrong: they would rather have a roast than the Lord. The cure for disordered appetite is not mere restraint but a greater delight.

[Application] The Anatomy of a Dying Ministry (1 Samuel 2:12–25). This section exposes the warning signs of a spiritual house about to collapse. First, the loss of the knowledge of God (v. 12). Second, personal appetite raised above God’s requirements (v. 15). Third, manipulation and force used to get one’s way (v. 16). Fourth, toleration of gross immorality (v. 22). Finally, the rationalization and passive management of sin by the senior leader (v. 23). When these line up, the lampstand of that ministry is about to be removed (Revelation 2:5).

[Christ & Life] “Who Will Intercede?” Is Answered in the Next Verse (1 Samuel 2:25–26; 7:5–9; 12:23). Eli asks, “If a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?” (v. 25). The very next sentence is, “And the child Samuel grew” (v. 26). Samuel becomes Israel’s great intercessor. At Mizpah he “cried out to the LORD for Israel, and the LORD answered him” (7:9). At Saul’s coronation he says, “far be it from me that I should sin against the LORD in ceasing to pray for you” (12:23), using the same “far be it” God spoke against Eli’s house (2:30). Psalm 99:6 lists Samuel “among those who called upon His name,” and God names Moses and Samuel as Israel’s great intercessors (Jeremiah 15:1). Eli’s question had an answer growing in his own courtyard, and that answer points beyond itself to the One who “always lives to make intercession” (Hebrews 7:25). God answers the hopeless questions of one generation with the people He is raising in the next.

[Application] Leaders Who Take and a Mother Who Gives (1 Samuel 2:13–16, 19–20; 8:9–17; 12:3–4). This section is full of taking: the priest “would take for himself” (v. 14); “you may take,” “I will take it by force” (v. 16). Against that stands Hannah, who brings a robe “year by year” (v. 19) and has given her son as “the loan … given to the LORD” (v. 20). The thread runs through the book. The priests’ “custom” (mishpat, v. 13) is echoed by “the behavior (mishpat) of the king,” which Samuel describes with a string of “he will take” (8:9–17). The corruption of leadership in Israel starts at the altar before it reaches the throne. And at the end of his ministry Samuel asks Israel, “Whose ox have I taken?” and they answer, “You have not … taken anything from any man’s hand” (12:3–4). The book measures every leader by whether he takes or gives. The question for every leader in God’s house is the same: are people poorer or richer for my ministry?

[Seed] The Robe and the Fork (1 Samuel 2:13–14, 19). Two objects capture this section: the priests’ three-pronged fork, stabbing into worshipers’ offerings to take what was not theirs, and Hannah’s little robe, carried each year to clothe what she had given away. One pictures religion that takes; the other pictures love that gives. The contrast is a quiet sermon on the difference between consuming ministry and self-giving devotion.

1 Samuel 2:27–36 — A Prophecy Against the House of Eli

Reflections

[Christ & Life] The Transition of Covenants (1 Samuel 2:27–36). This passage marks a monumental shift in redemptive history. God had promised that the house of Aaron, and Eli’s branch of it, would walk before Him forever (v. 30). Yet here God says, “Far be it from Me.” The Levitical priesthood required covenant faithfulness, and God would not allow His holiness to be endlessly mocked; He removed the lampstand from Eli’s line because it honored men above God. But God’s ultimate purpose cannot be thwarted by human failure. In the middle of devastating judgment He plants a seed of messianic hope: “I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest.” Israel’s access to God would not finally depend on the flawed sons of Aaron, but on a priest God Himself provides, who acts entirely according to His heart. The judgment on Eli’s house creates the vacuum that calls for the faithful Priest (v. 35). The failure of the Old Covenant mediators becomes the very stage for the New Covenant Mediator.

[Foundational Truth] The Idolatry of Permissive Love (1 Samuel 2:27–36). The man of God strikes at the root of Eli’s failure: “Why do you … honor your sons more than Me?” Eli was not an actively malicious man; he was a passive one. He rebuked his sons with words, but he did not remove them from office. He allowed the desecration of God’s glory in order to preserve his family’s comfort. This section reveals that passivity in leadership, especially tolerating sin out of misplaced family loyalty or fear of conflict, is not viewed by God as mere weakness. It is indicted as high treason and idolatry. Raising human relationships above the holiness of God brings the collapse of spiritual authority.

[Seed] The Tragedy of Misplaced Loyalty (1 Samuel 2:29–36). The whole indictment pivots on one devastating accusation: Eli honored his sons more than God. In trying to save his sons from discipline, he lost his sons entirely; they would die in one day. In trying to preserve his family’s comfort at God’s expense, he condemned his family to poverty and early death (vv. 33, 36). The spiritual paradox is clear: when we put family before God, we end up destroying the family. When we put God before family, we place our family under the covering of His grace, as Hannah did.

[Christ & Life] The Faithful Priest Who Walks Before the Anointed, and Is the Anointed (1 Samuel 2:10, 35; Zechariah 6:13; Hebrews 2:17; 3:1–6; 7:23–28). The chapter ends with two figures: “a faithful priest” and “My anointed” (v. 35), echoing “His king … His anointed” (v. 10). In Israel’s history they were separate. Samuel walked before Saul and David; Zadok walked before Solomon and anointed him with “a horn of oil from the tabernacle” (1 Kings 1:39). Some object that the faithful priest cannot be Christ, because he walks before the anointed. But the prophets foresaw the two offices joined: “He shall be a priest on His throne” (Zechariah 6:13), “a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4). Only Jesus perfectly does all that is in the Father’s heart (John 8:29). He is “a merciful and faithful High Priest” (Hebrews 2:17), “faithful … as a Son over His own house, whose house we are” (Hebrews 3:6), who “always lives to make intercession” (Hebrews 7:25). The faithful Priest and the Anointed King of 1 Samuel 2 meet in one Person, and He is building the sure house that Eli’s line could not keep.

[Foundational Truth] Inherited Office Is Not a Permanent Pass (1 Samuel 2:28, 30, 35; 13:13–14; 15:28). The house of Aaron had been chosen, given an ephod, and promised to “walk before Me forever” (vv. 28, 30), yet God said, “Far be it from Me.” The same pattern follows Saul: “now your kingdom shall not continue. The LORD has sought for Himself a man after His own heart” (13:14), and “The LORD has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today” (15:28). Priest and king are both measured by God’s heart (2:35; 13:14), not by title or lineage, and in 1 Samuel God forms and removes leaders by that standard. Office is nothing without character. The heaviest scrutiny falls on those with the greatest privilege, and priestly office, spiritual heritage, and years in ministry are no shelter. Every believer in leadership holds a calling that is a stewardship, not a possession.

[Foundational Truth] God’s House Never Falls for Want of Hands (1 Samuel 2:32, 35; Psalm 78:60, 68–70; Jeremiah 7:12). The judgment on Eli’s house is severe, and Shiloh itself would be abandoned: “He forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh, the tent He had placed among men” (Psalm 78:60). Yet the same psalm goes on: God “chose the tribe of Judah, Mount Zion which He loved,” and “He also chose David His servant” (Psalm 78:68–70). God’s work never falls to the ground for want of hands to carry it on. The chapter that announces the fall of a priestly house also announces a faithful priest and a sure house (v. 35). God’s purposes are never hostage to failing institutions; even as Shiloh decays, He is raising a boy in a linen ephod. When His house is corrupted, He is already preparing what comes next. His purpose to dwell with His people survives the failure of those who served His dwelling. It moved from Shiloh to Zion, from tabernacle to temple, and finally to Christ and His people, “whose house we are” (Hebrews 3:6).

Chapter-Wide Reflections

Reflections

[Discovery] Hannah’s Song Was Sung Over Eli’s House (1 Samuel 2:3–10, 16, 25, 29–36). The reversals Hannah sang (vv. 3–9) are acted out on Eli’s family before the chapter ends. Proud talk is silenced (v. 3) as the sons are left without an intercessor (v. 25). “By strength no man shall prevail” (v. 9) stands against “I will take it by force” (v. 16). “The LORD kills” (v. 6) becomes “in one day they shall die” (v. 34). “He brings low” (v. 7) becomes “they shall be lightly esteemed” (v. 30). “Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread” (v. 5) becomes Eli’s descendants begging “for a morsel of bread” (v. 36). “He lifts the beggar from the ash heap” (v. 8) is happening in the boy at Shiloh (vv. 18, 26), and “His anointed” (v. 10) appears again in verse 35. Hannah’s praise was not sentimental; it was prophecy about her own city. The Scriptures we sing in worship describe what God is actually doing around us.

[Discovery] The Song of Moses Runs Under the Whole Chapter (1 Samuel 2:2, 6, 29; Deuteronomy 32:4, 15, 39). Both halves of the chapter draw from Deuteronomy 32. Hannah takes her comfort from it: God the Rock (Deuteronomy 32:4, 15, 18, 30–31; 1 Samuel 2:2) and “I kill and I make alive” (Deuteronomy 32:39; 1 Samuel 2:6). The man of God takes his charge from it: “Jeshurun grew fat and kicked” (Deuteronomy 32:15), the only other place the verb “kick” appears (1 Samuel 2:29). And the same verse in Deuteronomy says Jeshurun “scornfully esteemed the Rock of his salvation,” the very Rock Hannah praised. One Scripture lifts the broken and indicts the comfortable. The Word is not divided; our hearts decide whether we hear its comfort or its warning.

[Discovery] The Weight of Glory (1 Samuel 2:3, 8, 12, 29–30; 4:18, 21). A thread of “weight” runs beneath the whole chapter. The Hebrew idea of honor and glory (kabod) is literally heaviness. God is the One who weighs actions (v. 3). He gives the beggar a “throne of glory,” a seat of weight (v. 8). Eli’s sons are “worthless” (v. 12), men without profit or substance, spiritually hollow. Eli gave more weight (kabed) to his sons than to God (v. 29), letting them, and himself, grow heavy on the fat of the sacrifices. And God declares the law of spiritual gravity: those who give Him weight He will give weight, and those who despise Him He will make light (qalal) (v. 30). Eli ends up “heavy” when he falls from his seat (4:18), and his grandson is named Ichabod, “the glory (kabod) has departed” (4:21). The chapter sets the heavy, eternal reality of God’s kingdom against the hollow, lightweight reality of fleshly religious striving. The story is finally about what a person gives weight to. When the priesthood made God light, the glory itself departed. What we load with weight in our lives, God weighs, and the glory we take for ourselves will not stay with us.

[Foundational Truth] The Pervasive Weighing of God (1 Samuel 2:1–36). “By Him actions are weighed” (v. 3) is the interpretive key to the chapter; we watch God’s scales working in real time. Peninnah’s pride is weighed and found wanting. Hannah’s brokenness is weighed and rewarded. The ritual activity of Hophni and Phinehas is weighed and judged profane. Eli’s passive parenting is weighed and judged as idolatry. Samuel’s quiet, unseen ministry in a linen ephod is weighed and met with divine favor. The heaviest scrutiny falls on those with the greatest privilege (vv. 27–29), and the leader who turns the flock away from worship carries grave responsibility. God does not look at the outward bulk of a life, a ministry, or a sacrifice; He looks at the true spiritual weight of the heart behind it.

[Foundational Truth] Holiness That Stoops, and Holiness That Will Not Be Handled Lightly (1 Samuel 2:2, 8, 13–17, 29–30). “No one is holy like the LORD” (v. 2). In one chapter the same holy God lifts a weeping woman from the dust and seats her among princes (v. 8), and refuses to let His altar be treated as a meat market (vv. 13–17, 29). Mercy to Hannah and judgment on Eli’s house are not two moods in God, nor competing impulses; they are one holy character meeting two different hearts, a heart that came empty and a heart that came full. His holiness is the reason He can be trusted by the poor and the reason He cannot be used by the comfortable. The God who stoops to the ash heap will not be handled lightly at His own table.

[Christ & Life] The Messianic Bookends: Clearing the Stage for the Anointed (1 Samuel 2:10, 35). First Samuel 2 is extraordinarily prophetic. It opens with Hannah prophesying the “Anointed” King (v. 10) and closes with God promising a faithful priest who will walk before His “Anointed” forever (v. 35). Everything in between is the systematic failure and announced destruction of the current religious order. The pattern is clear: the failure of human systems is never the end of God’s redemptive plan; it is the clearing of the stage. The collapse of the priesthood’s integrity is the necessary backdrop that reveals our desperate need for a better Priest and a better King. Jesus is the Anointed King who breaks His adversaries to pieces (v. 10) and the merciful and faithful High Priest who perfectly does the Father’s will (v. 35). The whole story of Eli’s failure and Samuel’s rise is a shadow play pointing to Christ, who replaces the failing systems of men with His eternal, indestructible life.

[Insight] From Natural Succession to Spiritual Anointing (1 Samuel 2:1–36). This chapter disrupts the ancient assumption of biological succession in spiritual things. Under the old order, priests were born, not called. Hophni and Phinehas were the biological heirs to the highest spiritual office in Israel, yet God rejects them and calls a boy from the hill country of Ephraim, not of Aaron’s line, to stand in the gap. The theme anticipates the New Testament: “it is not the children of the flesh who are the children of God, but the children of the promise” (Romans 9:8). Spiritual authority is born of obedience and anointing, not pedigree.

[Seed] The Weaponization of Intercession (1 Samuel 1–2; 3:1). History is shaped not by the people in institutional power but by those in hidden intercession. Eli, the high priest, sits on a seat by the door, powerless to stop the moral decay of the nation or his own family. But a marginalized, weeping woman in chapter 1 prays a prayer that shifts the destiny of the nation in chapter 2. Samuel arises in a day when “the word of the LORD was rare” (3:1), and he arises out of a barren woman’s prayer; God often births fresh prophetic ministry through desperate, persistent prayer in spiritually dry eras. Hannah’s intercession brings forth the prophet who will anoint David, who will establish the line of Christ. One surrendered, praying believer can carry more weight in the divine economy than an entire institution of compromised leaders.

[Insight] The Theology of the Outsider, the Blindness of the Insider (1 Samuel 1:13; 2:1–10, 22–25). A barren, grieving woman with no official standing sings the most profound theology in the chapter, including the first prophecy of God’s anointed King. Meanwhile the high priest, who once mistook her prayer for drunkenness (1:13), cannot discern or discipline his own household. God often gives revelation to the humble and hides it from those who assume their position guarantees insight.

[Insight] Eli’s Tragedy: Sound Words, Soft Will (1 Samuel 2:20, 23–25; 3:18). Eli is not a villain. He blesses Hannah graciously (v. 20), speaks orthodox theology (v. 25), and later receives judgment with humility (3:18). Yet he failed at the one point that mattered most: acting on what he knew. His story warns that correct doctrine and personal kindness cannot replace courageous obedience, especially at home.

[Christ & Life] The Good Shepherd and Those Who Eat the Fat (1 Samuel 2:13–17, 29; Ezekiel 34:3, 23; John 10:11). Ezekiel later indicts Israel’s leaders in words that sound like Hophni and Phinehas: “You eat the fat … but you do not feed the flock” (Ezekiel 34:3). God answers by promising to shepherd His people Himself through His servant David (Ezekiel 34:23). Jesus fulfills this as the Good Shepherd, who does not take the sheep’s life but lays down His own for them (John 10:11). Where Eli’s sons took the offering by force, Jesus became the offering.

[Discovery] From Song to Sentence to Promise (1 Samuel 2:1–36). First Samuel 2 is a large-scale revelation of the move from the chaos of the Judges to the establishment of the kingdom, a prophetic blueprint for the gospel. The chapter moves from God’s sovereignty (Hannah’s song), to human failure (Eli’s sons), to the divine promise of a messianic solution (the faithful priest). Its overarching theme is the futility of fleshly power and the necessity of divine intervention. Eli’s house represents the old order: failing, corrupt, and dying. Samuel is the transitional prophet, but the chapter’s hope is thrust forward to Jesus Christ. He is the exalted Horn of Hannah’s song, the true Boy growing in favor in Luke 2, and the faithful Priest of the prophet’s declaration.
