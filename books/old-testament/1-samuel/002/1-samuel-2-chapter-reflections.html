Chapter Reflections

1 Samuel 2

Reflect and Respond
1 Samuel 2:1–11 — Hannah’s Prayer of Thanksgiving

Reflections

1 Samuel 2:1

[Insight] She Praised the Giver, Not the Gift. “My heart rejoices in the LORD; my horn is exalted in the LORD.” Hannah sang this on the day she left her only son at Shiloh (1:28), yet the song never mentions Samuel. She does not say, “My heart rejoices in Samuel”; she overlooks the gift and praises the Giver. The verb for “rejoices” appears only eight times in the Old Testament, usually for the exultant joy of those God has made triumphant (Psalm 5:11; 9:2; 68:3). When God grants a long-awaited breakthrough, mature joy passes through the gift and rests in the Giver. To rejoice in the blessing alone is to quietly idolize it; to rejoice in the Lord is to keep the blessing in its proper place. Joy that rests in a gift leaves when the gift is given away. Joy that rests in the Lord can sing on the walk home. If one of His gifts were placed back in His hands, would we still be able to sing?

[Insight] The Horn of Power Rooted in Surrender. “My horn is exalted.” The horn pictures a wild ox lifting its head in strength; to have one’s horn raised is to be publicly vindicated, strength restored and honor visible (Deuteronomy 33:17; Psalm 92:10). Hannah’s dignity, trampled for years by Peninnah, was restored entirely by God. Grace is the great vindicator of the oppressed. And notice when her horn was lifted: not while she was striving with her rival, not while she was grasping for a child, but after she had surrendered her long-awaited son back to God. Here is a kingdom paradox: spiritual authority and unassailable strength are not secured by getting what we want, but by offering our most treasured desires back to heaven.

[Application] Let God Lift Your Horn. “My mouth is enlarged over my enemies, because I rejoice in Your salvation.” Literally, her mouth is opened wide, a gesture of vindication. Yet Hannah names no rival and settles no score; she never mentions Peninnah. Her answer to her antagonist is not petty retaliation but a magnificent declaration of God’s saving power. Real victory over human opposition is not found in defending ourselves but in magnifying God so loudly that the enemy’s taunts are drowned out by worship. The believer who waits for God’s vindication is freed from the exhausting work of defending himself. Where are we still trying to lift our own horn, defending our reputation or settling scores, instead of trusting God to vindicate us in His time?

[Christ & Life] Rejoice in a Salvation That Makes Room. “I rejoice in Your salvation.” The word is yeshu’ah, from a root whose core idea is to be wide or spacious, to bring someone out of a tight place into room. Barrenness had been Hannah’s narrow place; God’s deliverance gave her breathing space. The same root forms the names Joshua and Yeshua, Jesus. Every rescue God gives His people is a small picture of the great, spacious salvation He would one day give in His Son. And Christ Himself is our exalted horn: when we are weak, union with Him gives an unbreakable authority against the enemy.

[Seed] Back to the Spot Where She Wept. “And Hannah prayed.” It is the same verb used of her prayer in 1:10, but everything else has changed. Then her voice was not heard and only her lips moved (1:13); now she speaks aloud for anyone to hear. Then she prayed in bitterness of soul; now her mouth is opened wide. The woman who poured out her soul in bitterness now pours out praise in the same sanctuary. “Some day you will come to the spot where you wept and prayed,” wrote one old devotional writer, bringing praise instead. God often brings His people back to the place of their tears so that the same place can hear a new song. Is there such a place God may be bringing us back to?

[Insight] Prayer and Praise, One Language. Hannah “prayed,” yet what follows is a song. In the Spirit, deep intercession and high praise are the same language spoken from one heart. The same God who heard her sighs now receives her songs.

1 Samuel 2:2

[Foundational Truth] No Rock Like Ours. “No one is holy like the LORD, for there is none besides You, nor is there any rock like our God.” “Rock” (tsur) is first used as a name for God in the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32:4, 15, 18, 30–31), where it speaks of His changeless faithfulness, and Israelites carried it in their names. Hannah had leaned on a loving husband’s double portion and his “Am I not better to you than ten sons?” (1:5, 8). Now, holding the son she had begged for, she does not call Samuel her rock either. Even God’s best gifts cannot carry the weight of our lives. Build your emotional and spiritual stability on the unchangeable character of God, not on shifting circumstances. Paul says of the rock that followed Israel, “that Rock was Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4), the Rock struck for us in the wilderness, and He is the only ground that does not move. What are we leaning on as a rock that is really a gift and not the Giver?

[Foundational Truth] Holiness That Makes Him a Foundation. Hannah joins two attributes: His holiness, utterly separate and unlike any created thing, and His solidity, a Rock. Holiness is not one attribute among many; it is the overarching reality of His being, entirely “other.” And here is the profound truth: God’s utter separateness from us is precisely what makes Him a dependable foundation for us. Because He is not like anything else, He does not shift like anything else.

[Application] Praise Who God Is Before What God Gave. Hannah’s first confession is not “He gave me a son” but “There is none holy like the LORD.” God’s best gift is God Himself. Worship that only celebrates benefits quietly makes God a means rather than the end. Hannah had a child in her arms, yet her song is filled with God.

[Insight] A Rock That Is Also a Spring. An old rabbinic reading took “rock” applied to God as “source” or “spring,” a rock that is also a fountain, like the rock that gave water in the wilderness (Exodus 17:6). The Rock that holds us up is the Rock that pours life out.

1 Samuel 2:3

[Foundational Truth] The Proud Count; God Weighs. “For the LORD is the God of knowledge, and by Him actions are weighed.” “God of knowledge” is plural in Hebrew, “God of all knowings,” an intensive form for complete, exhaustive knowledge. “Weighed” means to measure against a standard, the word Proverbs uses when “the LORD weighs the hearts” (Proverbs 16:2; 21:2; 24:12). In Elkanah’s house everything had been counted: Peninnah’s children, the portions on the plate, the years of barrenness (1:4–7). God does not count; He weighs. He does not measure what we bring; He weighs it. Two actions can look identical on the outside and carry completely different spiritual weight. Human beings weigh the outcome of actions; God weighs the motives behind them. Loud words can be very light, and quiet faithfulness can be very heavy, on the scales that matter. Where do we count what is visible while God is weighing what is hidden?

[Insight] Busy and Weightless. A massive volume of religious activity can weigh nothing at all on the scales of heaven if it lacks the substance of love and reverence. Eli’s sons were performing the outward mechanics of the priesthood, yet their actions had no spiritual mass; they are called “worthless,” men of Belial, “without profit,” without weight (v. 12). True spiritual substance is determined by the hidden motive, the reverence, and the alignment with God’s heart that fill the action.

[Application] “Talk No More”. “Talk no more so very proudly; let no arrogance come from your mouth.” Hannah had lived for years under a rival’s tongue (1:6–7). Now that God has answered, she does not answer Peninnah by name, mock her, or recount her cruelty. She simply tells pride to stop talking, because the God of knowledge has already heard everything. Peninnah’s taunts are silenced not by Hannah’s retaliation but by the scales of divine justice. Vindication from God does not need to be followed by revenge from us; God’s scales are perfectly calibrated. When God has spoken for us, we can stop speaking against others. Is there proud or careless speech in our lives that we would be ashamed to have placed on those scales?

[Seed] Pride Is a Failure to Know Who Knows. Hannah ties human arrogance directly to human ignorance. Pride is at root a failure to realize that God sees and knows everything. When the reality of His knowledge dawns on a soul, the mouth naturally shuts. Every proud word, Peninnah’s or ours, is placed on God’s scales, and none escapes the final audit.

[Insight] Old Sayings Silenced. The word for “arrogance” may mean hard or worn-out sayings, perhaps the stock insults heaped on barren women. Through the books of Samuel, God humbles the proud one after another: Peninnah, Eli’s sons, the Philistines, Goliath, Saul, Nabal, Absalom. Hannah’s line is the first notice of a pattern the whole story will keep.

1 Samuel 2:4

[Insight] Strength You Put On. “The bows of the mighty men are broken, and those who stumbled are girded with strength.” “Girded” means to tie on, like a belt: strength here is not grown from within but fastened on from outside. The verb “broken” is used for the breaking of people, not just objects; the warriors are shattered inwardly, not merely disarmed. Every power determined to be something in itself is broken, and every weakness that despairs of itself is turned into power. “My strength is made perfect in weakness” (2 Corinthians 12:9). The stumbling believer is not disqualified; he is the one God dresses for battle.

[Insight] The Great Reversal of Human Strength. Here begins one of Scripture’s central motifs: God loves to dismantle human self-sufficiency while empowering human weakness. He breaks the bow of the strong because they rely on it; He girds the stumbler because the stumbler relies on Him. Natural strength must be broken before spiritual strength can be bound on, for God cannot use our self-sufficiency. Hannah moves here from a domestic dispute to military imagery, and the reversal she sings will run through Eli’s fall and Samuel’s rise, Saul’s fall and David’s rise. God delights to use the overlooked and the sidelined. Verses 4–7 hold seven such contrasts: mighty and stumbling, full and hungry, barren and fruitful, killing and making alive, bringing down and bringing up, poor and rich, low and lifted.

[Christ & Life] The Bow Broken at the Cross. At the cross the ultimate “bow of the mighty,” the power of Satan, was broken, and feeble humanity, through faith, was girded with resurrection life (Colossians 2:15).

1 Samuel 2:5

[Seed] The Barren Has Borne Seven. “Even the barren has borne seven, and she who has many children has become feeble.” When Hannah sang this she had one son; she would eventually have six children in all (v. 21). Seven is the number of full blessing and divine completion (Ruth 4:15), so she is not counting; she is confessing what God does. Her faith spoke of God’s fullness before she could see it in her own home, and in God’s eyes she was already complete. Her song reached beyond her own life to the fruitfulness of every spiritually barren soul who trusts God, a hope sung again in Psalm 113:9 and Isaiah 54:1, applied to the church in Galatians 4:27, and echoed in Mary’s Magnificat. The same God who fills the empty can empty the full; Jeremiah later turns the picture to judgment: “She who has borne seven has languished” (Jeremiah 15:9). Faith learns to speak of His fullness before the house is full.

[Application] Never Trust in Abundance. “Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread, and the hungry have ceased to hunger.” The well-fed are reduced to day labor. God delights in overturning human systems of security. Never place ultimate trust in material abundance; it is entirely subject to divine sovereignty. This very line will be fulfilled before the chapter ends, when Eli’s well-fed house begs for bread (v. 36). (An old Jewish legend said that for every child Hannah bore, Peninnah buried two; that is legend, not text.)

1 Samuel 2:6

[Christ & Life] The God Who Brings Up From Sheol. “The LORD kills and makes alive; He brings down to the grave and brings up.” Hannah is quoting the Song of Moses: “I kill and I make alive; I wound and I heal” (Deuteronomy 32:39). Her womb had been a kind of grave, and God had brought life out of it. Long before the resurrection was fully revealed, the Spirit moved a praying mother to declare God’s power over the grave, a striking flash of resurrection faith before any resurrection had been recorded. The full answer stands at the end of the story: the Son was brought down to death and brought up again (Psalm 16:10; Acts 2:27–32), securing eternal life for all who are united with Him, and He says, “I have the keys of Hades and of Death” (Revelation 1:18). The God of Hannah’s song is the God of the empty tomb. No grave is final when the God of Hannah holds it.

[Foundational Truth] The Descent Is in His Hand Too. No second cause is final; life and death are finally in God’s hand alone. For the believer walking through a “down” season, the comfort is precise: the descent is within His hand too, and the God who brings down is the God who brings up.

1 Samuel 2:7

[Foundational Truth] Both Seasons Come From One Hand. “The LORD makes poor and makes rich; He brings low and lifts up.” Hannah had been told twice that “the LORD had closed her womb” (1:5–6). She does not credit her low years to Peninnah, Elkanah, or fate, nor her lifting to her own persistence. Both seasons came from the same hand. Wealth, poverty, promotion, and demotion are under God’s purposeful administration. This verse overturns both the prosperity mindset (wealth proves God’s favor) and the self-made mindset (wealth proves my effort); it destroys the illusion of the self-made person and comforts the downtrodden. Station and circumstance are assignments, not verdicts on our worth. Perhaps God makes some poor because they could not bear riches without ruin. A believer who sees both the valley and the lifting as coming from his Father’s hand can be humble when lifted and hopeful when brought low.

[Application] The Curriculum of the Present Season. Accept your present station, abundance or lack, as God’s current curriculum for your soul. God allows seasons of plenty and seasons of want for His purposes; our part is to obey Him and leave the consequences to Him. True spiritual wealth often requires the stripping away of riches we made ourselves.

1 Samuel 2:8

[Christ & Life] From the Ash Heap to the Throne of Glory. “He raises the poor from the dust and lifts the beggar from the ash heap, to set them among princes and make them inherit the throne of glory.” The ash heap was the town’s refuse pile, where outcasts sat and begged, the place Job ended up (Job 2:8; Lamentations 4:5). “Throne of glory” is kisse’ kabod, literally a “seat of weight.” Eli sat on his seat (kisse’) and would fall from it because he was “heavy” (kabed, 4:18); Hannah sings that God hands the seat of weight to beggars. God does not merely improve a beggar’s condition in the ash heap; He changes his citizenship and seats him with royalty. God bypasses the self-righteous and goes to the ash heap of humanity to find His kings and priests. That is the exact path of a sinner saved by grace: raised from the dirt of spiritual death and made to “sit together in the heavenly places in Christ Jesus” (Ephesians 2:6). God’s honor does not go to those who sit heavily in religious places; it goes to those He lifts from the dust.

[Discovery] The Transfer of the Ash Heap. How does God lift us from the ash heap? Read through the lens of Christ, the answer is staggering. Jesus, the Prince of heaven, voluntarily left His seat of honor and came down to sit on the ash heap of humanity (Philippians 2:6–8): born in a borrowed stable, crucified outside the city. The great reversal of Hannah’s song is possible only because of the great exchange of the cross. We get the seat of honor because Christ took the ash heap.

[Seed] God Does Not Reform the Ash Heap. A powerful evangelistic text: God does not reform the ash heap; He removes the beggar from it and seats him with royalty. His grace reaches the lowest point of society to lift to the highest point of heaven.

[Insight] Inherited, Not Earned. “And make them inherit the throne of glory.” The beggar does not climb to the throne or buy his seat; he inherits it, the way a child receives an estate. An inheritance belongs to children and comes as a father’s gift. Every verb in the verse is God’s: He raises, lifts, sets, and makes them inherit. The honor of God’s people is not a promotion we work toward but an inheritance we receive as sons and daughters (Romans 8:17; Revelation 3:21).

[Seed] The Pillars of the Earth. “For the pillars of the earth are the LORD’s, and He has set the world upon them.” The line grounds God’s right to reverse human fortunes in His ownership of the world itself. The God who holds up the foundations of the earth is more than able to manage the affairs of one barren woman’s household, and to lift one beggar. (The first lines of the verse return almost word for word in Psalm 113:7–8, and Mary echoes the reversal in Luke 1:52.)

1 Samuel 2:9

[Foundational Truth] He Guards the Feet. “He will guard the feet of His saints.” “Guard” is the keeping word of Psalm 121, to guard as a watchman guards: “He will not allow your foot to be moved; He who keeps you will not slumber” (Psalm 121:3). “Saints” are the chasidim, those bound to Him by His covenant love (chesed). If God keeps their feet, how much more their heads and hearts. He keeps them from falling, from wandering, from wounds, from the net. The believer’s safety lies not in the firmness of his own step but in the faithfulness of the One who keeps it. In this chapter a heavy priest will one day fall from his seat (4:18), while a small boy is kept upright in a corrupt house. We are “kept by the power of God” (1 Peter 1:5), and Christ is our preserver; we do not maintain our salvation by our might but by His guarding of our steps. “Now to Him who is able to keep you from stumbling” (Jude 24) is the New Testament form of Hannah’s confidence.

[Foundational Truth] Not by Strength. “For by strength no man shall prevail.” This is one of the Old Testament’s clearest declarations of grace over works. Hannah did not overcome Peninnah by out-arguing or out-producing her; she prevailed by pouring out her soul before the LORD. Human strength, intellect, and willpower always fail in the final spiritual contest; prevailing is God’s keeping power, not human exertion. A few verses later a priest’s servant will say, “If not, I will take it by force” (v. 16); the song has already passed sentence on that method. “Not by might nor by power, but by My Spirit” (Zechariah 4:6). Stop striving in the flesh. In God’s house, force is a sign of weakness, and dependence is the real strength.

[Insight] Silent in Darkness. “The wicked shall be silent in darkness.” The wicked are struck speechless, cut off, when God withdraws the light of His grace. The mouths that talked so proudly (v. 3) end in silence.

1 Samuel 2:10

[Christ & Life] Grace Was the First to Say “His Anointed”. “He will give strength to His king, and exalt the horn of His anointed.” Israel had no king when Hannah sang, nor any human movement yet asking for one. Earlier Scripture had spoken of an anointed priest (Leviticus 4:3), but here, for the first time, mashiach, “anointed,” names a king, an exalted, conquering royal figure. The Greek Old Testament translated it “His Christ,” and even a medieval rabbi admitted that the King of whom Hannah speaks is the Messiah. Her song reaches past Samuel to David, and past David to Christ. The woman whose name means “grace” was the first to give the coming King His title. The Holy Spirit was speaking of Jesus through the lips of a praying mother, and the last word of her song is the first word of a hope that ends in Jesus the Christ.

[Seed] Suffering Became the Lens. Out of the crucible of her personal barrenness, Hannah’s spiritual vision was cracked wide open to see the ultimate King. Deep personal suffering, brought to the Lord, often becomes the very lens through which God grants revelation of His larger redemptive plan.

[Discovery] Her Son Would Carry the Horn. “And exalt the horn of His anointed.” Hannah sang of the anointed’s horn as a figure of speech. Years later her son would hear, “Fill your horn with oil,” and “Samuel took the horn of oil and anointed” David (16:1, 13). The boy she left at Shiloh became the man who poured the horn over the head of the king she had seen in her song. A mother’s Spirit-given praise became her son’s assignment. What God speaks into a praying parent’s heart can become the life’s work of the child.

[Insight] The Song Begins and Ends With a Lifted Horn. “Exalt the horn of His anointed” answers “my horn is exalted” in verse 1: the song begins with Hannah lifted and ends with the King lifted. One woman’s answered prayer becomes a window into God’s plan for the world. Zacharias calls Jesus “a horn of salvation … in the house of His servant David” (Luke 1:69). Our enemy is broken because the Anointed One’s horn has been exalted. (The thunder from heaven is fulfilled at Mizpah, 7:10, and in the harvest storm, 12:18.)

1 Samuel 2:11

[Application] Elkanah Went Home; the Boy Stayed. “Then Elkanah went to his house at Ramah. But the child ministered to the LORD before Eli the priest.” One short verse splits the family with a single “but.” The parents walk home, less than a day’s journey, to a house with one child fewer. The Greek Old Testament adds, “and she left him there before the LORD.” Having received the very thing she wept to obtain, Hannah surrendered it at once. The vow was not finished in the song but in the walk home. Real surrender includes the quiet trip back to an emptier house, and God sees that walk as clearly as He heard the song. True maturity is being as faithful to God in the fulfillment of the promise as we were in the agonizing wait for it.

[Insight] A Child’s Ministry Is Real Ministry. “The child ministered to the LORD.” The verb (sharat) is the official word for the service of priests and Levites and of Joshua’s service to Moses (Exodus 24:13); it means the willing service of a free person, not the labor of a slave, and certainly not babysitting. A small boy may have lit lamps, carried vessels, and opened doors (3:15). He served “to the LORD” and “before Eli”: by obeying the old priest he was serving God. Children in God’s house are not waiting to begin ministry later; small tasks done to the LORD are ministry now. Churches should treat children as real participants in worship.

[Foundational Truth] Ministry to the Lord Comes First. Before Samuel ever ministered to the people, he ministered to the LORD, and that order shaped his whole life. Ministry to the Lord is the highest calling, and every servant must remember that ministry begins as service to God before it is service to others. In the same verse the narrative sets the purity of the child against the corruption about to be exposed.

Section Reflections

[Insight] One Answered Prayer Taught Hannah How God Runs the World (1 Samuel 2:1–10). The song begins with “my heart,” “my horn,” “my mouth” (v. 1) and ends with “the ends of the earth,” “His king,” and “His anointed” (v. 10). Hannah starts with her personal deliverance (v. 1), turns to the character of God (vv. 2–3), widens to His dealings with all humanity, the proud and the poor, the living and the dead (vv. 4–8), and crescendos in prophetic revelation about the ends of the earth and the coming Messiah (vv. 9–10). Very quickly the song loses sight of Samuel altogether. She discerned in her own experience the general laws of God’s dealings with the world. Her prayer in 1:11 asked for one son; her praise in 2:1–10 sees a kingdom. This is the anatomy of mature worship: it uses the personal miracle as a launchpad to declare the universal majesty and eternal purposes of God. When a believer’s personal agony is resolved in God’s presence, the praise that follows breaks the boundaries of that individual life; true gratitude catapults the soul into prophetic alignment. God’s private kindness to one person is a window on how He governs history and where He is taking it. When God answers us, He is not only meeting a need; He is showing us Himself.

[Foundational Truth] The Principle of the Great Reversal (1 Samuel 2:1–11). The dominant theme of these verses is the great reversal. Bows are broken, stumblers are strengthened, the full go hungry, the barren bears seven, the poor are seated with princes. God consistently opposes the proud and exalts the humble. His kingdom runs on an inverted economy: He bypasses the proud, the resourced, and the powerful to accomplish His purposes through the broken, the empty, and the desperate. This section is the theological overture to the books of Samuel, and indeed to the whole Bible, and it introduces for the first time the “anointed” King, tying God’s final victory over darkness to a coming Messiah. The pattern plays out at once: God bypasses Eli’s powerful house for a little boy named Samuel, and later bypasses tall King Saul for a shepherd boy named David. Eli’s house falls, Samuel rises; Saul falls, David rises. History is not driven by strength, status, or institutions, but by the God who “brings low and lifts up.” We should expect His great reversals.

[Application] First the Sacrifice, Then the Song (1 Samuel 1:28; 2:1, 11). Hannah sang on the day she gave her son away (1:28; 2:11), not on the day she received him. First the sacrifice, then the song. So it was when Hezekiah restored temple worship: “when the burnt offering began, the song of the LORD also began” (2 Chronicles 29:27). Mary’s song came after her surrender: “Let it be to me according to your word” (Luke 1:38). The deepest worship in this chapter comes not from receiving but from releasing. Families who dedicate a child, ministries that hand off something precious, believers who place a dream back in God’s hands, often find that the song comes on the far side of the sacrifice, not before it.

[Discovery] A Mother’s Song and a King’s Song Frame the Books of Samuel (1 Samuel 2:1–10; 2 Samuel 22:1–51). The books of Samuel open with Hannah’s song and close, before the appendices, with David’s song, and the two share their vocabulary: horn (2:1, 10; 2 Samuel 22:3), rock (2:2; 2 Samuel 22:2–3, 32), salvation (2:1; 2 Samuel 22:3), the grave (2:6; 2 Samuel 22:6), thunder from heaven (2:10; 2 Samuel 22:14), king and anointed (2:10; 2 Samuel 22:51). Both celebrate the LORD as Rock and speak of His anointed king. David’s last line, “He is the tower of salvation to His king, and shows mercy to His anointed” (2 Samuel 22:51), answers Hannah’s last line. What God begins in a woman’s prayer He completes in a king’s praise, and everything in between, Samuel’s call, Saul’s fall, David’s rise, is the outworking of one song. It has rightly been called a golden key to the whole book.

[Christ & Life] Two Mothers’ Songs and One Horn of Salvation (1 Samuel 2:1–10; Luke 1:46–55, 68–69). Mary’s Magnificat follows Hannah’s song throughout: “He has put down the mighty from their thrones, and exalted the lowly. He has filled the hungry with good things, and the rich He has sent away empty” (Luke 1:52–53; compare 2:4–8). Both mothers celebrate a God who scatters the proud and lifts the humble, and both sing because of a miraculously given son. Zacharias takes Hannah’s last line and names it: God “has raised up a horn of salvation for us in the house of His servant David” (Luke 1:69). Hannah rejoiced in “Your salvation,” yeshu’ah (v. 1), and the child Mary carried was named Yeshua. Hannah’s song describes the reversal; Mary’s song names the Person who brings it. Hannah’s horn is exalted in Samuel; the world’s horn of salvation is raised in Jesus. The lowly mother lifted by God in Shiloh points to the lowly Son lifted by God from the grave (v. 6; Philippians 2:8–9), and everything Hannah praised finds its center in Him.

1 Samuel 2:12–26 — Eli’s Wicked Sons

Reflections

1 Samuel 2:12

[Foundational Truth] The Tragedy of Proximity Without Intimacy. “Now the sons of Eli were worthless men; they did not know the LORD.” Hophni and Phinehas grew up in the tabernacle. They were born into the priesthood, handled the holy vessels daily, breathed the incense, and ate the sacrifices. They knew all about the LORD, yet they did not know Him. “Know” (yada’) is the word of relationship and experience, not information; the Targum renders it, “did not know to fear before the LORD.” Proximity to the things of God does not equal intimacy with the Person of God. Religious familiarity without personal surrender produces the most dangerous kind of spiritual blindness. It is entirely possible to make a career of the holy while remaining spiritually dead, and spiritual pedigree and religious vocation do not equal a relationship with God. Here is a terrifying warning to all raised in the faith or serving in ministry: unregenerate people can hold professional positions in God’s house.

[Discovery] “Not” and “Not Yet”. In the next chapter the narrator says, “Now Samuel did not yet know the LORD” (3:7). The difference is one small word. The priests’ sons had handled the altar, the meat, and the incense for years and did not know Him; the boy who was only beginning to serve did not know Him yet. A man who holds a sacred office and is presumed to know the Lord, but does not, stands where blessing hardly reaches him. Nearness to holy things is not the same as knowing the Holy One, and the person who still says “not yet” is closer than the one who has stopped asking. Are we closer to “not” or to “not yet”?

[Insight] A Sentence That Implicates the Father. The Hebrew is a verbless clause: literally, “The sons of Eli, sons of Belial.” The wording can be heard as setting Eli beside Belial, a quiet hint that the father shares the guilt, which the man of God will make plain in verse 29. “Belial” means “without profit,” worthless, good for nothing, and by Paul’s day it had become a name for Satan (2 Corinthians 6:15). The Greek Old Testament calls them “pests” or “plagues,” men dangerous to everyone around them. Hannah had once begged Eli not to count her “a daughter of Belial” (1:16); the true sons of Belial were his own. Being related to a high priest is no substitute for a relationship with God. A godly family name can sit on top of a worthless life, and a father’s position cannot cover a son’s heart. “Because you have rejected knowledge, I also will reject you from being priest for Me” (Hosea 4:6).

1 Samuel 2:13

[Insight] The Custom That Replaced the Command. “The priests’ custom with the people was that when any man offered a sacrifice, the priest’s servant would come with a three-pronged fleshhook in his hand while the meat was boiling.” “Custom” is mishpat, the word for justice, judgment, and ordinance. The priests had an ordinance, and it was a fork. They wrote their own law. God had already set the priests’ share, the breast and right thigh of the peace offering, after the fat was burned (Leviticus 7:30–34). The fork was an innovation of greed, designed to grab more than God allotted. When leadership loses the fear of God, it begins to exploit the flock and builds self-serving systems to extract what belongs to God and to the people. When leaders turn their appetites into policy, people stop calling it sin and start calling it the way things are done. Every church practice needs to be measured again against the Word that first defined it. Where have our preferences quietly become “the way we’ve always done it,” serving us more than honoring God?

[Insight] Stabbing Into the Fellowship Meal. The offering in view is the peace offering, the only sacrifice in which the worshiper ate a share; his portion was boiling for his family’s meal when the servant arrived with the fork. (The word for the three-pronged fork occurs only here and in verse 14.)

1 Samuel 2:14

[Insight] Every Pot in Shiloh. “Then he would thrust it into the pan, or kettle, or caldron, or pot; and the priest would take for himself all that the fleshhook brought up. So they did in Shiloh to all the Israelites who came there.” The narrator names four different cooking vessels, as if to say that no family’s pot was safe. The priest’s share was decided by what the prongs happened to catch, not by what God had assigned. They monetized the worship of God. It was done “to all the Israelites,” rich and poor alike, and the yearly meal at which Elkanah handed portions to his family (1:4) took place in this same courtyard. Corruption at the center of worship reaches every household that comes to worship.

1 Samuel 2:15

[Foundational Truth] Served Before God Was Served. “Also, before they burned the fat, the priest’s servant would come and say, ‘Give meat for roasting to the priest, for he will not take boiled meat from you, but raw.’” The law was plain: “all the fat is the LORD’s” (Leviticus 3:16), and the priest’s portion came only after the fat had burned on the altar (Leviticus 7:30–31). The word “also” marks the worst of the crimes: this was robbery of God. They would serve themselves before God was served; they would have God wait on them rather than wait on God. The issue was not hunger but order. Whatever we take before God has received His portion, we have placed above Him. Does God receive our firstfruits, our first hours, first dollars, first affections, or whatever is left over?

[Application] The Flesh Hates the Altar’s Fire. The fat was the Lord’s portion, meant to be consumed by the fire on the altar, and the priests wanted the meat before the fire touched it. Unredeemed flesh always hates the altar’s fire. It wants the blessing, the sustenance, and the prestige without passing through the purifying flames of consecration.

[Insight] Raw, “Living” Meat. “Raw” is literally “living” meat. Roasting was specified in the law for the Passover lamb, not for the priest’s share, and raw meat let the priests pick the best cuts, perhaps even to sell them. Both practices, the fork and the raw-meat demand, stand condemned.

1 Samuel 2:16

[Insight] The Worshiper Who Corrected the Priest. “If the man said, ‘They should really burn the fat first; then you may take as much as your heart desires.’” An ordinary worshiper pleads for God’s portion while offering to give up his own. What a shame that priests needed to be reminded of their duty by the people; the worshipers were more righteous than the priests, more concerned for God’s honor than the men at the altar. Sometimes the clearest voice for God in the courtyard belongs to the person with no title. Leaders should listen when the people they serve say, “God first,” and every believer can be that voice, even when no one in charge wants to hear it.

[Application] Ministry That Bullies Is Not Ministry. “No, but you must give it now; and if not, I will take it by force.” The priest’s servant threatens a worshiper at God’s altar. Coercion had entered the house of God. Hannah had just sung, “By strength no man shall prevail” (v. 9). The law said of the peace offering, “his own hands shall bring” it (Leviticus 7:30); force tears from a worshiper’s hands what he came to give. Position and sacred office never excuse abuse, and God’s people should recognize spiritual bullying for what it is. Authority in God’s house that needs force to get its way has already lost its authority. Elders are to serve “not as being lords over those entrusted to you, but being examples to the flock” (1 Peter 5:3).

1 Samuel 2:17

[Foundational Truth] Sin That Gave Worship a Bad Name. “Therefore the sin of the young men was very great before the LORD, for men abhorred the offering of the LORD.” The verb means to treat with contempt, and it can be read as causative: the priests caused the offering to be despised. The worshipers themselves came to look down on the offerings because of what they saw. The most devastating consequence of their sin was not their own defilement but that ordinary Israelites learned to despise the things of God (compare Romans 2:24; 2 Samuel 12:14). People stop coming where their consciences have been wounded. And note “before the LORD”: what worshipers endured in the courtyard, God saw from the throne. When leaders abuse authority, they do catastrophic collateral damage to the faith of the people they were ordained to serve. A leader’s sin is measured not only by what it takes but by whom it drives away from God. Has anything in our leadership, parenting, or example made it harder for someone to love worshiping God?

[Seed] Familiarity Breeds a Deadly Contempt. Familiarity with holy things can breed a deadly, casual contempt. Manipulating the things of God to serve personal appetite invites severe judgment. The theft of meat was small compared with the contempt for worship it spread among the people.

1 Samuel 2:18

[Christ & Life] A White Flower on a Dunghill. “But Samuel ministered before the LORD, even as a child, wearing a linen ephod.” The narrator cuts from the fork in the pot to a child in a linen garment with a single “but,” and that word is a hinge. Samuel at Shiloh was like a white flower blooming on a dunghill, and Hannah had just sung that God lifts the beggar “from the ash heap” (v. 8). Amid gross sexual and financial corruption at the highest levels of spiritual leadership, God was quietly raising up a pure servant. Samuel was not removed from the corruption of Shiloh; he was kept pure inside it, his mother’s prayers around him like a wall of fire. Jesus prayed for His disciples in the same way: “not that You should take them out of the world, but that You should keep them from the evil one” (John 17:15). Samuel is a beautiful type of Christ, the faithful Son who ministers in spotless righteousness whatever the corruption around Him.

[Seed] Never Underestimate God’s “But”. While the old order was rotting, God was already clothing His answer. The plain linen ephod was a simple priestly garment, worn later by David before the ark (2 Samuel 6:14) and by the priests of Nob (22:18). Samuel, a Levite by descent though not of Aaron’s line (1 Chronicles 6), was by God’s providence clothed in priestly linen, quietly displacing Eli’s sons. In dark seasons God is often preparing a “but.” In a season when the glory seems to have departed from something we care about, are we looking for the quiet “but Samuel” God may be raising up?

[Insight] From “Before Eli” to “Before the LORD”. In verse 11 Samuel “ministered to the LORD before Eli the priest”; in verse 18 he “ministered before the LORD,” literally “before the face of the LORD.” The change marks growth: the boy who began under a human supervisor is now described as serving in God’s own presence. Faithful service under human oversight is the doorway into service consciously before God Himself. The goal of every mentor, as Eli in this one respect understood, is to bring a young servant to the place where he serves before the Lord and not merely before people.

[Christ & Life] Clothed in His Linen. Christ is our true Priest, and we, as God’s children, are clothed in the linen of His righteousness, ministering in the temple of His Spirit (Revelation 19:8).

1 Samuel 2:19

[Application] A Robe That Grew With Him. “Moreover his mother used to make him a little robe, and bring it to him year by year.” A boy grows, so each year’s robe had to be larger than the last. Hannah sewed by memory and estimate, measuring a son she saw once a year; only someone who was there would think to record such a detail. She could not keep Samuel, but she could clothe him. She did not abandon him; she nurtured his calling from a distance, stitching his growth into the garments she made. Surrender to God did not end a mother’s love; it turned it into faithful, yearly, mostly unseen acts. Giving a child to God does not mean letting go of caring for him; it means caring in a new way, releasing control while never ceasing to invest, visit, and pray.

[Application] The Woven Garment of Hidden Preparation. This little robe is a picture of incremental, hidden growth. Spiritual maturity is not handed over overnight. It is woven year by year through small acts of faithfulness, quiet devotion, and an expanding capacity to carry the presence of God. We cannot wear tomorrow’s mantle today; we must grow into our calling season by season.

[Seed] The Mother’s Ministry of the Robe. The robe is a picture of intercession. Hannah kept fashioning a covering for her son as he grew. So do the unseen, continual prayers of intercessors and parents who pray coverings over the next generation of spiritual leaders: love woven in secret and carried to the house of God. What hidden act of love can we keep carrying to God for someone we have entrusted to Him?

[Application] Parents Are Always Sewing Little Coats. Habits are the clothing of the soul, and parents are always making little coats their children will wear long after any fabric would have worn out. Paul uses clothing language for character: “put on tender mercies, kindness, humility, meekness, longsuffering” (Colossians 3:12). Habits are sewn not mainly in crises but in daily things: how parents speak to each other, how they spend their time, how they pray. Every home is a workroom where children’s inner garments are being made, for better or worse. What little coats are we sewing by our habits, our speech, and our prayer?

[Discovery] The Robe Comes Back. “Robe” is me’il, the word for the high priest’s robe (Exodus 28:31), and it follows Samuel through the book. When Saul seized the edge of his robe and it tore, Samuel said, “The LORD has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today” (15:27–28). Even at Endor, Samuel is recognized as “an old man … covered with a mantle,” a me’il (28:14). The garment a mother first sewed for a small boy became the identifying garment of the prophet’s life. What parents begin in love can mark their child’s calling to the very end.

1 Samuel 2:20

[Insight] Eli Could Bless Other Homes. “And Eli would bless Elkanah and his wife, and say, ‘The LORD give you descendants from this woman for the loan that was given to the LORD.’” The priest whose own house was collapsing still spoke blessing over another family, year after year, and the next verse shows that God honored his words (v. 21). His blessing was not an empty wish. God often honors the office of spiritual authority and the faith of those who receive, even when the vessel dispensing the blessing is badly flawed; His grace is not handcuffed by the weakness of His ministers. Yet Eli could pronounce good over Elkanah’s household while failing to bring order to his own. “If a man does not know how to rule his own house, how will he take care of the church of God?” (1 Timothy 3:5).

[Foundational Truth] What Is Lent to the LORD Is Never Lost. “The loan that was given to the LORD” repeats the wordplay of 1:27–28: the verb “ask” or “lend” echoes in the names Samuel and Saul. We can never out-give God. This is not a formula for getting a return; it is a revelation of His generous character. What we surrender fully to God, He answers with His own kindness, and often multiplies back in greater measure.

1 Samuel 2:21

[Foundational Truth] God Is No Man’s Debtor. “And the LORD visited Hannah, so that she conceived and bore three sons and two daughters.” Hannah gave God one son; God gave her five more children. Whatever is wholly consecrated to God is never truly lost. God will not be out-given; the surrender of an Isaac or the dedication of a Samuel always meets a multiplied return of grace, grace upon grace (John 1:16). And the “seven” of her song became reality in fullness. The Woman of Grace received five more children.

[Insight] Visited Like Sarah. “Visited” (paqad) is the verb of Genesis 21:1, “the LORD visited Sarah as He had said,” and of Exodus 4:31, when Israel learned God had visited them in Egypt. It means to attend to closely and act; Ruth 1:6 uses it when “the LORD had visited His people by giving them bread.” Hannah stands in the line of barren mothers God visited: Sarah, Rebekah, Rachel, Samson’s mother. God’s visit is covenant attention, not a casual call. When God visits, He does not drop by; He comes with His purpose in His hands.

[Christ & Life] Grew Up With God. “Meanwhile the child Samuel grew before the LORD,” or literally, “grew up with the LORD,” near Him and under His care. While Hannah’s house in Ramah filled with five more children, her firstborn grew up with God as his household. The same verb is used of Moses, who also grew up away from his mother (Exodus 2:10). Growth “before the LORD” is the only kind that lasts; Samuel’s roots went down into the soil of the sanctuary itself. While Eli’s sons withered in the same courtyard, Samuel flourished: the environment was identical; the orientation of the heart was different. So the life of Christ within us grows as we abide continually in His presence, untouched by surrounding corruption. The child given away was not lost; he grew up in the closest company there is.

1 Samuel 2:22

[Discovery] Defiling the Women Who Came to Serve. “And how they lay with the women who assembled at the door of the tabernacle of meeting.” The phrase repeats Exodus 38:8 almost word for word, where the bronze laver was made “from the bronze mirrors of the serving women who assembled at the door of the tabernacle of meeting.” Those women gave up their mirrors so priests could wash before serving. Here priests who should have washed at that laver defiled the women who came to serve. “Assembled” is the root of “hosts,” a word for enlisted, organized service; service at the tent was a kind of spiritual warfare. The sons made the tabernacle a place where sin was committed rather than confessed, mixing pure worship with the gross immorality of pagan fertility rites Israel was meant to reject. Leaders who prey on the devoted commit a double sin, against the person and against the house of God, and desecrate the most precious thing in it: people who came to serve Him. The church must protect the vulnerable, not the reputation of the powerful.

[Insight] Sin at the Very Door. The enemy’s greatest triumph is not bringing sin into the world but bringing it to the very door of the sanctuary. It reveals a seared conscience when men can move seamlessly from offering sacrifices to gross immorality in the same place.

[Discovery] Phinehas at the Door Again. Eli named his son after Phinehas son of Eleazar, the zealous priest who stopped Israel’s immorality at Shittim while the people wept “at the door of the tabernacle of meeting” (Numbers 25:6), and who received “a covenant of an everlasting priesthood, because he was zealous for his God” (Numbers 25:13). The second Phinehas did at the door of the tabernacle what the first Phinehas had executed men for. The name carried the zeal; the man carried the opposite. A godly name does not make a godly heart, and a family’s spiritual heritage can be spent in a single generation.

[Application] Knowing Is Not the Same as Acting. “Now Eli was very old, and he heard everything his sons did.” Eli was near ninety (he died at ninety-eight, 4:15), and he was not ignorant; he heard everything, again and again. He was a passive father and a permissive priest. Delayed obedience is disobedience, and information without action only increases accountability. What do we already know about sin in our lives, families, or ministries that we have addressed only with words and never with action?

1 Samuel 2:23

[Application] A Question Where a Command Was Needed. “So he said to them, ‘Why do you do such things? For I hear of your evil dealings from all the people.’” Eli asks why. The question was understandable but needless, because no good reason could exist. As father, high priest, and judge, Eli had authority to remove his sons from service (Deuteronomy 17:12; 21:18–21), and he asked them a question instead. His rebuke used the language of mild disappointment rather than the language of righteous authority, far too gentle for the size of the offense. In leadership and parenting, soft rebukes for hard sins do not produce repentance; they communicate complicity. Words without action told his sons everything they needed to know about how seriously he meant it. Some situations do not need another question; they need a decision. Love that only asks “Why?” when it should say “No more” leaves the sinner in charge.

[Insight] Reputation Instead of Righteousness. Eli’s appeal rests on what people are saying: “I hear of your evil dealings from all the people … it is not a good report.” He seems more concerned with the report spreading among the people than with the holiness of God being violated. The rebuke was general, framed as a question, and based on hearsay he had not troubled to examine. Correction built on reputation teaches children to hide sin, not to hate it. The question that changes a heart is not “What are people saying?” but “What does God see?” (Compare David with Adonijah: “his father had not rebuked him at any time by saying, ‘Why have you done so?’” 1 Kings 1:6.)

1 Samuel 2:24

[Application] Right Diagnosis, No Treatment. “No, my sons! For it is not a good report that I hear. You make the LORD’s people transgress.” Eli named the true weight of the sin: his sons were causing God’s people to cross a line. His diagnosis was correct and his treatment was missing. What he said was right, but it was not enough. It is possible to fall into grave sin by thinking it very important to be nice to people. Knowing exactly how serious a sin is and still speaking softly about it is a deeper failure than ignorance. (Note the tender “my sons” here against the man of God’s “your sons” in verse 29.)

1 Samuel 2:25

[Christ & Life] Who Will Intercede?. “If one man sins against another, God will judge him. But if a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?” There is a wordplay here: “judge” and “intercede” both come from palal, the verb used when Hannah “prayed” (1:10; 2:1), the root of the common Hebrew word for prayer. When people wrong each other, God can stand between them as judge; when a man wrongs God, who can stand between? This is one of the most terrifying questions in the Old Testament, and Eli asked it as if there were no answer. He identified the fatal problem of the old order: no human third party has the standing to mediate between God and a man who has offended God, and the Law had no remedy for high-handed rebellion against Yahweh Himself but death. This verse cries out for the incarnation. What was a rhetorical dead end in the Old Testament becomes the bedrock of the New. Here the lid comes off the Tupperware: Jesus Christ, the God-Man, holding the hand of the offended God and the hand of the offending man, is the very Intercessor Eli thought impossible to find. “There is one God and one Mediator between God and men, the Man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5). “If anyone sins, we have an Advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous” (1 John 2:1–2), and “He always lives to make intercession” (Hebrews 7:25). The most hopeless question in the chapter has its answer in Christ. Do we live daily in the relief that Jesus answers Eli’s question?

[Foundational Truth] When God Decides. “Nevertheless they did not heed the voice of their father, because the LORD desired to kill them.” “Desired” is the word God uses in Ezekiel: “I have no pleasure in the death of one who dies” (Ezekiel 18:32; 33:11). The two statements are not in conflict. God takes no pleasure in the death of sinners, yet after long, persistent, high-handed rebellion He may reach a point of judicial decision, confirming the heart’s chosen direction (Romans 1:24), as with Pharaoh, whose heart was hardened both by himself and by God. Their sins were capital offenses under the law (Leviticus 7:25; 22:9). Because they had continually rejected Him, God withdrew the grace of repentance and sealed their rebellion toward judgment; their refusal to listen was the seal of what was coming. God can will a hardening without being the author of evil. There is a “too late” no one can locate in advance, and so the call is to turn while God’s voice still pleads (Hebrews 3:15). God’s goodness gives long warning; it does not give endless permission. Are we presuming on His patience as if opportunities to repent will never run out?

[Insight] Sovereignty and Responsibility in One Verse. Verse 25 declares both that the sons “did not heed” and that “the LORD desired to kill them.” Scripture never asks us to choose between human guilt and divine sovereignty: their rebellion was fully their own; God’s judgment was fully His. The verse also answers an unspoken question. God did not tolerate their abuse because He was indifferent; He had already determined its end.

1 Samuel 2:26

[Christ & Life] Growing Both Ways at Once. “And the child Samuel grew in stature, and in favor both with the LORD and men.” The Hebrew reads literally “went on growing, and good,” and the verse stands in brilliant contrast to the darkening doom of Eli’s house. Luke borrows this sentence for Jesus: “Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men” (Luke 2:52). Samuel is set up as a type of Christ, the faithful one growing against the dark backdrop of a corrupt religious system. Samuel is a faithful servant who grows in a corrupt temple; Jesus is the Son who grows up to cleanse the temple and become its replacement (John 2:19–21). Favor with God and favor with people are not rivals; in Samuel and supremely in Jesus they grew together. Spiritual health needs vertical growth and horizontal grace. A young person who grows toward God does not need to grow away from others to do it.

[Application] Grow in Favor With God First. The growth is Godward and manward, but in that order. Favor with people that is not rooted in favor with God is fragile; favor with God will eventually shape a life others recognize as good. (The verb can also mean “became great,” fitting what Samuel became, 3:19–20.)

Section Reflections

[Insight] The Narrator’s Alternating Camera (1 Samuel 2:11–26). The section cuts back and forth like a cinematic montage: the sons’ greed (vv. 12–17); “But Samuel” (v. 18); Hannah’s robe and Eli’s blessing (vv. 19–21); the sons’ immorality and Eli’s weak rebuke (vv. 22–25); “And the child Samuel grew” (v. 26). The two families are woven together so that each sets off the other. The sons grew on in sin while the child grew on in favor; God loves to watch His lilies grow among thorns. On one side the established religious machinery is rotting from the inside, marked by greed, sexual immorality, and cynical profiteering from the things of God; on the other a quiet, obscure boy in a linen ephod is growing in favor with God and people. Light and darkness grow up side by side in the same house, and God is watching both. This juxtaposition shows that spiritual environment does not dictate spiritual destiny. Hophni and Phinehas had every advantage and became corrupt; Samuel was placed in a toxic, backslidden environment and grew in holiness. That is a word of hope for parents, and for believers in compromised churches and cultures: the atmosphere does not have to decide the outcome. Whenever there are men like Eli’s sons, God raises up someone like Samuel. God is never left without a witness. He does not wait for corruption to end before He raises its replacement; He grows the answer in the same courtyard, often in a child no one is watching.

[Insight] The Rotting Institution and the Hidden Seed (1 Samuel 2:12–26). God does not always tear down a corrupt religious structure with a lightning bolt. Often His judgment on a rotting institution is simply to let it die under the weight of its own compromise, while He quietly, almost unnoticed, grows a pure alternative seed right in its shadow. Extortion at the altar and immorality at the door are followed again and again by the quiet refrain: “But Samuel ministered before the LORD.” God’s answer to deep systemic corruption is a devoted child wearing a linen ephod.

[Insight] Two Kinds of Young Men at Shiloh (1 Samuel 2:11, 13, 15, 17, 18, 21, 26). The same Hebrew word, na’ar, “boy, young man, servant,” describes the priest’s servant with the fork (vv. 13, 15), the “young men” whose sin was very great (v. 17), and Samuel, “the child” who ministered (vv. 11, 18, 21, 26). Same age group, same courtyard, same sanctuary, opposite service. One young servant came to take from worshipers; the other came to serve the LORD. Young people around ministry are always being formed by what they see. Leaders should ask which kind of na’ar their example is producing, and young believers should know that their surroundings do not decide which one they become.

[Application] Eli Raised Another Woman’s Son to Faithfulness and Lost His Own (1 Samuel 2:11–26; 3:9–10). Eli supervised Samuel’s service (v. 11), blessed his parents (v. 20), and later taught him how to answer God’s call (3:9). He could not have been a wholly bad father, since he helped raise Samuel to godliness. Yet his own sons “did not know the LORD” (v. 12). Eli’s failure was not an inability to teach; it was an unwillingness to confront those closest to him (v. 29). Grace does not run in the blood: God gave it to the child of an obscure country Levite and not to the sons of the high priest. Leaders can be effective with other people’s children and blind at home. The test of a spiritual father is not only whom he mentors but whom he is willing to correct.

[Application] Correction That Came Too Late and Too Soft (1 Samuel 1:14; 2:22–25; 3:13). Eli was quick and severe with Hannah, accusing a praying woman of drunkenness (1:14), and slow and mild with his own sons. He seems to have spoken only after the immorality became public (v. 22), not when the greed began (vv. 13–17); had he rebuked them for their greed, the rest might have been prevented. His words were a question (v. 23), an appeal to reputation (v. 24), and a theological observation (v. 25), with no action. God later names the root: “his sons made themselves vile, and he did not restrain them” (3:13). The Lord’s rebuke of Eli was as hard as Eli’s rebuke of his sons had been soft. Parents and pastors who correct early, clearly, and with action are not being unkind; they are being merciful before mercy runs out.

[Christ & Life] The Table Where God Shared a Meal With His People (1 Samuel 2:13–17; Leviticus 7:11–21; Deuteronomy 12:7). The peace offering was the one sacrifice that became a meal. The fat went up to God, the priest received his assigned share, and the worshiper’s family ate the rest “before the LORD your God, and you shall rejoice” (Deuteronomy 12:7). The worshiper’s portion was a token of communion and fellowship with God. Hophni and Phinehas plunged their fork into that fellowship meal and seized God’s portion before Him. The result was that “men abhorred the offering of the LORD” (v. 17): people lost the joy of eating in God’s presence. God has always wanted to dwell and dine with His people; Jesus still says, “I will come in to him and dine with him, and he with Me” (Revelation 3:20). The gravest damage of corrupt ministry is not only stolen meat but a spoiled table, where people stop expecting to meet God.

[Application] Appetite Tells You Who Your God Is (1 Samuel 2:12–17, 22). The priests wanted meat, then the fat, then women (v. 22), a steady escalation of appetite. Paul describes such people as those “whose god is their belly” (Philippians 3:19). Sin is preferring lesser pleasures to God, and Hophni and Phinehas are a portrait of desire gone wrong: they would rather have a roast than the Lord. The cure for disordered appetite is not mere restraint but a greater delight.

[Application] The Anatomy of a Dying Ministry (1 Samuel 2:12–25). This section exposes the warning signs of a spiritual house about to collapse. First, the loss of the knowledge of God (v. 12). Second, personal appetite raised above God’s requirements (v. 15). Third, manipulation and force used to get one’s way (v. 16). Fourth, toleration of gross immorality (v. 22). Finally, the rationalization and passive management of sin by the senior leader (v. 23). When these line up, the lampstand of that ministry is about to be removed (Revelation 2:5).

[Christ & Life] “Who Will Intercede?” Is Answered in the Next Verse (1 Samuel 2:25–26; 7:5–9; 12:23). Eli asks, “If a man sins against the LORD, who will intercede for him?” (v. 25). The very next sentence is, “And the child Samuel grew” (v. 26). Samuel becomes Israel’s great intercessor. At Mizpah he “cried out to the LORD for Israel, and the LORD answered him” (7:9). At Saul’s coronation he says, “far be it from me that I should sin against the LORD in ceasing to pray for you” (12:23), using the same “far be it” God spoke against Eli’s house (2:30). Psalm 99:6 lists Samuel “among those who called upon His name,” and God names Moses and Samuel as Israel’s great intercessors (Jeremiah 15:1). Eli’s question had an answer growing in his own courtyard, and that answer points beyond itself to the One who “always lives to make intercession” (Hebrews 7:25). God answers the hopeless questions of one generation with the people He is raising in the next.

[Application] Leaders Who Take and a Mother Who Gives (1 Samuel 2:13–16, 19–20; 8:9–17; 12:3–4). This section is full of taking: the priest “would take for himself” (v. 14); “you may take,” “I will take it by force” (v. 16). Against that stands Hannah, who brings a robe “year by year” (v. 19) and has given her son as “the loan … given to the LORD” (v. 20). The thread runs through the book. The priests’ “custom” (mishpat, v. 13) is echoed by “the behavior (mishpat) of the king,” which Samuel describes with a string of “he will take” (8:9–17). The corruption of leadership in Israel starts at the altar before it reaches the throne. And at the end of his ministry Samuel asks Israel, “Whose ox have I taken?” and they answer, “You have not … taken anything from any man’s hand” (12:3–4). The book measures every leader by whether he takes or gives. The question for every leader in God’s house is the same: are people poorer or richer for my ministry?

[Seed] The Robe and the Fork (1 Samuel 2:13–14, 19). Two objects capture this section: the priests’ three-pronged fork, stabbing into worshipers’ offerings to take what was not theirs, and Hannah’s little robe, carried each year to clothe what she had given away. One pictures religion that takes; the other pictures love that gives. The contrast is a quiet sermon on the difference between consuming ministry and self-giving devotion.

1 Samuel 2:27–36 — A Prophecy Against the House of Eli

Reflections

1 Samuel 2:27

[Insight] A Nameless Man of God. “Then a man of God came to Eli and said to him, ‘Thus says the LORD.’” He has no name, no hometown, and no further role. He appears, speaks, and disappears; the message matters more than the messenger. In a time when “the word of the LORD was rare” (3:1), God bypassed the high priest’s own office and sent His word from outside it. When leadership will not correct itself, God raises outside voices to speak. When God sends a message, it matters little who brings it. His word does not need a famous mouth; it needs a faithful one, and the most important messages sometimes come through people we would never have chosen.

[Foundational Truth] Judgment Begins With Grace Remembered. “Did I not clearly reveal Myself to the house of your father when they were in Egypt in Pharaoh’s house?” The rebuke does not begin with Eli’s sin but with God’s self-revelation to Aaron in Egypt (Exodus 4:14, 27), a question Eli could only answer with a silent yes. God grounds His judgment in the grace He had shown Aaron’s house, which makes Eli’s failure a betrayal of grace, not merely of law. Before He says “you kicked,” He says, “I revealed Myself, I chose, I gave” (vv. 27–28). Sin looks most serious when it is seen against grace. God’s judgments on His people often begin by recounting His unmerited favor, and His correction always begins by reminding them who He has been to them.

1 Samuel 2:28

[Insight] Three Privileges at the Center of Worship. “Did I not choose him out of all the tribes of Israel to be My priest, to offer upon My altar, to burn incense, and to wear an ephod before Me?” The priest was first “My priest,” God’s before he was the people’s. Each privilege put him at the center of Israel’s meeting with God: the altar (atonement and access), the incense (prayer rising before God, Psalm 141:2), and the ephod, whose shoulder stones carried the names of Israel’s tribes before the LORD “as a memorial” (Exodus 28:12), and which held the means of seeking God’s guidance. Every privilege of nearness was given for God and for others. Eli’s house turned privileges into perks. Every believer is now part of “a royal priesthood” (1 Peter 2:9), and the same temptation and the same calling apply.

[Foundational Truth] Greater Privilege, Greater Accountability. God also gave the priests “all the offerings made by fire,” their inheritance in place of land (Deuteronomy 18:1; Numbers 18:8–20). They had plenty and still grabbed for more, which made the theft inexcusable. The higher the privilege and the deeper the grace given to a family or a leader, the stricter the accountability when that privilege is abused. “To whom much is given, from him much will be required” (Luke 12:48).

[Discovery] The Boy Already Wore What They Were Losing. The house chosen “to wear an ephod before Me” is about to lose its place, while a few verses earlier a boy was already ministering before the LORD “wearing a linen ephod” (v. 18). God had dressed the replacement before the sentence was read. He prepares His answer before He announces His judgment. Nothing God removes leaves Him without a servant.

1 Samuel 2:29

[Discovery] Kicking Like a Fattened Ox. “Why do you kick at My sacrifice and My offering which I have commanded in My dwelling place?” “Kick” appears only twice in the Old Testament: here and in the Song of Moses, “But Jeshurun grew fat and kicked … and scornfully esteemed the Rock of his salvation” (Deuteronomy 32:15). Picture an overfed ox that becomes unmanageable and refuses its kind master. The danger in God’s house was not hunger but fullness. And the sin was committed in God’s own dwelling place, His home. Well-fed people are in the most danger of kicking at the God who fed them.

[Foundational Truth] Silence Is a Way of Honoring. “And honor your sons more than Me, to make yourselves fat with the best of all the offerings of Israel My people.” Eli never stole meat with a fork; he simply did not stop the men who did. God calls that “honoring” his sons above Him. “Honor” is kabed, to give weight. Those who do not use their authority to restrain their children in effect honor them more than God. And the plural “yourselves” includes Eli: he was eating what his sons had stolen, sharing the plate, and he would later die because he was “heavy” (4:18). God’s glory had been stolen and wrapped around Eli’s belly. He gave weight to the wrong things and became weighty in the wrong way. Whatever we will not confront in those we love, we have made heavier than God.

[Insight] The Idolatry of Family. God diagnoses the root of Eli’s passive leadership. It was not merely weakness; it was idolatry. By refusing to remove his sons from office to protect God’s holiness, Eli effectively worshiped his children above his Creator. Idolatry is often found in raising legitimate loves above the supreme love. Whenever we tolerate sin in our loved ones at the expense of God’s honor, we have made idols of them. Whom or what have we honored above God, a child, a relationship, a career, our comfort?

1 Samuel 2:30

[Foundational Truth] Honor Has Weight; Contempt Has None. “For those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed.” This is the moral center of the chapter. “Honor” is kabed, to make heavy, give weight; “lightly esteemed” is qalal, to be light, trifling. To honor God is to give Him weight: first place, first portion, true reverence. To despise Him is to treat Him as light, and God will make us light. God does not merely observe this; He responds to it. People who give God weight are given weight; people who treat Him as light become light. If Eli had honored God in his household, God would have honored his family. This is not a reward formula but a description of how the God of glory relates to people, an unshakable law of the spiritual universe. While salvation is by unconditional grace, divine honor and usefulness in ministry are bound up with our reverence for God. He will strip the influence from anyone who handles His glory lightly. What would it look like for us to give God weight this week?

[Application] The Runner Who Gave God Weight. Eric Liddell famously received this verse on a slip of paper at the 1924 Paris Olympics after refusing to run on the Lord’s Day, and then won the 400 meters. Accounts differ on how he received the note, but the verse found a man who had already decided whom he would honor.

[Foundational Truth] A Promise Is Not a Permit. “I said indeed that your house and the house of your father would walk before Me forever.’ But now the LORD says: ‘Far be it from Me.’” God had promised the priesthood to Aaron’s line “by a perpetual statute” (Exodus 29:9; Numbers 25:13). “Far be it from Me” comes from a root meaning “to profane”: it would be a profane thing to Me to keep such men walking before Me. God does not revoke His promise to Aaron’s priesthood; He rejects a false trust that treats the promise as protection for unholy men. God’s promise to Aaron was enduring, but the participation of Eli’s particular line depended on faithfulness. The promise of priesthood was never a license for irreverence. No inherited privilege can shield anyone from God’s holiness (Matthew 3:9). Are we treating a privilege, position, or spiritual heritage as a permanent pass rather than a stewardship?

1 Samuel 2:31

[Insight] No Old Man in the House of an Old Man. “Behold, the days are coming that I will cut off your arm and the arm of your father’s house, so that there will not be an old man in your house.” This is said to a man the narrator has just called “very old” (v. 22). Eli’s long life had not been used to restrain evil; age made him cool and slack in his duty rather than wise. Now no one in his line would reach the age he had wasted. Long life is God’s gift for faithful use, not a guarantee that faithfulness can be postponed.

[Seed] Cutting Off the Arm. The “arm” is strength, influence, and authority. When a spiritual house loses its reverence, it inevitably loses its spiritual authority. It may keep a title, but it will lose the arm of God’s power.

[Discovery] Arms Cut Off Like Dagon’s. “I will cut off your arm” uses an image found nowhere else in the Old Testament, and it is acted out literally three chapters later, when the idol Dagon is found fallen before the ark with “both the palms of its hands broken off on the threshold” (5:4). The sentence on Eli’s house uses the language of God’s judgment on a pagan god. A priesthood that behaves like a pagan shrine will be judged like one.

[Application] The Long Shadow of Unchecked Sin. The fulfillment unfolds over generations. It began at Aphek, where Hophni and Phinehas died (4:11), continued at Nob, where Saul’s man Doeg killed eighty-five priests of Eli’s line in one day (22:18–19), and ended when Solomon removed Abiathar, “that he might fulfill the word of the LORD … concerning the house of Eli” (1 Kings 2:27). This is not guilt passed to innocent people, but a forfeited office and a broken legacy. A father’s passivity can cost his household for generations.

1 Samuel 2:32

[Foundational Truth] God Will Not Stay Where He Is Despised. “And you will see an enemy in My dwelling place, despite all the good which God does for Israel.” The word can mean an adversary or simply distress, the affliction of God’s own tabernacle. Eli lived to hear that the ark was taken (4:17–18), and within a few chapters Shiloh ceased to be God’s dwelling: “go now to My place which was in Shiloh … and see what I did to it” (Jeremiah 7:12; Psalm 78:60). His grandson would be named Ichabod, “the glory has departed” (4:21). God’s presence is His gift, not the guaranteed property of a corrupt priesthood. A religious institution can keep its building and its schedule long after the glory has left.

[Insight] Distress Amid Israel’s Good. Meanwhile God would do good for Israel through Samuel (7:3–14). The punishment mirrors the sin: they greedily took the fat of the land in the present, and they would be cut off from the good God poured out on Israel in the future. Blessing can be flowing all around a person while he experiences only the distress his own compromise has invited.

1 Samuel 2:33

[Discovery] Eyes That Would Be Consumed. “But any of your men whom I do not cut off from My altar shall consume your eyes and grieve your heart.” The phrase pictures eyes failing from weeping (Leviticus 26:16; Lamentations 2:11). In Eli’s own story the words became literal: his eyes “began to grow so dim that he could not see” (3:2), and at the end “his eyes were so dim that he could not see” (4:15). The man who would not look squarely at his sons’ sin lived to have his eyes fail at the sanctuary. Refusing to see sin in those we love does not make it disappear; it leaves our eyes to be consumed by its results.

[Application] The Anguish of the Survivor. Sometimes sudden death is a mercy compared with the judgment of outliving your legacy and watching everything you built rot from the inside out. Survival for Eli’s line would be a curse of grief rather than a blessing of continuation, its descendants dying in the prime of life, perhaps by the sword (the Greek text reads “by the sword of men”). The one man spared “from My altar” fits Abiathar, spared at Nob (22:20) and later sent home to Anathoth (1 Kings 2:26).

1 Samuel 2:34

[Foundational Truth] Two Sons, One Day. “Now this shall be a sign to you that will come upon your two sons, on Hophni and Phinehas: in one day they shall die, both of them.” The sign was given so that Eli would know the rest of the sentence was certain, and it was fulfilled precisely when the ark was captured (4:11). They had sinned together and would die together; bind these tares in a bundle. God graciously gives signs, not only for judgment but to confirm the authenticity of His word, so that when the blow falls it is recognized as His discipline, not tragic coincidence. Eli received the sign years in advance and still did not act. God gives signs not to satisfy curiosity but to awaken, and a warning that is heard but not obeyed becomes a sentence.

[Seed] His Warnings Are as Sure as His Promises. God’s threats are as true as His promises, and we must not believe only half His word. The God who kept His promise to Hannah kept His warning to Eli. His faithfulness cuts both ways. (The sign reached the next generation too: Phinehas’s widow named her son Ichabod, 4:19–22. Both brothers’ names are probably Egyptian; Phinehas likely means “the Nubian.”)

1 Samuel 2:35

[Christ & Life] The Faithful Priest. “Then I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest who shall do according to what is in My heart and in My mind. I will build him a sure house, and he shall walk before My anointed forever.” The prophecy found partial, temporary fulfillment in Samuel, “established as a prophet” (3:20), who replaced Eli as Israel’s leader, and in Zadok, who replaced Eli’s descendant Abiathar under Solomon (1 Kings 2:27, 35). But its ultimate trajectory is Jesus Christ. Unlike the Levitical priests who served by outward regulation and often failed, Jesus ministers entirely from the inner life, doing exactly what is in the Father’s heart and mind (John 8:29). He is the “merciful and faithful High Priest” (Hebrews 2:17), “faithful to Him who appointed Him” (Hebrews 3:2), whose house is eternal, and who works in perfect union with God forever. Here the container is opened: the hope sealed in this promise is Christ. We find our eternal security not in our own faithfulness but in the faithfulness of the Priest God raised up for Himself. His priesthood is our sure house.

[Discovery] Faithful Priest, Sure House: One Word Twice. “Faithful” and “sure” translate the same Hebrew word, ne’eman, “established, reliable.” It returns at key points: “Samuel had been established (ne’eman) as a prophet” (3:20); Abigail tells David the LORD will make him “an enduring (ne’eman) house” (25:28); God promises David, “your house … shall be established (ne’eman) forever” (2 Samuel 7:16), so that “sure house” is the very language of the Davidic covenant. God says of Moses, “He is faithful (ne’eman) in all My house” (Numbers 12:7), which Hebrews applies to Christ, “faithful … as a Son over His own house, whose house we are” (Hebrews 3:2–6). God builds sure houses for faithful servants, and the house He builds outlasts any house we inherit.

[Insight] According to What Is in My Heart. “Who shall do according to what is in My heart and in My mind.” “Mind” here is nephesh, “soul.” In verse 16 the priest’s servant was offered “as much as your heart (nephesh) desires”; the old priesthood served its own appetite. The faithful priest will serve God’s desire. The same measure is used of the king: “The LORD has sought for Himself a man after His own heart” (13:14). Faithfulness is measured by whose desire governs the servant: his own appetite, or God’s heart.

1 Samuel 2:36

[Insight] From Fork to Begging Bowl. “Everyone who is left in your house will come and bow down to him for a piece of silver and a morsel of bread.” The family that took meat by force (v. 16) will beg for a crust. The “piece of silver” was the smallest coin, and “a morsel of bread” is a mouthful. They wasted the Lord’s heritage, and now they beg their bread. “Bow down” is the posture of worship, now given to a man for food. Hannah had already sung it: “Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread” (v. 5). The chapter that began with a barren woman lifted up ends with a proud house brought low, the great reversal fulfilled; God’s poetic justice is perfectly symmetrical. What we grab from God’s table, we eventually lose at our own.

[Application] Serving God for Bread. “Please, put me in one of the priestly positions, that I may eat a piece of bread.” Eli’s descendants will ask for priestly work, not to serve God, but to eat. It is the same motive that ruined their fathers, now reduced to begging. When the priesthood is pursued for what it gives rather than for Whom it serves, God’s judgment finally reduces the ministers to beggars. A line meant to feast in the presence of God is reduced to begging for a crust because it traded the sacred for the secular. Paul warns of those who “do not serve our Lord Jesus Christ, but their own belly” (Romans 16:18). Whoever serves God for bread will end up begging for it. Those who serve Him for Himself find that He is their portion. Are we serving God for Himself, or for the bread His service brings us: approval, position, security?

[Insight] A Descendant of Eli at the Ruins of Shiloh?. Abiathar was sent to his fields at Anathoth (1 Kings 2:26). Jeremiah was “of the priests who were in Anathoth” (Jeremiah 1:1), and many suggest he descended from Abiathar’s line. If so, a descendant of Eli’s house later preached, “Go now to My place which was in Shiloh” (Jeremiah 7:12). This is possible, not certain, but it would mean God turned the heir of a failed house into the voice that told its story.

Section Reflections

[Christ & Life] The Transition of Covenants (1 Samuel 2:27–36). This passage marks a monumental shift in redemptive history. God had promised that the house of Aaron, and Eli’s branch of it, would walk before Him forever (v. 30). Yet here God says, “Far be it from Me.” The Levitical priesthood required covenant faithfulness, and God would not allow His holiness to be endlessly mocked; He removed the lampstand from Eli’s line because it honored men above God. But God’s ultimate purpose cannot be thwarted by human failure. In the middle of devastating judgment He plants a seed of messianic hope: “I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest.” Israel’s access to God would not finally depend on the flawed sons of Aaron, but on a priest God Himself provides, who acts entirely according to His heart. The judgment on Eli’s house creates the vacuum that calls for the faithful Priest (v. 35). The failure of the Old Covenant mediators becomes the very stage for the New Covenant Mediator.

[Foundational Truth] The Idolatry of Permissive Love (1 Samuel 2:27–36). The man of God strikes at the root of Eli’s failure: “Why do you … honor your sons more than Me?” Eli was not an actively malicious man; he was a passive one. He rebuked his sons with words, but he did not remove them from office. He allowed the desecration of God’s glory in order to preserve his family’s comfort. This section reveals that passivity in leadership, especially tolerating sin out of misplaced family loyalty or fear of conflict, is not viewed by God as mere weakness. It is indicted as high treason and idolatry. Raising human relationships above the holiness of God brings the collapse of spiritual authority.

[Seed] The Tragedy of Misplaced Loyalty (1 Samuel 2:29–36). The whole indictment pivots on one devastating accusation: Eli honored his sons more than God. In trying to save his sons from discipline, he lost his sons entirely; they would die in one day. In trying to preserve his family’s comfort at God’s expense, he condemned his family to poverty and early death (vv. 33, 36). The spiritual paradox is clear: when we put family before God, we end up destroying the family. When we put God before family, we place our family under the covering of His grace, as Hannah did.

[Christ & Life] The Faithful Priest Who Walks Before the Anointed, and Is the Anointed (1 Samuel 2:10, 35; Zechariah 6:13; Hebrews 2:17; 3:1–6; 7:23–28). The chapter ends with two figures: “a faithful priest” and “My anointed” (v. 35), echoing “His king … His anointed” (v. 10). In Israel’s history they were separate. Samuel walked before Saul and David; Zadok walked before Solomon and anointed him with “a horn of oil from the tabernacle” (1 Kings 1:39). Some object that the faithful priest cannot be Christ, because he walks before the anointed. But the prophets foresaw the two offices joined: “He shall be a priest on His throne” (Zechariah 6:13), “a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek” (Psalm 110:4). Only Jesus perfectly does all that is in the Father’s heart (John 8:29). He is “a merciful and faithful High Priest” (Hebrews 2:17), “faithful … as a Son over His own house, whose house we are” (Hebrews 3:6), who “always lives to make intercession” (Hebrews 7:25). The faithful Priest and the Anointed King of 1 Samuel 2 meet in one Person, and He is building the sure house that Eli’s line could not keep.

[Foundational Truth] Inherited Office Is Not a Permanent Pass (1 Samuel 2:28, 30, 35; 13:13–14; 15:28). The house of Aaron had been chosen, given an ephod, and promised to “walk before Me forever” (vv. 28, 30), yet God said, “Far be it from Me.” The same pattern follows Saul: “now your kingdom shall not continue. The LORD has sought for Himself a man after His own heart” (13:14), and “The LORD has torn the kingdom of Israel from you today” (15:28). Priest and king are both measured by God’s heart (2:35; 13:14), not by title or lineage, and in 1 Samuel God forms and removes leaders by that standard. Office is nothing without character. The heaviest scrutiny falls on those with the greatest privilege, and priestly office, spiritual heritage, and years in ministry are no shelter. Every believer in leadership holds a calling that is a stewardship, not a possession.

[Foundational Truth] God’s House Never Falls for Want of Hands (1 Samuel 2:32, 35; Psalm 78:60, 68–70; Jeremiah 7:12). The judgment on Eli’s house is severe, and Shiloh itself would be abandoned: “He forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh, the tent He had placed among men” (Psalm 78:60). Yet the same psalm goes on: God “chose the tribe of Judah, Mount Zion which He loved,” and “He also chose David His servant” (Psalm 78:68–70). God’s work never falls to the ground for want of hands to carry it on. The chapter that announces the fall of a priestly house also announces a faithful priest and a sure house (v. 35). God’s purposes are never hostage to failing institutions; even as Shiloh decays, He is raising a boy in a linen ephod. When His house is corrupted, He is already preparing what comes next. His purpose to dwell with His people survives the failure of those who served His dwelling. It moved from Shiloh to Zion, from tabernacle to temple, and finally to Christ and His people, “whose house we are” (Hebrews 3:6).

Chapter-Wide Reflections

Reflections

[Discovery] Hannah’s Song Was Sung Over Eli’s House (1 Samuel 2:3–10, 16, 25, 29–36). The reversals Hannah sang (vv. 3–9) are acted out on Eli’s family before the chapter ends. Proud talk is silenced (v. 3) as the sons are left without an intercessor (v. 25). “By strength no man shall prevail” (v. 9) stands against “I will take it by force” (v. 16). “The LORD kills” (v. 6) becomes “in one day they shall die” (v. 34). “He brings low” (v. 7) becomes “they shall be lightly esteemed” (v. 30). “Those who were full have hired themselves out for bread” (v. 5) becomes Eli’s descendants begging “for a morsel of bread” (v. 36). “He lifts the beggar from the ash heap” (v. 8) is happening in the boy at Shiloh (vv. 18, 26), and “His anointed” (v. 10) appears again in verse 35. Hannah’s praise was not sentimental; it was prophecy about her own city. The Scriptures we sing in worship describe what God is actually doing around us.

[Discovery] The Song of Moses Runs Under the Whole Chapter (1 Samuel 2:2, 6, 29; Deuteronomy 32:4, 15, 39). Both halves of the chapter draw from Deuteronomy 32. Hannah takes her comfort from it: God the Rock (Deuteronomy 32:4, 15, 18, 30–31; 1 Samuel 2:2) and “I kill and I make alive” (Deuteronomy 32:39; 1 Samuel 2:6). The man of God takes his charge from it: “Jeshurun grew fat and kicked” (Deuteronomy 32:15), the only other place the verb “kick” appears (1 Samuel 2:29). And the same verse in Deuteronomy says Jeshurun “scornfully esteemed the Rock of his salvation,” the very Rock Hannah praised. One Scripture lifts the broken and indicts the comfortable. The Word is not divided; our hearts decide whether we hear its comfort or its warning.

[Discovery] The Weight of Glory (1 Samuel 2:3, 8, 12, 29–30; 4:18, 21). A thread of “weight” runs beneath the whole chapter. The Hebrew idea of honor and glory (kabod) is literally heaviness. God is the One who weighs actions (v. 3). He gives the beggar a “throne of glory,” a seat of weight (v. 8). Eli’s sons are “worthless” (v. 12), men without profit or substance, spiritually hollow. Eli gave more weight (kabed) to his sons than to God (v. 29), letting them, and himself, grow heavy on the fat of the sacrifices. And God declares the law of spiritual gravity: those who give Him weight He will give weight, and those who despise Him He will make light (qalal) (v. 30). Eli ends up “heavy” when he falls from his seat (4:18), and his grandson is named Ichabod, “the glory (kabod) has departed” (4:21). The chapter sets the heavy, eternal reality of God’s kingdom against the hollow, lightweight reality of fleshly religious striving. The story is finally about what a person gives weight to. When the priesthood made God light, the glory itself departed. What we load with weight in our lives, God weighs, and the glory we take for ourselves will not stay with us.

[Foundational Truth] The Pervasive Weighing of God (1 Samuel 2:1–36). “By Him actions are weighed” (v. 3) is the interpretive key to the chapter; we watch God’s scales working in real time. Peninnah’s pride is weighed and found wanting. Hannah’s brokenness is weighed and rewarded. The ritual activity of Hophni and Phinehas is weighed and judged profane. Eli’s passive parenting is weighed and judged as idolatry. Samuel’s quiet, unseen ministry in a linen ephod is weighed and met with divine favor. The heaviest scrutiny falls on those with the greatest privilege (vv. 27–29), and the leader who turns the flock away from worship carries grave responsibility. God does not look at the outward bulk of a life, a ministry, or a sacrifice; He looks at the true spiritual weight of the heart behind it.

[Foundational Truth] Holiness That Stoops, and Holiness That Will Not Be Handled Lightly (1 Samuel 2:2, 8, 13–17, 29–30). “No one is holy like the LORD” (v. 2). In one chapter the same holy God lifts a weeping woman from the dust and seats her among princes (v. 8), and refuses to let His altar be treated as a meat market (vv. 13–17, 29). Mercy to Hannah and judgment on Eli’s house are not two moods in God, nor competing impulses; they are one holy character meeting two different hearts, a heart that came empty and a heart that came full. His holiness is the reason He can be trusted by the poor and the reason He cannot be used by the comfortable. The God who stoops to the ash heap will not be handled lightly at His own table.

[Christ & Life] The Messianic Bookends: Clearing the Stage for the Anointed (1 Samuel 2:10, 35). First Samuel 2 is extraordinarily prophetic. It opens with Hannah prophesying the “Anointed” King (v. 10) and closes with God promising a faithful priest who will walk before His “Anointed” forever (v. 35). Everything in between is the systematic failure and announced destruction of the current religious order. The pattern is clear: the failure of human systems is never the end of God’s redemptive plan; it is the clearing of the stage. The collapse of the priesthood’s integrity is the necessary backdrop that reveals our desperate need for a better Priest and a better King. Jesus is the Anointed King who breaks His adversaries to pieces (v. 10) and the merciful and faithful High Priest who perfectly does the Father’s will (v. 35). The whole story of Eli’s failure and Samuel’s rise is a shadow play pointing to Christ, who replaces the failing systems of men with His eternal, indestructible life.

[Insight] From Natural Succession to Spiritual Anointing (1 Samuel 2:1–36). This chapter disrupts the ancient assumption of biological succession in spiritual things. Under the old order, priests were born, not called. Hophni and Phinehas were the biological heirs to the highest spiritual office in Israel, yet God rejects them and calls a boy from the hill country of Ephraim, not of Aaron’s line, to stand in the gap. The theme anticipates the New Testament: “it is not the children of the flesh who are the children of God, but the children of the promise” (Romans 9:8). Spiritual authority is born of obedience and anointing, not pedigree.

[Seed] The Weaponization of Intercession (1 Samuel 1–2; 3:1). History is shaped not by the people in institutional power but by those in hidden intercession. Eli, the high priest, sits on a seat by the door, powerless to stop the moral decay of the nation or his own family. But a marginalized, weeping woman in chapter 1 prays a prayer that shifts the destiny of the nation in chapter 2. Samuel arises in a day when “the word of the LORD was rare” (3:1), and he arises out of a barren woman’s prayer; God often births fresh prophetic ministry through desperate, persistent prayer in spiritually dry eras. Hannah’s intercession brings forth the prophet who will anoint David, who will establish the line of Christ. One surrendered, praying believer can carry more weight in the divine economy than an entire institution of compromised leaders.

[Insight] The Theology of the Outsider, the Blindness of the Insider (1 Samuel 1:13; 2:1–10, 22–25). A barren, grieving woman with no official standing sings the most profound theology in the chapter, including the first prophecy of God’s anointed King. Meanwhile the high priest, who once mistook her prayer for drunkenness (1:13), cannot discern or discipline his own household. God often gives revelation to the humble and hides it from those who assume their position guarantees insight.

[Insight] Eli’s Tragedy: Sound Words, Soft Will (1 Samuel 2:20, 23–25; 3:18). Eli is not a villain. He blesses Hannah graciously (v. 20), speaks orthodox theology (v. 25), and later receives judgment with humility (3:18). Yet he failed at the one point that mattered most: acting on what he knew. His story warns that correct doctrine and personal kindness cannot replace courageous obedience, especially at home.

[Christ & Life] The Good Shepherd and Those Who Eat the Fat (1 Samuel 2:13–17, 29; Ezekiel 34:3, 23; John 10:11). Ezekiel later indicts Israel’s leaders in words that sound like Hophni and Phinehas: “You eat the fat … but you do not feed the flock” (Ezekiel 34:3). God answers by promising to shepherd His people Himself through His servant David (Ezekiel 34:23). Jesus fulfills this as the Good Shepherd, who does not take the sheep’s life but lays down His own for them (John 10:11). Where Eli’s sons took the offering by force, Jesus became the offering.

[Discovery] From Song to Sentence to Promise (1 Samuel 2:1–36). First Samuel 2 is a large-scale revelation of the move from the chaos of the Judges to the establishment of the kingdom, a prophetic blueprint for the gospel. The chapter moves from God’s sovereignty (Hannah’s song), to human failure (Eli’s sons), to the divine promise of a messianic solution (the faithful priest). Its overarching theme is the futility of fleshly power and the necessity of divine intervention. Eli’s house represents the old order: failing, corrupt, and dying. Samuel is the transitional prophet, but the chapter’s hope is thrust forward to Jesus Christ. He is the exalted Horn of Hannah’s song, the true Boy growing in favor in Luke 2, and the faithful Priest of the prophet’s declaration.
