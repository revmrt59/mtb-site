Chapter Study

1 Samuel 3

Verse-by-Verse
1 Samuel 3:1

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:1 Now the boy Samuel ministered to the LORD before Eli. And the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no widespread revelation.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:1 Meanwhile, the boy Samuel served the LORD by assisting Eli. Now in those days messages from the LORD were very rare, and visions were quite uncommon. (NLT)

Main Idea

Samuel faithfully serves the LORD during a spiritually dark period when prophetic revelation has become rare, preparing for God to speak again through him.

Verse Explanation

This verse opens a major transition in 1 Samuel. Chapter 2 ended with judgment pronounced against Eli’s house because of the corruption of his sons and Eli’s failure to restrain them. Against that dark background, Samuel has been quietly growing and serving before the LORD. Chapter 3 now explains how Samuel moved from faithful service in the tabernacle to becoming the recognized prophet through whom God would again speak to Israel. Verse 1 deliberately places two realities side by side: Samuel is faithfully ministering before the LORD, while Israel is experiencing a scarcity of divine revelation.

The phrase “before Eli” indicates that Samuel was serving under Eli’s authority and supervision. Eli was the priest responsible for Samuel’s training and service at Shiloh. This creates one of the striking tensions of the chapter. Eli’s own household had come under divine judgment, yet God still used Eli to help Samuel recognize what was happening when the LORD began calling him. In verses 8–9, Eli eventually understands that the LORD is calling Samuel and instructs him how to respond.

The verse then shifts from Samuel personally to the spiritual condition of Israel: “And the word of the LORD was rare in those days.” Here “the word of the LORD” refers particularly to divine revelation communicated through prophetic messages. It does not mean that Israel lacked the written revelation already given through Moses, nor does it mean God had completely ceased speaking. The point is that fresh prophetic revelation from God had become uncommon.

The word translated “rare” carries the sense of something precious, scarce, or uncommon. The problem was therefore not that God’s word lacked value, but that occasions when God openly revealed His message were infrequent. This scarcity should be understood against the spiritual condition described in Judges and the opening chapters of 1 Samuel. Israel had repeatedly turned away from the LORD, the priesthood had become corrupt, and even those responsible for the sanctuary were treating holy things with contempt. The rarity of revelation forms part of the spiritual darkness of the period.

The statement “there was no widespread revelation” further explains what it meant for the word of the LORD to be rare. The Hebrew refers literally to a vision being “breaking forth” or becoming widespread. Some older English translations render the phrase, “there was no open vision.” The idea is not that absolutely no revelation occurred, since a man of God had spoken to Eli in the previous chapter (1 Samuel 2:27–36). Rather, prophetic visions and divine messages were not occurring frequently or broadly throughout Israel.

He had been brought to the house of the LORD at Shiloh by Hannah after he was weaned and had been entrusted to Eli’s care in fulfillment of Hannah’s vow (1 Samuel 1:24–28). By this stage Samuel had already been serving there for some time. The emphasis is therefore not simply that he was young, but that God was preparing and calling someone who, from a human standpoint, might not have seemed the obvious person through whom to restore prophetic revelation to Israel.

The statement that “Samuel ministered to the LORD” continues a description already used of him in 1 Samuel 2:11 and 2:18. Samuel was serving in connection with the sanctuary at Shiloh, assisting Eli and participating in whatever duties were appropriate to his age and position. The wording is important because Samuel’s service was ultimately directed toward the LORD, even though much of it was carried out under Eli’s supervision. He was not merely helping an elderly priest; he was serving God.

This becomes especially significant because Samuel was serving in a deeply compromised religious environment. Eli’s sons, Hophni and Phinehas, were priests, yet they treated the LORD’s offerings with contempt and committed serious sexual immorality connected with the tabernacle (1 Samuel 2:12–17, 22–25). Samuel, however, is repeatedly presented in contrast with them. While they dishonored the LORD in positions of religious authority, Samuel faithfully “ministered to the LORD.” The corruption surrounding him did not prevent genuine faithfulness to God.

Spirit & Life

The significance of 1 Samuel 3:1 therefore lies partly in its contrast: faithful Samuel is serving God during a time when spiritual leadership is corrupt and revelation is scarce. The verse prepares the reader for God’s intervention. The silence of the period will be broken, and the boy serving faithfully in the sanctuary will become the prophet through whom “the word of the LORD” again comes to Israel.

For believers today, the verse does not promise that faithful service will result in receiving prophetic revelation like Samuel did. Samuel occupied a particular place in Israel’s redemptive history. The enduring principle is that spiritual darkness and the failure of established leaders do not prevent God from preserving faithful servants or accomplishing His purposes. Samuel did not create revival by ambition or position. He faithfully served the LORD where God had placed him, and when God chose to speak, Samuel was the servant God called.

1 Samuel 3:2

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:2 And it came to pass at that time, while Eli was lying down in his place, and when his eyes had begun to grow so dim that he could not see,(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:2 One night Eli, who was almost blind by now, had gone to bed. (NLT)

Main Idea

The aging and nearly blind Eli lies down as the narrative prepares for God to call the young Samuel and begin a transition in spiritual leadership.

Verse Explanation

This verse begins to set the scene for Samuel’s call by the Lord. The preceding verse has already established the spiritual condition of the period: “the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no widespread revelation” (1 Samuel 3:1). Verse 2 now moves from that broad spiritual condition to the particular circumstances of the night when God will speak to Samuel. Eli, the aging high priest, is lying down, his eyesight is failing, and Samuel is nearby in the sanctuary. These details are historical and practical, but they also contribute to the contrast developing in the narrative between an aging priestly order that is nearing judgment and a young servant whom God is preparing to receive His word.

The mention of Eli lying down is important to the mechanics of the story. When Samuel hears the voice, he assumes that the elderly Eli has called him. That is entirely reasonable. Samuel is serving under Eli, Eli is nearby, and Samuel has not yet learned to recognize the voice of the Lord (3:7). The narrative therefore unfolds naturally rather than presenting Samuel as immediately understanding that he has received supernatural revelation.

“and when his eyes had begun to grow so dim” explains Eli’s physical condition. He was becoming increasingly blind because of old age. Later, when Eli dies, Scripture describes him as ninety-eight years old and says that “his eyes were so dim that he could not see” (1 Samuel 4:15). Chapter 3 appears to describe an earlier stage of the same deterioration: his eyesight had “begun to grow so dim.”

Failing eyesight was a normal feature of advanced age in the ancient world, especially without modern treatment for conditions such as cataracts. Scripture records similar physical decline in other elderly figures. Isaac’s eyes became dim so that he could not see clearly in Genesis 27:1, and Jacob’s eyes were “dim with age” in Genesis 48:10. There is therefore no need to make Eli’s failing eyesight itself a supernatural punishment. It is first of all a straightforward description of an elderly man whose physical vision was deteriorating.

“that he could not see” emphasizes how severe Eli’s condition was becoming. His limited sight helps explain why Samuel may have had responsibilities during the night and why Eli depended increasingly upon him. Samuel is repeatedly portrayed as serving: “the boy Samuel ministered to the LORD before Eli” (3:1). His service was not merely ceremonial training. He was living in practical submission under the elderly priest and was apparently available when needed.

Eli’s house is moving toward judgment, while Samuel is moving toward prophetic responsibility.

“while Eli was lying down in his place” simply describes Eli retiring for the night in his customary sleeping place. Eli was the priest under whom Samuel served at the sanctuary in Shiloh. The text does not suggest that Eli was sleeping inside the Most Holy Place or immediately beside the ark. Verse 3 distinguishes Samuel’s location in relation to “the ark of God,” while Eli is described more generally as lying down “in his place.” The exact arrangement of the sleeping quarters is not given, but both Eli and Samuel were apparently close enough that Samuel could quickly go to Eli when he heard his name called (3:5).

Spirit & Life

The verse therefore quietly prepares the reader for a major transition in Israel’s spiritual leadership. Eli is still the recognized priest, but he is elderly, physically failing, and his household stands under divine judgment. Samuel is still only a young servant who does not yet recognize the Lord’s voice, but God is about to entrust His word to him. The transition does not happen because Samuel seeks prominence or because Eli appoints him to prophetic office. It begins because God chooses to speak.

For believers, the primary significance is not that physical weakness indicates spiritual decline. Scripture does not teach that. Rather, the verse contributes to a larger biblical truth seen throughout this chapter: human instruments may weaken and established structures may fail, but God is not dependent upon them. Even during a period when “the word of the LORD was rare,” God could raise up a servant who would hear Him and faithfully deliver His word. Samuel’s usefulness begins not with position, age, or experience, but with his availability before the Lord and, as the passage develops, his willingness to hear and obey what God says.

1 Samuel 3:3

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:3 and before the lamp of God went out in the tabernacle of the LORD where the ark of God was, and while Samuel was lying down,(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:3 The lamp of God had not yet gone out, and Samuel was sleeping in the Tabernacle near the Ark of God. (NLT)

Main Idea

Before dawn, while the lamp of God is still burning and Samuel is lying near the sanctuary, the LORD prepares to break the silence and call him.

Verse Explanation

This verse continues the nighttime setting introduced in verse 2. Eli had gone to lie down, his eyesight had become poor, and Samuel was also preparing to sleep. Verse 3 gives two important details before God speaks to Samuel: it was still before dawn, because the sanctuary lamp had not yet gone out, and Samuel was lying down near the sanctuary where the ark of God was kept. These details do more than establish the time and place. They place Samuel physically near the center of Israel’s worship at the very moment when the Lord is about to restore something that had become spiritually rare: the revelation of His word.

The expression “in the tabernacle of the LORD” identifies the sanctuary at Shiloh, where Israel’s central place of worship was located during this period. The tabernacle had originally been constructed in the wilderness according to God’s instructions to Moses (Exodus 25–40), and Joshua 18:1 records that Israel later set it up at Shiloh. By Samuel’s time, the sanctuary at Shiloh appears to have developed some more permanent architectural features. First Samuel 1:9 refers to a “doorpost of the tabernacle of the LORD,” and 3:15 mentions “the doors of the house of the LORD.” This may indicate that the original tabernacle was incorporated into, or surrounded by, a more permanent sanctuary structure. Scripture does not give enough architectural detail to reconstruct it with certainty.

The statement “where the ark of God was” emphasizes the sacred significance of this location. The ark of the covenant was the most sacred object associated with Israel’s sanctuary. It represented God’s covenant presence among His people and was associated particularly with the place where the Lord made Himself known to Israel. God had told Moses concerning the ark, “And there I will meet with you, and I will speak with you from above the mercy seat” (Exodus 25:22).

This makes the setting of Samuel’s call especially significant. The word of the Lord had become rare, but Samuel was lying near the sanctuary containing the ark, the place historically associated with God revealing Himself to His people. God was about to break the silence.

The final phrase, “while Samuel was lying down,” prepares directly for what happens next. Samuel was not praying, performing a ceremony, or seeking a supernatural experience. He was simply lying down for the night when God took the initiative and called him. Verse 4 immediately continues, “that the LORD called Samuel.” The emphasis throughout the account is on God initiating revelation. Samuel does not discover God’s voice through his own effort; the Lord chooses to speak.

According to the law of Moses, the lamps were to be tended so that they burned from evening until morning before the Lord (Exodus 27:20–21; Leviticus 24:2–4). Therefore, the statement gives us an approximate time. The night was far advanced, but morning had not yet arrived. The lamp was still burning when the Lord called Samuel.

The wording does not mean that allowing the lamp to go out in the morning was necessarily a failure of priestly duty. Exodus 27:21 specifically says Aaron and his sons were to tend the lamp “from evening until morning.” The picture is therefore probably of the final hours before dawn, when the oil in the lamps was nearing the end of its nightly burning. This also helps explain why Samuel and Eli were sleeping.

There may also be literary significance in “the lamp of God” still burning. The chapter has just told us that “the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no widespread revelation” (3:1), and verse 2 mentions Eli’s failing eyesight. Against that dark background, the lamp of God has not yet gone out. Some interpreters therefore see the lamp as carrying a secondary symbolic suggestion: although Israel’s spiritual condition was poor and Eli’s priestly house was under judgment, God had not abandoned His people. He was about to speak again through Samuel. That symbolism fits the narrative well, but the primary meaning remains literal. The sanctuary lamp was still burning because it was sometime before morning.

Spirit & Life

The verse therefore stands at an important transition in 1 Samuel. The night is nearly over. Eli’s eyesight is failing. The priestly house of Eli is moving toward judgment. The word of God has been rare. Yet “the lamp of God” is still burning, Samuel is present in the sanctuary, and God is about to call him. By the end of the chapter, Samuel will be established as the Lord’s prophet, and “the LORD revealed Himself to Samuel in Shiloh by the word of the LORD” (3:21).

For believers, the verse provides a restrained but meaningful lesson. God’s purposes are not extinguished simply because a generation has become spiritually dull or because His word seems uncommon. In Samuel’s day, the lamp was still burning and God was preparing a servant through whom He would speak again. The emphasis is not on manufacturing revelation but on being available and responsive when God speaks. Samuel was in his appointed place, carrying out his ordinary responsibilities, when the Lord called him.

1 Samuel 3:4

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:4 that the LORD called Samuel. And he answered, "Here I am!"(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:4 Suddenly the LORD called out, “Samuel!” “Yes?” Samuel replied. “What is it?” (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD personally calls Samuel, and Samuel immediately responds with the readiness of a servant even though he does not yet recognize God's voice.

Verse Explanation

This verse marks the decisive turning point in the opening scene of 1 Samuel 3. Samuel has been serving under Eli at Shiloh, during a time when “the word of the LORD was rare” and prophetic revelation was uncommon (3:1). Eli’s eyesight was failing, the lamp of God was still burning, and Samuel was lying down near the sanctuary (3:2–3). Into that quiet nighttime setting, God suddenly speaks. The spiritual silence described in verse 1 is now broken by the Lord Himself.

The words “the LORD called Samuel” are the central statement of the verse. The initiative belongs completely to God. Samuel is not seeking a revelation, attempting to hear a supernatural voice, or asking God to speak. The Lord chooses the moment, calls the person He has chosen, and begins the relationship through which Samuel will become His prophet.

The subject “the LORD” is also important in light of the larger context. This is Yahweh, Israel’s covenant God, the One whose word had become rare in those days. The narrator therefore wants the reader to recognize something Samuel himself does not yet recognize: the Lord is speaking again. The reader knows the identity of the caller from the beginning, while Samuel does not. This creates the tension that drives verses 4–10.

The words “And he answered, ‘Here I am!’” describe Samuel’s immediate response. The expression translated “Here I am” is the Hebrew hinneni, literally conveying the sense of “Here I am” or “I am ready.” It is more than simply stating one’s location. In many biblical settings it expresses availability and readiness to respond.

Eli’s eyesight was failing, the lamp of God was still burning, and Samuel was lying down near the sanctuary (3:2–3). Into that quiet nighttime setting, God suddenly speaks. This personal calling is significant. God does not simply issue a general announcement; He calls Samuel himself. The Hebrew wording contains Samuel’s name, and verse 10 later intensifies the call: “Samuel, Samuel!” Calling a person by name is used elsewhere in Scripture at significant moments of divine commissioning or intervention. God called “Abraham, Abraham!” in Genesis 22:11, “Jacob, Jacob!” in Genesis 46:2, and “Moses, Moses!” in Exodus 3:4. Samuel’s experience belongs to that broader biblical pattern in which God personally summons someone for a particular purpose.

At this point, however, Samuel does not know that it is the Lord speaking. Verse 7 explains, “Now Samuel did not yet know the LORD, nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him.” That statement does not necessarily mean Samuel had no faith in God or knew nothing about Him. Samuel had already been dedicated to the Lord by Hannah, had ministered before Him, and had grown “in stature, and in favor both with the LORD and men” (2:26). Rather, verse 7 explains that Samuel had not yet experienced the Lord revealing Himself directly through prophetic revelation. He knew about the Lord and served Him, but he had not yet learned to recognize the Lord’s prophetic voice.

That distinction matters because Samuel’s prophetic ministry does not begin with expertise. He must learn to discern the One who is calling him. This makes the account remarkably realistic. The future prophet does not immediately understand his first encounter with divine revelation. Recognition develops through repeated calling and through Eli’s eventual guidance.

Spirit & Life

For believers today, the application should remain tied closely to what the verse actually shows. God is the One who initiates revelation, while the proper human posture is responsiveness and readiness to obey. Samuel does not yet understand everything that is happening, but when he believes he is being called, he answers “Here I am!” The verse therefore introduces a principle that will characterize Samuel’s life: availability to God precedes understanding of everything God intends to do through him.

1 Samuel 3:5

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:5 So he ran to Eli and said, "Here I am, for you called me." And he said, "I did not call; lie down again." And he went and lay down.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:5 He got up and ran to Eli. “Here I am. Did you call me?” “I didn’t call you,” Eli replied. “Go back to bed.” So he did. (NLT)

Main Idea

Samuel promptly responds to the call but mistakes the LORD's voice for Eli's, showing both his readiness to serve and his need to grow in discernment.

Verse Explanation

This verse shows Samuel’s first response to the Lord’s call. In verse 4, the Lord called Samuel, but Samuel did not yet recognize that it was God speaking. Because he was serving under Eli and apparently sleeping nearby, Samuel naturally assumed that the elderly priest had called for him. The scene is simple, but it reveals both Samuel’s readiness to serve and his inexperience with direct revelation from God.

The opening words, “So he ran to Eli,” show how immediately Samuel responded. He did not ignore the voice, hesitate, or remain in bed. He got up and went quickly to Eli. The verb “ran” suggests urgency and attentiveness rather than casual obedience. Samuel was accustomed to serving Eli, and because Eli was elderly and nearly blind (3:2), it would have been reasonable for Samuel to assume that Eli might need assistance during the night.

The words “for you called me” are especially important because they show Samuel’s misunderstanding. The voice was real, but Samuel had misidentified who was speaking. Verse 7 will explain why: “Samuel did not yet know the LORD, nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him.” Samuel was not being disobedient or spiritually insensitive. He simply had no previous experience by which to recognize this kind of divine communication.

Eli responds, “I did not call; lie down again.” At this stage, Eli also does not recognize what is happening. He simply assumes that Samuel has been mistaken. There is no indication that Eli suspects divine activity until the call has been repeated several times. This fits the spiritual atmosphere already described in verse 1, where “the word of the LORD was rare in those days.” Direct revelation from God was unusual enough that neither Samuel nor Eli immediately considered it the obvious explanation.

Study Notes

Samuel then says, “Here I am, for you called me.” The expression “Here I am” again communicates readiness and availability. Samuel believes he has correctly identified the source of the call. From his perspective, there is no mystery yet. He heard his name, assumed Eli had summoned him, and came immediately.

Spirit & Life

For believers, the verse offers a careful lesson about responsiveness and discernment. Samuel’s willingness to respond is commendable, but willingness alone does not guarantee immediate understanding. A person may genuinely desire to obey God and still need time, instruction, and discernment to understand what God is doing. Samuel’s experience therefore encourages both readiness and humility: readiness to respond when called, and humility to recognize that spiritual understanding may develop gradually.

1 Samuel 3:6

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:6 Then the LORD called yet again, "Samuel!" So Samuel arose and went to Eli, and said, "Here I am, for you called me." He answered, "I did not call, my son; lie down again."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:6 Then the LORD called out again, “Samuel!” Again Samuel got up and went to Eli. “Here I am. Did you call me?” “I didn’t call you, my son,” Eli said. “Go back to bed.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD calls Samuel again, patiently continuing the process while Samuel remains responsive but still does not recognize who is speaking.

Verse Explanation

This verse records the second time the Lord calls Samuel. Samuel has already heard the voice once, gone to Eli, and been told that Eli did not call him. Yet Samuel still does not recognize that the Lord is speaking. The repetition prepares for verse 7, which explains that Samuel had not yet experienced the Lord revealing His word to him. At the same time, the verse begins to show the patience of God in making Himself known to Samuel.

The words “Then the LORD called yet again” emphasize that God takes the initiative a second time. Samuel’s failure to recognize the first call does not bring the encounter to an end. The Lord calls again. This repetition is important because Samuel is being introduced to something entirely new. First Samuel 3:1 has already told us that “the word of the LORD was rare in those days.” Samuel therefore had little reason to expect that the voice he heard was a direct revelation from God.

The phrase “yet again” also begins a pattern that continues through the passage. The Lord calls repeatedly until Samuel is able to recognize what is happening and respond appropriately. This reveals something about the nature of Samuel’s call. God does not merely make one obscure attempt to communicate and then leave Samuel confused. He persistently and patiently makes His call clear.

The Lord again says, “Samuel!” The call is personal. God knows the servant He is calling and addresses him by name. There is no message yet beyond the summons itself. Before Samuel receives the difficult prophetic word concerning Eli’s house, he must first learn who is speaking to him.

The next words, “So Samuel arose and went to Eli,” show that Samuel responds just as promptly the second time as he did the first. He does not dismiss the experience because Eli had already denied calling him. He hears the voice again, gets up, and goes back to Eli.

Study Notes

This personal calling fits a broader biblical pattern. God called Abraham by name in Genesis 22:11, Moses by name at the burning bush in Exodus 3:4, and Jacob by name in Genesis 46:2. Later in this same account the Lord will intensify the summons by saying, “Samuel! Samuel!” (1 Samuel 3:10). Such repeated naming often marks a significant moment of divine calling or intervention.

This continued responsiveness says something important about Samuel’s character. He is not yet discerning correctly, but he is attentive. He assumes that if Eli has called him, he should respond. Since Eli was elderly and nearly blind, as verse 2 has just explained, Samuel may also have understood that Eli could need help during the night. His service to Eli was part of his ministry at the sanctuary.

The fact that Samuel makes the same mistake twice should not be interpreted as spiritual dullness. The narrator will immediately explain the problem in verse 7: “Samuel did not yet know the LORD, nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him.” Samuel had not yet learned to recognize prophetic revelation because he had not previously experienced it in this way.

Eli answers, “I did not call, my son.” The expression “my son” is significant because it shows Eli’s relationship with Samuel. Samuel was not Eli’s biological son, but he had been placed under Eli’s care after Hannah dedicated him to the Lord (1 Samuel 1:24–28). Samuel had grown up serving under Eli at the sanctuary, and Eli speaks to him with the language of affection and paternal responsibility.

Spirit & Life

For believers, the verse offers a careful lesson in spiritual discernment. Samuel’s experience shows that a sincere servant of God may not immediately understand what God is doing. Recognition can develop through repeated experience, Scripture-shaped understanding, and wise guidance. At the same time, Samuel’s response remains exemplary: whenever he believes he is being called, he gets up and answers. The passage therefore joins humility in discernment with readiness to obey.

1 Samuel 3:7

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:7 (Now Samuel did not yet know the LORD, nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him.)(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:7 Samuel did not yet know the LORD because he had never had a message from the LORD before. (NLT)

Main Idea

Samuel serves the LORD faithfully but has not yet experienced or learned to recognize the LORD's prophetic revelation.

Verse Explanation

This verse explains why Samuel repeatedly mistakes the Lord’s voice for Eli’s. Samuel has already heard the Lord call him twice, but each time he has gone to Eli because he assumes that Eli is speaking (3:4–6). Verse 7 interrupts the action to explain Samuel’s confusion. The problem is not rebellion, indifference, or unwillingness to listen. Samuel has simply never before experienced the Lord revealing Himself to him in this prophetic way.

The statement “Samuel did not yet know the LORD” can sound surprising because Samuel has already been described as serving the Lord. First Samuel 2:11 says that “the child ministered to the LORD before Eli the priest,” and 2:26 says that Samuel “grew in stature, and in favor both with the LORD and men.” The verse therefore cannot reasonably mean that Samuel had absolutely no knowledge of God, no faith, or no relationship to Israel’s God.

The phrase “nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him” makes the meaning more precise. The issue is revelation. Samuel has not previously received the prophetic word of God personally.

The phrase “the word of the LORD” also carries special weight in this chapter. It refers not merely to religious information but to communication originating from God. Samuel is about to receive a specific message concerning the judgment of Eli’s house (3:11–14). This will become the beginning of his recognized prophetic ministry. First Samuel 3:19 later says that the Lord was with Samuel and “let none of his words fall to the ground,” and verse 20 says that Israel recognized Samuel as a prophet of the Lord.

Study Notes

The opening word “Now” introduces an explanatory comment from the narrator. The reader already knows that “the LORD called Samuel” (3:4), but Samuel does not know this. Verse 7 therefore lets the reader understand something Samuel himself has not yet understood: he is hearing the voice of God, but he does not yet recognize it as such.

The key is the immediate context. Here, “did not yet know the LORD” means that Samuel did not yet know the Lord through direct prophetic revelation. He had been taught about God, worshiped Him, and served in His sanctuary, but he had not yet personally encountered the Lord speaking His word to him as a prophet.

The Hebrew verb translated “know” is yada, a broad word that can refer to knowledge gained through acquaintance, recognition, or personal experience. In this context, the emphasis is not merely intellectual knowledge. Samuel had not yet come to know the Lord in the particular experience of receiving and recognizing divine revelation. This interpretation is confirmed by the second half of the verse, which explains the first: “nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him.”

The word “yet” is especially important. It indicates a condition that is about to change. Samuel does not yet recognize the Lord’s voice, but before the night is over he will. The verse therefore describes Samuel at the threshold of his prophetic calling. He is already serving God faithfully, but a new dimension of his relationship with the Lord is about to begin.

Spirit & Life

For believers, the verse shows that serving God faithfully and growing in spiritual discernment are not identical things. Samuel genuinely served the Lord before he understood how the Lord was speaking to him. Spiritual maturity includes learning to recognize what comes from God, and that discernment may develop over time. At the same time, the verse keeps the emphasis where the passage places it: revelation begins with God. Samuel comes to know the Lord in this deeper prophetic sense because “the word of the LORD” is revealed to him, not because he discovers it through his own effort.

Main Idea

After the third call, Eli finally recognizes that the LORD is calling Samuel and helps him understand how to respond.

Verse Explanation

This verse records the third time the Lord calls Samuel during the night. Samuel still assumes that Eli is calling him, but the repeated experience finally causes Eli to recognize what Samuel himself does not yet understand: the Lord is speaking to the boy. The verse therefore marks an important transition in the story. Samuel continues to respond faithfully, while Eli moves from confusion to discernment and becomes the one who will instruct Samuel how to answer God.

The words “And the LORD called Samuel again the third time” emphasize God’s persistence. Samuel failed to recognize the Lord the first two times, but God did not stop calling him. The repetition is especially significant in light of verse 7, which explains that “Samuel did not yet know the LORD, nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him.” Samuel's failure to recognize the voice was not rebellion. He was inexperienced in receiving direct revelation from God.

The turning point comes in the words “Then Eli perceived that the LORD had called the boy.” Eli finally recognizes what is happening. The verb “perceived” indicates that Eli came to understand or discern the meaning of the repeated events. Samuel's three visits can no longer easily be explained as imagination or confusion. Eli concludes that the Lord Himself is calling Samuel.

The statement “that the LORD had called the boy” reinforces a major theme running through the passage: the initiative belongs to God. Eli does not appoint Samuel as a prophet. Samuel does not decide to become one. The Lord calls him.

First Samuel 3:1 began by saying that “the word of the LORD was rare in those days.” Now the Lord is repeatedly calling Samuel. By the end of the chapter, “all Israel from Dan to Beersheba knew that Samuel had been established as a prophet of the LORD” (3:20). Verse 8 is therefore one step in the movement from a period of scarce revelation to the establishment of Samuel as God's recognized spokesman.

Spirit & Life

For believers, the verse shows that discernment can involve both persistent responsiveness and wise instruction. Samuel continues to answer even though he does not yet understand, and Eli helps him recognize what God is doing. It also shows God's patience. Samuel does not recognize the Lord immediately, yet the Lord continues calling until understanding comes. The passage therefore encourages readiness to respond while also recognizing that correctly discerning God's activity may require time, repeated confirmation, and mature biblical guidance.

Main Idea

Eli teaches Samuel to approach God's voice as a servant who is ready not merely to hear but to obey whatever the LORD says.

Verse Explanation

This verse follows Eli’s realization in verse 8 that the repeated nighttime calls were coming from the Lord. Samuel still does not yet recognize the Lord’s voice for himself, so Eli now gives him specific instruction about how to respond if the call comes again. The verse is important because it shows the transition from confusion to conscious readiness: Samuel is being taught not merely to hear a voice, but to place himself before the Lord as a servant prepared to listen.

Eli tells him, “Go, lie down.” Samuel is to return to the same place where he had been resting. He does not need to move to a more sacred location, perform a ritual, or create a special religious atmosphere. The Lord has already chosen to speak to him there. Samuel’s responsibility is simply to return and be ready.

The next words, “and it shall be, if He calls you,” show that Eli expects another call but does not presume upon God. The wording leaves the initiative with the Lord. Eli does not tell Samuel to make God speak or to seek some technique for producing revelation. He says, in effect, that if the Lord calls again, Samuel should answer appropriately.

That restraint matters in the context of the chapter. First Samuel 3:1 has already said that “the word of the LORD was rare in those days.” Revelation is something God gives; it is not something human beings can manufacture. Eli can prepare Samuel to respond, but only the Lord can initiate the encounter.

The phrase “for Your servant hears” expresses Samuel’s proper posture before God. By calling himself “Your servant,” Samuel acknowledges that he belongs under the Lord’s authority. The relationship is not one of equals. God speaks as Lord, and Samuel listens as servant.

Study Notes

The opening word “Therefore” connects Eli’s instruction directly to his conclusion in the previous verse. After Samuel comes to him for the third time, “Eli perceived that the LORD had called the boy” (3:8). Eli now acts on that discernment. He does not claim to know what the Lord intends to say, and he does not attempt to speak for God. Instead, he teaches Samuel how to respond when God speaks.

The word “Speak” expresses openness to divine communication. Samuel is not telling God what to say or asking for a particular kind of message. He is simply inviting the Lord to speak whatever He chooses. That becomes especially significant because the message Samuel is about to receive will not be pleasant. It will concern judgment on Eli’s house (3:11–14). Samuel’s posture must therefore be one of submission to God’s word whether the message is comforting or difficult.

The word “hears” carries more than the idea of receiving sound. In biblical usage, hearing frequently includes attentive reception and readiness to obey. The same Hebrew verb can carry the sense of listening with the intention of responding. Samuel is therefore being taught to present himself not merely as someone curious about what God might say, but as a servant prepared to receive and act upon His word.

There is also an important contrast with Eli’s sons. Hophni and Phinehas repeatedly disregarded God’s commands and refused correction, while Samuel is being taught to say, “Your servant hears.” Their lives were marked by refusal to listen; Samuel’s prophetic ministry will begin with a posture of listening.

Spirit & Life

The verse therefore presents a clear picture of spiritual responsiveness. God initiates the revelation, a mature servant helps Samuel recognize what is happening, and Samuel places himself before God as a servant ready to listen. The emphasis is not on seeking unusual experiences but on receiving God’s word with humility and obedience.

For believers, the enduring principle arises naturally from Samuel’s response: the proper posture toward God’s word is, “Speak, LORD, for Your servant hears.” That is not merely a desire to receive information. It is a willingness to come under what God says. Samuel is about to discover that hearing God may involve receiving difficult truth, but the servant’s posture remains the same. God speaks; His servant listens with the intention of obeying.

He places himself under God's authority before the message is given. Genuine hearing of God is therefore inseparable from a readiness to receive and obey what He says.

1 Samuel 3:11

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:11 Then the LORD said to Samuel: "Behold, I will do something in Israel at which both ears of everyone who hears it will tingle.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:11 Then the LORD said to Samuel, “I am about to do a shocking thing in Israel. (NLT)

Main Idea

Samuel's first prophetic message is a severe announcement of judgment, teaching from the beginning that God's word includes warning as well as promise.

Verse Explanation

This verse begins the actual message the Lord gives Samuel after calling him during the night. Samuel has now recognized that the Lord is speaking and has answered, “Speak, for Your servant hears” (3:10). The first prophetic word Samuel receives is not a comforting promise but an announcement of coming judgment. God is about to act in Israel in a way so severe and startling that the news will shock everyone who hears it.

It is significant that Samuel’s first recorded prophetic message concerns judgment on the house of Eli. Eli is the man who has raised him, instructed him, and just taught him how to respond to God. Samuel’s calling therefore immediately tests whether he will be faithful to God’s word even when the message is painful and personally difficult. Verse 15 will show that Samuel was afraid to tell Eli the vision, but he will ultimately report it faithfully.

God says, “I will do something in Israel.” The emphasis falls on divine action. The coming events will not merely be unfortunate circumstances or political accidents. The Lord Himself will be acting in judgment.

The immediate context identifies what this “something” will involve. In verses 12–14, God says He will carry out everything previously spoken against Eli’s house. That judgment had already been announced through the unnamed man of God in 1 Samuel 2:27–36. Eli’s sons, Hophni and Phinehas, would die on the same day, and Eli’s priestly house would lose its privileged position.

The phrase “everyone who hears it” also emphasizes that the coming judgment will become widely known. God’s action against Eli’s house will not remain a private family tragedy. It will become public knowledge throughout Israel. That fits what follows in chapter 4, where the deaths of Eli and his sons and the capture of the ark become defining national events.

Study Notes

The opening words, “Then the LORD said to Samuel,” mark a major transition in Samuel’s life. Until this point, Samuel has been learning to recognize the Lord’s voice. Now he actually receives the word that he will be responsible to carry. This is the beginning of the prophetic ministry that will eventually cause all Israel to recognize Samuel as a prophet of the Lord (3:19–20).

The word “Behold” calls attention to what follows. It signals that something important and decisive is about to be announced. The Lord is not speaking about a minor development. He is declaring an act of judgment that will affect not only Eli’s household but the wider nation of Israel.

The fulfillment begins dramatically in 1 Samuel 4. Israel is defeated by the Philistines, Hophni and Phinehas are killed, the ark of God is captured, and Eli dies when he hears the news (4:10–18). These events are therefore not merely military disaster. The narrative presents them as the outworking of the judgment announced here.

The words “in Israel” broaden the significance beyond Eli’s family. The judgment is directed particularly against Eli’s house, but its consequences will be experienced nationally. Israel’s army will be defeated, thousands will die, and the ark, the central symbol of God’s covenant presence, will be captured by the Philistines. What happens at Shiloh will therefore shake the entire nation.

Spirit & Life

For believers today, the verse reminds us that God’s word includes both promise and warning. God is patient, but His patience should not be mistaken for indifference toward persistent sin. Eli’s household had received warning before judgment came. When God finally acted, the consequences were severe enough that “both ears of everyone who hears it will tingle.” The verse therefore calls for reverence toward God’s holiness and seriousness in responding to His word while there is still opportunity to obey.

1 Samuel 3:12

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:12 In that day I will perform against Eli all that I have spoken concerning his house, from beginning to end.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:12 I am going to carry out all my threats against Eli and his family, from beginning to end. (NLT)

Main Idea

God declares that He will fully carry out the judgment previously spoken against Eli's house, emphasizing the certainty of His word.

Verse Explanation

This verse continues the Lord’s first prophetic message to Samuel. In verse 11, God announced that He was about to do something in Israel so shocking that “both ears of everyone who hears it will tingle.” Verse 12 identifies the heart of that coming judgment: God will carry out everything He had already declared against Eli and his household. The verse emphasizes not only judgment, but the certainty and completeness of God’s word.

The wording is especially significant because an earlier prophet had already given Eli a specific sign: “your two sons, Hophni and Phinehas; in one day they shall die, both of them” (2:34). First Samuel 4:11 records that both men were indeed killed in the same battle. Thus “in that day” connects Samuel’s message with the earlier prophecy and underscores that the time of fulfillment is approaching.

That background is crucial. God’s judgment on Eli is not arbitrary. Eli had received warning, understood the seriousness of his sons’ conduct, and still failed to act with the firmness his responsibility required. Verse 13 will make this even more explicit.

The phrase “all that I have spoken” refers back primarily to the prophetic message delivered by the unnamed man of God in 1 Samuel 2:27–36. That earlier message announced that Eli’s family would lose its privileged priestly position, that his descendants would experience premature death and humiliation, and that Hophni and Phinehas would die on the same day.

The expression “concerning his house” refers not merely to Eli’s physical dwelling but to his family line or dynasty. In biblical language, a person’s “house” often means his descendants and continuing family line. God’s judgment therefore extends beyond Eli personally.

Study Notes

The phrase “In that day” points to the time when God’s announced judgment would be carried out. It does not necessarily refer to one isolated twenty-four-hour period in every detail, but it does point to a decisive time of fulfillment. In the immediate narrative, that fulfillment begins dramatically in 1 Samuel 4, when Israel is defeated by the Philistines, Hophni and Phinehas die on the same day, the ark is captured, and Eli dies after hearing the news.

God then says, “I will perform against Eli.” The Lord presents Himself as the One who will bring the judgment to completion. Human agents will be involved, especially the Philistines in chapter 4, but the narrative interprets the events theologically. They are not merely the result of military weakness or historical circumstance. God is acting in judgment against Eli’s house.

The words “against Eli” do not mean that Eli committed the same sins as Hophni and Phinehas. His sons were openly corrupt, abusing the sacrifices and engaging in sexual immorality at the tabernacle (2:12–17, 22). Eli rebuked them, but the Lord had already charged him with a serious failure: “Why do you kick at My sacrifice and My offering which I have commanded in My dwelling place, and honor your sons more than Me?” (2:29). Eli knew what his sons were doing, possessed authority as father and high priest, yet failed to restrain them effectively.

The word “all” is important. God is not withdrawing part of His previous warning or reducing its severity. Everything He had declared would come to pass. This establishes an important theme in Samuel’s prophetic ministry: the Lord’s word is reliable. Later in the chapter, 1 Samuel 3:19 will say that the Lord “let none of his words fall to the ground.” Samuel’s first prophetic experience teaches him from the beginning that what God says will stand.

Spirit & Life

The verse therefore strongly emphasizes the certainty of God’s spoken word. Eli had already received warning through the man of God. Now the Lord tells Samuel that the warning is no longer merely future possibility. The time has come for God to begin performing what He has declared.

For believers, the verse underscores the reliability and seriousness of God’s word. What God declares, He is able to bring to completion. In this context that truth concerns judgment, but the broader principle applies to all that God has spoken. His promises are dependable, and His warnings are not empty threats. The appropriate response is therefore to take His word seriously while there is still opportunity to repent, obey, and respond to His correction.

1 Samuel 3:13

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:13 For I have told him that I will judge his house forever for the iniquity which he knows, because his sons made themselves vile, and he did not restrain them.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:13 I have warned him that judgment is coming upon his family forever, because his sons are blaspheming God and he hasn’t disciplined them. (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli is held accountable because he knew his sons' wickedness and possessed authority to restrain them, yet failed to act decisively.

Verse Explanation

This verse explains why God’s judgment against Eli’s house is coming. In verse 12, the Lord told Samuel that He would carry out everything previously spoken against Eli’s family. Verse 13 now states the reason. Eli’s sons had persisted in serious sin, Eli knew about it, and although he rebuked them verbally, he failed to restrain them. The focus is therefore not merely on the wickedness of Hophni and Phinehas, but on Eli’s responsibility as both father and priest.

The opening words, “For I have told him”, refer back to the warning already given to Eli through the unnamed man of God in 1 Samuel 2:27–36. God had not judged Eli without warning. He had already confronted the situation, identified the offense, and announced the consequences.

God says, “that I will judge his house forever.” As in verse 12, “his house” refers to Eli’s family line, not merely his physical household. The judgment extends beyond Eli himself to the priestly line descending from him.

The phrase “for the iniquity which he knows” is one of the most important statements in the verse. Eli’s guilt is aggravated by knowledge. He is not unaware of what his sons are doing. He knows their conduct is sinful.

The final phrase, “and he did not restrain them,” identifies Eli’s particular failure. The verb carries the idea of restraining, rebuking, or checking someone effectively. Eli did speak to his sons, so the verse cannot mean that he never verbally objected to their behavior. He did confront them in 2:23–25.

Study Notes

The word “forever” should be understood in relation to the nature of the judgment being announced. It does not mean that every descendant of Eli will personally suffer punishment without end. Rather, the judgment upon his priestly house is permanent in its effect: Eli’s line will lose the privileged priestly position that had been entrusted to it. The removal will not simply be temporary and then reversed.

This unfolds progressively in the biblical narrative. Hophni and Phinehas die in 1 Samuel 4. Later, Abiathar, a descendant of Eli, is removed from the priesthood by Solomon. First Kings 2:27 explicitly connects that event with the prophecy against Eli’s house, saying that Solomon removed Abiathar “that he might fulfill the word of the LORD which He spoke concerning the house of Eli at Shiloh.” The priestly line associated with Eli therefore loses its continuing claim to that office.

First Samuel 2:22 says that Eli “heard everything his sons did to all Israel.” He knew that they were abusing the sacrificial system and behaving immorally with the women who assembled at the tabernacle. He confronted them in 2:23–25, asking, “Why do you do such things?” His problem, then, was not ignorance.

The next phrase, “because his sons made themselves vile,” describes the seriousness of Hophni and Phinehas’s sin. The underlying Hebrew expression is difficult and has produced some textual discussion. The Masoretic Text can be understood as saying that the sons brought a curse upon themselves or acted in a way deserving divine contempt. Some ancient witnesses reflect wording closer to the idea that they blasphemed or cursed God.

Spirit & Life

For believers, the verse carries a direct warning about responsibility. Knowing that something is wrong while refusing to address it can itself become a form of disobedience, especially where God has entrusted genuine authority or stewardship. Eli’s failure was not that he lacked awareness, but that his response did not match the seriousness of what he knew. The verse therefore calls for more than verbal concern about sin; where responsibility has been given, faithfulness may require decisive action.

1 Samuel 3:14

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:14 And therefore I have sworn to the house of Eli that the iniquity of Eli's house shall not be atoned for by sacrifice or offering forever."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:14 So I have vowed that the sins of Eli and his sons will never be forgiven by sacrifices or offerings.” (NLT)

Main Idea

God declares the judgment on Eli's priestly house irreversible through ordinary sacrifice, showing that religious activity cannot substitute for obedience.

Verse Explanation

This verse concludes the judgment God reveals to Samuel concerning Eli and his household. In verses 11–13, the Lord announces that He is about to do something in Israel so severe that “both ears of everyone who hears it will tingle.” The reason is then stated plainly: Eli knew about the wickedness of his sons, Hophni and Phinehas, yet he failed to restrain them adequately. Verse 14 takes the judgment one step further. God declares that the sentence against Eli’s priestly house has become fixed and will not be reversed through the normal sacrificial system.

The phrase “shall not be atoned for” requires careful interpretation. The Hebrew idea behind atonement involves covering, removing, or dealing with guilt so that the consequences of sin are averted. Under the Mosaic system, sacrifice provided a God-appointed means by which sin could be dealt with and fellowship with God restored. Here, however, God declares that no sacrifice will cancel the judgment He has pronounced against Eli's house.

This does not necessarily mean that no individual descendant of Eli could ever personally repent and receive God's mercy. The focus of the passage is the irreversible judgment upon Eli's priestly dynasty. God is saying that the announced sentence cannot now be removed by bringing another animal to the altar. The family's privileged priestly position has been forfeited, and ordinary sacrifices cannot reverse that decision.

That distinction matters because Scripture elsewhere teaches that God forgives those who genuinely repent and turn to Him. The issue here is not that God's mercy has ceased to exist, but that certain consequences of persistent rebellion may become irreversible even though repentance remains morally necessary. A person can be forgiven and still experience consequences resulting from previous actions. In Eli's case, the judgment upon his household and its priestly position had reached that point.

There is also a striking appropriateness in the words “by sacrifice or offering.” Hophni and Phinehas had sinned specifically in connection with Israel's sacrifices. First Samuel 2:17 says that “the sin of the young men was very great before the LORD, for men abhorred the offering of the LORD.” They had turned God's sacrificial system into an opportunity for greed and self-indulgence. Now God declares that the very sacrifices they had treated with contempt would not become a means of escaping His judgment.

Study Notes

“And therefore I have sworn to the house of Eli” indicates the solemn and settled character of God’s judgment. This is stronger than simply saying that God had decided something. When God says, “I have sworn,” He is declaring the certainty of what He has determined. The language recalls the earlier prophecy delivered by the unnamed “man of God” in 1 Samuel 2:27–36, where God announced that Eli’s family would lose its privileged position in the priesthood and that both of Eli’s sons would die on the same day.

The expression “the house of Eli” refers not merely to Eli personally but to his family line, particularly in its priestly role. Eli belonged to the priesthood descended from Aaron, apparently through Ithamar rather than Eleazar. The judgment therefore had consequences extending beyond Eli's own lifetime. This becomes especially important later in the biblical narrative when Solomon removes Abiathar, a descendant of Eli, from the priesthood. First Kings 2:27 explicitly says that Solomon's action fulfilled the word of the Lord concerning the house of Eli. The priestly prominence that Eli's family had enjoyed would eventually be transferred to the line represented by Zadok.

“That the iniquity of Eli’s house” refers particularly to the sins described in 1 Samuel 2–3. Hophni and Phinehas treated the Lord's offerings with contempt, forcibly took portions of sacrifices that did not belong to them, and committed sexual immorality with the women who served at the entrance of the tabernacle (1 Samuel 2:12–17, 22). Their conduct was especially serious because they were priests. They were corrupting the very worship system through which Israel approached God.

Eli himself did not commit all the sins his sons committed, but God held him responsible for knowingly allowing their behavior to continue. The previous verse says, “his sons made themselves vile, and he did not restrain them” (1 Samuel 3:13). Eli rebuked them verbally in 1 Samuel 2:23–25, but he did not remove them from priestly service or exercise the authority necessary to stop their abuse. His failure was therefore more than parental weakness. As high priest and leader of the sanctuary, he allowed men whom he knew to be corrupt to continue dishonoring God in sacred office.

Spirit & Life

The verse therefore illustrates an important feature of biblical prophecy: God's judgment may be announced at one moment and then fulfilled progressively over many years. The death of Eli and his sons was part of the fulfillment, but the eventual displacement of Eli's priestly line completed another major aspect of what God had spoken.

The significance of 1 Samuel 3:14 is therefore severe but precise. Eli's family had repeatedly dishonored God while occupying positions of sacred responsibility. Eli knew what was happening and failed to stop it. After repeated warning, God declared that the consequences would no longer be reversed through sacrifice. The verse teaches that God's patience must never be mistaken for indifference and that religious activity cannot compensate for persistent disobedience. It also warns those entrusted with spiritual responsibility that knowingly tolerating serious evil can itself become culpable before God. At the same time, the verse concerns the irreversible consequences imposed upon Eli's priestly house, not a declaration that God's mercy is unavailable to every individual who genuinely repents.

Main Idea

After receiving a weighty revelation, Samuel returns to ordinary service while carrying a message he fears to deliver to Eli.

Verse Explanation

This verse marks the transition from Samuel receiving his first prophetic revelation to the difficult task of delivering it. During the night, the Lord had called Samuel and announced judgment against Eli’s house because Eli’s sons had made themselves vile and Eli had failed to restrain them (1 Samuel 3:11–14). Samuel now has to return to ordinary duties while carrying an extraordinary and painful word from God. His fear of telling Eli highlights both the severity of the message and Samuel’s youth and relationship with the man who had raised and instructed him.

Opening the doors was probably part of Samuel’s regular service at the sanctuary. First Samuel 2:11 says that “the child ministered to the LORD before Eli the priest,” and 3:1 likewise says that “the boy Samuel ministered to the LORD before Eli.” His service would have included ordinary practical responsibilities associated with caring for the sanctuary.

There is an important contrast in the narrative. Samuel has just heard the voice of God, yet the next thing Scripture records him doing is opening doors. A prophetic encounter did not make ordinary service beneath him. His new experience of divine revelation did not immediately change the humble tasks he had been performing. The narrator presents Samuel continuing faithfully in the responsibilities already entrusted to him.

“And Samuel was afraid” is entirely understandable in the context. Samuel had received devastating news about Eli and his family. God had told him that He would judge Eli’s house permanently because Eli knew about his sons’ wickedness and failed to restrain them. The message even included the declaration that “the iniquity of Eli’s house shall not be atoned for by sacrifice or offering forever” (3:14). Samuel now had to face the very man whose household had been condemned.

“To tell Eli the vision” identifies what Samuel had received as a divine revelation. The word “vision” can describe supernatural revelation from God, even when the text emphasizes hearing rather than seeing. In verses 10–14, Samuel primarily hears the Lord speaking. Therefore “vision” here does not require that Samuel saw a visual scene. The term can refer more broadly to the revelatory experience and the message God communicated to him.

During the night, the Lord had called Samuel and announced judgment against Eli’s house because Eli’s sons had made themselves vile and Eli had failed to restrain them (1 Samuel 3:11–14). Samuel now has to return to ordinary duties while carrying an extraordinary and painful word from God. His fear of telling Eli highlights both the severity of the message and Samuel’s youth and relationship with the man who had raised and instructed him.

“So Samuel lay down until morning” means that after the Lord finished speaking, Samuel returned to his place and remained there until daylight. The text does not say that Samuel went back to sleep. Given the weight of what he had just heard, it is entirely possible that he simply lay there until morning. Scripture does not tell us his exact thoughts, so it is better not to go beyond the text. What is clear is that Samuel did not immediately awaken Eli and deliver the message during the night.

This quiet interval is significant because Samuel has just experienced something completely new. Verse 7 explains that Samuel had not yet known the Lord in this prophetic sense because “the word of the LORD was not yet revealed to him.” By the end of the night, that has changed dramatically. The boy who went to sleep serving in the sanctuary has now received a direct revelation from God and has been entrusted with a message of judgment.

“And opened the doors of the house of the LORD” describes Samuel returning to his normal morning responsibilities. The “house of the LORD” here refers to the sanctuary at Shiloh, where the tabernacle and the ark were located. Although the Mosaic sanctuary was fundamentally a tabernacle or tent, by Samuel’s time the complex at Shiloh apparently included more permanent structural elements. First Samuel uses terms such as “house” and speaks here of “doors,” while 1 Samuel 1:9 refers to a “doorpost of the tabernacle of the LORD.” This suggests that the sanctuary at Shiloh had developed architectural features beyond a simple portable tent.

Spirit & Life

The verse therefore presents Samuel at the intersection of revelation, ordinary faithfulness, and costly obedience. He receives a profound word from God and then resumes the simple duty of opening the sanctuary doors. At the same time, he carries a message he is afraid to deliver. His fear is real, but it does not ultimately silence him. When Eli asks, Samuel tells him everything.

For believers, the application arises directly from that pattern. Genuine responsiveness to God is not measured only by significant spiritual experiences. It is also seen in faithfulness in ordinary responsibilities and in willingness to obey when obedience is uncomfortable. Samuel did not become careless about his service because God had spoken to him, nor did his fear ultimately cause him to conceal what God had said. First Samuel 3:15 begins to show the character that would define his prophetic ministry: he served faithfully, listened carefully, and, despite the personal difficulty involved, learned to speak God's word truthfully.

1 Samuel 3:16

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:16 Then Eli called Samuel and said, "Samuel, my son!" He answered, "Here I am."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:16 But Eli called out to him, “Samuel, my son.” “Here I am,” Samuel replied. (NLT)

Main Idea

Samuel remains available to Eli even though he knows a painful conversation is coming, joining humility with readiness to obey.

Verse Explanation

This verse follows immediately after Samuel’s first direct revelation from the Lord. God had told Samuel that judgment was coming upon Eli’s house because of the wickedness of Eli’s sons and Eli’s failure to restrain them. Samuel was afraid to tell Eli what he had heard. Now Eli calls for Samuel, beginning the conversation in which the young servant will have to deliver a painful word from God to the priest who had overseen him.

“Then Eli called Samuel” indicates that Eli takes the initiative. Samuel had not rushed to tell him what happened during the night. Verse 15 specifically says that Samuel was afraid to tell Eli the vision. Eli’s call therefore brings Samuel directly into the situation he was hesitant to face.

“And said, ‘Samuel, my son!’” is a warm and personal form of address. Eli does not speak to Samuel merely as an employee, temple servant, or subordinate. He calls him “my son.” This reflects the close relationship that had developed between them.

“He answered, ‘Here I am’” repeats a response Samuel had already used several times during the night. In verses 4, 6, and 8, whenever Samuel thought Eli was calling him, he responded and went to him. His answer here again communicates readiness and availability.

Study Notes

Samuel had been brought to the sanctuary at Shiloh by his mother Hannah while still young and had been placed in the Lord’s service under Eli’s supervision (1 Samuel 1:24–28; 2:11). Eli had therefore watched Samuel grow and had served in something of a parental and mentoring role toward him. The words “my son” fit that relationship.

The expression “Here I am” is more than a statement of physical location. In biblical usage it often carries the sense, “I am here and ready to respond.” Abraham answered God this way in Genesis 22:1, Jacob did so in Genesis 46:2, and Moses responded similarly at the burning bush in Exodus 3:4. In Samuel’s case, however, the immediate context is especially important: he is responding to Eli, not directly to God.

Spirit & Life

The verse therefore prepares for Samuel’s first test in prophetic faithfulness. God has spoken, Samuel has heard, and now he must decide whether fear will silence him when the moment comes to speak. Verse 16 shows him stepping into that moment without resistance. He answers Eli’s call even though he knows the conversation may be painful.

For believers, the significance is closely tied to that context. Faithfulness to God often includes remaining available when obedience leads into uncomfortable conversations. Samuel does not become disrespectful toward Eli because he has received revelation from God, nor does his affection for Eli excuse him from telling the truth. The verse begins to show the balance that will characterize faithful ministry: humility toward people, readiness to serve, and willingness to speak truth when God requires it.

1 Samuel 3:17

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:17 And he said, "What is the word that the LORD spoke to you? Please do not hide it from me. God do so to you, and more also, if you hide anything from me of all the things that He said to you."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:17 “What did the LORD say to you? Tell me everything. And may God strike you and even kill you if you hide anything from me!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli requires Samuel to disclose the entire word of the LORD, bringing Samuel to his first test of prophetic faithfulness.

Verse Explanation

This verse records Eli pressing Samuel to tell him exactly what the Lord had revealed during the night. Samuel had been afraid to tell Eli the vision because it concerned God’s judgment on Eli’s household. Eli, however, already knew that the Lord had spoken to Samuel and apparently suspected that the message might be serious. He therefore insists that Samuel conceal nothing. The verse is important because it brings Samuel to his first test as a prophetic messenger: will he faithfully report the whole word of God even when it is painful to speak?

“What is the word that the LORD spoke to you?” shows that Eli has no doubt that Samuel truly heard from God. During the night he had initially failed to recognize what was happening, but after Samuel came to him three times, Eli “perceived that the LORD had called the boy” (1 Samuel 3:8). He had then instructed Samuel to answer the Lord. By morning, Eli assumes that God did indeed speak and wants to know the message.

The phrase “the word” is significant because Samuel’s revelation was not merely a spiritual experience or private impression. God had communicated an intelligible message. In verses 11–14, that message concerned the judgment of Eli’s house. Throughout 1 Samuel, the “word of the LORD” becomes closely associated with Samuel’s prophetic ministry. The chapter began by saying that “the word of the LORD was rare in those days” (3:1), but it will end by saying that the Lord revealed Himself to Samuel “by the word of the LORD” (3:21). This question therefore stands near the center of Samuel’s transition from temple servant to recognized prophet.

“Please do not hide it from me” directly addresses Samuel’s fear. Verse 15 says that Samuel was afraid to tell Eli the vision. Eli apparently recognizes the possibility that Samuel may hesitate to disclose everything, whether because of fear, respect, affection, or the painful nature of the message.

“God do so to you, and more also” is an oath formula found several times in the Old Testament. It calls down divine judgment upon the speaker or hearer if the stated condition is violated. Similar language appears, for example, in Ruth 1:17, 1 Samuel 14:44, 2 Samuel 3:35, and elsewhere.

“If you hide anything from me” makes Eli’s demand comprehensive. He is not merely asking for the general idea. He wants everything God said. This prepares directly for verse 18, where Samuel “told him everything, and hid nothing from him.” The wording deliberately answers Eli’s command. Samuel does exactly what Eli requested.

Study Notes

The exact punishment is left unstated. That was part of the force of the formula. In effect, Eli says, “May God judge you severely if you refuse to tell me.” The expression is therefore much stronger than an ordinary request. Eli solemnly binds Samuel to complete disclosure.

Modern readers may find the wording severe, especially because Samuel is young. It reflects the seriousness with which prophetic revelation was regarded in ancient Israel. If God had spoken, Samuel did not have the right to alter, suppress, or selectively report the message. Eli treats concealment of God’s revelation as a matter serious enough to invoke divine judgment.

This becomes especially important in understanding Samuel’s prophetic character. A faithful prophet was required to communicate God’s message accurately rather than editing it according to the audience’s preferences. Later prophets faced the same responsibility. Jeremiah was commanded, “Whatever I command you, you shall speak” (Jeremiah 1:7), and Ezekiel was appointed as a watchman responsible to communicate God’s warnings faithfully (Ezekiel 3:17–19).

Spirit & Life

The significance of 1 Samuel 3:17 lies particularly in the seriousness of receiving and communicating God’s word. Eli demands the truth even though it may condemn him, and Samuel is required to speak it even though he is afraid. The verse therefore prepares for a defining quality of Samuel’s ministry: he must not conceal or modify what God has spoken.

For believers, the application should remain tied to that central principle. God’s truth is not ours to edit according to comfort, relationships, or fear of another person’s reaction. At the same time, Samuel’s example does not encourage harshness or delight in delivering difficult truth. He was genuinely afraid because he cared about Eli. Faithfulness required him to hold both realities together: real concern for the person and complete honesty about what God had said.

1 Samuel 3:18

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:18 Then Samuel told him everything, and hid nothing from him. And he said, "It is the LORD. Let Him do what seems good to Him."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:18 So Samuel told Eli everything; he didn’t hold anything back. “It is the LORD’s will,” Eli replied. “Let him do what he thinks best.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Samuel tells Eli everything the LORD said despite his fear, while Eli acknowledges God's authority and submits to His judgment.

Verse Explanation

This verse completes the first major test of Samuel’s prophetic calling. The Lord had given him a severe message of judgment against Eli’s house, and Samuel had been afraid to tell Eli. After Eli insisted that he conceal nothing, Samuel faithfully reported the entire revelation. Eli then responds by acknowledging that the message comes from the Lord and submitting himself to God’s judgment, even though that judgment concerns his own family.

“Then Samuel told him everything” shows that Samuel obeyed Eli’s request and, more importantly, proved faithful to the word God had entrusted to him. Verse 17 had pressed him to disclose “all the things that He said to you.” Samuel now does exactly that. He does not soften the message, omit the most painful parts, or substitute his own interpretation for what God had spoken.

“And hid nothing from him” deliberately echoes Eli’s command in the previous verse: “Please do not hide it from me.” The repetition emphasizes Samuel’s complete honesty. His fear in verse 15 was real, but fear did not ultimately determine his obedience.

“And he said, ‘It is the LORD’” records Eli’s response. The pronoun “he” refers to Eli. His words acknowledge that Samuel’s message truly comes from God. Eli does not accuse Samuel of misunderstanding, exaggerating, or speaking presumptuously. He accepts the revelation as the word of the Lord.

Study Notes

This becomes an important indication of Samuel’s fitness for prophetic ministry. A prophet could not be governed by fear of people, personal affection, or a desire to make the message easier to receive. He was responsible to communicate what God had actually said. Later Scripture repeatedly emphasizes the same responsibility. God tells Jeremiah, “Whatever I command you, you shall speak” (Jeremiah 1:7), and Ezekiel is warned that a watchman is accountable to give the warning entrusted to him (Ezekiel 3:17–19).

At the same time, Samuel’s example should not be interpreted as harshness. The previous verse tells us that he was afraid to tell Eli. His hesitation suggests that he understood the personal weight of the message. Faithfulness to truth and genuine concern for the person receiving it are not opposites. Samuel cared about Eli, but he did not allow that concern to become concealment.

“Let Him do what seems good to Him” expresses Eli’s submission to God’s sovereign right to judge. The phrase “what seems good to Him” does not imply that God merely acts according to preference without moral reason. In this context, it recognizes that the Lord has the authority to determine what is right and to carry out His judgment accordingly.

Similar language appears elsewhere in Scripture as an expression of submission to God’s will. When David fled from Absalom, he said that if the Lord did not favor him, “Here I am, let Him do to me as seems good to Him” (2 Samuel 15:26). The idea is that God is sovereign and His decision must finally be accepted.

Spirit & Life

The significance of 1 Samuel 3:18 therefore lies in both Samuel’s faithfulness and Eli’s submission. Samuel demonstrates that a servant entrusted with God’s word must not hide what God has spoken because of fear or personal relationships. Eli demonstrates that when God’s judgment is righteous and settled, the proper response is not to accuse God but to acknowledge His authority.

For believers today, the verse warns against allowing fear of people to determine what portions of God’s truth we are willing to acknowledge or communicate. It also warns that recognizing God’s authority after consequences have become unavoidable is not the same as obeying Him when He first gives opportunity to act. Samuel models courageous faithfulness, while Eli’s story reminds us that hearing and even agreeing with God’s word must lead to timely obedience.

1 Samuel 3:19

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:19 So Samuel grew, and the LORD was with him and let none of his words fall to the ground.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:19 As Samuel grew up, the LORD was with him, and everything Samuel said proved to be reliable. (NLT)

Main Idea

Samuel continues to grow under the LORD's presence, and God confirms the reliability of the prophetic words entrusted to him.

Verse Explanation

This verse summarizes the beginning of Samuel’s established prophetic ministry. After Samuel faithfully delivered the difficult message concerning Eli’s house, the narrative moves from his first revelation to the broader pattern that would characterize his life. Samuel continued to mature, the Lord’s presence rested upon him, and the words he spoke as God’s prophet proved trustworthy and effective.

“So Samuel grew” continues a theme that has appeared earlier in the book. First Samuel 2:21 says that “the child Samuel grew before the LORD,” and 2:26 says that “the child Samuel grew in stature, and in favor both with the LORD and men.” His growth therefore includes more than physical development. Samuel is maturing spiritually and moving into the calling God has placed upon his life.

“And the LORD was with him” is one of the most important statements in the verse. Samuel’s authority does not come merely from intelligence, personality, training under Eli, or public recognition. The decisive reality is that “the LORD was with him.”

The statement also creates an implicit contrast with Eli’s household. Eli’s sons held priestly office, but their conduct showed contempt for the Lord. Samuel, by contrast, does not yet possess their rank or institutional status, but “the LORD was with him.” The narrative therefore emphasizes that true spiritual authority depends upon God’s presence and approval, not merely upon position.

The phrase may also carry the idea that God did not allow Samuel’s words to be disregarded or rendered ineffective. His prophetic declarations possessed weight because God confirmed them. The emphasis, however, is not primarily on Samuel’s personal eloquence but on divine faithfulness. Samuel’s words stood because they were ultimately God’s words.

Study Notes

The word “So” connects this growth with what has just happened. Samuel has learned to recognize the Lord’s voice, receive His word, and communicate it faithfully even when it is painful. His prophetic ministry does not appear fully formed in an instant. He grows into it under the Lord’s direction.

That progression matters because Samuel’s calling develops through faithful response. He began as a boy serving in the sanctuary. He then learned to listen when God spoke. Next he was required to deliver a difficult message. Now the narrative begins to describe the continuing fruit of that calling.

In Scripture, this language commonly describes God’s enabling presence accompanying someone in the work He has appointed them to do. The Lord was with Joseph in Genesis 39:2–3, with Joshua as he succeeded Moses in Joshua 1:5, and later with David in 1 Samuel 18:12–14. In Samuel’s case, God’s presence confirms and sustains his prophetic ministry.

“And let none of his words fall to the ground” is a vivid Hebrew expression meaning that Samuel’s prophetic words did not fail or prove empty. Something that “falls to the ground” becomes ineffective, wasted, or unfulfilled. To say that none of Samuel’s words fell to the ground means that what he spoke as God’s prophet came to pass and proved reliable.

Spirit & Life

The verse therefore marks a major turning point in Israel’s spiritual history. The chapter began with revelation being rare, but now God is raising up a trustworthy prophetic voice. Samuel will become a central figure in Israel’s transition from the period of the judges to the monarchy, and he will later anoint both Saul and David. The foundation of that ministry is established here: he grows, the Lord is with him, and his prophetic word proves reliable.

For believers today, the verse highlights the importance of God’s presence, faithful growth, and truthfulness in spiritual ministry. Samuel’s credibility was not manufactured by position or reputation. It developed because the Lord was with him and because the words entrusted to him proved faithful. The verse therefore encourages a life in which spiritual maturity is measured not merely by activity or title, but by growing faithfulness to God and by handling His word with integrity.

1 Samuel 3:20

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:20 And all Israel from Dan to Beersheba knew that Samuel had been established as a prophet of the LORD.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:20 And all Israel, from Dan in the north to Beersheba in the south, knew that Samuel was confirmed as a prophet of the LORD. (NLT)

Main Idea

All Israel recognizes Samuel as an established prophet because God has authenticated his ministry and confirmed his words.

Verse Explanation

This verse summarizes the public confirmation of Samuel’s prophetic calling. In the preceding verses, Samuel had received his first revelation from the Lord, faithfully delivered it to Eli, continued to grow, and experienced the Lord’s presence in such a way that none of his prophetic words failed. Verse 20 now shows the result: Samuel’s calling was no longer merely a private experience at Shiloh. Israel as a whole came to recognize that God had appointed him as a true prophet.

“Knew that Samuel” indicates settled recognition rather than mere rumor. Israel came to understand that something objective had happened in Samuel’s life. He was not simply regarded as a promising young servant at Shiloh. The nation increasingly recognized that God had given him a prophetic office.

“Had been established” is especially significant. The wording conveys the idea that Samuel had been confirmed, authenticated, or made firm in his role. His prophetic calling was not self-appointed. The Lord had established him.

“As a prophet” identifies Samuel’s role clearly. A prophet in Israel was fundamentally one who received and communicated revelation from God. Prophecy could include prediction of future events, but it was broader than prediction. A prophet spoke God’s word to His people, bringing instruction, warning, judgment, correction, and at times promises concerning the future.

“Of the LORD” defines the source and authority of Samuel’s prophetic ministry. He was not merely a religious spokesman or national adviser. He was a prophet belonging to and commissioned by the Lord.

“And all Israel” does not necessarily mean that every individual Israelite personally knew Samuel at this point. It is a common biblical way of referring to the nation collectively. The point is that Samuel’s reputation had become widespread throughout Israel.

“From Dan to Beersheba” is a geographical expression meaning from the northern end of Israel to the southern end. Dan was located in the far north, while Beersheba was in the south. The phrase therefore functions much like saying “throughout the whole land.”

This expression becomes especially familiar later in the Old Testament as a way of describing the full extent of Israel. For example, Judges 20:1 says that Israel gathered “from Dan to Beersheba,” and the same wording appears during David’s reign in 2 Samuel 17:11 and 24:2. Here it emphasizes how widely Samuel’s prophetic reputation had spread.

Spirit & Life

The significance of 1 Samuel 3:20 is therefore that God publicly confirmed Samuel as His prophet, and Israel recognized that confirmation. Samuel’s authority did not come from self-promotion or inherited office but from the Lord’s presence, the reliability of the word entrusted to him, and God’s continuing activity through his ministry.

For believers today, the verse cautions against confusing spiritual authority with title, position, or personal claim. Samuel was recognized because God had established him and because his ministry consistently demonstrated faithfulness to God’s word. The pattern does not mean that modern believers should expect prophetic status in the same sense as Samuel, but it does establish a broader principle: genuine spiritual credibility is not manufactured. It develops as God’s servants faithfully handle His word, walk with Him, and demonstrate consistency over time.

1 Samuel 3:21

NKJV

1 Samuel 3:21 Then the LORD appeared again in Shiloh. For the LORD revealed Himself to Samuel in Shiloh by the word of the LORD.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:21 The LORD continued to appear at Shiloh and gave messages to Samuel there at the Tabernacle. (NLT)

Main Idea

The chapter closes with the LORD continuing to reveal Himself to Samuel by His word, reversing the scarcity of revelation announced at the beginning.

Verse Explanation

This verse closes the chapter by showing that Samuel’s encounter with the Lord was not an isolated event. The chapter began with a spiritual crisis: “the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no widespread revelation” (1 Samuel 3:1). By the end of the chapter, that situation has changed. Samuel has been established as a prophet throughout Israel, and the Lord continues revealing Himself to him at Shiloh. Verse 21 therefore brings the chapter full circle. The scarcity of divine revelation is giving way to a renewed season in which God regularly makes His word known through Samuel.

“For the LORD revealed Himself to Samuel” explains what the preceding statement means. God’s appearance at Shiloh was specifically connected with His self-revelation to Samuel.

The phrase also helps explain Samuel’s authority. Verse 20 says that Israel recognized that Samuel had been established as a prophet. Verse 21 tells us why. Samuel’s prophetic authority did not rest merely upon the people's opinion of him. The Lord Himself was repeatedly revealing Himself to Samuel.

“In Shiloh” appears again, reinforcing the connection between Samuel’s ministry and the sanctuary where he had grown up serving. Samuel had begun there as a child performing ordinary responsibilities before the Lord. Now that same location becomes a place where the Lord repeatedly reveals Himself to him.

“By the word of the LORD” is the climactic phrase of the verse and, in many respects, of the entire chapter. The Lord revealed Himself to Samuel specifically through His word.

The phrase also connects directly with the opening verse of the chapter. First Samuel 3:1 said, “the word of the LORD was rare in those days.” Verse 21 closes by saying that the Lord revealed Himself “by the word of the LORD.” The repetition is intentional. The problem introduced at the beginning of the chapter is being answered at the end.

“Then the LORD appeared again in Shiloh” indicates repeated divine revelation. The word “again” is important. Samuel’s encounter with God earlier in the chapter was the beginning of an ongoing relationship in which the Lord continued to reveal Himself.

The text does not tell us exactly what Samuel saw, if anything, during these subsequent revelations. It would therefore go beyond Scripture to reconstruct the physical form of these appearances. The emphasis is not upon what God looked like but upon the fact that He was making Himself known to Samuel.

“In Shiloh” is repeated twice in the verse, which gives the location particular emphasis. Shiloh, in the hill country of Ephraim, had become Israel’s central sanctuary after the conquest of Canaan. Joshua 18:1 says that the congregation of Israel assembled there and set up the tabernacle. By Samuel’s time, the ark of the covenant was there, and Eli and his sons served at the sanctuary.

Spirit & Life

The verse therefore presents both judgment and renewal occurring at Shiloh. Eli’s house is declining, but Samuel is rising. Revelation had become rare, but now the Lord is speaking again. Human unfaithfulness does not prevent God from raising up a faithful servant through whom He will continue His purposes.

For believers today, the verse gives particular weight to the relationship between God and His word. The Lord revealed Himself to Samuel “by the word of the LORD.” Spiritual experience was not separated from divine truth. God made Himself known through what He communicated. While Samuel occupied a unique prophetic office in Israel's history, the underlying principle remains important: we cannot separate knowing God from attending carefully to what He has spoken. The chapter that began with the rarity of God's word ends with its restoration, showing that one of God's great mercies to His people is that He makes Himself known.
