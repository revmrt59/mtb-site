Chapter Study

1 Samuel 3

Verse-by-Verse
1 Samuel 3:1
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:1 Now the boy Samuel ministered to the LORD before Eli. And the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no widespread revelation.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:1 Meanwhile, the boy Samuel served the LORD by assisting Eli. Now in those days messages from the LORD were very rare, and visions were quite uncommon. (NLT)

Main Idea

Young Samuel served the LORD under the guidance of Eli the priest during a time when God rarely spoke to Israel and prophetic visions were uncommon. The spiritual silence sets the stage for the LORD's call to Samuel, through whom He would once again reveal His word to His people.

Verse Explanation

Young Samuel served the LORD under the guidance of Eli the priest during a time when God rarely spoke to Israel and prophetic visions were uncommon. The spiritual silence sets the stage for the LORD's call to Samuel, through whom He would once again reveal His word to His people.

"Now the boy Samuel ministered to the LORD before Eli."

Samuel was the son Hannah had begged the LORD for when she was childless, promising to give him back to the LORD for life (1:11). Once he was weaned, she kept her word and left him at the tabernacle in Shiloh (1:24–28). The Hebrew word for "boy" covers a wide range of ages, from a young child to a young man, and Scripture does not say how old Samuel was here. He had been growing up at the sanctuary for some time, and he "grew in stature, and in favor both with the LORD and men" (2:26).

The word "ministered" means to serve as an attendant. It is regularly used for the work of priests and Levites at the sanctuary. Even as a boy, Samuel wore "a linen ephod" (2:18), a simple priestly garment that marked him as someone set apart for sanctuary duties. His service was "to the LORD," because God was the One he served. It was also "before Eli," meaning under the old priest's oversight and instruction. Samuel's daily work probably included ordinary tasks like opening the doors of the house of the LORD (3:15). This is the same faithful service already set in contrast with Eli's sons, who held the priesthood but "did not know the LORD" (2:12).

"And the word of the LORD was rare in those days;"

"The word of the LORD" means a message God Himself speaks, usually given to a prophet to pass on to His people. It is not human opinion about God. It is God making His will known. The Hebrew word translated "rare" also means precious or valuable. Something scarce becomes precious, and God's word had become scarce in Israel.

"In those days" refers to the closing years of the judges. The book of Judges ends with a summary of that period: "In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). God had not gone completely silent. A man of God had recently brought a message of judgment to Eli (2:27–36). But such messages were unusual. Scripture does not give a reason for the scarcity here. Elsewhere, though, God's silence often accompanies a people or a leader turning away from Him. Years later, when Saul had rejected God's commands, "the LORD did not answer him, either by dreams or by Urim or by the prophets" (28:6). Israel's moral confusion and the corruption at Shiloh make the rarity of God's word unsurprising.

"there was no widespread revelation."

The Hebrew word behind "revelation" means a vision, the kind of revelation God gave to prophets. In those days a prophet was commonly called a "seer" (9:9), because he saw what God showed him. The word translated "widespread" carries the idea of breaking out or spreading abroad. Occasional visions may have come, but none were spreading through the land so that God's people knew He was speaking.

This is the situation into which the LORD would call the boy serving at Shiloh. By the end of the chapter, God's word would again be known throughout Israel through Samuel (3:19–21).

Study Notes

Scripture does not give Samuel's age at the time of his call, but readers have long asked how young he was. The Jewish historian Josephus, writing in the first century AD, said Samuel began to prophesy when he was twelve years old. That figure is tradition, not part of the biblical text, and there is no way to confirm it. It probably reflects a Jewish custom of treating twelve or thirteen as the threshold of religious responsibility, and it may have been influenced by Jesus' visit to the temple at twelve (Luke 2:42). The biblical details point only to a boy no longer a small child. He had been at Shiloh for some years since his weaning, he could carry out sanctuary duties such as opening the doors, and he was old enough to understand and repeat a complex message of judgment. Josephus's figure is a reasonable guess, but it should not be presented as fact.

1 Samuel 3:2
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:2 And it came to pass at that time, while Eli was lying down in his place, and when his eyes had begun to grow so dim that he could not see,(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:2 One night Eli, who was almost blind by now, had gone to bed. (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli, now old and nearly blind, was lying down in his usual place when the events of this night began. His failing eyesight pictures the spiritual dimness of his leadership, since he had not stopped the corruption in his own household.

Verse Explanation

Eli, now old and nearly blind, was lying down in his usual place when the events of this night began. His failing eyesight pictures the spiritual dimness of his leadership, since he had not stopped the corruption in his own household.

"And it came to pass at that time,"

This places the night's events in the time when the LORD's word was rare. The boy Samuel was serving the LORD under Eli, and nothing yet suggested that God was about to break His long silence.

"while Eli was lying down in his place,"

Eli was resting in the spot where he usually slept. The verse does not say where that was, but it was near enough to Samuel that the boy could hear a voice in the night, assume Eli had called, and run straight to him (3:5). Both of them were lying down (3:3), so the call came while the household was asleep.

Eli had served as priest at the LORD's sanctuary at Shiloh for many years. Earlier he is pictured sitting on a seat by the doorpost of the tabernacle (1:9), and Scripture later says he "had judged Israel forty years" (4:18). He was the priest who blessed Hannah when she prayed for a son (1:17), and he received young Samuel into his care when Hannah brought him to the LORD (1:24–28). The old priest and the boy he was raising were now lying down on the same night when God would speak.

"and when his eyes had begun to grow so dim that he could not see,"

Eli's eyesight was failing with age. The words "had begun" show that this was still getting worse. By the time of his death he was ninety-eight, and his eyes had grown so dim that he could see nothing at all (4:15). A man in that condition would depend on others, especially at night, which helps explain why Samuel ran to him so quickly each time he heard his name.

Many interpreters also see Eli's dim eyes as a picture of how poorly he saw spiritual matters. Scripture describes his failing sight as a physical condition, but it fits his leadership. Eli had misread Hannah's silent prayer as drunkenness (1:13–14). He knew about his sons' sins and spoke to them about it (2:22–25), yet he did not stop them or remove them from serving as priests. The judgment the LORD would announce that night grew out of that failure (3:13). The priest who was supposed to watch over Israel's worship could no longer see clearly, in body or in leadership, and God was about to speak to the boy instead.

1 Samuel 3:3
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:3 and before the lamp of God went out in the tabernacle of the LORD where the ark of God was, and while Samuel was lying down,(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:3 The lamp of God had not yet gone out, and Samuel was sleeping in the Tabernacle near the Ark of God. (NLT)

Main Idea

The lamp in the tabernacle was still burning, meaning it was late at night but not yet morning, and Samuel was sleeping somewhere within the sanctuary area where the ark of God was kept. The scene is set in God's own house, where Samuel served but did not enter the restricted areas reserved for priests.

Verse Explanation

The lamp in the tabernacle was still burning, meaning it was late at night but not yet morning, and Samuel was sleeping somewhere within the sanctuary area where the ark of God was kept. The scene is set in God's own house, where Samuel served but did not enter the restricted areas reserved for priests.

"and before the lamp of God went out"

This was the golden lampstand that stood inside the tabernacle, in the room called the Holy Place. God had commanded that it be fueled with pure olive oil and that the priests tend it "from evening until morning before the LORD" (Exodus 27:21). It was lit each evening and burned through the night, so the fact that it had not yet gone out means it was still dark, probably in the early hours before dawn. When morning came, Samuel got up and opened the doors of the house of the LORD (3:15). Many interpreters also see meaning in the lamp itself. Israel's spiritual life was dim, God's word was rare, and the high priest's eyes were failing, but the lamp in God's house was still burning, and God had not abandoned His people.

"in the tabernacle of the LORD"

Joshua had set up the tabernacle at Shiloh after Israel entered the land (Joshua 18:1), and it had remained there through the period of the judges. Shiloh was the place where Israel came to worship and offer sacrifices, as Hannah's family did every year (1:3). The Hebrew word used here often means "temple" or "palace." Because this sanctuary had a doorpost where Eli sat (1:9) and doors that Samuel opened (3:15), it seems that by this time the tent was surrounded by more permanent buildings. Calling it "of the LORD" makes clear whose house it was. This was not Eli's place, and certainly not his sons' place. It belonged to God.

"where the ark of God was,"

The ark was a wooden chest overlaid with gold. Inside it were the stone tablets of the Ten Commandments, the core of the covenant, the binding agreement God had made with Israel at Mount Sinai. Its gold lid, called the mercy seat, had two golden cherubim on top, and God told Moses, "And there I will meet with you, and I will speak with you from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim" (Exodus 25:22). The ark was kept in the innermost room, the Most Holy Place, behind a veil, and only the high priest could enter there, once a year (Leviticus 16:2, 34). Mentioning the ark here shows that this was the place where God had promised to meet with His people and speak to them. In a time when His word was rarely heard, that promise was about to be kept again.

"and while Samuel was lying down,"

Samuel was asleep within the sanctuary area, near enough to Eli that he could hear a voice in the night and run to him (3:5). Exactly where he slept is not stated. He was not lying in the Most Holy Place or beside the ark, since no one but the high priest could enter there. He slept somewhere within the grounds of the LORD's house, where he served day by day. The scene is quiet and ordinary: an old priest resting in his usual place, a boy asleep in God's house, and the lamp still burning. That is when the LORD called him.

Study Notes

Anyone comparing translations will notice a difference here. In Hebrew the verse reads, word for word, "and the lamp of God had not yet gone out, and Samuel was lying down in the temple of the LORD, where the ark of God was." Translations such as the ESV and NIV follow that order and say Samuel was lying down in the temple where the ark was. The NKJV, like the KJV, rearranges the phrases so that "in the tabernacle of the LORD" describes where the lamp was burning. Ancient Jewish interpreters read the verse the same way the NKJV does. They were uneasy with the idea of anyone but a priest lying down inside the sanctuary building itself.

Either way of reading the verse is possible, and neither places Samuel beside the ark. The Hebrew word for "temple" can refer to the whole sanctuary complex. At Shiloh it already included a doorpost where Eli sat (1:9) and doors that Samuel opened (3:15). The phrase "where the ark of God was" identifies which sanctuary is meant, the one housing the ark. It does not mark the exact spot where Samuel slept. He was somewhere within the sanctuary grounds, and Scripture does not specify where.

1 Samuel 3:4
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:4 that the LORD called Samuel. And he answered, "Here I am!"(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:4 Suddenly the LORD called out, “Samuel!” “Yes?” Samuel replied. “What is it?” (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD broke His long silence by calling Samuel by name, and the boy immediately answered, "Here I am!" His quick response shows a willing and attentive heart, even though he did not yet realize who was speaking.

Verse Explanation

The LORD broke His long silence by calling Samuel by name, and the boy immediately answered, "Here I am!" His quick response shows a willing and attentive heart, even though he did not yet realize who was speaking.

"that the LORD called Samuel."

This finishes the sentence begun in verse 3. Samuel was lying down in the LORD's sanctuary at Shiloh, late in the night before the lamp went out, when the LORD spoke. In a time when the LORD's word was rare, these are the first words from God recorded in the chapter. "The LORD" translates God's personal name, the name He made known to Moses as the God who keeps His promises to Israel (Exodus 3:14–15).

The LORD called the boy by name. That shows personal knowledge, the same kind of knowing God expressed to Moses: "I know you by name" (Exodus 33:17). Samuel's name carried its own history. Hannah had named him because, she said, "I have asked for him from the LORD" (1:20). The God who heard a mother's prayer for a son now spoke directly to that son.

"And he answered, 'Here I am!'"

In Hebrew this is one word, hinneni, a simple way of saying, "I'm present and ready." Jacob gave the same answer when God spoke to him "in the visions of the night" and called his name (Genesis 46:2). Moses answered the same way when God called to him from the burning bush (Exodus 3:4). In each case the person is giving his full attention, waiting to hear what will be asked.

Samuel answered at once, even though he was woken in the dark. He did not yet realize it was the LORD speaking. He assumed the old priest Eli needed him and ran to him with the same reply (3:5). His readiness was real, but he still had to learn to recognize the voice that was calling him.

1 Samuel 3:5
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:5 So he ran to Eli and said, "Here I am, for you called me." And he said, "I did not call; lie down again." And he went and lay down.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:5 He got up and ran to Eli. “Here I am. Did you call me?” “I didn’t call you,” Eli replied. “Go back to bed.” So he did. (NLT)

Main Idea

Thinking Eli had called him, Samuel ran to the old priest, but Eli told him he had not called and sent him back to bed. Samuel's eagerness to serve Eli shows his faithfulness, even while the true source of the voice remained unknown to him.

Verse Explanation

Thinking Eli had called him, Samuel ran to the old priest, but Eli told him he had not called and sent him back to bed. Samuel's eagerness to serve Eli shows his faithfulness, even while the true source of the voice remained unknown to him.

"So he ran to Eli and said, 'Here I am, for you called me.'"

Samuel did not wait to be called twice or drag himself out of bed slowly. He ran. It was the middle of the night, and the old priest Eli, whose eyes had grown so dim that he could not see (3:2), might well need help. Samuel was ready to give it. He repeated the same ready reply, "Here I am," that he had just given to the voice, now spoken to the man he served every day.

His mistake was a natural one. The voice had called his name, and the only person nearby who would call for him was Eli. Samuel had no experience of God speaking to him, because "the word of the LORD" had "not yet" been "revealed to him" (3:7). So he took the LORD's call for the voice of his master. His heart was willing, but he did not yet know who was speaking.

"And he said, 'I did not call; lie down again.'"

Eli answered truthfully and simply. He had not called, and he sent the boy back to bed. At this point Eli did not recognize what was happening either. Only after the third call did he realize "that the LORD had called the boy" (3:8). At a time when the LORD's word was rare, neither the young servant nor the old priest first thought that God might be speaking.

"And he went and lay down."

Samuel obeyed without arguing or questioning Eli. He returned to where he had been sleeping in the LORD's sanctuary at Shiloh. Running to Eli when he thought he was called and lying down again when he was told to show the same quiet willingness to serve. His faithfulness in this small, ordinary obedience came before he learned to recognize the voice of the LORD Himself.

1 Samuel 3:6
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:6 Then the LORD called yet again, "Samuel!" So Samuel arose and went to Eli, and said, "Here I am, for you called me." He answered, "I did not call, my son; lie down again."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:6 Then the LORD called out again, “Samuel!” Again Samuel got up and went to Eli. “Here I am. Did you call me?” “I didn’t call you, my son,” Eli said. “Go back to bed.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD called a second time, and again Samuel went to Eli, who once more sent him back. Eli's tender words "my son" show his affection for the boy, and the repeated call reveals God's patience in reaching out to Samuel.

Verse Explanation

The LORD called a second time, and again Samuel went to Eli, who once more sent him back. Eli's tender words "my son" show his affection for the boy, and the repeated call reveals God's patience in reaching out to Samuel.

**"Then the LORD called yet again, "Samuel!""**

Samuel had answered the first call by running to the wrong person, but the LORD did not stop speaking because the boy misunderstood. He called again, and once more He used Samuel's own name. In a time when the LORD's word was rare, God was not waiting for Samuel to understand everything before He spoke. He kept calling until the boy would learn to listen.

**"So Samuel arose and went to Eli, and said, "Here I am, for you called me.""**

For the second time that night Samuel got up from his bed, went to the old priest Eli, and gave the same ready reply, "Here I am." He did not ignore the voice or wait to hear whether it would come again. Even after being sent back once, he answered as promptly as he had the first time, still sure that Eli needed him.

**"He answered, "I did not call, my son; lie down again.""**

Eli's answer is the same as before, with one difference: this time he adds "my son." It was a natural way for an older man to speak to a boy in his care, and it was also how a teacher addressed a pupil, as in "My son, hear the instruction of your father" (Proverbs 1:8). Samuel was not Eli's son by birth, but Eli had raised him in the sanctuary ever since Hannah left him there as a young child (1:24–28). Being awakened twice in the night did not make the old man harsh with the boy. Eli used the same tender address again when he asked Samuel to tell him what the LORD had said (3:16).

The words carry extra weight because of Eli's own sons. Hophni and Phinehas, Eli's corrupt priestly sons, "did not heed the voice of their father" when he warned them (2:25). Samuel, by contrast, came running every time he thought Eli was calling him. The boy who was not Eli's son by birth was the one who listened to him like a son.

1 Samuel 3:7
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:7 (Now Samuel did not yet know the LORD, nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him.)(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:7 Samuel did not yet know the LORD because he had never had a message from the LORD before. (NLT)

Main Idea

This does not mean Samuel was unbelieving or sinful, since he was already faithfully serving the LORD; it means he had not yet personally experienced God speaking to him. Because he had never heard God's voice before, he naturally assumed the call came from Eli, unlike Eli's sons, who "did not know the LORD" because of their wicked disregard for Him.

Verse Explanation

This does not mean Samuel was unbelieving or sinful, since he was already faithfully serving the LORD; it means he had not yet personally experienced God speaking to him. Because he had never heard God's voice before, he naturally assumed the call came from Eli, unlike Eli's sons, who "did not know the LORD" because of their wicked disregard for Him.

"(Now Samuel did not yet know the LORD,"

This comment pauses the story to explain why Samuel kept running to Eli. The LORD had called him twice, and both times Samuel answered "Here I am" and hurried to the old priest (3:4–6). The Hebrew word for "know" often means more than being aware of someone. It describes knowing by personal experience. Samuel already served the LORD faithfully: he "ministered before the LORD" as a child (2:18) and grew "in favor both with the LORD and men" (2:26). What he lacked was the experience of God speaking to him directly. He had never heard the LORD's voice, so he had nothing to compare it with. A voice calling his name in the night sounded like a person, and the only person likely to call him at that hour was Eli.

The same expression appears earlier about Eli's sons, who "did not know the LORD" (2:12). The difference is the word "yet." Hophni and Phinehas were grown priests who refused to honor God. Samuel was a willing young servant whose relationship with God was about to go deeper. Their ignorance came from rebellion; his came from inexperience, and it would soon change.

"nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him.)"

This second half explains what the first half means. "The word of the LORD" is God's spoken message, the very thing that had become so rare in Israel (3:1). The word translated "revealed" means to uncover or open up, as when something hidden is brought into view. Later Scripture uses this same word when it says the LORD had told Samuel something "in his ear" (9:15), which literally means He uncovered Samuel's ear. That uncovering had not yet happened. Samuel could serve in the sanctuary, open its doors, and help the old priest Eli, but he could not make God speak to him. Revelation had to come from God's side.

This is why Samuel needed help. Even Eli did not recognize what was happening until the third call (3:8). Once he did, he told Samuel how to answer (3:9). The "yet" here points forward: the boy who could not recognize the LORD's voice would become the prophet to whom the LORD revealed Himself again and again (3:21).

1 Samuel 3:8
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:8 And the LORD called Samuel again the third time. So he arose and went to Eli, and said, "Here I am, for you did call me." Then Eli perceived that the LORD had called the boy.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:8 So the LORD called a third time, and once more Samuel got up and went to Eli. “Here I am. Did you call me?” Then Eli realized it was the LORD who was calling the boy. (NLT)

Main Idea

When Samuel came a third time, Eli finally understood that it was the LORD who was calling the boy. Though Eli had failed in many ways, he still recognized God's work and was able to guide Samuel toward his calling.

Verse Explanation

When Samuel came a third time, Eli finally understood that it was the LORD who was calling the boy. Though Eli had failed in many ways, he still recognized God's work and was able to guide Samuel toward his calling.

"And the LORD called Samuel again the third time."

This was the third time that night the LORD had spoken Samuel's name, and each time the boy had run to the wrong person. God did not stop calling. He kept speaking until His voice was recognized, and this third call is the one that finally led Eli to understand what was happening.

"So he arose and went to Eli, and said, 'Here I am, for you did call me.'"

Twice already Eli had sent Samuel back to bed, telling him, "I did not call; lie down again" (3:5–6). Yet Samuel got up a third time in the dark and went to the old priest with the same ready reply. His willingness had not worn thin. But he was still mistaken about who was calling. As 3:7 explains, Samuel had not yet received the word of the LORD, so a voice calling his name in the night could only sound to him like Eli's. His words carry some insistence: "you did call me." Samuel was sure he had heard someone speak, and he was right. He simply did not yet know it was the LORD.

"Then Eli perceived that the LORD had called the boy."

The Hebrew word translated "perceived" means to understand or discern. Eli was nearly blind (3:2), and he had shown poor spiritual judgment before, but now he saw what Samuel could not. The boy had heard the voice. The old priest understood whose voice it was. In a time when the word of the LORD was rare (3:1), Eli may have been slow to suspect that God was speaking at all. Many interpreters think that is why it took three calls before he understood.

The wording is significant: the LORD had called "the boy." God was not speaking to the high priest, who lay only a short distance away, but to the young servant in his care. Eli recognized this and did not brush it aside. He told Samuel how to answer if the voice came again (3:9). Eli had failed to restrain his own sons, yet he could still recognize when God was at work and point Samuel toward Him.

1 Samuel 3:9
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:9 Therefore Eli said to Samuel, "Go, lie down; and it shall be, if He calls you, that you must say, "˜Speak, LORD, for Your servant hears.' " So Samuel went and lay down in his place.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:9 So he said to Samuel, “Go and lie down again, and if someone calls again, say, ‘Speak, LORD, your servant is listening.’” So Samuel went back to bed. (NLT)

Main Idea

Eli instructed Samuel to lie down again and, if the voice came, to answer, "Speak, LORD, for Your servant hears." This simple response models the right attitude toward God: a readiness to listen and obey whatever He says.

Verse Explanation

Eli instructed Samuel to lie down again and, if the voice came, to answer, "Speak, LORD, for Your servant hears." This simple response models the right attitude toward God: a readiness to listen and obey whatever He says.

"Therefore Eli said to Samuel,"

This was the third time Samuel had come running to Eli in the night, and this time "Eli perceived that the LORD had called the boy" (3:8). "Therefore" ties Eli's words to that realization. The old priest Eli had been slow to see many things, but he recognized that God was speaking, and he did not keep the moment for himself. He taught the boy how to answer.

"Go, lie down;"

Twice before, Eli had sent Samuel back to bed, telling him, "I did not call; lie down again" (3:5–6). Now the instruction sounds the same, but it comes with a different purpose. Samuel was not going back to sleep. He was going back to wait for the LORD.

"and it shall be, if He calls you,"

Eli spoke carefully. He did not promise that the voice would come again, and he did not claim to know what God would say. He simply prepared Samuel to be ready if the LORD called.

"that you must say, 'Speak, LORD, for Your servant hears.'"

These few words show how a person should come before God. "Speak" is a request. Samuel was not to ask for a particular kind of message or set any conditions. He was to invite the LORD to say whatever He wished.

"Your servant" was a humble way of referring to oneself, used when speaking to someone of higher rank, such as a king. Samuel had already been serving the LORD in the sanctuary (3:1). Now he would learn that a servant's first duty is to listen to his Master's word.

The Hebrew word translated "hears," shama, means more than noticing a sound. It often means to listen with the intention of doing what is said. It is the same word in Israel's great call, "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one!" (Deuteronomy 6:4), which leads directly into the command to love the LORD with all one's heart. The same word explains a sad contrast in the previous chapter. When Eli warned his sons about their sins, "they did not heed the voice of their father" (2:25). They heard Eli's words but refused to obey them. Samuel was being taught to say to God, "I am listening, and I will do what You say." Years later Samuel himself would tell King Saul, "Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, and to heed than the fat of rams" (15:22). There, too, "obey" translates shama. Truly hearing God and obeying Him belong together.

Eli gave Samuel these words without knowing what the LORD would say. The message would concern Eli's own family line and the judgment coming on it (3:11–14). Even so, Eli taught the boy to welcome God's word, whatever it might be.

"So Samuel went and lay down in his place."

Samuel had been woken three times, and each time he had found no one calling him. Yet he did not argue or ask questions. He did exactly what Eli told him and returned to where he had been sleeping within the sanctuary. He went back, now ready for the voice, with an answer prepared.

1 Samuel 3:10
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:10 Now the LORD came and stood and called as at other times, "Samuel! Samuel!" And Samuel answered, "Speak, for Your servant hears."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:10 And the LORD came and called as before, “Samuel! Samuel!” And Samuel replied, “Speak, your servant is listening.” (NLT)

Main Idea

This time the LORD came and stood near Samuel, calling his name twice as a sign of personal urgency, and Samuel answered as Eli had taught him. God's coming was a real, personal appearance, though the passage does not describe whether Samuel saw a visible form.

Verse Explanation

This time the LORD came and stood near Samuel, calling his name twice as a sign of personal urgency, and Samuel answered as Eli had taught him. God's coming was a real, personal appearance, though the passage does not describe whether Samuel saw a visible form.

"Now the LORD came and stood"

In the earlier calls, the LORD simply called (3:4, 6, 8). This time He "came and stood," which means the LORD was personally present with Samuel. This was not a voice Samuel imagined or a thought that came to him in the dark. Scripture uses similar language when "the LORD came down in the pillar of cloud and stood in the door of the tabernacle, and called Aaron and Miriam" (Numbers 12:5). Afterward this encounter is called "the vision" (3:15). Scripture does not say what, if anything, Samuel saw. What it makes clear is that God Himself came to speak with the boy in the LORD's sanctuary at Shiloh.

"and called as at other times, 'Samuel! Samuel!'"

"As at other times" means the LORD called the same way He had called three times before. Each time Samuel had run to Eli, thinking the old priest wanted him. The LORD did not give up on Samuel or turn to someone else. He kept calling until Eli understood what was happening and taught the boy how to answer (3:8–9).

This time, though, the LORD speaks Samuel's name twice. Before, He had called "Samuel!" only once (3:6). God called others this way at decisive moments: "Abraham, Abraham!" at Mount Moriah (Genesis 22:11), "Moses, Moses!" from the burning bush (Exodus 3:4), and "Saul, Saul" on the road to Damascus (Acts 9:4). The repeated name is personal and urgent. God had something important to say, and He was saying it to Samuel.

"And Samuel answered, 'Speak, for Your servant hears.'"

Samuel did not run to Eli this time. He stayed where he was and answered with the words Eli had given him: "Speak, LORD, for Your servant hears" (3:9). The old priest whose eyes had grown dim could still point the boy to God, and his guidance now bore fruit. Samuel's reply leaves out the word "LORD." Scripture does not explain why, and otherwise his answer matches Eli's instruction.

By calling himself "Your servant," Samuel takes the place of one who waits for his master's instructions. He had already been serving the LORD at the sanctuary for years (3:1). Now he offers that service directly to the God who is speaking to him. The Hebrew word for "hears" means more than catching a sound. It means listening with the intent to obey. It is the same word Moses used in "Hear, O Israel" (Deuteronomy 6:4). It is also the word used for Eli's sons, who "did not heed the voice of their father" (2:25). Those priests refused to listen, but the boy told the LORD, "Your servant hears."

Samuel's readiness had been there from the first night, when he answered, "Here I am!" (3:4). Now that readiness was directed to the right One. Samuel offered to listen before he knew what God would say. The message would be a hard one: the LORD's judgment on the house of Eli, the man who had raised him (3:11–14).

1 Samuel 3:13

Verse Explanation

Eli's household would face lasting judgment because his sons had behaved wickedly in their priestly service and Eli had not stopped them. Although Eli had scolded his sons, he never took real action to remove them from their abusive ministry, and as their father and the high priest, he bore responsibility for this failure.

"For I have told him that I will judge his house forever"

The LORD had already warned Eli through the man of God (2:27–36), and what He now tells Samuel confirms that message. "His house" means Eli's family line, the priestly descendants who would serve after him. "Forever" carries a painful irony. Through that same messenger God had said, "I said indeed that your house and the house of your father would walk before Me forever," and then, "But now the LORD says: 'Far be it from Me; for those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed'" (2:30). The family once promised a lasting place in God's service would instead face lasting judgment.

"for the iniquity which he knows,"

Eli could not claim ignorance. He "heard everything his sons did to all Israel" (2:22), and he even told them, "I hear of your evil dealings from all the people" (2:23). On top of that, God had sent a prophet to warn him directly. The judgment did not come without warning. Eli understood both what his sons were doing and what God had said about it, and that knowledge made his failure to act all the more serious.

"because his sons made themselves vile,"

Hophni and Phinehas served as priests at Shiloh, but "the sons of Eli were corrupt; they did not know the LORD" (2:12). When people came to offer sacrifices, the priests' servant took meat for them before the fat had been burned to the LORD, and he threatened force if anyone objected (2:13–16). God's portion was supposed to come first, but these priests treated His offerings as their own food supply. "Therefore the sin of the young men was very great before the LORD, for men abhorred the offering of the LORD" (2:17). They also slept with the women who served at the door of the tabernacle (2:22). The men responsible for leading Israel in worship were teaching the people to despise it.

The NKJV's "made themselves vile" follows the standard Hebrew text, which pictures the sons bringing a curse on themselves. Some ancient manuscripts instead say they were blaspheming God. Either way, their conduct poured contempt on the LORD and on His worship.

"and he did not restrain them."

Eli did speak to his sons. He asked them, "Why do you do such things?" and warned them that if a man sins against the LORD, no one can intercede for him (2:23–25). But "they did not heed the voice of their father" (2:25), and Eli left the matter there. As their father and as the high priest, he had the authority to stop them and remove them from serving at the altar, yet he allowed them to continue. The man of God had named the heart of the problem: Eli honored his sons more than he honored the LORD (2:29), and he too had grown fat on the offerings his sons took (2:29).

The Hebrew word translated "restrain" comes from the same root used for Eli's eyes growing "dim" in verse 2. The priest whose eyes were weakening had also been weak where it mattered most. Scolding his sons was not enough when their sin was dishonoring God before all Israel, and he never took the action that would have stopped them.

Study Notes

This is one of the places where the Hebrew text behind the NKJV differs from what most modern translations print. The standard Hebrew text reads literally "his sons were cursing for themselves," which the NKJV renders "made themselves vile." The ancient Greek translation, the Septuagint, reads instead "speaking evil of God." Translations such as the ESV, NIV, and NRSV follow that reading and say the sons were "blaspheming God."

The two Hebrew readings look very much alike. "For themselves" (lahem) and "God" (elohim) share most of their letters. The Hebrew verb normally means "to curse" and usually takes a direct object, so "cursing for themselves" is an unusual expression. Jewish tradition also preserved lists of "corrections of the scribes." These were places where copyists were said to have softened wording that seemed disrespectful toward God, and some forms of those lists include this verse. If that tradition is accurate, the original wording said the sons cursed God, and later scribes changed it out of reverence.

The question cannot be settled with certainty, and the overall meaning is not greatly affected. Under the Law, treating God with contempt was among the gravest sins (Leviticus 24:15–16). On either reading, Hophni and Phinehas were dishonoring the LORD through their conduct at His altar.

"that the iniquity of Eli's house shall not be atoned for"

"Iniquity" means guilt, the moral crookedness of sin and the blame that comes with it. The Hebrew word translated "atoned for" carries the idea of covering. Under the Law of Moses, the priest offered sacrifices to make atonement for the people's sins so that they could be forgiven (Leviticus 4:20). Eli's family were priests. Their whole calling was to offer the sacrifices that brought atonement for others, yet for their own guilt no atonement would be accepted.

"Sacrifice" refers to animals slaughtered at the altar, and "offering" commonly refers to gifts such as grain offerings. Together they cover every kind of sacrifice the Law provided. No sacrifice of any kind would remove this sentence from Eli's house. "Forever" marks it as permanent. The judgment began in chapter 4 and was finally completed long afterward (1 Kings 2:27).

This oath is God's unchangeable sentence on Eli's family line, given in response to its particular sin. It does not teach that God refuses forgiveness to anyone who has sinned seriously and turns to Him in repentance. The rest of Scripture repeatedly holds out that mercy (1 John 1:9). Here the LORD declared His final judgment on one priestly house that had despised the very sacrifices it was appointed to offer.

A word that "falls to the ground" is one that comes to nothing, like something dropped and left lying in the dirt. Here the LORD Himself made sure that Samuel's words never failed. What Samuel said in God's name happened.

This was the test God had given Israel for recognizing a true prophet: "when a prophet speaks in the name of the LORD, if the thing does not happen or come to pass, that is the thing which the LORD has not spoken" (Deuteronomy 18:22). Samuel passed that test again and again. Years later, a servant described him to Saul in just these terms: "he is an honorable man; all that he says surely comes to pass" (9:6).

The first message Samuel ever delivered was the hard word against Eli's family line. The LORD had promised, "I will perform against Eli all that I have spoken concerning his house, from beginning to end" (3:12), and that judgment was fulfilled in chapter 4 and afterward. The God who does everything He has spoken also kept every word spoken by His prophet from falling to the ground. Samuel's words could be trusted in the same way as God's own word, because Samuel was speaking the LORD's word.

1 Samuel 3:20
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:20 And all Israel from Dan to Beersheba knew that Samuel had been established as a prophet of the LORD.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:20 And all Israel, from Dan in the north to Beersheba in the south, knew that Samuel was confirmed as a prophet of the LORD. (NLT)

Main Idea

Everyone in Israel, from the far north (Dan) to the far south (Beersheba), came to recognize Samuel as a true prophet of the LORD. The period of rare revelation described in verse 1 had ended, and God once again had a trustworthy spokesman for His people.

Verse Explanation

Everyone in Israel, from the far north (Dan) to the far south (Beersheba), came to recognize Samuel as a true prophet of the LORD. The period of rare revelation described in verse 1 had ended, and God once again had a trustworthy spokesman for His people.

"And all Israel from Dan to Beersheba"

Dan was the northernmost city in Israel, near the foot of Mount Hermon. It was once called Laish until the tribe of Dan captured it and renamed it (Judges 18:27–29). Beersheba lay far to the south, at the edge of the dry Negev, a place tied to Abraham and Isaac (Genesis 21:31; 26:33). Together the two towns marked the ends of the land, so "from Dan to Beersheba" became a common way of saying "the whole nation, north to south" (Judges 20:1; 1 Kings 4:25).

That reach matters. During the time of the judges, the tribes were often scattered and divided, and many of the judges seem to have led only part of the nation. Samuel's reputation was not limited to Shiloh or to his home region. People in every part of Israel came to know who he was.

"knew that Samuel had been established"

This recognition grew over time as Samuel grew up and the LORD let none of his words fail (3:19). Israel had a way to test a prophet: if a man spoke in the LORD's name and his words did not come true, the LORD had not sent him (Deuteronomy 18:21–22). Samuel's words kept proving true, and the people drew the right conclusion.

The Hebrew word translated "established" means confirmed, trustworthy, or faithful, something that can be relied on. The same word appears in the man of God's message to Eli, where the LORD promised to raise up "a faithful priest" and build him "a sure house" (2:35). Eli's family would not stand, but God was already raising up a man whose word Israel could trust.

"as a prophet of the LORD."

A prophet was someone God chose to speak His message to His people. Samuel did not take this role for himself or receive it from Eli. He was a prophet "of the LORD," confirmed by the LORD, who kept speaking to him and making his words come true.

Samuel would serve Israel for the rest of his life. He judged the nation "all the days of his life," traveling a yearly circuit to Bethel, Gilgal, and Mizpah (7:15–16). He prayed for Israel, called the people back to the LORD, anointed Saul as the first king (10:1), and later anointed David (16:13). Scripture looks back on him as the start of a new line of prophets. Peter spoke of "all the prophets, from Samuel and those who follow" (Acts 3:24). The boy who once served quietly under Eli became the man through whom God spoke to the whole nation.

1 Samuel 3:21
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:21 Then the LORD appeared again in Shiloh. For the LORD revealed Himself to Samuel in Shiloh by the word of the LORD.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:21 The LORD continued to appear at Shiloh and gave messages to Samuel there at the Tabernacle. (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD continued to appear at Shiloh, making Himself known to Samuel through His word. God's ongoing revelation to Samuel shows that He had restored His communication with Israel through this faithful prophet.

Verse Explanation

The LORD continued to appear at Shiloh, making Himself known to Samuel through His word. God's ongoing revelation to Samuel shows that He had restored His communication with Israel through this faithful prophet.

"Then the LORD appeared again in Shiloh."

The Hebrew says that the LORD "added to appear," meaning He kept appearing. The night the LORD "came and stood" and called Samuel (3:10) was not a single event. It was the beginning of an ongoing pattern. Shiloh was the LORD's sanctuary, where the tabernacle had stood since the days of Joshua (Joshua 18:1). It was also where Eli's corrupt priestly sons had treated the LORD's offerings with contempt (2:17). Yet this was the place where the LORD chose to make Himself known again, through the boy who had been quietly serving there. The verse does not say what form these appearances took. What it emphasizes is that God was present and active at Shiloh once more.

"For the LORD revealed Himself to Samuel in Shiloh"

This explains what the LORD's appearing at Shiloh meant: He revealed Himself to Samuel. The Hebrew word for "revealed" is the same one used earlier, when "Samuel did not yet know the LORD, nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him" (3:7). At that point Samuel was already serving God faithfully, but he had never heard God speak to him personally and could not recognize His voice. That is why he ran to Eli three times. Now the same boy is the one to whom the LORD regularly makes Himself known. What had not yet been revealed to Samuel had become the ordinary pattern of his life, and this is what made him a prophet in the full sense.

"by the word of the LORD."

The chapter began with the statement that "the word of the LORD was rare in those days" (3:1). It ends with the LORD making Himself known to Samuel through that very word. God's silence during the time of the judges had come to an end. The LORD revealed Himself mainly by speaking. Whatever Samuel did or did not see, what he received was God's word, and that word did not stay at Shiloh. The next sentence says, "And the word of Samuel came to all Israel" (4:1). The LORD spoke to Samuel, and through Samuel He spoke again to His people.

Study Notes

This verse celebrates the LORD revealing Himself again at Shiloh, but Shiloh's role as Israel's central sanctuary was about to end. After the ark was captured (4:11), it never returned there. When the Philistines sent it back, it stayed at Kirjath Jearim for many years (7:1–2). By the time of Saul, the priests of Eli's line were serving at Nob (21:1; 22:11). Later Scripture looks back on Shiloh as a warning. Through Jeremiah the LORD told Jerusalem, "But go now to My place which was in Shiloh, where I set My name at the first, and see what I did to it because of the wickedness of My people Israel" (Jeremiah 7:12). Psalm 78:60 likewise says that God abandoned His tabernacle at Shiloh.

Excavations at the site, called Khirbet Seilun, have uncovered a settlement from this period that was destroyed by fire. Many archaeologists date that destruction to roughly the time of the Philistine victory described in chapter 4. Scripture never narrates Shiloh's destruction, and the dating and cause of the burn layer are still debated. Still, the evidence fits the biblical picture of a sanctuary town that lost its place after the ark was captured.
