1 Samuel 3

Verse-by-Verse
1 Samuel 3:1
1 Samuel 3:1
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:1 Now the boy Samuel ministered to the LORD before Eli. And the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no widespread revelation.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:1 Meanwhile, the boy Samuel served the LORD by assisting Eli. Now in those days messages from the LORD were very rare, and visions were quite uncommon. (NLT)

Main Idea

The boy Samuel ministers to the LORD before Eli at a time when the word of the LORD was rare and there was no widespread revelation. This scarcity of God’s word sets the scene for His call to Samuel.

Verse Explanation

The boy Samuel ministers to the LORD before Eli at a time when the word of the LORD was rare and there was no widespread revelation. This scarcity of God’s word sets the scene for His call to Samuel.

“Now the boy Samuel ministered to the LORD before Eli.”

Chapter 3 opens with Samuel at his post. “The boy” translates a word that covers a wide range of ages, from a small child to a young man. Samuel had been growing up at the sanctuary since he was weaned (1:24), so he was no longer a small child, but Scripture does not give his age. He “ministered to the LORD,” serving as an attendant at the sanctuary, as he had been doing since he was left at Shiloh (2:11, 18). His service was “to the LORD,” because God was the One he served, and “before Eli,” under the old priest's oversight.

“And the word of the LORD was rare in those days;”

The narrator describes the spiritual condition of Israel. “The word of the LORD” means a message God Himself speaks, usually through a prophet. It “was rare” in those days. The Hebrew word also means precious or costly; something scarce becomes precious. God had not gone completely silent, since a man of God had recently brought a message to Eli (2:27–36), but such messages were unusual. “Those days” were the closing years of the judges, when “everyone did what was right in his own eyes” (Judges 21:25), and the priesthood at Shiloh was corrupt. Scripture does not give a reason for the scarcity here. Elsewhere, God's silence often accompanies a people's turning away from Him. Years later, when Saul had rejected God's word, “the LORD did not answer him, either by dreams or by Urim or by the prophets” (28:6).

“there was no widespread revelation.”

“Revelation” refers to a prophetic vision, the kind of revelation God gave to prophets. In those days a prophet was called a “seer” (9:9), because he saw what God showed him. “Widespread” carries the idea of breaking out or spreading abroad. Visions were not breaking through to the people. This scarcity of God's word sets the scene for His call to Samuel. By the end of the chapter, God's word will again be known throughout Israel (3:19–21).

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Vocabulary. “Rare” (yaqar) means precious, valuable, or scarce. “Revelation” translates hazon, ‘vision.’ The same word appears in Proverbs 29:18: “Where there is no revelation, the people cast off restraint.” Amos later warned of a famine “Of hearing the words of the LORD” (Amos 8:11). Israel in Samuel's boyhood was living through such a scarcity.

Samuel's age. The Jewish historian Josephus, writing in the first century AD, said Samuel began to prophesy at twelve. That figure is tradition, not Scripture, and cannot be confirmed. The biblical details suggest a boy old enough to carry out sanctuary duties such as opening the doors (3:15) and to understand and repeat a complex message (3:18).

1 Samuel 3:2
1 Samuel 3:2
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:2 And it came to pass at that time, while Eli was lying down in his place, and when his eyes had begun to grow so dim that he could not see,(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:2 One night Eli, who was almost blind by now, had gone to bed. (NLT)

Main Idea

At that time Eli is lying down in his place, his eyes having grown so dim that he cannot see. His failing sight marks Eli’s age and pictures the spiritual dimness of his leadership, since he had not stopped the corruption in his own household.

Verse Explanation

At that time Eli is lying down in his place, his eyes having grown so dim that he cannot see. His failing sight marks Eli’s age and pictures the spiritual dimness of his leadership, since he had not stopped the corruption in his own household.

“And it came to pass at that time,”

This phrase places the night's events in the time when the LORD's word was rare. Nothing yet suggested that God was about to speak.

“while Eli was lying down in his place,”

Eli was resting “in his place,” where he usually slept. The verse does not say where that was, but it was near enough to Samuel that the boy could hear a voice in the night, assume Eli had called, and run to him (3:5). The call came while the household was asleep.

“and when his eyes had begun to grow so dim that he could not see,”

Eli's eyesight was failing with age. “Had begun” shows that it was still getting worse. By the time of his death he would be completely blind (4:15). A man in that condition depended on others, especially at night, which helps explain why Samuel ran to him so quickly. Eli's failing sight also pictures the spiritual dimness of his leadership. He had misread Hannah's prayer (1:13–14) and had not stopped the corruption in his own household (2:22–25). The narrator presents his blindness as a physical condition, but it fits a priest who could no longer see clearly in body or in leadership. God was about to speak to the boy instead.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Vocabulary. “Grow so dim” translates a verb meaning to become faint or weak. It differs from the expression in 4:15, literally ‘his eyes stood’ or were fixed. The narrative traces Eli's decline from failing sight to total blindness.

Interpretive note. The connection between Eli's physical and spiritual sight is a reasonable literary reading suggested by the narrative, not an explicit statement. It should be presented as such.

1 Samuel 3:3
1 Samuel 3:3
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:3 and before the lamp of God went out in the tabernacle of the LORD where the ark of God was, and while Samuel was lying down,(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:3 The lamp of God had not yet gone out, and Samuel was sleeping in the Tabernacle near the Ark of God. (NLT)

Main Idea

Before the lamp of God goes out in the tabernacle of the LORD, where the ark of God is, Samuel is lying down. The scene is set late in the night within the sanctuary, though the text does not place Samuel in the Most Holy Place or beside the ark.

Verse Explanation

Before the lamp of God goes out in the tabernacle of the LORD, where the ark of God is, Samuel is lying down. The scene is set late in the night within the sanctuary, though the text does not place Samuel in the Most Holy Place or beside the ark.

“and before the lamp of God went out”

The time was late at night. “The lamp of God” was the golden lampstand in the Holy Place of the tabernacle. God commanded that it burn with pure olive oil and that the priests tend it “from evening until morning before the LORD” (Exodus 27:21). It burned through the night. The fact that it had not yet gone out means that it was still dark, probably in the hours before dawn. When morning came, Samuel would open the doors of the house of the LORD (3:15). Israel's spiritual life was dim, God's word was rare, and the high priest's eyes were failing, but the lamp in God's house was still burning.

“in the tabernacle of the LORD”

The scene was “the tabernacle of the LORD,” the sanctuary at Shiloh. The Hebrew word here means a temple or palace, as in 1:9.

“where the ark of God was,”

The narrator identifies the sanctuary as the place “where the ark of God was.” The ark, which held the tablets of the law and over which the LORD was enthroned between the cherubim (4:4), was kept in the Most Holy Place. Its mention prepares for the next chapter, where the ark will be carried into battle and captured.

“and while Samuel was lying down,”

“Samuel was lying down,” asleep within the sanctuary precincts. The text does not say exactly where he slept. The language describes the sanctuary complex broadly. It does not mean that Samuel slept inside the Most Holy Place or beside the ark; that inner room was entered only by the high priest, once a year (Leviticus 16:2, 34). Samuel served and slept within the sanctuary precincts without entering the restricted areas reserved for priestly ministry. His precise sleeping location is not identified.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Interpretive note. Some readers have taken the verse to mean that Samuel slept next to the ark. The Hebrew word order places “where the ark of God was” as a description of the sanctuary, not of Samuel's bed. Samuel may have slept in a room attached to the sanctuary or in the courtyard area.

Lamp. Exodus 27:20–21 and Leviticus 24:2–3 required the lamp to burn “continually” from evening until morning. The lamp's still burning may suggest that the priests at least maintained this duty, though the narrator does not comment.

1 Samuel 3:4 that the LORD called Samuel. And he answered, "Here I am!"(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:4 Suddenly the LORD called out, “Samuel!” “Yes?” Samuel replied. “What is it?” (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD calls Samuel, and the boy answers at once, “Here I am!” God breaks the silence of those days by addressing a young servant by name.

Verse Explanation

The LORD calls Samuel, and the boy answers at once, “Here I am!” God breaks the silence of those days by addressing a young servant by name.

“that the LORD called Samuel.”

Into the silence of those days, “the LORD called Samuel.” God broke the scarcity of His word by speaking to a boy. He addressed Samuel by name. The call came not to the high priest or to his sons, but to the young servant faithfully lying in his place.

“And he answered, ‘Here I am!’”

Samuel answered at once: “Here I am!” The words express readiness and availability. They are the same words Abraham spoke when God called him (Genesis 22:1), the same words Moses spoke at the burning bush (Exodus 3:4), and the response Isaiah gave when he heard the Lord's voice (“Here am I! Send me,” Isaiah 6:8). Samuel did not yet know who was calling, but his response was the response of a servant ready to answer.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Textual note. The Hebrew text reads “the LORD called Samuel.” The Septuagint reads ‘the LORD called, Samuel, Samuel!’ anticipating 3:10. The NKJV follows the Hebrew.

Response. “Here I am” (hinneni) is the characteristic response of servants to God's call in Scripture. Its use here shows Samuel's readiness even before he recognized the voice.

1 Samuel 3:7
1 Samuel 3:7
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:7 (Now Samuel did not yet know the LORD, nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him.)(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:7 Samuel did not yet know the LORD because he had never had a message from the LORD before. (NLT)

Main Idea

The narrator explains that Samuel did not yet know the LORD, because the word of the LORD had not yet been revealed to him. This does not mean Samuel was ungodly like Eli’s sons, who “did not know the LORD” (2:12), but that he had not yet experienced God speaking to him personally.

Verse Explanation

The narrator explains that Samuel did not yet know the LORD, because the word of the LORD had not yet been revealed to him. This does not mean Samuel was ungodly like Eli’s sons, who “did not know the LORD” (2:12), but that he had not yet experienced God speaking to him personally.

“(Now Samuel did not yet know the LORD,”

The narrator pauses to explain why Samuel did not recognize the voice. “Samuel did not yet know the LORD.” This does not mean Samuel was ungodly, lacked faith, or had no relationship with God. He had been faithfully ministering to the LORD for years (2:18; 3:1), and he grew in favor with the LORD (2:26). The difference with Eli's sons is significant. They “did not know the LORD” because of their wickedness and disregard for Him (2:12). Samuel did not yet know the LORD in the specific sense explained in the next clause.

“nor was the word of the LORD yet revealed to him.)”

The second clause clarifies the first. “The word of the LORD” had not yet been “revealed to him.” Samuel had not yet personally experienced God speaking to him through prophetic revelation. He knew about the LORD and served Him, but he had not yet heard His voice. That is why he mistook God's call for Eli's. He was about to learn.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Interpretive note. The parenthesis contrasts sharply with 2:12. The same phrase, “did not know the LORD,” describes the wicked rebellion of Eli's sons and the inexperience of the boy Samuel. Context determines the meaning. In Samuel's case, the second clause defines what was lacking: prophetic revelation, not faith or devotion.

Word “yet.” The repeated word “yet” (terem) signals that this condition was about to change.

When the LORD calls the third time and Samuel again comes to him, Eli perceives that the LORD has been calling the boy. Despite his failures, Eli recognizes God’s work and is able to guide Samuel.

“And the LORD called Samuel again the third time.”

God called a third time. The repetition shows His patient persistence.

“So he arose and went to Eli, and said, ‘Here I am, for you did call me.’”

Samuel again went to Eli, this time insisting, “you did call me.” He was sure he had heard someone call.

“Then Eli perceived that the LORD had called the boy.”

Finally “Eli perceived” what was happening. “The LORD had called the boy.” Despite his failures, Eli recognized God's work and was able to guide Samuel. The old priest, whose eyes were dim and whose house was under judgment, still understood enough to recognize the LORD's call. His discernment here balances his earlier misjudgment of Hannah (1:13–14).

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Interpretive note. Eli's recognition of God's call prevents an absolute claim that he could never discern spiritual realities. His failure lay not in total blindness but in his unwillingness to act decisively against his sons' sin (3:13).

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Teaching role. Eli's instruction is one of the few positive acts recorded of him. The failed priest helped the next generation learn to listen to God.

1 Samuel 3:10
1 Samuel 3:10
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:10 Now the LORD came and stood and called as at other times, "Samuel! Samuel!" And Samuel answered, "Speak, for Your servant hears."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:10 And the LORD came and called as before, “Samuel! Samuel!” And Samuel replied, “Speak, your servant is listening.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD comes and stands and calls as at the other times, “Samuel! Samuel!” and Samuel answers, “Speak, for Your servant hears.” The LORD’s coming is a real divine manifestation, though the text does not describe what Samuel saw, and Samuel now answers Him directly.

Verse Explanation

The LORD comes and stands and calls as at the other times, “Samuel! Samuel!” and Samuel answers, “Speak, for Your servant hears.” The LORD’s coming is a real divine manifestation, though the text does not describe what Samuel saw, and Samuel now answers Him directly.

“Now the LORD came and stood”

This time something more happened. “The LORD came and stood.” God's coming and standing describes a genuine divine manifestation, not merely Samuel imagining or sensing a message. The LORD was personally present. The text does not specify whether Samuel saw a visible form. Scripture describes similar moments, such as when “the LORD descended in the cloud and stood with” Moses (Exodus 34:5). Here it simply affirms the reality of God's presence without describing what Samuel saw.

“and called as at other times, ‘Samuel! Samuel!’”

God called “as at other times,” but now He called the name twice: “Samuel! Samuel!” A double call appears at other significant moments in Scripture, as when God called “Abraham, Abraham!” (Genesis 22:11) and “Moses, Moses!” (Exodus 3:4). It expresses urgency and personal address.

“And Samuel answered, ‘Speak, for Your servant hears.’”

Samuel answered as Eli had taught him, with one difference. He said, “Speak, for Your servant hears,” without the word ‘LORD.’ The reason is not given. He may have been overwhelmed, or simply abbreviated the words. What matters is that Samuel now answered God directly, as a servant ready to hear and obey.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Interpretive note. The verse affirms the reality of God's personal communication without inventing physical details. Verse 21 confirms that the LORD continued revealing Himself to Samuel “by the word of the LORD.” Not every later revelation necessarily involved a visible appearance.

Omission of “LORD.” Some interpreters see Samuel's omission of the divine name as reverent hesitancy; others think it carries no special significance. The text does not explain it.

1 Samuel 3:13
1 Samuel 3:13
NKJV

1 Samuel 3:13 For I have told him that I will judge his house forever for the iniquity which he knows, because his sons made themselves vile, and he did not restrain them.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 3:13 I have warned him that judgment is coming upon his family forever, because his sons are blaspheming God and he hasn’t disciplined them. (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD has told Eli that He will judge his house forever for the iniquity Eli knew about: his sons made themselves vile, and he did not restrain them. Eli had rebuked them, but he failed to stop their wickedness or remove them from their priestly service.

Verse Explanation

The LORD has told Eli that He will judge his house forever for the iniquity Eli knew about: his sons made themselves vile, and he did not restrain them. Eli had rebuked them, but he failed to stop their wickedness or remove them from their priestly service.

“For I have told him that I will judge his house forever”

God had already “told” Eli through the man of God (2:27–36). The judgment on his house would be permanent: “forever.”

“for the iniquity which he knows,”

The judgment was “for the iniquity which he knows.” Eli was not ignorant. He knew what his sons were doing (2:22–24).

“because his sons made themselves vile,”

The sons “made themselves vile.” They brought contempt and curse upon themselves through their conduct.

“and he did not restrain them.”

The charge against Eli is that “he did not restrain them.” Eli had verbally confronted his sons (2:23–25), so this does not mean he never rebuked them. His failure was that he did not effectively stop their wickedness or remove them from their priestly service. As high priest he had the authority to do so. His words were not matched by action. A rebuke without consequence left the abuse in place.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Textual question. The Hebrew phrase translated “made themselves vile” (meqallelim lahem) is unusual. An ancient Jewish scribal tradition lists this verse among places where the scribes altered the text out of reverence, and the Septuagint reads ‘were cursing God’ (Elohim instead of lahem). On that reading, the sons' sin was blasphemy against God. The Hebrew text followed by the NKJV reads “made themselves vile.” Either reading describes grave sin; the alternative makes the offense directly against God more explicit.

Vocabulary. “Restrain” translates a verb meaning to rebuke effectively or hold back. The word implies more than speaking; it implies stopping.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Interpretive boundary. The verse should not be turned into a universal teaching that personal repentance or divine forgiveness is impossible for anyone who has sinned grievously. Scripture elsewhere promises forgiveness to those who confess their sins (1 John 1:9). The oath concerns the privileged position of Eli's house, which God would not restore.

Canonical comparison. Hebrews 10:26–27 warns that for those who sin willfully after receiving the truth, “there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins.” The comparison shows that persistent rejection of God's provision is serious, but Hebrews 10 addresses a different situation and should not be read back into this verse.

Samuel lies down until morning and then opens the doors of the house of the LORD, continuing his ordinary duties. He is afraid to tell Eli the vision he has received.

“So Samuel lay down until morning,”

“and opened the doors of the house of the LORD.”

When morning came, Samuel “opened the doors of the house of the LORD.” He continued his ordinary duties. The boy who had just received God's word went about his daily work. His faithfulness in ordinary service had prepared him to hear God, and hearing God did not release him from ordinary service.

Eli asks what the LORD has spoken and binds Samuel with a solemn oath not to hide anything from him. Eli insists on hearing the full word, however painful it may be.

“And he said, ‘What is the word that the LORD spoke to you?’”

Eli asked directly. He knew that the LORD had spoken to Samuel.

“Please do not hide it from me.”

Eli asked Samuel not to hold anything back. He suspected the message concerned him.

“God do so to you, and more also, if you hide anything from me of all the things that He said to you.”

Eli bound Samuel with a solemn oath. “God do so to you, and more also” was a common oath formula calling down God's judgment if the person failed to do what was required. Eli insisted on hearing the full word, however painful it might be. He would not allow Samuel to soften or withhold the message.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Oath formula. “God do so to you, and more also” appears often in Samuel and Kings (for example, 14:44; 20:13; 25:22). The formula implies a curse without stating it, usually accompanied by a gesture.

Verse Explanation

Samuel tells Eli everything and hides nothing, and Eli responds, “It is the LORD. Let Him do what seems good to Him.” Samuel proves a faithful messenger, and Eli submits to the LORD’s judgment, though his submission does not undo his earlier failure.

“Then Samuel told him everything, and hid nothing from him.”

Samuel obeyed. He “told him everything, and hid nothing.” Samuel proved a faithful messenger. His first task as God's prophet was to deliver a hard word to the man he served, and he did it completely. This faithfulness in delivering God's word would mark his whole ministry.

“And he said, ‘It is the LORD. Let Him do what seems good to Him.’”

Eli responded with submission. “It is the LORD” acknowledged that the message came from God. “Let Him do what seems good to Him” accepted God's right to judge. Eli submitted to the LORD's judgment, though his submission did not undo his earlier failure. He accepted the verdict, but he did not repent by removing his sons from office, and the judgment would come as God had said.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Interpretive note. Eli's response has been read as humble acceptance or as passive resignation. The narrator does not comment. His submission is genuine in acknowledging God's sovereignty, but it does not cancel his responsibility for honoring his sons above the LORD (2:29) and failing to restrain them (3:13).

NLT

1 Samuel 3:19 As Samuel grew up, the LORD was with him, and everything Samuel said proved to be reliable. (NLT)

Main Idea

Samuel grows, and the LORD is with him and lets none of his words fall to the ground. God Himself confirms Samuel’s words by bringing them to pass.

Verse Explanation

Samuel grows, and the LORD is with him and lets none of his words fall to the ground. God Himself confirms Samuel’s words by bringing them to pass.

“So Samuel grew,”

Samuel continued to grow, physically and in his role as God's servant.

“and the LORD was with him”

“The LORD was with him.” This phrase marks God's favor and presence. It will be used later of David (18:12, 14). God's presence with Samuel was the source of his authority.

“and let none of his words fall to the ground.”

“None of his words” fell “to the ground.” The image is of words that fail, dropping uselessly. God confirmed Samuel's words by bringing them to pass. When Samuel spoke for God, what he said happened. This was the test of a true prophet in the Law: if a prophet's word does not come to pass, “that is the thing which the LORD has not spoken” (Deuteronomy 18:22). God Himself confirmed Samuel's words by bringing them to pass.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Cross-reference. The image of words not falling to the ground echoes Joshua 21:45: “Not a word failed of any good thing which the LORD had spoken to the house of Israel.” God's faithfulness to His word is the ground of Samuel's prophetic credibility.

Recurring phrase. “The LORD was with him” marks God's presence with Samuel (3:19) and later with David (16:18; 18:12, 14, 28).

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

All Israel, from Dan in the north to Beersheba in the south, knows that Samuel has been established as a prophet of the LORD. His recognition as God’s prophet becomes national.

“And all Israel from Dan to Beersheba knew”

Samuel's reputation spread throughout the nation. “From Dan to Beersheba” describes all Israel from the northern border (Dan) to the southern border (Beersheba). The phrase became the standard way of saying ‘the whole land’ (compare 2 Samuel 24:2).

“that Samuel had been established as a prophet of the LORD.”

All Israel “knew that Samuel had been established as a prophet of the LORD.” “Established” means confirmed or proven trustworthy. The word is related to the word “sure” in 2:35 (“a sure house”). Samuel was recognized as a true prophet. His recognition as God's prophet became national. After years when the word of the LORD was rare, Israel again had a prophet.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Vocabulary. “Established” translates ne'eman, ‘faithful, confirmed, trustworthy.’ The same word describes the “sure house” promised to the faithful priest (2:35). Samuel's establishment as a trustworthy prophet stands in contrast to the fall of Eli's house.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

The LORD appears again in Shiloh and reveals Himself to Samuel there by the word of the LORD. The chapter that began with God’s word rare now ends with His word being revealed again.

“Then the LORD appeared again in Shiloh.”

The chapter ends with God's continued presence. “The LORD appeared again in Shiloh.” God did not speak to Samuel only once. He continued to reveal Himself at Shiloh.

“For the LORD revealed Himself to Samuel in Shiloh by the word of the LORD.”

The way God revealed Himself was “by the word of the LORD.” His self-revelation came through His spoken word to Samuel. The chapter that began with God's word rare (3:1) now ends with His word being revealed again. The LORD had broken the silence. Through the boy who had learned to say, “Speak, for Your servant hears,” God's word would again reach His people. The opening clause of 4:1 continues the thought: “And the word of Samuel came to all Israel.”

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Structure. Verses 19–21 summarize Samuel's establishment and close the first major part of the book (1:1–3:21). The opening clause of 4:1 completes the summary, though the next movement begins there.

Textual note. The Septuagint adds at the end of this verse that Samuel was trusted to be a prophet from one end of the land to the other, and that Eli was very old and his sons continued to act wickedly. The Hebrew text followed by the NKJV is shorter.

Contrast with 3:1. The chapter's frame moves from “the word of the LORD was rare” (3:1) to “the LORD revealed Himself to Samuel in Shiloh by the word of the LORD” (3:21). God's word is the subject of the chapter from beginning to end.
