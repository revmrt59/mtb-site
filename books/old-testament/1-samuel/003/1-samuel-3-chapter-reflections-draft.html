Chapter Reflections

1 Samuel 3

Reflect and Respond
1 Samuel 3:1–14 — Samuel Learns to Hear the LORD’s Voice

Insights

When the Word becomes common to us, we can forget how precious it is. The word translated “rare” in verse 1 is yaqar, a word associated with something costly or precious. Israel was living through a famine of revelation, while we can live with Bibles on our shelves, sermons readily available, and Scripture constantly within reach. Scarcity made the word precious to them; abundance can make it ordinary to us. The danger is not merely that God’s people stop possessing His Word, but that they stop treasuring it.

The lamp had not yet gone out. Eli’s sight was growing dim, revelation was scarce, and judgment was approaching, yet the lamp of God was still burning. The statement refers naturally to the sanctuary lamp, but its placement in the story is striking. Decline was real, but God was not finished. Even in a spiritually dim house, God was already raising up the servant through whom His word would return. There is also a beautiful resonance with the Servant whom Isaiah said would not extinguish even “smoking flax” (Isa. 42:3; Matt. 12:20). Where there is still the faintest burning, Christ does not come to snuff it out. He restores, strengthens, and brings light out of darkness. The lamp at Shiloh had not yet gone out, and neither had God’s purposes for His people.

Near the things of God is not the same as knowing God. Samuel had been born in answer to prayer, dedicated to the LORD, raised in the sanctuary, serving in ministry, and sleeping near the ark, yet the text still says that he “did not yet know the LORD” and that the word of the LORD had not yet been revealed to him. Spiritual heritage, church involvement, Bible knowledge, and ministry can place us near holy things, but none can substitute for God making Himself known to us personally.

God’s repetition is often mercy. Samuel heard the call repeatedly because he did not yet understand, and God did not rebuke him for being slow to recognize His voice. Eli’s house also received repeated warning, first through the man of God in chapter 2 and now through Samuel. In one case repetition trained a willing servant; in the other it warned a resistant leader. God saying something again does not mean His first word was weak. Sometimes repetition is His patience giving us another opportunity to hear and respond.

A failing leader can still do something profoundly right: help the next generation hear God. Eli had failed terribly in his own house, yet when he realized that the LORD was calling Samuel, he did not compete with the boy, protect his status, or make himself indispensable. He directed Samuel toward the LORD. One of the most honorable things an older believer can do is help someone younger recognize God’s voice, even when that person may eventually carry what the older generation could not.

To hear God is to listen with a readiness to obey. When Samuel says, “Speak, for Your servant hears,” the Hebrew shama means more than allowing words to reach the ear. It carries the sense of listening attentively with a readiness to respond and obey. Samuel therefore places himself before God as a servant before he knows what God will say. He is not asking merely for revelation; he is yielding himself to whatever the LORD speaks. Biblical hearing is incomplete when obedience has already been ruled out. We cannot truly say, “Speak, LORD,” while reserving the right to reject what He says.

.

The first word Samuel received was a hard word. God did not begin Samuel’s prophetic ministry with something flattering or easy to repeat. He entrusted him with a message of judgment against the household in which he had been raised. Hearing God cannot be reduced to receiving encouragement. The servant who wants God’s voice must be willing to receive truth that confronts sin as well as truth that comforts and strengthens.

Words without action are not restraint. Eli had spoken to his sons about their sin, but God says that he “did not restrain them” (v. 13). His failure was not ignorance or complete silence. He knew what they were doing and did not use the authority entrusted to him to stop it. There are moments when expressing concern is not obedience. Love, leadership, and responsibility before God may require decisive action rather than repeated words.

Dwelling in the Word

Do I treasure the Word of God, or has constant access made it ordinary to me? What would it look like for me to treat His Word as precious again?

Am I near the things of God, or do I genuinely know Him? Is my faith primarily inherited and familiar, or is God personally revealing Himself to me through His Word?

Can I say, “Speak, LORD, for Your servant hears,” before I know what He will say? Is there any answer from God that I have already decided I do not want to receive?

Is God repeating something because I have been slow to hear or slow to obey? What has He been patiently bringing before me again?

Is there a situation in which I have spoken about what is wrong but have failed to take the action God has given me responsibility to take?

1 Samuel 3:15–21 — Samuel Shares His First Prophecy

Insights

A profound encounter with God should make us more faithful in ordinary things, not less. Samuel spent the night hearing directly from the LORD, and in the morning he got up and opened the doors of the house of the LORD as usual. Revelation did not make ordinary service beneath him. The person who truly encounters God does not come away too important for small responsibilities. Spiritual experiences that are genuinely from God should deepen faithfulness, not produce self-importance.

There can be a night between receiving the Word and speaking it. Samuel knew something devastating before anyone else knew it. He carried the word until morning, opened the doors, and waited until Eli asked. Faithfulness means neither hiding God’s truth nor rushing to speak merely because we know something. Sometimes God gives a word before He gives the moment to speak it, and maturity is able to carry truth without either burying it or forcing it prematurely.

Faithfulness means telling the whole truth, including the part that costs us. Samuel was afraid to tell Eli the vision, but when Eli required an answer, “Samuel told him everything, and hid nothing from him” (v. 18). His first prophetic test was not simply whether he could hear God, but whether he would faithfully carry what he heard. A messenger is not free to soften God’s word to protect himself or the hearer from its weight.

Receiving God’s Word includes receiving the truth we would rather not hear. Eli insists that Samuel hide nothing from him, and after hearing the judgment he answers, “It is the LORD. Let Him do what seems good to Him” (v. 18). His earlier failures remain, but here he does not argue with God’s verdict. There is a deep submission in allowing God to be right about us, even when His Word exposes what we most wish were different.

Samuel’s words stood because they were God’s words. “The LORD was with him” and He “let none of his words fall to the ground” belong together. Samuel did not become authoritative because he developed a powerful voice or built a reputation. His words proved trustworthy because God stood behind what He had spoken. The servant does not make the Word powerful; the Word gives authority to the servant.

God establishes His servants; they do not have to establish themselves. All Israel eventually recognized that Samuel had been established as a prophet of the LORD. The order matters. Samuel did not promote himself into prophetic authority and then ask God to validate it. God called him, was with him, confirmed His word through him, and the people recognized what God had already done. Faithfulness can leave recognition in God’s hands.

The famine ends where it began because the LORD reveals Himself by His Word. The chapter begins at Shiloh with the word of the LORD rare and revelation scarce. It ends at Shiloh with “the LORD revealed Himself to Samuel…by the word of the LORD” (v. 21). The same verb used in verse 7, where the word had not yet been revealed to Samuel, now describes the LORD revealing Himself. God did not merely give Samuel information; through His Word, He made Himself known. A spiritually dry place is not necessarily an abandoned place. God can cause His Word to live again exactly where its absence has been most painfully felt.

Dwelling in the Word

After I meet with God, do I become more faithful in ordinary responsibilities, or do I begin to think some forms of service are beneath me?

Do I know how to carry a difficult truth until the right time to speak it, without either hiding it or forcing it prematurely?

When truth is costly, am I tempted to soften, edit, or omit part of what God has said? What would it mean for me to “hide nothing”?

Can I receive God’s Word when it exposes or corrects me, and still say, “It is the LORD”? Where am I tempted to argue with what He has shown me?

Am I trying to make my own words carry weight, or am I depending on the authority of what God has actually said?

When I come to Scripture, am I only seeking information, or am I expecting the LORD to reveal Himself to me through His Word?
