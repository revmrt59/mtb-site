1 Samuel 4:1

NLT

Main Idea

The word of Samuel comes to all Israel, and Israel goes out to battle against the Philistines, camping beside Ebenezer while the Philistines camp at Aphek. The opening clause closes the account of Samuel’s establishment as prophet, and the rest of the verse sets the scene for a new crisis.

Verse Explanation

The word of Samuel comes to all Israel, and Israel goes out to battle against the Philistines, camping beside Ebenezer while the Philistines camp at Aphek. The opening clause closes the account of Samuel’s establishment as prophet, and the rest of the verse sets the scene for a new crisis.

“And the word of Samuel came to all Israel.”

This opening clause looks back before it looks forward. Chapter 3 ended with the LORD revealing Himself to Samuel at Shiloh, and with all Israel “from Dan to Beersheba” recognizing him as a prophet (3:20–21). Now “the word of Samuel” reaches the whole nation. In context this is not Samuel's private opinion but the word of the LORD spoken through him, since the LORD “let none of his words fall to the ground” (3:19). After years in which “the word of the LORD was rare” (3:1), God again had a voice in Israel. That is the setting for what follows: Israel now had a prophet through whom God was speaking, yet in the crisis of this chapter no one is said to ask him what the LORD wanted.

“Now Israel went out to battle against the Philistines,”

The narrative turns abruptly to war. The Philistines were Israel's most dangerous enemy in this period, settled on the southern coastal plain and organized around five cities (6:17). They had already dominated Israel for a generation; the book of Judges says the LORD had delivered Israel “into the hand of the Philistines for forty years” (Judges 13:1). Scripture does not say who started this particular conflict or whether Israel went out at God's direction. The wording simply says “Israel went out,” and the absence of any inquiry of the LORD becomes significant in verse 3.

“and encamped beside Ebenezer; and the Philistines encamped in Aphek.”

The two armies faced each other at the edge of the hill country. “Aphek” was a town on the coastal plain, where the Philistines gathered their forces again many years later before the battle in which Saul died (29:1). “Ebenezer” was the Israelite campsite nearby. The name means ‘stone of help,’ and the reader may notice the irony that Israel camped at a place bearing that name and received no help. The name is used here by anticipation; in 7:12 Samuel sets up a stone and calls it Ebenezer after the LORD gives Israel victory, saying, “Thus far the LORD has helped us.” The place of defeat in this chapter will become, by God's mercy, a name for His help.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Structure. Verse 1 stands at the hinge between two major parts of the book. The opening clause closes the account of Samuel's establishment as prophet (3:19–21), while the rest of the verse opens the ark narrative that runs through 7:17. Samuel himself does not appear again until 7:3.

Textual note. The Septuagint has a longer opening, adding that in those days the Philistines gathered to make war against Israel. This would make the Philistines the aggressors. The Hebrew text followed by the NKJV is shorter and says only that Israel went out. The difference does not alter the main point: the narrative records no seeking of the LORD before the battle.

Geography. Aphek is commonly identified with Tel Aphek near the source of the Yarkon River, on the route along the coastal plain. Ebenezer's site is uncertain; Izbet Sartah, a small hill village a few miles east of Aphek, has been proposed. Shiloh lay roughly twenty miles to the east in the hill country, which explains why the elders could send for the ark and why a runner could reach Shiloh the same day (4:12).

1 Samuel 4:2 The Philistines attacked and defeated the army of Israel, killing 4,000 men. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Philistines draw up in battle array, and Israel is defeated, losing about four thousand men in the field. This first defeat raises the question of why the LORD has allowed it.

Verse Explanation

The Philistines draw up in battle array, and Israel is defeated, losing about four thousand men in the field. This first defeat raises the question of why the LORD has allowed it.

“Then the Philistines put themselves in battle array against Israel.”

The Philistines took the initiative on the field. The expression “put themselves in battle array” describes drawing up troops in ordered battle lines. The Philistines were experienced and well organized, and later in the book they controlled the working of iron so tightly that Israel had no blacksmith of its own (13:19).

“And when they joined battle, Israel was defeated by the Philistines,”

The clash came, and Israel lost. The verb translated “defeated” means to be struck down or routed. It is the same verb the elders use in the next verse when they ask why the LORD has “defeated” them. The narrator reports a military defeat; the elders will correctly recognize that something more than military weakness lay behind it.

“who killed about four thousand men of the army in the field.”

The losses were heavy for a tribal militia. “In the field” means on the open battleground, in contrast to the camp to which the survivors returned (4:3). The number prepares for the far greater slaughter of verse 10, where thirty thousand fall. The first defeat was a warning. Israel's response to it would reveal what the people trusted.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Numbers. The Hebrew word eleph normally means ‘thousand,’ but it can also refer to a clan or a military unit drawn from a clan. Some scholars therefore suggest that numbers such as “four thousand” in battle reports may count units rather than individual soldiers. This is a proposal, not a settled conclusion, and the NKJV's rendering represents the ordinary meaning of the word. Either way, the narrative presents a serious defeat followed by a much larger one.

Literary pattern. Defeat followed by a question of why it happened recalls Joshua's response after the defeat at Ai, when he tore his clothes and fell on his face “before the ark of the LORD” (Joshua 7:6). The comparison sharpens the contrast in 1 Samuel 4:3: Joshua brought his question to God; the elders brought the ark to the battle.

Main Idea

The elders rightly ask why the LORD has defeated them, but instead of seeking Him they decide to bring the ark of the covenant from Shiloh so that it may save them from their enemies. They treat the ark as a means of securing victory, and the text records no command from the LORD to bring it.

Verse Explanation

The elders rightly ask why the LORD has defeated them, but instead of seeking Him they decide to bring the ark of the covenant from Shiloh so that it may save them from their enemies. They treat the ark as a means of securing victory, and the text records no command from the LORD to bring it.

“And when the people had come into the camp, the elders of Israel said,”

The beaten army returned to its camp at Ebenezer, and the leaders met to decide what to do. “The elders of Israel” were the heads of families and tribes, the recognized leaders of the people in a time with no king. Their decision would govern the whole nation's response.

“Why has the LORD defeated us today before the Philistines?”

This is the right question. The elders did not blame bad tactics or Philistine weapons. They recognized that “the LORD” had allowed this defeat, since Israel's battles were never merely human contests (compare Deuteronomy 20:3–4). Their theology at this point was sound: victory and defeat were in God's hands. But they asked the question without waiting for an answer. No prayer, sacrifice, or inquiry of the LORD follows, and Samuel, through whom the word of the LORD was now coming to all Israel (4:1), is never consulted.

“Let us bring the ark of the covenant of the LORD from Shiloh to us,”

Instead of seeking the LORD, the elders proposed to bring His ark. “The ark of the covenant of the LORD” was the gold-covered chest kept in the Most Holy Place of the tabernacle at Shiloh. It held the tablets of the law, and above it, between the cherubim on its cover, the LORD had promised to meet with His people and speak to them (Exodus 25:22). It was the most sacred object in Israel. In Israel's past the ark had gone before the people at the Jordan and around Jericho at God's command (Joshua 3:11; 6:6). Here there is no command. The elders' plan is their own.

“that when it comes among us it may save us from the hand of our enemies.”

The purpose clause exposes the problem. The elders expected the ark to accomplish what only the LORD could do. They wanted the symbol of His presence without submitting to the One whose presence it represented. The ark was not an empty symbol; it truly belonged to God's holy presence among His people. But God cannot be carried into battle and made to fight on demand. Israel had forgotten that the presence of the LORD meant His holiness as well as His help, and that the house at Shiloh was under His announced judgment (2:30–34; 3:11–14). Psalm 78 later interprets this whole episode as the LORD giving “His strength into captivity, And His glory into the enemy's hand” (Psalm 78:61).

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Translation question. The Hebrew verb behind “it may save us” is masculine singular and can be read either as “it may save us” (referring to the ark) or “He may save us” (referring to the LORD). The NKJV, along with most translations, takes the ark as the subject, which fits the flow of the sentence (“when it comes among us”). Even if the LORD is meant, the elders' plan still assumes that bringing the ark will guarantee His action.

Historical background. Ancient Near Eastern armies commonly carried images or emblems of their gods into battle as a pledge of divine help. The elders' plan resembles that practice. Israel had earlier carried the ark at God's command, but Numbers 14:42–45 records the opposite lesson: when the people went up presumptuously against the LORD's word, “neither the ark of the covenant of the LORD nor Moses departed from the camp,” and they were driven back (Numbers 14:44).

Cross-reference. Contrast Judges 20:26–28, where Israel wept, fasted, offered sacrifices, and “inquired of the LORD” while the ark was among them, and then received an answer before going to battle. The elders in 1 Samuel 4 skip every one of these steps.

Verse Explanation

The people bring from Shiloh the ark of the covenant of the LORD of hosts, who dwells between the cherubim, and Eli’s sons Hophni and Phinehas come with it. The ark was truly bound up with God’s holy presence among His people, yet it arrives in the care of the very priests under His judgment.

“So the people sent to Shiloh,”

The elders' proposal was carried out at once. Messengers went back to “Shiloh,” where the tabernacle had stood since the days of Joshua (Joshua 18:1). The ark would now leave its place in the Most Holy Place, where only the high priest entered, and be carried to an army camp.

“that they might bring from there the ark of the covenant of the LORD of hosts, who dwells between the cherubim.”

The narrator gives the ark its fullest title, and the title itself rebukes the plan. “The LORD of hosts” presents God as commander of heaven's armies and ruler over all powers. The title appears in the book's opening chapter in connection with worship at Shiloh (1:3), and David will later face Goliath in this name (17:45). The God whose ark Israel was sending for already commanded all the armies there are; He did not need to be fetched.

“Who dwells between the cherubim” describes the LORD as enthroned above the golden cherubim on the ark's cover (Exodus 25:18–22). The ark was understood as the footstool of His throne (1 Chronicles 28:2; Psalm 132:7). The phrase speaks of real presence and real majesty. That is precisely why the elders' plan was dangerous: they were handling the throne of the holy King as if it were a battle charm.

“And the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, were there with the ark of the covenant of God.”

The final clause names the men who came with the ark. Eli's sons were priests, and priests were responsible for the ark, but these two were the men the narrator called “corrupt” and said “did not know the LORD” (2:12). The LORD had already announced that both would die “in one day” (2:34). The sacred ark came into the camp under the care of the very priests whose house was under God's judgment. The reader knows how this must end, even though Israel did not.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Divine title. The Hebrew phrase translated “who dwells between the cherubim” is literally ‘who sits (or is enthroned) on the cherubim.’ The same description appears in 2 Samuel 6:2 when David brings the ark to Jerusalem, and in Psalm 80:1 and Psalm 99:1. The expression draws on the imagery of a royal throne flanked by winged figures, adapted in Israel to the invisible LORD, who was never represented by an image.

Eli's absence. Eli himself does not go with the ark; he remains at Shiloh (4:13). Scripture does not say whether he approved the decision. His trembling heart in verse 13 suggests that he feared what might happen to the ark.

Name distinction. This Phinehas, Eli's son, is a different man from Phinehas the son of Eleazar, Aaron's grandson, who served before the ark in Judges 20:28 (compare Numbers 25:7–13).

1 Samuel 4:5 When all the Israelites saw the Ark of the Covenant of the LORD coming into the camp, their shout of joy was so loud it made the ground shake! (NLT)

Main Idea

When the ark of the covenant of the LORD comes into the camp, all Israel shouts so loudly that the earth shakes. Their celebration expresses confidence that the ark will secure victory, a confidence the coming defeat will expose as misplaced.

Verse Explanation

When the ark of the covenant of the LORD comes into the camp, all Israel shouts so loudly that the earth shakes. Their celebration expresses confidence that the ark will secure victory, a confidence the coming defeat will expose as misplaced.

“And when the ark of the covenant of the LORD came into the camp,”

The ark arrived from Shiloh, and the camp was transformed. The narrator again uses the full name, “the ark of the covenant of the LORD,” keeping before the reader whose ark this was.

“all Israel shouted so loudly that the earth shook.”

The shout was a battle cry and a cry of celebration combined. “All Israel” emphasizes that the whole army joined in. The sound was so great that “the earth shook,” an expression that describes the noise as overwhelming. The scene recalls Jericho, where the people “shouted with a great shout” and the wall fell (Joshua 6:20). But at Jericho the people followed the LORD's command, and the shout followed obedience. Here the shout expressed confidence in the ark's arrival rather than in the word of the LORD. Loud celebration is not the same as God's approval. Within a few verses this same army would be fleeing.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Vocabulary. The noun and verb for shouting (teru'ah, rua') are used of war cries, of trumpet blasts, and of shouts of joy in worship. The same word is used when David brings the ark to Jerusalem “with shouting and with the sound of the trumpet” (2 Samuel 6:15). The word itself is neither good nor bad; the setting determines its meaning. In this chapter the shout is premature confidence.

Literary irony. The verb translated “shook” describes a violent stirring or uproar. In verse 14 the city of Shiloh will also be in an uproar, but there the noise will be an outcry of grief. The narrative moves from one great noise to another.

1 Samuel 4:6 “What’s going on?” the Philistines asked. “What’s all the shouting about in the Hebrew camp?” When they were told it was because the Ark of the LORD had arrived, (NLT)

Main Idea

The Philistines hear the great shout from the camp of the Hebrews and learn that the ark of the LORD has arrived. Israel’s celebration carries across to the enemy camp.

Verse Explanation

The Philistines hear the great shout from the camp of the Hebrews and learn that the ark of the LORD has arrived. Israel’s celebration carries across to the enemy camp.

“Now when the Philistines heard the noise of the shout, they said,”

The shout carried across the short distance between Ebenezer and Aphek. The Philistines heard it before they understood it. The narrator now shows the scene from their side.

“What does the sound of this great shout in the camp of the Hebrews mean?”

“Hebrews” is the name foreigners commonly used for Israelites in the Old Testament. Potiphar's wife used it of Joseph in Egypt (Genesis 39:14), and the Philistines use it repeatedly in 1 Samuel (13:19; 14:11; 29:3). On Philistine lips it carries a tone of contempt for a people they regarded as subjects. The Philistines expected a beaten army to be silent, so the sudden roar puzzled them.

“Then they understood that the ark of the LORD had come into the camp.”

The Philistines grasped what had happened, probably through scouts or reports. Israel's celebration had announced its plans to the enemy. They now knew that Israel had brought the ark of its God into the camp, and that knowledge filled them with fear.

1 Samuel 4:7
1 Samuel 4:7
NKJV

1 Samuel 4:7 So the Philistines were afraid, for they said, "God has come into the camp!" And they said, "Woe to us! For such a thing has never happened before.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 4:7 they panicked. “The gods have come into their camp!” they cried. “This is a disaster! We have never had to face anything like this before! (NLT)

Main Idea

The Philistines are afraid, crying that God has come into the camp and that nothing like this has ever happened before. They sense that they now face something more than an army.

Verse Explanation

The Philistines are afraid, crying that God has come into the camp and that nothing like this has ever happened before. They sense that they now face something more than an army.

“So the Philistines were afraid, for they said, ‘God has come into the camp!’”

The Philistines reacted with dread. To them the arrival of the ark meant the arrival of Israel's God, because peoples of that time believed that a god was present with his sacred image or emblem. Their cry, “God has come into the camp,” was more accurate than they knew in one sense: the ark truly belonged to the presence of the LORD. Yet the coming battle would show that God's presence does not mean God is at the disposal of those who carry His ark.

The Philistines' fear has an older background. When Israel crossed the Red Sea, Moses sang that “Sorrow will take hold of the inhabitants of Philistia” (Exodus 15:14). The reputation of Israel's God had spread among the nations, as Rahab also testified at Jericho (Joshua 2:10–11).

“And they said, ‘Woe to us! For such a thing has never happened before.’”

“Woe to us” is a cry of alarm and lament. The Philistines had fought Israel before, but they had never faced Israel with its God in the camp in this way. In their eyes the battle had changed from a contest between armies into a contest with a god. Their speech continues into the next verse.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Grammar. In 4:7 the Hebrew uses the general word for God, Elohim, with a singular verb (“God has come”), but in 4:8 the Philistines speak of “these mighty gods,” using plural forms. The shift may reflect the Philistines' own polytheistic way of thinking, in which they understood Israel's deity in terms of their own many gods. Some translations render 4:7 as ‘a god has come into the camp.’ The NKJV's “God” is a legitimate rendering, but the speakers are pagans, and their words should be read as their perception rather than as a theological statement endorsed by Scripture.

Speaker and narrator. The Philistines' speech is recorded, not endorsed. Their fear acknowledges that Israel's God is powerful, but they understand Him through their own categories.

Verse Explanation

In their alarm the Philistines ask who will deliver them from “these mighty gods,” who struck the Egyptians with plagues in the wilderness. Their words recall Israel’s deliverance from Egypt in confused terms shaped by their own belief in many gods, and Scripture records their speech without endorsing it.

“Woe to us! Who will deliver us from the hand of these mighty gods?”

The Philistines repeat their cry of alarm and ask a desperate question: who could rescue them from such power? They speak of “these mighty gods” in the plural. They thought in terms of many gods, and they assumed that Israel had several. Scripture records their words without accepting their theology. Israel's faith was in one God, the LORD, and the ark represented His presence alone.

“These are the gods who struck the Egyptians with all the plagues in the wilderness.”

The Philistines had heard of the plagues on Egypt, the most famous display of the LORD's power against a nation and its gods (Exodus 12:12). Their memory, however, is confused. The plagues fell in Egypt, not “in the wilderness,” where Israel traveled afterward. The Philistines blended what they had heard into a single frightening report. Their words show that the LORD's acts in Egypt had become a story told among other nations, which was part of God's purpose: “the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD” (Exodus 7:5). Later, the Philistine priests will again recall Egypt and warn against hardening their hearts “as the Egyptians and Pharaoh hardened their hearts” (6:6). Here, however, the memory produces terror without repentance.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Textual and interpretive note. The Septuagint reads ‘with every plague and in the wilderness,’ separating the plagues from the wilderness. Some interpreters adopt that reading; others suggest that “wilderness” loosely refers to the region bordering Egypt or to the events at the Red Sea. The simplest explanation is that the Philistines' account is garbled, and the narrator reports it faithfully. Their words should not be used to teach that the plagues occurred in the wilderness or that Israel worshiped many gods.

1 Samuel 4:9

NKJV

1 Samuel 4:9 Be strong and conduct yourselves like men, you Philistines, that you do not become servants of the Hebrews, as they have been to you. Conduct yourselves like men, and fight!"(NKJV)

1 Samuel 4:9 Fight as never before, Philistines! If you don’t, we will become the Hebrews’ slaves just as they have been ours! Stand up like men and fight!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Instead of surrendering, the Philistines urge one another to be strong and fight like men, so that they will not become servants of the Hebrews as the Hebrews had been to them. Their fear drives them to fight harder.

Verse Explanation

Instead of surrendering, the Philistines urge one another to be strong and fight like men, so that they will not become servants of the Hebrews as the Hebrews had been to them. Their fear drives them to fight harder.

“Be strong and conduct yourselves like men, you Philistines,”

The Philistines' fear did not lead them to surrender. Their commanders, or the soldiers among themselves, urged each other to courage. “Be strong” is a call to take heart. “Conduct yourselves like men” means to act with courage and determination, to show themselves real fighting men in the face of danger.

“that you do not become servants of the Hebrews, as they have been to you.”

The motive was fear of reversal. The Philistines had dominated Israel for years (Judges 13:1). If they lost now, they feared, the roles would be reversed and they would become the servants of the people they had oppressed. The words “as they have been to you” confirm that Israel was living under Philistine oppression in this period.

“Conduct yourselves like men, and fight!”

The repeated command closes the speech. Fear drove the Philistines to fight harder. The ark's arrival, which Israel thought would secure victory, instead stirred the enemy to greater resolve. Israel had expected the ark to work for them automatically; the Philistines, who feared it, simply fought.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Literary note. The Philistines' speech (4:7–9) moves from panic to resolve. The narrator gives far more space to the enemy's words than to any Israelite prayer, of which there is none. The absence of any appeal to the LORD in Israel's camp stands out against the Philistines' urgent speech.

Historical background. The Philistines appear in Egyptian records as part of the ‘Sea Peoples’ who settled on the coast of Canaan in the twelfth century BC. Their military organization and their control of iron working gave them an advantage over Israel's tribal levies (13:19–22).

NKJV

Main Idea

The Philistines fight, Israel is defeated, and every man flees to his tent, with thirty thousand foot soldiers falling. The ark’s presence does not prevent a far greater slaughter than the first defeat.

Verse Explanation

The Philistines fight, Israel is defeated, and every man flees to his tent, with thirty thousand foot soldiers falling. The ark’s presence does not prevent a far greater slaughter than the first defeat.

“So the Philistines fought, and Israel was defeated,”

The battle is reported briefly. The Philistines did what they had urged one another to do, and Israel lost again. The ark was in Israel's camp, yet Israel was defeated. Its presence did not guarantee victory, because the LORD was not bound to fight for a people who had not sought Him and who carried His ark under the care of priests under His judgment.

“and every man fled to his tent.”

This expression means that Israel's army broke apart and the men fled to their homes. “His tent” reflects the older language of Israel's nomadic past, when homes were tents. The army did not merely retreat; it disbanded.

“There was a very great slaughter, and there fell of Israel thirty thousand foot soldiers.”

The second defeat was far worse than the first. “A very great slaughter” stresses the scale of the disaster. “Thirty thousand foot soldiers” fell, compared with about four thousand in the first battle (4:2). Israel had brought the ark to turn defeat into victory; instead, defeat became catastrophe. Psalm 78 remembers the day in mournful terms: the LORD “gave His people over to the sword,” and “Their priests fell by the sword” (Psalm 78:62, 64).

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Vocabulary. “Foot soldiers” translates a word meaning ‘men on foot,’ that is, infantry. Israel did not yet have chariots, while the Philistines did (13:5).

Numbers. As with verse 2, some scholars propose that eleph may refer to military units in this context. The NKJV renders the ordinary meaning, and the narrative's contrast between the two defeats remains clear on either reading.

Theological observation. Israel's defeat is the unfolding of God's announced judgment, not a defeat of God. The LORD had told Eli that he would “see an enemy in My dwelling place” (2:32), and Psalm 78:59–61 attributes the disaster to God's anger at Israel's unfaithfulness.

“Also the ark of God was captured;”

This is the climax of the battle report and the darkest line in the chapter. The sacred ark, which the elders had brought so that it might save them (4:3), was seized by the enemy. “The ark of God” is the name the narrator uses from here through most of the following chapters. It had never been taken in Israel's history. For Israel this was unthinkable: the throne of the LORD of hosts was in Philistine hands.

The capture of the ark was not a defeat of God. It was the LORD giving up what Israel had tried to use. Psalm 78 says that He “delivered His strength into captivity, And His glory into the enemy's hand” (Psalm 78:61). Chapter 5 will show that the LORD was fully able to defend His own honor without any Israelite army.

“and the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, died.”

The second half of the verse reports the fulfillment of God's word. The LORD had told Eli through a man of God, “in one day they shall die, both of them” (2:34), and He had confirmed the judgment to Samuel (3:11–14). Now both sons die on the same day, in the same battle where the ark they had carried was lost. The priests who had treated the LORD's offerings with contempt (2:17) died beside the ark they had presumed to handle.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Fulfillment. The deaths of Hophni and Phinehas fulfill the specific sign of 2:34. The wider judgment on Eli's house continued beyond this day: the massacre of the priests at Nob (22:17–20) and the removal of Abiathar from the priesthood under Solomon, which 1 Kings 2:27 says fulfilled “the word of the LORD which He spoke concerning the house of Eli at Shiloh.”

Shiloh. First Samuel does not narrate the destruction of Shiloh, but Jeremiah 7:12–14 and Psalm 78:60 indicate that the sanctuary there suffered a devastating judgment. Jeremiah warned the people of his own day not to trust in the temple as Israel had trusted in the ark: “go now to My place which was in Shiloh, where I set My name at the first, and see what I did to it” (Jeremiah 7:12).

The runner apparently passed Eli without stopping and told his news in the town. Shiloh broke into a great outcry of grief. The town had lost its sons in battle, and the sanctuary that gave Shiloh its identity had lost the ark. Eli heard the sound before he heard the news.

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Textual note. The Hebrew text has an unusual written form at “by the wayside.” The consonants written in the text appear to read differently from the traditional reading that the scribes marked for pronunciation, which means ‘beside the road.’ The NKJV follows the traditional reading. (Technical & Expository Notes)

It comes from the same root as the word for ‘glory’ or ‘honor.’ The LORD had accused Eli of honoring his sons above Him and of making themselves fat with the best of Israel's offerings (2:29). The man who failed to give the LORD His proper weight of honor died because of his own weight. In verse 21 the same root appears again in the name Ichabod, ‘no glory.’

“And he had judged Israel forty years.”

The verse closes with a summary of Eli's career. He had served as judge as well as priest for “forty years,” a full generation of leadership. That long service ended on the day the ark was lost. The narrator does not condemn Eli here, but the reader knows that his failure to restrain his sons contributed to the disaster that killed him.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Hebrew wordplay. The adjective “heavy” (kaved) belongs to the same root as kavod (‘glory, honor’) and the verb in 2:29–30, where the LORD says, “those who honor Me I will honor.” The root recurs at important points in the book, including 2:29–30, 4:18, 4:21–22, 6:5, and 15:30. This is a repetition of Hebrew vocabulary, not merely an English association, and it works as a literary link rather than a hidden code.

Textual note. The Septuagint reads “twenty years” instead of “forty years.” The NKJV follows the Hebrew text. Forty is also a common round figure for a generation in Scripture.

Eli as judge. Eli is the only priest in the book who is said to have judged Israel. He bridges the period of the judges and the ministry of Samuel, who will also judge Israel (7:15).

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Name meaning. The name Ichabod ('i-kavod) is usually explained as ‘no glory’ (taking 'i as a negative) or ‘Where is the glory?’ (taking it as a question). Either way, the second element is kavod, ‘glory,’ the same root as “heavy” in 4:18 and “honor” in 2:29–30. The narrator draws the threads together: Eli honored his sons above the LORD, Eli was heavy, and his grandson is named ‘no glory.’

Vocabulary. The verb galah (“departed”) is the ordinary word for going into exile. Hosea uses it of the false glory of the calf at Beth Aven: “its glory has departed from it” (Hosea 10:5). In Phinehas's wife's lament it is the true glory of Israel that has gone into exile.

Canonical comparison. Ezekiel later sees “the glory of the LORD” depart from the temple before its destruction (Ezekiel 10:18). That vision belongs to a different setting and should not be read back into this verse, but the comparison shows that Scripture repeatedly treats God's presence as something His people can lose through persistent unfaithfulness.

Study Notes

(Technical & Expository Notes)

Interpretive boundary. The mother's lament is a true expression of lost honor and the captured ark, but it does not mean that God became powerless, ceased to be present everywhere, or permanently rejected Israel. Chapters 5–7 show the LORD acting in Philistine territory, the ark returning, and Israel lamenting after the LORD (7:2).

Literary frame. The chapter begins with the word of the LORD coming to all Israel through Samuel (4:1) and ends with a dying woman's cry that the glory has departed. In between, Israel neglected the word that was available and trusted in the ark instead. The contrast frames the whole account.
