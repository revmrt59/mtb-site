1 Samuel 4:1
“And the word of Samuel came to all Israel. Now Israel went out to battle against the Philistines, and encamped beside Ebenezer; and the Philistines encamped in Aphek.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
Samuel’s word now reaches the whole nation, which shows that God has begun speaking to His people again after a long season when His word was rare (3:1, 19–21). Israel then marches out to fight the Philistines. Israel camps beside Ebenezer, and the Philistines camp at Aphek. The name Ebenezer, “stone of help,” appears again in 7:12, where Samuel gives it to a memorial stone after the LORD helps Israel.

Observe: Something to notice
The verse sets two sentences side by side with nothing to connect them: “the word of Samuel came to all Israel. Now Israel went out to battle.” For a generation “the word of the LORD was rare” (3:1). Now the LORD had “revealed Himself to Samuel in Shiloh by the word of the LORD” (3:21), and that word was reaching every tribe. Yet the account records no one asking Samuel whether Israel should fight, and Scripture records no command from the LORD to go. Israel’s disaster did not come in a time when God was silent. It came just after He had begun to speak again. His word was available, and the account gives no sign that anyone sought it.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me
I can have God’s word close at hand, open on my table and preached to me every week, and still make my plans without asking Him. Many of my defeats do not come from a lack of revelation. They come from going out before I have listened. Before I step into the decisions in front of me, I can stop and ask what God has already said, and then wait on Him for what He has not yet made clear.

Observe: Something to notice
Israel camped “beside Ebenezer,” a name that means “stone of help.” The name returns three chapters later. After Israel returns to the LORD and He routs the Philistines, Samuel sets up a stone “between Mizpah and Shen” and calls it Ebenezer, saying, “Thus far the LORD has helped us” (7:12). Many readers think the narrator is using that later name ahead of time for the same area. Others note that 7:12 places Samuel’s stone near Mizpah, so the two sites may not be identical. Either way, the words “stone of help” first meet us at the scene of Israel’s worst defeat, and the next time we hear them they mark help received. Samuel’s words at the stone also honor the LORD, not the place: “the LORD has helped us.”

Hear: What God may be speaking to me
I can live close to the reminders of God’s help and still not call on Him. I may belong to a church with a history of answered prayer, come from a godly family, or remember times when God came through for me. Those memories are gifts, but they cannot stand in for asking Him today. “The LORD is near to all who call upon Him, To all who call upon Him in truth” (Psalm 145:18). Am I camped by the stone of help without calling on the One it honors?

1 Samuel 4:2
“Then the Philistines put themselves in battle array against Israel. And when they joined battle, Israel was defeated by the Philistines, who killed about four thousand men of the army in the field.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
The Philistines line up for battle, and Israel is defeated. About four thousand Israelite soldiers die on the open battlefield.

Observe: Something to notice
The defeat is reported quickly, and the LORD is not mentioned in it. Moses had told Israel that in battle “the LORD your God is He who goes with you, to fight for you against your enemies, to save you” (Deuteronomy 20:4). This verse records no prayer, no word from God, and no sign that He sent them. As far as the account tells us, Israel went out on its own initiative, and it lost about four thousand men. Moses had once warned an earlier generation that set out without God’s word: “Do not go up, lest you be defeated by your enemies, for the LORD is not among you” (Numbers 14:42).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me
In my own strength I am no match for the world around me. Its pressures, temptations, and opposition are organized and relentless. When I go out without God, I should not be surprised when I lose. A defeat like that can still be a mercy if it sends me back to the One who fights for His people.

1 Samuel 4:3
“And when the people had come into the camp, the elders of Israel said, ‘Why has the LORD defeated us today before the Philistines? Let us bring the ark of the covenant of the LORD from Shiloh to us, that when it comes among us it may save us from the hand of our enemies.’” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
Back in camp, the elders ask why the LORD let them be defeated. In the same breath they propose an answer of their own: bring the ark of the covenant from Shiloh, hoping that its presence among them will rescue them from their enemies.

Observe: Something to notice
The elders ask the right question: “Why has the LORD defeated us today before the Philistines?” They do not blame the weather or their generals. They see God’s hand in the defeat. In the same breath, though, they answer their own question. The question was spiritual, but their answer was a battle plan. The verse records no consulting of Samuel, no prayer, and no searching of hearts. Compare Joshua after the defeat at Ai. He “fell to the earth on his face before the ark of the LORD until evening” (Joshua 7:6), and God answered, “Israel has sinned” (Joshua 7:11). Joshua brought his question to God before the ark and waited for His answer. The elders sent for the ark to serve their plan.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me
Many of my prayers are questions I have already answered for myself. I ask God why something went wrong and then hurry to fix it my own way. When I am hurting or confused, I can stay before Him long enough to hear His answer, even if that answer is about my own sin. “Search me, O God, and know my heart” (Psalm 139:23).

Observe: Something to notice
Under the elders’ plan lies a right desire. They wanted God among them: “that when it comes among us it may save us.” God Himself had said, “Let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them” (Exodus 25:8). The method was what went wrong. “Let us bring the ark … to us,” they said. They did not go to God; they arranged to bring a holy object to their plans. Moses had prayed the other way around: “If Your Presence does not go with us, do not bring us up from here” (Exodus 33:15). Centuries later God did come among His people, on His own terms and at His own cost. His Son was called “Immanuel,” which is translated, “God with us” (Matthew 1:23).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me
I do not bring God near by carrying something sacred into my plans. I draw near with my heart. “Draw near to God and He will draw near to you” (James 4:8). The elders had it backwards. They tried to bring God close without coming close to Him themselves. I want to ask honestly whether I am treating any Christian habit, such as carrying a Bible, taking communion, or attending church, as a charm that guarantees God’s blessing while I hold on to sin He has shown me. God has already come near in Jesus, and He invites me to come to Him.

1 Samuel 4:4
“So the people sent to Shiloh, that they might bring from there the ark of the covenant of the LORD of hosts, who dwells between the cherubim. And the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, were there with the ark of the covenant of God.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
The people send to Shiloh for the ark. The verse gives it its fullest title, the ark of the covenant of the LORD of hosts, who dwells between the cherubim. It also notes that Eli’s two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, were there with the ark.

Observe: Something to notice
The chapter’s most majestic description of God comes right before the names of Hophni and Phinehas. These men “were corrupt; they did not know the LORD” (2:12). They took offerings by force (2:16) and lay with the women at the tabernacle door (2:22), and Eli had already been told that both of them would die on one day (2:34). Now they are there with the ark, the throne of the LORD of hosts, as it is brought into the camp. Haggai asked the priests whether holy meat carried in a garment makes other food holy, and they answered, “No” (Haggai 2:12). Holiness does not rub off by contact. Being with the ark did not make these men holy.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me
Being near holy things does not make me holy. I can serve in church, handle sacred things, and even hold a ministry title while my heart is far from the Lord. What God wants is a heart that knows Him, not only hands kept busy with holy work. If I find myself close to the things of God and far from God Himself, the answer is not to step away from His things but to come near to Him.

Observe: Something to notice
“The LORD of hosts” appears for the first time in Scripture in 1 Samuel 1:3, where Elkanah went up to Shiloh to worship “the LORD of hosts,” and “the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas,” were there. Hannah was the first person in Scripture to pray using that name: “O LORD of hosts, if You will indeed look on the affliction of Your maidservant” (1:11). In chapter 4, the same name appears in the same setting, connected with Shiloh and with the same two priests present. Hannah poured out her soul to the LORD of hosts and received a son. The elders reached for the ark of the LORD of hosts, attended by corrupt priests, and they received disaster. The name was the same. The heart was different.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me
I can use the right name for God and still use it emptily. The third commandment warns against taking “the name of the LORD your God in vain” (Exodus 20:7). That includes taking up His name for my own purposes. God listens to the person who calls on Him from a humble heart, as Hannah did. When I use His name in prayer, in worship, or to support what I want to do, is my heart actually turned toward Him?

1 Samuel 4:5
NKJV

1 Samuel 4:5 And when the ark of the covenant of the LORD came into the camp, all Israel shouted so loudly that the earth shook.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 4:5 “And when the ark of the covenant of the LORD came into the camp, all Israel shouted so loudly that the earth shook.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

When the ark of the covenant of the LORD comes into the camp, all Israel shouts so loudly that the earth shakes. Their celebration expresses confidence that the ark will secure victory, a confidence the coming defeat will expose as misplaced.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


The Hebrew behind “shouted so loudly” is literally “shouted a great shout,” and the Philistines call it “this great shout” (v. 6). It is the same Hebrew phrase used at Jericho, where “the people shouted with a great shout, that the wall fell down flat” (Joshua 6:20). Here the ark is present, the shout is enormous, and the earth shakes, yet nothing falls. Acts records another shaking. When the believers in Jerusalem had prayed, “the place where they were assembled together was shaken; and they were all filled with the Holy Spirit” (Acts 4:31). In Acts, God shook the place in answer to prayer. At Ebenezer, the ground rang with the people’s own voices.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


A loud shout is not the same as spiritual power. I can be stirred, moved, and loud in worship and still be outside God’s will. Excitement can come with His presence, and it can also take the place of His presence. Human enthusiasm is not wrong, but it cannot carry what only God can carry. What matters is whether I am walking in His word, not how strongly I feel.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


At Jericho the shout came at the end of seven days of exact obedience. Joshua had commanded, “You shall not shout or make any noise with your voice … until the day I say to you, ‘Shout!’ Then you shall shout” (Joshua 6:10). Israel shouted when God’s word through Joshua told them to. The chapter does not say the army at Ebenezer was deliberately copying Jericho, but the matching phrase invites the comparison: the ark in the camp, a great shout, and great expectations. What is missing is any word from God telling them to go. The outward elements of an old victory were present without the obedience that had marked it.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The power was never in the method. It was in obedience. I can copy what worked in a past revival, or in an earlier season of my own walk with God, and expect the same results. God is not asking me to repeat old methods. He asks me to obey what He is saying now, and to walk in the holiness that marked the victories I admire.

1 Samuel 4:6
“Now when the Philistines heard the noise of the shout, they said, ‘What does the sound of this great shout in the camp of the Hebrews mean?’ Then they understood that the ark of the LORD had come into the camp.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
The Philistines hear the shouting, ask what it means, and learn that the ark of the LORD has come into Israel’s camp.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


The Philistines heard the noise and asked, “What does the sound of this great shout in the camp of the Hebrews mean?” Israel’s confidence could be heard across the valley, and outsiders wanted to know what it meant. They concluded that the ark had come. But the shout meant less than either side believed. Israel had the ark in the camp, yet the chapter gives no sign that the LORD had sent them or would fight for them.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


People around me hear the outward things: church attendance, Christian vocabulary, songs of worship. Sooner or later they will ask, at least in their own minds, what it all means. The question is what lies behind it. Is there a real walk with God behind what others hear from me, or only noise?

1 Samuel 4:7
“So the Philistines were afraid, for they said, ‘God has come into the camp!’ And they said, ‘Woe to us! For such a thing has never happened before.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
The Philistines are terrified. They say that God has come into the camp and cry out that nothing like this has happened to them before.

Observation 4

Observe: Something to notice


The Philistines trembled at the arrival of the ark, while Israel celebrated it. In the next verse they recall God’s power against Egypt. Psalm 78, which later retells this very chapter (78:60–64), describes Israel’s long pattern of forgetting: “They did not remember His power” (78:42). Israel had kept the ark for generations and now treated it as a guarantee. The Philistines had never handled it, and it frightened them. Jesus met the same pattern in Nazareth, where the people who knew Him best “were offended at Him,” and “He did not do many mighty works there because of their unbelief” (Matthew 13:57–58).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Familiarity can make the holy feel ordinary. The closer I live to the things of God, the more carefully I need to guard my reverence. I do not want people who barely know God to take Him more seriously than I do.

1 Samuel 4:8
“Woe to us! Who will deliver us from the hand of these mighty gods? These are the gods who struck the Egyptians with all the plagues in the wilderness.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
The Philistines fear the gods who struck Egypt with plagues. Their memory is muddled, since they speak of “gods” and place the plagues “in the wilderness,” but they remember that Israel’s God once broke the greatest empire of the age.

Observation 5

Observe: Something to notice


The Philistines know something true about Israel’s God: He “struck the Egyptians with all the plagues.” Yet what they know reaches them as a frightening report, and it is blurred. They speak of “mighty gods,” plural, and they place the plagues “in the wilderness.” The plagues had a purpose beyond displaying power. God told Moses that through them “the Egyptians shall know that I am the LORD” (Exodus 7:5), and He told Israel to retell those signs “that you may know that I am the LORD” (Exodus 10:2). Pharaoh saw more of God’s power than any Philistine, and he had begun by saying, “I do not know the LORD” (Exodus 5:2). The Philistines had heard what God did. They did not know who He is, and they did not call on Him. Their knowledge produced dread, not trust.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I can know a great deal about what God has done and still not know Him. I can repeat Bible stories, recount the history of the church, and admire the testimonies of other believers, while God remains to me a distant power to fear or a force to manage. God has not hidden Himself behind rumors. He has made His name known, and supremely in His Son. Jesus prayed, “And this is eternal life, that they may know You, the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom You have sent” (John 17:3). I do not want only to have heard about Him. I want to know Him as He has revealed Himself and answer Him with trust.

1 Samuel 4:9
“Be strong and conduct yourselves like men, you Philistines, that you do not become servants of the Hebrews, as they have been to you. Conduct yourselves like men, and fight!’” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
The Philistine commanders rally their troops: be strong and fight like men, or you will become servants of the Hebrews, as the Hebrews have been to you.

Observation 6

Observe: Something to notice


The Philistines’ fear did not paralyze them; it roused them. Twice they urged one another, “conduct yourselves like men,” and then, “fight!” Facing what they believed was a powerful God, they answered with discipline and resolve. Both armies went on to fight (v. 10). But the chapter lets us hear the Philistines steeling themselves for a hard battle, while the last words we hear from Israel’s side are the elders’ hope that the ark “may save us” (v. 3).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The world can be more serious about its own causes than I am about following God. People pour discipline and effort into careers, politics, and sports, while believers often coast on religious confidence. A resolved world can defeat a compromised church. Jesus observed that “the sons of this world are more shrewd in their generation than the sons of light” (Luke 16:8). Do I bring to my walk with God at least the seriousness the world brings to what it cares about?

1 Samuel 4:10
“So the Philistines fought, and Israel was defeated, and every man fled to his tent. There was a very great slaughter, and there fell of Israel thirty thousand foot soldiers.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
The Philistines fight, and Israel is defeated. Every man flees to his tent. The slaughter is great, and thirty thousand Israelite foot soldiers fall.

Observation 7

Observe: Something to notice


“Every man fled to his tent.” The account describes no rallying point and no ordered retreat; it simply says each man ran for his own tent. The phrase fits the closing words of Judges, the era that Eli’s life brings to an end: “everyone did what was right in his own eyes” (Judges 21:25). Whatever had stirred the great shout in the camp was not enough to hold the army together once the battle turned.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


When God is not Lord among a people, and His presence is not really with them, unity falls apart and everyone goes his own way. Excitement can gather a crowd, but it cannot keep one together under pressure. My unity with other believers depends on Christ being Lord among us, not on shared symbols or shared excitement. Is He actually Lord in my home and in my church?

Observation 8

Observe: Something to notice


Four thousand had died in the first battle (v. 2). Now, with the ark in the camp, thirty thousand fall. The text does not say that bringing the ark caused the greater loss. It does show that the plan meant to save Israel did not save them. Scripture records no command from the LORD to bring the ark, and He did not let His name be used to guarantee a plan He had not given.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I cannot stamp God’s name on what I have already decided to do and expect Him to bless it. Putting His name on my plans, whether by a quick prayer at the end or a verse on the banner, does not make them His. Before I ask Him to bless my plans, I need to ask whether my plans are His, and be willing to hear Him say no.

1 Samuel 4:11
NKJV

1 Samuel 4:11 Also the ark of God was captured; and the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, died.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 4:11 “Also the ark of God was captured; and the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, died.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

The ark of God is captured, and Eli’s two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, die, just as the LORD had announced (2:34). The capture of the ark is the unfolding of God’s judgment on Eli’s house, not a defeat of God Himself.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


Years earlier, a man of God had told Eli what the sign of judgment on his house would be: “in one day they shall die, both of them” (2:34). Now the sign comes to pass exactly. God had also told the boy Samuel, “I will perform against Eli all that I have spoken concerning his house, from beginning to end” (3:12), and this day is the beginning of that judgment, not the whole of it. Israel went out without seeking God’s word in 4:1, but His word was still at work.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


God’s word does not need my belief in order to be true or to be fulfilled. Ignoring it does not stop it. Jesus said, “Heaven and earth will pass away, but My words will by no means pass away” (Matthew 24:35). I would rather take His word seriously while it still comes to me as a warning.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


The most sacred object in Israel went into Philistine hands. Psalm 78 explains what really happened: God “forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh” and “delivered His strength into captivity, And His glory into the enemy’s hand” (Psalm 78:60–61). God was not overpowered. He let His own holy object go rather than let His people use it as a tool.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The Lord may let me lose things that I count as His, such as a building, a ministry, a program, or a tradition, if they have begun to replace my fellowship with Him. If He takes something like that away, the loss may be His way of bringing me back to Himself. He cares more about having me than about my keeping the things I associate with Him.

1 Samuel 4:12
“Then a man of Benjamin ran from the battle line the same day, and came to Shiloh with his clothes torn and dirt on his head.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
On the same day, a man from the tribe of Benjamin runs from the battle to Shiloh with his clothes torn and dirt on his head.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


Torn clothes and dust on the head were the customary signs of deep mourning. Before the runner said a word, anyone who saw him could tell the news was terrible. Joshua and the elders mourned the same way after Ai (Joshua 7:6), and Job’s friends did so when they saw his suffering (Job 2:12). Later in the chapter, Phinehas’ wife will do more than grieve. She will put the loss into words: “The glory has departed from Israel!” (v. 21).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


It is right to mourn real loss. God does not ask me to pretend that something terrible is fine. “Blessed are those who mourn, For they shall be comforted” (Matthew 5:4). My grief can also be honest before God. When I have lost something spiritually, naming that truthfully to Him is where the way back begins.

1 Samuel 4:13
“Now when he came, there was Eli, sitting on a seat by the wayside watching, for his heart trembled for the ark of God. And when the man came into the city and told it, all the city cried out.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
The runner arrives to find Eli sitting on a seat beside the road, watching, because his heart trembles for the ark of God. When the man tells the city what happened, all Shiloh cries out.

Observation 4

Observe: Something to notice


“His heart trembled for the ark of God.” This is the most sympathetic thing the narrative says about Eli. He cared about the things of God. Earlier, when Samuel told him God’s judgment on his house, Eli answered, “It is the LORD. Let Him do what seems good to Him” (3:18). His words sounded submissive, but the account records no change in his house. God says, “But on this one will I look: On him who is poor and of a contrite spirit, And who trembles at My word” (Isaiah 66:2). Eli trembled for the ark, but there is no sign that he had trembled at the word.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Trembling for God’s things has to become trembling at God’s word, and that trembling has to lead to obedience. I can be anxious for my church or for a cause I care about and still ignore what God has plainly told me to do. I want to tremble at His word while it is still a warning, not only when I see the consequences coming.

1 Samuel 4:14
“When Eli heard the noise of the outcry, he said, ‘What does the sound of this tumult mean?’ And the man came quickly and told Eli.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
Eli hears the uproar and asks what it means. The messenger hurries over to tell him.

Observation 5

Observe: Something to notice


“The man came quickly.” The judgment God had announced years before (2:27–36; 3:11–14) had been slow to arrive. Now it comes without delay. Solomon observed, “Because the sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily, therefore the heart of the sons of men is fully set in them to do evil” (Ecclesiastes 8:11). While judgment waited, Eli’s house went on as before. When it came, it came suddenly.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


God’s patience is not indifference. When nothing seems to happen after I have ignored His warning, I can easily conclude that it did not matter. The delay is mercy. It gives me time to turn.

1 Samuel 4:15
NKJV

1 Samuel 4:15 Eli was ninety-eight years old, and his eyes were so dim that he could not see.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 4:15 “Eli was ninety-eight years old, and his eyes were so dim that he could not see.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

Eli is ninety-eight years old, and his eyes are so dim that he cannot see. The note explains why he must ask about the noise and depend entirely on the messenger’s report.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


The narrator pauses the story, just as the runner reaches Eli, to tell us that Eli cannot see. That is why, in the next verse, the man has to identify himself: “I am he who came from the battle” (v. 16). Eli must depend on what he hears and on someone else to bring it to him. His eyes had begun to fail years earlier (3:2). Yet in that same chapter, with dimming eyes, Eli was the one who “perceived that the LORD had called the boy” and taught Samuel to answer, “Speak, LORD, for Your servant hears” (3:8–9). Failing sight did not keep Eli from recognizing God’s voice or from helping someone else hear it. Scripture does not treat his age or his blindness as his sin.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


There may be seasons when I cannot see what I once could, because of age, illness, weariness, or simply the limits of my understanding. Those limits are not proof that God has set me aside. They may teach me to listen more carefully, to receive help from others, and to lean on what God has said rather than on what I can see. “Faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God” (Romans 10:17). Whatever my limits, I may still be able to do what Eli did at his best: point someone younger toward the God who speaks.

1 Samuel 4:16
“Then the man said to Eli, ‘I am he who came from the battle. And I fled today from the battle line.’ And he said, ‘What happened, my son?’” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
The messenger identifies himself as the one who fled from the battle that day. Eli asks him, “What happened, my son?”

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


Eli’s question uses the Hebrew word davar, which can mean “word,” “thing,” or “matter.” The NKJV renders the question “What happened, my son?” A more literal rendering would be something like “What was the matter, my son?” The same noun appears in Eli’s earlier question to Samuel: “What is the word that the LORD spoke to you?” (3:17), and both times Eli calls the young man “my son.” Both times the high priest learns what God has said or done through a younger man. That was God’s choice, not Eli’s failure. The LORD had called Samuel and established him as His prophet (3:19–20), and Eli rightly urged him to hold nothing back (3:17). The first time, Eli heard the word as a warning in the night. Now he hears it again as fact at the gate. What separates the two scenes is not how the word reached Eli but what he did with it, and the account records no change in his house.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


God often brings His word to me through other people: a pastor’s sermon, a friend’s counsel, a parent’s warning, or a believer who simply reads me a verse. That is His gift, not a lesser way of hearing. The question is not whether the word came to me directly or through someone else, but whether I receive it as addressed to me and act on it. I do not want to hear God’s word once as a warning and hear it again only as a report of the consequences. Whether it comes as I read Scripture or through someone God sends, I can ask Him, “What are You saying to me in this, and what will I do about it?”

1 Samuel 4:17
“So the messenger answered and said, ‘Israel has fled before the Philistines, and there has been a great slaughter among the people. Also your two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, are dead; and the ark of God has been captured.’” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
The messenger reports four things: Israel has fled, many have been killed, Eli’s two sons are dead, and the ark of God has been captured.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


The report builds to its worst point. First comes the flight of the nation, then the slaughter of the army, then the deaths of Eli’s own sons. The heaviest news comes last, and it is not the death of his sons. It is that “the ark of God has been captured.” The next verse confirms what mattered most to Eli: it was when the messenger “made mention of the ark of God” that he fell (v. 18).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The worst loss is not the loss of anything in this life, whether health, money, or even family. It would be to lose God Himself. For Israel that day, the capture of the ark was the sign of God’s judgment on a people who had treated Him as a tool. For me, in Christ, the promise is different: “I am with you always” (Matthew 28:20). That promise does not make His presence something to take for granted. It lets me treasure Him above every other gift and face the other losses of life with Him. When I weigh my losses, is He the one I value most?

1 Samuel 4:18
“Then it happened, when he made mention of the ark of God, that Eli fell off the seat backward by the side of the gate; and his neck was broken and he died, for the man was old and heavy. And he had judged Israel forty years.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
When the messenger mentions the ark, Eli falls backward off his seat by the gate, breaks his neck, and dies, because he is old and heavy. He had judged Israel for forty years.

Observation 4

Observe: Something to notice


“The man was old and heavy.” The Hebrew word for “heavy” (kaved) comes from the same root as kavod, “glory,” the key word of verses 21–22. God had accused Eli of honoring his sons above Him, “to make yourselves fat with the best of all the offerings of Israel My people” (2:29). Then He added, “those who honor Me I will honor, and those who despise Me shall be lightly esteemed” (2:30). The best portions of the offerings belonged to God, and the same root that names God’s glory now names Eli’s weight.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The glory I take for myself does not lift me up. It brings me down. Whatever belongs to God, His praise, His credit, and the first and best of what I have, is not mine to keep. I can give Him the honor and receive the honor that comes from Him.

Observation 5

Observe: Something to notice


Eli’s heaviness was more than a symbol. God had charged him with making himself fat with the best of the offerings (2:29), and verse 18 names his weight as part of the reason the fall killed him: “for the man was old and heavy.” What he had indulged in became part of the means of his death.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Unchecked indulgence is not only a spiritual danger; it can harm my body as well. Paul reminds believers that “your body is the temple of the Holy Spirit who is in you” (1 Corinthians 6:19). What I take in matters to God, both at the table and in every other appetite, and He can help me practice self-control.

Observation 6

Observe: Something to notice


“His neck was broken.” The text does not comment further, but Proverbs describes the pattern of Eli’s life: “He who is often rebuked, and hardens his neck, Will suddenly be destroyed, and that without remedy” (Proverbs 29:1). The narrator does not make this connection himself; it is an application. Still, Eli had been rebuked twice, by the man of God (2:27–36) and by Samuel (3:11–18), and the account records no change. His hardness took the form of passivity rather than open defiance.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I can harden my neck quietly, simply by not acting on what God has told me. If He has corrected me more than once about the same thing, I do not want to wait until it is too late to change.

1 Samuel 4:19
“Now his daughter-in-law, Phinehas’s wife, was with child, due to be delivered; and when she heard the news that the ark of God was captured, and that her father-in-law and her husband were dead, she bowed herself and gave birth, for her labor pains came upon her.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
Phinehas’ pregnant wife hears that the ark is captured and that her father-in-law and husband are dead. She goes into labor and gives birth.

Observation 7

Observe: Something to notice


It was the news itself that brought on her labor. The child was born into the day the ark was taken and would carry the mark of it in his name for the rest of his life (v. 21). He did not choose the day he was born or the house he was born into.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Children are born into the spiritual atmosphere their parents live in. My walk with God, or my neglect of it, becomes the air my children breathe at home. Paul tells fathers to bring their children up “in the training and admonition of the Lord” (Ephesians 6:4). What spiritual climate are my children growing up in?

1 Samuel 4:20
“And about the time of her death the women who stood by her said to her, ‘Do not fear, for you have borne a son.’ But she did not answer, nor did she regard it.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
As she is dying, the women attending her tell her not to fear, because she has given birth to a son. She does not answer or pay attention.

Observation 8

Observe: Something to notice


“She did not answer, nor did she regard it.” The phrase means she did not set her heart on it. For a Hebrew mother, giving birth to a son was ordinarily a great joy. On this day the news could not reach her, because her grief over what had happened ran deeper even than the joy of a son.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Earthly joys, even good gifts from God like the birth of a child, cannot take the place of God Himself. If I have drifted from Him, no new purchase, success, or relationship will make up for it. And if I am simply in a dry season, when His nearness is hard to feel, that does not mean He has gone. In either case the answer is to seek Him, not to fill the empty place with something else.

1 Samuel 4:21
“Then she named the child Ichabod, saying, ‘The glory has departed from Israel!’ because the ark of God had been captured and because of her father-in-law and her husband.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
She names her son Ichabod, saying, “The glory has departed from Israel!” She grieves because the ark has been captured and because her father-in-law and husband are dead.

Observation 9

Observe: Something to notice


Ichabod means “no glory,” or, as some read it, “where is the glory?” Every time the boy’s name was spoken, it would recall the day the ark was taken. Samson “did not know that the LORD had departed from him” (Judges 16:20), but this mother named the loss and said it aloud. The most terrifying word that could be written over the doors of a church, a family, or a life is Ichabod.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Where is the glory of God among us, in my life, my home, and my church? If the manifest presence of God were to lift from us, would I even notice? Would it grieve me more than losing my health, my finances, or my reputation?

Observation 10

Observe: Something to notice


The verb translated “departed” is galah, the ordinary Hebrew word for going into exile. The glory went into exile before Israel did. Centuries later Ezekiel saw “the glory of the LORD” depart “from the threshold of the temple” (Ezekiel 10:18) before Jerusalem fell, and later he saw the glory come back into the temple (Ezekiel 43:4).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Ezekiel saw the glory leave, and he also saw it return. Because of Christ and the Spirit He has given, my situation is not the same as Shiloh’s, and God has promised never to leave His people. Still, I can ask whether there are places in my life, my home, or my church where we carry on as if nothing has changed, when God has been grieved and His blessing is no longer evident. If He shows me something like that, it is not a sign that He has abandoned me. It is His call to return, and the story of the ark does not end with Ichabod.

1 Samuel 4:22
NKJV

1 Samuel 4:22 And she said, "The glory has departed from Israel, for the ark of God has been captured."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 4:22 “And she said, ‘The glory has departed from Israel, for the ark of God has been captured.’” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

She repeats her lament, “The glory has departed from Israel, for the ark of God has been captured,” now naming the ark alone. Her words truly express the catastrophe of the glory lost with the ark, without meaning that the LORD has been defeated or has permanently abandoned His people.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


She was right that the glory had departed from Israel. But God Himself was not defeated. In the next chapter “the hand of the LORD was heavy” on the Philistines (5:6), and the LORD was still with Samuel, letting “none of his words fall to the ground” (3:19). The glory left Shiloh, but it was not extinguished, and chapter 7 shows Israel returning to the LORD.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Israel lost the ark, and with it the evident sign of God’s favor, under His judgment. Yet God Himself had not been defeated, and He had not finished with His people. For me in Christ, the promise stands: “I will never leave you nor forsake you” (Hebrews 13:5). His Spirit does not come and go with my feelings. There may be seasons when I cannot sense His nearness, and seasons when He lets me feel the consequences of my own neglect. The first calls for patient trust; the second calls for repentance. Either way, I can bring it honestly to Him and ask Him to search me, because He is the one calling me near.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


Her dying words repeat what she had already said: the glory has departed, for the ark of God has been captured. With her own death at hand, her last recorded sentence is about the ark of God.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Do I grieve more when Christ’s name is dishonored than when I suffer a personal loss? When His name is brought into reproach, even by His own people, does it grieve me, or do I hardly notice?
