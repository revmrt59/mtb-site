1 Samuel 5:1
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:1 Then the Philistines took the ark of God and brought it from Ebenezer to Ashdod.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:1 “Then the Philistines took the ark of God and brought it from Ebenezer to Ashdod.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

The Philistines take the captured ark of God and bring it from Ebenezer to Ashdod. The ark now enters Philistine territory as a prize of war.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


Israel had just lost thirty thousand soldiers, the priests Hophni and Phinehas were dead, and the ark was gone (4:10–11). It looked as though Israel’s God had been beaten along with Israel. But Israel had lost because God had allowed judgment to fall on a disobedient and presumptuous people, not because God had grown weak. Psalm 78 says plainly that He “delivered His strength into captivity, and His glory into the enemy’s hand” (Psalm 78:61). God was disciplining His people, and He had not stopped being their God. Hebrews says the Father disciplines those He loves, and does it for their good (Hebrews 12:6, 10).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


God may let me experience defeat because of my own disobedience or spiritual drift. But my failure does not make Him smaller. Israel’s sin cost them the battle and the ark; it did not weaken the LORD at all. My sin can cost me peace with Him and the joy of His blessing, yet He is still as strong and faithful as He was before I failed. When He disciplines me, He has not abandoned me; He is still claiming me as His own. So I don’t have to hide my defeat from Him. I can bring it to Him honestly, confess what caused it, and trust Him to restore me.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


The ark is carried away from Ebenezer, a name that means “stone of help.” Israel had camped there and lost (4:1), so the place of their defeat carried a name that promised help. They had tried to force God’s help by carrying the ark into battle like a good-luck charm (4:3). His help did not come their way. But it had not ended. In chapter 7, after Israel repents, Samuel will set up a stone of victory and give it this same name: “Ebenezer, saying, ‘Thus far the LORD has helped us’” (7:12). The road from defeat to that second Ebenezer runs through repentance and trust in the LORD Himself.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


God’s help has not ended just because it did not come the way I tried to force it. I may have treated something holy like a good-luck charm and watched it fail. He may first let my false confidence fail so that I learn to depend on Him. The place where I was defeated may become the place where I can say with Samuel that the LORD has helped me this far.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


The ark was the sign of God’s throne and the place where He promised to meet His people (Exodus 25:22). Now it is seized and carried into enemy territory, where it will humble the enemy’s god. Christians have long seen in this a picture of what happened to Jesus. Jesus was seized, bound and led away (John 18:12). At the cross it looked like the end. Yet there Christ “disarmed principalities and powers” and made “a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them in it” (Colossians 2:15). The ark is not Christ, and this verse is about the capture of the ark. But the pattern is the same: what looks like God’s defeat becomes the place of His victory.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


What looks like God’s defeat in my life may be the very place He is winning. Jesus looked defeated when He was led away, and that was the road to His victory. When God’s cause seems to lose, I do not have to read the outcome from the circumstances. I can read the circumstances in the light of the cross.

1 Samuel 5:2
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:2 When the Philistines took the ark of God, they brought it into the house of Dagon and set it by Dagon.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:2 “When the Philistines took the ark of God, they brought it into the house of Dagon and set it by Dagon.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

They bring the ark of God into the house of Dagon and set it beside their god. Placing it there presents Israel’s God as though He were a captive trophy in Dagon’s house.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


The Philistines had done this kind of thing before. When they captured Samson, they gathered to sacrifice to Dagon, saying, “Our god has delivered into our hands Samson our enemy!” (Judges 16:23). Setting the ark “by Dagon” said that Israel’s God was now one god among many, and the lesser one. But the LORD had said, “My glory I will not give to another, nor My praise to carved images” (Isaiah 42:8). He does not debate Dagon or bargain with him. The arrangement does not last one night. Paul asks the same question about believers: “What agreement has the temple of God with idols? For you are the temple of the living God” (2 Corinthians 6:16).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


God will not share a place in my heart. He does not ask for a spot on the shelf beside my other trusts. He asks for first place, because He alone is God. I don’t want to keep Him beside the things I rely on. Giving Him the whole room is not a loss; it is freedom.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


Nothing in this verse suggests the Philistines meant to give up Dagon. They simply added Israel’s God to the religion they already had. People can do the same with the true God. We welcome Him into our lives and expect Him to fit in alongside everything that already governs us. We want His protection, peace and blessing without giving Him the throne. Some have called this an “additive gospel”: Jesus plus my ambition, plus my comfort, plus my control. But receiving God is not adding one more influence to a life that is already arranged. It means yielding to Him the authority over that life.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


There is a difference between adding God to my life and surrendering my life to God. I can ask Him to bless my plans without ever handing Him my plans. I want to move from wanting God to take part in my life to yielding my life to His rule.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


Dagon stood in the place of honor in his own house, and the ark’s arrival challenged that place. Spurgeon preached this scene as a picture of grace entering a heart and confronting its idols, especially self-righteousness and cherished sin. An idol is not always something obviously evil. It can be something good that has been given a place of ultimate trust. Control, reputation, financial security, success in ministry, personal achievement, and even religious knowledge can become the real authority in a life. The question is not always whether something should stay, but whether it has taken a place that belongs to God alone.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


What sits in the place of honor in my life? It may be a good thing: my family, my work, my reputation, even my ministry. When the Lord comes near, He challenges whatever I have set up in His place. I can let Him show me what I have trusted most, and give that place back to Him.

1 Samuel 5:3
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:3 And when the people of Ashdod arose early in the morning, there was Dagon, fallen on its face to the earth before the ark of the LORD. So they took Dagon and set it in its place again.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:3 “And when the people of Ashdod arose early in the morning, there was Dagon, fallen on its face to the earth before the ark of the LORD. So they took Dagon and set it in its place again.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

Early the next morning the people of Ashdod find Dagon fallen on its face to the earth before the ark of the LORD, and they must set it back in its place. The idol lies prostrate before the LORD and depends on its worshipers to stand again.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


Overnight the whole arrangement was reversed. Dagon is found “fallen on its face to the earth before the ark of the LORD,” the posture of a worshiper bowing low. The NKJV uses the same words for Joshua, who “fell on his face to the earth and worshiped” before the Commander of the LORD’s army (Joshua 5:14). Notice that the narrator now calls it “the ark of the LORD,” using God’s covenant name. The story records no Israelite intervention and no human hand at work. It presents the fall as the LORD’s victory, won without anyone’s help. Dagon could not stand before the true God.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


God will not allow my idols to stand before Him. When I come into His presence, the things I have put in His place start to become uncomfortable, and that discomfort is His mercy. It is not a feeling that does the work for me. His presence exposes the idol; then I respond. I name the trust that has taken His place, confess it to Him, and give that place back to Him. I don’t have to wrestle my idols down in my own strength, but I do have to stop defending them.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


“So they took Dagon and set it in its place again.” The worshipers had to lift their god off the floor. Isaiah later mocks idols in exactly these terms: “They bear it on the shoulder, they carry it and set it in its place, and it stands; from its place it shall not move” (Isaiah 46:7). Against these carried gods the LORD says, “Even to your old age, I am He, and even to gray hairs I will carry you!” (Isaiah 46:4). Dagon needed human help to stand. The LORD needed no help to throw him down. Every false god depends on the people who serve it. The living God is the One His people depend on.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Whatever I have to keep propping up cannot carry me. Money is not the problem; caring for it well is good stewardship. The problem comes when my sense of safety rests on the balance, or my worth rests on what people think of me. Then I spend my life lifting that thing back into place every morning. The living God is the One who carries me, and He has promised to keep carrying me “even to gray hairs.” I can put that weight down and trust Him.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


The people of Ashdod did not let the fallen image challenge their beliefs. They repaired the visible disorder and set things back the way they had been. We can do the same thing. When God exposes pride, self-reliance, misplaced trust or an unhealthy attachment, we may see that something needs to change and still rush to restore the familiar arrangement. We can be more committed to keeping what we know than to receiving what God is showing us.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I often try to rebuild what God is tearing down in my life. When He lets a false support fall, my first instinct is to set it back up before anyone notices. I want to learn to leave on the floor what God has thrown down, and to ask Him what He is showing me instead.

1 Samuel 5:4
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:4 And when they arose early the next morning, there was Dagon, fallen on its face to the ground before the ark of the LORD. The head of Dagon and both the palms of its hands were broken off on the threshold; only Dagon's torso was left of it.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:4 “And when they arose early the next morning, there was Dagon, fallen on its face to the ground before the ark of the LORD. The head of Dagon and both the palms of its hands were broken off on the threshold; only Dagon’s torso was left of it.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

The following morning Dagon has fallen again before the ark, and its head and both palms lie broken off on the threshold, leaving only its torso. The second fall is a decisive humiliation, and the idol’s severed hands stand in contrast to the powerful hand of the LORD that follows.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


The first fall could be explained away, and the Philistines simply set Dagon back up. The second fall could not be explained away. An image that has fallen can be lifted. An image with its head and hands broken off cannot be fixed by standing it up again. The first morning was a warning; the second was full exposure. The text does not give a rule that God always works in two stages. But it does show a pattern found elsewhere in Scripture: when people ignore God’s first, gentler warning and keep propping up what He has toppled, His dealings with them can become plainer and more severe.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


When I ignore the Lord’s gentle warnings, His dealings with me can become plainer. He does not shout first. He often begins with a quiet tap on the shoulder. I would rather listen to the first fall than wait for the second. If He is showing me something today, I want to respond while His warning is still gentle.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


The parts broken off were the head and the hands. The head stands for thinking, direction and authority; the hands stand for action, strength and power. These are natural pictures, not hidden meanings in the Hebrew words. Without them Dagon had no mind to rule and no hands to act, which is all an idol ever really was. The psalmist says idols have hands “but they do not handle,” and that “those who make them are like them; so is everyone who trusts in them” (Psalm 115:7–8). Before the LORD, the claimed wisdom and power of a false god lay in pieces on the floor.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The idols of my own wisdom and my own strength cannot stand before God. I can trust my understanding and my ability until they quietly become my god. Before Him, they fall. I want to take God at His word: “Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and lean not on your own understanding” (Proverbs 3:5).

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


“Only Dagon’s torso was left of it.” The head and hands were gone, but the stump of the image still lay in the temple. Spurgeon used this detail as a picture of the believer’s life after Christ has come in. Sin’s authority is broken, but some of the old life remains, and the believer still has to resist it. That is an application, not the meaning of the verse. But it fits the New Testament. Paul tells believers that “sin shall not have dominion over you” (Romans 6:14), and also calls them to “put to death the deeds of the body” by the Spirit (Romans 8:13).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The old master in my life has been dethroned, even if some remnant of it is still lying around. I don’t have to be discouraged that I still struggle. Sin no longer has the right to rule me. I can keep putting to death by the Spirit what Christ has already broken, and look forward to the day when nothing of it is left.

1 Samuel 5:5
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:5 Therefore neither the priests of Dagon nor any who come into Dagon's house tread on the threshold of Dagon in Ashdod to this day.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:5 “Therefore neither the priests of Dagon nor any who come into Dagon’s house tread on the threshold of Dagon in Ashdod to this day.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

For this reason the priests of Dagon and all who enter his house do not tread on the threshold of Dagon in Ashdod to the writer’s day. The lasting custom preserves the memory of Dagon’s humiliation before the LORD.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


The priests of Dagon knew exactly what had happened. Their god had fallen twice and been broken in his own house. That should have led them to question him. Instead, they made up a new religious custom around the place of his defeat and went on worshiping him. They had clear evidence of the LORD’s power, and they built religion around something else. An encounter with God’s power does not guarantee a changed heart. People can adjust their practices rather than surrender their beliefs. Seeing is not the same as surrendering.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I can see God’s power and still not surrender to Him. I can hear a strong sermon, feel His presence in worship, or watch Him answer prayer, and then go on living the same way. What matters is not only what He shows me, but whether I bow to Him because of it.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


Every worshiper who came into Dagon’s house had to step carefully over the very spot where Dagon’s head and hands had fallen. They walked over the evidence of their god’s defeat and kept right on worshiping him. Centuries later the prophet Zephaniah condemned people in Judah who “leap over the threshold” (Zephaniah 1:9). Some think this was the same custom spreading into Judah, though that cannot be proven. Either way, the picture is striking: a threshold-leaper is someone who steps around what God has brought down rather than looking at it.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I can become a threshold-leaper. I can step carefully around what God has brought down in my life, or around something that has clearly stopped bearing fruit, so that I can keep my life arranged around it. I would rather stop at the threshold, look honestly at what God has done, and let it turn me to Him.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


The same events that hardened the Philistines could have opened their hearts. Scripture shows that people respond very differently to the same kind of evidence. Pharaoh saw plague after plague and hardened his heart. Rahab only heard reports of what the LORD had done at the Red Sea, and she said, “The LORD your God, He is God in heaven above and on earth beneath” (Joshua 2:11). The difference is not always how much evidence a person receives. It is how the heart responds to it.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The same truth that softens one heart can harden another, and the difference is my response. God has shown me enough of Himself to trust Him. I don’t want to wait for more proof while my heart grows harder. I want to respond like Rahab, with faith, to what He has already shown me.

1 Samuel 5:6
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:6 But the hand of the LORD was heavy on the people of Ashdod, and He ravaged them and struck them with tumors, both Ashdod and its territory.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:6 “But the hand of the LORD was heavy on the people of Ashdod, and He ravaged them and struck them with tumors, both Ashdod and its territory.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

The hand of the LORD is heavy on the people of Ashdod; He ravages them and strikes them with tumors, both the city and its territory. The LORD Himself is the agent of this judgment, acting without any Israelite army.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


The first five verses deal with Dagon in his temple. In verse 6 the scene moves outside the temple to the people who trusted in Dagon. God dealt with the false god first, and then with its hold on the people. The LORD had done the same in Egypt: “Against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgment: I am the LORD” (Exodus 12:12). What people worship shapes more than their religious practice. It shapes how they see the world and what they build their lives on. So when God confronts an idol, He is also confronting the trust placed in it.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


When God confronts what I trust in place of Him, He deals with the false god first and then with its hold on my life. It is not enough for an idol to fall in principle. If I admit that I have trusted my career for my identity, the next step is to change the decisions that trust has been making: how I spend my evenings, what I say yes to, where I turn when I am afraid. I want my repentance to reach my calendar and my choices, not only my feelings.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


Dagon could not prevent his own fall, and now he could not protect the people of Ashdod either. They trusted in a god who could not give them the protection they needed. This is the false promise of every idol. People turn to idols because they believe the idols can provide something essential: security, identity, peace, satisfaction, success or control. Sooner or later, whatever replaces God as the source of life shows that it cannot give what only God can give.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I turn to idols because I believe they can give me something I need. Money promises security, approval promises worth, control promises peace. But what cannot protect itself cannot protect me. I can bring my real needs to the God who actually meets them.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


The NKJV says the LORD struck the Philistines “with tumors.” The older King James Version called them “emerods,” meaning hemorrhoids, and many commentators think these were painful swellings in the groin or the rear of the body. The text does not let us be sure, and we should not assume that every tumor here was a hemorrhoid. What is clear is that God’s dealing with the Philistines was intensely personal, painful and impossible to ignore. They could not sit comfortably with the ark among them. This was a specific judgment on Philistia, and it does not mean that every illness or discomfort is God’s punishment.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


God knows how to make me uncomfortable when He is pursuing me. When I am clinging to something He is confronting, He may let me lose my comfort until I pay attention. That discomfort is not proof that He has rejected me. It can be His way of getting my attention so that I will turn to Him.

1 Samuel 5:7
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:7 And when the men of Ashdod saw how it was, they said, "The ark of the God of Israel must not remain with us, for His hand is harsh toward us and Dagon our god."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:7 “And when the men of Ashdod saw how it was, they said, ‘The ark of the God of Israel must not remain with us, for His hand is harsh toward us and Dagon our god.’” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

The men of Ashdod recognize that the hand of the God of Israel is harsh against them and against Dagon their god, and they insist that His ark must not remain with them. Their answer is to send the ark away, even as they go on calling Dagon their god.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


When God’s hand presses on people, it is meant to bring them to Him. David knew that pressure: “Day and night Your hand was heavy upon me.” That heaviness led him to confession: “I acknowledged my sin to You… and You forgave the iniquity of my sin” (Psalm 32:4–5). Ashdod felt the same kind of hand, and its only response was to want God gone. The same pressure can drive one person to God and drive another to try to get rid of Him. Peter tells believers what to do: “Humble yourselves under the mighty hand of God, that He may exalt you in due time” (1 Peter 5:6). James says the same: “Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and He will lift you up” (James 4:10).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


When God’s hand is heavy on me, it is meant to bring me home, not to make me want Him gone. I can humble myself under His mighty hand instead of trying to slip out from under it. When I do, He promises to lift me up in His time.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


The men of Ashdod read the situation exactly right: “His hand is harsh toward us and Dagon our god.” They knew the God of Israel had beaten their god. But their conclusion was that the ark must go, not that Dagon must go. They wanted God’s presence removed rather than their idol. The people of the Gadarenes did the same after Jesus delivered a man from demons: they “began to plead with Him to depart from their region” (Mark 5:17). Faced with holiness, the human heart often asks God to leave instead of asking Him to forgive.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


When God’s presence exposes something I cherish, I face a choice: give up the idol, or try to push God away. I can tell myself I am just changing the subject, or staying busy, or keeping a distance for a while. I don’t want to send the Lord away to keep my idol. I want to let the idol go and keep the Lord.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


Even after finding Dagon facedown and broken, the men of Ashdod still call him “Dagon our god.” Their loyalty outlasted every proof that he was empty. An idol does not always lose its hold when its weakness is shown. People can keep trusting something that has disappointed them again and again, because the issue is deeper than information. It is a matter of love, habit, fear and identity. The psalmist says of idols, “Those who make them are like them; so is everyone who trusts in them” (Psalm 115:8).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I can cling to something long after it has failed me. A habit, a relationship, a way of coping or a source of comfort can keep calling itself “mine” even when it has let me down many times. I can bring these things to the Lord honestly and ask Him to loosen their hold on my heart.

1 Samuel 5:8
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:8 Therefore they sent and gathered to themselves all the lords of the Philistines, and said, "What shall we do with the ark of the God of Israel?" And they answered, "Let the ark of the God of Israel be carried away to Gath." So they carried the ark of the God of Israel away.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:8 “Therefore they sent and gathered to themselves all the lords of the Philistines, and said, ‘What shall we do with the ark of the God of Israel?’ And they answered, ‘Let the ark of the God of Israel be carried away to Gath.’ So they carried the ark of the God of Israel away.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

They gather all the lords of the Philistines to ask what to do with the ark of the God of Israel, and the council decides to carry it to Gath. Relocating the ark carries the crisis into another Philistine city.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


The Philistine lords never ask what the God of Israel wants. Their question shows what was in their hearts: “What shall we do with the ark?” After everything that had happened, the better question was what they should do in response to the God who had shown His power. They treated Him as a problem to be moved, and their solution was a change of address. Israel had made a similar mistake in chapter 4, when they tried to use the ark to win a battle. Both treated God’s presence as something to handle. But the Philistines’ problem was not geography. They had a problem with God, and the God they were moving is the One who “is a consuming fire” (Hebrews 12:29). Changing circumstances can be wise, but it can never replace responding to Him.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


God is not a problem for me to place where He is convenient. I can keep the Lord in certain rooms of my life, like Sunday mornings or a crisis, and move Him away from the rest. And when trouble comes, I often ask how to change my circumstances when the more important question is how I should respond to God in them. Before I ask, “What should I do about this?” I want to ask, “Lord, what are You doing, and how do You want me to respond?”

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


“What shall we do with the ark of the God of Israel?” Centuries later another ruler asked a similar question about Jesus. Pilate said, “What then shall I do with Jesus who is called Christ?” (Matthew 27:22). The ark is not Christ, but the question is the same kind of question. When God comes near, everyone has to decide what to do with Him. There are really only two answers: send Him away, or bow before Him. Moving the question somewhere else does not answer it.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I have to decide what to do with Jesus, and putting off the decision is a way of sending Him away. I can’t pass Him to another room or another day. The honest answers are only two: send Him away, or bow before Him. I choose to bow.

1 Samuel 5:9
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:9 So it was, after they had carried it away, that the hand of the LORD was against the city with a very great destruction; and He struck the men of the city, both small and great, and tumors broke out on them.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:9 “So it was, after they had carried it away, that the hand of the LORD was against the city with a very great destruction; and He struck the men of the city, both small and great, and tumors broke out on them.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

After the ark is carried to Gath, the hand of the LORD comes against that city with very great destruction, striking its men, small and great, with tumors. Moving the ark does not escape the LORD’s judgment but only extends it.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


The Philistines thought moving the ark would move the problem. Instead, the same affliction broke out in a second city. That proved the trouble was not a local condition; it was the hand of the LORD. Moving God’s ark did not remove His hand. It only spread it. David asked, “Where can I go from Your Spirit? Or where can I flee from Your presence?” (Psalm 139:7). For the Philistines, that truth was a threat. For God’s children, it is one of the great comforts of Scripture, because the God we cannot escape is the God who never leaves us.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


There is nowhere I can go to get away from God. If I am running from Him, that is a sobering truth. If I belong to Him, it is a deep comfort. Wherever I go, even “the uttermost parts of the sea,” His hand will lead me and hold me (Psalm 139:9–10).

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


The Philistines moved the ark but did not change their response to God. So the confrontation followed them to Gath. People can do the same with God’s conviction. We change jobs, churches, relationships or surroundings without dealing with what God is confronting in our hearts. Or we pass the problem to someone else. The Philistine lords solved Ashdod’s problem by handing it to Gath. But we cannot get rid of God’s conviction by moving it somewhere else or putting it on someone else. A change of setting may bring relief, but it cannot do what only repentance can do.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I cannot get rid of God’s conviction by moving it somewhere else or blaming someone else. If I keep running into the same issue in new places, the issue may be in me. I want to stop passing it along and bring it to the Lord in confession.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


The Philistine leaders tried a solution in Ashdod, and it failed in Gath. They would try it again in Ekron. They kept changing the method without questioning their understanding of the problem. The crisis exposed that they thought they faced a problem of location when they faced the LORD Himself. That gives a principle for discernment: repeated failure may show that we have misunderstood the nature of a problem, not just chosen the wrong way to solve it.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


When I keep failing at the same thing, I may need to ask whether I have understood the problem at all. I can keep trying new methods for something that is really a matter of the heart. I can ask the Lord to show me what is really going on, not just how to fix it.

1 Samuel 5:10
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:10 Therefore they sent the ark of God to Ekron. So it was, as the ark of God came to Ekron, that the Ekronites cried out, saying, "They have brought the ark of the God of Israel to us, to kill us and our people!"(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:10 “Therefore they sent the ark of God to Ekron. So it was, as the ark of God came to Ekron, that the Ekronites cried out, saying, ‘They have brought the ark of the God of Israel to us, to kill us and our people!’” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

The ark of God is sent on to Ekron, and as it arrives the Ekronites cry out that it has been brought to kill them and their people. Their immediate alarm suggests they already knew what the ark had brought on Ashdod and Gath.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


The people of Ekron were terrified before the text describes any plague reaching them. The news of what the LORD had done had run ahead of His ark. Their alarm shows how hostile powers react when God draws near. Years later Ekron’s god was called Baal-Zebub (2 Kings 1:2), and the Gospels use a similar name, Beelzebub, for the ruler of demons (Matthew 12:24). First Samuel 5 does not mention that god, and the Ekronites here are frightened people, not demons. But the New Testament shows the same reaction in the spiritual realm: demons cried out in fear when Jesus came near (Mark 1:24). James gives believers the right order: “Submit to God. Resist the devil and he will flee from you. Draw near to God and He will draw near to you” (James 4:7–8).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The best way to deal with the darkness in my life is not to fix my attention on the enemy, but to draw near to God. That does not mean temptation disappears the moment I feel close to Him. Drawing near means submitting to God: confessing what He shows me, obeying what He says, and refusing to make room for the devil. When I do that, I can resist the enemy from a place of nearness to God instead of fear.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


The Ekronites cry, “They have brought the ark of the God of Israel to us.” Throughout the chapter the Philistines keep naming whose ark it is: “the ark of the God of Israel,” three times in verse 8 alone. They speak the right name for God, but they want nothing to do with Him. Their words confess what their hearts refuse. It is possible to use the right language about God, to speak His name accurately and even often, without knowing Him or yielding to Him.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I can have the right language about God without knowing Him. I can say “Lord” and “God” correctly, sing the right songs and use the right words, and still keep Him at arm’s length. I don’t just want to speak about God accurately. I want to know Him and yield to Him.

1 Samuel 5:11
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:11 So they sent and gathered together all the lords of the Philistines, and said, "Send away the ark of the God of Israel, and let it go back to its own place, so that it does not kill us and our people." For there was a deadly destruction throughout all the city; the hand of God was very heavy there.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:11 “So they sent and gathered together all the lords of the Philistines, and said, ‘Send away the ark of the God of Israel, and let it go back to its own place, so that it does not kill us and our people.’ For there was a deadly destruction throughout all the city; the hand of God was very heavy there.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

The lords of the Philistines are gathered again, and the people plead that the ark of the God of Israel be sent back to its own place so that it does not kill them, for deadly destruction fills the city and the hand of God is very heavy there. The repeated judgment forces the Philistines to give up the trophy they had taken.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


In Egypt, God said to Pharaoh, “Let My people go” (Exodus 5:1), and Pharaoh refused until God’s hand forced him. Here the Philistines beg, “Send away the ark… let it go back.” The Hebrew verb behind “send away” is the same verb God used in “Let My people go” (shalach, meaning to send off or release). Just as Egypt could not hold God’s people, Philistia could not hold God’s ark. Nothing that belongs to God can be held by His enemies. The New Testament shows the same truth at the empty tomb: God raised Jesus up, “because it was not possible that He should be held by it” (Acts 2:24).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Nothing that belongs to God can be held by His enemies, and that includes me. If I belong to Christ, no power of sin, death or darkness can keep its grip on me forever. Death could not hold Him, and it will not hold those who are His.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


The word “heavy” from verse 6 returns here, made stronger: “the hand of God was very heavy there.” The longer they kept the ark, the heavier God’s hand became. Yet the Philistines want only one thing: that the ark “does not kill us.” Their goal is survival, not knowing the God who sent the judgment. They wanted relief from the consequences rather than a changed relationship with the One who was confronting them. Their greatest need was not relief but a right relationship with God.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


God wants me to seek Him for Himself, not only for relief from my trouble. I can pray hard for a problem to go away and never seek the One who is speaking through it. I want more than relief. I want Him.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


Even the Philistines recognize that the ark has “its own place.” It belonged among God’s people, and God was bringing it home by His own power. But notice what they do: they give back the ark without giving themselves. They let go of the sacred object because keeping it hurt too much, but they do not turn to the LORD. It is possible to give back something that belongs to God because holding it has become painful, and still not surrender our hearts to Him.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I can give back to God what belongs to Him and still keep my heart to myself. I may return my time, my gifts or the credit for what He has done simply because holding on has become uncomfortable. The Philistines did exactly that with the ark. The Lord wants more than the things I hand back. He wants me to hand over myself.

1 Samuel 5:12
NKJV

1 Samuel 5:12 And the men who did not die were stricken with the tumors, and the cry of the city went up to heaven.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 5:12 “And the men who did not die were stricken with the tumors, and the cry of the city went up to heaven.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

The men who do not die are stricken with tumors, and the cry of the city goes up to heaven. The chapter ends with the Philistines overwhelmed under the LORD’s hand.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


“The cry of the city went up to heaven,” yet the chapter does not record the Philistines praying to the God of Israel or turning to Him. The words recall Israel in Egypt, when “their cry came up to God because of the bondage” (Exodus 2:23). Two chapters later, Israel faces the Philistines again and begs Samuel, “Do not cease to cry out to the LORD our God for us,” and “the LORD answered him” (7:8–9). Scripture is full of cries of faith: blind Bartimaeus calling, “Jesus, Son of David, have mercy on me!” (Mark 10:47), and Peter crying, “Lord, save me!” as he sank (Matthew 14:30). What matters is not how loud the cry is, but to whom it is addressed.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Crying out in pain is not the same as turning to God. I can groan under pressure, complain and weep, and still not turn toward Him. God does not need polished words; He hears the groaning of those who come to Him. But I want my pain to bring me to Him, not just to rise somewhere in the air. And I can remember that Jesus “always lives to make intercession” for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25).

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


Ekron wanted the ark sent away. Yet the cry of the city still went up to heaven. The people wanted distance from the God whose power they feared, and their anguish rose toward heaven anyway. The text does not say what each person meant by that cry or whether anyone addressed it to the LORD. It simply shows that the God they wanted to shut out remained the Lord over their circumstances, and their suffering was not hidden from heaven.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


People can push God away and still find themselves crying toward heaven when trouble comes. I have done it myself, keeping Him at a distance until I was desperate. Scripture tells me “the LORD is near to those who have a broken heart” (Psalm 34:18), and His invitation to come to Him still stands. I don’t want to wait for a crisis. I want to welcome Him near before the cry comes.
