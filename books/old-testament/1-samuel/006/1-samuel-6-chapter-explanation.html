1 Samuel 6:1
1 Samuel 6:1
NKJV

1 Samuel 6:1 Now the ark of the LORD was in the country of the Philistines seven months.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 6:1 The Ark of the LORD remained in Philistine territory seven months in all. (NLT)

Main Idea

The ark of the LORD remains in Philistine territory for seven months. The long stay shows how much the Philistines suffered under the LORD’s hand before they were ready to give up their prize.

Verse Explanation

The ark of the LORD remains in Philistine territory for seven months. The long stay shows how much the Philistines suffered under the LORD’s hand before they were ready to give up their prize.

“Now the ark of the LORD was in the country of the Philistines seven months.”

The narrator steps back to measure the ark's captivity. “The ark of the LORD” is still called the LORD's ark, even while it sits “in the country of the Philistines.” Its capture had not changed its owner. “Seven months” marks how long the Philistines held on to their trophy while the plague spread from city to city (5:6–12). The length of time shows how stubbornly they clung to their prize before they were ready to let it go, and how long the LORD's hand rested on them before they asked what to do.

Study Notes

None.

1 Samuel 6:2
1 Samuel 6:2
NKJV

1 Samuel 6:2 And the Philistines called for the priests and the diviners, saying, "What shall we do with the ark of the LORD? Tell us how we should send it to its place."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 6:2 Then the Philistines called in their priests and diviners and asked them, “What should we do about the Ark of the LORD? Tell us how to return it to its own country.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The Philistines call their priests and diviners and ask what to do with the ark of the LORD and how to send it back to its place. The nation that captured the ark now wants only to be rid of it, and its own religious experts must find the way.

Verse Explanation

The Philistines call their priests and diviners and ask what to do with the ark of the LORD and how to send it back to its place. The nation that captured the ark now wants only to be rid of it, and its own religious experts must find the way.

“And the Philistines called for the priests and the diviners,”

Having failed to solve the problem by moving the ark from city to city (5:8–10), the Philistines turn to their religious experts. “The priests” served their gods, and “the diviners” claimed to discern hidden things by reading signs and omens. These are the very practices the Law forbade in Israel (Deuteronomy 18:10). The counsel that follows comes from these men, not from the LORD, and it must be read that way.

“saying, ‘What shall we do with the ark of the LORD? Tell us how we should send it to its place.’”

The question has moved on since 5:8. The lords then asked what to do with the ark; now the people assume it must go and ask only “how we should send it.” They speak of “its place,” recognizing that the ark belongs somewhere other than Philistia. The nation that captured the ark as a prize now wants only to return it safely.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “the diviners” is related to the word Deuteronomy 18:10 uses for the forbidden practice the NKJV renders “one who practices witchcraft.” Both come from a verb meaning to practice divination.

1 Samuel 6:3
1 Samuel 6:3
NKJV

1 Samuel 6:3 So they said, "If you send away the ark of the God of Israel, do not send it empty; but by all means return it to Him with a trespass offering. Then you will be healed, and it will be known to you why His hand is not removed from you."(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 6:3 “Send the Ark of the God of Israel back with a gift,” they were told. “Send a guilt offering so the plague will stop. Then, if you are healed, you will know it was his hand that caused the plague.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The priests and diviners advise that the ark of the God of Israel must not be sent back empty but returned to Him with a trespass offering. They acknowledge that their suffering is tied to guilt before Israel’s God, and they promise healing and an explanation of why His hand has not lifted.

Verse Explanation

The priests and diviners advise that the ark of the God of Israel must not be sent back empty but returned to Him with a trespass offering. They acknowledge that their suffering is tied to guilt before Israel’s God, and they promise healing and an explanation of why His hand has not lifted.

“So they said, ‘If you send away the ark of the God of Israel, do not send it empty;”

The priests and diviners answer with a warning: the ark must not go back “empty.” They assume that the God of Israel has been wronged and that the wrong must be acknowledged with a gift. Calling Him “the God of Israel” shows they now see the ark as belonging to a God who has acted against them.

“but by all means return it to Him with a trespass offering.”

“By all means” stresses how necessary they think the gift is. “A trespass offering” is an offering that admits guilt and makes amends. The Philistine priests use Israel's own word for it, but the content of the offering will be their own design, not something the LORD prescribed. Their counsel confesses guilt before Israel's God without placing them under His law.

“Then you will be healed, and it will be known to you why His hand is not removed from you.’”

They promise two results: healing from the plague, and understanding of “why His hand is not removed.” The second phrase admits that the LORD's hand is still on them. Their confidence about healing is the claim of pagan religious advisers; the chapter will show the LORD's hand at work, but it does not endorse their method as a pattern for healing.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “empty” here is the same word used in the Law's command that no one appear before the LORD “empty” (Exodus 23:15).

“Trespass offering” translates the Hebrew word for the guilt offering, the offering Leviticus prescribes when someone has wronged the holy things of the LORD (Leviticus 5:15). Some translations, such as the ESV and NIV, render it ‘guilt offering.’

NKJV

NLT

1 Samuel 6:4 “What sort of guilt offering should we send?” they asked. And they were told, “Since the plague has struck both you and your five rulers, make five gold tumors and five gold rats, just like those that have ravaged your land. (NLT)

Main Idea

When asked what the trespass offering should be, they prescribe five golden tumors and five golden rats, one for each Philistine lord, since the same plague struck the people and their rulers alike. The gifts are the Philistine priests’ own remedy, not a pattern God gave to Israel.

Verse Explanation

When asked what the trespass offering should be, they prescribe five golden tumors and five golden rats, one for each Philistine lord, since the same plague struck the people and their rulers alike. The gifts are the Philistine priests’ own remedy, not a pattern God gave to Israel.

“Then they said, ‘What is the trespass offering which we shall return to Him?’”

The people ask for specifics. They accept that a trespass offering must be made, and they want to know what will satisfy the God whose hand is on them.

“They answered, ‘Five golden tumors and five golden rats, according to the number of the lords of the Philistines.”

The answer is gold shaped like their affliction: “five golden tumors” and “five golden rats.” The number five matches “the lords of the Philistines,” the rulers of the five chief cities (6:17). The gifts confess in visible form what the LORD had done to them. They are the Philistine priests' own remedy, not a pattern God gave to Israel.

“For the same plague was on all of you and on your lords.”

The reason for one gift per lord is that “the same plague” had struck everyone, rulers and people alike. No city and no class had escaped the LORD's hand.

Study Notes

In 6:4 and 6:5, as in 5:6, 9, and 12, the written Hebrew text has one word for “tumors,” while the Jewish tradition of reading the text aloud substitutes a different Hebrew word, possibly a less blunt term for the same affliction.

The Hebrew word translated “rats” is the general word for a mouse or small rodent. The KJV and ESV render it ‘mice’; the NKJV and NIV have “rats.” The Hebrew text of 1 Samuel first mentions these animals here, though 6:5 shows they had been ravaging the land.

1 Samuel 6:5
1 Samuel 6:5
NKJV

1 Samuel 6:5 Therefore you shall make images of your tumors and images of your rats that ravage the land, and you shall give glory to the God of Israel; perhaps He will lighten His hand from you, from your gods, and from your land.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 6:5 Make these things to show honor to the God of Israel. Perhaps then he will stop afflicting you, your gods, and your land. (NLT)

Main Idea

They tell the people to make images of their tumors and of the rats ravaging the land and so give glory to the God of Israel, hoping He will lighten His hand from them, their gods, and their land. Even pagan priests must admit that the God of Israel is the one pressing down on them, though they can only say “perhaps” He will relent.

Verse Explanation

They tell the people to make images of their tumors and of the rats ravaging the land and so give glory to the God of Israel, hoping He will lighten His hand from them, their gods, and their land. Even pagan priests must admit that the God of Israel is the one pressing down on them, though they can only say “perhaps” He will relent.

“Therefore you shall make images of your tumors and images of your rats that ravage the land,”

The priests explain the gifts. “Images of your tumors” and “images of your rats” are models of the affliction itself. The phrase “that ravage the land” shows that the plague had struck the fields as well as the people. The Philistines were suffering both in body and in harvest.

“and you shall give glory to the God of Israel;”

The purpose of the gifts is to “give glory to the God of Israel.” In this setting, giving glory means openly acknowledging that He is the one who has acted and that He is in the right. Joshua used the same expression when he urged Achan to confess his sin (Joshua 7:19). The Philistines are admitting the truth about the God of Israel, though nothing in the chapter suggests they turn to worship Him.

“perhaps He will lighten His hand from you, from your gods, and from your land.”

Their hope is uncertain: “perhaps.” They cannot know whether the God of Israel will relent. “Lighten His hand” answers the description of His hand as “heavy” on them in 5:6 and 5:11. The words “from your gods” quietly admit that Dagon and their other gods had been struck as well (5:3–4, 7).

Study Notes

In Hebrew, “glory” in this verse comes from the word group built on the idea of weight, the same group behind the LORD's hand being “heavy” in 5:6 and 5:11. “Lighten” is its opposite, from a verb meaning to make light. The priests hope that giving the LORD His due weight will make His hand light on them.

1 Samuel 6:6 Don’t be stubborn and rebellious as Pharaoh and the Egyptians were. By the time God was finished with them, they were eager to let Israel go. (NLT)

Main Idea

The priests warn the Philistines not to harden their hearts as Pharaoh and the Egyptians did, recalling that Egypt finally let Israel go after the LORD did mighty things among them. The memory of the exodus becomes a warning against resisting the God whose hand they now feel.

Verse Explanation

The priests warn the Philistines not to harden their hearts as Pharaoh and the Egyptians did, recalling that Egypt finally let Israel go after the LORD did mighty things among them. The memory of the exodus becomes a warning against resisting the God whose hand they now feel.

“Why then do you harden your hearts as the Egyptians and Pharaoh hardened their hearts?”

The priests reach back to the exodus for a warning. The Philistines had already spoken of the God who struck Egypt (4:8). Now their own priests use Pharaoh as an example of what not to do. To “harden your hearts” is to refuse to yield after God has made His power plain.

“When He did mighty things among them, did they not let the people go, that they might depart?”

Egypt resisted, but in the end the Egyptians “let the people go.” The priests' point is practical: resistance did not change the outcome for Egypt; it only multiplied Egypt's suffering. The Philistines would be wise to release the ark now. The LORD's “mighty things” in Egypt were still remembered by Israel's enemies centuries later.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “harden” here means to make heavy. It is the same word group used for the LORD's heavy hand in chapter 5 and for Pharaoh hardening his heart in Exodus 8:15, 32.

1 Samuel 6:7
1 Samuel 6:7
NKJV

1 Samuel 6:7 Now therefore, make a new cart, take two milk cows which have never been yoked, and hitch the cows to the cart; and take their calves home, away from them.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 6:7 “Now build a new cart, and find two cows that have just given birth to calves. Make sure the cows have never been yoked to a cart. Hitch the cows to the cart, but shut their calves away from them in a pen. (NLT)

Main Idea

The priests direct them to make a new cart, hitch two milk cows that have never been yoked, and take the cows’ calves home away from them. The arrangement is set against nature, since untrained cows would not ordinarily pull a cart or leave their young.

Verse Explanation

The priests direct them to make a new cart, hitch two milk cows that have never been yoked, and take the cows’ calves home away from them. The arrangement is set against nature, since untrained cows would not ordinarily pull a cart or leave their young.

“Now therefore, make a new cart,”

The priests now give practical instructions. “A new cart” had never been used for ordinary work. To the Philistines this showed respect for the ark. It was not, however, the way God had told Israel to carry it. The Law assigned the ark to the Levites of Kohath, who carried the holy things on their shoulders (Numbers 4:15; 7:9).

“take two milk cows which have never been yoked, and hitch the cows to the cart;”

The cows are chosen to make the test difficult. “Milk cows” were nursing their young, and cows that “have never been yoked” had no training to pull a cart. Untrained animals would not be expected to pull steadily in one direction.

“and take their calves home, away from them.”

The final instruction makes the test harder still. With their calves shut up at home, the cows' natural instinct would be to turn back toward them. If the cows went away from their calves and toward Israel, the Philistines could not explain it as ordinary animal behavior.

Study Notes

The Law also required certain sacrificial animals that had never borne a yoke (Numbers 19:2). The Philistine priests may have been following a similar sense that unused animals were fitting for sacred purposes.

Verse Explanation

The ark of the LORD is to be set on the cart, with the gold articles of the trespass offering in a chest beside it, and then the cart is to be sent off and left to go. No one will drive or guide it.

“Then take the ark of the LORD and set it on the cart;”

The ark is to be placed on the cart itself, not carried by people. The Philistines handle it as they think best, without the poles and Levitical carriers God had appointed.

“and put the articles of gold which you are returning to Him as a trespass offering in a chest by its side.”

The golden tumors and rats are to travel in “a chest by its side,” separate from the ark. They are described as “articles of gold which you are returning to Him,” an admission that the gifts are owed to the God of Israel.

“Then send it away, and let it go.”

No one is to drive or lead the cart. The words “let it go” leave its direction entirely to the animals, which is the point of the test. The phrase recalls the language of release from 5:11 and the exodus memory of 6:6.

Verse Explanation

The Philistines are to watch the cart’s direction: if it goes up toward Beth Shemesh in Israel’s territory, then the God of Israel brought this great evil on them, but if not, the plague was mere chance. The test is meant to settle whether the LORD’s hand truly struck them.

“And watch: if it goes up the road to its own territory, to Beth Shemesh,”

The Philistines are told to “watch” where the cart goes. “Its own territory” means Israel's land, and “Beth Shemesh” was an Israelite town on the border of Judah (Joshua 15:10).

“then He has done us this great evil.”

If the cart goes to Beth Shemesh, the Philistines will know that the God of Israel brought “this great evil” on them. The word evil here means disaster or calamity, not moral wrong.

“But if not, then we shall know that it is not His hand that struck us—it happened to us by chance.”

The test leaves room for another explanation: that the plague “happened to us by chance.” Even after seven months, the Philistines want proof before they will be sure the LORD struck them. The outcome will settle the question.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “by chance” describes an event that happens without being planned or foreseen. The same word appears in Ruth 2:3, where Ruth “happened” to come to the part of the field that belonged to Boaz.

1 Samuel 6:12 Then the cows headed straight for the road to Beth Shemesh, and went along the highway, lowing as they went, and did not turn aside to the right hand or the left. And the lords of the Philistines went after them to the border of Beth Shemesh.(NKJV)

NLT

1 Samuel 6:12 And sure enough, without veering off in other directions, the cows went straight along the road toward Beth-shemesh, lowing as they went. The Philistine rulers followed them as far as the border of Beth-shemesh. (NLT)

Main Idea

The cows head straight for the road to Beth Shemesh, lowing as they go and never turning to the right or left, while the Philistine lords follow them to the border. Against their instinct to return to their calves, the cows carry the ark home, and the LORD’s hand is unmistakably shown.

Verse Explanation

The cows head straight for the road to Beth Shemesh, lowing as they go and never turning to the right or left, while the Philistine lords follow them to the border. Against their instinct to return to their calves, the cows carry the ark home, and the LORD’s hand is unmistakably shown.

“Then the cows headed straight for the road to Beth Shemesh, and went along the highway,”

The cows do what untrained cows separated from their calves would not normally do. They head “straight for the road to Beth Shemesh” and keep to “the highway,” the main road, without wandering.

“lowing as they went,”

“Lowing” is the sound cattle make. The cows were crying as they went, a sign that their instinct still pulled them back toward their calves. They felt the pull of home but did not follow it. Nothing in their nature explains their course.

“and did not turn aside to the right hand or the left.”

The steadiness of their path is stressed. They “did not turn aside” in either direction. The LORD's hand guided animals that had no reason of their own to go to Israel.

“And the lords of the Philistines went after them to the border of Beth Shemesh.”

The five rulers follow the cart all the way “to the border of Beth Shemesh.” They become eyewitnesses that no one interfered with the test and that the cows went straight to Israel.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “the highway” describes a raised or built-up public road, a main route rather than a field path.

The expression “did not turn aside to the right hand or the left” is the same kind of language used in the Law for keeping exactly to the LORD's command (Deuteronomy 5:32).

“The Levites took down the ark of the LORD and the chest that was with it, in which were the articles of gold,”

“The Levites” handle the ark. Beth Shemesh was one of the towns given to the priests, Aaron's descendants (Joshua 21:13–16), so men of Levi's tribe would have been present. The ark and the chest of gold are lifted down by those appointed to care for holy things.

“and put them on the large stone.”

They set the ark and the chest “on the large stone,” treating it as a fitting place to rest the ark in an open field.

“Then the men of Beth Shemesh offered burnt offerings and made sacrifices the same day to the LORD.”

The day of the ark's return becomes a day of worship. “Burnt offerings” were offered wholly to the LORD, and other “sacrifices” were made “the same day.” The town's first response to the ark's return is to worship.

The account does not explain how the sacrifice of the cows in 6:14 relates in time to the Levites' lifting down the ark here; 6:15 may summarize what was done that day.

NLT

Main Idea

The LORD strikes the men of Beth Shemesh because they looked into the ark of the LORD, and the NKJV reports fifty thousand and seventy men struck, while some translations read seventy. The people mourn the great slaughter, learning that the LORD’s holiness governs Israel just as it governed the Philistines.

Verse Explanation

The LORD strikes the men of Beth Shemesh because they looked into the ark of the LORD, and the NKJV reports fifty thousand and seventy men struck, while some translations read seventy. The people mourn the great slaughter, learning that the LORD’s holiness governs Israel just as it governed the Philistines.

“Then He struck the men of Beth Shemesh, because they had looked into the ark of the LORD.”

The scene turns sharply. The LORD “struck the men of Beth Shemesh,” and the reason is stated plainly: “because they had looked into the ark of the LORD.” Their joy at seeing the ark in 6:13 was not the offense. Looking into it was. The Law forbade even the Kohathites, who carried the holy things, to look on them while they were being covered (Numbers 4:20). The ark was holy, and treating it as an object for inspection was a violation of that holiness. The LORD who humbled the Philistines also requires His own people to honor His holiness (Leviticus 10:3).

“He struck fifty thousand and seventy men of the people,”

The NKJV gives the number as “fifty thousand and seventy men.” Some translations read seventy instead, and the number is difficult in the Hebrew text (see Study Notes). On either reading, the judgment was severe.

“and the people lamented because the LORD had struck the people with a great slaughter.”

The people “lamented,” mourning “because the LORD had struck the people with a great slaughter.” They understood the deaths as the LORD's judgment, not an accident. The day that began with joy at harvest ends in grief.

Study Notes

The traditional Hebrew text reads literally ‘seventy men, fifty thousand men,’ an unusual way of giving a number. A few Hebrew manuscripts read only ‘seventy men,’ and the NIV follows that reading. Other translations keep the traditional text, as the NKJV does.

The Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, reads differently in the first part of the verse, saying that some of the men were struck because they did not rejoice. The NRSV follows that reading. The Hebrew text followed by the NKJV states that they looked into the ark.
