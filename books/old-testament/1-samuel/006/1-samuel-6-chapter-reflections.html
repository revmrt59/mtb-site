Chapter Reflections

1 Samuel 6

Reflect and Respond
1 Samuel 6:1–9 — The Philistines Plan the Ark’s Return

Reflections

1 Samuel 6:3

[Foundational Truth] Guilty People Want to Give Something. The Philistine priests did not call the plague bad luck. They told their people, “do not send it empty; but by all means return it to Him with a trespass offering.” They knew they had wronged Israel’s God by seizing His ark, and they knew something had to be given. That is how guilt works in the human heart. People who know they are guilty feel that they must give or do something to make it right. The instinct is true: guilt before a holy God does not disappear when we move it somewhere else, and during the ark’s seven-month stay in Philistia the Philistines moved it from city to city, but the trouble followed it. But the remedy was their own invention. The priests even promised, “Then you will be healed,” yet the chapter never reports that the plague ended in Philistia. A confident religious promise is not the same as a promise God has made, and a procedure that seems to work does not prove that God is behind it. The question is not whether I feel I must give something, but whether I am trusting what God Himself has said and provided. “He who covers his sins will not prosper, But whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy” (Proverbs 28:13).

[Christ & Life] The Offering God Himself Would Provide. The Hebrew word for “trespass offering” is asham, the guilt offering Israel’s law required when something holy had been wronged (Leviticus 5:15). Isaiah uses that same word when he describes the suffering Servant of the LORD: “When You make His soul an offering for sin” (Isaiah 53:10). The Philistine priests promised, “Then you will be healed.” Isaiah says of the Servant, “by His stripes we are healed” (Isaiah 53:5). The priests sensed a true principle, that guilt before God must be paid for, but they could only guess at what might satisfy Him, and golden images could never pay that debt. God answered the need Himself. The golden tumors and rats are not a picture of Christ; the connection is the word and the need. What the Philistines could not provide, God provided in His Servant, Jesus. I do not bring God a payment and hope it is enough. I trust the offering He has already given.

1 Samuel 6:5

[Christ & Life] The Light Burden in Place of the Heavy Hand. The priests hoped that if the Philistines gave glory to the God of Israel, “perhaps He will lighten His hand.” For months that hand had been “heavy” on them (5:6, 11). Jesus speaks to people who are weighed down: “Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest… For My yoke is easy and My burden is light” (Matthew 11:28, 30). The connection is one of theme, not of a shared word. Samuel’s “lighten” is a Hebrew verb and Jesus’ “light” is a Greek word, and Jesus is speaking of discipleship, not of a plague. But the contrast is worth dwelling on. The Philistines could only hope that the weight of God’s judgment might be eased. In Christ, the weight of judgment against sin has been carried, and He gives His people a different load, His yoke, which He calls light. Am I still carrying a weight Jesus has invited me to lay down?

1 Samuel 6:7

[Application] The New Cart: A Reverent-Looking Method God Did Not Give. By pagan standards, a new cart and cows that had never been yoked showed respect. Things set apart from ordinary use seemed fitting for something holy. But God had already said how the ark was to travel: on the shoulders of the Kohathites, a clan of Levites (Numbers 4:15; 7:9). God brought His ark home on the Philistines’ cart anyway. They did not know His law. Years later, though, David copied their method: “they set the ark of God on a new cart” (2 Samuel 6:3). When the oxen stumbled, Uzzah took hold of the ark and died (2 Samuel 6:6–7). David afterward confessed, “we did not consult Him about the proper order” (1 Chronicles 15:13), and the Levites carried the ark “on their shoulders, by its poles, as Moses had commanded” (1 Chronicles 15:15). What God bore with from Philistines who did not have His word, He did not accept from His own people who did. A method can look reverent and still not be obedience. When I decide how to serve or worship God, have I asked what He has actually said?

Section Reflections

[Insight] True Words, Wrong Guidance. The Philistine priests and diviners said several true things. They knew that guilt must be answered (v. 3). They told their people to give glory to the God of Israel (v. 5). They remembered Pharaoh’s hard heart and warned against repeating it (v. 6). In some ways they remembered the exodus better than Israel had in chapter 4. Yet the same advisers prescribed golden images of tumors and rats, promised healing God had not promised, and set up a test by divination. True observations were mixed with guidance God never gave. That is still a danger. A speaker can use biblical words, tell true stories about God and quote real principles, and still lead people into a practice Scripture does not support. Biblical vocabulary is not the same as biblical authority. Paul’s instruction is simple: “Test all things; hold fast what is good” (1 Thessalonians 5:21). The Philistines also show that knowing true things about God is not the same as knowing God. Their acknowledgment of the God of Israel was real, but it did not make them His worshipers. When I weigh advice about God, do I weigh it against what He has said, or only against how spiritual it sounds?

1 Samuel 6:10–18 — The Ark Comes to Beth Shemesh

Reflections

1 Samuel 6:10

[Insight] Careful Obedience to the Wrong Voice. “Then the men did so.” The Philistines followed their priests’ instructions to the letter: two milk cows, the cart, the calves shut up at home. Their advice came from priests and diviners, and they obeyed it exactly. Israel in chapter 4 had something far better, the word of the LORD coming through Samuel to all Israel (4:1), and the account gives no sign that anyone sought it before the army marched out. Chapter 4 does not record Samuel giving a command that Israel disobeyed, so the contrast is not between two broken orders. It is between people who did carefully what their advisers said and people who did not ask what God had said. Many people are careful to follow a doctor, a financial adviser or a coach, and casual about the word of God.

1 Samuel 6:12

[Discovery] The Highway. The cows “went along the highway.” The Hebrew word for highway here is mesillah, a built-up road. Isaiah uses the same word in his call to prepare for the LORD’s coming: “Make straight in the desert A highway for our God” (Isaiah 40:3). In 1 Samuel 6 the LORD brings His ark home along a highway. In Isaiah the highway is prepared for the LORD Himself to come to His people, and the Gospels apply Isaiah’s voice in the wilderness to John the Baptist preparing the way for Jesus. The narrator of 1 Samuel does not point to Isaiah, and Isaiah was written later, so this is an echo rather than a prophecy. But it is a fitting one: the God who brought His ark home on a highway is the God who came to His people in His Son.

1 Samuel 6:13

[Discovery] Wheat Harvest and the Feast of Weeks. The ark came home while the people of Beth Shemesh “were reaping their wheat harvest.” In Israel’s calendar, wheat harvest was the season of the Feast of Weeks: “you shall observe the Feast of Weeks, of the firstfruits of wheat harvest” (Exodus 34:22). Centuries later that feast was called Pentecost, and “When the Day of Pentecost had fully come” (Acts 2:1) the Holy Spirit was poured out on the believers. The text of 1 Samuel 6 does not mention the feast, and we should not claim the ark arrived on the feast day. But the season invites a quiet comparison. At wheat harvest God brought the sign of His presence back to His people, and at the harvest feast of Pentecost He gave His people His own presence by the Spirit.

1 Samuel 6:14

[Christ & Life] The Carriers Became the Offering. The cows that carried the ark home did not go back to the field. They were offered “as a burnt offering to the LORD,” and the cart was split for the fire. What had carried God’s presence became the offering. Christians have long seen in this a picture of Christ. He came bearing the glory of God, “and we beheld His glory” (John 1:14), and then gave Himself as the offering: Christ “has loved us and given Himself for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God” (Ephesians 5:2). Some go further and see the wood of the cart as the wood of the cross. That is a devotional picture, not something the narrator says, and the cows were not willing as Christ was. The picture should not be pressed into a meaning for every detail. But the shape of the scene is worth holding: the One who brought God near to us is the One who was offered for us.

[Discovery] The Field of Joshua. The cart stopped “in the field of Joshua of Beth Shemesh.” This Joshua was a local farmer, not Joshua the son of Nun. His name was common in Israel. Yet it means “the LORD saves,” and it is the Hebrew form of the name Jesus. The angel told Joseph, “you shall call His name JESUS, for He will save His people from their sins” (Matthew 1:21). The narrator gives the name no special meaning, so this is an echo for meditation, not an interpretation of the verse. Still, it is fitting that the ark came to rest, and offerings were made, in the field of a man whose name proclaimed that salvation belongs to the LORD.

1 Samuel 6:15

[Application] The Ark in the Proper Hands. After its ride on a Philistine cart, the ark was taken down by “the Levites,” the tribe the LORD had set apart “to bear the ark of the covenant of the LORD” (Deuteronomy 10:8). Beth Shemesh was one of the towns given to the priests (Joshua 21:13–16), so Levites were at hand. The narrator makes a point of telling us who took the ark down. He does not describe every detail of how it was handled, and he does not pronounce the whole celebration correct. The burnt offering of verse 14 is one example: Israel’s law said a burnt offering from the herd was to be “a male without blemish” (Leviticus 1:3), and these were cows. The chapter does not name that as the reason for the judgment that follows; verse 19 gives a different reason. But the report is not a blanket approval either. Gladness and the presence of the right people did not make every part of the day obedient. Honoring God in the way He has shown is part of loving Him well. Grateful hearts still need to ask what He has said.

1 Samuel 6:16

[Application] Witnesses Who Go Home. The five lords of the Philistines had seen everything: cows going straight up the road against their instincts, reapers rejoicing, offerings rising to the LORD. They had their answer to their own test. It was His hand. Then “they returned to Ekron the same day.” The text records no turning to the LORD. In verse 15 Israel worshiped “the same day”; in verse 16 the lords went home “the same day.” One day, one field, two responses. Seeing God’s work, and even admitting it, is not the same as turning to Him. Many people have watched God answer prayer, heard a testimony they could not explain, or felt His hand in a crisis, and gone home unchanged. Am I a witness of what God does, or one who turns to Him?

1 Samuel 6:18

[Discovery] A Stone That Remains. The narrator notes that the large stone where the ark was set “remains to this day in the field of Joshua of Beth Shemesh.” Scripture often marks God’s works with stones. Jacob set up the stone he had slept on as a pillar at Bethel (Genesis 28:18). Joshua set up stones from the Jordan “for a memorial to the children of Israel forever” (Joshua 4:7). In the next chapter Samuel will set up a stone and call it Ebenezer, “Thus far the LORD has helped us” (7:12). This stone marked the place where God brought the ark home, and it stood near the place where His holiness was solemnly shown in 6:19. It remembered both mercy and holiness. God’s people need both memories. Remembering only His kindness makes us careless; remembering only His holiness makes us afraid. What reminders of His faithfulness am I keeping where I will see them?

Section Reflections

[Foundational Truth] God Brings Himself Home. Across this section Israel does nothing to recover the ark. No army marches out, no priest goes to fetch it, and no one drives the cart. Two untrained cows walk a straight road, the enemy’s own lords follow behind, and the ark arrives in a field where people are reaping wheat. God brought the sign of His presence back to His people by His own initiative. That is the shape of grace. Jesus said, “the Son of Man has come to seek and to save that which was lost” (Luke 19:10), and John writes, “We love Him because He first loved us” (1 John 4:19). Salvation does not begin with our search for God. It begins with His coming to us. The people of Beth Shemesh only had to lift their eyes. The same God rules the circumstances we cannot steer. Two untrained cows did what no one could have made them do, while the lords of Philistia could only follow. Paul writes, “we know that all things work together for good to those who love God, to those who are the called according to His purpose” (Romans 8:28). That does not mean God approves every act that happens, or that the road will be easy. It means His purposes do not depend on our controlling everything. Before I took a single step toward God, He was already on the road toward me, and He is still directing the road.

1 Samuel 6:19–21 — Judgment at Beth Shemesh

Reflections

1 Samuel 6:19

[Christ & Life] The Holy Law and the Mercy Seat. Inside the ark lay the tablets of God’s law (Deuteronomy 10:5). Over the ark was the mercy seat, where the high priest sprinkled atoning blood on the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16:14) and where God promised to meet His people (Exodus 25:22). Read as a picture, the holy law apart from the covering God provides can only condemn. Spurgeon put it in one sentence: the law, when it is not covered by the mercy seat, is death to anyone. The narrator does not describe how the ark was opened, and he names the looking, not this picture, as the reason for the judgment. But the picture fits the rest of Scripture. Paul says God set Christ forth “as a propitiation by His blood” (Romans 3:25). A propitiation is a sacrifice that turns away God’s just anger against sin. The Greek word Paul uses is the same word Hebrews uses for the “mercy seat” (Hebrews 9:5). Christ is the covering God has provided. The only safe place for a sinner to come near a holy God is under the mercy He Himself has given.

1 Samuel 6:20

[Christ & Life] Who Is Able to Stand? The men of Beth Shemesh asked exactly the right question: “Who is able to stand before this holy LORD God?” Hannah had sung, “No one is holy like the LORD” (2:2), and now a whole town felt it. Scripture keeps asking the same question. “Who may ascend into the hill of the LORD? Or who may stand in His holy place?” (Psalm 24:3). “If You, LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand?” (Psalm 130:3). “Who can stand when He appears?” (Malachi 3:2). At the end, “who is able to stand?” (Revelation 6:17). And Scripture gives the answer. No one can stand in his own right. But through Christ “we have access by faith into this grace in which we stand” (Romans 5:2), and John saw a great multitude standing before the throne in robes made “white in the blood of the Lamb” (Revelation 7:14). The question of Beth Shemesh finds its answer at the cross.

[Application] Holiness Felt, and God Sent Away. The second question of Beth Shemesh tells a sadder story: “to whom shall it go up from us?” Like the Philistines before them, they wanted the ark gone. The Philistines had said, “The ark of the God of Israel must not remain with us” (5:7). Pagan cities and an Israelite town both felt God’s holiness and both wanted His presence somewhere else. Beth Shemesh did not ask how to receive the ark rightly, though Levites were among them (6:15). Feeling God’s holiness can lead to repentance and a right approach, or it can lead to keeping Him at a distance. Holiness is uncomfortable to people who have not been cleansed. David later asked almost the same thing, “How can the ark of the LORD come to me?” (2 Samuel 6:9), until he learned to bring it God’s way (1 Chronicles 15:13–15). David’s fear turned into obedience, and the ark came to Jerusalem with gladness. The gospel does not answer God’s holiness by sending Him away. It makes sinners fit to come near: “let us draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, having our hearts sprinkled from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water” (Hebrews 10:22). When God’s holiness makes me uncomfortable, the answer is not distance but cleansing. When I sense how holy He is, do I back away from Him, or ask how to come near in the way He has made?

1 Samuel 6:21

[Christ & Life] The Ark Waits for a People and a King. The chapter ends unfinished. The ark goes to Kirjath Jearim, where it will be received with care (7:1) and will stay until David, the LORD’s anointed king, comes to bring it up (2 Samuel 6:2–3). The twenty years of 7:2 lead up to Israel’s lament; they are not the whole of the ark’s stay. Psalm 132 remembers the search for the ark, “We found it in the fields of the woods” (Psalm 132:6), words many interpreters take as a reference to Kirjath Jearim, “town of forests.” The same psalm prays, “Do not turn away the face of Your Anointed” (Psalm 132:10). Even when His people respond poorly, God keeps working out His purposes. His presence among His people moves toward the anointed king and, beyond him, toward Christ, the Son of David, in whom God has come to stay.

[Application] The Glory Comes Near, but Are We Ready? Beth Shemesh rejoiced to see the ark and then sent it on to Kirjath Jearim. It is not enough for God’s presence to come near. His people must be ready to receive Him. Beth Shemesh had the joy of the ark’s return without the readiness to keep it rightly, and a people not ready for God’s presence will keep it at arm’s length. Israel would not be ready until chapter 7, when they lamented after the LORD, put away their foreign gods and returned to Him with all their hearts (7:2–3). The same is true for us. God may come near in a season of blessing, in a revival, or in a moment of answered prayer, and we may be glad. The question is whether our hearts have been prepared to welcome Him and keep Him, or only to enjoy the moment.

Section Reflections

[Application] Joy, Worship, Death, Fear. The second half of the chapter moves through four responses to God’s presence: joy at the ark’s return (v. 13), worship with offerings (vv. 14–15), the irreverence of looking into the ark and the death that followed (v. 19), and finally fear (v. 20). The day began with harvest gladness and ended with “the people lamented.” The joy was not the sin; the looking was. Yet joy did not protect them from treating holy things lightly. God’s presence is both a delight and a danger, and both are true at the same time. Scripture holds them together: “Serve the LORD with fear, And rejoice with trembling” (Psalm 2:11). A church can be warm, glad and full of energy and still grow careless about the holiness of God.

1 Samuel 6:1–21 — The Whole Chapter

Reflections

Section Reflections

[Christ & Life] The Holy Presence Needs a Mercy Seat. Chapter 6 is framed by guilt and atonement. It begins with the Philistines trying to answer their guilt with a trespass offering of gold that their own priests devised (vv. 3–5). It ends with the men of Beth Shemesh struck for looking into the ark and asking, “Who is able to stand before this holy LORD God?” (vv. 19–20). In between, God’s holiness is the same whether the ark is in Philistia or in Israel. But the two peoples were not in the same position. The Philistines had no word from God and could only invent a remedy. Israel had what God had given: a priesthood, sacrifices of atonement, and clear instructions for approaching His holy things. Levites were there at Beth Shemesh to take down the ark (6:15). Their problem was not that God had provided no way, but that they did not honor the way He had revealed. The ark itself pointed to that way: over the tablets of the law God placed the mercy seat, where atoning blood was sprinkled. That covering was fulfilled in Christ, as the reflection at 6:19 explains. God’s presence is good news only for those who come under the atonement He has provided, and in Christ that atonement has been made once for all.

1 Samuel 6. The ark comes home, and God brings it Himself. The Philistines, who could only guess at God through priests and diviners, send it back with gold and a test they hoped might point to chance, and God answers in a way no one can explain away. Two cows walk a straight road, and the reapers of Beth Shemesh rejoice. But joy that grows careless with God’s holiness ends in lament, and the chapter closes with the question, “Who is able to stand before this holy LORD God?” God came near; His people were not yet ready to receive Him. The answer to their question is the offering God Himself provided in Christ.

What God may be saying: Perhaps something like this: I cannot get rid of my guilt by moving it, and I cannot satisfy God with my own remedies. He has provided the offering. He does not wait for me to bring Him home; He comes to me. And when He comes near, the right response is not to look on Him casually or to send Him away, but to rejoice with trembling and come close in the way He has made, the way of Christ.
