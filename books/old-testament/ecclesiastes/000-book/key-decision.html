Book Questions

Did Solomon write Ecclesiastes? (Ecclesiastes 1:1, 12)

The book opens with “the words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem” (1:1), and the Preacher says, “I, the Preacher, was king over Israel in Jerusalem” (1:12). His unmatched wisdom, wealth, and building projects (1:16; 2:4–9) fit Solomon. MTB holds that Solomon is the author and the Preacher.

Many scholars think a later writer spoke through a Solomon-like voice. Others point to the places where the book speaks about the Preacher in the third person (1:1–2; 7:27; 12:8–10). MTB treats those as literary framing that does not require a second author. The book gives no date, and it does not describe a specific moment of repentance in Solomon’s life. Some scholars also propose that a later editor added the closing words (12:9–14) to give the book an orthodox ending. MTB reads those words as part of the one inspired book, and its conclusion governs how the whole is heard.

Read: Ecclesiastes 1:1–2, Ecclesiastes 1:12–18, Ecclesiastes 2:4–11, Ecclesiastes 12:8–10, 1 Kings 4:29–34

How should we read a book that says “all is vanity”? (Ecclesiastes 1:2–3; 12:13–14)

“Vanity of vanities, all is vanity” (1:2). The Hebrew word behind “vanity” pictures breath or vapor. MTB understands it as “meaningless”: the emptiness and futility of life pursued without God. In some places the stress falls on how brief life is or how effort is frustrated.

MTB reads the book as Solomon’s deliberate test. He asks what life amounts to “under the sun,” when human reasoning, earthly achievement, and death set the limits, and God’s revelation and final judgment are left out of the calculation. “What profit has a man from all his labor in which he toils under the sun?” (1:3). The answer, within that horizon, is that it all comes to nothing.

That emptiness prepares the reader for God’s answer. Along the way the book speaks of God’s gifts (2:24), of eternity in the heart (3:11), and of His judgment (3:17), and it ends with the conclusion that governs everything: “Fear God and keep His commandments, for this is man’s all. For God will bring every work into judgment” (12:13–14). The gospel gives the fuller answer in Christ, who is “greater than Solomon” (Matthew 12:42) and who makes labor in the Lord not in vain (1 Corinthians 15:58).

Not every sentence is a mistaken conclusion. Follow each passage: some voice the earthly calculation, some recognize God, and some state the book’s answer.

Read: Ecclesiastes 1:2–3, Ecclesiastes 12:8–14, Matthew 12:42, Romans 8:20–21, 1 Corinthians 15:20–26, 58 · Related: Does the call to eat, drink, and enjoy mean the Preacher recommends living for pleasure?, Does “the dead know nothing” mean there is no conscious life after death?

Does the call to eat, drink, and enjoy mean the Preacher recommends living for pleasure? (Ecclesiastes 2:24; 9:7–10)

Several times the book commends enjoying food, drink, work, marriage, and youth (2:24–26; 3:12–13, 22; 5:18–20; 8:15; 9:7–10; 11:7–10). MTB reads these passages positively. They are part of the book’s answer, not cynical advice.

The key is where the joy comes from. “This also, I saw, was from the hand of God” (2:24). Solomon’s attempt to manufacture satisfaction through pleasure and possessions failed (2:1–11). Receiving life gratefully as God’s gift is something different. That joy always stays joined to reverence and accountability: “know that for all these God will bring you into judgment” (11:9). The New Testament speaks the same way of “the living God, who gives us richly all things to enjoy” (1 Timothy 6:17).

Read: Ecclesiastes 2:24–26, Ecclesiastes 5:18–20, Ecclesiastes 9:7–10, Ecclesiastes 11:9, 1 Timothy 6:17 · Related: What does “God has already accepted your works” mean?

Does “the dead know nothing” mean there is no conscious life after death? (Ecclesiastes 3:19–21; 9:5, 10)

Solomon writes that humans and animals “all have one breath” and “all return to dust” (3:19–20), that “the dead know nothing” (9:5), and that “there is no work or device or knowledge or wisdom in the grave” (9:10).

MTB reads these as death seen within the earthly horizon of Solomon’s test. He says so himself: the dead “nevermore will... have a share in anything done under the sun” (9:6). These verses do not give a full teaching on the afterlife, and they do not teach soul sleep or annihilation. The same book says “the spirit will return to God who gave it” (12:7) and that God will judge every work (12:14). Scripture elsewhere speaks of conscious life with Christ after death (Luke 23:43; Philippians 1:23), and Christ “has abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel” (2 Timothy 1:10).

The weight of Solomon’s questions should still be felt, especially by those who grieve.

Read: Ecclesiastes 3:18–22, Ecclesiastes 9:4–10, Ecclesiastes 12:7, 14, Luke 23:43, Philippians 1:21–23, 2 Timothy 1:10 · Related: How should we read a book that says “all is vanity”?

Does Ecclesiastes contradict itself and Proverbs? (Ecclesiastes 2:17, 24; 9:11)

Ecclesiastes can sound as if it disagrees with itself. Solomon says, “Therefore I hated life” (2:17), yet soon commends enjoying food and work as “from the hand of God” (2:24). It can also seem to question Proverbs, which teaches that “the hand of the diligent makes rich” (Proverbs 10:4), when it observes that “The race is not to the swift, Nor the battle to the strong” (9:11).

MTB reads the two books as complementary, not contradictory. Proverbs describes the patterns God has built into life, and its sayings are general patterns rather than unconditional promises. Ecclesiastes adds that time and chance, injustice, and death can overturn those patterns in a fallen world. Within Ecclesiastes, some passages voice the earthly calculation of life without God, some recognize God’s gifts and judgment, and the conclusion governs the whole: “Fear God and keep His commandments, For this is man’s all” (12:13).

Read: Ecclesiastes 2:17–26, Ecclesiastes 9:11–12, Ecclesiastes 12:13–14, Proverbs 10:4 · Related: How should we read a book that says “all is vanity”?

How should we read the book’s harsh laments over oppression and suffering? (Ecclesiastes 4:1–3)

Solomon sees “the tears of the oppressed, But they have no comforter” (4:1). He praises “the dead who were already dead, More than the living” and says that better than both “is he who has never existed” (4:2–3).

MTB hears these as honest protest against real injustice. The praise of the dead and the unborn shows how bleak suffering looks when life is measured only “under the sun.” They are anguished comparisons made from that horizon, not God’s final verdict on the worth of human life. The book itself answers that “God shall judge the righteous and the wicked” (3:17) and will bring every work into judgment (12:14), so oppressors are not beyond His reach.

The weight of these laments should still be felt, especially by those who suffer. The gospel answers that pain in Christ without dismissing it.

Read: Ecclesiastes 3:16–17, Ecclesiastes 4:1–3, Ecclesiastes 12:13–14 · Related: Does “the dead know nothing” mean there is no conscious life after death?

Ecclesiastes 3 · 2 questions

Does “a time to kill... a time to hate” make these things right? (vv. 1–8)

“To everything there is a season, a time for every purpose under heaven” (v. 1). The poem surveys the changing circumstances of human life, and verses 9–15 set them within God’s ordering of time and the limits of human understanding.

The poem does not declare every act of killing, hatred, or war morally right because it appears in the list. It calls for humility and discernment about fitting responses. It also does not give any reader a precise divine timetable, and it does not explain every church’s decline as a missed season. As a spiritual application, MTB draws from these verses discernment for personal life, relationships, work, ministry, and church life.

Read: Ecclesiastes 3:1–15

What does “He has put eternity in their hearts” mean? (v. 11)

“He has made everything beautiful in its time. Also He has put eternity in their hearts, except that no one can find out the work that God does from beginning to end” (v. 11). MTB understands this as a longing for eternity that God has placed in every person. We were made for life with Him, and earthly achievements can never finally satisfy that longing.

The verse also states a limit: we cannot understand all God’s work. Translations and interpreters differ on the expression; some take it as an awareness of the whole span of time. MTB finds the longing fulfilled in knowing God through Jesus Christ: “this is eternal life, that they may know You” (John 17:3).

Read: Ecclesiastes 3:9–15, John 17:3

Ecclesiastes 4 · 1 question

Is the “threefold cord” a picture of God in a marriage? (v. 12)

“Two are better than one” (v. 9). The passage commends companionship: two share the reward of work, lift each other up, keep each other warm, and stand together against attack. “And a threefold cord is not quickly broken” (v. 12) strengthens the picture of strength through union.

MTB applies the threefold cord to marriage and Christian fellowship sustained by God, where He is present among His people (Matthew 18:20). That is a fitting application, but the verse itself does not name God as the third strand, and the number three does not teach the Trinity. These verses speak of companionship broadly, not only of marriage.

Read: Ecclesiastes 4:9–12, Matthew 18:20

Ecclesiastes 5 · 1 question

Who is “the messenger of God” in verse 6? (v. 6)

“Do not let your mouth cause your flesh to sin, nor say before the messenger of God that it was an error” (v. 6). The setting is the house of God and the making of vows (vv. 1, 4–5). MTB strongly favors a priest or temple official who received the vow. Malachi 2:7 calls the priest “the messenger of the LORD of hosts.”

The Hebrew word can also mean an angel, and some read it that way. Either way, the warning is plain: do not try to excuse a broken promise to God as a mistake.

Read: Ecclesiastes 5:1–7, Malachi 2:7

Ecclesiastes 7 · 2 questions

Does “Do not be overly righteous” mean a little sin is acceptable? (vv. 15–18)

Solomon has seen “a just man who perishes in his righteousness, and... a wicked man who prolongs life in his wickedness” (v. 15). Then he says, “Do not be overly righteous... Do not be overly wicked” (vv. 16–17).

MTB reads verses 16–17 as the calculation that follows if visible outcomes are all that count: avoid righteousness that costs too much, and avoid wickedness that ruins you early. Solomon is exposing where earthly expediency leads. Verse 18 brings the fear of God into view, and 12:13–14 gives the book’s answer.

Many interpreters read verse 16 instead as a warning against self-righteousness or pride in one’s own wisdom. Either way, the passage never teaches moderation in obedience or a permitted amount of wickedness.

Read: Ecclesiastes 7:15–18, Ecclesiastes 12:13–14 · Related: How should we read a book that says “all is vanity”?

Is verse 28 saying women are worse than men? (vv. 26–29)

Solomon finds “more bitter than death the woman whose heart is snares and nets” (v. 26), and says, “One man among a thousand I have found, but a woman among all these I have not found” (v. 28).

MTB reads this against Solomon’s own life. He had “seven hundred wives, princesses, and three hundred concubines,” and in his old age “his wives turned his heart after other gods” (1 Kings 11:3–4). The king who looked for satisfaction outside God’s will was caught in the very snare he describes. The thousand recalls his own household, though Ecclesiastes does not say so outright.

The passage exposes Solomon’s disobedience and his distorted search, not the inferiority of women. Verse 29 widens the diagnosis to all humanity: “God made man upright, but they have sought out many schemes.” The numbers are not a statistic about men and women.

Read: Ecclesiastes 7:23–29, 1 Kings 11:1–8

Ecclesiastes 9 · 2 questions

What does “God has already accepted your works” mean? (v. 7)

“Go, eat your bread with joy, and drink your wine with a merry heart; for God has already accepted your works” (v. 7). MTB reads this as encouragement to receive ordinary life and faithful work with confidence in God’s favor, instead of suspecting that every enjoyment displeases Him.

It is not a statement that salvation comes by works, and it is not blanket approval of whatever anyone does. The same book calls for the fear of God and warns of judgment (11:9; 12:13–14).

Read: Ecclesiastes 9:7–10, Ecclesiastes 11:9 · Related: Does the call to eat, drink, and enjoy mean the Preacher recommends living for pleasure?

Is the poor wise man a picture of Christ? (vv. 13–16)

A great king besieged a little city, “and he by his wisdom delivered the city. Yet no one remembered that same poor man” (v. 15). The immediate lesson is that “wisdom is better than strength,” yet “the poor man’s wisdom is despised” (v. 16).

MTB also sees here a picture of Christ: poor for our sakes (2 Corinthians 8:9), the wisdom of God that saves (1 Corinthians 1:23–25), yet despised and rejected. The besieged city may picture humanity under the bondage of sin and death (Hebrews 2:14–15). This picture grows out of the story’s own lesson, and not every detail has a spiritual counterpart.

Read: Ecclesiastes 9:13–18, 2 Corinthians 8:9, 1 Corinthians 1:23–25, Hebrews 2:14–15

Ecclesiastes 10 · 1 question

Does “money answers everything” mean money is the answer to life? (v. 19)

“A feast is made for laughter, and wine makes merry; but money answers everything” (v. 19). MTB’s preferred reading is a practical observation: money supplies ordinary needs, including the food and wine just mentioned. Some read it instead as the outlook of the self-indulgent rulers described in verses 16–17.

Either way, the book has already shown that money cannot satisfy those who love it or save anyone from death: “He who loves silver will not be satisfied with silver” (5:10). Money is useful. It cannot buy righteousness, lasting satisfaction, or escape from God’s judgment.

Read: Ecclesiastes 10:16–19, Ecclesiastes 5:10–16

Ecclesiastes 11 · 1 question

Does “Cast your bread upon the waters” promise a return on giving? (vv. 1–2)

“Cast your bread upon the waters, for you will find it after many days. Give a serving to seven, and also to eight, for you do not know what evil will be on the earth” (vv. 1–2). MTB’s preferred reading is a call to generosity: do good and share broadly, even when the future is uncertain. Some take it as advice about overseas trade or spreading out investments.

It is not a prosperity formula. It does not promise financial multiplication, set a date for a return, or require exactly seven or eight recipients. It calls for open-handed trust, not an attempt to control God through giving.

Read: Ecclesiastes 11:1–6, Proverbs 11:24–25

Ecclesiastes 12 · 2 questions

Is Ecclesiastes 12 describing old age? (vv. 1–7)

“Remember now your Creator in the days of your youth, before the difficult days come” (v. 1). MTB’s preferred reading takes the images that follow as a picture of the aging body. The trembling “keepers of the house” suggest hands and arms; the “strong men” bowing, weakening legs; the “grinders” that cease, teeth; the darkening “windows,” failing eyes; the almond blossom, white hair (vv. 3, 5). Other images suggest failing hearing, disturbed sleep, fear, frailty, and fading desire.

The silver cord, golden bowl, pitcher, and wheel (v. 6) picture life’s final collapse. Some compare them to the spinal cord, head, lungs, and heart; these are suggestions, not medical claims. Some interpreters read the poem instead as a household or a wider calamity. The purpose is clear: remember, love, and serve your Creator now, before “the spirit will return to God who gave it” (v. 7). The poem does not lessen the worth of older people.

Read: Ecclesiastes 12:1–7, 2 Corinthians 4:16

Who is the “one Shepherd” in verse 11?

“The words of the wise are like goads, and the words of scholars are like well-driven nails, given by one Shepherd” (v. 11). MTB’s preferred reading identifies the one Shepherd as God, the source of true wisdom. Scripture often calls God the Shepherd of His people (Genesis 49:24; Psalm 23:1; Ezekiel 34:11–16), and Jesus calls Himself “the good shepherd” (John 10:11).

The verse does not name Jesus directly, and the goads and nails are not a prediction of the crucifixion. They picture instruction that prods and holds firm.

Read: Ecclesiastes 12:9–12, Psalm 23:1, Ezekiel 34:11–16, John 10:11
