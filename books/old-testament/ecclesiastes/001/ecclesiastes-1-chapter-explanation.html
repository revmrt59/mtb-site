Ecclesiastes 1

Verse-by-Verse
Ecclesiastes 1:1
Ecclesiastes 1:1
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:1 The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:1 These are the words of the Teacher, King David’s son, who ruled in Jerusalem. (NLT)

Main Idea

The book opens by naming its speaker as “the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem,” titles that identify him as Solomon. A framing voice introduces him in the third person, so what follows comes to us as the teaching of a king who had every resource to test what life can offer.

Verse Explanation

The book opens by naming its speaker as “the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem,” titles that identify him as Solomon. A framing voice introduces him in the third person, so what follows comes to us as the teaching of a king who had every resource to test what life can offer.

The words of the Preacher tells the reader whose teaching fills the book. “The Preacher” is the NKJV’s title for the speaker; in Hebrew the title is Qoheleth. The title presents him as someone who speaks to others, and the book’s closing frame confirms that role: “because the Preacher was wise, he still taught the people knowledge” (12:9). What follows is instruction given to God’s people, not a private journal of complaints.

the son of David, king in Jerusalem gives two titles that identify the Preacher as Solomon: he is David’s son, and he reigns from Jerusalem, the royal city. In verse 12 he makes the same claim in his own voice, “I, the Preacher, was king over Israel in Jerusalem.” His later account of unmatched wisdom (verse 16) and of houses, gardens, servants, and treasure (2:4–9) fits the king who followed David on the throne. This identification matters for everything that follows. No one in Israel had more wisdom, wealth, or freedom to test what life can offer, so when this king reports that his search came up empty, his verdict cannot be blamed on a lack of opportunity.

The verse also speaks about the Preacher rather than in his voice. A framing voice introduces him in the third person, and the same voice reports his verdict in verse 2. This is a literary frame placed around Solomon’s own account, which begins in the first person at verse 12. It does not mean that a second author wrote the book.

Study Notes

The NLT renders this verse “These are the words of the Teacher, King David’s son, who ruled in Jerusalem.” “Teacher” and the NKJV’s “Preacher” are two English renderings of the same Hebrew title, Qoheleth. The NLT also states outright that David’s son is the one who ruled, which the NKJV says more briefly with “king in Jerusalem.”

The third-person frame that opens the book returns at a few points. The words “says the Preacher” appear again in verse 2, in 7:27, and in 12:8, and 12:9–10 describes the Preacher’s work from the outside. Between those points, Solomon tells the search in his own first-person voice, starting at verse 12.

Ecclesiastes 1:2
Ecclesiastes 1:2
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:2 "Vanity of vanities," says the Preacher; "Vanity of vanities, all is vanity."(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:2 “Everything is meaningless,” says the Teacher, “completely meaningless!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Before the search begins, the framing voice reports Solomon’s verdict: “Vanity of vanities, all is vanity.” The Hebrew word behind “vanity” pictures a breath or vapor, and the doubled phrase means “utterly meaningless.” The verdict concerns life pursued without God, which cannot be held or made to last, and it does not declare that God, love, or creation itself has no value.

Verse Explanation

Before the search begins, the framing voice reports Solomon’s verdict: “Vanity of vanities, all is vanity.” The Hebrew word behind “vanity” pictures a breath or vapor, and the doubled phrase means “utterly meaningless.” The verdict concerns life pursued without God, which cannot be held or made to last, and it does not declare that God, love, or creation itself has no value.

Vanity of vanities is the first thing the book reports from the Preacher. The Hebrew word behind “vanity” pictures a breath or vapor, like the breath you can see for a moment on a cold morning but cannot grasp or keep. Ecclesiastes uses the word to call things meaningless: empty and futile when life is pursued without God. Doubling the word as “vanity of vanities” strengthens the statement, so the phrase means “utterly meaningless.”

says the Preacher shows that the framing voice is reporting the Preacher’s verdict before his search has been described at all. The reader learns the outcome first and then, from verse 12 onward, watches how the king reached it. This order gives the book its direction: each experiment that follows serves as evidence for a conclusion already announced.

Vanity of vanities, all is vanity repeats the doubled phrase and then widens it to “all.” Within the book’s argument, “all” covers everything Solomon examines under the sun, including wisdom, pleasure, great works, and wealth, when those things are measured only by earthly gain and the grave. The verdict does not say that God, love, obedience, or creation itself has no value. The same book speaks of food, work, and enjoyment received “from the hand of God” (2:24), and it closes by calling readers to “Fear God and keep His commandments” (12:13). Verse 2 exposes the emptiness of life pursued without God, but it is not the book’s last word.

Study Notes

The refrain returns almost word for word near the end of the book: “Vanity of vanities,” says the Preacher, “All is vanity” (12:8). Because the two statements bracket Solomon’s search, everything between them belongs to one examination. After the second statement, the closing voice gives “the conclusion of the whole matter” in 12:13–14.

The NLT translates the sense of this verse rather than its image: “Everything is meaningless,” says the Teacher, “completely meaningless!” The NKJV keeps the traditional word “vanity,” and the NLT’s “meaningless” matches how that word is understood throughout Ecclesiastes. Neither English word carries the Hebrew picture of breath or vapor, which is why that picture needs to be explained.

Ecclesiastes 1:3
Ecclesiastes 1:3
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:3 What profit has a man from all his labor In which he toils under the sun?(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:3 What do people get for all their hard work under the sun? (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon asks the question the whole book will test: what lasting “profit” does a person keep from “all his labor In which he toils under the sun”? “Under the sun” marks the limited horizon of his search, life measured by what people can observe and achieve before the grave. Within that horizon the question asks whether any gain from work can outlast death.

Verse Explanation

Solomon asks the question the whole book will test: what lasting “profit” does a person keep from “all his labor In which he toils under the sun”? “Under the sun” marks the limited horizon of his search, life measured by what people can observe and achieve before the grave. Within that horizon the question asks whether any gain from work can outlast death.

What profit has a man asks about lasting gain. “Profit” here means what is left over after the work is done, the gain a person actually keeps. “A man” stands for any person, not one individual. Solomon asks this as a question rather than stating a conclusion, and the rest of the book sets out to test it.

from all his labor widens the question to cover a whole lifetime of work, not one disappointing project. In which he toils adds the cost: this labor is wearying, draining effort. Solomon does not deny that work gets things done, since fields are planted and houses are built. He asks whether anything gained through all that strain lasts.

under the sun? marks the limited horizon of Solomon’s search. He measures life by what people can observe and achieve before the grave, leaving God’s revelation and His future judgment out of the calculation. Within that horizon death ends every account, so the question becomes whether any gain from work can outlast death. The poem in verses 4–11 presses the question further by picturing a world that keeps moving and never arrives anywhere.

Ecclesiastes answers the question within its own pages by setting every work before God, who will bring it into judgment (12:13–14). The rest of Scripture supplies the fuller answer: because Christ has risen, Paul assures believers that their labor in the Lord is not in vain (1 Corinthians 15:58). This chapter, however, leaves Solomon’s question open, so that its weight is felt before the answer comes.

Study Notes

This verse is the first use of “under the sun,” a phrase that appears in 27 verses of Ecclesiastes in the NKJV. In this chapter it appears again in verses 9 and 14. Verse 13 describes the field of Solomon’s search with the similar phrase “all that is done under heaven,” and verse 14 then speaks of “all the works that are done under the sun.”

The question about profit returns as the search unfolds. Solomon answers it for his own achievements in 2:11, “There was no profit under the sun.” He asks it again in 3:9, “What profit has the worker from that in which he labors?” and comes back to it in 5:16, “what profit has he who has labored for the wind?”

Verse 3 can also be read as the first line of the poem in verses 4–11, since the endless cycles that follow show why labor produces no lasting profit. Read with verses 1–2, the question joins the refrain to state the issue the whole book tests. On either reading, the verse belongs to the book’s opening movement in 1:1–11.

2. Ecclesiastes 1:4–11 — The Endless Cycles That Never Arrive

Ecclesiastes 1:4
Ecclesiastes 1:4
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:4 One generation passes away, and another generation comes; But the earth abides forever.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:4 Generations come and generations go, but the earth never changes. (NLT)

Main Idea

The poem begins by setting short human lives against the lasting earth: generations come and go, “But the earth abides forever.” The earth’s persistence is a poetic contrast rather than a technical claim about how long the earth will last, and it shows how quickly people pass through a world that their labor leaves unchanged.

Verse Explanation

The poem begins by setting short human lives against the lasting earth: generations come and go, “But the earth abides forever.” The earth’s persistence is a poetic contrast rather than a technical claim about how long the earth will last, and it shows how quickly people pass through a world that their labor leaves unchanged.

One generation passes away, and another generation comes — The poem opens with people. A generation is everyone living at one time: parents and children working, building, and raising families. In a single line Solomon lets a whole generation leave and the next one arrive, as if a lifetime took no longer than a step through a doorway. The order matters too. The passing away comes first, so before anyone can ask what the new generation will achieve, the old one is already gone. This is the setting for the question in verse 3. If the worker himself passes away, what profit from his labor can he keep?

But the earth abides forever — Against that steady procession of people stands the ground under their feet. The earth was there before each generation arrived, and it is still there after that generation leaves. The contrast is poetic. It compares the short stay of people with the lasting stage they walk across, and it does not say how long the physical earth will finally last. The weight of the line falls on the people. The earth does not need them in order to remain, and their coming and going does not alter it. Measured only under the sun, human labor leaves no lasting mark on the world it works so hard to change.

Study Notes

The NLT translates the second line as “the earth never changes,” which stresses that the earth stays stable. The NKJV’s “abides forever” stresses that the earth lasts. Either way, the earth stays while the generations pass, and that contrast is the point of the line.

Ecclesiastes 1:5
Ecclesiastes 1:5
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:5 The sun also rises, and the sun goes down, And hastens to the place where it arose.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:5 The sun rises and the sun sets, then hurries around to rise again. (NLT)

Main Idea

The sun rises, sets, and “hastens” back to the place where it rose, as if straining to begin the same course again. Its daily circuit pictures tireless effort that only returns to its starting point.

Verse Explanation

The sun rises, sets, and “hastens” back to the place where it rose, as if straining to begin the same course again. Its daily circuit pictures tireless effort that only returns to its starting point.

The sun also rises, and the sun goes down — From people, Solomon turns to the most visible thing in the sky. The word “also” links the sun to the generations of verse 4, because the sun comes and goes as they do. The sun is named twice in this short line, and the repetition sounds like the daily routine it describes. Solomon is using ordinary language for what anyone sees from the ground, the same way people still speak of sunrise and sunset.

And hastens to the place where it arose — After setting, the sun does not rest. It hurries back to its starting point so that it can rise again. “Hastens” suggests urgency, as if the sun were straining to keep a schedule, yet all that effort only brings it back to where it began. Verse 3 asked about the labor a person does “under the sun.” Here the sun itself becomes a picture of that labor: busy every day, yet never arriving anywhere new. A life lived only for earthly gain follows the same course, with constant effort and the same circuit waiting tomorrow.

Study Notes

The NLT reads “then hurries around to rise again,” which presents the sun’s return as getting ready for the next sunrise. The NKJV stresses the destination: the sun hastens “to the place where it arose,” so all its effort ends at the spot where it began.

Ecclesiastes 1:6
Ecclesiastes 1:6
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:6 The wind goestoward the south, And turns around to the north; The wind whirls about continually, And comes again on its circuit.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:6 The wind blows south, and then turns north. Around and around it goes, blowing in circles. (NLT)

Main Idea

The wind swings south and then north, whirling “continually” and coming back “on its circuit.” Even the wind, which seems free to go anywhere, only travels the same rounds again.

Verse Explanation

The wind swings south and then north, whirling “continually” and coming back “on its circuit.” Even the wind, which seems free to go anywhere, only travels the same rounds again.

The wind goes toward the south, And turns around to the north — The sun follows a fixed path, but the wind seems different. It changes direction without warning, and no one can see it. Yet even the wind, which looks so free, only swings from south to north and back again. Its changes of direction do not free it from the pattern.

The wind whirls about continually — “Continually” means the motion never stops. Solomon piles up words of turning and whirling so that the reader feels the restlessness of a force that is always moving and never settles.

And comes again on its circuit — A circuit is a round trip that ends where it started, so the wind’s apparent freedom turns out to be travel along a set route. Like the sun in verse 5, the wind keeps moving and keeps coming back. It adds another witness to a world full of motion that never reaches a final goal.

Study Notes

None.

Ecclesiastes 1:7
Ecclesiastes 1:7
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:7 All the rivers runinto the sea, Yet the sea is not full; To the place from which the rivers come, There they return again.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:7 Rivers run into the sea, but the sea is never full. Then the water returns again to the rivers and flows out again to the sea. (NLT)

Main Idea

Rivers pour endlessly into the sea, “Yet the sea is not full,” and their waters return to the place they came from. The flow never stops, and it never finishes anything. A sea that is never filled prepares for the unsatisfied eye and ear of the next verse.

Verse Explanation

Rivers pour endlessly into the sea, “Yet the sea is not full,” and their waters return to the place they came from. The flow never stops, and it never finishes anything. A sea that is never filled prepares for the unsatisfied eye and ear of the next verse.

All the rivers run into the sea — Rivers look like steady progress. They move in one direction day and night, and the word “All” stresses that every one of them ends at the sea. If anything in nature seemed to be getting somewhere, it would be a river.

Yet the sea is not full — “Yet” introduces the surprise. All that water pours in, and the sea never reaches the brim, so the rivers never finish the work they seem to be doing. This picture prepares for verse 8. A sea that is never filled anticipates an eye that is never satisfied and an ear that is never filled.

To the place from which the rivers come, There they return again — The waters do not stay in the sea. They go back to where they started and make the same trip again. Solomon does not explain how they return. He reports only what he observes: the same waters keep making the same journey. That fits the earthly viewpoint of the whole poem, in which the flowing never ends and nothing is ever completed.

Study Notes

The NLT reads “Then the water returns again to the rivers and flows out again to the sea,” which spells out the full loop from sea to river and back to the sea. The NKJV names only the return “To the place from which the rivers come.”

Ecclesiastes 1:8
Ecclesiastes 1:8
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:8 All things are full of labor; Man cannot express it. The eye is not satisfied with seeing, Nor the ear filled with hearing.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:8 Everything is wearisome beyond description. No matter how much we see, we are never satisfied. No matter how much we hear, we are not content. (NLT)

Main Idea

Here the poem turns from nature to people. “All things are full of labor,” wearying beyond what anyone can put into words. Human desire follows the same restless pattern as the sun, wind, and rivers, for “The eye is not satisfied with seeing, Nor the ear filled with hearing.”

Verse Explanation

Here the poem turns from nature to people. “All things are full of labor,” wearying beyond what anyone can put into words. Human desire follows the same restless pattern as the sun, wind, and rivers, for “The eye is not satisfied with seeing, Nor the ear filled with hearing.”

All things are full of labor — This line gathers up everything the poem has shown so far. The passing generations, the hurrying sun, the circling wind, and the rivers that never fill the sea are all “full of labor.” The word ties the poem back to the question in verse 3 about “all his labor.” Here labor carries the weariness of effort that never ends. Nature is never idle, and neither are people, yet all that activity wears everyone out without producing lasting gain.

Man cannot express it — This weariness is greater than words can describe. People feel the tiredness of the endless round, but no one can fully put it into words. Even a king who gained more wisdom than all before him in Jerusalem (verse 16) admits that this burden is beyond his power to describe.

The eye is not satisfied with seeing, Nor the ear filled with hearing — Now the restlessness moves inside the person. The eyes keep looking and the ears keep listening, but neither ever has enough. In the NKJV the wording echoes verse 7: the ear is never “filled,” just as the sea “is not full.” Human desire behaves like the rivers, always taking in more and never reaching the brim. Solomon is not condemning sight or hearing. He is describing a hunger that more experience cannot satisfy. Scripture later shows where that hunger is met: Jesus offers water that ends thirst (John 4:13–14).

Study Notes

Verse 8 joins the two halves of the poem. Verses 4–7 survey the natural world: the earth, the sun, the wind, and the rivers. Verses 9–11 turn to human history and memory. The opening line of verse 8 sums up the natural world, and its closing lines move to the human eye and ear.

The NLT translates the first part of the verse as “Everything is wearisome beyond description.” That rendering reads “labor” as weariness and merges it with “Man cannot express it” into one statement. The NKJV keeps two separate statements, but the meaning is close: the tiredness of endless effort is more than words can describe.

Ecclesiastes 1:9
Ecclesiastes 1:9
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:9 That which has been is what will be, That which is done is what will be done, And there is nothing new under the sun.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:9 History merely repeats itself. It has all been done before. Nothing under the sun is truly new. (NLT)

Main Idea

What has been and what has been done will happen and be done again, “And there is nothing new under the sun.” Within the earthly horizon Solomon surveys, human life repeats the same patterns, so no achievement breaks the cycle. The verse describes this recurring experience; it does not limit God’s power to do new things.

Verse Explanation

What has been and what has been done will happen and be done again, “And there is nothing new under the sun.” Within the earthly horizon Solomon surveys, human life repeats the same patterns, so no achievement breaks the cycle. The verse describes this recurring experience; it does not limit God’s power to do new things.

That which has been is what will be — Solomon now applies the cycles of nature to human history. What has existed before will exist again. The future looks open and full of possibility, but it turns out to repeat the past.

That which is done is what will be done — The second line moves from what exists to what people do. People build and trade, marry and grieve, fight and make peace, and the next generation does the same. Taken together, the two lines cover both what the world contains and what people make of it.

And there is nothing new under the sun — The cycles of the poem have been leading to this conclusion. “Under the sun” marks the limited view of Solomon’s search: life as it looks through human observation alone, with God’s revelation and judgment left out of the calculation. From that viewpoint, no human achievement breaks the pattern or gives anyone a lasting advantage over those who came before. The statement describes recurring human experience. It does not say that God cannot act in new ways. The wider Bible tells of God’s new acts, above all the resurrection, which lie beyond the earthly view this search adopts.

Study Notes

This is the second time “under the sun” appears in the chapter, after verse 3. The phrase returns in verse 14, where Solomon reports what he found after he began his own search.

Ecclesiastes 3:15 repeats the observation in similar words: “That which is has already been, And what is to be has already been.” There, however, the line continues, “And God requires an account of what is past,” so the repeated patterns of history are placed before God.

Ecclesiastes 1:10
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:10 Is there anything of which it may be said, "See, this is new"? It has already been in ancient times before us.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:10 Sometimes people say, “Here is something new!” But actually it is old; nothing is ever truly new. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon answers the objection that something is truly new: it “has already been in ancient times before us.” Something that looks new to one generation was usually known by earlier ones, and it seems new because, as verse 11 explains, they have forgotten it.

Verse Explanation

Solomon answers the objection that something is truly new: it “has already been in ancient times before us.” Something that looks new to one generation was usually known by earlier ones, and it seems new because, as verse 11 explains, they have forgotten it.

Is there anything of which it may be said, “See, this is new”? — Solomon expects an objection. Surely someone can point to something and say, “See, this is new.” By putting the claim as a question, he invites the reader to try to name such a thing.

It has already been in ancient times before us — His answer is that whatever looks new has already been seen in earlier times. Something may seem fresh to the people who first notice it, but the pattern behind it was known long before them. The words “before us” place Solomon himself among the generations that came late into an old world. Verse 11 explains why something can still seem new: the people who knew it before have been forgotten. Solomon is speaking about the recurring patterns of human life. He does not deny that later generations learn things their ancestors did not know.

Study Notes

The NLT makes the opening a report of what people say: “Sometimes people say, ‘Here is something new!’” The NKJV keeps it as a question, which challenges the reader to come up with an example before Solomon gives his answer.

Ecclesiastes 1:11
Ecclesiastes 1:11
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:11 There is no remembrance of former things, Nor will there be any remembrance of things that are to come By those who will come after.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:11 We don’t remember what happened in the past, and in future generations, no one will remember what we are doing now. (NLT)

Main Idea

The poem ends with forgetting: former things are not remembered, and what is still to come will not be remembered “By those who will come after.” Even a person’s name and accomplishments fade, so labor under the sun cannot secure lasting gain by being remembered.

Verse Explanation

The poem ends with forgetting: former things are not remembered, and what is still to come will not be remembered “By those who will come after.” Even a person’s name and accomplishments fade, so labor under the sun cannot secure lasting gain by being remembered.

There is no remembrance of former things — The poem closes with memory. What happened in earlier times is no longer remembered. That explains verse 10: something seems new only because the people who knew it before have been forgotten, along with what they did. Forgetting is part of the cycle itself.

Nor will there be any remembrance of things that are to come — The same forgetting will reach into the future. What is still ahead, including the work of people living now, will fade from memory in its turn. Working harder or achieving more will not prevent it.

By those who will come after — The verse ends with the generations still to come, which brings the poem back to its opening in verse 4, where one generation goes and another comes. Those who come later will not remember the people living now, just as the present generation does not remember those who came before. For anyone who hoped labor would at least be remembered, this closes the last door. Within life under the sun, even memory cannot preserve what a life accomplished. The question of verse 3 is still unanswered when Solomon begins his own search in verse 12.

Study Notes

Ecclesiastes 2:16 applies the same forgetting directly to people: “For there is no more remembrance of the wise than of the fool forever, Since all that now is will be forgotten in the days to come.” That later verse makes clear that the forgetting described here reaches people and their lives, not only events.

3. Ecclesiastes 1:12–15 — The King Begins His Search

Ecclesiastes 1:12
Ecclesiastes 1:12
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:12 I, the Preacher, was king over Israel in Jerusalem.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:12 I, the Teacher, was king of Israel, and I lived in Jerusalem. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon now speaks in his own voice: “I, the Preacher, was king over Israel in Jerusalem.” This first-person statement begins the royal search that runs through 2:26, and his place as king explains why he could test what life offers more fully than anyone else.

Verse Explanation

Solomon now speaks in his own voice: “I, the Preacher, was king over Israel in Jerusalem.” This first-person statement begins the royal search that runs through 2:26, and his place as king explains why he could test what life offers more fully than anyone else.

I, the Preacher, Up to this point another voice has spoken about the Preacher, introducing him in verse 1 and reporting his verdict with “says the Preacher” in verse 2. Now the Preacher speaks for himself. The change to “I” marks the beginning of Solomon’s own account of his search, which runs through 2:26. Everything reported from here comes as first-hand testimony from the man who carried out the investigation.

was king over Israel in Jerusalem. Verse 1 called him “king in Jerusalem.” Here he adds that he ruled over Israel, the whole nation and not only its capital city. Solomon reigned when Israel’s united kingdom was at the height of its wealth, so he had the power and resources to test life more fully than any ordinary person could. That background matters for the rest of this chapter and for chapter 2. When this king reports that his search failed, his disappointment cannot be blamed on a lack of opportunity.

Study Notes

The Hebrew title behind “the Preacher” is Qoheleth, the same title used in 1:1–2. The NLT translates it “the Teacher.” Both English words render one title for Solomon as the speaker of the book, so the difference between them is a translation choice and not a difference in who is speaking.

The past tense “was king” does not by itself tell us when Solomon wrote or whether he was still reigning at the time. Ecclesiastes gives no date of composition and does not say that Solomon had left the throne, so the verb tense alone does not establish a later setting for the book.

Ecclesiastes 1:13
Ecclesiastes 1:13
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 1:13 And I set my heart to seek and search out by wisdom concerning all that is done under heaven; this burdensome task God has given to the sons of man, by which they may be exercised.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 1:13 I devoted myself to search for understanding and to explore by wisdom everything being done under heaven. I soon discovered that God has dealt a tragic existence to the human race. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon set his heart “to seek and search out by wisdom” everything done under heaven, a deliberate and thorough investigation. He calls this work a “burdensome task God has given to the sons of man, by which they may be exercised.” God has laid on people the drive to understand life, and the effort wears them down.

Verse Explanation

Solomon set his heart “to seek and search out by wisdom” everything done under heaven, a deliberate and thorough investigation. He calls this work a “burdensome task God has given to the sons of man, by which they may be exercised.” God has laid on people the drive to understand life, and the effort wears them down.

And I set my heart The search begins with a decision. To set one’s heart on something is to give oneself to it on purpose, with mind and will fully engaged. Solomon did not drift into these questions. He chose to pursue them.

to seek and search out by wisdom Putting the two verbs together describes a thorough investigation. Solomon meant to look for answers and then follow them all the way through. Wisdom, the skill for living that he possessed in great measure, was the tool he used. Wisdom is also the first thing his search tests. Both this report and the second one in verses 16–18 examine what wisdom itself can achieve, before chapter 2 moves on to pleasure, great works, and wealth.

concerning all that is done under heaven; The scope is as wide as human activity itself. Solomon did not limit himself to one field of knowledge or one corner of life. He wanted to understand everything people do on earth.

this burdensome task God has given to the sons of man, This is the only place in the chapter where God is named. Solomon calls the work of searching out life a heavy assignment, and he traces it to God, who has given it to “the sons of man,” meaning humanity as a whole. The drive to understand life comes from God and belongs to every person, not only to a curious king. Naming God does not change Solomon’s method, because his search still measures life by what can be observed and achieved on earth. It does show that even this search takes place under God’s hand.

by which they may be exercised. In the NKJV’s older English, to be “exercised” by something means to be kept busy by it and worn down by it. The task occupies people and drains them. The verse describes the strain of a search that people cannot set aside, a search that keeps them laboring to understand a world they cannot fully explain.

Study Notes

The NLT translates the second half of the verse much more darkly: “God has dealt a tragic existence to the human race.” The NKJV speaks instead of a “burdensome task” that God has given and that keeps people occupied. Both translations agree that the weight comes from God and presses on all humanity. They differ over whether the wording describes the burden of the search itself or the hardship of human life as a whole.

Solomon comes back to this idea in 3:10: “I have seen the God-given task with which the sons of men are to be occupied.” The similar wording confirms that the “burdensome task” of 1:13 is an occupation God has given to people generally, not a burden peculiar to kings.

Verse 13 describes the field of the search as “all that is done under heaven,” and verse 14 describes the same field as “all the works that are done under the sun.” Ecclesiastes uses “under heaven” again in 2:3 and 3:1. In these two verses the two phrases describe the same sphere of human activity on earth.

After looking at “all the works that are done under the sun,” Solomon gives his first verdict: “all is vanity and grasping for the wind.” Measured only by earthly gain, human achievement is meaningless and as impossible to hold onto as the wind.

I have seen all the works that are done under the sun; Solomon now reports what his investigation showed him. The words “I have seen” present the verdict as the result of his own observation. The phrase “the works” refers to the deeds and achievements of people. “Under the sun” marks the limited horizon of the search: life measured by what people can observe and accomplish before the grave, with God’s revelation and His future judgment left out of the calculation.

and indeed, all is vanity The word “indeed” introduces what Solomon found. The verdict that the framing voice reported in verse 2 now comes from Solomon himself. The word “vanity” translates a Hebrew word that pictures a breath or vapor. Here it means meaningless: measured only by earthly gain, human achievement cannot be held or made to last. The verdict concerns the works people do under the sun. It does not say that God, love, or creation itself has no value.

and grasping for the wind. The phrase pictures someone closing a hand around moving air and finding nothing there when the hand opens. Paired with “vanity,” it describes effort that leaves nothing behind to keep. This is Solomon’s first verdict in the search. He gives the same verdict when he studies wisdom and folly in verse 17 and again when he looks back on his own great works in 2:11.

Study Notes

The phrase “grasping for the wind” first appears here and occurs in nine verses of Ecclesiastes, all between 1:14 and 6:9 (1:14, 17; 2:11, 17, 26; 4:4, 6, 16; 6:9). It does not appear after that point in the book.

The NLT renders the verdict as “it is all meaningless — like chasing the wind.” Its “meaningless” expresses the sense of the NKJV’s “vanity” in this verse, and “chasing the wind” pictures the same futility as a pursuit that never catches its object.

A proverb sums up what Solomon found: “What is crooked cannot be made straight, And what is lacking cannot be numbered.” Wisdom can see what is twisted or missing in the world, but it cannot straighten what is bent or supply what is absent.

What is crooked cannot be made straight, Solomon sums up his first report with a short proverb. Something crooked is bent or twisted out of its proper shape. Wisdom let Solomon see what was twisted in the world, but seeing a problem is not the same as fixing it. However much he understood, he could not straighten what was bent.

And what is lacking cannot be numbered. The second line makes the same point from another angle. You can count what is present, but you cannot count what is missing, and no amount of counting will make an absent thing appear. Solomon’s wisdom could identify what the world lacks without being able to supply it. Together the two lines explain why the search left him troubled: his understanding exposed problems it had no power to repair. This limit does not make wisdom worthless or ignorance a virtue. Chapter 2 still affirms that “wisdom excels folly As light excels darkness” (2:13).

Study Notes

The proverb does not say exactly what is crooked or lacking. Its range may include moral wrongs, the injustices Solomon later observes (3:16; 4:1), and the ordinary limits of human life.

Ecclesiastes 7:13 uses the same picture: “Consider the work of God; For who can make straight what He has made crooked?” That later verse attributes the crookedness to God’s work and asks the question directly, while 1:15 states the human inability in the form of a proverb.

The NLT renders the lines “What is wrong cannot be made right” and “What is missing cannot be recovered.” The NKJV keeps the image of counting in “cannot be numbered,” while the NLT takes the second line to mean that what has been lost cannot be gotten back.

Verse 15 closes the first of two matching reports. In verses 12–15 and again in verses 16–18, Solomon sets his heart to search (verses 13, 17), names the result as grasping for the wind (verses 14, 17), and ends with a proverb (verses 15, 18).

Main Idea

Solomon begins a second report by reflecting on his own greatness: he had “gained more wisdom than all who were before me in Jerusalem,” and his heart “understood great wisdom and knowledge.” The disappointment that follows therefore cannot be blamed on a lack of understanding.

Verse Explanation

Solomon begins a second report by reflecting on his own greatness: he had “gained more wisdom than all who were before me in Jerusalem,” and his heart “understood great wisdom and knowledge.” The disappointment that follows therefore cannot be blamed on a lack of understanding.

I communed with my heart, saying, Solomon opens his second report by talking the matter over with himself. What follows is an inward reckoning, not a speech to an audience. In these verses Solomon’s heart is what understands and what decides to search, so “my heart” means his inner self, including his mind and will.

Look, I have attained greatness, Solomon calls his own attention to what he has become. His greatness is the starting point for the test that follows. A king with his standing and resources could pursue understanding with every advantage, so his findings cannot be dismissed as the complaint of someone who never had the opportunity.

and have gained more wisdom than all who were before me in Jerusalem. Solomon measures himself against everyone who had gone before him in the city where he reigned. He is not simply praising himself. He is establishing himself as a credible witness. If the wisest person Jerusalem had known finds that wisdom cannot give him what he is looking for, the problem lies in what wisdom can do within this search, not in a lack of ability in the man who used it.

My heart has understood great wisdom and knowledge. Solomon claims more than a store of facts. His heart has grasped wisdom, the skill of living well, together with knowledge, what he has learned and understood about life. These two words return together in verse 18, where the very wisdom and knowledge he prized become the source of his grief. Verse 16 prepares for that reversal: the greater his understanding, the more striking his disappointment.

Study Notes

The NKJV reads “all who were before me in Jerusalem,” wording that does not say whether Solomon is comparing himself with earlier kings or with everyone who had lived there before him. The NLT reads “any of the kings who ruled in Jerusalem before me,” which narrows the comparison to earlier rulers. The NLT wording is one interpretation of the broader phrase the NKJV preserves.

Solomon repeats almost the same claim in 2:9: “So I became great and excelled more than all who were before me in Jerusalem. Also my wisdom remained with me.” The echo links his testing of wisdom here with his testing of pleasure and great works in chapter 2, and it shows that his wisdom stayed with him throughout that later search.

Main Idea

Solomon set his heart to know both wisdom and “madness and folly,” studying the foolish side of life as well as the wise. The result matched his first report: “this also is grasping for the wind.”

Verse Explanation

Solomon set his heart to know both wisdom and “madness and folly,” studying the foolish side of life as well as the wise. The result matched his first report: “this also is grasping for the wind.”

And I set my heart to know wisdom The phrase “set my heart” repeats Solomon’s language from verse 13, where he “set my heart to seek and search out by wisdom.” There wisdom was the tool of his search. Here wisdom also becomes the subject under examination, as Solomon deliberately sets out to understand what wisdom is and what it can achieve.

and to know madness and folly. Solomon does not limit his study to the wise side of life. He also sets out to understand madness and folly, the senseless and foolish ways people think and live. Studying both sides lets him compare them and test whether wisdom really holds the advantage. Chapter 2 takes up this comparison again (2:12) and affirms that “wisdom excels folly As light excels darkness” (2:13). Solomon’s aim here is to know madness and folly, not to approve them.

I perceived that this also is grasping for the wind. The word “also” ties this verdict to Solomon’s first report, where he judged “all the works that are done under the sun” to be “vanity and grasping for the wind” (verse 14). Now the pursuit of wisdom itself receives the same verdict. Trying to secure lasting gain through understanding is like closing a hand around the wind: the effort is real, but nothing stays in the grasp. Solomon does not call wisdom worthless, and chapter 2 shows that it has real value. His point is that wisdom cannot supply lasting meaning when life is measured only by what people can observe and achieve before the grave.

Study Notes

Verses 16–18 follow the same pattern as verses 13–15. In each report Solomon sets his heart to search (verses 13, 17), calls the result “grasping for the wind” (verses 14, 17), and closes with a proverb (verses 15, 18). The repeated pattern marks verse 16 as the beginning of a second report rather than a continuation of the first.

Solomon pairs wisdom with “madness and folly” again in 2:12, where he “turned myself to consider wisdom and madness and folly,” and in 7:25, where he sought “To know the wickedness of folly, Even of foolishness and madness.” These passages show that he examined folly in order to understand it, in a search he kept “guiding my heart with wisdom” (2:3).

The expression “grasping for the wind” appears in nine NKJV verses of Ecclesiastes, all between 1:14 and 6:9 (1:14, 17; 2:11, 17, 26; 4:4, 6, 16; 6:9). Verse 17 is its second occurrence, only three verses after the first.

Verse Explanation

A second proverb closes the chapter: “For in much wisdom is much grief, And he who increases knowledge increases sorrow.” The more Solomon understood, the more clearly he saw the world’s disorder and his own limits. His knowledge deepened his pain without giving him the lasting answer he sought, although this does not make ignorance a virtue.

For in much wisdom is much grief, The word “For” gives the reason behind the verdict of verse 17. Solomon sums up his second report in a proverb, just as verse 15 closed his first. Much wisdom brings much grief because understanding opens a person’s eyes. Solomon saw more clearly than others what verse 15 calls “crooked” and “lacking” in the world, and seeing those things clearly did not give him the power to straighten or supply them. The more he understood, the more he grieved over what he could not change.

And he who increases knowledge increases sorrow. The second line restates the first in matching words: knowledge corresponds to wisdom, and sorrow to grief. The line also widens Solomon’s experience into a general statement about anyone who “increases knowledge.” Within this search, knowledge sharpens a person’s awareness of the world’s disorder and of human limits without giving the lasting answer Solomon set out to find. That does not make ignorance a virtue or turn careful thinking into rebellion against God. Wisdom keeps its real value, as chapter 2 affirms. The proverb exposes what wisdom cannot do when it is asked to supply life’s meaning on its own.

Chapter 1 ends here, in the middle of the search, with the wisest king in Israel admitting that his understanding has deepened his sorrow. Scripture carries the matter further. Jesus speaks of Himself as greater than Solomon (Matthew 12:42), and in Him all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden (Colossians 2:3). The wisdom that brought Solomon grief finds its full setting in life with God through Christ.

Study Notes

The NLT puts the first line in the first person, “The greater my wisdom, the greater my grief,” tying the saying directly to Solomon’s own experience. The NKJV keeps the general form of a proverb, “in much wisdom is much grief.” In both translations the second line speaks of anyone who increases knowledge. Both readings fit the context, since Solomon states a general truth he has proved in his own life.
