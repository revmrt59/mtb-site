Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 1
Main Idea:

Solomon declares that all is “vanity,” meaningless when life is pursued without God, and then asks what lasting profit labor can gain, pictures a world of endless cycles, and begins a search by wisdom that only deepens his grief.

Setting:

The book opens with the words of “the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem” (1:1), that is, Solomon. Verses 1–11 introduce the whole book, and verse 12 begins the royal search that runs through 2:26.

Chapter Outline:

  • Ecclesiastes 1:1–3 — The Preacher’s Verdict and Question

  • Ecclesiastes 1:4–11 — The Endless Cycles That Never Arrive

  • Ecclesiastes 1:12–15 — The King Begins His Search

  • Ecclesiastes 1:16–18 — Great Wisdom That Increases Grief

Key Verse:

“What profit has a man from all his labor In which he toils under the sun?” (Ecclesiastes 1:3)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Ecclesiastes 1 gives the verdict before it tells the story. Before Solomon describes anything he tried, the opening voice of the book reports what he concluded: “Vanity of vanities, all is vanity” (verse 2). Then comes the question the rest of the book will test: “What profit has a man from all his labor In which he toils under the sun?” (verse 3). The reader learns the outcome first and then watches how the king reached it.

The poem in verses 4–11 shows why that question presses so hard. Generations come and go while the earth remains. The sun hurries back to where it rose, the wind returns on its circuit, and the rivers keep pouring into a sea that “is not full.” In verse 8 the poem turns from nature to people: “The eye is not satisfied with seeing, Nor the ear filled with hearing.” Human desire moves like those rivers, always flowing and never filled. What looks new has already been, and earlier generations are forgotten, so even memory cannot preserve what a life accomplished.

At verse 12 Solomon begins speaking for himself, and his search starts with wisdom. Twice he sets his heart to understand, twice he calls the result “grasping for the wind” (verses 14, 17), and twice a proverb sums up what he found. Wisdom let him see what was crooked and missing in the world, but it could not straighten or supply it, and “in much wisdom is much grief” (verse 18). God is named only once in the chapter, as the One who has given this “burdensome task” to humanity (verse 13). Even a search that measures life by earthly gain takes place under His hand. The chapter ends in the middle of that search. Chapter 2 goes on to test pleasure, great works, and wealth, and 2:24 offers the first glimpse of an answer.

Chapter Sections

Ecclesiastes 1:1–3 — The Preacher’s Verdict and Question. The opening voice introduces Solomon by his royal titles and reports his verdict that everything is “vanity,” utterly meaningless when life is pursued without God. Solomon then asks whether any profit from a lifetime of labor can last within the limits of life “under the sun.”

Ecclesiastes 1:4–11 — The Endless Cycles That Never Arrive. Generations pass while the earth remains, and the sun, the wind, and the rivers keep circling without ever finishing anything. In verse 8 the poem turns to people whose eyes and ears are never satisfied, and it ends with nothing new under the sun, with past generations forgotten, and with the warning that those still to come will be forgotten too.

Ecclesiastes 1:12–15 — The King Begins His Search. Now speaking as “I,” the king sets out to search by wisdom all that is done under heaven, a burdensome task God has given to humanity. He judges it all “vanity and grasping for the wind,” and his proverb in verse 15 admits that wisdom can see what is crooked or lacking but cannot repair it.

Ecclesiastes 1:16–18 — Great Wisdom That Increases Grief. Solomon recalls that he gained more wisdom than all who were before him in Jerusalem, and he studies “madness and folly” as well as wisdom. He reaches the same verdict, and the closing proverb shows that his growing knowledge sharpened his sorrow without giving him the lasting answer he wanted.

Why This Chapter Matters

Ecclesiastes 1 sets the question that the whole book tests. A reader who holds onto “What profit has a man from all his labor” will be able to follow Solomon through the chapters that come next. The chapter does not answer that question itself. Read alongside 1 Corinthians 15:58, though, the resurrection supplies the fuller answer, because labor done in the Lord is not in vain.

The chapter also gives honest words to a restlessness that neither achievement nor knowledge can quiet, and those words come from the wisest king Israel had. Jesus speaks of Himself as greater than Solomon (Matthew 12:42), and in Him all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge are hidden (Colossians 2:3). The wisdom that brought Solomon grief finds its full setting in life with God through Christ.

Finally, the opening verdict is a judgment on life pursued without God. It does not say that God, love, or creation itself has no value. Ecclesiastes 1 begins a search rather than concluding one, and the book’s governing answer comes in 12:13–14.

Key Themes to Watch For

Lasting profit from labor. The opening question asks whether anything gained from work can outlast death and forgetting. The chapter’s pictures of endless cycles and lost memory press that question home (1:3, 8, 11).

Motion that never arrives. The sun, wind, and rivers keep moving without finishing anything, and human desire follows the same restless pattern (1:4–8).

Nothing new, and no one remembered. Within the earthly patterns Solomon surveys, what seems new has already been, and each generation fades from memory. These lines describe recurring human experience. They do not limit God’s power to do new things (1:9–11).

Wisdom’s reach and its limits. Wisdom lets Solomon see what is crooked and missing, but it cannot straighten or supply it, and greater knowledge brings him greater grief. Chapter 2 still affirms that wisdom excels folly (1:13–18; 2:13).

Key Words & Concepts

“Vanity.” The Hebrew word that the NKJV translates “vanity” pictures a breath or vapor, something that cannot be held. In Ecclesiastes it means meaningless: empty and futile when life is pursued without God. The doubled phrase “Vanity of vanities” means utterly meaningless (1:2, 14).

“Under the sun.” This phrase marks the limited view of Solomon’s search. It measures life only by what people can observe and achieve before the grave, leaving God’s revelation and His future judgment out of the calculation (1:3, 9, 14).

“Profit.” Profit is the lasting gain left over after all of one’s labor. Solomon asks whether any such gain survives death (1:3).

“Grasping for the wind.” This phrase pictures trying to hold onto what cannot be held. It pairs with “vanity” to describe effort that leaves nothing in the hand (1:14, 17).

“The Preacher.” This is the NKJV title for Solomon as the speaker of the book. The Hebrew title is Qoheleth. In verses 1–2 another voice speaks about the Preacher, and in verse 12 Solomon begins speaking as “I.” This shift is a literary frame around his account and does not mean that a second author wrote the book (1:1, 2, 12).
