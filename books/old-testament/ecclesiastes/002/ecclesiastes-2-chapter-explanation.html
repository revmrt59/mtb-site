Chapter Study

Ecclesiastes 2

Verse-by-Verse
Ecclesiastes 2:1
Ecclesiastes 2:1
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:1 I said in my heart, "Come now, I will test you with mirth; therefore enjoy pleasure"; but surely, this also was vanity.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:1 I said to myself, “Come on, let’s try pleasure. Let’s look for the ‘good things’ in life.” But I found that this, too, was meaningless. (NLT)

Main Idea

Having found that wisdom only increased his grief (1:18), Solomon turns to pleasure as his next test, telling his own heart to “enjoy pleasure.” Before he describes the experiment, he gives its verdict: “this also was vanity,” meaning that pleasure, too, proved meaningless as a source of lasting satisfaction.

Verse Explanation

Having found that wisdom only increased his grief (1:18), Solomon turns to pleasure as his next test, telling his own heart to “enjoy pleasure.” Before he describes the experiment, he gives its verdict: “this also was vanity,” meaning that pleasure, too, proved meaningless as a source of lasting satisfaction.

I said in my heart: Solomon reports a conversation with himself. In Scripture the heart is a person’s inner self, the place where thinking, desire, and decision meet. He has just ended his test of wisdom with the admission that “in much wisdom is much grief” (1:18). Now he turns to his own inner life and sets out the next step of his search.

Come now, I will test you with mirth: The word “test” shows that this is a deliberate experiment, not a slide into indulgence. Mirth is gladness and merriment, the lighthearted joy people feel at a feast or among friends. Solomon makes his own heart the subject of the trial. He will fill it with gladness and watch what happens.

therefore enjoy pleasure: Solomon gives himself permission, even a command, to take in pleasure fully. Nothing in the verse treats enjoyment as sinful. Verse 10 confirms that the pleasure he found was real: “my heart rejoiced in all my labor.” The question he is testing is whether pleasure can carry the weight of a life’s meaning.

but surely, this also was vanity: Before he describes the experiment, Solomon announces how it turned out. The NKJV word “vanity” translates a Hebrew word that pictures a breath or vapor, something that cannot be held. Solomon uses it to call pleasure meaningless as a source of lasting satisfaction. The word “also” places pleasure beside wisdom, the pursuit he has just tested and found wanting. His verdict concerns what pleasure can finally gain when it is made the foundation of life. It does not deny that he enjoyed it.

Study Notes

Solomon’s words “I said in my heart” continue a pattern from the opening of his search. In 1:16 he writes, “I communed with my heart,” and in 2:15 he uses the same form again: “So I said in my heart.” Solomon reports each stage of the experiment as a conversation with himself, so the reader hears both his plans and his conclusions.

The short phrase “this also” links the verdict on pleasure to the earlier verdict on wisdom: “I perceived that this also is grasping for the wind” (1:17). The same words mark each new verdict later in the chapter (verses 15, 19, 21, 23, 26). In verse 24, however, they introduce something different: “This also, I saw, was from the hand of God.”

Ecclesiastes 2:2
Ecclesiastes 2:2
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:2 I said of laughter"”"Madness!"; and of mirth, "What does it accomplish?"(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:2 So I said, “Laughter is silly. What good does it do to seek pleasure?” (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon calls laughter “Madness!” and asks of mirth, “What does it accomplish?” He is weighing what laughter achieves when it is made the goal of life, and he finds that it cannot produce the lasting gain he is seeking. He is not condemning laughter itself as sinful.

Verse Explanation

Solomon calls laughter “Madness!” and asks of mirth, “What does it accomplish?” He is weighing what laughter achieves when it is made the goal of life, and he finds that it cannot produce the lasting gain he is seeking. He is not condemning laughter itself as sinful.

I said of laughter—“Madness!”: Solomon’s one-word verdict is sharp. Laughter is good in its place, and the book itself later speaks of “a time to laugh” (3:4). But when laughter is made the goal of life, Solomon judges it senseless. It gives a moment of relief and then is gone, leaving nothing lasting behind. By calling it madness, he means that laughter cannot answer the question of what makes a life worthwhile.

and of mirth, “What does it accomplish?”: This question asks for a result. The book opened by asking, “What profit has a man from all his labor” (1:3), and Solomon now asks the same kind of question about gladness. He expects the answer that mirth, however pleasant, produces no lasting gain. He is measuring what laughter achieves, not passing moral judgment on laughter itself.

Study Notes

The word “Madness!” comes from the vocabulary of Solomon’s search. He had set his heart “to know wisdom and to know madness and folly” (1:17), and in verse 12 he turns “to consider wisdom and madness and folly.” His verdict therefore puts laughter, when it is made life’s goal, on the side opposite wisdom.

The NLT translates the verse, “Laughter is silly. What good does it do to seek pleasure?” “Silly” is much milder than the NKJV’s “Madness!”, and the NLT’s second question speaks of seeking pleasure instead of mirth. The NKJV keeps the sharper verdict and its verbal link to 1:17 and 2:12.

Ecclesiastes 2:3
Ecclesiastes 2:3
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:3 I searched in my heart how to gratify my flesh with wine, while guiding my heart with wisdom, and how to lay hold on folly, till I might see what was good for the sons of men to do under heaven all the days of their lives.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:3 After much thought, I decided to cheer myself with wine. And while still seeking wisdom, I clutched at foolishness. In this way, I tried to experience the only happiness most people find during their brief life in this world. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon next tries wine and deliberately takes hold of “folly,” yet he keeps “guiding my heart with wisdom,” so wisdom continues to direct his investigation even while he tests wine and folly. His aim is to discover “what was good for the sons of men to do under heaven all the days of their lives,” the question his whole search is trying to answer.

Verse Explanation

Solomon next tries wine and deliberately takes hold of “folly,” yet he keeps “guiding my heart with wisdom,” so wisdom continues to direct his investigation even while he tests wine and folly. His aim is to discover “what was good for the sons of men to do under heaven all the days of their lives,” the question his whole search is trying to answer.

I searched in my heart how to gratify my flesh with wine: This test is also deliberate. Solomon planned how to use wine to please his “flesh,” meaning his body with its appetites and senses. Wine was a familiar source of cheer, and Solomon himself observes elsewhere that “wine makes merry” (10:19). Here wine becomes one more way to test whether pleasure can satisfy.

while guiding my heart with wisdom: This phrase keeps the experiment from turning into drunken abandon. Even while he tested wine, Solomon kept wisdom in charge of his inner life so that he could observe and judge what he experienced. Verse 9 makes the same point about the larger experiment: “Also my wisdom remained with me.” His verdict comes from a clear-minded observer, not from someone lost in excess.

and how to lay hold on folly: Solomon deliberately took hold of what he knew to be foolish. Folly is the opposite of wisdom, a way of living that sets sound judgment aside. He grasped it on purpose to examine it from the inside, so that his conclusion would come from experience and not from a safe distance.

till I might see what was good for the sons of men to do: Here Solomon states the purpose of the experiment. He wanted to discover what is truly good for people to do with their lives. Ecclesiastes uses “the sons of men” for human beings in general (2:8; 3:10), so the question concerns everyone, not only a king. It is the practical question behind the whole search: how should a person spend his life?

under heaven all the days of their lives: “Under heaven” sets the question in earthly life, the world people can see and touch. “All the days of their lives” reminds the reader that each person has only a limited number of days, and Solomon wants to know how those days are best spent. The chapter gives its first real answer in verse 24, where Solomon finds that enjoyment received “from the hand of God” is better than anything he could produce for himself.

Study Notes

The NLT words the purpose of the experiment differently: “I tried to experience the only happiness most people find during their brief life in this world.” The NKJV presents the aim as a question to be answered, “what was good for the sons of men to do.” In the NKJV, the experiment is a search for the right way to live. In the NLT, Solomon is sampling the pleasures ordinary people pursue. Both translations set the experiment within the limited span of human life.

Verse 3 uses “under heaven,” as 1:13 and 3:1 also do, while the rest of chapter 2 uses “under the sun” (verses 11, 17–20, 22). Both expressions refer to life on earth. The text does not explain why “under heaven” appears in this verse.

Solomon’s question about what is good for people to do returns in similar wording in 6:12: “who knows what is good for man in life, all the days of his vain life.” This repetition shows that the question reaches beyond the pleasure experiment and runs through the book.

Ecclesiastes 2:4–11 — Great Works, Great Wealth, and No Profit

Ecclesiastes 2:4
Ecclesiastes 2:4
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:4 I made my works great, I built myself houses, and planted myself vineyards.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:4 I also tried to find meaning by building huge homes for myself and by planting beautiful vineyards. (NLT)

Main Idea

The test moves from pleasure to achievement as Solomon says, “I made my works great.” Houses and vineyards begin a long list of projects, and the repeated “myself” shows that he built and planted for his own satisfaction.

Verse Explanation

The test moves from pleasure to achievement as Solomon says, “I made my works great.” Houses and vineyards begin a long list of projects, and the repeated “myself” shows that he built and planted for his own satisfaction.

I made my works great announces a new stage in Solomon’s search. In verses 1–3 he tested laughter and wine; now he turns to accomplishment. The word “works” is broad enough to cover everything that follows, from buildings and gardens to servants and treasure. The phrase describes a deliberate program of achievement on a scale only a king could attempt, and the flow of the passage shows him undertaking it to see whether accomplishment could supply what laughter and wine did not.

I built myself houses, and planted myself vineyards names the first two projects. Houses and vineyards are the ordinary goods of a settled life, a place to live and a supply of fruit and wine. Solomon speaks of them in the plural, and the list that follows shows a scale far beyond ordinary need. The small word “myself” appears twice in this one verse, because Solomon was both the builder and the one meant to benefit. The experiment tests whether a man can make his own life satisfying through what he builds for himself.

Study Notes

In the NKJV, “myself” or “for myself” appears five times in verses 4–8: “built myself houses,” “planted myself vineyards,” “made myself gardens,” “made myself water pools,” and “gathered for myself silver and gold.” The repetition runs through the whole inventory and keeps Solomon at the center of every undertaking.

The NLT begins this verse with “I also tried to find meaning,” words that do not appear in the NKJV. The NLT states openly a purpose that the NKJV leaves to the flow of the passage, where Solomon is searching for “what was good for the sons of men to do” (verse 3).

Ecclesiastes 2:5
Ecclesiastes 2:5
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:5 I made myself gardens and orchards, and I planted all kinds of fruit trees in them.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:5 I made gardens and parks, filling them with all kinds of fruit trees. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon adds gardens and orchards planted with “all kinds of fruit trees,” places of beauty and delight that only great wealth could create. Like the houses and vineyards, he made them for himself, so they add abundance and pleasure to his experiment.

Verse Explanation

Solomon adds gardens and orchards planted with “all kinds of fruit trees,” places of beauty and delight that only great wealth could create. Like the houses and vineyards, he made them for himself, so they add abundance and pleasure to his experiment.

I made myself gardens and orchards moves from necessities toward beauty and delight. A house gives shelter and a vineyard produces a crop, but gardens and orchards are places to walk, rest, and enjoy. Creating them took land, workers, and money in abundance, and once again Solomon made them “myself,” for his own enjoyment.

and I planted all kinds of fruit trees in them stresses variety. Solomon did not settle for a few familiar trees; he gathered “all kinds.” Fruit trees take years to reach their full yield, so this was patient, long-range investment rather than a passing indulgence. The detail adds both abundance and pleasure to the experiment, and it prepares for the next verse, where those trees need water.

Study Notes

None.

Ecclesiastes 2:6
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:6 I made myself water pools from which to water the growing trees of the grove.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:6 I built reservoirs to collect the water to irrigate my many flourishing groves. (NLT)

Main Idea

To keep his groves growing, Solomon built “water pools,” an undertaking that required planning and labor on a large scale. The detail shows the reach of his work: he did more than plant, because he also built what was needed to keep his plantings alive.

Verse Explanation

To keep his groves growing, Solomon built “water pools,” an undertaking that required planning and labor on a large scale. The detail shows the reach of his work: he did more than plant, because he also built what was needed to keep his plantings alive.

I made myself water pools shows how far Solomon’s work extended. Planting trees is one task; keeping a large planting alive is another. Pools that could water whole groves required planning, construction, and continuing labor. The “myself” of the earlier verses appears again, because these pools also served Solomon’s own designs.

from which to water the growing trees of the grove explains why the pools were built. They existed to sustain the trees he had planted in verse 5, so one work supported another. Solomon’s achievement was a connected whole, carried through with resources, skill, and follow-through. Whatever verdict he reaches in verse 11 therefore cannot be blamed on a half-hearted attempt.

Study Notes

None.

Ecclesiastes 2:7
Ecclesiastes 2:7
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:7 I acquired male and female servants, and had servants born in my house. Yes, I had greater possessions of herds and flocks than all who were in Jerusalem before me.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:7 I bought slaves, both men and women, and others were born into my household. I also owned large herds and flocks, more than any of the kings who had lived in Jerusalem before me. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon’s possessions include people and livestock: male and female servants, servants born in his household, and herds and flocks greater “than all who were in Jerusalem before me.” The comparison shows that no one before him had been better placed to test whether abundance can satisfy.

Verse Explanation

Solomon’s possessions include people and livestock: male and female servants, servants born in his household, and herds and flocks greater “than all who were in Jerusalem before me.” The comparison shows that no one before him had been better placed to test whether abundance can satisfy.

I acquired male and female servants moves the inventory from land and buildings to people. A household of this size needed many hands to run it, and Solomon lists these servants among the possessions that made up his greatness.

and had servants born in my house adds a further mark of size and permanence. Besides the servants he acquired, his household included servants born within it, so the royal establishment was large and settled enough to grow from within.

Yes, I had greater possessions of herds and flocks adds livestock, another visible measure of wealth. The opening “Yes” presses the point home: his herds and flocks really were greater than anyone else’s.

than all who were in Jerusalem before me supplies the standard of comparison. Solomon measures himself against everyone who had lived in Jerusalem before him and finds no equal. The claim matters for the argument because it establishes that no earlier resident of Jerusalem was better placed to test abundance. If great possessions could make a life meaningful, they should have done so for him.

Study Notes

The NLT translates the comparison as “more than any of the kings who had lived in Jerusalem before me,” which limits it to earlier kings. The NKJV’s “all who were in Jerusalem before me” is broader and does not mention kings.

Where the NKJV says Solomon “acquired male and female servants,” the NLT reads “I bought slaves, both men and women.” Both translations list these people among the possessions of Solomon’s household.

Ecclesiastes 2:8
Ecclesiastes 2:8
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:8 I also gathered for myself silver and gold and the special treasures of kings and of the provinces. I acquired male and female singers, the delights of the sons of men, and musical instruments of all kinds.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:8 I collected great sums of silver and gold, the treasure of many kings and provinces. I hired wonderful singers, both men and women, and had many beautiful concubines. I had everything a man could desire! (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon gathers royal wealth, “silver and gold and the special treasures of kings and of the provinces,” and he surrounds himself with singers and what the NKJV renders “musical instruments of all kinds.” By naming “the delights of the sons of men,” he shows that his search reached for the pleasures people prize most.

Verse Explanation

Solomon gathers royal wealth, “silver and gold and the special treasures of kings and of the provinces,” and he surrounds himself with singers and what the NKJV renders “musical instruments of all kinds.” By naming “the delights of the sons of men,” he shows that his search reached for the pleasures people prize most.

I also gathered for myself silver and gold turns to precious metals, the most direct measure of wealth. The words “for myself” continue the pattern of the earlier verses, because this treasure was also collected for Solomon’s own enjoyment.

and the special treasures of kings and of the provinces raises the scale again. The wording points to treasure of the kind that belongs to kings and comes from the provinces, the regions within a ruler’s domain. Ordinary wealth could buy houses and herds, but only a king could assemble a collection like this.

I acquired male and female singers moves from possessions to experience. Singers filled the court with music, and with them the inventory returns to the kind of pleasure Solomon began testing in verse 1.

the delights of the sons of men sums up what these acquisitions represent. “The sons of men” means human beings in general. Solomon used the same expression in verse 3 when he set out to discover “what was good for the sons of men to do.” By gathering the delights people everywhere value most, he made his test cover what humanity in general values.

and musical instruments of all kinds completes the list as the NKJV renders it. The Hebrew expression behind these words is uncertain, and translations differ widely in how they understand it. Whatever it names, the clause finishes a picture of extraordinary royal resources and unrestricted pursuit of delight. The argument of the chapter rests on that abundance, not on this one expression.

Study Notes

The Hebrew words translated “musical instruments of all kinds” are uncertain in meaning. The NKJV understands them as instruments, while other translations understand women or concubines; the NLT, for example, reads “had many beautiful concubines.” Some older renderings understand drinking vessels instead. Whichever meaning is correct, the verse describes extraordinary royal resources and pleasures.

Solomon’s many wives and concubines are recorded independently in 1 Kings 11:1–8. What Scripture says about his marriages therefore does not depend on how this uncertain phrase is translated.

Ecclesiastes 2:9
Ecclesiastes 2:9
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:9 So I became great and excelled more than all who were before me in Jerusalem. Also my wisdom remained with me.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:9 So I became greater than all who had lived in Jerusalem before me, and my wisdom never failed me. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon sums up the result: he became greater than anyone before him in Jerusalem. He adds that “my wisdom remained with me,” so he stayed a careful observer throughout his pursuit of pleasure and achievement, and his verdict is a wise man’s judgment, not the regret of someone lost in excess.

Verse Explanation

Solomon sums up the result: he became greater than anyone before him in Jerusalem. He adds that “my wisdom remained with me,” so he stayed a careful observer throughout his pursuit of pleasure and achievement, and his verdict is a wise man’s judgment, not the regret of someone lost in excess.

So I became great and excelled more than all who were before me in Jerusalem brings the inventory to its result. The word “So” gathers up everything listed in verses 4–8. The man who set out to make his works “great” (verse 4) now says that he himself “became great.” His achievements raised him above everyone who had lived in Jerusalem before him, completing the comparison he began with his herds and flocks in verse 7.

Also my wisdom remained with me explains how Solomon could judge his own experiment. Wisdom, the skill for living and judging well, stayed with him while he pursued pleasure and achievement. This matches verse 3, where he tested wine “while guiding my heart with wisdom.” He was fully immersed in the pursuit and still kept the clear sight to evaluate it. So when he gives his verdict in verse 11, he speaks as someone who saw the experiment clearly from the inside.

Study Notes

This verse restates Solomon’s claim in 1:16, where he said, “I have attained greatness, and have gained more wisdom than all who were before me in Jerusalem.” The same three elements return here: greatness, wisdom, and comparison with all who were before him in Jerusalem. In 1:16 the claim belongs to his search into wisdom. Here it concludes his search through pleasure and achievement.

Ecclesiastes 2:10
Ecclesiastes 2:10
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:10 Whatever my eyes desired I did not keep from them. I did not withhold my heart from any pleasure, For my heart rejoiced in all my labor; And this was my reward from all my labor.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:10 Anything I wanted, I would take. I denied myself no pleasure. I even found great pleasure in hard work, a reward for all my labors. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon held nothing back: “Whatever my eyes desired I did not keep from them.” He also admits that his work brought him real enjoyment, “my heart rejoiced in all my labor,” and he calls that enjoyment his reward. The verdict that follows therefore does not deny the pleasure he felt. It asks whether that passing pleasure was all his labor could give.

Verse Explanation

Solomon held nothing back: “Whatever my eyes desired I did not keep from them.” He also admits that his work brought him real enjoyment, “my heart rejoiced in all my labor,” and he calls that enjoyment his reward. The verdict that follows therefore does not deny the pleasure he felt. It asks whether that passing pleasure was all his labor could give.

Whatever my eyes desired I did not keep from them shows that the experiment held nothing back. The eyes stand for desire awakened by what a person sees. Earlier Solomon observed that “The eye is not satisfied with seeing” (1:8). Here he gives his eyes everything they want, to learn whether a full supply could finally satisfy them.

I did not withhold my heart from any pleasure carries the same freedom inward. Here “heart” means the inner person, the seat of desire and choice. Solomon gave himself fully to the pursuit, so his experiment cannot be dismissed as a test that never went far enough.

For my heart rejoiced in all my labor makes an honest admission. Solomon genuinely enjoyed his work. The building, planting, and gathering delighted him while he was doing them. This matters for the whole chapter, because his coming verdict does not claim that achievement gave him no pleasure.

And this was my reward from all my labor identifies that enjoyment as what his labor paid him. The reward was real, but the sentence also sets its limits: the enjoyment he felt along the way was what all that labor produced. Verse 11 then asks whether anything more lasting remained once that enjoyment had been received.

Study Notes

In the NKJV, “For” and “And” begin with capital letters in the middle of this verse because the translation sets these clauses as separate poetic lines. Verse 11 is laid out the same way.

Ecclesiastes 2:11
Ecclesiastes 2:11
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:11 Then I looked on all the works that my hands had done And on the labor in which I had toiled; And indeed all was vanity and grasping for the wind. There was no profit under the sun.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:11 But as I looked at everything I had worked so hard to accomplish, it was all so meaningless — like chasing the wind. There was nothing really worthwhile anywhere. (NLT)

Main Idea

When Solomon looks back over “all the works that my hands had done,” he concludes that “all was vanity and grasping for the wind,” as meaningless as trying to hold the wind in your hand. “There was no profit under the sun”: measured within the limits of earthly life, his great undertaking left no lasting gain beyond the enjoyment he had already received along the way.

Verse Explanation

When Solomon looks back over “all the works that my hands had done,” he concludes that “all was vanity and grasping for the wind,” as meaningless as trying to hold the wind in your hand. “There was no profit under the sun”: measured within the limits of earthly life, his great undertaking left no lasting gain beyond the enjoyment he had already received along the way.

Then I looked on all the works that my hands had done marks the moment of assessment. After all the building, planting, and gathering, Solomon steps back to survey the whole. “The works” recalls “I made my works great” in verse 4, and “my hands” stresses that this was his own effort, the achievement of a life spent making things for himself.

And on the labor in which I had toiled widens the review to include the effort itself. Solomon weighs both the results and the work that produced them, including the very labor he admits he enjoyed in verse 10.

And indeed all was vanity and grasping for the wind gives the verdict. “Vanity” translates a Hebrew word whose basic image is breath or vapor, and Solomon uses it to call his whole undertaking meaningless, empty of lasting substance and gone like a breath. “Grasping for the wind” pictures a man closing his hand on moving air and finding it empty. The word “all” leaves nothing out: houses, vineyards, gardens, pools, servants, herds, treasure, and singers fall under the same judgment.

There was no profit under the sun answers the question that opened the book (1:3). Profit is the lasting gain left over after all of one’s labor, and Solomon finds none. “Under the sun” marks the limits of his examination. He measures life by what people can observe and achieve before the grave, and he leaves God’s revelation and future judgment out of the calculation. Within those limits, his great undertaking left nothing beyond the enjoyment he had already received along the way.

This verdict is not the chapter’s last word. God is not named in this chapter until verse 24, where Solomon recognizes that the enjoyment of food, drink, and labor comes “from the hand of God.” The wider witness of Scripture carries the answer further. Because Christ has risen, labor is “not in vain in the Lord” (1 Corinthians 15:58). Work done in Him is not erased by death, the very loss Solomon could not get past under the sun.

Study Notes

The vocabulary of the book’s opening question returns here almost word for word. In 1:3 Solomon asked, “What profit has a man from all his labor In which he toils under the sun?” Verse 11 repeats “labor,” “profit,” and “under the sun,” and its “toiled” matches “toils.”

“Grasping for the wind” appears in nine verses of Ecclesiastes, all between 1:14 and 6:9 (1:14, 17; 2:11, 17, 26; 4:4, 6, 16; 6:9). Within this chapter it returns in verses 17 and 26, linking the verdict on great works with the later verdicts on life and on the sinner’s gathering.

The NLT renders the final sentence “There was nothing really worthwhile anywhere.” That keeps the verdict but does not reproduce “profit” or “under the sun,” and the NLT does not use “profit” in 1:3 either. As a result, the link between this verse and the book’s opening question is less visible in the NLT than in the NKJV.

Ecclesiastes 2:12–17 — Wisdom Excels Folly, Yet Both Die

Ecclesiastes 2:12
Ecclesiastes 2:12
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 2:12 Then I turned myself to consider wisdom and madness and folly; For what can the man do who succeeds the king?"” Only what he has already done.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:12 So I decided to compare wisdom with foolishness and madness (for who can do this better than I, the king? ). (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon now sets “wisdom and madness and folly” side by side to weigh them. He asks, “what can the man do who succeeds the king?” and answers, “Only what he has already done.” The question suggests that no one coming after Solomon could take the experiment further than he had, and it also introduces the successor who will trouble him in verses 18–19.

Verse Explanation

Solomon now sets “wisdom and madness and folly” side by side to weigh them. He asks, “what can the man do who succeeds the king?” and answers, “Only what he has already done.” The question suggests that no one coming after Solomon could take the experiment further than he had, and it also introduces the successor who will trouble him in verses 18–19.

Then I turned myself to consider wisdom and madness and folly — Solomon has just looked over his great works and found “no profit under the sun” (verse 11). Now he takes up a different question: what is wisdom itself worth when it is set beside its opposites? Madness and folly are not new subjects for him. He called laughter “Madness!” in verse 2, and in verse 3 he deliberately set out “to lay hold on folly” while still “guiding my heart with wisdom.” Having lived on both sides of that line, he is in a position to weigh one against the other.

For what can the man do who succeeds the king? — The question explains why Solomon’s assessment carries weight. He is the king who had every resource and denied himself nothing (verses 4–10). Whoever comes after him will work with the same kinds of materials and face the same limits, so Solomon asks what such a person could possibly add.

Only what he has already done — The answer closes the question. A successor can only repeat what has already been tried, so no one will be better placed than Solomon to reach a verdict, and no later king will find a gain that he missed. The words also bring a new figure into view. “The man who will come after me” returns in verses 18–19, where Solomon grieves that he must leave everything he built to him. The passage does not tell us who that successor is.

Study Notes

The three terms in this verse repeat the wording of Ecclesiastes 1:17, “I set my heart to know wisdom and to know madness and folly.” After the tests of pleasure (2:1–3) and great works (2:4–11), Solomon picks up the comparison he began in chapter 1 and carries it through to its conclusion.

The NKJV and NLT understand the second half of the verse differently. The NKJV asks what the king’s successor can do and answers that he can only repeat what has been done. The NLT reads it as Solomon’s own claim to be the best person for the comparison: “for who can do this better than I, the king?” Both renderings arrive at the same point, that no one is better qualified than Solomon to judge. Only the NKJV wording, however, introduces the successor who reappears in verses 18–19.

Main Idea

Solomon affirms that wisdom has a real advantage: it is better than folly as surely as light is better than darkness. What follows does not cancel this statement. Wisdom really does help a person see clearly and live well.

Verse Explanation

Solomon affirms that wisdom has a real advantage: it is better than folly as surely as light is better than darkness. What follows does not cancel this statement. Wisdom really does help a person see clearly and live well.

Then I saw that wisdom excels folly As light excels darkness — Before Solomon describes the limit of wisdom, he states its value without hedging. “Then I saw” presents the statement as a conclusion he reached through his own testing. To make the comparison he chooses light and darkness, two things no one would treat as equal options. Anyone who has tried to cross a room at night knows how much better it is to have light. Wisdom has that kind of advantage over folly, because it lets a person see what is in front of him, judge situations accurately, and choose a good path.

Solomon never takes this statement back. In the verses that follow he will find that wisdom cannot stop death, but its advantage still holds for the whole of life. Ecclesiastes treats wisdom as skill for living. What the book exposes is the hope that wisdom could be enough on its own to secure a lasting gain.

The chapter turns. Nothing is better than to eat, drink, and let one’s soul “enjoy good in his labor,” and Solomon now sees that this enjoyment is “from the hand of God.” He could not produce lasting satisfaction by pursuing pleasure and achievement, but simple enjoyment can be received as God’s gift. This is the first passage in the book that commends joy as His gift.

Nothing is better for a man than that he should eat and drink — After the despair of verses 18–23, Solomon reaches a very different conclusion. Eating and drinking are the most ordinary parts of daily life. Solomon had already tried wine as a way to find what was good (verse 3), and that experiment failed. Here food and drink are no longer a test meant to produce lasting gain. They are simple goods, enjoyed for what they are. Solomon is not saying that nothing else matters. He is saying that, after everything he has tried, nothing open to a person is better than receiving these daily goods.

and that his soul should enjoy good in his labor — The word “soul” places this enjoyment in the person’s inner life, not merely in the body’s appetite. What is surprising is where the enjoyment is found: “in his labor.” Only a few lines earlier Solomon said, “Then I hated all my labor” (verse 18), and he described a worker whose days are sorrowful, whose work is burdensome, and whose heart “takes no rest” even at night (verse 23). The same labor that could not deliver lasting gain can still give real, present good. Solomon had tasted this before, when “my heart rejoiced in all my labor” (verse 10). The difference now lies in where he sees that enjoyment coming from.

This also, I saw, was from the hand of God — “I saw” marks this as something Solomon came to recognize, not something he produced. “The hand of God” pictures God as the giver who places these good things into a person’s life. This is the first time God is named in Ecclesiastes 2. Through verses 1–23 Solomon built, planted, gathered, and weighed everything by what he himself could achieve. When he finally speaks of God, he is not reporting another achievement. He is receiving a gift. Pleasure failed when Solomon tried to make it the foundation of life, yet eating, drinking, and work are not condemned by the verdict of verse 11. Received from God, those same ordinary joys are good.

Study Notes

The wording of verse 24 echoes the end of verse 23 with the opposite result. Verse 23 closes, “This also is vanity,” while verse 24 closes, “This also, I saw, was from the hand of God.” The repeated “This also” sets the meaningless toil of the anxious worker directly beside the enjoyment that comes as God’s gift.

The phrase “under the sun” appears five times in verses 17–22 (verses 17, 18, 19, 20, and 22) but not at all in verses 24–26. Its absence fits the shift from Solomon’s earthbound calculation to his recognition of God’s gift. Later enjoyment passages do use the phrase (5:18; 8:15), so the absence here is worth noticing but is not a rule for the whole book.

Ecclesiastes returns to this conviction later and names it directly as a gift. Solomon says that every man should “eat and drink and enjoy the good of all his labor—it is the gift of God” (3:13), and of the power “to receive his heritage and rejoice in his labor” he says, “this is the gift of God” (5:19). These parallels confirm that “from the hand of God” in verse 24 describes God’s giving.

Ecclesiastes 2:25 For who can eat or enjoy anything apart from him? (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon backs this conclusion with his own experience: no one could eat or enjoy more than he had. Because he had tried every pleasure, his testimony that enjoyment comes from God’s hand carries special weight.

Verse Explanation

Solomon backs this conclusion with his own experience: no one could eat or enjoy more than he had. Because he had tried every pleasure, his testimony that enjoyment comes from God’s hand carries special weight.

For who can eat — The opening “For” ties this verse to verse 24 and gives a reason to trust Solomon’s conclusion. His question is rhetorical, and the answer he expects is “no one.” Solomon had a king’s resources and had used them without restraint. Earlier in the chapter he said, “Whatever my eyes desired I did not keep from them” (verse 10).

or who can have enjoyment, more than I? — No one had pursued enjoyment more fully than Solomon, and that is why his testimony matters. When the man who tried every pleasure says that simple enjoyment comes from God’s hand, he speaks from experience, not from lack of opportunity. He had learned that abundance could not produce lasting gain, yet he does not now despise eating and enjoyment. He puts them in their proper place, as gifts received from God rather than achievements that could secure meaning.

Study Notes

The NLT renders this verse quite differently: “For who can eat or enjoy anything apart from him?” In that rendering the pronoun “him” refers back to God in verse 24, so the question points to God as the source of enjoyment. The NKJV’s “more than I” points instead to Solomon’s own unmatched experience as support for verse 24. The two translations reflect different understandings of the underlying wording, but both connect enjoyment with God’s gift in verse 24: the NKJV through Solomon’s testimony, the NLT through its pronoun “him,” which points back to God.

Ecclesiastes 2:26 For God gives wisdom and knowledge and joy to a man who is good in His sight; but to the sinner He gives the work of gathering and collecting, that he may give to him who is good before God. This also is vanity and grasping for the wind.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 2:26 God gives wisdom, knowledge, and joy to those who please him. But if a sinner becomes wealthy, God takes the wealth away and gives it to those who please him. This, too, is meaningless — like chasing the wind. (NLT)

Main Idea

God gives “wisdom and knowledge and joy to a man who is good in His sight,” but to the sinner He gives “the work of gathering and collecting” for the benefit of the one who is good before God. This places enjoyment within a right relationship with God, but it is not a promise that the faithful will grow rich. The closing words, “This also is vanity and grasping for the wind,” describe the futility of the sinner’s gathering, which he never finally keeps. They do not cancel the gift affirmed in verse 24.

Verse Explanation

God gives “wisdom and knowledge and joy to a man who is good in His sight,” but to the sinner He gives “the work of gathering and collecting” for the benefit of the one who is good before God. This places enjoyment within a right relationship with God, but it is not a promise that the faithful will grow rich. The closing words, “This also is vanity and grasping for the wind,” describe the futility of the sinner’s gathering, which he never finally keeps. They do not cancel the gift affirmed in verse 24.

For God gives wisdom and knowledge and joy — Once again “For” explains what came before. Enjoyment comes from God’s hand because God is the One who gives. Wisdom and knowledge were the very things Solomon pursued when his search began, and by his own effort they only increased his grief (1:18). Here they stand beside joy as gifts that God gives, not prizes a person can seize.

to a man who is good in His sight — God’s gift is connected with a person’s standing before Him. “In His sight” means as God sees and judges, not as people measure success. This places enjoyment within a right relationship with God. The verse does not explain how someone comes to be good in God’s sight. It shows that God’s gifts are not detached from a person’s relationship with Him.

but to the sinner He gives the work of gathering and collecting — The sinner also receives something from God, but what he receives is a task rather than enjoyment. “The work of gathering and collecting” pictures restless accumulation: piling up goods without ever coming to rest in them. The phrase recalls the worker of verses 22–23, whose labor and “striving of his heart” left him sorrowful by day and sleepless at night.

that he may give to him who is good before God — What the sinner gathers ends up with someone else, the person who is good before God. This speaks to the bitter problem of verses 18–21, where a worker had to leave his labor to “a man who has not labored for it.” Here the transfer of what a person gathers is placed under God’s hand. Verse 26 describes a general pattern in God’s ordering of life. It does not promise that every faithful person will become wealthy or inherit a sinner’s estate. Ecclesiastes itself admits that just people sometimes receive what the wicked deserve (8:14), and it leaves the final settling of every account to God’s judgment: “God will bring every work into judgment” (12:14).

This also is vanity and grasping for the wind — Solomon closes the chapter with a refrain he has used before (verses 11 and 17). Here it most naturally describes the sinner’s gathering. He works to collect and keep, yet what he gathers passes to another, so his effort is meaningless, as empty as trying to hold the wind in your hand. The refrain does not withdraw the gift of verse 24. The enjoyment God gives remains good. What proves empty is the grasping that tries to hold what it can never finally keep.

Study Notes

The pairing of “wisdom and knowledge” repeats the pair Solomon used near the start of his search: “My heart has understood great wisdom and knowledge” (1:16), followed by the discovery that “he who increases knowledge increases sorrow” (1:18). The search that began with wisdom and knowledge as personal attainments ends with them listed among God’s gifts.

The NKJV speaks only of “the work of gathering and collecting.” The NLT gives the clause a financial meaning: “if a sinner becomes wealthy, God takes the wealth away.” The NKJV wording is broader. It describes the activity of accumulating without naming wealth or explaining how the transfer takes place.

In the NKJV, the sinner’s “gathering” uses the same English verb Solomon used for his own accumulation: “I also gathered for myself silver and gold” (verse 8). The verse does not identify Solomon as the sinner. Still, the repeated word sets the restless gathering of verse 26 beside the self-directed acquiring that the chapter has already found empty.

The refrain itself does not say what “This” refers to. On its own it could be heard as a summary of the whole verse or even of the whole search, not only of the sinner’s gathering that immediately precedes it.
