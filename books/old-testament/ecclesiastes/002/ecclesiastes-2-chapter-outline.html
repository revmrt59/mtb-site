Ecclesiastes 2 Chapter Outline

Chapter Focus

Ecclesiastes 2 carries Solomon’s royal search through to its verdict as he tests pleasure, then great works and wealth, weighs wisdom against folly in the face of death, and grieves over leaving his labor to an unknown successor. The chapter closes with Solomon recognizing that food, drink, and enjoyment in work come “from the hand of God” (verse 24).

Chapter Sections

1. Ecclesiastes 2:1–3 — Laughter and Wine Put to the Test

Solomon moves from the test of wisdom in chapter 1 to the test of pleasure: “I will test you with mirth.” He judges laughter “Madness!” and then tries wine “while guiding my heart with wisdom,” looking for what is good for people to do all the days of their lives.

2. Ecclesiastes 2:4–11 — Great Works, Great Wealth, and No Profit

Solomon lists his houses, vineyards, gardens, pools, servants, herds, treasure, and singers, and he admits that “my heart rejoiced in all my labor.” Yet when he looks back over everything his hands had done, his verdict is that “all was vanity and grasping for the wind. There was no profit under the sun.”

3. Ecclesiastes 2:12–17 — Wisdom Excels Folly, Yet Both Die

Solomon affirms that “wisdom excels folly As light excels darkness,” but he sees that “the same event happens to them all,” and the wise man is forgotten just as the fool is. Faced with that shared death, he says, “Therefore I hated life.”

4. Ecclesiastes 2:18–23 — Labor Left to an Unknown Successor

Solomon hates his labor because he must leave it to “the man who will come after me,” and no one knows whether that heir will be wise or a fool. His despair widens into a picture of anxious toil: the worker’s days are sorrowful, and “even in the night his heart takes no rest.”

5. Ecclesiastes 2:24–26 — Enjoyment Received from the Hand of God

The chapter turns: nothing is better than to eat, drink, and enjoy good in one’s labor, and Solomon sees that this comes “from the hand of God.” God gives wisdom, knowledge, and joy to the person who is good in His sight, while the sinner gathers for someone else, and the closing words, “This also is vanity and grasping for the wind,” describe the futility of gathering what one never finally keeps.
