Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 2
Main Idea:

Solomon tests pleasure, achievement, and wisdom and finds that none of them can overcome death or secure lasting gain, until at the chapter’s close he recognizes that the simple enjoyment of food, drink, and work comes “from the hand of God.”

Setting:

Ecclesiastes 2 continues the royal search Solomon began in 1:12, after his test of wisdom ended in grief (1:18). The chapter completes that search before a new poem about God’s times opens chapter 3.

Chapter Outline:

  • Ecclesiastes 2:1–3 — Laughter and Wine Put to the Test

  • Ecclesiastes 2:4–11 — Great Works, Great Wealth, and No Profit

  • Ecclesiastes 2:12–17 — Wisdom Excels Folly, Yet Both Die

  • Ecclesiastes 2:18–23 — Labor Left to an Unknown Successor

  • Ecclesiastes 2:24–26 — Enjoyment Received from the Hand of God

Key Verse:

“Nothing is better for a man than that he should eat and drink, and that his soul should enjoy good in his labor. This also, I saw, was from the hand of God.” (Ecclesiastes 2:24)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Ecclesiastes 2 records what happened when a king who could try anything actually did. Solomon gave himself first to pleasure and then to building and gathering on a scale no one in Jerusalem had matched. One small word keeps coming back: he built himself houses, planted himself vineyards, made himself gardens and pools, and gathered for himself silver and gold (verses 4–8). Solomon was trying to build a satisfying life with his own hands, and as far as it went, the attempt worked. He admits that “my heart rejoiced in all my labor” (verse 10). Yet when he looked over the whole undertaking, his verdict was plain: “There was no profit under the sun” (verse 11).

In the end, wisdom fared no better. Solomon insists that wisdom excels folly “As light excels darkness” (verse 13), but the wise man dies like the fool and is forgotten just as quickly. Death also does more than end the worker. It hands everything he built to “the man who will come after me” (verse 18), who may turn out to be a fool. Solomon does not soften what this did to him. “Therefore I hated life,” he writes (verse 17), and he describes a worker whose heart “takes no rest” even at night (verse 23). These lines honestly report the low point of his search. They are not the book’s counsel for living.

Then the chapter turns. The same eating, drinking, and work that could not carry the weight of a self-made life are now received “from the hand of God” (verse 24). God is not named anywhere in verses 1–23, and it fits the whole search that He first appears at this turning point, where joy is received as a gift. Verse 26 places that joy within a relationship with Him, for God gives “wisdom and knowledge and joy to a man who is good in His sight.” The good things Solomon tried to seize for himself are given freely by a generous God.

Chapter Sections

Ecclesiastes 2:1–3 — Laughter and Wine Put to the Test

Having found that wisdom only brought grief, Solomon tells his heart, “I will test you with mirth,” and gives the verdict before he describes the experiment: “this also was vanity.” He calls laughter “Madness!” because, made the goal of life, it cannot produce lasting gain, and he tries wine while still “guiding my heart with wisdom,” searching for what is good for people to do “all the days of their lives.”

Ecclesiastes 2:4–11 — Great Works, Great Wealth, and No Profit

Solomon builds, plants, and gathers on a greater scale than anyone in Jerusalem before him, denies himself nothing his eyes desire, and genuinely enjoys the work. When he looks back over all of it, he concludes that it was “vanity and grasping for the wind” and that “There was no profit under the sun,” meaning that nothing lasting remained beyond the pleasure he had already received along the way.

Ecclesiastes 2:12–17 — Wisdom Excels Folly, Yet Both Die

Solomon weighs wisdom against folly and affirms that wisdom truly is better, as light is better than darkness. Yet “the same event happens to them all”: the wise man dies and is forgotten just like the fool, and that shared end brings Solomon to the words “Therefore I hated life.”

Ecclesiastes 2:18–23 — Labor Left to an Unknown Successor

Solomon hates his labor because he must leave it to a successor who may be wise or a fool, yet who will rule over everything he built. He then widens his own case into a general one: a person may work with wisdom, knowledge, and skill and still have to leave what he has gained to someone who never worked for it, while such toil brings sorrowful days and sleepless nights.

Ecclesiastes 2:24–26 — Enjoyment Received from the Hand of God

The chapter turns as Solomon sees that eating, drinking, and enjoying good in one’s labor come “from the hand of God,” a conclusion his own unmatched experience supports (verse 25). God gives wisdom, knowledge, and joy to the person who is good in His sight, while the sinner gathers for someone else, and the closing refrain most likely describes the futility of that gathering without cancelling the gift affirmed in verse 24.

Why This Chapter Matters

The verdict in this chapter comes from a witness who cannot easily be dismissed. Solomon had the wealth, the wisdom, and the freedom to try everything, and he found real pleasure along the way. When he reports that his achievements still left “no profit” (verse 11), he answers the book’s opening question about labor (1:3) with the weight of experience.

Scripture also lets grief over death and lost work be spoken aloud here. Ecclesiastes 2 does not name Christ, but read alongside 1 Corinthians 15, the problem it raises finds its answer in Him. Because Christ has risen as the firstfruits of the resurrection, labor in the Lord is not in vain. Work done in Him is neither erased by death nor lost to an unknown heir.

Finally, the chapter shows God as a generous giver. The enjoyment Solomon could not manufacture is received from God’s hand within a right relationship with Him. Pleasure failed as the foundation of life, but Scripture never forbids it, and received as God’s gift, it can be enjoyed with gratitude.

Key Themes to Watch For

Pleasure and achievement put to the test. Solomon withholds nothing from himself and enjoys his work, yet the whole undertaking leaves no lasting gain (verses 1–11).

Wisdom’s real advantage and its limit. Wisdom excels folly as light excels darkness, but it cannot stop death or keep the wise from being forgotten (verses 13–16).

Labor that must be left behind. Death hands a worker’s achievements to a successor, and the worker cannot ensure that the heir will be wise or control what becomes of the work. Anxious toil cannot secure the gain it strives for (verses 18–23).

Enjoyment received from God’s hand. The simple pleasures that failed as a self-made foundation return as gifts God gives within a relationship with Him (verses 24–26).

Key Words & Concepts

“Vanity.” The NKJV uses this word to translate a Hebrew word that pictures a breath or vapor, something that cannot be held. Solomon uses it to call life meaningless when it is pursued without God. In this chapter he applies it to pleasure, wisdom, and labor alike (verses 1, 15, 19, 26).

“Grasping for the wind.” This phrase pictures trying to hold what cannot be held. It pairs with “vanity” to describe effort that leaves nothing in the hand (verses 11, 17, 26).

“Under the sun.” The phrase marks Solomon’s examination of life inside earthly limits. He measures life by what people can see and achieve before the grave, and he leaves God’s revelation and future judgment out of the calculation. That limited view explains why his verdicts on labor sound so bleak (verses 11, 17–20, 22).

“Profit.” Profit is the lasting gain left after all of one’s labor, the very thing the book’s opening question asks about (1:3). Verse 11 answers that there was none, and verse 22 asks the same question again in personal terms.

“The same event.” This expression refers to death, which comes to the wise and the fool alike (verse 14). Death gives them the same earthly end, but it does not make them equal in character or in their standing before God.

“From the hand of God.” The enjoyment of food, drink, and work is received as God’s gift (verse 24). Solomon had already enjoyed his labor (verse 10), yet his achievements could not secure lasting satisfaction. Here enjoyment is something received from God’s hand, not a lasting gain won through achievement. This is the first of the book’s passages that commend joy as His gift.
