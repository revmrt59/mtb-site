Ecclesiastes 3

Verse-by-Verse
Ecclesiastes 3:1
Ecclesiastes 3:1
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 3:1 To everything there is a season, A time for every purpose under heaven:(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 3:1 For everything there is a season,a time for every activity under heaven. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon opens the poem by declaring that every activity in human life has its season, "a time for every purpose under heaven." This opening line sets the frame for the paired times that follow: life's changing circumstances unfold within an order that people did not set and cannot control, an order that verses 10–11 trace back to God.

Verse Explanation

Solomon opens the poem by declaring that every activity in human life has its season, "a time for every purpose under heaven." This opening line sets the frame for the paired times that follow: life's changing circumstances unfold within an order that people did not set and cannot control, an order that verses 10–11 trace back to God.

To everything there is a season. Chapter 2 ended with the king's search judged "vanity and grasping for the wind" (2:26). Here Solomon makes a fresh start, and his first word is sweeping: "everything." No part of human life is left outside the claim. Every activity he is about to name has a season, a span of time when it comes into its own and then gives way to something else.

A time for every purpose under heaven. The second line says the same thing in new words, so "season" and "time" explain each other. "Purpose" here means the matters people take up or go through, as the list that follows makes clear: birth and death, planting, building, grieving, speaking, war. "Under heaven" places all of it in the earthly world where human life is lived. The verse ends with a colon, and verses 2–8 unfold what this opening line announces.

The verse does not yet say who sets these times. It observes that people do not choose them; no one picks the hour of their own birth or death. This is humbling coming from a king who had just tried to take hold of life on his own terms: "Whatever my eyes desired I did not keep from them" (2:10). In verses 10–11 Solomon names God as the One who gives the task and has made "everything beautiful in its time." Read with those verses, the poem describes changing circumstances held within God's ordering, not a blind cycle.

Study Notes

The NLT renders this line "a time for every activity under heaven." That wording fits the list that follows, which names activities and experiences rather than private intentions. It also helps a modern reader hear the NKJV's "purpose" as a matter or undertaking.

Verse 17 picks up the language of this opening line: "there is a time there for every purpose and for every work." The echo ties God's coming judgment to the same appointed times this poem describes.

Ecclesiastes uses "under heaven" in 1:13 and 2:3 as well as here. In both earlier verses it describes the whole range of human activity that Solomon set out to examine: "all that is done under heaven" (1:13) and what the sons of men do "under heaven all the days of their lives" (2:3).

Ecclesiastes 3:2
Ecclesiastes 3:2
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 3:2 A time to be born, And a time to die; A time to plant, And a time to pluck what is planted;(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 3:2 A time to be born and a time to die.A time to plant and a time to harvest. (NLT)

Main Idea

The first pair, being born and dying, places the whole span of human life within times people cannot fully control. The second pair moves to the field, where planting has its time and so does uprooting "what is planted," so even removals and endings belong to the ordered seasons of life.

Verse Explanation

The first pair, being born and dying, places the whole span of human life within times people cannot fully control. The second pair moves to the field, where planting has its time and so does uprooting "what is planted," so even removals and endings belong to the ordered seasons of life.

A time to be born, And a time to die. The poem begins where every human life begins and ends. No one arranges their own birth, and no one sets the day of their death. Because these two moments come first, every other season in the poem happens somewhere between them. The pairing also keeps death in plain view. Solomon has already grieved that the wise man dies "As the fool!" (2:16), and here death stands as one of life's appointed times.

A time to plant, And a time to pluck what is planted. The second pair moves from the human body to the field. Planting is the hopeful beginning of a farmer's work. "Pluck what is planted" describes pulling up what has been planted, so the phrase speaks of uprooting and removal, not of gathering a harvest. The pair sets a beginning beside an ending: what went into the ground in one season may be taken out in another. Endings belong to the ordered seasons too, and an ending is not in itself a sign that life has gone wrong.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the second pair "a time to plant and a time to harvest." The NKJV's "pluck what is planted" is closer to uprooting, and that sense keeps the contrast between beginning and removal. Harvest should not be assumed as the meaning of the line.

Planting recalls Solomon's own projects in chapter 2, where he "planted myself vineyards" (2:4) and "planted all kinds of fruit trees" (2:5). The king who planted on a grand scale now counts planting among times he does not control, set beside a time when what is planted is pulled up.

Ecclesiastes 3:3
Ecclesiastes 3:3
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 3:3 A time to kill, And a time to heal; A time to break down, And a time to build up;(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 3:3 A time to kill and a time to heal.A time to tear down and a time to build up. (NLT)

Main Idea

This verse sets destruction beside restoration: there is a time to kill and a time to heal, a time to break down and a time to build up. The poem names these as real seasons of human experience, but listing an act here does not make every killing right, and knowing what a moment truly calls for requires discernment.

Verse Explanation

This verse sets destruction beside restoration: there is a time to kill and a time to heal, a time to break down and a time to build up. The poem names these as real seasons of human experience, but listing an act here does not make every killing right, and knowing what a moment truly calls for requires discernment.

A time to kill, And a time to heal. The poem sets the taking of life beside the restoring of health. The verse does not say who kills or why, and it gives no permission for any particular act. It names killing as something that actually happens in human life, alongside the healing that mends wounds and sickness. Being listed among the seasons does not make every killing right. Later in this chapter Solomon declares that "God shall judge the righteous and the wicked" (verse 17), so a time for an action never removes accountability for how and why it is done.

A time to break down, And a time to build up. The second pair turns to buildings: some structures are torn down and others are raised. Solomon knew the building side well, for he says, "I built myself houses" (2:4). Here building is not a permanent achievement but one season among others, and tearing down has its place too. Telling which of the two a moment calls for takes discernment, because the same work can be wise at one time and harmful at another.

Study Notes

In verse 2 both pairs put the life-giving action first: being born before dying, planting before uprooting. Verse 3 reverses the order, so each pair moves from damage toward restoration: killing to healing, breaking down to building up. Throughout the poem the order within the pairs keeps changing, so no single direction governs the whole list.

Ecclesiastes 3:4
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 3:4 A time to weep, And a time to laugh; A time to mourn, And a time to dance;(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 3:4 A time to cry and a time to laugh.A time to grieve and a time to dance. (NLT)

Main Idea

Grief and joy each have their time. The verse moves from private weeping and laughter to the public expressions of mourning and dancing, showing that sorrow and celebration both belong to life and that neither one tells the whole story.

Verse Explanation

Grief and joy each have their time. The verse moves from private weeping and laughter to the public expressions of mourning and dancing, showing that sorrow and celebration both belong to life and that neither one tells the whole story.

A time to weep, And a time to laugh. Grief and joy each have their hour. Weeping and laughing are personal responses that rise from within, often without any planning. The poem does not rank them, and it does not tell the reader to cut short the first and hurry to the second. Both belong to an honest human life.

A time to mourn, And a time to dance. The second pair moves from private feeling to its outward expression. Mourning is grief shown openly when a death has come, and dancing is joy shown openly in celebration. Together the two lines say that sorrow and celebration both belong to life, and that neither tells the whole story. Wisdom recognizes which of these a moment holds and does not force joy into a time of grief or grief into a time of joy.

Study Notes

Laughter has already come up in Solomon's search. When he tested pleasure, he said of laughter, "Madness!" and asked of mirth, "What does it accomplish?" (2:2). In this poem laughter is neither the goal of life nor something to dismiss; it simply has its time.

Ecclesiastes 12:5 pictures the end of a life with the words "the mourners go about the streets." That image of mourning as something seen in public supports reading the second pair of this verse as outward expression rather than private feeling.

Ecclesiastes 3:5
Ecclesiastes 3:5
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 3:5 A time to cast away stones, And a time to gather stones; A time to embrace, And a time to refrain from embracing;(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 3:5 A time to scatter stones and a time to gather stones.A time to embrace and a time to turn away. (NLT)

Main Idea

The poem does not explain the stones, but casting them away and gathering them clearly picture opposite kinds of work, such as clearing ground and collecting material to build. Embracing and refraining from embracing carry the same rhythm into human closeness: there are times to draw near to someone and times when restraint is right.

Verse Explanation

The poem does not explain the stones, but casting them away and gathering them clearly picture opposite kinds of work, such as clearing ground and collecting material to build. Embracing and refraining from embracing carry the same rhythm into human closeness: there are times to draw near to someone and times when restraint is right.

A time to cast away stones, And a time to gather stones. This is the most puzzling pair in the poem, because Solomon does not say what the stones are for. What is clear is that throwing stones away and gathering them are opposite kinds of work. One natural picture is clearing stones off a piece of ground at one time and collecting stones as building material at another. Because the verse itself does not say, any such picture is an example rather than a settled meaning. The main point is not in doubt: scattering and collecting each have their season.

A time to embrace, And a time to refrain from embracing. The second pair carries the same rhythm into human closeness. An embrace shows affection, welcome, or comfort, and there are times to draw near to someone in that way. There are also times when restraint is right, when holding back respects another person or suits the situation better than closeness would. The poem does not set embracing against rejecting. It sets embracing beside refraining, the same act held back for a time.

Study Notes

In the NKJV this is the only pair in verses 2–8 whose second half negates the first instead of naming a different action: "to embrace" and "to refrain from embracing." The NLT's "a time to turn away" uses a separate verb, which can sound more like rejection than the NKJV's simple holding back.

Ecclesiastes 3:6
Ecclesiastes 3:6
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 3:6 A time to gain, And a time to lose; A time to keep, And a time to throw away;(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 3:6 A time to search and a time to quit searching.A time to keep and a time to throw away. (NLT)

Main Idea

Possessions also come and go in their seasons, with a time to gain and a time to lose, a time to keep and a time to throw away. Coming after the king's great gathering of wealth in chapter 2, the verse admits that losing and letting go are part of life too, so wisdom includes knowing when to release what one holds.

Verse Explanation

Possessions also come and go in their seasons, with a time to gain and a time to lose, a time to keep and a time to throw away. Coming after the king's great gathering of wealth in chapter 2, the verse admits that losing and letting go are part of life too, so wisdom includes knowing when to release what one holds.

A time to gain, And a time to lose. Possessions come and go in their seasons too. Some stretches of life bring increase, and others bring losses that no amount of effort can prevent. The poem does not treat loss as proof of poor skill. It names losing as one of life's appointed times, set beside gaining.

A time to keep, And a time to throw away. The second pair moves from what happens to a person to what a person decides. Keeping holds on to something because it is valuable or needed; throwing away lets it go on purpose. In chapter 2 the king "gathered for myself silver and gold and the special treasures of kings" (2:8). After that, this verse admits that gathering is not the whole of wisdom. Knowing when to let go of what one holds is part of living within times no one controls.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the first pair "a time to search and a time to quit searching," while the NKJV reads "a time to gain, And a time to lose." The NLT stresses the effort of seeking and the decision to stop. The NKJV stresses the outcome of gaining or losing. Both readings set pursuit beside letting go, and the second pair, "to keep" and "to throw away," is the same in both translations.

Main Idea

Tearing and sewing set damage beside repair. The second pair turns to speech: words have their right moments, and so does silence, so knowing when to speak and when to stay quiet is part of the discernment the poem calls for.

Verse Explanation

Tearing and sewing set damage beside repair. The second pair turns to speech: words have their right moments, and so does silence, so knowing when to speak and when to stay quiet is part of the discernment the poem calls for.

A time to tear, And a time to sew. Tearing and sewing set damage beside repair, using the everyday picture of cloth. A garment can be torn, and it can be stitched back together. The verse does not say what causes the tearing, but the pair makes its point plainly: there are times when things come apart and times for patient mending.

A time to keep silence, And a time to speak. The second pair turns to speech. Words have their right moments, and so does silence. Silence comes first in this pair. Wise speech means more than knowing what to say; it includes knowing whether a moment calls for saying anything at all.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the first pair "a time to tear and a time to mend." "Mend" brings out the sense of repair in the NKJV's "sew."

Ecclesiastes comes back to speech in chapter 5: "Do not be rash with your mouth" and "Therefore let your words be few" (5:2). That later counsel shows the book values restraint in speech. It supports reading the silence in this verse as well-timed restraint, not mere passivity.

Ecclesiastes 3:8
Ecclesiastes 3:8
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 3:8 A time to love, And a time to hate; A time of war, And a time of peace.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 3:8 A time to love and a time to hate.A time for war and a time for peace. (NLT)

Main Idea

The poem closes with the strongest human attachments and hostilities, love and hate between people and war and peace between nations, and it ends on "a time of peace." These pairs describe what actually happens in human life without approving every hatred or every war, and they complete the picture of a life whose times no one can control.

Verse Explanation

The poem closes with the strongest human attachments and hostilities, love and hate between people and war and peace between nations, and it ends on "a time of peace." These pairs describe what actually happens in human life without approving every hatred or every war, and they complete the picture of a life whose times no one can control.

A time to love, And a time to hate. The last verse of the poem reaches the strongest attachments and hostilities between people. Love draws people together, and hatred sets them against one another. The poem records that both happen in human life. It does not approve every hatred, and a person meeting hatred, in others or in themselves, still has to weigh it with discernment.

A time of war, And a time of peace. The final pair widens from individuals to nations. Few people can start a war or stop one, which makes war and peace a fitting close to a poem about times beyond human control. Naming war as a time describes what happens in history; it does not declare every war just. The poem then ends on "a time of peace." After fourteen pairs that swing between building and tearing down, gaining and losing, the final word is peace.

Taken together, verses 2–8 cover the body, the field, building, grief and joy, closeness, possessions, speech, and the life of nations. No area of life is left outside the seasons. The poem thus prepares for Solomon's question in verse 9 about what lasting profit labor can bring.

Study Notes

In the NKJV this last pair changes form. Every earlier pair in verses 2–8 uses "a time to" followed by an action. Here the wording becomes "a time of war" and "a time of peace," which names conditions instead of actions.

The four terms in this verse run love, hate, war, peace. The verse opens and closes with a favorable term and puts the two hostile terms together in the middle, so the last word of the poem is peace.

Ecclesiastes 3:9–15 — God’s Enduring Work and Eternity in the Heart

NKJV

Ecclesiastes 3:9 What profit has the worker from that in which he labors?(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 3:9 What do people really get for all their hard work? (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon returns to the question that opened the book (1:3): if every activity has its set time and then passes, what lasting gain does the worker have from all his labor? The question presses the limit of human effort, and the following verses answer it by turning to God's work.

Verse Explanation

Solomon returns to the question that opened the book (1:3): if every activity has its set time and then passes, what lasting gain does the worker have from all his labor? The question presses the limit of human effort, and the following verses answer it by turning to God's work.

What profit has the worker asks again the question that opened the book in 1:3. Profit is the lasting gain left in a person's hand once the work is done, something that remains after the effort is spent. Coming straight after the poem of times, the question gains new force. If planting gives way to uprooting and building gives way to breaking down, what does the worker actually keep when each season has passed?

from that in which he labors? The question centers on labor itself, the daily effort that fills a human life. Solomon has already measured his own great works this way and reported, "There was no profit under the sun" (2:11). This time he does not answer by examining the worker. The verses that follow turn to what God has assigned, made, given, and done, and the chapter's answer to the question comes from there.

Study Notes

Verse 9 repeats the question of 1:3 in a shorter form. There the NKJV reads, "What profit has a man from all his labor In which he toils under the sun?" Here the question speaks of "the worker" and leaves out the words "under the sun," which do not appear again in this chapter until Solomon looks at the courts in verse 16.

Main Idea

Solomon calls the labor and changing seasons that fill human life a "God-given task." The verse echoes the "burdensome task" of 1:13, but here the emphasis falls on God as the One who assigns it, which points the question of profit toward Him.

Verse Explanation

Solomon calls the labor and changing seasons that fill human life a "God-given task." The verse echoes the "burdensome task" of 1:13, but here the emphasis falls on God as the One who assigns it, which points the question of profit toward Him.

I have seen introduces Solomon's report as an observer. He speaks as someone who has watched human work closely, including his own, and he now names what he has seen.

the God-given task refers to the labor and changing seasons just described, the whole range of activity that verses 1–8 set out. In 1:13 Solomon called this a "burdensome task God has given to the sons of man," and the weight of it has not disappeared. What this verse brings forward is the Giver. The work that fills human days has been assigned by God, so the question of profit in verse 9 cannot be settled by looking at the worker alone; it has to be taken to Him.

with which the sons of men are to be occupied describes people in general, since "the sons of men" simply means human beings. God's assignment keeps them busy throughout their lives. Being busy, though, is not the same as gaining something that lasts, and Solomon takes up that gap in the next verse by looking at what God Himself has made.

Study Notes

The NLT reads, "I have seen the burden God has placed on us all." Its word "burden" stays close to the "burdensome task" of 1:13, while the NKJV's "God-given task" highlights where the task comes from. Both translations agree that God is the One who assigns it.

Ecclesiastes 3:11
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 3:11 He has made everything beautiful in its time. Also He has put eternity in their hearts, except that no one can find out the work that God does from beginning to end.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 3:11 Yet God has made everything beautiful for its own time. He has planted eternity in the human heart, but even so, people cannot see the whole scope of God’s work from beginning to end. (NLT)

Main Idea

God has made everything beautiful in its time, and He has put eternity in human hearts. That eternity is a longing that reaches beyond anything earthly achievement can satisfy, because people were made for life with Him. Yet the same verse sets a firm limit: no one can find out the work God does from beginning to end, so the longing and the limit stand side by side.

Verse Explanation

God has made everything beautiful in its time, and He has put eternity in human hearts. That eternity is a longing that reaches beyond anything earthly achievement can satisfy, because people were made for life with Him. Yet the same verse sets a firm limit: no one can find out the work God does from beginning to end, so the longing and the limit stand side by side.

He has made everything beautiful in its time. "He" is God, the Giver named in verse 10. "Everything" reaches back over the seasons of verses 2–8, including the hard ones: dying, uprooting, weeping, mourning, and losing. The beauty Solomon speaks of belongs to each thing "in its time," in its place within God's ordering. The verse does not call grief pleasant or make evil good. It says that the times of life are not random, because God has fitted each one into His work, even when people cannot see how.

Also He has put eternity in their hearts. "Their" points back to "the sons of men" in verse 10. God has placed in human hearts a longing for eternity, a longing that reaches beyond anything earthly achievement can satisfy, because people were made for life with Him. This explains why the worker of verse 9 is never content with gains that pass with the seasons: the heart was made to want what lasts. The wider Bible shows where that longing is fulfilled, for Jesus describes eternal life as knowing the only true God and Jesus Christ whom He sent (John 17:3).

except that no one can find out the work that God does from beginning to end. In the same sentence Solomon sets a firm limit. People long for eternity, yet they cannot trace God's work from its beginning to its end. They live through one season at a time and never see the whole design. The longing gives no one the ability to explain all that God is doing, and that inability does not cancel the longing. Both stand together, and the rest of the section shows how to live within them: by receiving God's gifts with gladness and standing in reverence before Him.

Study Notes

Translations and interpreters differ over the word "eternity" in this verse. Some understand it as an awareness of the whole span of time, past and future, rather than a longing for what is eternal. The NKJV and the NLT both keep the word "eternity"; the NLT reads, "He has planted eternity in the human heart." On each of these readings, what God has placed in the heart stands beside the limit stated in the same verse.

The words "in its time" pick up the word "time" that runs through every pair of the poem in verses 2–8. The same word returns in verse 17, "there is a time there for every purpose and for every work," where Solomon ties God's judgment to the times He has appointed.

The limit in this verse returns in similar words in 8:17: "a man cannot find out the work that is done under the sun." There Solomon adds that even when a wise man "attempts to know it, he will not be able to find it."

than to rejoice. The first part of that course is gladness. Solomon once tested pleasure as a way to find meaning and called it vanity (2:1). Gladness here is no longer an experiment for manufacturing meaning. It is sound counsel for people living within the times God orders.

and to do good in their lives. Solomon joins gladness to right conduct. The joy he commends walks together with doing what is good, so it never becomes an excuse for self-indulgence. and enjoy the good of all his labor uses the same word, "labor," that appeared in the question of verse 9. The worker does find something good in his work after all. It is not a stored-up profit that outlasts death. It is good that can be enjoyed in the work itself, day by day.

it is the gift of God. Here the answer to verse 9 begins. The ability to enjoy food, drink, and work is received from God rather than produced by human effort. In the NKJV, verses 12 and 13 form a single sentence. Verse 12 ends with a comma, and verse 13 continues with "and also." The sentence holds together rejoicing, doing good, eating, drinking, and enjoying one's labor before it closes with "it is the gift of God."

Solomon uses the same expression again in 5:19, where God gives a man riches and "power to eat of it, to receive his heritage and rejoice in his labor—this is the gift of God." The close of the royal search had already said that enjoying food, drink, and labor "was from the hand of God" (2:24).

Ecclesiastes 3:14
NKJV

Main Idea

Solomon knows that whatever God does lasts forever and cannot be added to or reduced, while human work passes with its seasons. God acts this way "that men should fear before Him," so His enduring work calls for reverence, a right view of God that leads to worship, trust, and obedience.

Verse Explanation

Solomon knows that whatever God does lasts forever and cannot be added to or reduced, while human work passes with its seasons. God acts this way "that men should fear before Him," so His enduring work calls for reverence, a right view of God that leads to worship, trust, and obedience.

I know that whatever God does. These words introduce the second thing Solomon is sure of. Verse 12 spoke about what is best for people. This verse speaks about God's own work, set against the labor of the worker in verse 9.

It shall be forever. What God does lasts. Human labor passes with its seasons, as the poem showed: what is built is later broken down, and what is planted is later uprooted. The lasting gain the worker could not secure is found in God's work, which time does not wear away.

Nothing can be added to it, And nothing taken from it. God's work is complete. No one can improve it by adding to it, and no one can reduce it by taking something away. Solomon is describing God's work, not giving permission for passivity. In this same chapter he still commends doing good (verse 12), and he is troubled by injustice in the courts (verse 16).

God does it, that men should fear before Him. Solomon names God's purpose. His enduring work is meant to bring people to reverence. To fear God is to hold a right view of Him, and that right view shows itself in worship, trust, and obedience. People who cannot find out God's work from beginning to end can still stand before Him rightly, and that reverence supplies what the worker's question about profit was missing.

Study Notes

The NLT reads, "whatever God does is final," and "God's purpose is that people should fear him." "Final" brings out the completeness of God's work, while the NKJV's "It shall be forever" stresses how long it lasts. The NLT's "God's purpose" makes plain that the last line states why God acts as He does.

The expression "fear before Him" returns in 8:12–13. There Solomon says "it will be well with those who fear God, who fear before Him," and that it will not be well with the wicked "because he does not fear before God."

Ecclesiastes 3:16
Ecclesiastes 3:16
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 3:16 Moreover I saw under the sun: In the place of judgment, Wickedness was there; And in the place of righteousness, Iniquity was there.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 3:16 I also noticed that under the sun there is evil in the courtroom. Yes, even the courts of law are corrupt! (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon turns to a troubling sight "under the sun": wickedness in the very place where judgment should be given, and iniquity where righteousness should rule. The courts meant to set wrongs right had themselves become a source of wrong, and this sight leads to the two responses that follow.

Verse Explanation

Solomon turns to a troubling sight "under the sun": wickedness in the very place where judgment should be given, and iniquity where righteousness should rule. The courts meant to set wrongs right had themselves become a source of wrong, and this sight leads to the two responses that follow.

Moreover I saw under the sun. With "Moreover," Solomon adds another observation to the ones he has already made in this chapter. He has just said that God requires an account of what is past (verse 15). Now he looks at the place on earth where human wrongs were supposed to be called to account. The phrase "under the sun" marks this as something he watched happen in ordinary human life, in the world as people can observe it. What he saw there does not fit easily with the ordered times of verses 1–8.

In the place of judgment, Wickedness was there. The place of judgment is where cases were heard and verdicts given. It was the court a wronged person turned to for help. Solomon found wickedness sitting in that very place. The people responsible for condemning wrong were doing wrong themselves. The verse does not name the particular abuses, but a court becomes wicked when its decisions are bought, bent by favoritism, or turned against the innocent.

And in the place of righteousness, Iniquity was there. The second line repeats the first in different words. The place of righteousness is most naturally the same setting, named for its purpose: it existed to establish what is right and to give each party a just outcome. Iniquity, meaning wrongdoing or moral crookedness, had taken over that place instead. The repeated "was there" lands with the weight of an eyewitness report. This is worse than ordinary crime. When the courts themselves are corrupt, the people they wrong have nowhere left to appeal. Solomon returns to this grief when he sees the oppressed who "have no comforter" (4:1) and "the violent perversion of justice and righteousness in a province" (5:8). Here the sight leads into the two responses of verses 17 and 18.

Study Notes

The NLT turns the verse into a statement about the legal system: "there is evil in the courtroom. Yes, even the courts of law are corrupt!" The NKJV keeps two parallel lines, "the place of judgment" and "the place of righteousness," each followed by "was there." Both translations understand the scene as the courts. The NKJV keeps the repetition that gives the verse its weight.

Solomon's first response is confidence that "God shall judge the righteous and the wicked." Because God has appointed a time "for every purpose and for every work," the injustice of human courts will not have the last word, although the verse does not say when or how His judgment will come.

I said in my heart. Solomon does not answer the corrupt courts with a public speech. As he does throughout the book, he reasons with himself and weighs what he has seen. This is the first of two inward responses to the sight of verse 16. Verse 18 begins with the same words and gives the second.

God shall judge the righteous and the wicked. Where human judges failed, God will not. Solomon names God Himself as the Judge, and he names both groups who stand before Him. Human courts can treat the righteous as guilty and the wicked as innocent. God's judgment will deal with each as they really are. The word "shall" looks ahead: Solomon did not see this judgment in the courtroom he describes, yet he is confident it will come. The verse does not say when or how God will judge. It does not promise that people will see every case set right within their own lifetimes.

For there is a time there for every purpose and for every work. This line gives the reason for Solomon's confidence, and it echoes the opening of the chapter: "A time for every purpose under heaven" (verse 1). The God who appoints the times for being born and dying, planting and uprooting, has also appointed a time for judgment. Solomon adds "every work," so even the deeds done in the corrupt court will be weighed. This fits verse 15, where God requires an account of what is past, and it looks ahead to the book's conclusion: "For God will bring every work into judgment" (12:14). Solomon's confidence here is real teaching that brings God's perspective into the search, not a passing hope he later abandons. Waiting for God's appointed time does not make corrupt courts acceptable. It means their verdicts are not final.

Study Notes

Verses 17 and 18 both begin "I said in my heart," so the single sight of verse 16 receives two distinct responses. The first looks to God's coming judgment, and the second looks at the death people share with animals. Because both responses come from the same sight, some readers treat verses 16–22 as one unit.

The NKJV reads "a time there" without saying what "there" refers to. Because it comes right after the promise that God will judge, "there" most naturally points to that judgment as the place where every purpose and work receives its time. The NLT leaves out the word and renders the thought as "In due season God will judge everyone, both good and bad, for all their deeds," which puts the emphasis on God's timing.

Main Idea

In a second response to the corrupt courts, Solomon reflects that God tests people so they will see that they themselves are like animals. Verse Explanation

In a second response to the corrupt courts, Solomon reflects that God tests people so they will see that they themselves are like animals. The comparison humbles human pride by exposing how fragile and mortal people are; as the next verses show, it concerns the death they share with animals, not the whole of human identity.

I said in my heart. Solomon reports a conclusion he reached in private reflection. He used the same words at the start of verse 17, and both verses answer what he saw in verse 16: wickedness sitting in the place of judgment. His first response looked ahead to God’s judgment. This second response considers what the corrupt courts reveal about human beings themselves.

Concerning the condition of the sons of men. The expression “sons of men” is an ordinary way of saying human beings, and the word “condition” directs attention to what people are rather than to any one person’s deeds. Solomon widens his view from the judges who twisted justice to humanity as a whole, because the people who abuse power and the people who suffer under it share the same human condition.

God tests them. God is the One acting. To test people here is to examine them and bring what is true about them into the open. People who sit in judgment over others can behave as though they stand above ordinary limits, and God’s testing exposes that illusion. The verse does not describe the test itself, but the verses that follow point to what every person shares with animals: breath that stops and a body that returns to dust.

that they may see that they themselves are like animals. The purpose of God’s test is that people would see. He wants them to recognize something about themselves, and the word “themselves” keeps the lesson personal. Human beings are not studying animals from a distance; they are discovering their own likeness to them. The comparison is humbling, and it is meant to be. Yet its scope is limited. Verses 19–20 define the likeness as shared breath, shared death, and a shared return to dust. Solomon is exposing human mortality, not saying that people are nothing more than animals.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the clause “how God proves to people that they are like animals.” Both translations present God as the One who shows people their condition. The NKJV’s “tests them” keeps the idea of examination, while the NLT’s “proves” stresses the result: the matter is demonstrated.

For what happens to the sons of men also happens to animals. The word “For” shows that Solomon is now explaining the likeness he named in verse 18. Whatever else separates people from animals, the events that come upon them do not distinguish between the two.

one thing befalls them: as one dies, so dies the other. The “one thing” is death. Solomon states it plainly and evenly: the person dies, and the animal dies. Rank, learning, and wealth make no difference. The judge who perverted justice in verse 16 is as mortal as the ox in his field.

Surely, they all have one breath. “Surely” marks the statement as something Solomon regards as beyond dispute. People and animals alike live only as long as they breathe. Breath is the sign of life they share, and when breath stops, life ends for both.

man has no advantage over animals. An advantage is something left over that puts one ahead of another. Measured by death alone, a person has no such edge. Whatever a man gains in his lifetime does not change the fact that he dies just as an animal does.

for all is vanity. The book’s key word appears here for the only time in this chapter. The Hebrew word translated “vanity” pictures a breath or vapor, something that cannot be held. Within the earthly horizon Solomon is examining, where the grave has the final word, life looks meaningless. The verdict is honest about how death looks from this side of the grave, and the next two verses press the same observation until Solomon reaches the edge of what he can see.

Study Notes

The word “advantage” belongs with the book’s question about “profit” (1:3; 3:9). Both words ask what lasting gain a person has. Here Solomon answers that question within the narrow terms of death alone.

The NLT translates the closing words as “How meaningless!” This brings out the sense of the NKJV’s “for all is vanity.” It also renders “they all have one breath” as “both breathe and both must die,” which turns the shared breath into a statement about the shared ending.

Who knows the spirit of the sons of men, which goes upward. Having followed the body to the dust, Solomon turns to the spirit, the life within a person that the body alone cannot account for. and the spirit of the animal, which goes down to the earth? The question sets two directions side by side: upward for people and downward for animals. Verse Explanation

Solomon closes the chapter by commending joy in one's own work, "for that is his heritage," the portion given to him for this life. Since no one can show a person what will happen after him, he should receive and enjoy that portion now instead of grasping at a future he cannot see.

So I perceived that nothing is better than. The word “So” draws a conclusion from everything in verses 18–21. Solomon has faced shared death and the limit of what can be seen, and his response is not despair. The words “nothing is better than” repeat the language of verse 12, where he said that nothing is better for people “than to rejoice, and to do good in their lives.” At its close the chapter returns to the counsel it gave in the middle.

that a man should rejoice in his own works. The gladness Solomon commends is found in a person’s own work, the labor of his own hands in the life he actually has. He does not tell the reader to rejoice in someone else’s achievements or in what might come later. Earlier in the chapter he called eating, drinking, and enjoying the good of one’s labor “the gift of God” (verse 13), and this closing counsel belongs with that gift. It is real teaching, not one more bleak conclusion from the search.

for that is his heritage. A heritage is a portion assigned to someone, the share that belongs to him. Joy in his work is the portion given to a person for this life. He did not manufacture that joy, and he cannot carry it beyond the grave, but he can receive it and enjoy it while he lives.

For who can bring him to see what will happen after him? The expected answer is no one. Just as no one can find out God’s work from beginning to end (verse 11), no one can show a person what will follow his life. That limit is the reason for the counsel. Since the future lies beyond his sight, he should receive the portion in front of him now instead of grasping at what he cannot see.

Study Notes

Ecclesiastes uses “heritage” in two directions. In 2:21 a man “must leave his heritage to a man who has not labored for it,” so the heritage is what a worker loses at death. Here, as in 5:18–19, the heritage is the portion a person receives and enjoys while he is alive.

The closing question returns in similar form later in the book: “Who can tell a man what will happen after him under the sun?” (6:12) and “Who can tell him what will be after him?” (10:14).

The NLT renders the question as “what happens after we die,” while the NKJV keeps the more open “what will happen after him.” Within Ecclesiastes, that wording can refer to events on earth after a person is gone, such as Solomon’s worry about the successor who will rule over his labor (2:18–19). The phrase “under the sun” in 6:12 points the same way.
