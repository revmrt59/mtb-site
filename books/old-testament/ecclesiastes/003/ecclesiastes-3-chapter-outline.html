Ecclesiastes 3 Chapter Outline

Chapter Focus

After the royal search of chapters 1–2, Ecclesiastes 3 places the whole range of human experience inside the times God appoints. It moves from a poem of paired seasons to God’s enduring work and the eternity He has put in human hearts, then faces corrupt justice and the death people share with animals, and closes by commending joy in one’s work as a God-given heritage.

Chapter Sections

1. Ecclesiastes 3:1–8 — A Season for Every Purpose Under Heaven

The poem opens with “To everything there is a season, A time for every purpose under heaven” (verse 1) and then sets out fourteen pairs of opposites, from being born and dying to war and peace. Together the pairs show how much of life’s changing circumstances lie in God’s ordering rather than in human control, though being named in the poem does not make every killing, hatred, or war right.

2. Ecclesiastes 3:9–15 — God’s Enduring Work and Eternity in the Heart

Solomon repeats the book’s question, “What profit has the worker from that in which he labors?” (verse 9), and answers it by turning to God’s work: He has made everything beautiful in its time and “put eternity in their hearts,” even though “no one can find out the work that God does from beginning to end” (verse 11). Solomon then commends rejoicing, doing good, and enjoying one’s labor as “the gift of God” (verse 13), and he declares that whatever God does lasts forever, “that men should fear before Him” (verse 14), because “God requires an account of what is past” (verse 15).

3. Ecclesiastes 3:16–17 — Wickedness in the Place of Judgment, and God Who Will Judge

Solomon sees that “In the place of judgment, Wickedness was there” (verse 16). He answers that sight with confidence that “God shall judge the righteous and the wicked,” because there is a time for every purpose and every work (verse 17).

4. Ecclesiastes 3:18–22 — Humans and Animals Return to Dust

Solomon reflects that God tests people so they will see their mortality: humans and animals share one breath and one end, for “all are from the dust, and all return to dust” (verse 20), and observation alone leaves unanswered the question, “Who knows the spirit of the sons of men, which goes upward, and the spirit of the animal, which goes down to the earth?” (verse 21). The chapter closes by commending joy in one’s work, “for that is his heritage,” since no one can see what will happen after him (verse 22).
