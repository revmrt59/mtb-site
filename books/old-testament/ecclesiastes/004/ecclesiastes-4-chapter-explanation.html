NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:1 Again, I observed all the oppression that takes place under the sun. I saw the tears of the oppressed, with no one to comfort them. The oppressors have great power, and their victims are helpless. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon begins a fresh round of observation with “all the oppression that is done under the sun,” the injustice he can see in earthly life, and he looks steadily at the tears of its victims. He says twice that “they have no comforter,” because “On the side of their oppressors there is power,” so the oppressed are left alone with both their grief and a strength they cannot resist.

Verse Explanation

Solomon begins a fresh round of observation with “all the oppression that is done under the sun,” the injustice he can see in earthly life, and he looks steadily at the tears of its victims. He says twice that “they have no comforter,” because “On the side of their oppressors there is power,” so the oppressed are left alone with both their grief and a strength they cannot resist.

Then I returned and considered marks a fresh start. Chapter 3 closed with Solomon commending joy in one’s own work as a man’s heritage (3:22). Now he turns back to look again, and this time he looks at the way people treat one another. “Considered” describes deliberate, careful looking, not a passing glance. Solomon is about to describe something he has studied closely.

all the oppression that is done under the sun sets the scope of what he saw. He is not describing one cruel ruler or one bad season. He surveys oppression wherever it happens in earthly life. “Under the sun” marks his examination of life as it appears within earthly limits, measured by what people can see and achieve before the grave. Inside that horizon, nothing he observes puts the oppression right. He has already seen wickedness sitting “in the place of judgment” (3:16), and now he looks at the people who suffer under that kind of corrupted power.

And look! The tears of the oppressed calls the reader to see what Solomon saw. He does not begin with the oppressors’ schemes or with a theory about injustice. He fixes his eyes on tears. The suffering is personal and visible, written on the faces of real people.

But they have no comforter is the first of two identical lines. A comforter is someone who comes alongside a sufferer to console and support them. The victims weep, and no one comes to them.

On the side of their oppressors there is power explains why no help comes. The strength to enforce, to punish, and to silence is all on one side. Anyone who might console the victims would have to face that power too. Then Solomon repeats, But they have no comforter. Because the same words stand on either side of the line about power, the verse closes in on the oppressed. They have grief and no comfort, power against them and no comfort. Solomon does not hurry past the scene with a quick answer. He has elsewhere affirmed that “God shall judge the righteous and the wicked” (3:17), but here he lets the reader feel what injustice looks like when no earthly helper steps in.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the first line “with no one to comfort them” and replaces the second with “their victims are helpless.” The NKJV keeps the two identical lines, so its reader can see how the statement about the oppressors’ power is framed by the absence of comfort on either side.

The opening words “Then I returned” appear again at 4:7, where Solomon begins his portrait of the solitary worker. The repeated words mark the start of a new observation, which is why the chapter’s sections begin at verses 1 and 7.

Oppression comes up more than once in the book. Solomon has already seen wickedness “in the place of judgment” (3:16), and he returns to “the oppression of the poor” in 5:8.

Main Idea

Weighed down by the oppression he has witnessed, Solomon declares that the dead are better off than the living, who must still endure it. Verse Explanation

Weighed down by the oppression he has witnessed, Solomon declares that the dead are better off than the living, who must still endure it. This is an anguished comparison made from within the earthly limits of his search. It is not God’s final verdict on the worth of human life, and it is not advice to seek death.

Therefore I praised the dead who were already dead follows directly from what Solomon has just described. “Therefore” tells the reader that the tears and the unanswered power of verse 1 produced this verdict. To praise the dead here means to commend them, to count them fortunate. The dead “who were already dead” are people whose lives are finished. Oppression can no longer reach them, and they weep no more tears under it.

More than the living who are still alive completes the comparison. The word “still” carries the weight. The living are still exposed to the oppressor’s power and still without a comforter. Solomon is measuring how heavy present suffering is, not how valuable a person is.

Solomon speaks this verdict from inside the earthly horizon of his search. When life is measured only by what happens before the grave, a life spent under oppression can look worse than no further life at all. The verse honestly records how heavy that grief is. It is not God’s final judgment on the value of human life, and it gives no counsel to seek death. Nor does it treat the oppressed as worthless. Their suffering is the very thing that grieves Solomon. Scripture lets this lament stand without rushing it toward a tidy answer.

Study Notes

The NLT reads “So I concluded that the dead are better off than the living,” which presents Solomon’s statement as a reasoned conclusion. The NKJV’s “I praised” keeps the language of commendation, so Solomon speaks of the dead as fortunate rather than simply drawing an inference about them.

Ecclesiastes 4:3
Ecclesiastes 4:3
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 4:3 Yet, better than both is he who has never existed, Who has not seen the evil work that is done under the sun.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:3 But most fortunate of all are those who are not yet born. For they have not seen all the evil that is done under the sun. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon goes further still and counts the one “who has never existed” better off than both the living and the dead, because that person has never had to see “the evil work that is done under the sun.” The bitterness of this comparison shows how heavily injustice weighs when life is judged only by what happens on earth.

Verse Explanation

Solomon goes further still and counts the one “who has never existed” better off than both the living and the dead, because that person has never had to see “the evil work that is done under the sun.” The bitterness of this comparison shows how heavily injustice weighs when life is judged only by what happens on earth.

Yet, better than both pushes the comparison one step further. “Both” refers to the two groups of verse 2, the living who still suffer and the dead who no longer do.

is he who has never existed names a third figure, one who never came into being at all. Solomon is not pointing to an actual person. He imagines someone who never lived in order to show how heavily the scene of verse 1 presses on him.

Who has not seen the evil work that is done under the sun gives his reason. The only advantage of the one who never existed is that he has never had to watch it. “The evil work” points back to the oppression of verse 1: the tears, the power on the oppressors’ side, and the missing comforter. Solomon calls what he saw evil. His comparison is not a cold calculation about whether life is worth living. It is a moral protest against what people do to one another.

The bitterness of the comparison shows how heavily injustice weighs when life is judged only by what can be seen on earth. Ecclesiastes does not leave the matter there. The book ends by declaring that “God will bring every work into judgment, Including every secret thing, Whether good or evil” (12:14). The oppression Solomon watched remains accountable before God, including what no earthly court ever corrected. The power on the oppressors’ side does not have the last word.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the third figure “those who are not yet born,” which could be read as people who will be born later. The NKJV’s “he who has never existed” points instead to someone who never comes into being at all. That reading fits the comparison with both the living and the dead.

The words “that is done under the sun” close verse 3 just as they closed the first clause of verse 1, “all the oppression that is done under the sun.” The repeated phrase brackets the section and ties “the evil work” of verse 3 to the oppression Solomon first described.

Verse 3 contains the first of four “better” comparisons in the chapter. The others are “Better a handful with quietness” (4:6), “Two are better than one” (4:9), and “Better a poor and wise youth” (4:13). This first comparison is the darkest; the later ones commend contentment, companionship, and teachable wisdom.

A similar comparison appears in 6:3. There Solomon says of a man whose soul is not satisfied with goodness, “I say that a stillborn child is better than he.” Both passages weigh a life by what it suffers or lacks on earth.

Ecclesiastes 4:4–6 — Envy, Idleness, and the Quiet Handful

Ecclesiastes 4:4
Ecclesiastes 4:4
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 4:4 Again, I saw that for all toil and every skillful work a man is envied by his neighbor. This also is vanity and grasping for the wind.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:4 Then I observed that most people are motivated to success because they envy their neighbors. But this, too, is meaningless — like chasing the wind. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon turns from the victims of oppression to the world of work. He observes that toil and skillful work are often bound up with rivalry, so that a man’s achievement stirs up his neighbor’s envy. He calls work driven by that rivalry “vanity and grasping for the wind,” meaningless effort that tries to hold onto what cannot be held.

Verse Explanation

Solomon turns from the victims of oppression to the world of work. He observes that toil and skillful work are often bound up with rivalry, so that a man’s achievement stirs up his neighbor’s envy. He calls work driven by that rivalry “vanity and grasping for the wind,” meaningless effort that tries to hold onto what cannot be held.

Again, I saw carries on the fresh round of observation that began with “Then I returned and considered” in verse 1. Solomon has looked at the tears of the oppressed, who are crushed by a power stronger than their own. Now he turns to the world of work, where people are driven by one another. The word “Again” marks this as another scene in the same survey of life among other people, seen from the same earthly horizon.

for all toil and every skillful work covers the whole range of human labor, from hard physical toil to work that takes real skill and craftsmanship. Solomon is not criticizing skill itself. Earlier he speaks with sympathy of a man whose labor is done with wisdom, knowledge, and skill (2:21). These sweeping words show how often rivalry runs through people’s work. They do not mean that every achievement grows out of envy.

a man is envied by his neighbor names what Solomon finds tangled up with that work: rivalry. When one person succeeds, the person nearby feels it as a loss and resents it. That resentment pushes people to work harder to keep up or get ahead. The “neighbor” is not a distant enemy but someone close at hand, and that closeness is what makes envy so corrosive. It turns the people who could share life with each other into competitors. In Solomon’s picture, achievement stirs up envy and envy drives more achievement, so work becomes a contest that never ends.

This also is vanity and grasping for the wind gives Solomon’s verdict. “Vanity” translates a Hebrew word whose basic image is a breath or vapor. Here it means that work driven by rivalry is meaningless: whatever it gains slips away and cannot satisfy. “Grasping for the wind” pictures trying to hold what cannot be held. Work of this kind never reaches its goal, because there is always another neighbor to outdo. The word “also” connects this verdict to earlier ones in Solomon’s search, especially his conclusion about his own great works: “all was vanity and grasping for the wind” (2:11).

Study Notes

The NKJV and NLT look at envy from different sides. In the NKJV, a man “is envied by his neighbor,” so the envy is the neighbor’s response to someone else’s success. In the NLT, “most people are motivated to success because they envy their neighbors,” so the envy is the worker’s own motive. How far the envy extends in this verse is debated. Either way, Solomon ties achievement to rivalry between neighbors.

The phrase “grasping for the wind” appears in nine verses of Ecclesiastes, all between 1:14 and 6:9 (1:14, 17; 2:11, 17, 26; 4:4, 6, 16; 6:9). Three of them are in this chapter. In verses 4 and 6 the phrase frames the observations about work, and in verse 16 it closes the chapter’s verdict on fading acclaim.

Ecclesiastes 4:5
Ecclesiastes 4:5
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 4:5 The fool folds his hands And consumes his own flesh.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:5 “Fools fold their idle hands,leading them to ruin.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Beside the restless competitor, Solomon sets the opposite error: the fool who refuses to work at all. By folding his hands, he “consumes his own flesh,” a vivid picture of idleness that slowly destroys the idle person himself.

Verse Explanation

Beside the restless competitor, Solomon sets the opposite error: the fool who refuses to work at all. By folding his hands, he “consumes his own flesh,” a vivid picture of idleness that slowly destroys the idle person himself.

The fool folds his hands places a second figure beside the restless competitor of verse 4. Folded hands are hands that refuse to work. Solomon calls this man a “fool,” a familiar type in Israel’s wisdom writing: someone who lacks the skill for living well. Envy drives one person into endless labor, while laziness keeps another out of labor altogether. Solomon does not let his readers escape the first error by falling into the second.

And consumes his own flesh shows where idleness leads. The man produces nothing, so he is left with nothing to live on but himself. Whether the picture is hunger or wider ruin, his refusal to work slowly destroys him. Solomon makes the image vivid on purpose. Envy may make work meaningless, but idleness is no safe alternative. The man who pulls back from work does not find rest; he feeds on his own life. By placing this saying right after verse 4, Solomon affirms honest labor even as he exposes the rivalry that can corrupt it.

Study Notes

Ecclesiastes uses a similar picture later: “Because of laziness the building decays, And through idleness of hands the house leaks” (10:18). In both sayings, idle hands lead to steady loss that the idle person brings on himself.

The NLT puts this verse in quotation marks, presenting it as a familiar saying. It also replaces the image of consuming one’s own flesh with the plain phrase “leading them to ruin.” The NKJV has no quotation marks here and keeps the more vivid picture.

Ecclesiastes 4:6
Ecclesiastes 4:6
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 4:6 Better a handful with quietness Than both hands full, together with toil and grasping for the wind.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:6 And yet, “Better to have one handful with quietnessthan two handfuls with hard workand chasing the wind.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Between envious striving and destructive idleness, Solomon commends a better measure: a modest “handful with quietness” is worth more than “both hands full” gained through endless toil and grasping. The saying values a contented and peaceful life over restless accumulation, but it does not commend the laziness of verse 5.

Verse Explanation

Between envious striving and destructive idleness, Solomon commends a better measure: a modest “handful with quietness” is worth more than “both hands full” gained through endless toil and grasping. The saying values a contented and peaceful life over restless accumulation, but it does not commend the laziness of verse 5.

Better a handful with quietness is Solomon’s answer to the two errors he has just described. A “handful” is a modest amount, as much as one hand can hold. “Quietness” is rest and peace of heart, the freedom from restless striving that the competitor of verse 4 never has. A handful is still something gathered, so these are not the folded hands of verse 5. The picture is of a person who works, receives a modest portion, and is at peace with it. Solomon calls that life “better” because peace with a modest portion is worth more than abundance that costs a person all rest.

Than both hands full pictures the opposite measure: both hands occupied, each holding as much as it can carry. By itself, having more might sound like the better outcome. Solomon’s point lies in what comes along with it.

together with toil and grasping for the wind names that hidden cost. The full hands come with exhausting labor and with the same futility Solomon has just pronounced on envious work in verse 4. Grasping for the wind leaves nothing in the hand that can truly be kept. The saying does not condemn possessions as such, and it does not praise poverty. It weighs two ways of living and finds contentment worth more than restless accumulation. The quiet handful fits Solomon’s earlier counsel that a person should enjoy the good of his labor, which he calls “the gift of God” (3:13). A modest portion received with peace is closer to that gift than full hands that never rest.

Study Notes

Hands appear throughout verses 5 and 6: the fool’s folded hands, the quiet “handful,” and “both hands full.” The repeated image links the three portraits, so verse 6 reads as a direct reply to hands that do nothing and hands that grasp for everything.

Verse 6 is one of several comparisons in this chapter built on the word “better.” The never-born are “better than both” the living and the dead (4:3), “Two are better than one” (4:9), and Solomon says, “Better a poor and wise youth Than an old and foolish king” (4:13). Through this pattern, Solomon weighs one way of life against another across the chapter.

The NLT reads “one handful” and “two handfuls,” which makes the contrast in number explicit. It also introduces the verse with “And yet” and quotation marks, presenting it as a saying that replies to verse 5.

Solomon makes a similar comparison in the next chapter: “The sleep of a laboring man is sweet, Whether he eats little or much; But the abundance of the rich will not permit him to sleep” (5:12). In both places, rest belongs to the worker with a modest portion, while abundance brings restlessness.

Ecclesiastes 4:7–8 — The Solitary Worker Whose Eye Is Never Satisfied

Ecclesiastes 4:7
Ecclesiastes 4:7
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 4:7 Then I returned, and I saw vanity under the sun:(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:7 I observed yet another example of something meaningless under the sun. (NLT)

Main Idea

With the same words that opened verse 1, “Then I returned,” Solomon begins another observation and announces that he has seen something else meaningless in earthly life. The verse introduces the portrait of the solitary worker that follows in verse 8.

Verse Explanation

With the same words that opened verse 1, “Then I returned,” Solomon begins another observation and announces that he has seen something else meaningless in earthly life. The verse introduces the portrait of the solitary worker that follows in verse 8.

Then I returned, Solomon repeats the words that opened the chapter in verse 1, “Then I returned and considered.” The phrase signals a fresh look, not a change of place. He has just set the envious competitor, the idle fool, and the quiet handful side by side in verses 4–6, and now he turns his attention to one more scene from the world of work.

and I saw vanity under the sun: What Solomon sees, he calls “vanity,” the word that runs through the whole book. The Hebrew word behind it pictures a breath or vapor, something that cannot be grasped or kept, and Solomon uses it to show that life pursued without God is meaningless. “Under the sun” places the scene within the earthly limits of his search, where life is measured only by what a person can see and gain before the grave. Solomon names his verdict before he describes the case, so the reader comes to the man of verse 8 already knowing how Solomon will judge him. The colon that ends the verse in the NKJV points straight ahead to that portrait.

Study Notes

The repeated “Then I returned” (4:1, 7) marks a new observation and sets the solitary worker apart as a portrait of his own. The scene also connects naturally with what surrounds it. Verses 4–6 share its concern with the motive, measure, and human cost of work, and the description “one alone, without companion” (4:8) prepares for the answer “Two are better than one” (4:9).

Solomon's verdict frames the portrait at both ends. Verse 7 announces “vanity” before the man is described, and verse 8 closes with “This also is vanity.”

Ecclesiastes 4:8
Ecclesiastes 4:8
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 4:8 There is one alone, without companion: He has neither son nor brother. Yet there is no end to all his labors, Nor is his eye satisfied with riches. But he never asks, "For whom do I toil and deprive myself of good?" This also is vanity and a grave misfortune.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:8 This is the case of a man who is all alone, without a child or a brother, yet who works hard to gain as much wealth as he can. But then he asks himself, “Who am I working for? Why am I giving up so much pleasure now?” It is all so meaningless and depressing. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon describes a man with “neither son nor brother” whose labor never ends and whose eye is never “satisfied with riches,” yet who never stops to ask, “For whom do I toil and deprive myself of good?” Solomon calls this lonely accumulation “vanity and a grave misfortune,” because the man drives himself for no one and denies himself the very good his work could provide.

Verse Explanation

Solomon describes a man with “neither son nor brother” whose labor never ends and whose eye is never “satisfied with riches,” yet who never stops to ask, “For whom do I toil and deprive myself of good?” Solomon calls this lonely accumulation “vanity and a grave misfortune,” because the man drives himself for no one and denies himself the very good his work could provide.

There is one alone, without companion: Solomon introduces a single man and stresses his isolation twice. He is “alone,” and he has no “companion” to share his work or his life. The emphasis matters for the whole passage, because the next verse answers this man directly with “Two are better than one.”

He has neither son nor brother. A son would inherit what his father earned, and a brother would share in the family's life and goods. This man has neither, so no one close to him will receive or enjoy what his work produces. Earlier Solomon grieved that he would have to leave his own labor to a successor who might turn out to be a fool (2:18–19). This man lacks even the son or brother who would ordinarily receive what he leaves behind. The point concerns the purpose of his labor. The verse does not teach that the work of every person without a spouse or children is futile.

Yet there is no end to all his labors, The word “Yet” brings out the contradiction. With no one to provide for, he might be expected to work less, but his work never stops. His labor has become an end in itself, and he carries it on without any purpose he could name.

Nor is his eye satisfied with riches. The “eye” here stands for desire, the part of him that looks at what he has and wants more. He is not poor; he already has riches. His trouble is a craving that riches cannot fill, so however much he gathers, it never looks like enough.

But he never asks, “For whom do I toil and deprive myself of good?” Here is the heart of the portrait. Solomon puts into words the question the man ought to face but never does, and it has two parts. “For whom do I toil” asks who the work is for, and from everything Solomon has said, the answer is no one. “And deprive myself of good” admits what his drive costs him: he denies himself the rest, enjoyment, and ordinary good things his labor could supply. Elsewhere Ecclesiastes says that for a person to “enjoy the good of all his labor” is “the gift of God” (3:13). This man has the labor and the means, yet he keeps the good from himself and misses the gift his work could have brought him.

This also is vanity and a grave misfortune. Solomon now gives his verdict. “This also” ties the solitary worker to the envious toil of verse 4, which received the same judgment. His lonely accumulation is “vanity” because it piles up wealth that no one will enjoy, not even the man himself. It is also “a grave misfortune,” a serious calamity, because he spends his life working for nothing and gives up the good his life could have held. Measured within Solomon's earthly search, endless work done for no one and enjoyed by no one leaves nothing that lasts.

Study Notes

The word “alone” appears three times in this chapter (4:8, 10, 11). The solitary worker is the first use, and verses 10 and 11 return to the same word to show the danger and discomfort of being without a companion.

The unsatisfied eye echoes other lines in Ecclesiastes: “The eye is not satisfied with seeing” (1:8) and “He who loves silver will not be satisfied with silver” (5:10). Verse 8 applies that same restlessness to one man's endless accumulation.

The NKJV says the man “never asks” whom he toils for, while the NLT presents the question as one he does ask himself: “Who am I working for? Why am I giving up so much pleasure now?” In both translations the question comes in the man's own first-person words. For the closing verdict the NLT has “meaningless and depressing,” where the NKJV reads “vanity and a grave misfortune.”

Ecclesiastes 4:9–12 — Two Are Better Than One

Ecclesiastes 4:9
Ecclesiastes 4:9
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 4:9 Two are better than one, Because they have a good reward for their labor.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:9 Two people are better off than one, for they can help each other succeed. (NLT)

Main Idea

In answer to the man who works alone, Solomon declares that “Two are better than one” and gives his first reason: those who work together “have a good reward for their labor.” Shared effort produces fruit that companions can enjoy together, which is exactly what the lonely worker of verse 8 never had.

Verse Explanation

In answer to the man who works alone, Solomon declares that “Two are better than one” and gives his first reason: those who work together “have a good reward for their labor.” Shared effort produces fruit that companions can enjoy together, which is exactly what the lonely worker of verse 8 never had.

Two are better than one answers the portrait Solomon has just painted. In verse 8 he described “one alone, without companion,” a man who never stopped working and never asked whom his labor served. Now Solomon sets a different way of living beside that man and weighs the two, much as he weighed “a handful with quietness” against “both hands full” in verse 6. “Two” is companionship in its simplest form: one person standing beside another as a partner, friend, or fellow worker.

Because they have a good reward for their labor. This is the first of several reasons Solomon gives. The lonely worker labored endlessly and still deprived himself of good, but companions see a good return for what they do. The verse does not spell out how that happens, yet the flow of the passage points in two directions at once: people working together accomplish more, and the fruit of their work has someone to share it. After calling envious work (verse 4) and lonely accumulation (verse 8) “vanity,” Solomon now describes labor that yields “a good reward.” The difference is not in how hard anyone works but in whether the work is done alongside and for another person.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the second line “for they can help each other succeed,” describing the reward as the success companions help one another reach. The NKJV keeps the focus on the return from the labor itself, “a good reward for their labor.” Both readings picture shared effort producing more than solitary striving.

Verse 9 states the main claim, “Two are better than one,” and introduces its first reason with “Because.” The following verses add further examples, and in the NKJV each one opens with a connecting word: “For if” (verse 10), “Again, if” (verse 11), and “Though” (verse 12). The examples move from shared work to falling, cold, and attack.

Ecclesiastes 4:10
Ecclesiastes 4:10
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 4:10 For if they fall, one will lift up his companion. But woe to him who is alone when he falls, For he has no one to help him up.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:10 If one person falls, the other can reach out and help. But someone who falls alone is in real trouble. (NLT)

Main Idea

When one companion falls, the other can lift him up, but the person who falls alone “has no one to help him up.” Solomon’s “woe” turns this simple picture into a warning about how exposed an isolated person is when trouble comes.

Verse Explanation

When one companion falls, the other can lift him up, but the person who falls alone “has no one to help him up.” Solomon’s “woe” turns this simple picture into a warning about how exposed an isolated person is when trouble comes.

For if they fall, one will lift up his companion. The word “For” introduces a second reason. The picture is as ordinary as a stumble on a rough path: one person goes down, and the other is close enough to reach out and pull him back to his feet. The image is literal, but it naturally fits any setback that knocks a person down. The word “companion” points back to verse 8, where the solitary worker was “without companion.” What that man lacked, this person has at the moment it matters most.

But woe to him who is alone when he falls, turns from the person who is helped to the person who is not. “Woe” is a cry of lament and warning. Solomon does not treat falling itself as the tragedy, because everyone falls sooner or later. The tragedy is falling “alone,” with no one near enough to notice or to help.

For he has no one to help him up. This final line explains the woe. The fallen person may be strong, capable, even wealthy, but none of that lifts him off the ground. The words echo the oppressed of verse 1, who “have no comforter.” Across the chapter Solomon keeps returning to the same loss: victims with no one to comfort them, a worker with “neither son nor brother,” and now a fallen person with no one to help.

Study Notes

The NKJV says “if they fall” with a plural subject while describing one person lifting the other, so the sense is “if either of them falls.” The NLT makes that explicit: “If one person falls, the other can reach out and help.” The picture is the same in both translations.

In the NKJV the word “alone” appears three times in this chapter: “one alone” (verse 8), “alone when he falls” (verse 10), and “warm alone” (verse 11). The repetition ties the lonely worker of verses 7–8 to the companionship passage that answers him.

Ecclesiastes 4:11
Ecclesiastes 4:11
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 4:11 Again, if two lie down together, they will keep warm; But how can one be warm alone?(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:11 Likewise, two people lying close together can keep each other warm. But how can one be warm alone? (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon adds another benefit drawn from ordinary life: two who lie down together in the cold keep each other warm. His question, “how can one be warm alone?” shows that companionship gives a comfort no one can provide for himself.

Verse Explanation

Solomon adds another benefit drawn from ordinary life: two who lie down together in the cold keep each other warm. His question, “how can one be warm alone?” shows that companionship gives a comfort no one can provide for himself.

Again, if two lie down together, they will keep warm; “Again” adds a third reason, and the scene moves from daytime work and falls to a cold night. Two people lying side by side share their body heat and stay warm. The verse does not say who the two are. Like the rest of verses 9–12, it illustrates companionship in general rather than one particular relationship.

But how can one be warm alone? The question expects the answer “he can’t.” Some needs cannot be met by effort or resources, because the help has to come from someone else. The solitary worker of verse 8 could keep adding to his riches, but no amount of wealth could give him the warmth of another person beside him. Solomon presents this comfort as something received, not something a person can produce for himself.

Study Notes

None.

Main Idea

One person alone can be overpowered by an attacker, but two standing together can hold him off, so companionship also brings protection. The closing image, “a threefold cord is not quickly broken,” strengthens the picture of strength through union, because strands bound together hold far better than any single strand.

Verse Explanation

One person alone can be overpowered by an attacker, but two standing together can hold him off, so companionship also brings protection. The closing image, “a threefold cord is not quickly broken,” strengthens the picture of strength through union, because strands bound together hold far better than any single strand.

Though one may be overpowered by another, introduces the fourth benefit, and the danger grows more serious. Solomon has moved from sharing work to lifting the fallen and warming each other in the cold. Now he pictures violence: an attacker who can overpower a person standing alone.

two can withstand him. The “him” is the attacker. Someone who would overpower one person meets resistance when two stand together. Companionship protects as well as helps and comforts, and the isolated person of this chapter is exposed on every side: to falling, to cold, and to harm.

And a threefold cord is not quickly broken. Solomon closes with a proverb-like image that goes one step beyond the “two” he has been describing. A rope is made by twisting strands together. Any one strand can be snapped, but three twisted into a single cord bear a load none of them could carry alone. The image heightens everything verses 9–12 have said about strength through union. Solomon’s words are also measured: the cord is “not quickly broken,” which is not the same as unbreakable. Companionship makes people far stronger, but it does not promise that they will never be strained.

Beyond this immediate meaning, the threefold cord can rightly be applied to marriage and to Christian fellowship sustained by God. Jesus promised His presence where two or three gather in His name (Matthew 18:20). He spoke those words about the gathered community’s accountability and prayer, and read alongside that wider witness, the image pictures companions whose bond is strengthened by the Lord Himself. The verse does not identify the third strand as God, and the number three does not by itself point to the Trinity. The application grows out of the verse’s plain teaching about companionship and does not replace it.

Study Notes

Verse 12 completes a numerical progression that runs through the passage: one alone, two together, and finally a cord of three strands. The move from two to three comes only in the closing image, which shows that the cord is meant to heighten the case for companionship rather than to introduce a separate subject.

The NLT expands the verse with “two can stand back-to-back” and adds the sentence “Three are even better.” Neither phrase matches the NKJV wording, which moves straight from “two can withstand him” to the cord image without saying anything explicit about three people. The NLT’s additions interpret the image as well as translate it.

Ecclesiastes 4:13–16 — The Wise Youth, the Foolish King, and Fading Acclaim

Ecclesiastes 4:13
Ecclesiastes 4:13
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 4:13 Better a poor and wise youth Than an old and foolish king who will be admonished no more.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:13 It is better to be a poor but wise youth than an old and foolish king who refuses all advice. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon turns to public life and says that a poor youth who has wisdom is better than “an old and foolish king who will be admonished no more.” Age, rank, and wealth count for less than a teachable spirit, and the king’s folly lies in his refusal to accept correction.

Verse Explanation

Solomon turns to public life and says that a poor youth who has wisdom is better than “an old and foolish king who will be admonished no more.” Age, rank, and wealth count for less than a teachable spirit, and the king’s folly lies in his refusal to accept correction.

Better a poor and wise youth opens the chapter’s last observation with the same “better … than” comparison Solomon used for the quiet handful in verse 6. The youth has none of the things people usually respect. He is poor, and his youth gives him little standing or experience to recommend him. What he does have is wisdom, the skill for living well, and Solomon counts it worth more than everything he lacks. Solomon does not say that poverty makes anyone wise. The youth is better because he is wise, not because he is poor.

Than an old and foolish king sets the opposite figure beside him. The king has age, rank, and the resources of a throne, yet Solomon calls him foolish. Age normally brings experience, and people expect experience to produce wisdom. Putting “old” beside “foolish” is meant to jar the reader: years on the throne have not made this man wise.

who will be admonished no more names the king’s folly. To be admonished is to be warned or corrected. This king has reached the point where he no longer receives correction from anyone. The NLT puts it bluntly: he “refuses all advice.” His problem is a closed heart rather than a lack of ability. A ruler who will not be warned has shut himself off from the counsel that could protect both him and his people. A poor young man who can still be taught stands in a far better place than a king who has stopped listening.

Study Notes

The NKJV’s “will be admonished no more” suggests a change over time: the king may once have accepted counsel but no longer does. The NLT’s “refuses all advice” stresses his present refusal rather than the change. Both renderings place his folly in his unwillingness to be corrected.

The NKJV uses the same English word near the end of the book, where the closing voice tells the young reader, “And further, my son, be admonished by these” (12:12). Ecclesiastes closes by asking the young to accept the very correction this old king refuses.

Verse Explanation

Solomon supports that comparison with a dramatic rise: one who was born poor comes “out of prison to be king.” The verse shows that neither humble birth nor confinement can keep wisdom from rising, though Solomon leaves the figures unnamed and ties the story to no particular king.

For he comes out of prison to be king begins with “For,” which ties this verse to the comparison in verse 13 and shows why the wise youth is better off. The “he” is most naturally the poor and wise youth. He moves from confinement to the throne, about as sharp a reversal as public life allows. Solomon does not say why the youth was imprisoned or how he was released. The rise itself is the point: the man shut away from power is the one who ends up holding it.

Although he was born poor in his kingdom adds a second disadvantage to the first. The youth began life poor as well as confined, yet neither his humble birth nor his imprisonment kept him from rising. The verse shows wisdom’s real advantage: a teachable youth can rise where a king who will not be corrected cannot hold on. Solomon gives no names and links the story to no known king. It works as an illustration of wisdom and of how unstable acclaim is, not as a record of a particular reign.

Study Notes

The NKJV tells the rise as something that happens: he “comes out of prison to be king.” The NLT presents it as a possibility: such a youth “could rise” and “might even become king.” Either way the passage names no one and gives no date or place.

The English “his kingdom” does not settle whose kingdom is meant. It may be the realm the youth would one day rule, or the realm of the old king of verse 13. Both readings describe the same humble beginning, and the passage leaves the relationship unresolved.

Solomon pictures all the living crowding to the side of “the second youth who stands in his place,” a successor who takes the place of the ruler before him. The crowd’s eager support shows how completely public favor can shift to a new leader, though the passage does not make clear whether this is the youth of verse 13 or a later rival.

I saw all the living who walk under the sun brings Solomon into the story as an eyewitness. “All the living” is sweeping language for the whole population, the crowd as a body rather than a few supporters. This is the fourth time “under the sun” appears in the chapter (verses 1, 3, 7, and 15). It reminds the reader that Solomon is weighing public life by what can be seen on earth, where popularity looks like the measure of success.

They were with the second youth who stands in his place shows where the crowd went. To be “with” him means to stand on his side and give him their support. “The second youth who stands in his place” is a successor who takes the place of the ruler before him. The scene shows how quickly and completely public favor moves to a new face. The passage does not make clear whether this second youth is the youth of verse 13, who replaced the old king, or a later rival who replaces that youth in turn. Either way the lesson holds: the crowd moves toward whoever is newest.

Study Notes

The NLT reads “yet another youth who replaces him,” which commits to the later-rival reading. The NKJV’s “the second youth” leaves the question open, since the youth of verse 13 could be called “second” in relation to the old king. Both readings fit the wording, and neither changes the verse’s point about shifting favor.

In “stands in his place,” the word “his” could refer to the old king of verse 13 or to the youth whose rise verse 14 describes. Which one depends on how “the second youth” is understood, and the passage does not settle it.

Ecclesiastes 4:16
Ecclesiastes 4:16
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 4:16 There was no end of all the people over whom he was made king; Yet those who come afterward will not rejoice in him. Surely this also is vanity and grasping for the wind.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 4:16 Endless crowds stand around him, but then another generation grows up and rejects him, too. So it is all meaningless — like chasing the wind. (NLT)

Main Idea

The new king rules over countless people, yet “those who come afterward will not rejoice in him,” so even favor that seemed limitless fades with the next generation. Solomon ends the chapter by calling this unstable acclaim “vanity and grasping for the wind,” because even wisdom’s real advantage cannot secure a lasting place in public memory.

Verse Explanation

The new king rules over countless people, yet “those who come afterward will not rejoice in him,” so even favor that seemed limitless fades with the next generation. Solomon ends the chapter by calling this unstable acclaim “vanity and grasping for the wind,” because even wisdom’s real advantage cannot secure a lasting place in public memory.

There was no end of all the people over whom he was made king describes the new ruler at the height of his popularity. His subjects seem countless, and his support looks as if it could never run out. Whether this new king is the youth who rose from poverty and prison or a later rival, he now rules a following too large to count.

Yet those who come afterward will not rejoice in him turns the scene with a single word. The generation that grows up later did not see his rise and does not share the delight of the people who first welcomed him. The NLT says that generation “rejects him.” Favor that seemed endless does not outlast the people who gave it. Whichever youth verse 15 means, the same thing happens to him that happened to the old king: someone newer takes his place in the people’s affection.

Surely this also is vanity and grasping for the wind gives Solomon’s verdict and closes the chapter. “This also” puts fading acclaim alongside the envious work of verse 4 and the lonely accumulation of verse 8. “Vanity” means meaningless. The Hebrew word behind it pictures a breath or vapor, and public favor is exactly that kind of breath, there for a moment and then gone. “Grasping for the wind” pictures trying to hold what cannot be held. The verdict does not cancel verse 13: a teachable youth really is better than a king who will not be corrected. Yet when life is measured only by what happens under the sun, even wisdom cannot secure a lasting place in public memory.

Study Notes

The full phrase “vanity and grasping for the wind” appears twice in this chapter, in verse 4 and here in verse 16. It begins and ends Solomon’s observations about work and public life, from the rivalry behind skillful labor to the popularity that fades after a ruler’s rise.

“Those who come afterward” echoes the book’s opening poem: “Nor will there be any remembrance of things that are to come By those who will come after” (1:11). Verse 16 applies that general statement to one particular case, the popularity of a king.

Ecclesiastes 9:13–16 tells a similar story of a poor wise man who delivered a city: “Yet no one remembered that same poor man” (9:15), and “Nevertheless the poor man’s wisdom is despised” (9:16). Like the story in 4:13–16, it holds together wisdom’s real worth and its failure to secure lasting recognition.
