Chapter Study

Ecclesiastes 5

Verse-by-Verse
Ecclesiastes 5:1
Ecclesiastes 5:1
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 5:1 Walk prudently when you go to the house of God; and draw near to hear rather than to give the sacrifice of fools, for they do not know that they do evil.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 5:1 As you enter the house of God, keep your ears open and your mouth shut. It is evil to make mindless offerings to God. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon turns from watching life in society to giving direct instruction: whoever goes to “the house of God” must come carefully, ready to listen. Offerings from people who will not listen to God are “the sacrifice of fools,” and such worshipers do wrong without even realizing it.

Verse Explanation

Solomon turns from watching life in society to giving direct instruction: whoever goes to “the house of God” must come carefully, ready to listen. Offerings from people who will not listen to God are “the sacrifice of fools,” and such worshipers do wrong without even realizing it.

Walk prudently when you go to the house of God opens the chapter with a sudden change of voice. Through chapter 4 Solomon has been observing life in society: the tears of the oppressed, work driven by envy, lonely toil, and popular favor that fades. Now he speaks directly to his hearers, and the first place he takes them is God’s house, most likely the temple in Jerusalem, where Israel brought its sacrifices and paid its vows. To walk prudently is to watch one’s steps and come carefully, aware of where one is going and before whom one will stand. These commands are plain, positive instruction within the book. They are not one more conclusion of the search that Solomon exposes as empty.

and draw near to hear shows what coming carefully looks like. The worshiper is not told to stay away. God’s house is a place to draw near, but the purpose of coming is to listen. Listening comes first because the worshiper comes to receive from God, not to impress Him, and a person who truly hears what God says is ready to respond to it.

rather than to give the sacrifice of fools sets that listening against a different kind of worship. Sacrifices were part of the worship God gave Israel, so the problem is not the offering itself but the person offering it. In Ecclesiastes, as in Proverbs, the fool is someone who will not heed wisdom. Here the fool brings a gift to God with no intention of listening to Him. The offering becomes a substitute for attention, as if giving something could take the place of hearing.

for they do not know that they do evil explains why this matters so much. These worshipers think they are doing something good. They go through the motions of devotion and leave satisfied, never realizing that careless worship is itself wrong. Their blindness to their own fault is part of their folly. That blindness is why Solomon begins with a warning instead of assuming that everyone who comes to God’s house comes rightly.

Study Notes

Printed Hebrew Bibles count this verse as the last verse of chapter 4 (4:17). Hebrew chapter 5 therefore begins with the verse English Bibles number 5:2, and it has one fewer verse. The English division, followed by both the NKJV and NLT, keeps the call to “draw near to hear” together with the counsel about speech that it introduces in verses 2–3.

The NLT paraphrases this verse as “keep your ears open and your mouth shut” and calls the fools’ sacrifices “mindless offerings.” Its wording brings forward the contrast between listening and speaking that verse 2 develops, and it interprets the folly as worship offered without thought. The NKJV keeps the separate images of walking and drawing near, which the NLT combines in “As you enter.”

Ecclesiastes 5:2
Ecclesiastes 5:2
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 5:2 Do not be rash with your mouth, And let not your heart utter anything hastily before God. For God is in heaven, and you on earth; Therefore let your words be few.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 5:2 Don’t make rash promises, and don’t be hasty in bringing matters before God. After all, God is in heaven, and you are here on earth. So let your words be few. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because “God is in heaven, and you on earth,” the worshiper must not rush into rash words or hasty promises before Him, and so “let your words be few.” This humility does not mean God is distant or unwilling to hear. It means that words spoken to Him carry real weight and should be measured.

Verse Explanation

Because “God is in heaven, and you on earth,” the worshiper must not rush into rash words or hasty promises before Him, and so “let your words be few.” This humility does not mean God is distant or unwilling to hear. It means that words spoken to Him carry real weight and should be measured.

Do not be rash with your mouth moves from coming to God’s house to speaking there. Rash words are words that run ahead of thought. In the setting of worship they include hasty prayers, careless claims, and especially promises made to God on impulse, which verses 4–6 address directly.

And let not your heart utter anything hastily before God carries the warning inward. The mouth speaks, but the heart is where words begin, so Solomon addresses both the spoken words and the inner impulse behind them. The words “before God” show why haste is so dangerous here: the worshiper is speaking in God’s presence, not into empty air.

For God is in heaven, and you on earth gives the reason. God rules over all from heaven, while the worshiper is a creature standing on the earth He made. That difference calls for humility and accountability. It does not mean that God is far away or unwilling to listen. This same verse assumes the worshiper will speak to Him, and the previous verse tells the worshiper to draw near. God’s greatness is what gives weight to every word spoken to Him. Ecclesiastes calls this reverence the fear of God, which is a right view of who He is.

Therefore let your words be few draws the conclusion. Because words spoken before God carry real weight, they should be measured and meant. Solomon is not setting a numerical limit on prayer. He is warning against piling up words without thought, as though many words could impress God or make up for not listening.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the first line “Don’t make rash promises” and the second “don’t be hasty in bringing matters before God.” This wording points ahead to the vows of verses 4–6. The NKJV’s “rash with your mouth” and “utter anything hastily” are broader and cover any careless speech before God. That broader wording fits the structure of the passage: verses 1–3 concern listening and restrained speech in general, and verses 4–7 narrow the subject to the vow.

Ecclesiastes 5:3
Ecclesiastes 5:3
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 5:3 For a dream comes through much activity, And a fool's voice is known by his many words.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 5:3 Too much activity gives you restless dreams; too many words make you a fool. (NLT)

Main Idea

A proverb backs up the call for few words: just as a busy, restless life produces dreams, so a flood of words gives away the fool. The comparison exposes restless activity and careless talk; it is not a statement about every dream.

Verse Explanation

A proverb backs up the call for few words: just as a busy, restless life produces dreams, so a flood of words gives away the fool. The comparison exposes restless activity and careless talk; it is not a statement about every dream.

For a dream comes through much activity begins a short proverb that supports the call for few words, and the opening “For” ties it to verse 2. “Much activity” describes a crowded life full of effort and concern, and dreams are what such a life produces at night. The line draws on ordinary experience: a restless day leads to restless sleep. Solomon uses this as a comparison. It is not a teaching about the meaning or source of every dream, and it says nothing against God’s ability to speak to His people.

And a fool’s voice is known by his many words completes the comparison. Just as busy days produce dreams, a fool produces a flood of words, and that flood gives him away. His voice is “known,” recognized for what it is, by how much he talks. In the setting of worship, the proverb explains why few words are wise in God’s presence. A stream of careless talk is no proof of devotion. Instead, it shows a person who has not stopped to listen.

Study Notes

Verse 7 deliberately echoes this verse: “For in the multitude of dreams and many words there is also vanity. But fear God.” Because dreams and many words appear together in both verses, verses 1–7 hold together as one unit, and the closing command “But fear God” sums up the whole passage on worship and vows.

The NLT renders the second line “too many words make you a fool,” which suggests that excessive talk produces foolishness. The NKJV’s “a fool’s voice is known by his many words” presents many words as the evidence that reveals a fool who is already there. Both renderings support the proverb’s warning, but the NKJV keeps the focus on recognition.

Later in the book Solomon makes a similar observation about ordinary speech: “A fool also multiplies words” (10:14). Too many words mark the fool in worship and in daily life alike.

The general purpose of the dream comparison is clear, but its precise force is less certain. The line may stress that anxious busyness leads to restless dreams. It may also point more broadly to activity that, like many words, produces little of substance, a reading supported by the link verse 7 draws between many dreams and vanity. Either way, the comparison supports the call for few words before God.

Ecclesiastes 5:4–7 — Paying What You Vow to God

Ecclesiastes 5:4
Ecclesiastes 5:4
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 5:4 When you make a vow to God, do not delay to pay it; For He has no pleasure in fools. Pay what you have vowed"”(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 5:4 When you make a promise to God, don’t delay in following through, for God takes no pleasure in fools. Keep all the promises you make to him. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon moves from speech in general to one specific kind of speech, the vow, and says that a promise made to God must be kept without delay. God takes no pleasure in fools who promise and then put off keeping their word, so the verse presses the plain command, “Pay what you have vowed.”

Verse Explanation

Solomon moves from speech in general to one specific kind of speech, the vow, and says that a promise made to God must be kept without delay. God takes no pleasure in fools who promise and then put off keeping their word, so the verse presses the plain command, “Pay what you have vowed.”

When you make a vow to God, do not delay to pay it — Solomon now narrows the counsel of verses 1–3. He has warned against rash speech before God in general; here he takes up one specific kind of speech, the vow. A vow was a voluntary promise made to God, and Israel brought sacrifices and paid vows at the house of God, most likely the temple in Jerusalem. The word “When” assumes that worshipers will sometimes make vows, and Solomon does not forbid them. His command concerns timing. A vow that is put off again and again is broken by degrees: the person keeps speaking as though payment is coming while holding on to what was promised.

For He has no pleasure in fools — This is the reason for the command. The fool in this verse is the person who makes a promise to God and then delays keeping it. God takes no delight in words offered to Him that are never matched by action, however sincere or impressive they sounded when they were spoken. What pleases Him is a worshiper whose word to Him can be trusted.

Pay what you have vowed — Solomon repeats the duty as a plain, unqualified command. Once a vow is spoken it is owed to God, so paying it simply gives Him what was promised. Solomon’s concern is honesty before God; he is not commending a promise that would require doing what God forbids.

Study Notes

The NKJV ends this verse with a dash after “Pay what you have vowed,” so the command runs directly into the comparison of verse 5. The punctuation presents the two verses as one connected thought: pay the vow, because it is better never to vow than to leave a vow unpaid.

The word “fools” ties this verse to “the sacrifice of fools” in verse 1 and to “a fool’s voice” in verse 3. In all three verses the fool is marked by careless worship or speech before God, so the warning about vows continues the same portrait instead of opening a new subject.

Ecclesiastes 5:5
Ecclesiastes 5:5
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 5:5 Better not to vow than to vow and not pay.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 5:5 It is better to say nothing than to make a promise and not keep it. (NLT)

Main Idea

No one had to make a vow, but once made, a vow was binding. That is why it is “Better not to vow than to vow and not pay”: staying silent is wiser than promising God something and then failing to give it.

Verse Explanation

No one had to make a vow, but once made, a vow was binding. That is why it is “Better not to vow than to vow and not pay”: staying silent is wiser than promising God something and then failing to give it.

Better not to vow than to vow and not pay — The whole verse is a single comparison. No one in Israel was required to make a vow, so choosing not to vow was no failure before God. Once a vow was spoken, however, it was binding, and leaving it unpaid was a real wrong. Silence is therefore the wiser course than a promise that will not be kept.

This saying applies the warning of verse 2, “let not your heart utter anything hastily before God,” to the vow in particular. The verse does not discourage generous devotion to God. It discourages promises made in a moment of feeling without weighing whether they can be kept. A worshiper who stays silent has said nothing false before God, while one who vows and fails to pay has spoken a promise that proved untrue.

Study Notes

This verse weighs two options in the same way as several sayings of the previous chapter: “Better a handful with quietness Than both hands full” (4:6), “Two are better than one” (4:9), and “Better a poor and wise youth Than an old and foolish king” (4:13). Each saying sets two possibilities side by side and names the wiser one. Here the two possibilities are silence and an unpaid vow.

Ecclesiastes 5:6
Ecclesiastes 5:6
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 5:6 Do not let your mouth cause your flesh to sin, nor say before the messenger of God that it was an error. Why should God be angry at your excuse and destroy the work of your hands?(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 5:6 Don’t let your mouth make you sin. And don’t defend yourself by telling the Temple messenger that the promise you made was a mistake. That would make God angry, and he might wipe out everything you have achieved. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon warns against letting careless words draw the whole person into sin, first by making a vow and then by telling “the messenger of God” that it was only “an error.” This messenger was most likely a priest or another temple representative (some understand an angel). Either way, the excuse cannot escape God, who may be angered by it and bring “the work of your hands” to nothing.

Verse Explanation

Solomon warns against letting careless words draw the whole person into sin, first by making a vow and then by telling “the messenger of God” that it was only “an error.” This messenger was most likely a priest or another temple representative (some understand an angel). Either way, the excuse cannot escape God, who may be angered by it and bring “the work of your hands” to nothing.

Do not let your mouth cause your flesh to sin — The mouth is one small part of a person, yet careless words can bring the whole person into guilt. “Flesh” here refers to the person in ordinary bodily life, the one who must live with what the mouth has promised. A rash vow is the first step in that direction, because words spoken in a moment commit the whole life that follows.

nor say before the messenger of God that it was an error — The second step is the attempted escape. Having vowed and failed to pay, the worshiper tells the messenger of God that the vow was a mistake. This messenger was most likely a priest or another temple representative who received or oversaw the vow, since the whole passage is set at the house of God and concerns vows brought there. Malachi 2:7 calls the priest God’s messenger, which supports this reading, though this verse does not name the office. Some interpreters understand an angel instead. Either way, the excuse is spoken in God’s presence. Calling a deliberate promise “an error” treats it as an innocent slip, and the next clause names that claim for what it is: an excuse.

Why should God be angry at your excuse and destroy the work of your hands? — Solomon closes with a question meant to stop the worshiper short. Why provoke God? The verse places His anger on the excuse itself, the attempt to talk one’s way out of an accountable promise. “The work of your hands” means the labor and achievements of a person’s life. Someone who holds back what was promised in order to protect possessions is shortsighted, because God can bring all that work to nothing. The question warns of what God may do; it does not turn every setback in a person’s work into proof of a broken vow.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “messenger” covers a wide range. It can describe any person sent to carry a message or act on someone else’s behalf, including a person sent on God’s behalf, and it is also the word the Old Testament uses for angels. The word by itself therefore cannot settle who is meant here. The identification depends on the setting of the verse.

The ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament presents the excuse as spoken before God Himself. That wording stresses the worshiper’s accountability to God, but it does not by itself show whether the messenger in the Hebrew text is a priest.

The NLT translates the phrase as “the Temple messenger,” which builds the human, temple-based reading into the translation itself. The NKJV’s “the messenger of God” keeps the wording open, so the identification remains an interpretation of the phrase and not something the text states outright.

Ecclesiastes 5:7
Ecclesiastes 5:7
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 5:7 For in the multitude of dreams and many words there is also vanity. But fear God.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 5:7 Talk is cheap, like daydreams and other useless activities. Fear God instead. (NLT)

Main Idea

Echoing the dreams and many words of verse 3, Solomon sums up the whole passage: piling up dreams and words is meaningless, empty activity that achieves nothing before God. Against all of it he sets one command, “But fear God,” which calls the worshiper to a right view of God that shows itself in listening and in honest promises.

Verse Explanation

Echoing the dreams and many words of verse 3, Solomon sums up the whole passage: piling up dreams and words is meaningless, empty activity that achieves nothing before God. Against all of it he sets one command, “But fear God,” which calls the worshiper to a right view of God that shows itself in listening and in honest promises.

For in the multitude of dreams and many words there is also vanity — Solomon returns to the two images of verse 3, where “a dream comes through much activity” and “a fool’s voice is known by his many words.” Bringing them back here gathers up the whole passage that began in verse 1. “Vanity” is the NKJV word for a Hebrew term that pictures a breath or vapor, something that cannot be held, and in Ecclesiastes it means meaningless. Dreams and words piled up before God are empty in that sense: they produce activity and sound but leave nothing of substance. As in verse 3, the comparison exposes restless activity and careless speech; it is not a statement about every dream.

But fear God — Against all that empty talk Solomon sets one short command. To fear God is to have a right view of Him, a view that shows itself in reverence, worship, trust, and obedience. In this passage that right view takes specific forms: drawing near to hear (verse 1), keeping words few because “God is in heaven, and you on earth” (verse 2), and paying what has been vowed (verses 4–6). A worshiper who truly sees who God is will listen before speaking and will keep the promises made to Him.

Study Notes

Translations handle this verse differently. The NKJV keeps the pairing of dreams and many words from verse 3, while the NLT paraphrases more freely: “Talk is cheap, like daydreams and other useless activities.” Both renderings make the same basic point, that multiplied words and dreams are empty before God, although the precise force of the dream comparison is less certain than that main point.

The command “But fear God” is the first time Ecclesiastes directly tells the reader to fear God. Other passages describe the fear of God (3:14; 7:18; 8:12–13), but the only other direct command is the book’s conclusion: “Fear God and keep His commandments” (12:13).

Ecclesiastes 5:8–9 — Oppression Among Layers of Officials

Ecclesiastes 5:8
Ecclesiastes 5:8
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 5:8 If you see the oppression of the poor, and the violent perversion of justice and righteousness in a province, do not marvel at the matter; for high official watches over high official, and higher officials are over them.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 5:8 Don’t be surprised if you see a poor person being oppressed by the powerful and if justice is being miscarried throughout the land. For every official is under orders from higher up, and matters of justice get lost in red tape and bureaucracy. (NLT)

Main Idea

Turning to public life, Solomon tells his hearers not to be astonished when they see the poor oppressed and justice violently twisted in a province. His reason is that “high official watches over high official, and higher officials are over them.” Those layered officials may be checking one another or protecting one another's interests, but in either case higher authority is no guarantee of justice for the poor, and not being surprised by oppression does not mean accepting it as right.

Verse Explanation

Turning to public life, Solomon tells his hearers not to be astonished when they see the poor oppressed and justice violently twisted in a province. His reason is that “high official watches over high official, and higher officials are over them.” Those layered officials may be checking one another or protecting one another's interests, but in either case higher authority is no guarantee of justice for the poor, and not being surprised by oppression does not mean accepting it as right.

If you see the oppression of the poor — Solomon leaves the house of God and turns to public life, but he keeps speaking directly to his hearer, just as he did in verses 1–7. The word “if” does not suggest that oppression is rare. It pictures an ordinary observer coming across something that happens often enough to be seen. The victims are “the poor,” the people with the least money and the least power to defend themselves. Solomon has looked at this before. In 4:1 he considered “all the oppression that is done under the sun” and saw the tears of the oppressed, who had no comforter while power stood on the side of those who oppressed them.

and the violent perversion of justice and righteousness — The second scene concerns justice itself. A perversion is something twisted out of its proper form, and “violent” adds force: justice is bent deliberately and under pressure. “Justice and righteousness” belong together here, describing both the fair decision that should protect the weak and the right dealing that should mark the whole community. The very things meant to shelter the poor have become the means of wronging them. Solomon has seen this too, for in 3:16 he observed that “In the place of judgment, Wickedness was there.”

in a province — A province is a governed district with officials and administration. The injustice Solomon describes does not happen on a lawless frontier where no one is in charge. It happens inside an organized system of government, and that is exactly why the verse goes on to speak about the officials themselves.

do not marvel at the matter — Solomon tells his hearer not to be astonished. He is preparing the reader for what life in a fallen world actually looks like, so that the sight of oppression does not leave him shaken as though something unheard of had happened. Not being surprised is different from approving. Nothing in the verse calls the oppression right or tells the hearer to stop caring about the poor. What it removes is the naive expectation that a system of authority will automatically produce fair results.

for high official watches over high official, and higher officials are over them — Here Solomon gives his reason. Officials stand in layers, each beneath another, with still higher officials above them all. “Watches over” can describe supervision, with each level keeping an eye on the one below it. It can also describe officials watching out for one another and protecting a shared interest. The verse does not settle which picture is in view, and in either case the poor stand at the bottom of the chain. Each added layer can mean one more official taking a share or one more official looking away, so higher authority offers no guarantee that justice will reach the people who need it. The verse does not promise that a superior will step in and put things right, and it does not identify the highest authority as God. Solomon has already declared that “God shall judge the righteous and the wicked” (3:17), but in this verse he describes the chain of human power as an observer sees it under the sun.

Study Notes

The NLT renders the second half of the verse, “For every official is under orders from higher up, and matters of justice get lost in red tape and bureaucracy.” That translation reads the chain of officials as a system in which responsibility is passed upward and justice is delayed until it disappears. The NKJV’s “high official watches over high official” is less specific and leaves room for either oversight or mutual protection among the officials. The precise way the hierarchy operates is debated.

The NKJV word “province” appears in the plural in 2:8, where Solomon gathered “the special treasures of kings and of the provinces.” In both places the word belongs to the world of royal administration that Solomon knew from the throne. The NLT’s “throughout the land” in 5:8 is more general and does not keep that administrative picture.

Ecclesiastes 5:9
Ecclesiastes 5:9
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 5:9 Moreover the profit of the land is for all; even the king is served from the field.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 5:9 Even the king milks the land for his own profit! (NLT)

Main Idea

Beneath the whole chain of officials lies the land itself, whose produce sustains everyone in society. The wording of this verse is difficult and should not be pressed into a theory of government, but its plain point is that “even the king is served from the field.”

Verse Explanation

Beneath the whole chain of officials lies the land itself, whose produce sustains everyone in society. The wording of this verse is difficult and should not be pressed into a theory of government, but its plain point is that “even the king is served from the field.”

Moreover the profit of the land is for all — With “Moreover” Solomon adds a further observation to what he has just said about the officials. “Profit” is a key word in Ecclesiastes for the gain left over from labor, the very thing the book’s opening question asks about (1:3). Here the profit is the produce of the land, the harvest that the fields yield, and that produce benefits everyone in society. Beneath the whole chain of officials in verse 8 lies the soil, and every layer of that hierarchy lives on what the land brings forth.

even the king is served from the field — The word “even” points to the top of the chain. The king, the highest human authority over every official, still depends on the field for his food. The verse does not draw out every implication, but placed beside verse 8 it shows that the ruler who sits above all the officials is sustained by land that ordinary people work, the same people who stand at the bottom of the chain. Because the wording of this verse is difficult, its details should not carry a theory of government. Its plain point is enough: all human power, however high it rises, rests on the provision of the field.

Study Notes

Translations handle this verse quite differently. The NLT reads, “Even the king milks the land for his own profit!” That rendering ties verse 9 to the oppression of verse 8 by making the king one more beneficiary at the land’s expense. The NKJV presents the land’s produce as benefiting everyone, with the king served from the field like the rest of society. The difference reflects the difficulty of the verse’s wording.

Verse 9 introduces the word “profit,” which returns twice in the verses that follow: “what profit have the owners” (5:11) and “what profit has he who has labored for the wind?” (5:16). The repeated word links this short observation about the land to the teaching on wealth that comes next, even though the subject of verses 8–9 is injustice in public life rather than private riches.

God is not named from verse 8 through verse 17. After the command “But fear God” (5:7), He is not mentioned again until verse 18, where Solomon speaks of the days of life “which God gives him.” The officials, the land, and the king of verses 8–9 belong to the stretch of the chapter in which life is described without reference to Him.

Ecclesiastes 5:10–12 — The Love of Money Never Satisfies

Ecclesiastes 5:10
Ecclesiastes 5:10
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 5:10 He who loves silver will not be satisfied with silver; Nor he who loves abundance, with increase. This also is vanity.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 5:10 Those who love money will never have enough. How meaningless to think that wealth brings true happiness! (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon now turns to private wealth: the one who loves money is never satisfied by money, and the one who loves abundance never finds enough in more. The problem is the love of money, not money itself, and because that desire can never be filled, “This also is vanity”: chasing wealth for satisfaction is meaningless.

Verse Explanation

Solomon now turns to private wealth: the one who loves money is never satisfied by money, and the one who loves abundance never finds enough in more. The problem is the love of money, not money itself, and because that desire can never be filled, “This also is vanity”: chasing wealth for satisfaction is meaningless.

He who loves silver will not be satisfied with silver moves Solomon’s attention from injustice in the province (verses 8–9) to the private pursuit of wealth. Silver stands for money here, which is why the NLT simply says “money.” Notice how Solomon describes this man. He does not say the man has too little. He names what the man loves, and the thing he loves is the very thing that cannot fill him. Each gain sharpens the appetite instead of ending it. So the trouble lies in loving money, not in money itself. Later in this chapter Solomon calls riches and wealth “the gift of God” (verse 19).

Nor he who loves abundance, with increase repeats the thought in wider terms. “Abundance” reaches beyond coins to possessions of every kind, and “increase” is the growth the lover of abundance keeps hoping will finally be enough. The second line rules out the idea that some other form of wealth will succeed where silver failed. Whatever form the abundance takes, having more of it does not bring rest.

This also is vanity gives Solomon’s verdict. The Hebrew word the NKJV translates “vanity” pictures a breath or vapor, something that cannot be held. In Ecclesiastes it marks a pursuit as meaningless when life is pursued without God. The word “also” places the love of money beside the other pursuits Solomon has already tested and found empty. God is not named from verse 8 through verse 17, and the verdict fits that silence: when silver is loved as the source of satisfaction, the search for enough never ends. In the wider witness of Scripture, the thirst this verse exposes is met by Christ, who offers water that ends thirst (John 4:13–14).

Study Notes

The NLT expands the short verdict “This also is vanity” into an explanation: “How meaningless to think that wealth brings true happiness!” That rendering states openly what the NKJV leaves implied: the futile thing is expecting wealth to satisfy.

This complaint belongs to a thread that runs through this part of the book. In 4:8 the lonely worker labors without end, “Nor is his eye satisfied with riches,” and in 6:7 Solomon observes that “All the labor of man is for his mouth, And yet the soul is not satisfied.”

Ecclesiastes 5:11
Ecclesiastes 5:11
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 5:11 When goods increase, They increase who eat them; So what profit have the owners Except to see them with their eyes?(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 5:11 The more you have, the more people come to help you spend it. So what good is wealth — except perhaps to watch it slip through your fingers! (NLT)

Main Idea

As goods increase, so does the number of people who live off them: household, servants, and everyone else with a claim on the wealth. What grows also flows out to others, so the owner's only real “profit” is to look at what he has.

Verse Explanation

As goods increase, so does the number of people who live off them: household, servants, and everyone else with a claim on the wealth. What grows also flows out to others, so the owner's only real “profit” is to look at what he has.

When goods increase, They increase who eat them describes what happens as wealth grows. The number of people who live from it grows too: household members, servants, workers, and everyone else with a claim on the estate. “Eat” is plain language for consumption. The goods are used up by mouths other than the owner’s, so growing possessions do not pile up in one place. They flow out to others almost as fast as they come in.

So what profit have the owners raises the question that runs through the whole book. “Profit” is the lasting gain left after labor, the very thing asked about in 1:3. The word appeared just before this in verse 9, where “the profit of the land is for all.” Now Solomon asks the question about the wealthy themselves: if the people around them consume the increase, what is actually left for the owners?

Except to see them with their eyes? gives the answer with quiet irony. The owner’s one remaining benefit is the sight of his goods. He can look at what he has, but he cannot stop the stream of people who consume it, and looking is a thin return for so much effort. The verse does not condemn owning goods or providing for a household. It shows how little the lover of abundance actually gains by having more.

Study Notes

The NLT translates the final line differently: “So what good is wealth — except perhaps to watch it slip through your fingers!” The NKJV pictures the owner looking at goods he still has, while the NLT pictures him watching them disappear. In both versions, all the owner gets is what his eyes take in.

The word “profit” links this verse to verse 9 just before it and to verse 16, where the question returns at the close of the next example: “And what profit has he who has labored for the wind?” All three echo the book’s opening question in 1:3.

The phrase “under the sun” places the case inside Solomon’s examination of life measured only by earthly gain and the grave. Within that horizon, the story he is about to tell has no happy ending.

Riches kept for their owner to his hurt. The man has wealth, and he guards it, holding it back for his own benefit. Yet the very riches he keeps for his good become the source of his harm. Solomon does not yet say how the harm comes; the following verses tell it: the riches are lost, the son inherits nothing, the man leaves the world with empty hands, and his days fill with gloom. Verse 12 has already hinted at the problem, since “the abundance of the rich will not permit him to sleep.” The irony is sharp. Keeping wealth was supposed to make him secure, and keeping it is what hurt him. Solomon is describing what happened in this case, not saying that every person who owns or saves money comes to harm.

Study Notes

The opening words of this verse are echoed in verse 16, “And this also is a severe evil,” and the two statements frame a single case running through verses 13–16. A similar introduction returns at 6:1, “There is an evil which I have seen under the sun,” where Solomon describes a man to whom God gives riches but “does not give him power to eat of it” (6:2).

The NLT renders the second line “Hoarding riches harms the saver,” reading “kept” as hoarding and “his hurt” as harm to the one who saves. The NKJV’s “kept for their owner” states the same idea more generally.

Here is what I have seen: Solomon opens his counsel with the same kind of first-hand report he used in verse 13, where he described “a severe evil which I have seen under the sun.” That earlier sight was riches hoarded, lost, and leaving nothing behind. Now he reports something he has seen to be good. Placing the two observations side by side invites the reader to compare a life spent clutching wealth with a life that receives what God gives.

It is good and fitting for one to eat and drink, Only one verse earlier, the man who “labored for the wind” was said to eat “in darkness” all his days (verse 17). Solomon now commends the very same ordinary act. Eating and drinking are called “good,” something of real worth, and “fitting,” something that properly belongs to human life. The meal itself was never the problem. What separates the two lives is the way the same daily bread is received.

and to enjoy the good of all his labor in which he toils under the sun Solomon does not pretend that work is easy. It is still labor in which a person “toils.” Yet the work that left the hoarder with empty hands can yield good that is actually tasted and enjoyed along the way. Here the phrase “under the sun” does not mark another mistaken conclusion in Solomon’s search. It simply places this enjoyment in ordinary life on earth, where people work, eat, and live out their days.

all the days of his life which God gives him; God has not been named since verse 7, while Solomon described oppressive officials, restless money, and lost riches. Now He returns as the Giver. The days themselves are His gift, and the wording quietly admits that they are limited in number. A person does not own his lifetime or produce it by effort; he receives it one day at a time from God.

for it is his heritage. A heritage is the share assigned to someone, the portion that is his to receive. Solomon calls this enjoyment of food, drink, and work a person’s God-given portion in life. It is something received from God’s hand, not seized or manufactured. That stands in sharp contrast to the hoarder of verse 14, whose riches perished so that there was “nothing in his hand” for his son.

Study Notes

Within this chapter the phrase “under the sun” appears only twice, in verse 13 and here in verse 18. It frames both the “severe evil” of riches lost and the good of enjoyment received from God, so the same earthly setting holds both outcomes.

The NLT renders “all the days of his life which God gives him” as “during the short life God has given them,” making explicit the brevity that verse 20 addresses. It also renders “it is his heritage” as “to accept their lot in life,” bringing out the sense of a share assigned to a person.

Almost the same wording returns in 8:15, where Solomon commends eating, drinking, and being merry “all the days of his life which God gives him under the sun.” The repetition ties the two enjoyment passages together.
