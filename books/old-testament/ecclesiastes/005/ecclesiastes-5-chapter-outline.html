Ecclesiastes 5 Chapter Outline

Revision 3 · November 26, 2026

Chapter Focus

Ecclesiastes 5 brings Solomon’s survey of life in society into the house of God, where he calls for attentive listening, honest vows, and the fear of God. Turning back to the province and the marketplace, he shows that oppression, the love of money, and the loss of riches leave a person with nothing to carry away, and he closes by setting against that empty pursuit the gift of God: the power to enjoy food, work, and wealth as one’s heritage from His hand.

Chapter Sections

1. Ecclesiastes 5:1–3 — Drawing Near to Hear in the House of God

Solomon tells the worshiper to come to God’s house ready to listen rather than to offer “the sacrifice of fools,” and to keep speech few, “For God is in heaven, and you on earth.” A proverb about dreams and many words shows that a flood of words reveals the fool.

2. Ecclesiastes 5:4–7 — Paying What You Vow to God

A vow made to God must be paid without delay, for it is “Better not to vow than to vow and not pay.” Solomon warns against excusing a broken promise before the messenger of God as a mistake, and he gathers the whole call to worship into one command: “But fear God.”

3. Ecclesiastes 5:8–9 — Oppression Among Layers of Officials

Solomon tells his hearers not to be astonished when they see the poor oppressed and justice twisted in a province, because “high official watches over high official” within a chain of authority. Yet the produce of the land sustains everyone, and “even the king is served from the field.”

4. Ecclesiastes 5:10–12 — The Love of Money Never Satisfies

“He who loves silver will not be satisfied with silver.” More goods only bring more people to consume them, and while “The sleep of a laboring man is sweet,” the rich man’s abundance keeps him awake.

5. Ecclesiastes 5:13–17 — Riches Lost and Nothing Carried Away

Solomon describes a “severe evil”: riches hoarded to the owner’s harm, lost through misfortune, and leaving nothing for his son. Everyone leaves this world as he entered it, “naked,” taking nothing from his labor, and the man who “labored for the wind” spends his days in darkness, sorrow, and anger.

6. Ecclesiastes 5:18–20 — Enjoyment as the Gift of God

Against that emptiness Solomon commends eating, drinking, and enjoying one’s labor as one’s heritage, and he calls both wealth and the power to enjoy it “the gift of God.” The person who receives these gifts is not consumed by the brevity of life, because “God keeps him busy with the joy of his heart.”
