Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 5
Main Idea:

Solomon calls worshipers to listen, keep their vows, and fear God, then shows that the love of money and lost riches leave a person empty-handed, and names the power to enjoy food, work, and wealth as the gift of God.

Setting:

Chapter 5 sits at the center of Ecclesiastes 4:1–6:12, Solomon’s look at life in society. After the oppression and fading fame of chapter 4, he turns from observing to commanding, starting in “the house of God,” most likely the temple in Jerusalem.

Chapter Outline:

  • Ecclesiastes 5:1–3 — Drawing Near to Hear in the House of God

  • Ecclesiastes 5:4–7 — Paying What You Vow to God

  • Ecclesiastes 5:8–9 — Oppression Among Layers of Officials

  • Ecclesiastes 5:10–12 — The Love of Money Never Satisfies

  • Ecclesiastes 5:13–17 — Riches Lost and Nothing Carried Away

  • Ecclesiastes 5:18–20 — Enjoyment as the Gift of God

Key Verse:

“As for every man to whom God has given riches and wealth, and given him power to eat of it, to receive his heritage and rejoice in his labor—this is the gift of God.” (Ecclesiastes 5:19)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Ecclesiastes 5 opens with a sudden change of voice. Through chapter 4 Solomon has been watching: the tears of the oppressed, envious work, lonely toil. Now he speaks directly to his hearers, and the first place he takes them is God’s house. There he tells them to “draw near to hear rather than to give the sacrifice of fools.” Worshipers who pour out sacrifices and words without listening do wrong without even knowing it. “For God is in heaven, and you on earth; Therefore let your words be few.” God’s greatness does not keep the worshiper away. It makes every word, and especially every vow, weigh something before Him. Solomon gathers the whole counsel into three words, “But fear God,” which call for a right view of who God is.

From there the chapter moves out into the province and the marketplace, and from verse 8 through verse 17 God is not named. This suggests what life looks like when it is measured only by officials, silver, and labor: it ends in empty hands. The rich man returns to the grave “naked,” and Solomon asks the book’s opening question again: “what profit has he who has labored for the wind?” Then God comes back into view as the Giver. The same ordinary act describes both lives. The man who labored for the wind “eats in darkness” all his days, while the one who receives from God eats and drinks “all the days of his life which God gives him.” What separates them is whether they receive their days, their goods, and even the power to enjoy them from His hand.

Chapter Sections

Ecclesiastes 5:1–3 — Drawing Near to Hear in the House of God

Solomon tells worshipers to come to God’s house ready to listen, because offerings from people who will not listen are “the sacrifice of fools.” Since God is in heaven and they are on earth, their words before Him should be few and weighed, and a proverb about dreams and much activity shows that a flood of words gives away the fool.

Ecclesiastes 5:4–7 — Paying What You Vow to God

No one had to make a vow, but once a vow was made to God it had to be paid without delay, for it is “Better not to vow than to vow and not pay.” Calling a broken promise “an error” before the messenger of God does not escape God Himself, and Solomon sums up all of verses 1–7 with the command “But fear God.”

Ecclesiastes 5:8–9 — Oppression Among Layers of Officials

Solomon tells his hearers not to be astonished when the poor are oppressed and justice is twisted in a province, because “high official watches over high official.” Whether those officials are checking one another or protecting one another, higher authority is no guarantee of justice; being unsurprised by oppression is not the same as approving it, and beneath the whole chain of power lies the land, for “even the king is served from the field.”

Ecclesiastes 5:10–12 — The Love of Money Never Satisfies

“He who loves silver will not be satisfied with silver”: the trouble lies in loving money, and that desire can never be filled. As goods increase, so do the people who consume them, and the rich man’s abundance often costs him the sweet sleep that the laboring man enjoys.

Ecclesiastes 5:13–17 — Riches Lost and Nothing Carried Away

With the formal words “There is a severe evil which I have seen under the sun,” Solomon describes riches hoarded to their owner’s harm, lost through misfortune, and leaving nothing for his son. Every person leaves this world as naked as he entered it, carrying nothing from his labor, and the man who “labored for the wind” spends even his present days in darkness, sorrow, and anger.

Ecclesiastes 5:18–20 — Enjoyment as the Gift of God

Against that emptiness Solomon commends eating, drinking, and enjoying one’s work as one’s “heritage,” and he calls both wealth and the power to enjoy it “the gift of God.” Joy from God keeps a person from being consumed by how brief life is, without asking him to pretend hardship is unreal, because “God keeps him busy with the joy of his heart.”

Why This Chapter Matters

Ecclesiastes 5 shows that worship begins with listening and truthful speech. God invites His people to draw near, yet because He is in heaven He takes their words seriously, and a promise made to Him cannot be brushed aside as a mistake.

The chapter also explains why money cannot satisfy. Wealth multiplies claims and worries, and death takes all of it away. The thirst Solomon exposes in verse 10 is met, in the wider witness of Scripture, by Christ, who offers water that ends thirst (John 4:13–14).

Finally, the chapter separates having from enjoying. Possessions and the power to enjoy them are both gifts from God, so wealth is neither evil in itself nor proof of His approval, and contentment rests on knowing the generous Giver. Chapter 6 opens with a man who has riches without that second gift (6:1–2).

Key Themes to Watch For

Reverent listening and honest words. Worship starts with hearing God, and words spoken before Him, especially vows, carry real weight (5:1–7).

Injustice among layers of authority. Solomon expects oppression in public life and refuses to treat higher officials as a guarantee of justice, without excusing the wrong (5:8–9; see 4:1).

The restless love of money. Desire for wealth grows with every increase and robs its lover of rest (5:10–12; compare 1:8; 4:8).

Empty hands at death. Hoarded riches can vanish, no one carries anything out of this world, and the book’s opening question about profit returns (5:13–17; see 1:3).

Enjoyment as the gift of God. Food, work, wealth, and the ability to enjoy them are received from God as a person’s heritage (5:18–20; see 6:1–2).

Key Words & Concepts

The house of God (5:1). Most likely the temple in Jerusalem, where Israel brought sacrifices and paid vows.

Vow (5:4–6). A voluntary promise made to God. No one had to vow, but a vow once made was binding. Solomon’s point is honesty before God; he is not commending a promise that would require doing what God forbids.

The messenger of God (5:6). Most likely a priest or another temple representative who received the vow; Malachi 2:7 describes the priest as God’s messenger. Some understand an angel instead. Either way, the excuse cannot escape God.

Fear God (5:7). A right view of God that shows itself in reverence, worship, trust, and obedience. Here it sums up the whole call to listen and to keep one’s word.

Vanity (5:7, 10). The NKJV word for a Hebrew term that pictures a breath or vapor, something that cannot be held. In Ecclesiastes it means meaningless: empty and futile when life is pursued without God. In verse 7 it describes piled-up dreams and words; in verse 10, the endless chase after more.

Heritage and the gift of God (5:18–19). A person’s God-given portion in life. In verse 19 both the riches and the “power to eat of it” come from God, so having and enjoying are two separate gifts.
