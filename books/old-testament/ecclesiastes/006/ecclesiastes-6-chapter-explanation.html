A man to whom God has given riches and wealth and honor begins the case with a man who has everything. Solomon names God as the giver of all of it.

so that he lacks nothing for himself of all he desires adds that every possession he could want is already his.

yet God does not give him power to eat of it, but a foreigner consumes it turns the case. The man cannot enjoy what he has, and an outsider ends up consuming it. This is the exact reverse of 5:19, where God gives a person riches and also the power to enjoy them. Abundance alone cannot produce the good life.

This is vanity, and it is an evil affliction gives Solomon’s verdict: the situation is empty and fleeting, a painful sickness. He attributes both the possessions and the withheld enjoyment to God, but he gives no explanation for why this particular man suffers. The text does not say the man is under special judgment, and it does not offer a diagnosis of his character.

Ecclesiastes 6:3–6 — A Stillborn Child Compared With an Unsatisfied Long Life

Ecclesiastes 6:3
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 6:3 If a man begets a hundred children and lives many years, so that the days of his years are many, but his soul is not satisfied with goodness, or indeed he has no burial, I say that a stillborn child is better than he"”(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 6:3 A man might have a hundred children and live to be very old. But if he finds no satisfaction in life and doesn’t even get a decent burial, it would have been better for him to be born dead. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon pushes the case to an extreme: a man might have a hundred children and a long life, both counted as rich blessings, yet if “his soul is not satisfied with goodness” and he does not even receive a burial, “a stillborn child is better than he.” The severe comparison measures the emptiness of a life without satisfaction; it is not a verdict on the worth of a child who dies before birth or on the parents who grieve that child.

Verse Explanation

Solomon pushes the case to an extreme: a man might have a hundred children and a long life, both counted as rich blessings, yet if “his soul is not satisfied with goodness” and he does not even receive a burial, “a stillborn child is better than he.” The severe comparison measures the emptiness of a life without satisfaction; it is not a verdict on the worth of a child who dies before birth or on the parents who grieve that child.

If a man begets a hundred children and lives many years, so that the days of his years are many pushes the case to an extreme. A hundred children and a long life were among the richest blessings a person could imagine in Israel (Psalm 127:3–5; Proverbs 10:27).

but his soul is not satisfied with goodness, or indeed he has no burial names what is missing. He never enjoys the good he has, and his life ends without even a burial, which in Israel was a mark of disgrace and abandonment (Jeremiah 22:18–19). The text does not explain why he is not buried; it simply adds this to the picture of a life that ends without honor.

I say that a stillborn child is better than he gives a severe verdict. The comparison measures how empty a life without satisfaction is. It is not a judgment on the worth of a child who dies before birth, and it is not a word against grieving parents. Many have known the sorrow of losing a child in pregnancy, and Solomon is not calling such a loss good or preferable. He uses the starkest comparison available to show that even the greatest earthly advantages cannot make up for a life that never knows satisfaction in goodness. Job and Jeremiah used similar language in their anguish (Job 3:11–16; Jeremiah 20:14–18).

None.

Ecclesiastes 6:7
Ecclesiastes 6:7
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 6:7 All the labor of man is for his mouth, And yet the soul is not satisfied.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 6:7 All people spend their lives scratching for food, but they never seem to have enough. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon turns from particular cases to a general truth: people work to feed themselves, “And yet the soul is not satisfied.” Here “soul” stands for a person’s inner appetite and longing, which no amount of labor finally fills; even after the body is fed, the deeper hunger returns.

Verse Explanation

Solomon turns from particular cases to a general truth: people work to feed themselves, “And yet the soul is not satisfied.” Here “soul” stands for a person’s inner appetite and longing, which no amount of labor finally fills; even after the body is fed, the deeper hunger returns.

All the labor of man is for his mouth turns from particular cases to a general truth: people work to feed themselves.

And yet the soul is not satisfied shows that the hunger remains. Here “soul” stands for a person’s inner appetite and longing. Even after the body is fed, the deeper hunger returns, and human labor never finally fills human desire.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “soul” (nephesh) has a wide range of meaning, including life, person, appetite, and throat, which suits a verse that moves from the mouth to the inner longing.

And he cannot contend with Him who is mightier than he points most likely to God. No one can successfully argue with God over the lot he has been given. The verse acknowledges human limits before God, but it does not make human choices meaningless or turn God’s sovereignty into fatalism.

Verse Explanation

The chapter ends with two questions that human wisdom cannot answer: “who knows what is good for man in life,” during days that pass “like a shadow,” and “Who can tell a man what will happen after him under the sun?” These remain real questions within Solomon’s search. The question of what is good prepares for the “better” sayings of chapter 7, and the book’s larger answer comes through God’s gifts and His final judgment (12:13–14).

For who knows what is good for man in life, all the days of his vain life which he passes like a shadow? asks what human wisdom cannot fully answer. Life passes like a shadow, brief and fleeting, and no one can say from within it what is truly good.

Who can tell a man what will happen after him under the sun? asks a second question. No one can see the future from within the limits of life under the sun. These remain real questions in Solomon’s search, but they do not mean God can reveal nothing about goodness or the future. The book’s larger answer comes through God’s gifts and His final judgment: “Fear God and keep His commandments, For this is man’s all” (12:13–14).

Study Notes
