Ecclesiastes 6 Chapter Outline

Chapter Focus

Ecclesiastes 6 completes the book’s reflection on toil and wealth (4:1–6:12) by pressing the contrast drawn in 5:18–20 between owning good things and being given the power to enjoy them: riches, a large family, and an extraordinarily long life all fail to satisfy when that gift is missing. The chapter then turns from these cases to restless human desire and closes with two unanswered questions about what is good and what comes after, exposing the limits of human knowledge just before the wisdom sayings of chapter 7.

Chapter Sections

1. Ecclesiastes 6:1–2 — Wealth Without the Power to Enjoy It

Solomon describes “an evil which I have seen under the sun,” one that is “common among men”: a man has received riches, wealth, and honor from God, yet “God does not give him power to eat of it, but a foreigner consumes it.” This case is the reverse of the gift described in 5:19 and shows that having possessions is not the same as being able to enjoy them.

2. Ecclesiastes 6:3–6 — A Stillborn Child Compared With an Unsatisfied Long Life

Solomon pushes the case to an extreme: a man may have a hundred children and many years, yet if his “soul is not satisfied with goodness” and he has no burial, “a stillborn child is better than he,” for the child “has more rest than that man.” Even if he lived “a thousand years twice” without seeing goodness, his life would end where every life ends: “Do not all go to one place?”

3. Ecclesiastes 6:7–9 — Desire That Labor Never Fills

“All the labor of man is for his mouth, And yet the soul is not satisfied,” and Solomon asks what advantage the wise man or the poor man who knows how to conduct himself really has. He commends “the sight of the eyes,” enjoying what is actually at hand, over “the wandering of desire,” while the closing words “This also is vanity and grasping for the wind” keep even that counsel within the limits of the search.

4. Ecclesiastes 6:10–12 — Human Limits and Unanswered Questions

Whatever a person is “has been named already,” and he “cannot contend with Him who is mightier than he”; adding more only increases vanity, and leaves the question, “How is man the better?” The chapter ends by asking “who knows what is good for man in life” and “Who can tell a man what will happen after him under the sun?”
