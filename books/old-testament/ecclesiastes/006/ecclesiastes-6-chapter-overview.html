Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 6
Main Idea:

Solomon shows that riches, a large family, and a very long life cannot satisfy when God does not also give the power to enjoy them, then exposes desire that labor never fills and closes with two unanswered questions about what is good for a person and what will happen under the sun after he is gone.

Setting:

Chapter 6 completes Ecclesiastes 4:1–6:12, Solomon’s look at life in society. It continues straight from 5:18–20, where God gives both wealth and the “power to eat of it,” and its closing questions prepare for the wisdom sayings of chapter 7.

Chapter Outline:

  • Ecclesiastes 6:1–2 — Wealth Without the Power to Enjoy It

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Ecclesiastes 6 picks up where chapter 5 left off. Solomon has just called both riches and the ability to enjoy them “the gift of God” (5:19). Now he describes the reverse, an evil he calls “common among men.” God has given a man riches, wealth, and honor, so that he lacks nothing, “yet God does not give him power to eat of it, but a foreigner consumes it” (verse 2). Solomon does not explain why this man suffers, and he does not blame him. He simply shows that having good things and being able to enjoy them are two different gifts.

Then he pushes the case as far as it will go. Imagine a man with a hundred children and “a thousand years twice” of life, whose “soul is not satisfied with goodness” (verses 3, 6). Solomon says “a stillborn child is better than he,” because the child “has more rest than that man” (verses 3, 5). The comparison is meant to shock. It measures how empty a full life can be without satisfaction, and it is not a verdict on the worth of a child or the grief of parents. Both the child and the man end in death: “Do not all go to one place?” (verse 6).

The rest of the chapter widens the point. “All the labor of man is for his mouth, And yet the soul is not satisfied” (verse 7). Solomon commends enjoying what is at hand over “the wandering of desire” (verse 9), yet he keeps even that counsel within the limits of his search. The chapter ends with no human answer to its two questions: what is good for a person, and what will happen under the sun after he is gone (verse 12).

Chapter Sections

Ecclesiastes 6:1–2 — Wealth Without the Power to Enjoy It

Solomon describes an evil that is common among people: a man receives riches, wealth, and honor from God, yet God does not give him power to enjoy them, and a stranger consumes them instead. The case is the exact reverse of the gift in 5:19, and Solomon calls it “vanity” and “an evil affliction” without explaining why this man suffers.

Ecclesiastes 6:3–6 — A Stillborn Child Compared With an Unsatisfied Long Life

If a man with a hundred children and many years is never satisfied with goodness and does not even receive a burial, Solomon judges that a stillborn child, spared that restless and deprived life, has more rest than he. Even two thousand years without seeing goodness would gain him nothing, and the severe comparison, which weighs earthly experience only, ends where every life ends: “Do not all go to one place?”

Ecclesiastes 6:7–9 — Desire That Labor Never Fills

People work to feed themselves, yet the deeper appetite is never filled, and neither the wise man nor the well-mannered poor man escapes it. Solomon commends “the sight of the eyes,” enjoying what is actually in front of a person, over restless craving, but he adds that this too is “vanity and grasping for the wind.”

Ecclesiastes 6:10–12 — Human Limits and Unanswered Questions

A person’s nature and limits have been set already, and he cannot contend with the One mightier than he, most likely God. Piling up more only adds to what is meaningless, and the chapter closes by asking who knows what is good for man in his brief life and who can tell him what will happen under the sun after he is gone.

Why This Chapter Matters

Ecclesiastes 6 shows that abundance cannot produce a good life on its own. Wealth, family, and long life are real blessings, but the power to enjoy them is a separate gift from God (5:19; 6:2). Contentment comes from receiving what He gives, not from acquiring more.

The chapter also speaks honestly about loss and dissatisfaction without explaining every case. It gives no reason for the unhappy rich man’s lot, and it does not teach that every unhappy person is under special judgment. Its comparison with the stillborn child should be read with compassion for grieving families.

Finally, the closing questions expose the limits of human knowledge. No one can find by searching what is good in this life or what will happen under the sun after a person is gone. The book’s answer comes through God’s gifts and His final judgment (12:13–14), and the wider witness of Scripture points to Christ, who offers water that ends thirst (John 4:13–14).

Key Themes to Watch For

Having without enjoying. Possessions and the power to enjoy them are two separate gifts from God (6:1–2; see 5:19).

Abundance that cannot satisfy. Many children and a very long life cannot make up for a soul that never finds satisfaction (6:3–6).

Desire that labor never fills. Work feeds the body, but the deeper appetite returns (6:7–9; compare 1:8; 5:10).

Contentment with what is at hand. Enjoying what is present is better than restless craving, though even this does not answer the search (6:9).

Human limits before God. No one can contend with the One mightier than himself or know what is good and what lies ahead (6:10–12).

Key Words & Concepts

Power to eat of it (6:2). The God-given ability to enjoy what one owns. The phrase echoes 5:19, where God gives this power along with riches.

Stillborn child (6:3–5). A child who dies before birth. Solomon uses the comparison to measure the emptiness of an unsatisfied life, not to judge the child’s worth or to describe the child’s condition after death.

Soul (6:3, 7). Here the inner appetite and longing of a person, which riches and labor never finally fill.

The wandering of desire (6:9). Restless craving that roams after what a person does not have, set against enjoying what is actually in front of him.

Him who is mightier (6:10). Most likely God. A person, as a creature, cannot successfully argue with Him over the lot he has been given.

Vanity (6:2, 4, 9, 11, 12). The NKJV word for a Hebrew term that pictures a breath or vapor, something that cannot be held. In Ecclesiastes it means meaningless: empty and futile when life is pursued without God. In verse 12 it also stresses how brief life is, passing “like a shadow.”
