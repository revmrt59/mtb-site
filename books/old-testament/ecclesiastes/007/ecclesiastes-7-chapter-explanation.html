Ecclesiastes 7:1
Ecclesiastes 7:1
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 7:1 A good name is better than precious ointment, And the day of death than the day of one's birth;(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 7:1 A good reputation is more valuable than costly perfume.And the day you die is better than the day you are born. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon begins a series of “better” sayings by valuing “a good name,” the lasting reputation of a person’s character, above “precious ointment,” the fragrant luxury of a feast. He adds that the day of death is better than the day of birth, because at death a life’s course is complete and its true worth can be seen, while a birth only begins that course.

Verse Explanation

Solomon begins a series of “better” sayings by valuing “a good name,” the lasting reputation of a person’s character, above “precious ointment,” the fragrant luxury of a feast. He adds that the day of death is better than the day of birth, because at death a life’s course is complete and its true worth can be seen, while a birth only begins that course.

A good name is better than precious ointment, begins a series of “better” comparisons that answer the question Solomon left open in 6:12, “who knows what is good for man in life.” A name stands for a person’s reputation and character as others know them. Precious ointment was perfumed oil used at feasts and on special occasions, a sign of luxury and celebration. Solomon values the lasting fragrance of a good character above the passing fragrance of expensive perfume.

And the day of death than the day of one’s birth; is a surprising comparison. A birth opens a life with all its possibilities still unknown, while death closes it, so that its true character and worth can finally be seen. The verse does not despise life or praise death for its own sake. It reflects on the fact that a life is weighed by how it ends, the same point Solomon makes in verse 8: “The end of a thing is better than its beginning.”

Study Notes

In Hebrew, “name” (shem) and “ointment” (shemen) sound almost alike, so the first line contains a play on words that links a good name with fine perfume.

Ecclesiastes 7:2

NLT

Ecclesiastes 7:2 Better to spend your time at funerals than at parties.After all, everyone dies —so the living should take this to heart. (NLT)

Main Idea

It is better to visit the house where people mourn than the house of feasting, because death “is the end of all men,” and the living who face it “will take it to heart.” Mourning teaches what celebration lets people forget: life is short and must be lived wisely.

Verse Explanation

It is better to visit the house where people mourn than the house of feasting, because death “is the end of all men,” and the living who face it “will take it to heart.” Mourning teaches what celebration lets people forget: life is short and must be lived wisely.

Better to go to the house of mourning Than to go to the house of feasting, contrasts two places a person might visit. The house of mourning is a home where someone has died and family and friends gather to grieve. The house of feasting is a place of celebration and plenty. Solomon says the first is the better visit.

For that is the end of all men; gives the reason. Death is the destination of every human life, and the house of mourning makes that fact impossible to ignore.

And the living will take it to heart. shows what mourning teaches. Those who stand beside a grave are moved to reflect on their own lives and to live with wisdom while they can. A feast can help people forget their mortality; a funeral reminds them of it.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

“Sorrow is better than laughter” because grief honestly faced can make the heart better, deepening understanding in a way that easy amusement cannot. The verse does not command constant sadness; it values the sober reflection that sorrow brings.

Sorrow is better than laughter, continues the comparisons. “Sorrow” here is grief or sadness, the kind that comes when people face loss honestly. “Laughter” is the light amusement that passes quickly.

For by a sad countenance the heart is made better. explains why. A sad face reflects a heart that is taking life seriously, and grief honestly faced can deepen understanding and soften the heart. The verse does not command constant sadness or condemn joy, which Solomon commends elsewhere as God’s gift (8:15; 9:7). It values the sober reflection that sorrow can produce.

The NLT renders the second line, “for sadness has a refining influence on us,” which brings out the idea that sorrow can improve character.

Ecclesiastes 7:16
Ecclesiastes 7:16
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 7:16 Do not be overly righteous, Nor be overly wise: Why should you destroy yourself?(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 7:16 So don’t be too good or too wise! Why destroy yourself? (NLT)

Main Idea

From that earthly horizon comes a calculated counsel: do not be “overly righteous” or “overly wise,” lest you destroy yourself. Solomon is voicing where expediency leads when visible outcomes are the only measure: avoid righteousness that costs too much.

Verse Explanation

From that earthly horizon comes a calculated counsel: do not be “overly righteous” or “overly wise,” lest you destroy yourself. Solomon is voicing where expediency leads when visible outcomes are the only measure: avoid righteousness that costs too much.

Do not be overly righteous, is the first part of a counsel that follows from verse 15. Solomon is best understood here as voicing the calculation that arises when visible outcomes are the only measure. If righteousness can lead to an early death, the expedient course is to avoid righteousness that costs too much.

Nor be overly wise: extends the same reasoning to wisdom. Too much commitment to wisdom might also bring trouble.

Why should you destroy yourself? states the motive of this counsel: self-preservation. Solomon is exposing where earthly calculation leads. It produces a morality measured only by personal safety. The passage does not teach that believers should hold back from obedience.

Study Notes

Some interpreters read verse 16 differently, as a warning against self-righteousness, legalism, or pride in one’s own wisdom. The Hebrew form translated “overly wise” can carry the sense of making oneself out to be wise. On either reading the passage does not commend moderation in obedience.

Verse Explanation

Still, Solomon applied his heart to search out wisdom and “the reason of things,” and to understand the wickedness of folly and madness. His search continued, and it now turns to what he found in his own life.

I applied my heart to know, resumes the account of Solomon’s search.

To search and seek out wisdom and the reason of things, describes its aim. He wanted to understand the explanation behind events.

To know the wickedness of folly, Even of foolishness and madness. adds another aim. He sought to understand folly and its destructive nature. This verse introduces what he found in the following verses.

Verse Explanation

Solomon finds “more bitter than death” the woman whose heart is snares and nets and whose hands are fetters, and he says that one who pleases God will escape her while the sinner is caught. Read against Solomon’s own compromised relationships (1 Kings 11:1–8), the verse records the snare he himself fell into.

And I find more bitter than death introduces a painful discovery, something worse than death itself.

The woman whose heart is snares and nets, Whose hands are fetters. describes a woman who entraps, using images of hunting and captivity. The passage is best read against Solomon’s own life: his many foreign wives turned his heart away from God (1 Kings 11:1–8). The king who sought satisfaction outside God’s will became caught in the snare he describes.

He who pleases God shall escape from her, shows the way of escape. The one who pleases God is kept from this trap.

But the sinner shall be trapped by her. states the opposite outcome. Solomon’s own history shows the truth of this warning.

Study Notes

First Kings 11:3 records that Solomon “had seven hundred wives, princesses, and three hundred concubines; and his wives turned away his heart.” Verse 4 adds the separate account of his later years: when he was old, his wives turned his heart after other gods.

Speaking of the Preacher in the third person, the book reports his method: he added one thing to another to find the reason. Solomon’s search was careful and deliberate, not careless.

Here is what I have found, introduces Solomon’s report of what his careful search turned up.

says the Preacher, is one of the book’s third-person references to the Preacher (1:1–2; 7:27; 12:8–10). These words frame Solomon’s report without signaling another author.

Adding one thing to the other to find out the reason, describes his method. He put observations together one by one, seeking the explanation of things.

Study Notes

This is the only place in the body of the book where the Preacher is referred to in the third person; the other references come in the opening and closing frame (1:1–2; 12:8–10).

Verse Explanation

What his soul still sought he could not find: he found one man among a thousand, but not a woman among them. Read against the seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines of 1 Kings 11:3, the verse reflects the failure of Solomon’s own search, not a verdict on women in general.

Which my soul still seeks but I cannot find: admits that his search remains incomplete.

One man among a thousand I have found, reports a rare discovery of one man among a thousand.

But a woman among all these I have not found. reports that he found no woman. The thousand most likely recalls Solomon’s own seven hundred wives and three hundred concubines (1 Kings 11:3), though Ecclesiastes does not say so outright. The verse exposes the failure of his own disobedient search; it is not a verdict on women in general or a statistic about men and women.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Solomon’s one sure finding is that “God made man upright, But they have sought out many schemes.” The problem lies not with God’s design but with all humanity, men and women alike, who have turned to their own devices.

Truly, this only I have found: introduces Solomon’s one sure conclusion.

That God made man upright, affirms that God created humanity good and morally straight.

But they have sought out many schemes. names the cause of human corruption. People have turned from God’s design to their own devices. This conclusion widens the diagnosis from Solomon’s own failure to all humanity, men and women alike.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “man” in verse 29 is the general term for humanity (adam), and the following “they” confirms that all people are in view.
