Ecclesiastes 8:1
Ecclesiastes 8:1
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 8:1 Who is like a wise man? And who knows the interpretation of a thing? A man's wisdom makes his face shine, And the sternness of his face is changed.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 8:1 How wonderful to be wise,to analyze and interpret things.Wisdom lights up a person’s face,softening its harshness. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon asks who is like the wise man who can interpret matters, and he answers that wisdom makes a person’s face shine and softens its sternness. Wisdom shows itself outwardly in a gracious bearing.

Verse Explanation

Solomon asks who is like the wise man who can interpret matters, and he answers that wisdom makes a person’s face shine and softens its sternness. Wisdom shows itself outwardly in a gracious bearing.

Who is like a wise man? asks a question that honors wisdom. Few people combine real understanding with sound judgment.

And who knows the interpretation of a thing? continues the praise. The wise person can explain what things mean, discerning the significance of events and words.

A man’s wisdom makes his face shine, describes wisdom’s outward effect. Wisdom shows in a person’s face, giving it brightness and graciousness.

And the sternness of his face is changed. completes the picture. Wisdom softens a hard or harsh expression. This prepares for the counsel that follows, since a gracious bearing matters greatly in the presence of a king.

Study Notes

Verse 1 works as a hinge between the reflections of chapter 7 and the counsel about the king that follows, praising wisdom just before Solomon applies it to life under a king’s authority.

Ecclesiastes 8:2
Ecclesiastes 8:2
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 8:2 I say, "Keep the king's commandment for the sake of your oath to God.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 8:2 Obey the king since you vowed to God that you would. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon counsels keeping “the king’s commandment for the sake of your oath to God.” Obedience to the king is tied to a solemn commitment made before God, which gives it moral weight.

Verse Explanation

Solomon counsels keeping “the king’s commandment for the sake of your oath to God.” Obedience to the king is tied to a solemn commitment made before God, which gives it moral weight.

I say, “Keep the king’s commandment gives direct counsel. A subject should obey the king’s orders.

for the sake of your oath to God. gives the reason. The obedience rests on a solemn commitment made before God, so loyalty to the king carries moral and religious weight. The text does not describe the oath further, and it should not be identified with any particular ceremony.

Study Notes

The Hebrew behind “for the sake of your oath to God” is literally “because of the oath of God,” a literal gloss that the KJV keeps almost word for word (“in regard of the oath of God”). It may refer to an oath of loyalty to the king sworn before God, as the NIV takes it (“because you took an oath before God”), or to God’s own oath concerning the king, as the ESV takes it (“because of God’s oath to him”). Either way, God stands behind the commitment.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

The one who keeps the king’s command will usually avoid harm, and the wise heart “discerns both time and judgment,” knowing when and how to act. This describes wisdom’s protective value under a king; it is not a promise that the obedient will never suffer.

He who keeps his command will experience nothing harmful; describes the usual result of wise obedience. The subject who keeps the king’s command generally avoids trouble. This describes wisdom’s protective value in this setting; it is not a guarantee that the obedient will never suffer, and it does not require obeying commands to do evil (compare Acts 5:29).

And a wise man’s heart discerns both time and judgment, adds that the wise know when and how to act. Discernment of the right moment and the right course is part of wisdom’s protection.

No one has power over the spirit to retain the spirit, declares that no one can hold back the breath of life when it departs.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “spirit” (ruach) can also mean wind. Some translations read the first line as no one having power to restrain the wind, which makes a similar point about human limits.

Ecclesiastes 8:10
Ecclesiastes 8:10
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 8:10 Then I saw the wicked buried, who had come and gone from the place of holiness, and they were forgotten in the city where they had so done. This also is vanity.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 8:10 I have seen wicked people buried with honor. Yet they were the very ones who frequented the Temple and are now praised in the same city where they committed their crimes! This, too, is meaningless. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon saw the wicked buried, people who had come and gone from “the place of holiness,” and they were forgotten in the city where they had acted. Religious association did not make them righteous, and Solomon calls this “vanity.”

Verse Explanation

Solomon saw the wicked buried, people who had come and gone from “the place of holiness,” and they were forgotten in the city where they had acted. Religious association did not make them righteous, and Solomon calls this “vanity.”

Then I saw the wicked buried, introduces a new scene. Solomon observes the funerals of wicked people.

who had come and gone from the place of holiness, describes these wicked people as regular visitors to the holy place, probably the temple. Their religious association did not make them righteous.

and they were forgotten in the city where they had so done. reports that they were soon forgotten in the city where they lived. The NKJV wording describes their memory fading.

This also is vanity. calls the whole scene meaningless. Wicked people moved in religious circles, died, and passed out of memory, and nothing seemed to distinguish their end.

Study Notes

The ESV and NIV read “praised” instead of “forgotten,” so that the wicked are honored in the city where they acted; the NLT displayed above reads the same way. The NKJV, KJV, and NASB read “forgotten.” This study follows the NKJV, and the wider argument about delayed justice is clear on either reading.

So Solomon “commended enjoyment,” because a person has nothing better under the sun than to eat, drink, and be merry. This joy will accompany him in his labor through “the days of his life which God gives him,” and it is received as God’s gift.

So I commended enjoyment, draws a conclusion from the injustice and limits he has observed. Rather than despair, he commends receiving joy.

because a man has nothing better under the sun than to eat, drink, and be merry; says the best thing within earthly life is to enjoy food, drink, and gladness. This is not the self-sufficient pleasure of one who leaves God out.

for this will remain with him in his labor all the days of his life which God gives him under the sun. explains that such joy accompanies a person through his work and through the days God gives. Life itself is God’s gift, and so is the joy within it. This contrasts with the rich fool in Jesus’ parable, who planned to eat, drink, and be merry while ignoring God (Luke 12:19–21).

Study Notes
