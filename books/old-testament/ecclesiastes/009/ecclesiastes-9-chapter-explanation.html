After reflecting on all this, Solomon declares that “the righteous and the wise and their works are in the hand of God.” Even so, no one can tell from present circumstances whether love or hatred awaits him.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of the last line is brief and can also be read to mean that people do not know whether love or hatred awaits them in the future. Both readings stress the limits of what people can know from circumstances.

“One event happens to the righteous and the wicked,” to the clean and unclean, the worshiper and the one who never sacrifices. Death comes to all alike, whatever their character or religious practice.

All things come alike to all: introduces the observation that the same outcome comes to everyone.

One event happens to the righteous and the wicked; names that outcome, death, which comes to righteous and wicked alike.

To the good, the clean, and the unclean; extends the list. Moral and ceremonial differences do not change the common end.

To him who sacrifices and him who does not sacrifice. adds religious practice. The worshiper and the one who neglects worship both die.

As is the good, so is the sinner; summarizes the point.

He who takes an oath as he who fears an oath. adds one more pair: the one who swears carelessly and the one who treats oaths with reverence. Death does not distinguish them. The righteous and the wicked remain morally different; they simply share the same physical death.

Ecclesiastes 9:5
Ecclesiastes 9:5
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 9:5 For the living know that they will die; But the dead know nothing, And they have no more reward, For the memory of them is forgotten.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 9:5 The living at least know they will die, but the dead know nothing. They have no further reward, nor are they remembered. (NLT)

Main Idea

The living know that they will die, but “the dead know nothing,” have no further reward, and are forgotten. Within the earthly horizon of Solomon’s examination, death ends a person’s knowledge of and part in life under the sun; it does not teach that the soul ceases to exist.

Verse Explanation

The living know that they will die, but “the dead know nothing,” have no further reward, and are forgotten. Within the earthly horizon of Solomon’s examination, death ends a person’s knowledge of and part in life under the sun; it does not teach that the soul ceases to exist.

For the living know that they will die; says the living at least know something: that death is coming. That knowledge can guide how they live.

But the dead know nothing, says the dead have no knowledge. This describes death within the earthly horizon of Solomon’s examination: the dead have no awareness of or part in life under the sun. Verse 6 makes that limit explicit. The verse does not teach that the soul sleeps or ceases to exist; the same book says that “the spirit will return to God who gave it” (12:7).

And they have no more reward, adds that the dead receive no further earthly wages or gain.

For the memory of them is forgotten. adds that even their memory fades among the living.

Study Notes

The Hebrew words for “reward” (sakar) and “memory” (zeker) sound alike, so the last two lines contain a play on words.

NLT

Ecclesiastes 9:7 So go ahead. Eat your food with joy, and drink your wine with a happy heart, for God approves of this! (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon says, “Go, eat your bread with joy, And drink your wine with a merry heart; For God has already accepted your works.” In this setting, the words encourage a person to receive ordinary life and faithful labor with assurance of God’s favor; they do not describe a way of earning salvation.

Verse Explanation

Solomon says, “Go, eat your bread with joy, And drink your wine with a merry heart; For God has already accepted your works.” In this setting, the words encourage a person to receive ordinary life and faithful labor with assurance of God’s favor; they do not describe a way of earning salvation.

Go, eat your bread with joy, turns from death to a call to enjoy life. Ordinary food is to be eaten with gladness.

And drink your wine with a merry heart; adds wine, a common sign of celebration, enjoyed with a glad heart.

For God has already accepted your works. gives the reason. Because God has accepted the works of those who fear Him, the reader can receive ordinary life and faithful work with gladness and with assurance of His favor, rather than suspecting that every enjoyment displeases Him. This does not describe a way of earning salvation, and it is not blanket approval of wickedness, since the book holds every work accountable to God (12:14).

Ecclesiastes 9:11–12 — Time and Chance

Ecclesiastes 9:11
Ecclesiastes 9:11
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 9:11 I returned and saw under the sun that"” The race is not to the swift, Nor the battle to the strong, Nor bread to the wise, Nor riches to men of understanding, Nor favor to men of skill; But time and chance happen to them all.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 9:11 I have observed something else under the sun. The fastest runner doesn’t always win the race, and the strongest warrior doesn’t always win the battle. The wise sometimes go hungry, and the skillful are not necessarily wealthy. And those who are educated don’t always lead successful lives. It is all decided by chance, by being in the right place at the right time. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon observes that the race does not always go to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, nor bread to the wise, nor riches to the discerning, nor favor to the skilled; “time and chance happen to them all.” Ability does not guarantee its expected reward, because unforeseen events can overturn human plans.

Verse Explanation

Solomon observes that the race does not always go to the swift, nor the battle to the strong, nor bread to the wise, nor riches to the discerning, nor favor to the skilled; “time and chance happen to them all.” Ability does not guarantee its expected reward, because unforeseen events can overturn human plans.

I returned and saw under the sun that introduces another observation.

The race is not to the swift, Nor the battle to the strong, observes that speed and strength do not always win.

Nor bread to the wise, Nor riches to men of understanding, Nor favor to men of skill; adds that wisdom, understanding, and skill do not guarantee food, wealth, or favor.

But time and chance happen to them all. gives the reason. Unforeseen events and circumstances affect everyone. This does not describe a universe outside God’s control, since verse 1 says the righteous and the wise are “in the hand of God.” It describes life as people experience it, where outcomes cannot be predicted. Proverbs describes the usual patterns of life, such as diligence leading to prosperity; Ecclesiastes adds that those patterns are not unconditional guarantees, and the two books complement each other.

Ecclesiastes 9:15
Ecclesiastes 9:15
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 9:15 Now there was found in it a poor wise man, and he by his wisdom delivered the city. Yet no one remembered that same poor man.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 9:15 A poor, wise man knew how to save the town, and so it was rescued. But afterward no one thought to thank him. (NLT)

Main Idea

A poor wise man was found in the city, and he delivered it by his wisdom, yet no one remembered him. His wisdom saved the city, but his poverty kept him from being honored.

Verse Explanation

A poor wise man was found in the city, and he delivered it by his wisdom, yet no one remembered him. His wisdom saved the city, but his poverty kept him from being honored.

Now there was found in it a poor wise man, introduces the hero, a man with no wealth or status but with wisdom.

and he by his wisdom delivered the city. says his wisdom saved the city. The NKJV states that he delivered it.

Yet no one remembered that same poor man. reports the sad end. Though he saved the city, he was forgotten because he was poor. The immediate lesson concerns wisdom that saves yet is despised, the point verse 16 draws out. Read alongside the rest of Scripture, the story also offers a secondary picture. The small city, helpless before a mighty enemy, can picture humanity in need of rescue, and the poor wise man can picture Christ, who became poor and whose saving wisdom was counted foolishness (2 Corinthians 8:9; 1 Corinthians 1:23–25). Through His death He broke the power of death and set free those who were held in bondage all their lives by the fear of death (Hebrews 2:14–15), delivering His people from sin and death. Like the poor man, He is often unremembered by the very people He came to deliver.

Study Notes

The NET reads the verb as a possibility: the poor man “could have delivered the city by his wisdom,” but no one listened to him. That reading ties the story closely to verse 16, where the poor man’s wisdom is despised. The NKJV states that he delivered the city, and this study follows that reading.
