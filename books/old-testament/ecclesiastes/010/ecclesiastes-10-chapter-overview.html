Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 10
Main Idea:

Solomon gathers wisdom sayings showing how a little folly spoils honor, how calm restraint helps under an angry ruler, how wisdom gives an edge in risky work, and how foolish words, careless leaders, and laziness bring ruin.

Setting:

Chapter 10 closes Ecclesiastes 9:1–10:20, the movement on the one event for all and on wisdom and folly. It picks up the last line of chapter 9, “one sinner destroys much good” (9:18), and shows what folly does in daily life and public life.

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Chapter 9 ended with a warning that one sinner can destroy much good. Ecclesiastes 10 opens with a picture of how that happens. “Dead flies putrefy the perfumer’s ointment,” and “So does a little folly to one respected for wisdom and honor” (verse 1). A reputation built over years can be spoiled by a little foolishness. The chapter that follows is a collection of short sayings, each showing folly at work in a different setting.

Some sayings concern life under rulers. When a ruler’s anger rises, the wise person stays at his post, “For conciliation pacifies great offenses” (verse 4). Solomon also sees folly raised to high places and servants riding while princes walk (verses 6–7). Other sayings concern ordinary work. Digging, quarrying, and splitting wood all carry risk, and a dull ax demands more strength, “But wisdom brings success” (verse 10). The wise person cannot remove every danger, but he works with forethought.

The chapter returns again and again to speech. “The words of a wise man’s mouth are gracious,” but the words of a fool “begin with foolishness,” and his talk ends in “raving madness” (verses 12–13). He multiplies words about a future no one can know. The closing sayings warn of the harm that comes to a land whose king is immature and whose princes feast in the morning, and they set beside it a land whose princes eat “For strength and not for drunkenness” (verse 17). The point is maturity and self-control, not age or rank alone. Laziness, too, lets a house leak, and the sayings end with a warning that even a curse whispered in a bedroom may be carried by “a bird of the air” (verse 20). Wisdom has real advantages, and folly, even in small amounts, does real damage.

Chapter Sections

Ecclesiastes 10:1–3 — A Little Folly Spoils Wisdom

As dead flies make perfume stink, a little folly can spoil the reputation of a person respected for wisdom and honor. The wise and the foolish differ in the direction of their hearts, and a fool shows his lack of wisdom in ordinary conduct.

Ecclesiastes 10:4–7 — Calm Before a Ruler and Honor Misplaced

When a ruler is angry, the wise person does not abandon his post, because a calm response can settle great offenses. Solomon also describes an error that comes from rulers: folly is set in great dignity while the rich sit in a lowly place, and servants ride on horses while princes walk on the ground, a picture of honor given to the wrong people.

Ecclesiastes 10:8–11 — Everyday Risks and the Advantage of Wisdom

Ordinary work carries risk, whether digging a pit, breaking through a wall, quarrying, or splitting wood, and a dull ax requires more strength while wisdom brings success. The NKJV compares a serpent that bites before it is charmed with the babbler, though some translations read the verse as a charmer who gains nothing when the snake has already bitten.

Ecclesiastes 10:12–15 — The Words and Labor of Fools

The words of the wise are gracious, but the fool is swallowed up by his own lips, and his talk moves from foolishness to raving madness. He piles up words about a future no one knows, and his labor wears him out because he cannot even find the way to the city.

Ecclesiastes 10:16–20 — Rulers, Laziness, Provision, and Careful Speech

Woe is pronounced on a land whose king is a child and whose princes feast in the morning, while a land is blessed when its king is the son of nobles and its princes eat at the proper time for strength, not drunkenness, a contrast that shows why maturity and self-control matter in leadership. Laziness lets a house decay, feasting and wine bring gladness while money supplies ordinary needs, and even private curses against the king or the rich may become known.

Why This Chapter Matters

Ecclesiastes 10 shows how much damage a little folly can do. A careless word, a moment of rashness, or a habit of laziness can spoil years of good character. Wisdom guards ordinary conduct, speech, and work, and the chapter calls believers to take small choices seriously.

The chapter also gives practical counsel for living under imperfect leaders. Calm restraint can prevent a conflict from growing, and careful speech protects against harm. This restraint is not agreement with wrongdoing, and it does not forbid truthful accountability or seeking protection from abuse.

Finally, the sayings show both the usefulness and the limits of wisdom and money. Wisdom gives real advantage, like a sharpened ax, and money meets everyday needs (10:19). The book has already shown that money cannot satisfy those who love it or rescue anyone from death (5:10–16). These sayings describe patterns of life, not guarantees.

Key Themes to Watch For

Folly’s outsized damage. A small amount of foolishness can spoil a good name (10:1; see 9:18).

Restraint under authority. Calm responses can quiet a ruler’s anger, even when rule is imperfect (10:4–7).

Wisdom in ordinary work. Work carries risks, but forethought and skill make effort more effective (10:8–11).

Gracious and foolish words. The words of the wise are gracious, while the fool’s talk ruins him (10:12–15, 20).

Responsible leadership and diligence. Self-indulgent leaders and laziness bring decay, while disciplined leaders bless the land (10:16–19).

Key Words & Concepts

Folly (10:1–3). A failure of judgment and conduct, not a lack of intelligence. The fool chooses a way that ignores wisdom and shows it in how he lives.

Right hand and left (10:2). Conventional images for the wise and foolish direction of the heart. They say nothing about which hand a person uses or about modern politics.

Conciliation (10:4). A calm, gentle response that can settle a serious offense and keep a dispute from growing.

The babbler (10:11). In the NKJV, the person whose careless talk does harm, compared with a serpent that bites before it is charmed. Other translations read the verse as a charmer who gains nothing after the snake has bitten.

Money answers everything (10:19). Most likely a practical observation that money supplies ordinary needs, including the feast and wine just mentioned. Some read it instead as the outlook of the indulgent rulers in verse 16. Either way, the book denies that money can satisfy or save (5:10–16).

A bird of the air (10:20). A picture of how words spoken in private can still become known.
