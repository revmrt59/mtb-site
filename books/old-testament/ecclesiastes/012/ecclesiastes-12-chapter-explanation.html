Chapter Study

Ecclesiastes 12

Verse-by-Verse
Ecclesiastes 12:1
Ecclesiastes 12:1
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:1 Remember now your Creator in the days of your youth, Before the difficult days come, And the years draw near when you say, "I have no pleasure in them":(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:1 Don’t let the excitement of youth cause you to forget your Creator. Honor him in your youth before you grow old and say, “Life is not pleasant anymore.” (NLT)

Main Idea

“Remember now your Creator in the days of your youth,” before the difficult days come when a person says, “I have no pleasure in them.” The call is to turn to God while life and strength remain, not to wait until age and hardship arrive.

Verse Explanation

“Remember now your Creator in the days of your youth,” before the difficult days come when a person says, “I have no pleasure in them.” The call is to turn to God while life and strength remain, not to wait until age and hardship arrive.

Remember now your Creator in the days of your youth, continues the address to the young from 11:9–10. To remember God is more than recalling Him; it means to keep Him in mind and to live in relationship with Him. Calling God “your Creator” reminds the young that their life comes from Him.

Before the difficult days come, gives the urgency. Hard days of old age and decline are coming.

And the years draw near when you say, “I have no pleasure in them”: describes those years. A time will come when life’s pleasures fade. The young should turn to God while life and strength remain, not wait until age and hardship arrive.

Study Notes

None.

Ecclesiastes 12:2
Ecclesiastes 12:2
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:2 While the sun and the light, The moon and the stars, Are not darkened, And the clouds do not return after the rain;(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:2 Remember him before the light of the sun, moon, and stars is dim to your old eyes, and rain clouds continually darken your sky. (NLT)

Main Idea

Remember Him before the sun, the light, the moon, and the stars grow dark and the clouds return after the rain. These images picture the gloom of old age, when brightness fades and troubles keep returning.

Verse Explanation

Remember Him before the sun, the light, the moon, and the stars grow dark and the clouds return after the rain. These images picture the gloom of old age, when brightness fades and troubles keep returning.

While the sun and the light, The moon and the stars, Are not darkened, pictures the dimming of life’s brightness. As age advances, the joys and clarity of life seem to darken.

And the clouds do not return after the rain; adds the picture of storms that keep returning. In old age troubles and weakness follow one after another without the relief of clear skies.

Study Notes

Some interpreters read the darkening lights as a picture of failing sight or of a coming calamity. Read within the whole poem, which begins with the call to remember before the difficult days of verse 1, the images describe old age and its approach to death.

Ecclesiastes 12:3
Ecclesiastes 12:3
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:3 In the day when the keepers of the house tremble, And the strong men bow down; When the grinders cease because they are few, And those that look through the windows grow dim;(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:3 Remember him before your legs — the guards of your house — start to tremble; and before your shoulders — the strong men — stoop. Remember him before your teeth — your few remaining servants — stop grinding; and before your eyes — the women looking through the windows — see dimly. (NLT)

Main Idea

In that day “the keepers of the house tremble” and “the strong men bow down,” the grinders cease because they are few, and those who look through the windows grow dim. These images picture the aging body, with the keepers suggesting trembling hands or arms, the strong men weakening legs, the grinders failing teeth, and the windows dimming eyes.

Verse Explanation

In that day “the keepers of the house tremble” and “the strong men bow down,” the grinders cease because they are few, and those who look through the windows grow dim. These images picture the aging body, with the keepers suggesting trembling hands or arms, the strong men weakening legs, the grinders failing teeth, and the windows dimming eyes.

In the day when the keepers of the house tremble, begins a picture of a household in decline. The images are best read as a picture of the aging body. The keepers of the house suggest the hands and arms, which begin to tremble.

And the strong men bow down; suggests the legs and the body’s support, which weaken and bend.

When the grinders cease because they are few, suggests the teeth. Grinders were the women who ground grain; when few remain, chewing becomes difficult.

And those that look through the windows grow dim; suggests the eyes, which grow dim with age.

Study Notes

These correspondences are suggestions rather than certainties. Some interpreters read the poem as a literal description of a great house falling into decay, or as a picture of a coming calamity. The overall picture of the aging body is clear even where individual images are debated.

Ecclesiastes 12:4
Ecclesiastes 12:4
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:4 When the doors are shut in the streets, And the sound of grinding is low; When one rises up at the sound of a bird, And all the daughters of music are brought low.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:4 Remember him before the door to life’s opportunities is closed and the sound of work fades. Now you rise at the first chirping of the birds, but then all their sounds will grow faint. (NLT)

Main Idea

The doors to the street are shut, the sound of grinding is low, one wakes at the sound of a bird, and all the daughters of music are brought low. The images suggest diminished hearing and speech, lighter sleep, and fading pleasure in sound and song.

Verse Explanation

The doors to the street are shut, the sound of grinding is low, one wakes at the sound of a bird, and all the daughters of music are brought low. The images suggest diminished hearing and speech, lighter sleep, and fading pleasure in sound and song.

When the doors are shut in the streets, suggests the closing off of life’s activity, perhaps failing hearing or lips that open less.

And the sound of grinding is low; suggests diminished hearing or the quieting of a once busy household.

When one rises up at the sound of a bird, suggests light sleep. Many older people wake easily, even at the chirp of a bird.

And all the daughters of music are brought low. suggests failing voice and hearing, so that song and music lose their pleasure. These are possible features of aging, not experiences shared by every older person; the poem gathers them into one picture.

Study Notes

None.

Ecclesiastes 12:5
Ecclesiastes 12:5
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:5 Also they are afraid of height, And of terrors in the way; When the almond tree blossoms, The grasshopper is a burden, And desire fails. For man goes to his eternal home, And the mourners go about the streets.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:5 Remember him before you become fearful of falling and worry about danger in the streets; before your hair turns white like an almond tree in bloom, and you drag along without energy like a dying grasshopper, and the caperberry no longer inspires sexual desire. Remember him before you near the grave, your everlasting home, when the mourners will weep at your funeral. (NLT)

Main Idea

People become afraid of heights and dangers along the way, the almond tree blossoms, “The grasshopper is a burden,” and desire fails, “For man goes to his eternal home, And the mourners go about the streets.” The white blossom suggests white hair, even something as light as a grasshopper weighs on the frail, and the verse ends with fading desire, death, and mourning.

Verse Explanation

People become afraid of heights and dangers along the way, the almond tree blossoms, “The grasshopper is a burden,” and desire fails, “For man goes to his eternal home, And the mourners go about the streets.” The white blossom suggests white hair, even something as light as a grasshopper weighs on the frail, and the verse ends with fading desire, death, and mourning.

Also they are afraid of height, And of terrors in the way; may describe the fear of falling and the anxiety about going out that can come with age.

When the almond tree blossoms, suggests white hair. The almond tree blooms with white blossoms.

The grasshopper is a burden, pictures frailty. Even something as light as a grasshopper weighs on a frail person, so that small burdens feel heavy.

And desire fails. pictures appetite and desire fading, as they often do with age.

For man goes to his eternal home, names the end of the process: death and burial, the passage from this life to one’s lasting home. The phrase describes death from the earthly side, as a departure from which no one returns to this life. It does not give a full account of what follows death; verse 7 adds that the spirit returns to God who gave it.

And the mourners go about the streets. pictures the funeral, with mourners gathering in the streets.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “desire” may refer to the caper berry, which was used to stimulate appetite. The image would then be that even such a stimulant no longer works.

Some translations take the grasshopper line differently. The ESV reads, “the grasshopper drags itself along,” picturing stiff and slow movement, and the NIV reads similarly.

Ecclesiastes 12:6–7 — The Silver Cord Loosed and the Spirit’s Return

Ecclesiastes 12:6
Ecclesiastes 12:6
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:6 Remember your Creator before the silver cord is loosed, Or the golden bowl is broken, Or the pitcher shattered at the fountain, Or the wheel broken at the well.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:6 Yes, remember your Creator now while you are young, before the silver cord of life snaps and the golden bowl is broken. Don’t wait until the water jar is smashed at the spring and the pulley is broken at the well. (NLT)

Main Idea

Remember your Creator before the silver cord is loosed, the golden bowl is broken, the pitcher is shattered at the fountain, and the wheel is broken at the well. These images picture the final collapse of what sustains life.

Verse Explanation

Remember your Creator before the silver cord is loosed, the golden bowl is broken, the pitcher is shattered at the fountain, and the wheel is broken at the well. These images picture the final collapse of what sustains life.

Remember your Creator before the silver cord is loosed, resumes the call to remember. A silver cord might hold a lamp.

Or the golden bowl is broken, pictures a precious lamp bowl breaking, so its light goes out.

Or the pitcher shattered at the fountain, pictures a water jar breaking at the spring.

Or the wheel broken at the well. pictures the wheel used to draw water breaking. Together these images portray the final collapse of what sustains life. Some suggest the cord pictures the spinal cord, the bowl the head, the pitcher the lungs, and the wheel the heart. These are comparisons that may illustrate the picture, not established medical meanings.

Study Notes

None.

Ecclesiastes 12:7
Ecclesiastes 12:7
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:7 Then the dust will return to the earth as it was, And the spirit will return to God who gave it.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:7 For then the dust will return to the earth, and the spirit will return to God who gave it. (NLT)

Main Idea

“Then the dust will return to the earth as it was, And the spirit will return to God who gave it.” At death the body returns to dust and the spirit returns to God, its Giver, before whom every life must stand.

Verse Explanation

“Then the dust will return to the earth as it was, And the spirit will return to God who gave it.” At death the body returns to dust and the spirit returns to God, its Giver, before whom every life must stand.

Then the dust will return to the earth as it was, describes the body’s return to the ground from which it was formed (Genesis 3:19).

And the spirit will return to God who gave it. says the spirit returns to God, who gave it. Death is not the end of a person’s relationship with God. The spirit stands before its Giver, and the book soon declares that God will judge every work (12:14). The verse does not teach that every spirit is saved; fuller teaching on life after death and resurrection comes elsewhere in Scripture (Philippians 1:23; 1 Corinthians 15:20–26).

Study Notes

The wording echoes Genesis 2:7, where God formed man of the dust of the ground and breathed into him the breath of life.

Ecclesiastes 12:8 — The Refrain Closes the Search

Ecclesiastes 12:8
Ecclesiastes 12:8
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:8 "Vanity of vanities," says the Preacher, "All is vanity."(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:8 “Everything is meaningless,” says the Teacher, “completely meaningless.” (NLT)

Main Idea

“Vanity of vanities,” says the Preacher, “All is vanity.” The refrain that opened the search in 1:2 now closes it, summing up life measured only under the sun as meaningless.

Verse Explanation

“Vanity of vanities,” says the Preacher, “All is vanity.” The refrain that opened the search in 1:2 now closes it, summing up life measured only under the sun as meaningless.

“Vanity of vanities,” says the Preacher, repeats the words that opened the search in 1:2. The words “says the Preacher” are one of the book’s third-person references to Solomon (1:1–2; 7:27; 12:8–10).

“All is vanity.” completes the refrain. Measured only under the sun, all of life is meaningless and fleeting. This verdict closes Solomon’s search, but it is not the book’s last word. The conclusion that follows in 12:13–14 gives the answer.

Study Notes

“Vanity of vanities” is a Hebrew way of expressing the superlative, meaning the utmost vanity, much as the book title Song of Songs means the greatest song.

Ecclesiastes 12:9–10 — The Preacher’s Truthful Teaching

Ecclesiastes 12:9
Ecclesiastes 12:9
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:9 And moreover, because the Preacher was wise, he still taught the people knowledge; yes, he pondered and sought out and set in order many proverbs.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:9 Keep this in mind: The Teacher was considered wise, and he taught the people everything he knew. He listened carefully to many proverbs, studying and classifying them. (NLT)

Main Idea

The closing voice says that because the Preacher was wise, he taught the people knowledge, pondering, searching out, and arranging many proverbs. Solomon’s search served as careful teaching for others.

Verse Explanation

The closing voice says that because the Preacher was wise, he taught the people knowledge, pondering, searching out, and arranging many proverbs. Solomon’s search served as careful teaching for others.

And moreover, because the Preacher was wise, begins the closing frame. The book now speaks about the Preacher, commending his wisdom.

he still taught the people knowledge; says he taught others, not merely reflecting for himself.

yes, he pondered and sought out and set in order many proverbs. describes his careful work. He weighed, studied, and arranged many wise sayings. This fits Solomon, who spoke three thousand proverbs (1 Kings 4:32).

Study Notes

The book speaks of the Preacher in the third person at its opening (1:1–2), once within its body (7:27), and here in its closing frame (12:8–10). This framing presents the Preacher’s work and does not point to a second author.

Ecclesiastes 12:11–12 — Wisdom Given by One Shepherd

Study Notes

None.

Ecclesiastes 12:11
Ecclesiastes 12:11
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:11 The words of the wise are like goads, and the words of scholars are like well-driven nails, given by one Shepherd.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:11 The words of the wise are like cattle prods — painful but helpful. Their collected sayings are like a nail-studded stick with which a shepherd drives the sheep. (NLT)

Main Idea

“The words of the wise are like goads,” prodding people in the right direction, and the words of scholars are like well-driven nails that hold firm, “given by one Shepherd.” The one Shepherd is God, the source of true wisdom.

Verse Explanation

“The words of the wise are like goads,” prodding people in the right direction, and the words of scholars are like well-driven nails that hold firm, “given by one Shepherd.” The one Shepherd is God, the source of true wisdom.

The words of the wise are like goads, compares wise words to goads, sharp sticks used to prod oxen in the right direction. Wise words can sting, but they move people where they should go.

and the words of scholars are like well-driven nails, compares the collected sayings of the wise to nails driven firmly. They fix truth securely and hold it in place.

given by one Shepherd. names the source of these words. The one Shepherd is best understood as God, the source of true wisdom. Scripture calls God the Shepherd of His people (Genesis 49:24; Psalm 23), a title Jesus takes up as the Good Shepherd (John 10:11).

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase translated “the words of scholars” is literally “masters of collections,” a literal gloss; the KJV renders it “masters of assemblies.” It probably refers to collected sayings or to those who gathered them, and the ESV renders it “the collected sayings.”

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:12 But, my child, let me give you some further advice: Be careful, for writing books is endless, and much study wears you out. (NLT)

Main Idea

Addressing the reader as “my son,” the closing voice warns that “Of making many books there is no end, and much study is wearisome to the flesh.” Endless study cannot replace the wisdom God has given.

Verse Explanation

Addressing the reader as “my son,” the closing voice warns that “Of making many books there is no end, and much study is wearisome to the flesh.” Endless study cannot replace the wisdom God has given.

And further, my son, be admonished by these. addresses the reader as “my son,” as in Proverbs. The reader is urged to heed the words of the wise just described.

Of making many books there is no end, warns that writing and producing books could go on forever.

and much study is wearisome to the flesh. adds that endless study exhausts the body. The warning keeps learning in its place, subordinate to the wisdom God gives. It does not condemn careful study, but it warns that endless books cannot replace the words given by the one Shepherd.

Ecclesiastes 12:13–14 — The Conclusion of the Whole Matter

Study Notes

None.

Ecclesiastes 12:13
Ecclesiastes 12:13
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:13 Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God and keep His commandments, For this is man's all.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:13 That’s the whole story. Here now is my final conclusion: Fear God and obey his commands, for this is everyone’s duty. (NLT)

Main Idea

“Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God and keep His commandments, For this is man’s all.” After all the searching, the answer is reverence for God and obedience to Him, which is the whole duty and meaning of human life.

Verse Explanation

“Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God and keep His commandments, For this is man’s all.” After all the searching, the answer is reverence for God and obedience to Him, which is the whole duty and meaning of human life.

Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: introduces the final answer to all the searching. After everything, here is what matters.

Fear God and keep His commandments, states the conclusion. To fear God is to have a right view of Him, expressed in reverence, worship, trust, and obedience. Keeping His commandments is the fruit of that reverence.

For this is man’s all. says this is the whole of what it means to be human. Life finds its meaning and purpose in relationship with God and obedience to Him.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of the last line is very brief; a literal gloss is “for this is all the man.” The ESV renders it “this is the whole duty of man,” and the NKJV “this is man’s all.”

Ecclesiastes 12:14
Ecclesiastes 12:14
NKJV

Ecclesiastes 12:14 For God will bring every work into judgment, Including every secret thing, Whether good or evil.(NKJV)

NLT

Ecclesiastes 12:14 God will judge us for everything we do, including every secret thing, whether good or bad. (NLT)

Main Idea

“For God will bring every work into judgment, Including every secret thing, Whether good or evil.” Because God will judge everything, even what is hidden, every act matters, and life lived before Him is not meaningless.

Verse Explanation

“For God will bring every work into judgment, Including every secret thing, Whether good or evil.” Because God will judge everything, even what is hidden, every act matters, and life lived before Him is not meaningless.

For God will bring every work into judgment, gives the reason for the conclusion. God will judge everything people do.

Including every secret thing, adds that God’s judgment reaches even hidden deeds and motives.

Whether good or evil. says both good and evil will be judged. Because God will judge every work, nothing done before Him is meaningless. His judgment answers what earthly observation could never settle. The New Testament reveals that Christ will be the Judge (John 5:22–29).
