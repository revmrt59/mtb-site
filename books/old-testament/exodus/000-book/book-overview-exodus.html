Book Overview

At a Glance

Exodus
Author

Moses, according to long Jewish and Christian tradition. Exodus records that Moses wrote down parts of what it contains (17:14; 24:4; 34:27), and Jesus refers to “the book of Moses, in the burning bush passage” (Mark 12:26).

Audience

Israel, the people God brought out of Egypt, and the generations after them, whose children were to ask, “What do you mean by this service?” (12:26).

Date

The events are dated either to the mid-1400s BC, following 1 Kings 6:1, or to the 1200s BC; the question is debated. Exodus never names its pharaohs. The passages it says Moses wrote belong to the wilderness years at Rephidim and Sinai.

Historical Setting

Egypt, where Jacob’s descendants have multiplied and been forced into slavery under a king “who did not know Joseph” (1:8), then the wilderness journey to Mount Sinai.

Purpose

To show the LORD redeeming Israel from slavery, bringing them to Himself in covenant, and making Himself known as He comes to dwell among His people.

Central Message

The LORD hears His people’s cry and remembers His covenant. By His mighty hand and the blood of the Passover lamb He redeems Israel from bondage and brings them to Himself as His own people. At Sinai He gives His covenant. After Israel rebels, Moses intercedes, and the LORD spares the people, reveals His mercy, and renews the covenant. At the end His glory fills the tabernacle, and He dwells among them.

Key Verse

“And they shall know that I am the LORD their God, who brought them up out of the land of Egypt, that I may dwell among them. I am the LORD their God” (29:46).

Key Themes

God’s presence with His people; knowing the LORD; His name; from bondage to service; the firstborn; testing and provision; holiness; remembering; intercession; willing hearts.

Book Flow

  • I. Israel Enslaved and Moses Preserved (Exodus 1:1–2:25)

  • II. God Calls Moses and Reveals His Name (Exodus 3:1–7:7)

  • III. The Plagues on Egypt (Exodus 7:8–11:10)

  • IV. Passover and the Crossing of the Sea (Exodus 12:1–15:21)

  • V. From the Sea to Sinai (Exodus 15:22–18:27)

  • VI. The Covenant at Sinai (Exodus 19:1–24:18)

  • VII. The Pattern of the Tabernacle (Exodus 25:1–31:18)

  • VIII. The Golden Calf and the Covenant Renewed (Exodus 32:1–34:35)

  • IX. The Tabernacle Built and Filled with Glory (Exodus 35:1–40:38)

Go Deeper

Background

Author

Exodus does not give its writer’s name in a heading, but it tells us that Moses wrote. After the battle with Amalek the LORD tells him, “Write this for a memorial in the book” (17:14). At Sinai “Moses wrote all the words of the LORD” (24:4), and when the LORD renews the covenant He again tells Moses, “Write these words” (34:27). Jewish and Christian tradition has long received the book as the work of Moses, and Jesus Himself, quoting God’s words at the bush, asks, “Have you not read in the book of Moses?” (Mark 12:26).

Moses was uniquely placed to write it. Raised in Pharaoh’s household, he “was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians” (Acts 7:22), and he was God’s chosen servant at the heart of Israel’s deliverance from Egypt and the covenant at Sinai. Yet the book does not flatter him. It records his killing of the Egyptian (2:12), his pleading for God to send someone else (4:13), and his anger at the foot of the mountain (32:19). The man through whom God spoke is shown as a servant God made faithful, and the glory belongs to the LORD.

Audience

Exodus does not say who first heard the finished book, but its natural first audience is the generation that had lived it: the people who groaned in Egypt, crossed the sea, and stood trembling at the foot of Sinai. They needed to know who had rescued them, what He required of them, and how a holy God could live among a people like them.

Exodus also has later readers built into it. The LORD expects children who were not there to ask, “What do you mean by this service?” (12:24–27), and parents are to answer in the first person: “This is done because of what the LORD did for me when I came up from Egypt” (13:8). The book is written to be retold, so that every generation of God’s people hears the deliverance as its own story.

Date

Exodus gives no date for its events and never names the kings of Egypt. Two main views exist. One follows 1 Kings 6:1, which places the exodus 480 years before Solomon began the temple, pointing to the middle of the 1400s BC. The other draws on the city of Raamses (1:11) and historical arguments for a date in the 1200s BC. Faithful readers hold both views. Whichever date is right, the passages Exodus says Moses wrote belong to the wilderness years after the exodus, at Rephidim and at Sinai (17:14; 24:4; 34:27). The book’s attention is fixed on the LORD and what He did, not on the name of the pharaoh who opposed Him.

Historical Setting

Exodus picks up where Genesis ends. Jacob’s family settled in Egypt in Joseph’s day. Exodus counts seventy descendants of Jacob, including Joseph, who was already there (1:5; compare Genesis 46:26–27), and in Egypt they “were fruitful and increased abundantly” until “the land was filled with them” (1:7). God had told Abraham centuries earlier that his descendants would be strangers in a foreign land, would serve and be afflicted, and would afterward “come out with great possessions” (Genesis 15:13–14). The opening chapters of Exodus show that word coming to pass.

Egypt was a great and wealthy power, watered by the Nile. The new pharaoh fears Israel’s numbers, sets taskmasters over them, and makes them build “supply cities, Pithom and Raamses” (1:11). When forced labor does not stop their growth, he orders every Hebrew son thrown into the river (1:22). Pharaoh has armies, taskmasters, and the power of life and death over Israel’s children, yet he will learn that he cannot stand against the LORD, who executes judgment on him and on “all the gods of Egypt” (12:12).

Moses later flees east to Midian, a desert region where he lives with Jethro, a priest, and tends his flocks. After the exodus, Israel travels through the wilderness to “the mountain of God” (3:1), called both Horeb and Sinai, where they remain for the rest of the book. Its exact location is debated.

Occasion

Exodus does not state an occasion for the whole book, but it shows why preserving Israel’s experience mattered. The LORD commands Moses to write the victory over Amalek “for a memorial in the book” (17:14), and He builds memory into Israel’s life through the Passover, the consecration of the firstborn, and the questions children will ask (12:24–27; 13:14–16). A people who forget who redeemed them will soon serve other gods, as the golden calf shows while Moses is still on the mountain. The book also gives Israel the covenant they had entered and the pattern of worship God had shown Moses, which they would need for every step of the journey still ahead.

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Exodus is about the LORD rescuing a people and coming to live among them. It opens with Israel crushed under slavery and crying out, and with four plain statements about God: He “heard their groaning,” He “remembered His covenant,” He “looked upon the children of Israel,” and He “acknowledged them” (2:24–25). Everything that follows flows from those verbs. At the bush God tells Moses, “I have surely seen the oppression of My people,” and adds, “So I have come down to deliver them” (3:7–8).

The first half of the book tells how God brings Israel out. He confronts Pharaoh through Moses and Aaron, judges Egypt with plagues, shelters Israel under the blood of the Passover lamb, and parts the sea. On the far shore Israel sings a great song of praise to the LORD (15:1–18). Then comes the wilderness, where God provides bread from heaven and water from the rock for a people who complain at every hardship.

The second half tells how God brings Israel to Himself. At Sinai He makes a covenant with them, speaks the Ten Commandments, and gives laws for their worship, justice, and daily life (20–23). Then He gives Moses detailed instructions for a sanctuary, because His purpose is to live in their midst (25–31). Israel’s worship of the golden calf nearly ends everything, but Moses intercedes, God proclaims His name, and the covenant is renewed (32–34). The book closes as the people build the tabernacle exactly as commanded and the glory of the LORD fills it (35–40).

Exodus contains several kinds of writing: narrative, poetry, law, and long passages of building instructions. The instructions are repeated almost word for word when the tabernacle is built. That repetition is part of the message. It shows a people who once worshiped a calf now doing everything “as the LORD had commanded” (39:43).

Why This Book Matters

In Exodus God tells His people His name. At the bush He says, “I AM WHO I AM” (3:14), and calls Himself “the LORD,” adding, “This is My name forever” (3:15). After Israel’s rebellion He proclaims that name again: “The LORD, the LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abounding in goodness and truth” (34:6). These words became Israel’s confession of who God is. Psalm 103 sings, “The LORD is merciful and gracious” (Psalm 103:8), and Jonah repeats the same description when he complains that God spared Nineveh (Jonah 4:2). Exodus 34:6 supplies the very words later Scripture uses to describe the heart of God.

The exodus also became the defining memory of God’s people. The Ten Commandments begin, “I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage” (20:2), and the prophets keep returning to it: “Out of Egypt I called My son” (Hosea 11:1). That order matters. God rescued Israel before He gave them commandments. Obedience was their response to His redeeming love, never the way they earned it.

Above all, Exodus reveals that God wants to live with His people. “Let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them” (25:8). The great danger after the golden calf is not hardship but the loss of His presence, and Moses refuses to go on without Him (33:15). The book ends with that purpose fulfilled: “the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle” (40:34). This desire of God runs from the garden to the end of Scripture, and Exodus is where it first takes visible shape among a whole people.

Book Flow

I. Israel Enslaved and Moses Preserved (Exodus 1:1–2:25)

Jacob’s descendants multiply in Egypt until a new king “who did not know Joseph” (1:8) treats their growth as a threat and turns it into slavery. His orders to kill Israel’s sons are resisted by the midwives, who “feared God” (1:17), and by Moses’ mother, his sister, and Pharaoh’s own daughter, who draws the child from the river. Moses’ attempt to defend his people by his own hand ends in flight to Midian. The movement ends with a turning point: God hears Israel’s groaning and remembers “His covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob” (2:24).

II. God Calls Moses and Reveals His Name (Exodus 3:1–7:7)

At Horeb the Angel of the LORD appears in a bush that burns but “was not consumed” (3:2), and the LORD calls Moses to bring His people out. Moses raises objection after objection, and God answers with His presence, “I will certainly be with you” (3:12), and with His name, “I AM WHO I AM” (3:14). Pharaoh answers, “Who is the LORD, that I should obey His voice” (5:2), and makes Israel’s burdens heavier. God renews His promise, “I will take you as My people, and I will be your God” (6:7), and sends Moses and Aaron back to Pharaoh (7:1–7).

III. The Plagues on Egypt (Exodus 7:8–11:10)

Through a series of signs and judgments, from water turned to blood to three days of darkness, the LORD shows Egypt and Israel that He is the LORD. Pharaoh’s magicians admit, “This is the finger of God” (8:19), and Pharaoh himself confesses, “I have sinned” (9:27), yet he hardens his heart again and again once the pressure lifts. The LORD has a purpose in it: that His name “may be declared in all the earth” (9:16). The movement ends with the last plague announced and Pharaoh still refusing to let Israel go.

IV. Passover and the Crossing of the Sea (Exodus 12:1–15:21)

Israel’s households prepare a lamb without blemish, with small households sharing one with their neighbors (12:3–5), and put its blood on the doorposts. God promises, “when I see the blood, I will pass over you” (12:13). At midnight the LORD strikes Egypt’s firstborn, and Israel goes out on a night to be remembered throughout their generations. At the sea, trapped between the water and Pharaoh’s army, they hear, “Stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD” (14:13), and walk through on dry ground. On the far shore Israel sings, “The LORD shall reign forever and ever” (15:18), and Miriam leads the women in reply.

V. From the Sea to Sinai (Exodus 15:22–18:27)

Three days later the water is bitter, and the journey to Sinai becomes a school of trust. The LORD makes the water sweet and calls Himself “the LORD who heals you” (15:26), rains bread from heaven and teaches the Sabbath through it, and brings water from the rock, though the people ask, “Is the LORD among us or not?” (17:7). He gives victory over Amalek while Aaron and Hur hold up Moses’ hands. Jethro hears all that God has done, confesses that the LORD is “greater than all the gods” (18:11), and helps Moses share the work of judging.

VI. The Covenant at Sinai (Exodus 19:1–24:18)

This movement is a hinge of the book. God tells Israel, “I bore you on eagles’ wings and brought you to Myself” (19:4), and calls them to be “a kingdom of priests and a holy nation” (19:6). He descends on the mountain in fire and thunder and speaks the Ten Commandments, then gives the laws of the Book of the Covenant. The people answer, “All that the LORD has said we will do” (24:7), the covenant is sealed with blood, and Israel’s leaders eat and drink in God’s presence before Moses enters the cloud for forty days.

VII. The Pattern of the Tabernacle (Exodus 25:1–31:18)

On the mountain the LORD gives His purpose in a single sentence: “let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them” (25:8). He shows Moses the pattern of the ark and mercy seat, the table, the lampstand, the tabernacle, and the altar, and He appoints Aaron and his sons as priests. The center of the movement states the goal of the whole book: “I will dwell among the children of Israel and will be their God” (29:45). The instructions close with Bezalel filled with God’s Spirit for the work, the Sabbath as a sign, and the tablets written “with the finger of God” (31:18).

VIII. The Golden Calf and the Covenant Renewed (Exodus 32:1–34:35)

While Moses is still on the mountain, the people make a golden calf and call it the god who brought them out of Egypt (32:4). Moses breaks the tablets, judgment falls, and the question becomes whether God will still go with them at all. Moses pleads, even asking to be blotted out of God’s book for their sake (32:32), and says, “If Your Presence does not go with us, do not bring us up from here” (33:15). The LORD makes His goodness pass before Moses and proclaims His name, the covenant is renewed and its words written on new tablets, and Moses comes down with his face shining.

IX. The Tabernacle Built and Filled with Glory (Exodus 35:1–40:38)

The people bring their offerings so willingly that they must be told to stop (36:6–7), and Bezalel, Aholiab, and the skilled workers make everything “according to all that the LORD had commanded Moses” (39:42). Moses raises the tabernacle, and the book reaches its goal: “the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle” (40:34). The God who once appeared to Moses in a bush now lives among His people, leading them by cloud and fire “throughout all their journeys” (40:38).

Key Themes to Watch For

God’s presence. From the promise at the bush to the glory in the tabernacle, the book moves toward God living among His people (3:12; 13:21–22; 25:8; 33:14–16; 40:34–38).

Knowing the LORD. God acts so that Egypt and Israel “shall know that I am the LORD” (6:7; 7:5; 10:2; 14:18; 29:46; 31:13).

The name of the LORD. God reveals His name at the bush and proclaims it after the golden calf (3:14–15; 6:2–3; 33:19; 34:5–7).

From bondage to service. Israel is set free from serving Pharaoh to serve the LORD (1:13–14; 4:23; 6:6; 20:2).

The firstborn. Israel is God’s firstborn son; Egypt’s firstborn fall, and Israel’s firstborn belong to the LORD (4:22–23; 12:29; 13:2, 11–15).

Testing and provision. Hardship reveals Israel’s hearts, and God keeps providing (15:24–26; 16:4; 17:1–7).

Holiness. God is “glorious in holiness” and calls His people to be set apart for Him (3:5; 15:11; 19:6; 28:36; 31:13).

Remembering. Feasts, signs, and questions keep the deliverance alive in every generation (12:14, 26–27; 13:8–9, 14; 17:14).

Intercession. Moses stands between God and the people and pleads for them (17:11–12; 32:11–14, 30–32; 33:12–17; 34:9).

Willing hearts. The sanctuary is built from what the people give freely (25:2; 35:5, 21–29; 36:5–7).

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

The LORD and “I AM.” When the NKJV prints “LORD” in small capitals, it stands for God’s personal name, which He reveals to Moses at the bush. God answers Moses’ question about His name with “I AM WHO I AM” (3:14) and calls Himself “the LORD God of your fathers” (3:15). The name speaks of the God who is, who does not change, and who is present with His people, as He had just promised: “I will certainly be with you” (3:12). How the name is formed in Hebrew is debated, but its meaning in Exodus is clear from what God says and does.

Pharaoh. Pharaoh is a title for the king of Egypt, not a personal name. Exodus mentions more than one pharaoh (1:8; 2:23) and names none of them.

Signs, wonders, and plagues. The plagues are both signs that reveal the LORD and judgments on Egypt. God says that on Passover night, “against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgment” (12:12). Many readers connect particular plagues with particular Egyptian gods. Those links are illustrative; the book’s own claim is that no power Egypt trusted could stand before the LORD.

Passover and Unleavened Bread. The Passover is the meal of a lamb whose blood was put on the doorposts the night God struck Egypt’s firstborn and passed over Israel’s houses (12:1–13). It is joined to seven days of eating bread without yeast, remembering the haste of the departure (12:14–20, 34, 39; 13:3–10). Israel was to keep both every year.

Covenant. A covenant is a binding relationship established by solemn promises. At Sinai the LORD, who has already redeemed Israel, binds Himself to them as their God and them to Himself as His people (19:5–6). Moses writes down the laws God gives (20:22–23:33; 24:4) and reads them to the people from “the Book of the Covenant” (24:7), and the covenant is sealed with “the blood of the covenant” (24:8).

The Ten Commandments. God speaks them from the mountain (20:1–17) and writes them on two tablets of stone (31:18; 34:28). Christians have numbered them in more than one way. The Reformed numbering, used here, counts the command against carved images separately (20:4–6) and treats coveting as one command (20:17).

The tabernacle. The tabernacle is a portable tent-sanctuary set in a fenced courtyard. Inside the courtyard stand the bronze altar and the laver for washing. The tent itself has two rooms. The holy place holds the table of showbread, the lampstand, and the altar of incense. Behind the veil, the Most Holy holds the ark of the Testimony, covered by the mercy seat, where God promises, “there I will meet with you” (25:22). The NKJV also uses “tabernacle of meeting” both for this sanctuary and, before it was built, for Moses’ tent outside the camp (33:7).

Priests. Aaron and his sons are set apart to serve as priests (28:1). They offer sacrifices, tend the lamps, burn incense, and represent the people before God. Aaron’s garments carry the names of Israel’s tribes on his shoulders and over his heart (28:12, 29).

Glory and the cloud. God’s glory is the visible weight and brightness of His presence. The LORD leads Israel in a pillar of cloud and fire (13:21–22), and His glory appears in the cloud in the wilderness (16:10), on the mountain (24:16–17), and finally in the tabernacle (40:34–35).

The Angel of the LORD. At the bush “the Angel of the LORD” appears (3:2), yet the One who speaks from the bush is the LORD Himself, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (3:4–6). Christ and Life below explains how this Angel is understood. At the sea the Angel of God, who had gone before Israel, moves behind the camp along with the pillar of cloud (14:19–20), and God promises an Angel who bears His name to lead them (23:20–21).

The Sabbath. The seventh day is set apart as holy rest. Israel learns it through the manna before Sinai (16:22–30), receives it as a commandment rooted in creation (20:8–11), and is given it as a sign “that I am the LORD who sanctifies you” (31:13).

Names for the same person or place. Moses’ father-in-law is called both Reuel (2:18) and Jethro (3:1). The mountain of God is called both Horeb (3:1) and Sinai (19:11).

Key people. Aaron is Moses’ older brother and spokesman, later the first high priest, who also makes the golden calf (4:14–16; 32:1–6). Miriam, their sister, is the prophetess who leads the women in song (15:20–21). Joshua leads Israel against Amalek and stays at the tent where Moses meets God (17:9–14; 33:11). Bezalel and Aholiab lead the craftsmen who build the tabernacle (31:1–6).

Christ and Life

At the center of Israel’s deliverance stands a lamb. The households of Israel, sharing where a household was too small (12:3–4), chose a lamb “without blemish” (12:5), sheltered under its blood, and ate it ready to leave. Not a bone of it was to be broken (12:46). The New Testament sees Jesus in that night. John the Baptist calls Him “The Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!” (John 1:29), John notes that at the cross “Not one of His bones shall be broken” (John 19:36), and Paul writes, “Christ, our Passover, was sacrificed for us” (1 Corinthians 5:7). Jesus gave the Lord’s Supper at a Passover meal, calling the cup “My blood of the new covenant” (Matthew 26:28), words that echo Moses at Sinai: “This is the blood of the covenant” (24:8). Those who trust Him are sheltered by His blood and fed for the journey out of bondage.

Christ is present in Exodus before the cross is ever in view. The Angel of the LORD who speaks from the burning bush as the LORD Himself (3:2–6) is best read as an appearance of the Son before His incarnation. When Jesus says, “before Abraham was, I AM” (John 8:58), He takes up the very name God gave at the bush. Paul says that Israel was “baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea” (1 Corinthians 10:2) and drank from “that spiritual Rock that followed them, and that Rock was Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4). Jesus says that His Father gives “the true bread from heaven” (John 6:32), and then says, “I am the bread of life” (John 6:35). The God who fed Israel in the wilderness gives Himself to His people in His Son.

The tabernacle points to Christ as a whole and in its parts. Hebrews calls it “the copy and shadow of the heavenly things” (Hebrews 8:5) and says that Christ has entered “heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God for us” (Hebrews 9:24). He is the way into God’s presence and the sacrifice at the altar. He is the bread and the light of the holy place, and He has opened a way “through the veil, that is, His flesh” (Hebrews 10:20), so that believers have “boldness to enter the Holiest by the blood of Jesus” (Hebrews 10:19). Aaron carried Israel’s names on his shoulders and over his heart before the LORD (28:12, 29). Jesus, our High Priest, carries His people in the same way: He “always lives to make intercession for them” (Hebrews 7:25).

What the tabernacle pictures, believers share in union with Christ. Israel was called to be “a kingdom of priests and a holy nation” (19:6), and Peter applies those words to the church, “a royal priesthood, a holy nation” (1 Peter 2:9), built up as “a spiritual house, a holy priesthood” (1 Peter 2:5). In Christ God’s people come near, are cleansed, receive light and nourishment, offer prayer and worship, and carry others before the Father.

Moses himself foreshadows Christ. He asked to be blotted out of God’s book if God would not forgive the people (32:32), and his face shone after he spoke with God (34:29). Moses was “faithful in all His house as a servant,” but Christ is faithful “as a Son over His own house” (Hebrews 3:5–6). The Son did what Moses could only offer: He gave Himself for His people. Paul adds that believers, “beholding as in a mirror the glory of the Lord, are being transformed into the same image” (2 Corinthians 3:18). The glory that made Moses veil his face now changes those who look to Jesus.

Exodus ends with God dwelling among His people, and the whole Bible ends the same way. John writes that “the Word became flesh and dwelt among us, and we beheld His glory” (John 1:14), and the final vision of Scripture announces, “Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them” (Revelation 21:3). As God draws His people into His presence, He also invites them to share His rest (Exodus 33:14; Hebrews 4:9–10). The Sabbath rest God gave Israel also finds its center in Jesus, the “Lord of the Sabbath” (Mark 2:28), who says, “Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest” (Matthew 11:28). Exodus gives believers a pattern of weekly rest in Him: ceasing from ordinary work to remember His deliverance and enjoy His presence, with freedom about which day is set apart (Romans 14:5–6).

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Exodus is real history. God truly delivered Israel from Egypt. Through that history He also gives a picture of what He does in Jesus. Begin with what happened, then follow the connections Scripture itself makes.

God redeemed Israel before He gave the commandments (20:2). The laws are the way of life for a rescued people, not the price of their rescue. Read them through Jesus, who brings their intent to bear on the heart (Matthew 5:17–28).

Pharaoh hardens his own heart (8:15, 32; 9:34), and the LORD also hardens it (9:12; 14:4). Both are stated, and Pharaoh remains responsible for refusing God under repeated warning. The same sun that softens wax hardens clay: God’s pressure on a rebellious heart can harden it further as the person refuses to yield.

The long tabernacle chapters and their repetition are not filler. They show God’s pattern faithfully carried out, and they bring the book to its goal.

Exodus does not caricature Israel. They suffered real bondage, and their failures in the wilderness are written as examples for us, not so that we can feel superior: “all these things happened to them as examples” (1 Corinthians 10:11).

Some laws regulate practices in Israel’s ancient setting. “Eye for eye” (21:24) is a rule of proportionate justice for judges, not permission for personal revenge (compare Matthew 5:38–39). The laws on servants limit and regulate an existing practice, and kidnapping a person to sell him is a capital crime (21:16). The Levites’ judgment after the golden calf (32:25–29) was a specific act at Moses’ command, not a pattern for God’s people.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

Naming the pharaoh. Because Exodus does not name the pharaoh of the oppression or of the exodus, any identification depends on which date is preferred (see Date above) and remains a proposal.

The 430 years. Exodus says Israel’s sojourn in Egypt was 430 years (12:40–41). Paul says the law came 430 years after the promise (Galatians 3:17), and two ancient witnesses to the text of Exodus, the Greek translation known as the Septuagint and the Samaritan Pentateuch, count the time in Canaan as well. Different ways of counting are offered; the length of Israel’s stay in Egypt is debated.

The Red Sea. The Hebrew name usually translated “Red Sea” may also mean Sea of Reeds. The exact place of the crossing and the location of Mount Sinai are debated. Exodus presents the crossing as a real miracle: the LORD drove the sea back, and the waters were “a wall to them on their right hand and on their left” (14:22).

What Exodus 6:3 means. God says that “by My name LORD I was not known” to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (6:3). Genesis uses the name in their stories, so this most likely means they had not known Him in the full meaning of that name, which He now displays by redeeming Israel.

Seeing God. Moses and the elders “saw the God of Israel” and ate and drank (24:10–11), yet God later says, “no man shall see Me, and live” (33:20). Both are true. They saw a real but limited vision of His glory, not His full face. John writes that “No one has seen God at any time” and that the Son “has declared Him” (John 1:18).

“The LORD relented.” After Moses’ intercession, “the LORD relented from the harm which He said He would do” (32:14). God truly responds to prayer, and His character does not change (34:6–7). Exodus holds both truths without letting go of either.

Plundering the Egyptians. Israel asked the Egyptians for silver, gold, and clothing, and “the LORD had given the people favor” so that they gave freely (12:35–36). God was providing for His people and giving some recompense for their years of slavery. The account is not theft and is no precedent for taking from others.

The night encounter. On the way back to Egypt the LORD confronts someone at the encampment, most likely Moses, and the danger passes when Zipporah circumcises their son (4:24–26). The passage is brief and difficult, and the details are uncertain, but it shows that the covenant sign mattered even in the household of the man called to lead Israel.
