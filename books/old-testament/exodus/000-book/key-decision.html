Exodus Key Questions

Important questions in the text, and how MTB understands them.

Book Questions

Did Moses write Exodus?

Exodus does not name an author for the whole book, but it says Moses wrote particular things at the LORD’s command: the record of the battle with Amalek (17:14), “all the words of the LORD” (24:4), and the words of the covenant (34:27). Jesus quotes the burning bush passage from “the book of Moses” (Mark 12:26). On these grounds MTB holds that Moses wrote Exodus.

Many modern scholars hold that the book reached its present form later. MTB does not follow that view, while recognizing that the passages above name particular things Moses wrote rather than every word of the book.

Read: Exodus 17:14, Exodus 24:4, Exodus 34:27, Mark 12:26

When did the exodus happen, and who was Pharaoh?

Exodus never names the king of Egypt. Bible readers hold two main dates for the exodus. The earlier date rests especially on 1 Kings 6:1, which counts 480 years from the exodus to the start of Solomon’s temple. The later date draws on the city of Rameses that Israel built (1:11) and on historical arguments from Egypt.

MTB does not name any pharaoh as fact. The date matters for history, but the meaning of the book does not depend on settling it.

Read: Exodus 1:11, 1 Kings 6:1

Is Exodus history or a picture of salvation?

Both. God truly delivered Israel from Egypt and brought them to Himself. Through that real history He also gives a picture of what He does in Jesus. The New Testament draws these lines itself: Christ is our Passover (1 Corinthians 5:7), Israel was “baptized into Moses” (1 Corinthians 10:2), Jesus is the true bread from heaven (John 6:32–35), and the Rock was Christ (1 Corinthians 10:4).

The picture never replaces the history, and the history is more than an illustration. Paul says these things happened to Israel as examples and were written to instruct us (1 Corinthians 10:11).

Read: 1 Corinthians 5:7, 1 Corinthians 10:1–11, John 6:32–35

Was each plague aimed at a particular Egyptian god?

The LORD says, “against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgment” (12:12). Taken together, the plagues show that Egypt’s gods could not stand against Him.

Many teachers connect individual plagues with individual Egyptian deities. MTB treats those links as possible illustrations, to be checked before use, not as claims the text itself makes.

Read: Exodus 12:12

Who hardened Pharaoh’s heart? (Exodus 4–14)

Exodus says both. Pharaoh hardens his own heart (8:15, 32; 9:34), and the LORD announces and carries out His own hardening (4:21; 9:12; 14:4). MTB holds both together and stresses that Pharaoh remains responsible. He is commanded again and again, he is able to respond, and he refuses.

Pressing a rebellious heart can harden it further. The same sun hardens clay and softens wax. Alongside that picture, Exodus also states God’s own judgment and purpose, “that My name may be declared in all the earth” (9:16). Paul cites this in Romans 9:17–18; wider questions about election belong to the study of Romans.

Read: Exodus 4:21, Exodus 9:12–16, Exodus 9:34, Exodus 14:4, Romans 9:17–18

Who is the Angel of the LORD? (Exodus 3:2; 14:19; 23:20–23)

At the burning bush “the Angel of the LORD” appears in the flame (3:2), and then the speaker is named as the LORD, the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (3:4–6). MTB reads this Angel as a Christophany, an appearance of Christ before He became man. Jesus’ words “before Abraham was, I AM” (John 8:58) bring His identity into view beside Exodus 3:14.

The Angel of God who moves behind Israel at the sea (14:19) and the Angel in whom God’s name rests (23:20–23) can be read the same way, though with less certainty than at the bush. The angel offered in 33:2 needs care: there the LORD says He will not go among the people Himself (33:3), and Moses refuses to go on without the LORD’s own presence (33:14–16).

Other interpreters understand the Angel as a created messenger who speaks with God’s full authority.

Read: Exodus 3:2–6, Exodus 14:19, Exodus 23:20–23, Exodus 33:1–16, John 8:58

How far does the tabernacle point to Christ? (Exodus 25–31; 35–40)

MTB reads the whole tabernacle as pointing to Jesus. Hebrews calls it a copy and shadow of the heavenly sanctuary (Hebrews 8:5), and the New Testament presents Christ as our sacrifice, our High Priest, and the way through the veil (Hebrews 9:11–14; 10:19–22). In Him His people receive cleansing, light, nourishment, fellowship, and access to God, and in union with Him they share in what the sanctuary pictures.

Each item is explained first in its own setting: what it was and what it did. Readings of colors, metals, fabrics, and measurements are used only where the Bible gives a solid basis for them.

Read: Exodus 25:8–9, Exodus 25:40, Hebrews 8:5, Hebrews 9:11–14, Hebrews 10:19–22

How do Christians relate to the law given at Sinai? (Exodus 19–24)

God rescued Israel before He gave the commandments: “I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt” (20:2). Obedience answers redemption. It never earned it, for Israel or for us.

MTB follows the historic Reformed understanding, which sees three uses of the law. It is a mirror that exposes sin and leads us to Christ (Romans 3:20; Galatians 3:24). It restrains evil in society (1 Timothy 1:9–10). And for believers, who are no longer condemned (Romans 8:1), it is a guide to a life that pleases God, written on the heart by His Spirit (Jeremiah 31:33; Ezekiel 36:26–27).

Reformed teaching also sees three kinds of law in the books of Moses. The moral law, summed up in the Ten Commandments, reflects God’s unchanging character and still binds all people. Believers keep it out of love and by the Spirit’s power, not to earn salvation (Romans 13:8–10). The civil laws governed Israel as a nation. Those specific laws ended with that nation, but their underlying principles reflect the eternal character and goodness of God and still give wisdom (1 Corinthians 9:8–10). The ceremonial laws of sacrifice, feasts, and cleanness were shadows pointing to Christ and our walk with Him, and He has fulfilled them (Hebrews 10:1; Colossians 2:16–17).

Other Christians see it differently. Some hold that the law of Moses stands as one whole, fulfilled in Christ, and that believers now live under “the law of Christ” (1 Corinthians 9:21; Galatians 6:2), keeping the moral commands as the New Testament restates them. Both views agree that we are saved by grace and that Jesus carries God’s commands into the heart (Matthew 5:17–28).

Read: Exodus 20:1–17, Matthew 5:17–28, Ephesians 6:1–3, Romans 3:20, Galatians 3:24, Jeremiah 31:33, 1 Corinthians 9:21 · Related: Death penalties today?

Must Christians keep a Sabbath, and on which day?

The Sabbath runs through Exodus. Israel learns it through the manna before Sinai (16:22–30). God commands it (20:8–11), gives it as a sign that He makes Israel holy (31:12–17), and repeats it before the tabernacle work begins (35:1–3). It rests on God’s own holy rest at creation, before sin, Israel, or the law (Genesis 2:2–3).

MTB understands the Sabbath as part of God’s moral law, the fourth commandment: “Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy” (20:8). Believers do not keep it to be saved, yet neglecting it slowly starves and exhausts the soul and leads to spiritual dryness and burnout. Its heart is a holy time set apart to be with the Lord, kept as a delight rather than a burden (Isaiah 58:13–14).

Jesus is Lord of the Sabbath. He did not abolish it. He revealed its true purpose, using it for mercy, healing, and restoration (Mark 2:27–3:5), and He gives the rest it points to (Matthew 11:28–30). “There remains therefore a rest for the people of God” (Hebrews 4:9). MTB teaches a serious, intentional weekly rhythm of ceasing from ordinary work to be with Jesus, remember His deliverance, worship, and receive His rest. Which day is set apart is a matter of freedom, and believers are not to judge one another over it (Romans 14:5–6; Colossians 2:16–17).

Christians hold different views. Some require a specific day, and others hold that the command is fulfilled in Christ with no weekly obligation. The penalties attached to the Sabbath in Exodus (31:14–15; 35:2) belonged to Israel’s covenant setting.

Read: Exodus 16:22–30, Exodus 20:8–11, Exodus 31:12–17, Mark 2:27–28, Romans 14:5–6, Isaiah 58:13–14, Mark 2:27–3:5, Hebrews 4:9, Colossians 2:16–17

Chapter Questions

Grouped by the Major Movements of the approved Book Outline. Chapters without key questions are not listed. Questions that run across many chapters are under Book Questions: Pharaoh’s hardening, the Angel of the LORD, the tabernacle, the law, the Sabbath.

I. Israel Enslaved and Moses Preserved (Exodus 1:1–2:25)

Exodus 1 · 1 question

Did God reward the midwives for lying? (vv. 15–21)

Shiphrah and Puah “feared God” and let the boys live, and God dealt well with them (vv. 17, 20–21). When Pharaoh questioned them, they gave him an answer meant to protect the children (v. 19). Scripture commends their fear of God and their refusal to kill, a clear case of obeying God rather than a ruler who commands evil (Acts 5:29).

The passage does not set out a general rule about deception. Its moral weight rests on their allegiance to God and their protection of life.

Read: Exodus 1:15–21, Acts 5:29

Exodus 2 · 1 question

Was Moses right to kill the Egyptian? (vv. 11–15)

Exodus records the killing without commending it. Moses looks this way and that, hides the body, is challenged by a fellow Hebrew, and flees when Pharaoh hears of it (vv. 12–15). Stephen explains that Moses expected his people to understand that God would deliver them by his hand, but they did not (Acts 7:23–29).

Moses’ concern for his people was real, but God had not yet sent him. The commission comes at the burning bush, after his own first attempt has ended in rejection and flight (3:10).

Read: Exodus 2:11–15, Acts 7:23–29

II. God Calls Moses and Reveals His Name (Exodus 3:1–7:7)

Exodus 3 · 2 questions

What does “I AM WHO I AM” mean? (v. 14)

When Moses asks God’s name, God answers, “I AM WHO I AM,” and gives the name the LORD as “My name forever” (vv. 14–15). First, it is God’s declaration that He is self-existent. He was not made, depends on no one, and does not change.

The name also invites the question, “I am what?” God answers through the rest of Exodus by what He is for His people. He is “the LORD who heals you” (15:26). He provides bread from heaven and water from the rock (16:4; 17:6). He is The-LORD-Is-My-Banner (17:15). His first promise to Moses, “I will certainly be with you” (v. 12), stands behind them all.

Jesus takes up the same name: “before Abraham was, I AM” (John 8:58). He also fills it out: “I am the bread of life,” “I am the light of the world,” “I am the good shepherd” (John 6:35; 8:12; 10:11).

Read: Exodus 3:12–15, John 8:58, Exodus 15:26, Exodus 17:15, John 6:35 · Related: Who is the Angel of the LORD?

Did Israel steal from the Egyptians? (vv. 21–22)

The NKJV says Israel would “plunder the Egyptians,” but the account describes asking and giving. Israel asked their neighbors for silver, gold, and clothing, and the Egyptians gave because the LORD gave Israel favor (12:35–36). God had promised Abraham that his descendants would leave with great possessions (Genesis 15:13–14).

MTB reads this as God’s provision for the journey and, most likely, a partial repayment for years of forced labor. It is not theft, and it gives no precedent for taking from others.

Read: Exodus 3:21–22, Exodus 12:35–36, Genesis 15:13–14

Exodus 4 · 1 question

Why did the LORD seek to kill Moses on the way? (vv. 24–26)

Moses had failed to obey God’s command to circumcise his son, and God took that failure seriously. On the way to Egypt, “the LORD met him and sought to kill him” (v. 24). MTB understands this as a sudden, life-threatening illness. Zipporah recognized the cause and circumcised their son, and God released Moses to continue his journey (vv. 25–26).

For Abraham’s descendants, circumcision was not optional. It was the sign of God’s covenant, and an uncircumcised male was to be cut off from his people (Genesis 17:10–14). The man sent to lead Israel under that covenant could not ignore it in his own home. God can discipline His own servants, even severely, and public ministry does not excuse disobedience in what may seem a small command.

Why the circumcision was delayed is not recorded. Possibly Zipporah had recently given birth to their second son, Eliezer (18:4), and the eighth day arrived on the journey. Moses may have neglected it while busy with his mission, or he may have given in to Zipporah, who was not an Israelite and seems from her words to have resisted the practice (v. 25). Scripture does not say which. What is clear is that Moses had failed to carry out God’s command.

Read: Exodus 4:24–26, Genesis 17:9–14

Exodus 6 · 1 question

Had the patriarchs never heard the name LORD? (v. 3)

God says He appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as God Almighty, “but by My name LORD I was not known to them” (v. 3). Yet Genesis uses the name LORD in their stories (Genesis 15:7; 28:13).

MTB’s preferred reading is that the fathers knew the name but had not yet experienced its full meaning. Israel would come to know the LORD through what He was about to do: redeem them, take them as His people, and bring them into the land (vv. 6–8).

Read: Exodus 6:2–8, Genesis 15:7, Genesis 28:13

IV. Passover and the Crossing of the Sea (Exodus 12:1–15:21)

Exodus 12 · 3 questions

Was the death of Egypt’s firstborn just? (vv. 29–30)

Yes. Exodus does not hide the weight of this night: “there was a great cry in Egypt” (v. 30). But the judgment was neither sudden nor arbitrary. Before the first plague, God had told Pharaoh, “Israel is My son, My firstborn,” and warned him, “if you refuse to let him go, indeed I will kill your son, your firstborn” (4:22–23). Pharaoh held God’s firstborn in bondage and, after nine judgments, still refused.

The same night, God gave shelter under the blood of the lamb to every household that trusted His word (vv. 12–13). Matthew later applies “Out of Egypt I called My Son” (Hosea 11:1) to Jesus (Matthew 2:15).

Read: Exodus 12:12–13, Exodus 12:29–30, Exodus 4:22–23, Matthew 2:15

Was Israel in Egypt for 430 years? (vv. 40–41)

The NKJV says the sojourn of the children of Israel “who lived in Egypt” was 430 years (v. 40). Other passages seem to count the years differently, and two main views result.

The longer view takes the 430 years as the time spent in Egypt itself. God told Abraham that his descendants would be afflicted in a foreign land “four hundred years” (Genesis 15:13; Acts 7:6), a rounded number for the same span.

The shorter view counts the 430 years from God’s promise to Abraham, so that about half the time was spent in Canaan and about half in Egypt. Paul counts 430 years from the promise to the giving of the law (Galatians 3:17). The ancient Greek and Samaritan copies of verse 40 include the time “in the land of Canaan.” Supporters also point to the short family line from Levi to Kohath, Amram, and Moses (6:16–20) and to God’s word that Israel would return “in the fourth generation” (Genesis 15:16).

MTB takes the 430 years as the NKJV gives them. Either way, verse 41 stresses that the LORD brought Israel out “on that very same day,” exactly as He had appointed.

Read: Exodus 12:40–41, Galatians 3:17, Genesis 15:13–16, Exodus 6:16–20

Were there really 600,000 men? (v. 37)

Yes. Exodus counts “about six hundred thousand men on foot, besides children” (v. 37). The later census gives 603,550 (38:26), and Numbers repeats the total (Numbers 1:46; 11:21). With women and children, Israel was a very large people, which fits God’s promise to multiply Abraham’s descendants and Pharaoh’s fear of their numbers (1:7–10).

Some propose that the Hebrew word for “thousand” here means a clan or military unit, which would make the numbers much smaller. MTB holds the numbers as written. The repeated, detailed counts read as real totals, and the size of the people shows the scale of God’s deliverance and provision in the wilderness.

Read: Exodus 12:37, Exodus 38:26, Numbers 1:46

Exodus 14 · 1 question

Did Israel cross the Red Sea or a “Sea of Reeds”? (vv. 21–22)

The Hebrew name usually translated “Red Sea” may mean “Sea of Reeds.” Where exactly Israel crossed, and where Mount Sinai stands, are both debated.

Whatever the location, the crossing was a real miracle. The LORD drove the sea back, Israel went through “on dry ground,” and the waters returned on the army that pursued them (vv. 21–22, 27–29). The location question does not reduce the miracle.

Read: Exodus 14:21–31 · Related: Who is the Angel of the LORD?

V. From the Sea to Sinai (Exodus 15:22–18:27)

Exodus 17 · 1 question

Do Moses’ raised hands picture prayer? (vv. 8–13)

Joshua fights while Moses holds up his hand with the rod of God. When Moses’ hands grow heavy, Aaron and Hur hold them up until the battle is won. Moses then builds an altar and names it The-LORD-Is-My-Banner (v. 15), giving the victory to the LORD.

MTB’s preferred reading, shared by many teachers, sees the raised hands as a picture of intercession, and the scene supports it: Israel prevails while Moses’ hands are lifted up (v. 11). It also shows that even Moses could not hold them up alone. Aaron and Hur shared the burden, and the LORD received the glory.

Read: Exodus 17:8–16

VI. The Covenant at Sinai (Exodus 19:1–24:18)

Exodus 20 · 1 question

How are the Ten Commandments numbered?

The Ten Commandments say the same thing in every tradition. What differs is only how the commands are grouped and counted. Some traditions treat the ban on images as part of the first commandment and divide the command against coveting into two.

MTB follows the Reformed numbering: verses 1–2 introduce the commandments, the ban on carved images for worship is the second commandment (vv. 4–6), and coveting is the tenth (v. 17). Counting the image ban on its own highlights how seriously God forbids worshiping Him through images, the very sin of the golden calf (32:1–6).

Read: Exodus 20:1–17 · Related: Do Christians keep the law?, The Sabbath

Exodus 21 · 3 questions

Does the Bible endorse slavery? (vv. 1–11, 16, 20–21, 26–27)

No. These laws did not invent or celebrate slavery. Like the law about divorce, they regulated a practice in a fallen world because of the hardness of human hearts (Matthew 19:8). They set guardrails that gave dignity, protection, and a way out.

Kidnapping was a capital crime: “He who kidnaps a man and sells him, or if he is found in his hand, shall surely be put to death” (v. 16). That law strikes at the root of the slave trade most modern readers picture.

A Hebrew servant, usually someone who had sold his labor to escape debt or poverty, went free in the seventh year (v. 2) and was not to be sent away empty-handed (Deuteronomy 15:13–14). He stayed for life only if he chose to, out of love for his master and family (vv. 5–6). A woman sold into a household had to be given food, clothing, and her marital rights, or she went free without paying anything (vv. 7–11).

Masters were held accountable. A master who struck and killed a servant was to be punished (v. 20). A servant who lost an eye, or even a tooth, went free (vv. 26–27). Other law codes of that world generally gave masters far wider power over their slaves.

These laws still reflect a fallen world. The servant is called the master’s “property” (v. 21), and another passage allowed Israel to buy foreign servants for life (Leviticus 25:44–46). The laws limit that power. They cannot be used to defend human trafficking or treating people as property.

Read: Exodus 21:1–11, Exodus 21:16, Exodus 21:20–27, Deuteronomy 15:13–14, Leviticus 25:44–46, Matthew 19:8

What happens to the unborn child in verse 22? (vv. 22–25)

Verse 22 describes men fighting who strike a pregnant woman “so that she gives birth prematurely, yet no harm follows.” Verse 23 continues, “But if any harm follows, then you shall give life for life.” The question is whether that second clause protects the child as well as the mother.

MTB follows the NKJV. The woman gives birth early, and the child is born alive. If neither mother nor child is hurt, the offender pays a fine (v. 22). If either is harmed, the penalty matches the harm, up to “life for life” (vv. 23–25). On this reading, the law protects the unborn child as fully as the mother.

Another reading takes verse 22 to describe a miscarriage, with the fine as the penalty for the child’s death and the “harm” in verse 23 referring only to the mother. Some have used that reading to argue that the unborn child was valued less. The NKJV wording, and the fact that verse 23 does not limit “harm” to the mother, favor MTB’s reading.

Read: Exodus 21:22–25 · Related: Does “eye for eye” permit revenge?

Does “eye for eye” permit revenge? (vv. 23–25)

No. “Eye for eye, tooth for tooth” is a principle for judges: the penalty must fit the harm and go no further. It set a legal ceiling that stopped blood feuds, in which a broken tooth might be answered with a life. It limits revenge; it does not license it. Jesus addresses personal retaliation in Matthew 5:38–42, and the public responsibility of a court remains distinct from that.

Read: Exodus 21:23–25, Matthew 5:38–42

Exodus 22 · 1 question

Do the death penalties in these laws apply today? (vv. 18–20)

Verses 18–20 require death for sorcery, for sexual relations with an animal, and for sacrificing to any god but the LORD. In Israel these were crimes against the covenant that bound the whole nation to the LORD, and the penalties guarded its faithfulness.

Those specific penalties belonged to Israel’s covenant order. No believer or church is authorized to carry them out, and the church does not advance God’s kingdom with the sword (John 18:36; 2 Corinthians 10:4). Their moral purpose still teaches: God treats these sins as deadly serious.

Whether governments may use capital punishment at all is a different question. The principle runs through Scripture, from God’s word to Noah (Genesis 9:6) to Paul’s teaching that the governing authority “does not bear the sword in vain” (Romans 13:4). MTB holds that a government may justly use it, with careful safeguards against error, such as the requirement of two or three witnesses (Deuteronomy 17:6).

Read: Exodus 22:18–20, Genesis 9:6, Romans 13:4, Deuteronomy 17:6 · Related: Do Christians keep the law?

Exodus 23 · 1 question

Why did God forbid boiling a young goat in its mother’s milk? (23:19; 34:26)

“You shall not boil a young goat in its mother’s milk” (v. 19). Scripture gives this command three times, each time beside Israel’s harvest feasts and firstfruits (23:19; 34:26; Deuteronomy 14:21). MTB understands it on three complementary levels.

First, in its setting right after the firstfruits offering, it separated Israel from the harvest rituals of the nations around them. An ancient Ugaritic text is often cited for such a Canaanite rite, but the tablet is damaged and its reading is debated, so MTB treats it as supporting background, not proof. Second, it taught compassion and reverence for God’s created order: the milk meant to nourish a young animal must not become the means of cooking it (compare Leviticus 22:28; Deuteronomy 22:6–7).

As a spiritual application, what God provides to nourish young believers, the pure milk of His word (1 Peter 2:2), must never be twisted to harm them.

Read: Exodus 23:14–19, Exodus 34:26, Deuteronomy 14:21, Leviticus 22:28, Deuteronomy 22:6–7, 1 Peter 2:2

Exodus 24 · 1 question

Did the elders really see God? (vv. 9–11)

Moses, Aaron, and seventy elders “saw the God of Israel,” with what looked like sapphire stone under His feet. God did not strike them, and “they saw God, and they ate and drank” (vv. 10–11). Yet God later tells Moses, “no man shall see Me, and live” (33:20).

MTB holds both. What the elders saw was a real but limited sight of God’s glory, not His full face. Others in Scripture were given the same kind of vision: Isaiah saw the Lord on His throne (Isaiah 6:1–5), Ezekiel saw “the appearance of the likeness of the glory of the LORD” (Ezekiel 1:28), and John saw One seated on the throne (Revelation 4:2–3). The account describes only what was under His feet and says no more. Compare John 1:18.

Read: Exodus 24:9–11, Exodus 33:20, John 1:18, Isaiah 6:1–5, Ezekiel 1:28, Revelation 4:2–3

VIII. The Golden Calf and the Covenant Renewed (Exodus 32:1–34:35)

Exodus 32 · 3 questions

Was the golden calf another god, or an image of the LORD? (vv. 1–6)

The people say of the calf, “This is your god, O Israel, that brought you out of the land of Egypt!” and Aaron announces “a feast to the LORD” (vv. 4–5). MTB’s preferred reading sees this as an attempt to worship the LORD through an image, which the second commandment forbids (20:4–6). Others see the calf as a different god altogether.

Either way it was idolatry and a breach of the covenant, not a harmless symbol. Moses breaks the tablets when he sees it (v. 19). Aaron made the calf and announced the feast, and his later account leaves out his own part (vv. 21–24).

Read: Exodus 32:1–6, Exodus 32:19–24, Exodus 20:4–6

Did God change His mind? (v. 14)

“So the LORD relented from the harm which He said He would do to His people” (v. 14). Moses appealed to God’s own name and His sworn promise to Abraham, Isaac, and Israel (vv. 11–13), and God genuinely responded. At the same time, His character does not change (34:6–7; Numbers 23:19).

God has chosen to make prayer matter. Within His unchanging purposes, He permits things and responds to His people’s requests and actions. A father may plan to stay on the couch, yet gladly go to the playground when his son asks. His character has not changed; he has chosen to respond. In the same way, God’s response to Moses was real, and it flowed from who He always is. He still holds sinners responsible (vv. 33–35).

Read: Exodus 32:9–14, Exodus 32:30–35, Numbers 23:19

Do the Levites give a pattern for religious violence? (vv. 25–29)

No. The Levites carried out a one-time judicial judgment to save the nation and protect God’s redemptive work. Israel had just entered a covenant with God (24:3–8), so the golden calf was high treason, and the people were “unrestrained” (v. 25). The rebellion threatened to destroy the whole people before God’s mission had even begun.

The Levites did not act from personal zeal. They answered Moses’ call and acted under his authority as God’s appointed leader, to stop the rebellion and preserve the covenant people (vv. 26–29). In a similar way, God judged Ananias and Sapphira at the birth of the church (Acts 5:1–11). Under the New Covenant, God Himself carries out such judgment. He does not authorize believers to take up the sword for Him (John 18:36; 2 Corinthians 10:4).

Read: Exodus 32:25–29, Exodus 24:3–8, Acts 5:1–11, 2 Corinthians 10:4

Exodus 34 · 1 question

Does God punish children for their parents’ sins? (v. 7)

The LORD proclaims Himself merciful, gracious, and forgiving, yet “by no means clearing the guilty, visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children and the children’s children to the third and the fourth generation” (vv. 6–7).

Sin passes down through example. Children see, absorb, and practice their parents’ sins and values, so a family’s bent toward a particular sin can carry on for generations. The commandments speak of the generations “of those who hate Me” (20:5): the consequences continue where the children continue in their parents’ ways.

MTB reads these consequences alongside Ezekiel 18:20, which teaches that each person answers for his own sin. The son does not bear the guilt of the father. Mercy and justice stand together in God’s name, and His mercy reaches to “thousands” (v. 7).

Read: Exodus 34:6–7, Exodus 20:5, Ezekiel 18:20
