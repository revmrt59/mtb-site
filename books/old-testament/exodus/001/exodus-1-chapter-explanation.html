Exodus 1

Verse-by-Verse
Exodus 1:1
Exodus 1:1
NKJV

Exodus 1:1 Now these are the names of the children of Israel who came to Egypt; each man and his household came with Jacob:(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 1:1 These are the names of the sons of Israel (that is, Jacob) who moved to Egypt with their father, each with his family: (NLT)

Main Idea

Exodus opens by naming the sons of Israel who came down to Egypt with Jacob, each with his household. The opening words tie the book directly to the end of Genesis, so the story of Israel in Egypt continues the story of God’s dealings with Jacob’s family.

Verse Explanation

Exodus opens by naming the sons of Israel who came down to Egypt with Jacob, each with his household. The opening words tie the book directly to the end of Genesis, so the story of Israel in Egypt continues the story of God’s dealings with Jacob’s family.

“Now these are the names of the children of Israel who came to Egypt;”

Exodus opens with “Now,” a joining word in Hebrew that links this book to what came before. Genesis ended with Jacob’s family settled in Egypt and Joseph’s death (Genesis 50:22–26). Exodus picks up the same story. “These are the names” repeats almost word for word the list of Jacob’s family going down to Egypt in Genesis 46:8. The people about to suffer in Egypt are the family God chose and made promises to.

“The children of Israel” here means the sons of Jacob, whom God renamed Israel (Genesis 32:28). Within a few verses the same phrase will describe a whole nation.

“each man and his household came with Jacob:”

Each son came “and his household,” his wives, children, and servants. Jacob’s move to Egypt was a family move, made at God’s invitation: “do not fear to go down to Egypt, for I will make of you a great nation there” (Genesis 46:3).

Study Notes

The Hebrew title of Exodus comes from its opening words, “These are the names.” The English title comes from the Greek translation and means “going out” or “departure.”

Main Idea

The list begins with four sons of Leah: Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and Judah. Naming them one by one shows that the people about to suffer in Egypt are the real descendants of the patriarch to whom God made His promises.

Verse Explanation

The list begins with four sons of Leah: Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and Judah. Naming them one by one shows that the people about to suffer in Egypt are the real descendants of the patriarch to whom God made His promises.

“Reuben, Simeon, Levi, and Judah;”

The list begins with four sons of Leah, in birth order: Reuben, the firstborn, then Simeon, Levi, and Judah. Each name stands at the head of a tribe that will play its part in Israel’s story. Levi’s line will produce Moses and Aaron (2:1; 6:16–20), and Judah’s line will produce David and, in time, the Messiah.

Naming the sons one by one shows that the people about to suffer in Egypt are the real descendants of the patriarch to whom God made His promises. Their names will appear again on the high priest’s shoulder stones and breastplate (28:9–12, 21).

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

The list continues with Leah’s sons Issachar and Zebulun and with Rachel’s son Benjamin. Joseph, Rachel’s other son, is not named here because he was already in Egypt.

“Issachar, Zebulun, and Benjamin;”

The list continues with Issachar and Zebulun, Leah’s fifth and sixth sons, and Benjamin, Rachel’s younger son. Joseph, Rachel’s firstborn, is missing because he was already in Egypt (1:5). The list groups the sons by their mothers rather than strictly by birth order.

Study Notes

The grouping by mothers follows the pattern of Genesis 35:23–26, where Jacob’s twelve sons are listed as sons of Leah, of Rachel, of Bilhah, and of Zilpah.

The last four names are the sons born through the maidservants: Dan and Naphtali through Rachel’s maid Bilhah, and Gad and Asher through Leah’s maid Zilpah (Genesis 30:3–13). Each one belongs fully to Israel. With Joseph already in Egypt, all twelve tribes are accounted for at the start of the book.

Exodus 1:5
Exodus 1:5
NKJV

Exodus 1:5 All those who were descendants of Jacob were seventy persons (for Joseph was in Egypt already).(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 1:5 In all, Jacob had seventy descendants in Egypt, including Joseph, who was already there. (NLT)

Main Idea

Jacob’s descendants who came into Egypt numbered seventy persons, counting Joseph, who was already there. The small number sets up the striking growth the next verses describe.

Verse Explanation

Jacob’s descendants who came into Egypt numbered seventy persons, counting Joseph, who was already there. The small number sets up the striking growth the next verses describe.

“All those who were descendants of Jacob were seventy persons”

The family that came into Egypt numbered “seventy persons.” The count matches Genesis 46:27. Seventy was not a large number for a family that God had promised would become a great nation. That small beginning sets up the striking growth the next verses describe.

“(for Joseph was in Egypt already).”

The note in parentheses explains why Joseph was not in the list of names. He had been sold into Egypt years earlier, and God had used him to preserve the family during famine (Genesis 45:5–7).

Study Notes

The phrase “descendants of Jacob” reads literally those who came out of Jacob’s thigh, a Hebrew way of speaking about physical descent.

The Greek translation of the Old Testament reads seventy-five here and in Genesis 46:27. Stephen follows that count in Acts 7:14. The Hebrew text reads seventy. The two totals count the family differently, most likely by including or leaving out certain grandsons of Joseph.

Exodus 1:7
Exodus 1:7
NKJV

Exodus 1:7 But the children of Israel were fruitful and increased abundantly, multiplied and grew exceedingly mighty; and the land was filled with them.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 1:7 But their descendants, the Israelites, had many children and grandchildren. In fact, they multiplied so greatly that they became extremely powerful and filled the land. (NLT)

Main Idea

Yet the children of Israel “were fruitful and increased abundantly, multiplied and grew exceedingly mighty,” until “the land was filled with them.” The piled-up words of increase echo God’s blessing on Abraham’s descendants and show His promise being fulfilled in Egypt.

Verse Explanation

Yet the children of Israel “were fruitful and increased abundantly, multiplied and grew exceedingly mighty,” until “the land was filled with them.” The piled-up words of increase echo God’s blessing on Abraham’s descendants and show His promise being fulfilled in Egypt.

“But the children of Israel were fruitful and increased abundantly, multiplied and grew exceedingly mighty;”

“But” brings a contrast. A generation died, but the family did not shrink. Moses piles up four expressions of growth: they “were fruitful,” they “increased abundantly,” they “multiplied,” and they “grew exceedingly mighty.” He then adds that the land was filled with them. The words echo God’s blessing at creation, “Be fruitful and multiply” (Genesis 1:28), and His promise to Abraham’s descendants (Genesis 17:6; 35:11). What God promised is happening, even in a foreign land.

“and the land was filled with them.”

The growth was so great that Israel spread through the land. The land here is Egypt, or at least the region of Goshen where Israel lived (Genesis 47:27). The family of seventy had become a people.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “increased abundantly” is often used for swarming creatures that teem in great numbers (Genesis 1:20–21). It pictures abundant, almost uncountable growth.

Exodus 1:8–14 — A New King Turns Israel’s Growth into Hard Bondage

Exodus 1:8
Exodus 1:8
NKJV

Exodus 1:8 Now there arose a new king over Egypt, who did not know Joseph.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 1:8 Eventually, a new king came to power in Egypt who knew nothing about Joseph or what he had done. (NLT)

Main Idea

A new king rises over Egypt “who did not know Joseph.” The change of ruler leaves Israel without the standing Joseph once gave them, and the new regime will treat them very differently.

Verse Explanation

A new king rises over Egypt “who did not know Joseph.” The change of ruler leaves Israel without the standing Joseph once gave them, and the new regime will treat them very differently.

“Now there arose a new king over Egypt, who did not know Joseph.”

A turning point comes with “a new king over Egypt.” This king “did not know Joseph.” He may simply have never heard of him, or he may have refused to acknowledge what Joseph had done for Egypt. Either way, Joseph’s legacy no longer protected Israel. The family that had been honored guests became outsiders under a ruler who owed them nothing.

The text does not name this king. Exodus never gives the names of its pharaohs.

Study Notes

Interpreters debate the identity of this king and the date of the exodus. An earlier date draws on 1 Kings 6:1, which places the exodus 480 years before Solomon began building the temple. A later date draws on the city name Raamses (1:11) and on historical arguments. Exodus itself leaves the kings unnamed.

Pharaoh speaks with alarm. What God gave Israel as a blessing, Pharaoh sees as a threat. His words may exaggerate the danger, but they reveal his fear. The blessing God has given Israel now appears to Egypt’s king as a threat.

Exodus 1:10
Exodus 1:10
NKJV

Exodus 1:10 come, let us deal shrewdly with them, lest they multiply, and it happen, in the event of war, that they also join our enemies and fight against us, and so go up out of the land."(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 1:10 We must make a plan to keep them from growing even more. If we don’t, and if war breaks out, they will join our enemies and fight against us. Then they will escape from the country. ” (NLT)

Main Idea

Pharaoh proposes to “deal shrewdly” with Israel so they will not multiply, join Egypt’s enemies in war, and leave the land. He wants to keep them in Egypt and keep them small, which sets him against the very growth God has promised.

Verse Explanation

Pharaoh proposes to “deal shrewdly” with Israel so they will not multiply, join Egypt’s enemies in war, and leave the land. He wants to keep them in Egypt and keep them small, which sets him against the very growth God has promised.

“come, let us deal shrewdly with them, lest they multiply,”

Pharaoh proposes a plan: “let us deal shrewdly with them.” He wants to act cleverly, using policy and pressure to control Israel. His goal is “lest they multiply,” to stop the very growth God has promised. Without knowing it, Pharaoh has set himself against God’s purpose.

“and it happen, in the event of war, that they also join our enemies and fight against us,”

Pharaoh names his fear. In a war, Israel might “join our enemies and fight against us.” A large foreign population inside Egypt’s borders could turn against the nation in a time of crisis.

“and so go up out of the land.”

He also fears that Israel might “go up out of the land.” He wants to keep them, not lose them. He wants their labor but not their strength. That is exactly the tension the rest of the story will resolve. God will bring Israel up out of the land.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “deal shrewdly” is related to the word for wisdom. Pharaoh thinks his plan is wise, but his wisdom sets him against God.

Exodus 1:11
Exodus 1:11
NKJV

Exodus 1:11 Therefore they set taskmasters over them to afflict them with their burdens. And they built for Pharaoh supply cities, Pithom and Raamses.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 1:11 So the Egyptians made the Israelites their slaves. They appointed brutal slave drivers over them, hoping to wear them down with crushing labor. They forced them to build the cities of Pithom and Rameses as supply centers for the king. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Egyptians set taskmasters over Israel to afflict them with heavy burdens, and Israel builds the supply cities Pithom and Raamses for Pharaoh. The free family of Jacob is now forced into labor for Egypt’s king.

Verse Explanation

The Egyptians set taskmasters over Israel to afflict them with heavy burdens, and Israel builds the supply cities Pithom and Raamses for Pharaoh. The free family of Jacob is now forced into labor for Egypt’s king.

“Therefore they set taskmasters over them to afflict them with their burdens.”

Pharaoh’s plan begins with forced labor. The Egyptians “set taskmasters over them,” officials who supervised the work and drove the workers. The purpose was “to afflict them with their burdens.” The labor was meant to crush them, to wear them down so they would not grow.

The word “afflict” recalls God’s word to Abraham: his descendants would be strangers in a land not their own and would be afflicted, but God would judge that nation and bring them out with great possessions (Genesis 15:13–14). What God foretold is now coming to pass, and so will what He promised.

“And they built for Pharaoh supply cities, Pithom and Raamses.”

Israel was put to work building “supply cities” for Pharaoh, cities where grain and other goods were stored. Two are named: Pithom and Raamses. Both were in the eastern part of the Nile delta, near the land of Goshen. The free family of Jacob is now forced into labor for Egypt’s king.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “afflict” in this verse is the same root God used in Genesis 15:13: “they will afflict them four hundred years.”

The sites of Pithom and Raamses have been identified with various locations in the eastern delta. The name Raamses appears earlier, in Genesis 47:11, for the region where Joseph settled his family.

“But the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and grew,” and the Egyptians came to dread the children of Israel. Oppression cannot stop what God is doing, and Egypt’s fear only grows as its plan fails.

The Egyptians make Israel’s lives bitter with hard bondage in mortar, in brick, and in every kind of field work. The repeated note that all their service was “with rigor” closes the description of slavery and shows how complete the oppression has become.

Verse Explanation

The Egyptians make Israel’s lives bitter with hard bondage in mortar, in brick, and in every kind of field work. The repeated note that all their service was “with rigor” closes the description of slavery and shows how complete the oppression has become.

“And they made their lives bitter with hard bondage—”

The Egyptians “made their lives bitter.” The word “bitter” would be remembered every Passover, when Israel ate bitter herbs (12:8). The “hard bondage” was more than heavy work. It was slavery that crushed the spirit.

“in mortar, in brick, and in all manner of service in the field.”

Moses names the work: “in mortar, in brick,” the building work of making mud bricks and laying them, and “in all manner of service in the field,” agricultural labor of every kind. Every part of their lives was taken up by forced work.

“All their service in which they made them serve was with rigor.”

The verse ends by repeating that “all their service” was “with rigor.” The repetition closes the description of slavery and shows how complete the oppression had become. Nothing in their lives was free from it.

Study Notes

The Hebrew words for “serve,” “service,” and “bondage” in 1:13–14 come from the same root. The repetition presses home that Israel’s whole life had been reduced to forced labor.

Exodus 1:15–21 — The Midwives Fear God and Save the Sons

Exodus 1:15
Exodus 1:15
NKJV

Exodus 1:15 Then the king of Egypt spoke to the Hebrew midwives, of whom the name of one was Shiphrah and the name of the other Puah;(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 1:15 Then Pharaoh, the king of Egypt, gave this order to the Hebrew midwives, Shiphrah and Puah: (NLT)

Main Idea

The king of Egypt turns to a new strategy and speaks to the Hebrew midwives, named Shiphrah and Puah. Naming these two women gives them an honored place in the story while the king himself remains unnamed.

Verse Explanation

The king of Egypt turns to a new strategy and speaks to the Hebrew midwives, named Shiphrah and Puah. Naming these two women gives them an honored place in the story while the king himself remains unnamed.

“Then the king of Egypt spoke to the Hebrew midwives,”

When forced labor failed, “the king of Egypt” turned to a new strategy. He spoke to “the Hebrew midwives,” the women who helped Hebrew mothers give birth. If he could not stop Israel’s growth by labor, he would try to stop it at birth.

“of whom the name of one was Shiphrah and the name of the other Puah;”

Moses records their names: Shiphrah and Puah. The king remains unnamed, but these two women are named. In God’s story, the faithful midwives are remembered while the powerful king is not.

There were likely more than two midwives for such a large population. These two may have been the chief midwives who represented the rest.

Study Notes

“Hebrew midwives” can mean midwives who were Hebrews or midwives who served the Hebrews. Their names are Semitic, which suggests they were Hebrews themselves.

Exodus 1:16
NKJV

Exodus 1:16 and he said, "When you do the duties of a midwife for the Hebrew women, and see them on the birthstools, if it is a son, then you shall kill him; but if it is a daughter, then she shall live."(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 1:16 “When you help the Hebrew women as they give birth, watch as they deliver. If the baby is a boy, kill him; if it is a girl, let her live.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Pharaoh orders the midwives to kill every Hebrew son at birth and let the daughters live. His policy has moved from forced labor to the secret killing of Israel’s newborn boys.

Verse Explanation

Pharaoh orders the midwives to kill every Hebrew son at birth and let the daughters live. His policy has moved from forced labor to the secret killing of Israel’s newborn boys.

“and he said, ‘When you do the duties of a midwife for the Hebrew women, and see them on the birthstools,”

Pharaoh gives his instructions. When the midwives help “the Hebrew women” give birth and see them “on the birthstools,” they are to watch for the sex of the child. Birthstools were stones or a stool on which a woman sat or knelt to give birth.

“if it is a son, then you shall kill him; but if it is a daughter, then she shall live.”

The order is brutal: “if it is a son, then you shall kill him.” The killing was to happen at the moment of birth, at the midwives’ own hands. Daughters could live. Pharaoh may have thought that without men, Israel could not fight or grow as a nation. His policy has moved from forced labor to the secret killing of Israel’s newborn boys.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “birthstools” is literally “the two stones.” It most likely refers to the stones or stool used for support during childbirth.

When a ruler commands evil, obedience to God comes first (Acts 5:29).

The phrase “provided households” can mean that God gave them children and descendants, or that He established their families in some other lasting way. Either way, God blessed them with families.

Main Idea

Pharaoh then commands all his people to cast every newborn son into the river and to let every daughter live. The secret order becomes a public decree against Israel’s sons, and the chapter ends with that danger hanging over the next generation.

Verse Explanation

Pharaoh then commands all his people to cast every newborn son into the river and to let every daughter live. The secret order becomes a public decree against Israel’s sons, and the chapter ends with that danger hanging over the next generation.

“So Pharaoh commanded all his people, saying,”

Pharaoh’s third plan is the worst. He no longer works in secret. He commands “all his people.” Every Egyptian is now enlisted in the attack on Israel’s children.

“Every son who is born you shall cast into the river, and every daughter you shall save alive.”

The decree is public and total: “Every son who is born you shall cast into the river.” The river is the Nile, the source of Egypt’s life. Pharaoh makes it a place of death for Hebrew boys. “Every daughter you shall save alive” repeats the earlier order (1:16).

The chapter ends with this danger hanging over the next generation. The very next verses tell of a Hebrew baby born under this decree and of how God preserved him at the river.

Study Notes

The Hebrew text reads simply “every son who is born.” The Samaritan Pentateuch and the Greek translation add to the Hebrews, which makes explicit what the context already implies.
