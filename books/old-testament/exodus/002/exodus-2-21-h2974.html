CONTENT - ( ya'al , Strong's H2974 )

Summary:

In Exodus 2:21, the word "content" translates the Hebrew verb ya'al (in the Hiphil stem), which conveys a decisive willingness, consent, or resolve to undertake a course of action. Rather than describing a mere emotional state of satisfaction, the word denotes a volitional choice. Moses did not just tolerate his new circumstances in Midian; he made a deliberate, settled decision to dwell with Reuel (Jethro), marking a profound transition from a prince of Egypt to a humble shepherd in the wilderness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root ya'al historically carries the sense of starting or beginning an action with resolve, or showing willingness to enter into a new state or agreement. In the ancient Near Eastern context, taking up residence with a foreign clan was not a casual decision; it involved a formal agreement and mutual acceptance. The word-picture behind ya'al implies overcoming initial hesitation, grief, or cultural barriers to reach a point of willing consent. It suggests a movement of the will where a person voluntarily yields to or adopts a proposed path, establishing a firm resolution to proceed in a new direction.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew verb ya'al occurs primarily in the Hiphil stem in the Old Testament. It means to resolve, undertake, determine, or willingly consent. It expresses a conscious act of the will to begin or agree to something. While older English translations often use "be content" or "please," the core lexical meaning centers on willingness, determination, and consent. It is the transition from hesitation or contemplation to a settled, active commitment to a specific course of action.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In classical Hebrew usage, ya'al frequently precedes another verb in the infinitive (as it does in Exodus 2:21, "to dwell"). It functions to show the disposition with which the main action is performed:

• It can express a polite request to "please" do something, where the speaker asks the hearer to "willingly resolve" to act (e.g., Genesis 18:27, where Abraham takes it upon himself to speak to God).

• It can denote a bold undertaking or determination to do something difficult (e.g., Deuteronomy 1:5, where Moses "undertook" to explain the law).

• It illustrates a settled agreement to stay or work in a certain place, indicating a voluntary submission or alliance (e.g., Judges 17:11, where the Levite "was content" to dwell with Micah).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, ya'al highlights the role of human volition within the providence of God. Moses' willingness to dwell in Midian represents a crucial stage of his preparation. God does not drag Moses kicking and screaming into the desert; Moses willingly consents to this season of obscurity. This reflects a quiet surrender to God's hand. In the broader biblical narrative, when a person is content (ya'al) to accept a humble path, it often precedes a significant work of God. It shows that God shapes the desires and wills of His servants so that they willingly step into the precisely designed environments needed for their spiritual refinement.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Exodus 2:21, Moses has fled Egypt as a fugitive, leaving behind royalty, wealth, and status. He is a stranger in a strange land. When Reuel invites him to eat bread and stay, Moses' decision to "be content" (or "willingly resolve") to dwell with him is a pivotal turning point. It shows that Moses did not remain in a state of perpetual grief, anger, or longing for Egypt. Instead, he accepted his new reality. He consented to become a shepherd—an occupation detestable to Egyptians—and to build a family in the wilderness. His willingness to dwell with Reuel demonstrates his adaptability, humility, and submission to the path God had laid before him, preparing him for the burning bush forty years later.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) translates wayyo'el ("was content") in Exodus 2:21 using the Greek verb katoikisthē (from katoikizō), which means "to cause to dwell" or "to settle down." Rather than focusing purely on his emotional willingness, the Greek translation highlights the concrete outcome of his resolve—he became an established resident in Midian. This underscores that his "contentment" was not a passing emotion but a life-altering, structural relocation of his heart, home, and identity.
