Exodus 2

Verse-by-Verse
Exodus 2:1
Exodus 2:1
NKJV

Exodus 2:1 And a man of the house of Levi went and took as wife a daughter of Levi.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 2:1 About this time, a man and woman from the tribe of Levi got married. (NLT)

Main Idea

A man from the house of Levi marries a daughter of Levi. The story of Israel’s deliverer begins quietly with an ordinary Levite household living under Pharaoh’s decree.

Verse Explanation

A man from the house of Levi marries a daughter of Levi. The story of Israel’s deliverer begins quietly with an ordinary Levite household living under Pharaoh’s decree.

“And a man of the house of Levi went and took as wife a daughter of Levi.”

The story of Israel’s deliverer begins quietly. “A man of the house of Levi” marries “a daughter of Levi.” Neither is named here. Later we learn that the father was Amram and the mother Jochebed (6:20). For now the focus falls on an ordinary Levite household living under Pharaoh’s decree that every newborn son be thrown into the river (1:22).

God often begins His great works in ways that look small. No one watching would have guessed that a baby born to this couple would lead Israel out of Egypt.

Study Notes

Numbers 26:59 names the mother as Jochebed, “the daughter of Levi who was born to Levi in Egypt,” and lists Aaron, Moses, and Miriam as her children.

Aaron was three years older than Moses (7:7). The sister of 2:4 was also older than Moses.

Exodus 2:2
Exodus 2:2
NKJV

Exodus 2:2 So the woman conceived and bore a son. And when she saw that he was a beautiful child, she hid him three months.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 2:2 The woman became pregnant and gave birth to a son. She saw that he was a special baby and kept him hidden for three months. (NLT)

Main Idea

The woman bears a son, and seeing that he is a beautiful child, she hides him for three months. Her hiding the baby defies Pharaoh’s order, and Hebrews 11:23 credits the parents’ action to faith.

Verse Explanation

The woman bears a son, and seeing that he is a beautiful child, she hides him for three months. Her hiding the baby defies Pharaoh’s order, and Hebrews 11:23 credits the parents’ action to faith.

“So the woman conceived and bore a son.”

Under Pharaoh’s decree, the birth of a son meant mortal danger.

“And when she saw that he was a beautiful child, she hid him three months.”

The mother “saw that he was a beautiful child.” The word “beautiful” translates the ordinary Hebrew word for “good.” It recalls how God saw that His creation was good (Genesis 1). She saw something special in this child.

So “she hid him three months.” Hiding the baby defied Pharaoh’s order. Hebrews credits the parents’ action to faith: “By faith Moses, when he was born, was hidden three months by his parents, because they saw he was a beautiful child; and they were not afraid of the king’s command” (Hebrews 11:23).

Study Notes

Stephen says Moses “was well pleasing to God” (Acts 7:20). Stephen’s wording echoes the Greek translation of Exodus 2:2, which uses the same Greek word that Stephen uses where the NKJV of Exodus has “beautiful.”

Exodus 2:3
Exodus 2:3
NKJV

Exodus 2:3 But when she could no longer hide him, she took an ark of bulrushes for him, daubed it with asphalt and pitch, put the child in it, and laid it in the reeds by the river's bank.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 2:3 But when she could no longer hide him, she got a basket made of papyrus reeds and waterproofed it with tar and pitch. She put the baby in the basket and laid it among the reeds along the bank of the Nile River. (NLT)

Main Idea

When she can no longer hide him, she makes an ark of bulrushes, seals it with asphalt and pitch, and lays the child in it among the reeds by the river’s bank. The river Pharaoh meant for the death of Hebrew sons becomes the place where this son is protected.

Verse Explanation

When she can no longer hide him, she makes an ark of bulrushes, seals it with asphalt and pitch, and lays the child in it among the reeds by the river’s bank. The river Pharaoh meant for the death of Hebrew sons becomes the place where this son is protected.

“But when she could no longer hide him,”

A baby grows louder and harder to hide. After three months, “she could no longer hide him.” She had to find another way to protect him.

“she took an ark of bulrushes for him, daubed it with asphalt and pitch,”

She made “an ark of bulrushes,” a small basket woven from papyrus reeds. She “daubed it with asphalt and pitch,” sealing it with tar to keep out the water. The word “ark” is the same Hebrew word used for Noah’s ark (Genesis 6:14). In both stories, God preserves life through an ark sealed with pitch as waters threaten.

“put the child in it, and laid it in the reeds by the river’s bank.”

She placed the basket at the river’s edge. The reeds along the edge of the Nile would keep the basket from floating away. The river Pharaoh meant for the death of Hebrew sons becomes the place where this son is protected.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “ark” appears only in the stories of Noah and of Moses. A different word is used for the ark of the covenant.

The word translated “reeds” is the same word used in the phrase often translated “Red Sea,” which can be rendered Sea of Reeds (for example, 13:18).

Verse Explanation

The child’s sister stands at a distance to see what will happen to him. Her watchfulness prepares for the part she will play when he is found.

“And his sister stood afar off, to know what would be done to him.”

The baby’s “sister” stayed nearby. She “stood afar off,” close enough to watch but far enough not to draw attention. She waited “to know what would be done to him.” The text does not name her here. She is most likely Miriam, who later appears as Moses’ sister and as a prophetess (15:20; Numbers 26:59). Her watchfulness prepares for the part she will play when he is found.

“The daughter of Pharaoh” came to the river “to bathe.” The rescue of the Hebrew child begins in the household of the king who ordered his death.

“And her maidens walked along the riverside;”

Her “maidens,” her attendants, walked along the bank while she bathed.

“and when she saw the ark among the reeds, she sent her maid to get it.”

She “saw the ark among the reeds” and “sent her maid to get it.” Her curiosity brought the basket to her hands. Behind what looks like chance stands the God who watches over His people.

The text does not name Pharaoh’s daughter. Later traditions gave her various names, but Scripture does not.

When she opens the ark and sees the baby crying, she has compassion on him and says, “This is one of the Hebrews’ children.” She recognizes the child as one of the Hebrews under her father’s decree and still responds with compassion.

She “opened it” and “saw the child.” At that moment “the baby wept.” The cry of a helpless infant reached her.

“So she had compassion on him,”

She felt pity for the crying baby. Compassion moved her before policy did.

“and said, ‘This is one of the Hebrews’ children.”

She knew at once what she was looking at: “This is one of the Hebrews’ children.” She understood that this was one of the boys her father had ordered drowned. She recognized the child as one of the Hebrews under her father’s decree and still responded with compassion.

Exodus 2:7 Then the baby’s sister approached the princess. “Should I go and find one of the Hebrew women to nurse the baby for you?” she asked. (NLT)

Main Idea

The child’s sister boldly offers to find a Hebrew nurse for Pharaoh’s daughter. Her question opens the way for the baby to be returned to his own family.

Verse Explanation

The child’s sister boldly offers to find a Hebrew nurse for Pharaoh’s daughter. Her question opens the way for the baby to be returned to his own family.

“Then his sister said to Pharaoh’s daughter,”

The watching sister stepped forward and spoke “to Pharaoh’s daughter.” It was a bold move for a slave girl to address a princess.

“Shall I go and call a nurse for you from the Hebrew women, that she may nurse the child for you?”

She offered to find “a nurse for you from the Hebrew women.” A baby needed a nursing mother. A Hebrew woman could nurse the child for the princess. Her question opens the way for the baby to be returned to his own family.

Study Notes

In the ancient world it was common for wealthy women to hire a wet nurse. Contracts for wet nurses have survived from the ancient Near East.

Pharaoh’s daughter says, “Go,” and the girl goes and calls the child’s own mother. The sister’s proposal is accepted, and the baby is about to be returned to the woman who hid him.

Pharaoh’s daughter says, “Go,” and the girl goes and calls the child’s own mother. The sister’s proposal is accepted, and the baby is about to be returned to the woman who hid him.

“And Pharaoh’s daughter said to her, ‘Go.”

Pharaoh’s daughter gave a one-word answer: “Go.” The sister’s proposal was accepted.

“So the maiden went and called the child’s mother.”

The sister did not bring a stranger. She brought the baby’s own mother. The woman who had hidden him and placed him in the river would now hold him again.

Exodus 2:9
Exodus 2:9
NKJV

Exodus 2:9 Then Pharaoh's daughter said to her, "Take this child away and nurse him for me, and I will give you your wages." So the woman took the child and nursed him.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 2:9 “Take this baby and nurse him for me,” the princess told the baby’s mother. “I will pay you for your help.” So the woman took her baby home and nursed him. (NLT)

Main Idea

Pharaoh’s daughter tells the mother to take the child and nurse him, and promises to pay her wages. The mother is paid to raise her own son in safety, an outcome no one in Israel could have arranged.

Verse Explanation

Pharaoh’s daughter tells the mother to take the child and nurse him, and promises to pay her wages. The mother is paid to raise her own son in safety, an outcome no one in Israel could have arranged.

“Then Pharaoh’s daughter said to her, ‘Take this child away and nurse him for me, and I will give you your wages.”

Pharaoh’s daughter told the mother, “Take this child away and nurse him for me.” The princess claimed the child as hers, but she gave him back to his mother to raise. She even promised, “I will give you your wages.” The mother would be paid to care for her own son.

“So the woman took the child and nursed him.”

The baby who had been condemned to death now lived safely in his own home, under the protection of Pharaoh’s own daughter. No one in Israel could have arranged such an outcome. God turned Pharaoh’s decree upside down.

Study Notes

Children in the ancient world were often nursed until about two or three years old (compare 1 Samuel 1:22–24). Moses likely spent his earliest years with his own family.

Exodus 2:10
Exodus 2:10
NKJV

Exodus 2:10 And the child grew, and she brought him to Pharaoh's daughter, and he became her son. So she called his name Moses, saying, "Because I drew him out of the water."(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 2:10 Later, when the boy was older, his mother brought him back to Pharaoh’s daughter, who adopted him as her own son. The princess named him Moses, for she explained, “I lifted him out of the water.” (NLT)

Main Idea

When the child is grown, his mother brings him to Pharaoh’s daughter, he becomes her son, and she names him Moses, “Because I drew him out of the water.” The name remembers how he was rescued, and he now enters Pharaoh’s own household.

Verse Explanation

When the child is grown, his mother brings him to Pharaoh’s daughter, he becomes her son, and she names him Moses, “Because I drew him out of the water.” The name remembers how he was rescued, and he now enters Pharaoh’s own household.

“And the child grew, and she brought him to Pharaoh’s daughter, and he became her son.”

“The child grew.” When he was weaned, his mother “brought him to Pharaoh’s daughter,” and “he became her son.” Moses was adopted into Pharaoh’s household. Stephen says he “was learned in all the wisdom of the Egyptians” (Acts 7:22). The future deliverer of Israel was raised in the palace of Israel’s oppressor.

“So she called his name Moses, saying, ‘Because I drew him out of the water.”

Pharaoh’s daughter “called his name Moses.” She explained, “Because I drew him out of the water.” The name remembers how he was rescued. It also points ahead. The one drawn out of the water would one day draw Israel out of Egypt and through the waters of the sea.

Study Notes

The name Moses sounds like the Hebrew verb meaning to draw out. It also resembles an Egyptian word meaning “son” or “born,” found in Egyptian names. The narrator connects it with the Hebrew verb.

Exodus 2:11–15 — Moses Kills an Egyptian and Flees from Pharaoh

Exodus 2:11
Exodus 2:11
NKJV

Exodus 2:11 Now it came to pass in those days, when Moses was grown, that he went out to his brethren and looked at their burdens. And he saw an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, one of his brethren.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 2:11 Many years later, when Moses had grown up, he went out to visit his own people, the Hebrews, and he saw how hard they were forced to work. During his visit, he saw an Egyptian beating one of his fellow Hebrews. (NLT)

Main Idea

When Moses is grown, he goes out to his brethren, sees their burdens, and sees an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, “one of his brethren.” Though raised in Pharaoh’s house, Moses identifies with the oppressed Hebrews as his own people.

Verse Explanation

When Moses is grown, he goes out to his brethren, sees their burdens, and sees an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, “one of his brethren.” Though raised in Pharaoh’s house, Moses identifies with the oppressed Hebrews as his own people.

“Now it came to pass in those days, when Moses was grown,”

The story jumps ahead. “When Moses was grown,” he was a man. Stephen says he was about forty years old (Acts 7:23).

“that he went out to his brethren and looked at their burdens.”

Moses “went out to his brethren.” Though raised as an Egyptian prince, he knew who his people were. He “looked at their burdens,” seeing their forced labor with his own eyes. The verb suggests more than a glance. He looked with concern.

“And he saw an Egyptian beating a Hebrew, one of his brethren.”

What he saw was violence against one of his own people. The narrator stresses that the man being beaten was “one of his brethren.” Moses identified with the oppressed Hebrews as his own people. Hebrews says Moses chose “to suffer affliction with the people of God” rather than enjoy the pleasures of Egypt (Hebrews 11:24–26).

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb translated “beating” is the same verb translated “killed” in 2:12. The Egyptian struck the Hebrew, and Moses struck the Egyptian.

Exodus 2:12
NKJV

Exodus 2:12 After looking in all directions to make sure no one was watching, Moses killed the Egyptian and hid the body in the sand. (NLT)

Main Idea

Moses looks this way and that, and seeing no one, kills the Egyptian and hides him in the sand. The text records the killing and its concealment without commending it, and the secrecy shows Moses acting on his own initiative.

Verse Explanation

Moses looks this way and that, and seeing no one, kills the Egyptian and hides him in the sand. The text records the killing and its concealment without commending it, and the secrecy shows Moses acting on his own initiative.

“So he looked this way and that way, and when he saw no one,”

Before acting, Moses “looked this way and that way.” He checked to see if anyone was watching. “When he saw no one,” he acted. The secrecy shows that Moses knew what he was doing would be seen as a crime.

“he killed the Egyptian and hid him in the sand.”

He struck the man down and buried the body.

The text records the killing and its concealment without commending it. Moses was moved by real concern for his people, and Stephen says he expected Israel to understand that God would deliver them by his hand (Acts 7:25). But Moses acted on his own initiative, in secret, and by violence. God had not yet sent him. The LORD would commission him forty years later at the burning bush.

Exodus 2:14
Exodus 2:14
NKJV

Exodus 2:14 Then he said, "Who made you a prince and a judge over us? Do you intend to kill me as you killed the Egyptian?" So Moses feared and said, "Surely this thing is known!"(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 2:14 The man replied, “Who appointed you to be our prince and judge? Are you going to kill me as you killed that Egyptian yesterday?” Then Moses was afraid, thinking, “Everyone knows what I did.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The man replies, “Who made you a prince and a judge over us?” and asks whether Moses means to kill him as he killed the Egyptian. Moses’ own people reject his leadership, and he realizes in fear that his deed is known.

Verse Explanation

The man replies, “Who made you a prince and a judge over us?” and asks whether Moses means to kill him as he killed the Egyptian. Moses’ own people reject his leadership, and he realizes in fear that his deed is known.

“Then he said, ‘Who made you a prince and a judge over us?”

The man shot back, “Who made you a prince and a judge over us?” He rejected Moses’ authority. Moses had no recognized right to rule or judge them.

“Do you intend to kill me as you killed the Egyptian?”

His second question exposed what Moses had done in secret. The secret was out. Moses’ own people reject his leadership.

“So Moses feared and said, ‘Surely this thing is known!”

“Moses feared.” He realized, “Surely this thing is known!” If one Hebrew knew, others did too, and word would reach Pharaoh. His deed was no longer hidden.

Stephen draws out the meaning: Moses’ own people “did not understand” that God would deliver them by his hand, and they pushed him away (Acts 7:25–27, 35).

Exodus 2:15
Exodus 2:15
NKJV

Exodus 2:15 When Pharaoh heard of this matter, he sought to kill Moses. But Moses fled from the face of Pharaoh and dwelt in the land of Midian; and he sat down by a well.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 2:15 And sure enough, Pharaoh heard what had happened, and he tried to kill Moses. But Moses fled from Pharaoh and went to live in the land of Midian. When Moses arrived in Midian, he sat down beside a well. (NLT)

Main Idea

When Pharaoh hears of it, he seeks to kill Moses, and Moses flees to the land of Midian and sits down by a well. His first attempt to help his people ends in exile, and the well sets the scene for his new life.

Verse Explanation

When Pharaoh hears of it, he seeks to kill Moses, and Moses flees to the land of Midian and sits down by a well. His first attempt to help his people ends in exile, and the well sets the scene for his new life.

“When Pharaoh heard of this matter, he sought to kill Moses.”

“Pharaoh heard of this matter.” He “sought to kill Moses.” The adopted grandson of Pharaoh was now a wanted man.

“But Moses fled from the face of Pharaoh and dwelt in the land of Midian;”

“Moses fled from the face of Pharaoh.” He escaped and went to “the land of Midian,” a region east of Egypt, generally placed in northwest Arabia or the Sinai peninsula. The Midianites were descendants of Abraham through Keturah (Genesis 25:1–2).

“and he sat down by a well.”

His first attempt to help his people ended in exile. The well sets the scene for his new life. In Genesis, wells were places where marriages began: Abraham’s servant found Rebekah at a well (Genesis 24), and Jacob met Rachel at a well (Genesis 29).

Study Notes

Hebrews says Moses left Egypt “not fearing the wrath of the king” (Hebrews 11:27). Interpreters differ on whether Hebrews refers to this flight or to the later exodus, since Exodus 2:14 says Moses was afraid.

Reuel asked, “And where is he?” He rebuked his daughters: “Why is it that you have left the man?” In that culture, failing to offer hospitality to someone who had helped you was shameful.

“Call him, that he may eat bread.”

Reuel invited Moses to a meal. “Eat bread” means to share a meal. The hospitality of this Midianite household opens a door for the exiled Moses.

In the course of time the king of Egypt dies, but Israel’s bondage continues, and the people groan and cry out because of it. Their cry rises to God, and the story turns from Moses in Midian back to the suffering people in Egypt.

“Now it happened in the process of time that the king of Egypt died.”

“In the process of time” marks a long period. Stephen says Moses spent forty years in Midian (Acts 7:30). During that time “the king of Egypt died.” The Pharaoh who sought to kill Moses was gone.

“Then the children of Israel groaned because of the bondage, and they cried out;”

But Israel’s situation did not improve. “The children of Israel groaned because of the bondage.” The new king continued the oppression. They “cried out,” calling for help.

“and their cry came up to God because of the bondage.”

“Their cry came up to God.” Their groaning was not lost. It reached the God of their fathers. The story turns from Moses in Midian back to the suffering people in Egypt.

Study Notes

The text does not say that Israel prayed to God, only that they groaned and cried out. Their cry nevertheless “came up to God.”

“and God remembered His covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob.”

God does not forget. To remember here means that God now turns to act on the promises He made to the fathers. He had promised Abraham that his descendants would be afflicted in a foreign land, but that He would judge that nation and bring them out (Genesis 15:13–14). That time has come.

Study Notes
