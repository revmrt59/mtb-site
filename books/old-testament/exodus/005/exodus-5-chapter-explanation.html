Exodus 5:1
Exodus 5:1
NKJV

Exodus 5:1 Afterward Moses and Aaron went in and told Pharaoh, "Thus says the LORD God of Israel: "˜Let My people go, that they may hold a feast to Me in the wilderness.' "(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 5:1 After this presentation to Israel’s leaders, Moses and Aaron went and spoke to Pharaoh. They told him, “This is what the LORD, the God of Israel, says: Let my people go so they may hold a festival in my honor in the wilderness.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Moses and Aaron go to Pharaoh with the LORD’s word: “Let My people go, that they may hold a feast to Me in the wilderness.” The LORD claims Israel as His own people and calls them to worship Him.

Verse Explanation

Moses and Aaron go to Pharaoh with the LORD’s word: “Let My people go, that they may hold a feast to Me in the wilderness.” The LORD claims Israel as His own people and calls them to worship Him.

“Afterward Moses and Aaron went in and told Pharaoh,”

After the elders believed and worshiped (4:29–31), Moses and Aaron took God’s message to the palace. Moses, who had spent years tending sheep in Midian (3:1), and his brother Aaron walked in to address the most powerful king of their world.

“Thus says the LORD God of Israel:”

They spoke with a prophet’s formula. These were not their words but the words of “the LORD God of Israel.” Pharaoh was used to being treated as divine. Now he heard a command from a God he did not acknowledge.

“Let My people go, that they may hold a feast to Me in the wilderness.”

God calls Israel “My people.” They were Pharaoh’s slaves, but they belonged to the LORD. The purpose of their release was worship: to “hold a feast,” a sacred festival in God’s honor. The whole conflict of the next chapters turns on one question: who has the right to Israel’s service?

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “hold a feast” refers to celebrating a pilgrimage festival. Later Israel would keep three such feasts each year (23:14–17).

Exodus 5:2
Exodus 5:2
NKJV

Exodus 5:2 And Pharaoh said, "Who is the LORD, that I should obey His voice to let Israel go? I do not know the LORD, nor will I let Israel go."(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 5:2 “Is that so?” retorted Pharaoh. “And who is the LORD? Why should I listen to him and let Israel go? I don’t know the LORD, and I will not let Israel go.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Pharaoh answers, “Who is the LORD, that I should obey His voice to let Israel go?” and declares that he does not know the LORD and will not let Israel go. He refuses to acknowledge the LORD’s right to command him, and the rest of the plague story answers his question.

Verse Explanation

Pharaoh answers, “Who is the LORD, that I should obey His voice to let Israel go?” and declares that he does not know the LORD and will not let Israel go. He refuses to acknowledge the LORD’s right to command him, and the rest of the plague story answers his question.

“And Pharaoh said, ‘Who is the LORD, that I should obey His voice to let Israel go?”

Pharaoh’s reply is contempt. He does not ask in order to learn. He asks to dismiss. In his eyes, the God of a nation of slaves had no claim on Egypt’s king. The rest of the plague story answers his question. By the end, Egypt will know exactly who the LORD is (7:5; 14:4, 18).

“I do not know the LORD, nor will I let Israel go.”

Pharaoh says plainly that he does not know the LORD and will not release His people. The two refusals go together. Because he would not acknowledge the LORD, he would not obey Him. To know the LORD in Exodus is more than knowing about Him. It is recognizing His authority. Pharaoh refuses both.

Study Notes

Throughout the plague narrative, the LORD’s acts show Pharaoh and Egypt who He is: that He is the LORD (7:17), that there is no one like Him (8:10; 9:14), that He is the LORD in the midst of the land (8:22), and that the earth is His (9:29). The first of these reads, “By this you shall know that I am the LORD” (7:17). Pharaoh’s “I do not know the LORD” sets up that theme.

Main Idea

Moses and Aaron explain that the God of the Hebrews has met with them and ask to go three days’ journey to sacrifice, lest He strike them with pestilence or the sword. They present the request as a matter of obedience to their God.

Verse Explanation

Moses and Aaron explain that the God of the Hebrews has met with them and ask to go three days’ journey to sacrifice, lest He strike them with pestilence or the sword. They present the request as a matter of obedience to their God.

“So they said, ‘The God of the Hebrews has met with us.”

Moses and Aaron answer without anger. They explain that “the God of the Hebrews” has met with them. They name God by His relationship with the despised people Pharaoh ruled.

“Please, let us go three days’ journey into the desert and sacrifice to the LORD our God,”

They repeat the modest request God had given them (3:18): a three-day journey into the desert to offer sacrifice. They ask politely, with the word “Please.”

“lest He fall upon us with pestilence or with the sword.”

They add a reason: failing to obey their God would be dangerous. He might strike them with “pestilence,” disease, or “the sword,” violence. They present the request as a matter of obedience to their God, and they speak of His judgment as falling on them, not on Pharaoh.

Verse Explanation

The king asks why Moses and Aaron are taking the people from their work and orders them back to their labor. Pharaoh treats the call to worship as an interruption of Egypt’s work.

“Then the king of Egypt said to them, ‘Moses and Aaron, why do you take the people from their work?”

Pharaoh answers as a slave master. He calls the brothers by name and accuses them of distracting the people from “their work.” In his view, Israel existed to serve Egypt. A call to worship was simply an interruption.

“Get back to your labor.”

Pharaoh dismisses Moses and Aaron with an order to get back to work. He treats the whole matter as a labor dispute.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “labor” here means burdens or forced labor. It is the same word translated “burdens” in 1:11 and 2:11.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Pharaoh complains that the people of the land are now many and that Moses and Aaron are making them rest from their labor. To Pharaoh, Israel’s value lies only in their work for Egypt.

“And Pharaoh said, ‘Look, the people of the land are many now,”

Pharaoh notes how numerous the Israelites have become. The growth that alarmed an earlier king (1:9) is now a workforce Egypt depends on.

“and you make them rest from their labor!”

To Pharaoh, a three-day journey for worship meant lost labor. The word “rest” is related to the word for Sabbath. Pharaoh resents any interruption of their work. To him, Israel’s value lies only in their work for Egypt.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb translated “make them rest” is the verb from which the word “Sabbath” comes. Later God would give Israel a weekly day of rest (16:23–30; 20:8–11).
