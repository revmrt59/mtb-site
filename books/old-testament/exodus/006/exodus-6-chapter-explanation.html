Exodus 6:1
Exodus 6:1
NKJV

Exodus 6:1 Then the LORD said to Moses, "Now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh. For with a strong hand he will let them go, and with a strong hand he will drive them out of his land."(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 6:1 Then the LORD told Moses, “Now you will see what I will do to Pharaoh. When he feels the force of my strong hand, he will let the people go. In fact, he will force them to leave his land!” (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD answers Moses, “Now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh,” and declares that with a strong hand Pharaoh will let Israel go and even drive them out. God answers Moses’ complaint with a promise of His own powerful action.

Verse Explanation

The LORD answers Moses, “Now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh,” and declares that with a strong hand Pharaoh will let Israel go and even drive them out. God answers Moses’ complaint with a promise of His own powerful action.

“Then the LORD said to Moses, ‘Now you shall see what I will do to Pharaoh.”

The LORD does not rebuke Moses for his honest complaint (5:22–23). He answers with a promise. “Now” marks a turning point. Moses has seen what Pharaoh can do. Now he will see what God will do.

“For with a strong hand he will let them go, and with a strong hand he will drive them out of his land.”

Pharaoh had refused to let Israel go. God says that under the pressure of His “strong hand,” Pharaoh will not only release them but “drive them out.” The king who would not let them leave will one day push them out. This happened on the night of the Passover (12:31–33). God answers Moses’ complaint with a promise of His own powerful action.

Study Notes

The phrase “strong hand” appears twice in this verse. It is unclear in Hebrew whether the first refers to God’s hand on Pharaoh or Pharaoh’s hand on Israel. Either way, God’s power stands behind the release.

NKJV

Exodus 6:2 And God spoke to Moses and said to him: "I am the LORD.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 6:2 And God said to Moses, “I am Yahweh — ‘the LORD.’ (NLT)

Main Idea

God speaks to Moses and says, “I am the LORD.” His renewed promise begins with His own name, the foundation for everything He is about to say.

Verse Explanation

God speaks to Moses and says, “I am the LORD.” His renewed promise begins with His own name, the foundation for everything He is about to say.

“And God spoke to Moses and said to him: ‘I am the LORD.”

God begins His renewed promise with His name: “I am the LORD.” Everything He is about to promise rests on who He is. The phrase will frame this whole speech, appearing again in 6:6 and 6:8. Moses had questioned why God had not acted. God answers by reminding him whom he is dealing with.

Study Notes

“I am the LORD” is a formula of self-identification found often in the books of Moses, especially in Leviticus (for example, Leviticus 18:2–5). It grounds God’s commands and promises in His own name.

Exodus 6:3
Exodus 6:3
NKJV

Exodus 6:3 I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, as God Almighty, but by My name LORD I was not known to them.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 6:3 I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob as El-Shaddai — ‘God Almighty’ — but I did not reveal my name, Yahweh, to them. (NLT)

Main Idea

God says He appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as God Almighty, “but by My name LORD I was not known to them.” The patriarchs knew the name, but they had not yet experienced its full meaning, which God now displays by redeeming Israel.

Verse Explanation

God says He appeared to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as God Almighty, “but by My name LORD I was not known to them.” The patriarchs knew the name, but they had not yet experienced its full meaning, which God now displays by redeeming Israel.

“I appeared to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob, as God Almighty,”

God reminds Moses of His dealings with the fathers. He appeared to them “as God Almighty.” This name, El Shaddai in Hebrew, appears in God’s promises to Abraham and Jacob (Genesis 17:1; 35:11). It speaks of God’s power to fulfill what He promises.

“but by My name LORD I was not known to them.”

This does not mean the patriarchs never heard the name LORD. The name appears in their stories (Genesis 15:7; 28:13). The patriarchs knew the name, but they had not yet experienced its full meaning. They saw some of God’s promises kept, such as the birth of Isaac. But the promises named in 6:4–8, the land and the redemption of their descendants, they did not live to see fulfilled. Now God will display the meaning of His name by redeeming Israel and keeping His word. Israel will know Him as the LORD by what He does for them (6:7).

Study Notes

Some interpreters read the clause as a question expecting a yes, asking whether God was not known to the fathers by His name. The NKJV reading, understood as the full experience of the name, fits the following promises.

The Hebrew name El Shaddai is traditionally translated “God Almighty.” Its exact derivation is debated.

Exodus 6:4
NKJV

Exodus 6:4 I have also established My covenant with them, to give them the land of Canaan, the land of their pilgrimage, in which they were strangers.(NKJV)

Exodus 6:4 And I reaffirmed my covenant with them. Under its terms, I promised to give them the land of Canaan, where they were living as foreigners. (NLT)

Main Idea

God says He established His covenant with the patriarchs to give them the land of Canaan, where they lived as strangers. His promise of the land stands behind everything He is about to do.

Verse Explanation

God says He established His covenant with the patriarchs to give them the land of Canaan, where they lived as strangers. His promise of the land stands behind everything He is about to do.

“I have also established My covenant with them,”

God reminds Moses that He made a binding promise with the patriarchs. A “covenant” is a solemn commitment. God bound Himself to them by His own word.

“to give them the land of Canaan, the land of their pilgrimage, in which they were strangers.”

The covenant included “the land of Canaan.” The patriarchs lived there as “strangers,” foreigners who did not own the land. It was “the land of their pilgrimage,” the land where they wandered. They died without possessing it. God’s promise of the land stands behind everything He is about to do.

Study Notes

The phrase “the land of their pilgrimage” reads literally the land of their sojournings. Abraham bought only a burial place there (Genesis 23).

Exodus 6:6
Exodus 6:6
NKJV

Exodus 6:6 Therefore say to the children of Israel: "˜I am the LORD; I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians, I will rescue you from their bondage, and I will redeem you with an outstretched arm and with great judgments.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 6:6 “Therefore, say to the people of Israel: ‘I am the LORD. I will free you from your oppression and will rescue you from your slavery in Egypt. I will redeem you with a powerful arm and great acts of judgment. (NLT)

Main Idea

God tells Moses to say, “I am the LORD,” and promises, “I will bring you out,” rescue them from bondage, and redeem them with an outstretched arm and great judgments. The promises describe a complete deliverance by God’s own power.

Verse Explanation

God tells Moses to say, “I am the LORD,” and promises, “I will bring you out,” rescue them from bondage, and redeem them with an outstretched arm and great judgments. The promises describe a complete deliverance by God’s own power.

“Therefore say to the children of Israel: ‘I am the LORD;”

God gives Moses a message for Israel. It begins again with God’s name. Before God tells them what He will do, He tells them who He is.

“I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians,”

The first promise: God will bring them “out from under the burdens,” the crushing forced labor (1:11; 5:4).

“I will rescue you from their bondage,”

The second promise: God will “rescue” them from slavery. He will snatch them out of Egypt’s grip.

“and I will redeem you with an outstretched arm and with great judgments.”

The third promise: God will “redeem” them. To redeem means to set free by paying a price or by acting as a family protector. In Israel, a kinsman redeemer bought back a relative who had fallen into slavery (Leviticus 25:47–49). God acts as Israel’s Redeemer, the family member who comes to free His people.

He will do it “with an outstretched arm,” a picture of power in action, and “with great judgments” on Egypt. The promises describe a complete deliverance by God’s own power.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb translated “redeem” is the word used for the kinsman redeemer, the family member who protects and recovers what belongs to his relatives (for example, Ruth 3:9–13; 4:4–6). This is its first use in Exodus. Moses’ song later celebrates the people “whom You have redeemed” (15:13).

Exodus 6:7
NKJV

NLT

Exodus 6:7 I will claim you as my own people, and I will be your God. Then you will know that I am the LORD your God who has freed you from your oppression in Egypt. (NLT)

Main Idea

God promises, “I will take you as My people, and I will be your God,” so that they will know that He is the LORD who brought them out. The goal of deliverance is a relationship in which Israel belongs to God and knows Him.

Verse Explanation

God promises, “I will take you as My people, and I will be your God,” so that they will know that He is the LORD who brought them out. The goal of deliverance is a relationship in which Israel belongs to God and knows Him.

“I will take you as My people, and I will be your God.”

God’s purpose goes beyond rescue. God does not only free Israel from Egypt. He takes them for Himself. This promise is the heart of God’s relationship with His people throughout Scripture. It appears again at the end of the Bible: “they shall be His people. God Himself will be with them and be their God” (Revelation 21:3).

“Then you shall know that I am the LORD your God who brings you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians.”

Through the deliverance Israel “shall know” God. They will know Him by experience as the God who rescued them. The goal of deliverance is a relationship in which Israel belongs to God and knows Him.

Study Notes

The language of 6:7 resembles marriage and adoption formulas, in which one party takes another as his own.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Moses speaks these words to Israel, “but they did not heed Moses, because of anguish of spirit and cruel bondage.” The narrator explains their failure to listen by their crushed spirit and harsh slavery.

“So Moses spoke thus to the children of Israel;”

Moses delivered God’s message faithfully.

The people “did not heed Moses.” Earlier they had believed and worshiped (4:31). Now they could not listen. The narrator explains their failure to listen with compassion. They were not stubborn like Pharaoh. They were broken.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase translated “anguish of spirit” reads literally shortness of breath or of spirit. It describes discouragement and exhaustion.

Verse Explanation

Aaron marries Elisheba, the daughter of Amminadab and sister of Nahshon, and she bears Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar. The line of Aaron’s sons, who will serve as priests, is recorded.

“Aaron took to himself Elisheba, daughter of Amminadab, sister of Nahshon, as wife;”

Aaron married Elisheba. She came from the tribe of Judah. Her brother Nahshon became the leader of Judah (Numbers 2:3) and appears in the line of David (Ruth 4:20).

“and she bore him Nadab, Abihu, Eleazar, and Ithamar.”

Aaron’s four sons would become priests. Nadab and Abihu died for offering strange fire (Leviticus 10:1–2). Eleazar succeeded Aaron as high priest (Numbers 20:28). The line of Aaron’s sons, who will serve as priests, is recorded.

Study Notes

The name Elisheba is the Hebrew form of the name Elizabeth. Luke notes that Elizabeth, the mother of John the Baptist, was “of the daughters of Aaron” (Luke 1:5).
