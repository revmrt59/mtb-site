Verse Explanation

The LORD sends Moses to Pharaoh with the word of the God of the Hebrews: “Let My people go, that they may serve Me.” The demand is repeated before the fifth plague.

“Then the LORD said to Moses, ‘Go in to Pharaoh and tell him,”

The fifth plague begins, like the others, with God sending Moses back into the palace. Pharaoh has broken his word twice (8:15, 32), but God still speaks to him before He strikes.

“Thus says the LORD God of the Hebrews: ‘Let My people go, that they may serve Me.”

God again names Himself by His tie to the despised Hebrews, and the demand is unchanged. The issue remains the same: Israel belongs to the LORD and must be free to serve Him.

Exodus 9:3
NKJV

Exodus 9:3 behold, the hand of the LORD will be on your cattle in the field, on the horses, on the donkeys, on the camels, on the oxen, and on the sheep"”a very severe pestilence.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 9:3 the hand of the LORD will strike all your livestock — your horses, donkeys, camels, cattle, sheep, and goats — with a deadly plague. (NLT)

Main Idea

The hand of the LORD will be on Pharaoh’s cattle in the field, horses, donkeys, camels, oxen, and sheep, with “a very severe pestilence.” The plague will strike Egypt’s working animals and wealth.

Verse Explanation

The hand of the LORD will be on Pharaoh’s cattle in the field, horses, donkeys, camels, oxen, and sheep, with “a very severe pestilence.” The plague will strike Egypt’s working animals and wealth.

“behold, the hand of the LORD will be on your cattle in the field,”

The plague will come from “the hand of the LORD” directly. This is the first time the phrase appears in the plagues. No rod is mentioned for this plague. God Himself will strike.

The target is livestock “in the field,” animals out at pasture. That detail matters for understanding 9:6.

“on the horses, on the donkeys, on the camels, on the oxen, and on the sheep—a very severe pestilence.”

The list covers Egypt’s working animals and its wealth: horses for chariots, donkeys and camels for transport, oxen for plowing, sheep for wool and food. A “very severe pestilence” would cripple farming, trade, and transport.

Study Notes

Camels are sometimes said to be out of place in Egypt at this period. Evidence for their use in the ancient Near East is debated, but the text lists them among Egypt’s livestock, and they would not have been the main animal in view.

NLT

Main Idea

The next day the LORD does it, “all the livestock of Egypt died,” but not one of Israel’s livestock dies. God’s word is fulfilled exactly, including the distinction between the peoples.

Verse Explanation

The next day the LORD does it, “all the livestock of Egypt died,” but not one of Israel’s livestock dies. God’s word is fulfilled exactly, including the distinction between the peoples.

“So the LORD did this thing on the next day, and all the livestock of Egypt died;”

God did what He said, on the day He said. The word “all” should be read with 9:3. The plague struck the livestock in the field. Later the Egyptians still had animals to bring in from the hail (9:19–21), so “all” here means all within the scope God announced, or all kinds of livestock, not every single animal in Egypt.

“but of the livestock of the children of Israel, not one died.”

The contrast is total. Egypt’s herds died, and “not one” of Israel’s animals died. God’s word was fulfilled exactly, including the distinction between the peoples.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “all” often means all of a kind or a large group, not every single individual. The context of 9:3 and 9:19–21 shows that limited sense here.

Pharaoh sent to check. He wanted to know whether the distinction was real. His messengers found that “not even one” of Israel’s animals had died. He had the evidence he needed.

“But the heart of Pharaoh became hard, and he did not let the people go.”

Even with the proof in hand, Pharaoh did not yield. His heart “became hard.” Pharaoh saw the evidence of God’s hand and still refused. Evidence alone does not change a heart set against God.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “became hard” here means to become heavy, the same root used in 7:14 and 8:15, 32.

The sixth plague, like the third, comes without warning. God tells the brothers to take “ashes from a furnace,” most likely a kiln where bricks were fired or a furnace used in Egypt’s industry.

Moses is to throw the ashes into the air “in the sight of Pharaoh.” The sign is done publicly so that Pharaoh sees where the plague comes from.

Some interpreters see a fitting irony in the furnace ashes: the kilns that fired the bricks Israel was forced to make become the source of Egypt’s affliction. The text does not draw this connection, so it remains a suggestion.

Exodus 9:12

NLT

Main Idea

“But the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh,” and he did not heed them, just as the LORD had told Moses. For the first time the narrator says that the LORD hardened Pharaoh’s heart, as He announced in Exodus 4:21.

Verse Explanation

“But the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh,” and he did not heed them, just as the LORD had told Moses. For the first time the narrator says that the LORD hardened Pharaoh’s heart, as He announced in Exodus 4:21.

“But the LORD hardened the heart of Pharaoh;”

For the first time, the narrator says directly that “the LORD hardened” Pharaoh’s heart. God had announced this from the beginning (4:21; 7:3). Pharaoh has repeatedly hardened his own heart (8:15, 32). Now God confirms him in the course he has chosen. Pharaoh remains responsible for his refusal, and God carries out His judgment through it.

“and he did not heed them, just as the LORD had spoken to Moses.”

Nothing is out of God’s control. Pharaoh’s refusal unfolds “just as the LORD had spoken.”

Study Notes

Before this verse, the hardening of Pharaoh’s heart is described either as Pharaoh’s own act (8:15, 32) or without naming who hardened it (7:13, 22; 8:19; 9:7). From here on, God is named as hardening Pharaoh in several places (10:1, 20, 27; 11:10; 14:4, 8), though Pharaoh hardens his own heart once more (9:34).

Exodus 9:16
Exodus 9:16
NKJV

Exodus 9:16 But indeed for this purpose I have raised you up, that I may show My power in you, and that My name may be declared in all the earth.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 9:16 But I have spared you for a purpose — to show you my power and to spread my fame throughout the earth. (NLT)

Main Idea

God declares, “for this purpose I have raised you up, that I may show My power in you, and that My name may be declared in all the earth.” God has kept Pharaoh standing so that His power and name may be made known everywhere.

Verse Explanation

God declares, “for this purpose I have raised you up, that I may show My power in you, and that My name may be declared in all the earth.” God has kept Pharaoh standing so that His power and name may be made known everywhere.

“But indeed for this purpose I have raised you up,”

God explains why Pharaoh is still standing. “For this purpose I have raised you up.” The phrase means God has kept Pharaoh in his position and allowed him to remain alive. Pharaoh’s survival is not a sign that he has escaped God. It serves God’s purpose.

“that I may show My power in you, and that My name may be declared in all the earth.”

The purpose is twofold. God will “show My power” through His dealings with Pharaoh, and His “name” will be “declared in all the earth.” The deliverance from Egypt would become known far beyond Egypt. Rahab in Jericho later told the spies, “we have heard how the LORD dried up the water of the Red Sea for you when you came out of Egypt” (Joshua 2:10). God has kept Pharaoh standing so that His power and name may be made known everywhere.

Study Notes

Paul quotes this verse in Romans 9:17: “For this very purpose I have raised you up.” The Greek translation of Exodus reads differently here, roughly “you were preserved.” In Exodus 9:15–16 the point is that God had kept Pharaoh alive instead of cutting him off. Paul’s own wording, “raised you up,” stands closer to the Hebrew.

The Hebrew verb translated “raised you up” means to cause to stand. It can mean to keep someone standing, that is, to preserve him alive.
