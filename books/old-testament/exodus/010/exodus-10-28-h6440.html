FACE - ( panim , Strong's H6440 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word panim refers fundamentally to the physical face or countenance of a person, but it is frequently used in biblical Hebrew as a spatial preposition denoting "presence," "before," or "surface." In Exodus 10:28, when Pharaoh tells Moses, "Get away from me!... take heed to yourself and see my face no more," the phrase carries the weight of a final royal banishment from the king's presence, signifying that any future audience with Pharaoh is terminated under penalty of death.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The lexical form panim is grammatically plural in Hebrew (literally "faces"), which likely reflects the varied features and expressions that make up a human face, or the multifaceted ways a person presents themselves.

• In the ancient Near Eastern royal court, access to the king’s face was the ultimate mark of favor, while being banished from the king's face meant disgrace, exile, or impending doom.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary physical definition is the face, countenance, or surface of a person, animal, or object.

• By extension, it frequently means "presence," "before," "ahead," or "forward," indicating spatial orientation relative to someone.

• In legal or relational contexts, it can signify personal encounter, attention, or favor (e.g., "face to face").

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The word is often used in compound prepositional phrases such as lipnei ("to the face of" / "before").

• It captures the vivid imagery of personal confrontation and direct gaze, where hiddenness is impossible.

4. Theological Implications

• The concept of panim is central to biblical theology regarding human interaction with God. To seek God's face is to seek His living presence and favor (Psalm 27:8), while hiding His face signifies judgment and abandonment (Deuteronomy 31:17).

• In Exodus, the narrative tension builds around competing "faces"—Pharaoh shutting his face to Moses versus Moses and Israel standing before the face of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Exodus 10:28, Pharaoh's command—"see my face no more"—is an act of tyrannical finality.

• Pharaoh is cutting off all diplomatic and relational access, effectively sealing his own doom as the plagues reach their climax.

• Moses immediately accepts the terms, recognizing that Pharaoh's face is now entirely closed to grace: "You have spoken well. I will never see your face again" (Exodus 10:29).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 33:10 - And Jacob said, "No, please, if I have now found favor in your sight, then receive my present from my hand, for I have seen your face as though I had seen the face of God, and you were pleased with me."

• Exodus 33:11 - So the Lord spoke to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend.

• Numbers 6:25 - The Lord make His face shine upon you, and be gracious to you;

• Psalm 27:8 - When You said, "Seek My face," my heart said to You, "Your face, Lord, I will seek."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Because panim is plural in form, translating it often requires contextual adaptation based on whether it refers to physical anatomy, spatial direction, or abstract presence.

• The absolute refusal to look upon someone's face in ancient diplomacy was a declaration of total hostility.
