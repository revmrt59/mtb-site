SERVICE - ( ʿăbōdâ , Strong's H5656 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun ʿăbōdâ refers to labor, work, service, or acts of worship. Rooted in the verb ʿābad (to work or serve), it encompasses both physical toil—such as the hard labor of the Israelites in Egypt—and sacred service directed toward Yahweh. In the context of Exodus 12:26, ʿăbōdâ describes the Passover ritual as a sacred service. Rather than a burdensome obligation, this service is a commemorative act of worship that defines the community's identity and transmits the narrative of redemption to the next generation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun ʿăbōdâ is derived from the common Semitic root ʿ-b-d, which expresses the idea of serving, working, or performing labor. In ancient Near Eastern cultures, service was primarily understood in the context of a servant or slave serving a master, or a subject serving a king. The word-picture behind ʿăbōdâ carries a dual nature: it can represent the heavy, crushing slavery imposed by human taskmasters, or the joyful, voluntary service offered to a sovereign deity. In the book of Exodus, there is a deliberate linguistic shift. The narrative moves the Israelites from the ʿăbōdâ of Pharaoh (crushing bondage) to the ʿăbōdâ of Yahweh (liberated worship and covenantal service).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of ʿăbōdâ includes:

• Labor or physical work associated with field labor, construction, or daily chores.

• Compulsory labor or servitude, such as slavery or vassalage.

• Levitical or priestly service in the Tabernacle and Temple, which includes administrative, physical, and ceremonial duties.

• Acts of worship, religious observance, or ritual devotion directed to God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word ʿăbōdâ presents a powerful visual contrast throughout the Old Testament. In Egypt, the Israelites' "service" was marked by mortar, brick, and rigorous field labor designed to break their spirit. When God delivers His people, He does not liberate them to absolute autonomy; rather, He transfers their allegiance. They are freed from Pharaoh's ʿăbōdâ so they can engage in Yahweh's ʿăbōdâ. This service is not burdensome slavery but life-giving worship. The Tabernacle duties of the Levites are repeatedly described as ʿăbōdâ, portraying religious ritual as a form of holy work or spiritual labor.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, ʿăbōdâ demonstrates that true worship is active and costly; it is not merely passive contemplation but a "service" rendered to a King. The redemption of Israel is directly tied to this service. Yahweh redeems His people to make them a "kingdom of priests" (Exodus 19:6). Furthermore, the word highlights the connection between work and worship. In the biblical worldview, there is no sharp division between the sacred and the secular; the same word used for plowing a field is used for serving at the altar. Worship is the ultimate purpose of human labor.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Exodus 12:26, ʿăbōdâ refers specifically to the Passover ritual. The children of Israel ask, "What do you mean by this service?" Here, the "service" is the slaughtering of the lamb, the smearing of blood, and the eating of the meal in haste. The use of ʿăbōdâ in this redemptive context is highly ironic and profound. The very night they are set free from Egypt's forced ʿăbōdâ, they institute a new ʿăbōdâ of remembrance. This new service is to be an annual, perpetual ordinance. It is designed to provoke questions from future generations, serving as a pedagogical tool to pass down the memory of God's saving power.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In modern Hebrew, the word avodah still carries this rich, double meaning: it is the standard word for "work" or "job," but in religious contexts, it specifically refers to "worship" or "service" (particularly temple service, Avodat HaMikdash). For the Christian reader, this highlights the New Testament reality that our daily work (avodah) can and should be done as an act of worship (avodah) to the Lord (Colossians 3:23-24). Additionally, the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) often translates ʿăbōdâ in ritual contexts using latreia (service, worship), which Paul uses in Romans 12:1 to describe our bodies as a living sacrifice, which is our "reasonable service" (logikē latreia).
