Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 12
Main Idea:

On the night the LORD strikes Egypt’s firstborn, He shelters Israel’s households under the blood of an unblemished lamb, brings His people out after 430 years, and makes that night a memorial to be kept and explained throughout their generations.

Setting:

Exodus 12 opens the fourth major movement of the book. The final plague announced in chapter 11 now falls, and the LORD gives His instructions before it does.

Chapter Outline:

  • Exodus 12:1–13 — The LORD Gives the Passover Lamb and the Sign of the Blood

Key Verse:

“Now the blood shall be a sign for you on the houses where you are. And when I see the blood, I will pass over you; and the plague shall not be on you to destroy you when I strike the land of Egypt.” (Exodus 12:13)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Before the judgment falls, God resets Israel’s calendar: “This month shall be your beginning of months” (12:2). Their year will now begin with redemption. Each household takes a lamb “without blemish” (12:5), kills it at twilight, puts its blood on the doorposts and lintel, and eats it dressed for travel. The blood is God’s appointed sign: “when I see the blood, I will pass over you” (12:13). Protection rests on the sign God gives, not on Israel’s own worthiness, and the people trust His word and obey before anything has happened.

At midnight the LORD strikes every firstborn in Egypt and executes judgment “against all the gods of Egypt” (12:12). There is not a house without a death, except where the blood is. Pharaoh, who once asked “Who is the LORD?”, now tells Israel to go and serve Him, and even asks for a blessing. A family of seventy leaves as about six hundred thousand men on foot, with their children and a mixed multitude, carrying unleavened dough in their haste.

Woven through the story are instructions for remembering it: a feast of unleavened bread, children’s questions to answer, and a Passover open to any outsider willing to join God’s people. The New Testament sees the fulfillment plainly: “Christ, our Passover, was sacrificed for us” (1 Corinthians 5:7), and not one of His bones was broken (John 19:36).

Chapter Sections

Exodus 12:1–13 — The LORD Gives the Passover Lamb and the Sign of the Blood

The LORD makes this month the first of Israel’s year and commands each household to take an unblemished lamb, kill it at twilight, put its blood on the doorposts and lintel, and eat it in haste, ready to leave. He will strike Egypt’s firstborn and judge its gods, but He will pass over every house marked by the blood.

Exodus 12:14–20 — The Day Becomes a Memorial and the Feast of Unleavened Bread

The day is to be kept as a memorial and a feast to the LORD in every generation. For seven days Israel must remove all leaven and eat unleavened bread, with holy gatherings on the first and seventh days, because on that day the LORD brought their armies out of Egypt.

Exodus 12:21–28 — Moses Instructs the Elders and Israel Worships and Obeys

Moses tells the elders to kill the Passover lamb, apply the blood with hyssop, and stay inside until morning, for the LORD will not let the destroyer enter. When their children ask what the service means, they are to tell what the LORD did, and the people bow, worship, and obey.

Exodus 12:29–36 — The LORD Strikes Egypt’s Firstborn and Pharaoh Sends Israel Out

At midnight the LORD strikes every firstborn in Egypt, and a great cry rises in Egypt, for there is not an Egyptian house where someone is not dead. Pharaoh sends Israel out to serve the LORD, the Egyptians urge them to hurry, and the LORD gives Israel favor so that the Egyptians give them silver, gold, and clothing.

Exodus 12:37–42 — Israel Departs After 430 Years on a Night to Be Observed

About six hundred thousand men on foot, besides children, set out from Rameses with a mixed multitude and many animals, baking unleavened cakes because they left in haste. At the end of 430 years, on the very same day, the LORD’s armies go out, and the night becomes one Israel must keep for all generations.

Exodus 12:43–51 — Who May Keep the Passover

The LORD sets the terms of the Passover: no foreigner or hired worker may eat it, but a servant or stranger whose males are circumcised may keep it as one of Israel, under one law for all. It is eaten in one house without breaking a bone of the lamb, and on that same day the LORD brings Israel out of Egypt.

Why This Chapter Matters

Exodus 12 is the heart of the exodus and one of the clearest pictures of salvation in the Old Testament. God provides the lamb, appoints the blood as the sign, and promises to pass over those who shelter under it. Jesus ate the Passover with His disciples the night before He died and gave it new meaning in His own body and blood (Matthew 26:17–29).

The chapter also shows that God wants His saving acts remembered. He builds the memory into a calendar, a meal, and the questions of children, so that every generation can say what the LORD did for them. The door to the Passover stood open to any outsider who joined the LORD’s people. From the beginning, God’s redemption was larger than one family line.

Key Themes to Watch For

The lamb and the blood. God provides an unblemished lamb and makes its blood the sign of protection (12:3–7, 13, 21–23).

Judgment on Egypt and its gods. The LORD strikes the firstborn and judges “all the gods of Egypt” (12:12, 29–30).

Memorial and teaching. The night is to be remembered by feast and explained to children in every generation (12:14, 24–27, 42).

Haste and readiness. Israel eats dressed to travel and leaves with unleavened dough (12:11, 34, 39).

One law for all. Outsiders may join the Passover through circumcision and share one law with the native-born (12:43–49).

Key Words & Concepts

Passover (12:11). The LORD’s passing over the houses marked with blood, and the meal that remembers it. Paul calls Christ “our Passover” (1 Corinthians 5:7).

Unleavened bread (12:8, 15–20). Bread made without yeast. It recalls the haste of leaving Egypt, and leaven is removed from every house for the seven days of the feast.

Hyssop (12:22). A bushy plant used to brush the blood onto the doorposts and lintel.

The destroyer (12:23). The agent of judgment that the LORD did not allow into houses marked by the blood.

Four hundred and thirty years (12:40–41). The length of Israel’s sojourn as the NKJV records it. Faithful readers differ over whether it covers Israel’s time in Egypt alone or reaches back to the promise to Abraham (compare Galatians 3:17).

Mixed multitude (12:38). Non-Israelites who left Egypt with Israel. Their presence hints at God’s welcome to outsiders, though they later became a source of complaint (Numbers 11:4).
