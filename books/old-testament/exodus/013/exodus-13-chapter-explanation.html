Exodus 13:2
Exodus 13:2
NKJV

Exodus 13:2 "Consecrate to Me all the firstborn, whatever opens the womb among the children of Israel, both of man and beast; it is Mine."(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 13:2 “Dedicate to me every firstborn among the Israelites. The first offspring to be born, of both humans and animals, belongs to me.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD commands, “Consecrate to Me all the firstborn,” both of man and beast, saying, “it is Mine.” Because God spared Israel’s firstborn when He struck Egypt’s, every firstborn in Israel belongs to Him.

Verse Explanation

The LORD commands, “Consecrate to Me all the firstborn,” both of man and beast, saying, “it is Mine.” Because God spared Israel’s firstborn when He struck Egypt’s, every firstborn in Israel belongs to Him.

“Consecrate to Me all the firstborn, whatever opens the womb among the children of Israel, both of man and beast;”

To “consecrate” means to set apart as holy, belonging to God. The phrase “whatever opens the womb” describes the first offspring a mother bears. Here God states His claim broadly, “both of man and beast.” Verses 12–15 then show how the law worked in practice: the firstborn males of the animals belonged to the LORD, and among the people it was the firstborn sons who were redeemed (13:12–13, 15).

“it is Mine.”

God gives the reason in a few words. On the night of judgment, God struck Egypt’s firstborn and spared Israel’s (12:12–13). The firstborn who lived now belong to Him. Numbers 3:13 states the reason directly: when God struck Egypt’s firstborn, “I sanctified to Myself all the firstborn in Israel.”

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb translated “consecrate” means to make holy or set apart. It is related to the word for “holy” used throughout the law.

Later God took the Levites in place of Israel’s firstborn sons for service at the tabernacle (Numbers 3:11–13, 40–51).

“And Moses said to the people: ‘Remember this day in which you went out of Egypt, out of the house of bondage;”

Moses tells the people to “remember this day.” Egypt was “the house of bondage,” literally the house of slaves. The day they left it must never be forgotten.

“for by strength of hand the LORD brought you out of this place.”

The deliverance was not Israel’s own doing. The phrase “strength of hand” pictures God’s power at work against Egypt. The phrase returns three more times in this chapter (13:9, 14, 16).

“No leavened bread shall be eaten.”

The remembrance is tied to the bread. Unleavened bread recalls the haste of the night they left (12:34, 39).

Verse Explanation

Moses names the month: “Abib.” The name means fresh ears of grain, the season of early harvest in spring. The exodus is fixed in time so that its remembrance can return every year.

The land was sworn to the fathers, Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob (Genesis 15:18–21; 26:3; 28:13). “A land flowing with milk and honey” describes rich pasture for flocks and abundant produce. God had used the same words at the burning bush (3:8, 17).

“that you shall keep this service in this month.”

In the land they will keep “this service.” The feast is not only for the wilderness. It will mark Israel’s life in the land God gives.

The feast is to be like “a sign” on the hand and “a memorial between your eyes.” The picture is of something always before them, shaping what they do with their hands and what they keep in view.

The reason again is the LORD’s “strong hand.” God’s power in the exodus is the foundation for Israel’s obedience.

Jesus mentions phylacteries in Matthew 23:5.

Exodus 13:13
Exodus 13:13
NKJV

Exodus 13:13 But every firstborn of a donkey you shall redeem with a lamb; and if you will not redeem it, then you shall break its neck. And all the firstborn of man among your sons you shall redeem.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 13:13 A firstborn donkey may be bought back from the LORD by presenting a lamb or young goat in its place. But if you do not buy it back, you must break its neck. However, you must buy back every firstborn son. (NLT)

Main Idea

Every firstborn donkey is to be redeemed with a lamb or else have its neck broken, and every firstborn son must be redeemed. Firstborn sons are never sacrificed; they are redeemed, because God claims them as His own.

Verse Explanation

Every firstborn donkey is to be redeemed with a lamb or else have its neck broken, and every firstborn son must be redeemed. Firstborn sons are never sacrificed; they are redeemed, because God claims them as His own.

“But every firstborn of a donkey you shall redeem with a lamb;”

A donkey was an unclean animal and could not be offered in sacrifice. Its firstborn had to be redeemed “with a lamb.” To redeem means to buy back by paying a price. The lamb was given in its place.

“and if you will not redeem it, then you shall break its neck.”

If the owner would not redeem the donkey, it had to be killed. It could not simply be kept. Either way, God’s claim on the firstborn had to be honored.

“And all the firstborn of man among your sons you shall redeem.”

Firstborn sons were never to be sacrificed. Every one of them was to be redeemed. God claimed them as His own, and the redemption price acknowledged His claim. This set Israel apart from nations around them that sacrificed children (Leviticus 18:21; Deuteronomy 12:31).

Study Notes

Numbers 18:15–16 sets the redemption price for a firstborn son at five shekels of silver, paid when the child was a month old.

Luke records that Mary and Joseph brought Jesus to the temple “to present Him to the Lord,” citing this law of the firstborn (Luke 2:22–24).

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

The father explains that when Pharaoh stubbornly refused, the LORD killed all Egypt’s firstborn, so Israel sacrifices the firstborn males of its animals and redeems its firstborn sons. The practice remembers both God’s judgment on Egypt and His sparing of Israel’s firstborn.

“And it came to pass, when Pharaoh was stubborn about letting us go,”

The father explains the background. Pharaoh “was stubborn,” hardening himself against letting Israel go.

“that the LORD killed all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, both the firstborn of man and the firstborn of beast.”

The Hebrew verb translated “was stubborn” means to make hard. It is a different word from the ones used for Pharaoh’s heart in the plague chapters, though it describes the same stubbornness.

Verse Explanation

When Pharaoh lets the people go, God does not lead them by the near road through the land of the Philistines, lest they change their minds when they see war and return to Egypt. God chooses the route with care for His people’s weakness.

“Then it came to pass, when Pharaoh had let the people go, that God did not lead them by way of the land of the Philistines, although that was near;”

The narrative resumes with Israel’s route. The shortest road to Canaan ran along the Mediterranean coast through “the land of the Philistines.” It was “near,” but God did not lead them that way.

“for God said, ‘Lest perhaps the people change their minds when they see war, and return to Egypt.”

God gives His reason. The coastal road was guarded by fortresses and would have meant battle. Israel had just come out of slavery and was not ready for war. God knew they might “change their minds” and go back. God chooses the route with care for His people’s weakness.

Study Notes

Egypt maintained a line of forts along the coastal road into Canaan, sometimes called the Way of Horus.

The same pillar appears again at the sea (14:19–20, 24) and stays with Israel to the end of the book (40:34–38; compare Numbers 9:15–23).

Main Idea

The LORD does not take away the pillar of cloud by day or the pillar of fire by night from before the people. His guiding presence remains with them continually.

Verse Explanation

The LORD does not take away the pillar of cloud by day or the pillar of fire by night from before the people. His guiding presence remains with them continually.

“He did not take away the pillar of cloud by day or the pillar of fire by night from before the people.”

God’s presence did not come and go. He “did not take away” the pillar. Day after day and night after night, it remained before the people. His guiding presence was with them continually.
