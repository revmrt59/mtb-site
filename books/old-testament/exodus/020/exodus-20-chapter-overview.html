Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 20
Main Idea:

The LORD who brought Israel out of the house of bondage speaks the Ten Commandments to His redeemed people, calling them to wholehearted worship of Him and faithful love toward their neighbors, and the trembling people learn the fear of God that keeps them from sin.

Setting:

Exodus 20 follows the LORD’s descent on Sinai in fire (19:16–25). Verse 22 begins the laws Moses later calls “the Book of the Covenant” (24:7), running through 23:33.

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

God speaks, and His first words are not a command but a reminder of who He is and what He has done: “I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage” (20:2). The commandments are given to a people already rescued. Obedience is their response to redeeming love, never the price of it.

The first four commandments concern Israel’s worship. They are to have no other gods, make no image to bow down to, honor His name, and keep the Sabbath holy, because God Himself rested on the seventh day and blessed it. He is “a jealous God” (20:5), not because He is insecure, but because He rightly claims the whole heart of the people He has made His own, and His mercy reaches “to thousands, to those who love Me” (20:6). The last six concern life with others: honoring parents and protecting life, marriage, property, truth, and the desires of the heart. The tenth commandment reaches past action to longing.

The people tremble and ask Moses to speak for God. Moses gives them a better kind of fear: “Do not fear; for God has come to test you, and that His fear may be before you, so that you may not sin” (20:20). Then he draws near the thick darkness where God is.

Chapter Sections

Exodus 20:1–2 — The LORD Who Brought Israel Out Speaks

God speaks all these words and begins by naming Himself as the LORD their God who brought them out of slavery in Egypt. The commandments are given to people He has already set free.

Exodus 20:3–11 — The Commandments Concerning Israel’s Worship of the LORD

Israel is to have no other gods, make no carved image to bow down to, and not misuse the LORD’s name, for He is a jealous God whose mercy reaches thousands who love Him. They are to keep the Sabbath holy, resting on the seventh day as God rested after creation.

Exodus 20:12–17 — The Commandments Concerning Life with One’s Neighbor

Israel is to honor father and mother, with the promise of long life in the land. They must not murder, commit adultery, steal, lie about a neighbor, or covet anything that belongs to a neighbor.

Exodus 20:18–21 — The People Fear and Moses Draws Near the Thick Darkness

Terrified by the thunder, lightning, trumpet, and smoke, the people stand far off and ask Moses to speak to them instead of God. Moses tells them that God has come to test them so that reverent fear will keep them from sin, and he draws near the thick darkness where God is.

Exodus 20:22–26 — No Gods of Silver or Gold, and an Altar for the LORD

The LORD reminds Israel that He has spoken from heaven and forbids them to make gods of silver or gold alongside Him. He tells them to build an altar of earth or uncut stone without steps and promises to come and bless them wherever He causes His name to be remembered.

Why This Chapter Matters

Exodus 20 shows that God’s law is a gift to a redeemed people. It exposes sin, restrains evil, and guides those who already belong to Him. Of its moral, civil, and ceremonial laws, the Ten Commandments are moral law and still bind believers, not as a way to be saved, but as the life the Spirit writes on their hearts. Israel’s civil laws governed the nation, and the ceremonial laws are fulfilled in Christ (Hebrews 10:1). Some Christians understand believers to live instead under the law of Christ (1 Corinthians 9:21; Galatians 6:2). Jesus carries the law’s demands into the heart, to anger and lust (Matthew 5:17–28).

The Sabbath, the fourth commandment, belongs to this moral law and rests on God’s own pattern in creation. Believers are not saved by keeping a day, and they have liberty about which day to set apart, but they are called to a deliberate weekly rhythm of ceasing from work to rest and delight in the Lord. Neglecting that rest starves the soul.

Key Themes to Watch For

Redemption before commandment. God names Himself as Israel’s Redeemer before He gives any command (20:2).

Wholehearted worship. The first four commandments claim Israel’s exclusive worship of the LORD (20:3–11).

Love for neighbor. The last six commandments protect family, life, marriage, property, truth, and the heart’s desires (20:12–17).

The fear of God. Reverent fear, not panic, keeps God’s people from sin (20:18–20).

Worship on God’s terms. God directs how His altar is built and promises to come and bless (20:22–26).

Key Words & Concepts

The Ten Commandments (20:3–17). The Reformed numbering treats verses 1–2 as the introduction, the ban on images (20:4–6) as the second commandment, and coveting (20:17) as the tenth.

A jealous God (20:5). God’s rightful, loving claim on His people’s whole devotion. The consequences of sin pass down as children learn and continue in their parents’ ways, among “those who hate Me” (20:5), while each person answers for his own guilt (Ezekiel 18:20).

Sabbath (20:8–11). The seventh day, blessed and set apart by God at creation. Israel had already learned its rest through the manna (16:23–30).

Covet (20:17). To desire for oneself what belongs to someone else. This commandment reaches the heart itself.

Book of the Covenant (24:7). The collection of laws that begins at 20:22 and runs through chapter 23.
