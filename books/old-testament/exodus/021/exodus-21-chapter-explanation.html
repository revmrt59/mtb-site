Exodus 21:1
Exodus 21:1
NKJV

Exodus 21:1 "Now these are the judgments which you shall set before them:(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 21:1 “These are the regulations you must present to Israel. (NLT)

Main Idea

“Now these are the judgments which you shall set before them.” God begins the laws that will govern Israel’s life together as His people.

Verse Explanation

“Now these are the judgments which you shall set before them.” God begins the laws that will govern Israel’s life together as His people.

“Now these are the judgments which you shall set before them:”

The Ten Commandments state the basic law of the covenant. Now God gives “judgments,” legal rulings that apply those commands to specific cases in Israel’s life. Moses is to “set” them “before them,” placing them before the people to be known and followed. These chapters (20:22–23:33) form what 24:7 calls the Book of the Covenant.

Study Notes

Many of these laws are written as case law, in the form if a man does this, then this follows. They show how God’s justice applies to the ordinary disputes of life. They were given to Israel as a nation under God, and their particular penalties belong to that covenant order.

Exodus 21:2
Exodus 21:2
NKJV

Exodus 21:2 If you buy a Hebrew servant, he shall serve six years; and in the seventh he shall go out free and pay nothing.(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 21:2 “If you buy a Hebrew slave, he may serve for no more than six years. Set him free in the seventh year, and he will owe you nothing for his freedom. (NLT)

Main Idea

A Hebrew servant who is bought serves six years and goes free in the seventh without paying anything. The law limits servitude and guarantees release.

Verse Explanation

A Hebrew servant who is bought serves six years and goes free in the seventh without paying anything. The law limits servitude and guarantees release.

“If you buy a Hebrew servant, he shall serve six years;”

The first laws concern a “Hebrew servant.” Such a person usually entered service to escape debt or poverty (Leviticus 25:39). The law limits his service to “six years.”

“and in the seventh he shall go out free and pay nothing.”

In the seventh year he goes “free.” He does not have to buy his freedom. Deuteronomy 15:13–14 adds that the master must send him away with generous provision from his flock, threshing floor, and winepress. The law limits servitude and guarantees release. These laws regulated a practice that existed in a fallen world. They did not invent or endorse it, much as the law regulated divorce because of hardness of heart (Matthew 19:8). The protections limited the power of masters and kept the servant’s dignity in view.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

“He who strikes a man so that he dies shall surely be put to death.” Taking a human life deserves the most serious penalty.

“He who strikes a man so that he dies shall surely be put to death.”

The law turns to capital crimes. A man who kills another “shall surely be put to death.” The principle goes back to Genesis 9:6: whoever sheds man’s blood, by man his blood shall be shed, for God made man in His image. Taking a human life deserves the most serious penalty. The principle of capital punishment runs through Scripture (Genesis 9:6; Romans 13:4). The law also required at least two or three witnesses before a death sentence (Deuteronomy 17:6), guarding against error.

“Notwithstanding, if he remains alive a day or two, he shall not be punished;”

If the servant lives “a day or two,” the master is not punished. One possible explanation of the distinction is that the delay suggested the master had not intended to kill. The text itself gives no finding about his intent. It states only the time condition and the reason that follows.

The reason given is that the servant “is his property,” literally his money. The master has already suffered financial loss. The case reflects the limits of servitude in Israel’s ancient setting while still placing the master under the law. It also reveals how far that setting was from God’s design for human dignity. The New Testament later calls masters to give their servants what is just and fair, knowing that they too have a Master in heaven (Colossians 4:1), and Paul asks Philemon to receive Onesimus as a beloved brother (Philemon 16).

Exodus 21:24
Exodus 21:24
NKJV

Exodus 21:24 eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot,(NKJV)

NLT

Exodus 21:24 an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth, a hand for a hand, a foot for a foot, (NLT)

Main Idea

The penalty continues “eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot.” The rule sets a limit for judges so that punishment matches the harm and does not become revenge.

Verse Explanation

The penalty continues “eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot.” The rule sets a limit for judges so that punishment matches the harm and does not become revenge.

“eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot,”

This principle is a rule for judges. Punishment must match the injury, no more and no less. It stopped endless blood feuds, where one injury led to a greater one in revenge. Jesus addresses personal revenge, not the work of courts, when He says, “But I tell you not to resist an evil person” (Matthew 5:38–42). The public duty of proportionate justice remains distinct from the private refusal to retaliate.

Study Notes

The Latin name for this principle is lex talionis, the law of retaliation. Leviticus 24:19–20 and Deuteronomy 19:21 repeat it. In practice, many cases were settled by payment rather than physical penalty (compare 21:30).
