Exodus 21 Chapter Outline

Chapter Focus

Exodus 21 begins the judgments of the Book of the Covenant with laws that limit and regulate servitude, deal with taking human life and attacks on parents, and set penalties for injuries in proportion to the harm. The chapter closes with cases about goring oxen and open pits that hold people responsible for harm they could have foreseen.

Chapter Sections

1. Exodus 21:1–11 — Laws Protecting Hebrew Servants

A Hebrew servant goes free in the seventh year without payment and may stay for life only by his own declaration, “I love my master, my wife, and my children” (Exodus 21:5). If the master who betrothed a daughter sold as a female servant is displeased with her, he must let her be redeemed and may not sell her to a foreign people, and if he takes another wife, he must not reduce her food, clothing, and marriage rights, or she goes out free.

2. Exodus 21:12–17 — Capital Offenses Against Life and Parents

Whoever strikes a man so that he dies shall be put to death, but the LORD appoints a place of refuge for one who killed without lying in wait, while a deliberate murderer may be taken even from His altar. Striking or cursing father or mother is a capital offense, and so is kidnapping a person, whether he has been sold or is still found in the kidnapper’s hand.

3. Exodus 21:18–27 — Penalties for Injury in Proportion to the Harm

An assailant pays for lost time and healing, a master is punished if a servant he beats dies under his hand but not if the servant survives a day or two, and if a pregnant woman is struck and gives birth prematurely, the offender is fined when no harm follows but must give “life for life, eye for eye” (Exodus 21:23–24) when harm does follow. A master who destroys a servant’s eye or knocks out a tooth must let the servant go free for its sake.

4. Exodus 21:28–32 — The Goring Ox and Its Owner

An ox that gores a person to death is stoned, but its owner is acquitted unless he knew that it had gored before and did not confine it. In that case the owner’s life is forfeit unless a ransom is imposed, and the same judgment applies if the ox gores a son or a daughter, but if it gores a male or female servant, the owner pays thirty shekels of silver to the servant’s master and the ox is stoned.

5. Exodus 21:33–36 — Liability for a Pit and for Animals Harmed

A man who leaves a pit uncovered must pay for an animal that falls into it. When one ox kills another, the owners share the loss, unless the owner knew his ox tended to gore and did not confine it, in which case he must pay ox for ox.
