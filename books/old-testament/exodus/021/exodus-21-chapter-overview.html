Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 21
Main Idea:

The LORD begins the judgments of the Book of the Covenant with laws that limit servitude, protect human life and the honor of parents, set penalties in proportion to the harm done, and hold people responsible for dangers they could have foreseen.

Setting:

Exodus 21 follows the Ten Commandments and the instructions about the altar (20:1–26). Its case laws applied God’s commands to the everyday life of Israel as a nation.

Chapter Outline:

  • Exodus 21:1–11 — Laws Protecting Hebrew Servants

  • Exodus 21:12–17 — Capital Offenses Against Life and Parents

  • Exodus 21:18–27 — Penalties for Injury in Proportion to the Harm

  • Exodus 21:28–32 — The Goring Ox and Its Owner

  • Exodus 21:33–36 — Liability for a Pit and for Animals Harmed

Key Verse:

“‘He who kidnaps a man and sells him, or if he is found in his hand, shall surely be put to death.” (Exodus 21:16)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

The judgments open with servants. A Hebrew servant usually entered service because of debt or poverty, while the law elsewhere allowed foreign servants to be bought for life (Leviticus 25:44–46). These laws regulated a practice that already existed in a fallen world, much as the law regulated divorce (Matthew 19:8); they did not invent or endorse it. A Hebrew servant went free in the seventh year and paid nothing. He could stay for life only by his own declaration: “I love my master, my wife, and my children” (21:5). A woman sold into service kept her food, clothing, and marriage rights, or she went free. A master who destroyed a servant’s eye or even a tooth had to set the servant free. The cases also show the limits of that ancient setting, for verse 21 calls the servant the master’s “property.”

Life is guarded with the strongest penalties. Murder, violence or cursing against parents, and kidnapping, which strikes at the root of the slave trade, are capital crimes. Yet God provides a place of refuge for one who kills without intent. Injuries are answered in proportion to the harm, “eye for eye, tooth for tooth” (21:24), a limit for judges that stopped blood feuds. The chapter ends with goring oxen and open pits. A person who knows about a danger and does nothing is held responsible for the harm it causes.

Chapter Sections

Exodus 21:1–11 — Laws Protecting Hebrew Servants

A Hebrew servant goes free in the seventh year without paying anything and stays for life only by his own declared choice. A daughter sold into service must be allowed redemption if her master rejects her, may not be sold abroad, and goes free if he denies her food, clothing, or marriage rights.

Exodus 21:12–17 — Capital Offenses Against Life and Parents

Whoever strikes a person so that he dies is to be put to death, but God provides a place of refuge for one who kills without intent, while a deliberate murderer may be taken even from the altar. Striking or cursing a parent and kidnapping a person are also capital crimes.

Exodus 21:18–27 — Penalties for Injury in Proportion to the Harm

An attacker pays for the injured man’s lost time and healing, and a master who beats a servant to death is punished. If a pregnant woman is struck and gives birth early, a fine applies when no harm follows, but harm to mother or child is answered life for life and eye for eye, and a servant who loses an eye or a tooth to his master goes free.

Exodus 21:28–32 — The Goring Ox and Its Owner

An ox that gores a person to death is stoned, but its owner goes free unless he knew it was dangerous and did not confine it. In that case his life is forfeit unless a ransom is set, and if the ox kills a servant, the owner pays thirty shekels of silver.

Exodus 21:33–36 — Liability for a Pit and for Animals Harmed

A man who leaves a pit uncovered must pay for any animal that falls in. When one ox kills another, the owners share the loss, unless the owner knew his ox was dangerous, in which case he pays ox for ox.

Why This Chapter Matters

Exodus 21 shows that God cares about justice in the ordinary details of life. These civil laws belonged to Israel as a nation and are not binding on the church in their specific form, but their principles reflect God’s own character and still give wisdom. Human life is sacred, punishment must fit the crime, and people are accountable for dangers they know about.

The chapter also limits revenge. “Eye for eye” (21:24) was a ceiling for judges, not permission for private payback, and Jesus forbids personal retaliation (Matthew 5:38–42). Scripture still affirms that a just government may punish the gravest crimes, with careful safeguards against error (Genesis 9:6; Romans 13:4; Deuteronomy 17:6). These laws must never be used to defend trafficking or treating people as property.

Key Themes to Watch For

Limits on servitude. Service ends in the seventh year, permanent service requires the servant’s own choice, and lasting injury frees the servant (21:2–11, 26–27).

The sanctity of life. Murder, attacks on parents, and kidnapping carry the heaviest penalty (21:12–17).

Proportionate justice. Penalties match the harm and limit revenge (21:23–25).

Responsibility for foreseeable harm. Owners who know of a danger and ignore it are held accountable (21:29, 33–36).

Key Words & Concepts

Judgments (21:1). Case laws that applied God’s commands to particular situations in Israel’s common life.

Pierce his ear (21:6). A public sign at the doorpost that a servant chose to stay in the household for life out of love.

A place where he may flee (21:13). A refuge for someone who killed without intent, later established as the cities of refuge.

Gives birth prematurely (21:22). The NKJV describes a premature live birth, so the harm clause protects both mother and child. Some read the verse as describing a miscarriage instead.

Eye for eye (21:24). A rule for judges that the penalty must match the injury, which set a limit and prevented endless revenge.
