TRESPASS - ( peshaʿ , Strong's H6588 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun peshaʿ fundamentally refers to rebellion, transgression, or a willful violation of a standard, boundary, or covenant. While frequently used in theological contexts to denote rebellion against God's sovereign authority, its occurrence in Exodus 22:9 highlights its civil and legal application. In this context, peshaʿ represents a legal offense or property violation—specifically, the dishonest appropriation or withholding of another person's property. The law of Moses treats this breach of trust not merely as a private dispute, but as a serious infraction requiring divine arbitration and restitution.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb pashaʿ means to rebel, revolt, or break away from authority. In ancient Near Eastern contexts, this word described a vassal state breaking its treaty or rebelling against its sovereign lord. The noun peshaʿ, therefore, carries a heavy connotation of willful defiance, crossing a boundary, or breaking a covenantal relationship. It is not an accidental slip or a mistake of ignorance; it is a conscious act of stepping over a line of authority or violating a relationship of trust. When applied to civil law, as in Exodus 22:9, it depicts an act where an individual deliberately oversteps the boundaries of neighborly property rights and covenant obligations, treating another's goods as their own.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun peshaʿ has a semantic range that includes:

• Rebellion or revolt against authority (political or divine).

• Transgression, offense, or crime (moral or legal).

• Property violation, breach of trust, or civil trespass.

Unlike other Hebrew words for sin, such as chata'at (missing the mark) or 'avon (iniquity/crookedness), peshaʿ specifically emphasizes the rebellious attitude and the relational breach involved in the act. In a legal context like Exodus 22:9, it denotes a civil offense—specifically, the illegal withholding, misappropriation, or claiming of another's lost or entrusted property.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Old Testament, peshaʿ is frequently used in parallel with other terms for sin to show the completeness of human rebellion and God's forgiveness.

• The primary word picture is that of crossing a boundary. God sets physical, moral, and covenantal boundaries; peshaʿ is the act of stepping over those lines.

• In personal relationships, it represents a breach of trust or betrayal of a covenant partner.

• In the legal codes of the Torah, the word shifts from a cosmic or national rebellion to an interpersonal civil crime. To take or keep what belongs to a neighbor is to rebel against the social fabric established by God's covenant.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, peshaʿ demonstrates that sin is never a victimless, private affair. Even when the "trespass" involves physical property (an ox, a donkey, or clothing), it is still classified as a peshaʿ. This teaches several critical theological truths:

• The Interconnection of Holy and Common: A crime against a neighbor's property is a sin against God. The neighborly covenant is underwritten by Yahweh Himself.

• The Reality of Depravity: Human beings willfully violate established boundaries for personal gain.

• The Necessity of Justice: Because a trespass violates God’s order, it cannot simply be overlooked. It requires formal resolution, restitution, and occasionally divine judgment by bringing the matter before God's representatives.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Exodus 22:9, peshaʿ is translated as "trespass" or "property violation." The context is a dispute over lost or entrusted property. If someone is holding another person's property (such as an ox, donkey, sheep, or clothing) and another claims, "This is mine," it becomes a "matter of trespass."

• This is not a violent crime like highway robbery, but a covert breach of trust—withholding what belongs to another or lying about finding a lost item.

• The law requires both parties to present their case "before the judges" (literally ha-elohim, which can refer to God's representatives/judges or to God Himself via an oath or sanctuary inquiry).

• The determination of peshaʿ carries a heavy penalty: the guilty party must pay "double" to his neighbor. This punitive restitution is designed to deter dishonest appropriation and restore covenantal balance.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 31:36: "Then Jacob was angry and rebuked Laban, and Jacob answered and said to Laban: 'What is my trespass (peshaʿ)? What is my sin, that you have so hotly pursued me?'"

• Genesis 50:17: "'I beg you, please forgive the trespass (peshaʿ) of your brothers and their sin; for they did evil to you.' Now please, forgive the trespass (peshaʿ) of the servants of the God of your father."

• Exodus 34:7: "...forgiving iniquity and transgression (peshaʿ) and sin..."

• Psalm 32:1: "Blessed is he whose transgression (peshaʿ) is forgiven, whose sin is covered."

• Proverbs 17:9: "He who covers a transgression (peshaʿ) seeks love, but he who repeats a matter separates friends."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Septuagint (LXX) Translation: The Greek translators of the Septuagint translated peshaʿ in Exodus 22:9 using the term adikēma (unrighteousness, wrongdoing, injury, crime). This underscores that the translators understood the Hebrew word here to mean a legal and moral injury inflicted on a neighbor, rather than merely an administrative error.

• Double Restitution: Under Mosaic law, the penalty for peshaʿ in this context is double (shnayim) payment. This contrasts with voluntary confession of a trespass (Leviticus 6:1-5), which required restoring the principal plus only one-fifth (twenty percent) and a guilt offering. This difference shows that denying a trespass and forcing a legal or divine trial carries a much higher penalty when guilt is proven.
