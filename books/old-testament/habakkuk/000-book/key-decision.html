Important questions in the text, and how MTB understands them.

Book Questions

If God raised up Babylon, did He approve of its cruelty? (Habakkuk 1:6, 11; 2:6–20)

God says, “I am raising up the Chaldeans” (1:6), the Babylonians, to judge Judah. Yet the same book condemns Babylon for pride, plunder, bloodshed, exploitation, and idolatry, and announces five woes against it (2:6–20). Both are true. God governs the nations, and Babylon is fully guilty for what it chooses to do.

God’s use of a nation never approves that nation’s sin.

Read: Habakkuk 1:5–11, Habakkuk 2:6–20, Isaiah 10:5–12 · Related: What is Babylon’s offense in verse 11?

Chapter Questions

Habakkuk 1 · 3 questions

Whose violence is Habakkuk complaining about? (vv. 2–4)

Habakkuk cries out over “plundering and violence,” strife, a law that is “powerless,” and justice that “never goes forth,” because “the wicked surround the righteous” (vv. 3–4). MTB understands this most likely as injustice inside Judah, among Habakkuk’s own people. God’s answer, sending Babylon in judgment, fits that reading.

This matters for how the book flows. Habakkuk’s first complaint is about God’s people. His second complaint is about the far more violent nation God will use to judge them (vv. 12–17).

Read: Habakkuk 1:2–6, Habakkuk 1:12–17

What is Babylon’s offense in verse 11?

The NKJV reads, “He commits offense, ascribing this power to his god” (v. 11). Other translations render the line as Babylon making its own strength its god. The Hebrew can be taken either way.

Both readings say the same basic thing: Babylon takes for itself, or gives to a false god, the honor that belongs to the LORD. That pride is the root of its guilt.

Read: Habakkuk 1:11, Habakkuk 2:4–5, Habakkuk 2:18–19 · Related: If God raised up Babylon, did He approve of its cruelty?

Does “We shall not die” promise that no believer will be harmed? (v. 12)

Habakkuk says, “Are You not from everlasting, O LORD my God, my Holy One? We shall not die” (v. 12). MTB’s preferred reading is confidence that God’s people as a whole will survive the judgment. The LORD has appointed Babylon “for correction,” not to wipe His people out.

It is not a promise that no individual will suffer or die. Habakkuk himself expects real loss (3:16–17).

Read: Habakkuk 1:12, Habakkuk 3:16–17

Habakkuk 2 · 3 questions

Does “Write the vision and make it plain” teach us to write down our goals? (vv. 2–3)

God tells Habakkuk, “Write the vision and make it plain on tablets, that he may run who reads it” (v. 2). The “vision” is God’s own answer about the coming judgment and the fate of the proud. It must be written plainly because it is sure: “Though it tarries, wait for it; because it will surely come” (v. 3).

The verse is often used to teach people to write down personal goals or dreams. That is not its contextual meaning. The point is that God’s word is reliable, and His people wait for it in trust.

Readers differ on the runner. He may be a messenger who carries the message, or simply someone who can read it easily and act on it.

Read: Habakkuk 2:1–3, Hebrews 10:36–37

Does “the just shall live by his faith” mean faith or faithfulness? (v. 4)

“Behold the proud, his soul is not upright in him; but the just shall live by his faith” (v. 4). In Habakkuk the contrast is between the proud conqueror who trusts his own power and the righteous person who trusts God while waiting for what God has promised.

MTB defines faith as the persuasion that God is who He has revealed Himself to be in His Word and that He is faithful to perform all He has promised. More fully, faith is the wholehearted persuasion that God is who He has revealed Himself to be in His Word and that He is faithful to perform all He has promised, resulting in a confident reliance upon Him.

In Habakkuk that persuasion takes the form of waiting. God has spoken, and the righteous person relies on Him until the vision comes (vv. 2–3). Such faith endures and produces faithful obedience, so MTB holds faith and faithfulness together. It is not a choice between believing and obeying.

The New Testament quotes this verse three times. Paul uses it to show that righteousness comes by faith (Romans 1:17; Galatians 3:11). Hebrews uses it to call believers to endure and not draw back (Hebrews 10:38–39). Each use fits its own argument, and each grows from Habakkuk’s meaning.

Read: Habakkuk 2:4–5, Romans 1:17, Galatians 3:11, Hebrews 10:37–39

Does “Let all the earth keep silence” forbid honest complaint? (v. 20)

“But the LORD is in His holy temple. Let all the earth keep silence before Him” (v. 20). This closes the woes against Babylon. Unlike the “mute idols” that cannot speak (vv. 18–19), the living God reigns and judges. The right response is awed reverence.

The verse is not a rebuke of Habakkuk’s honest questions. The same prophet goes on to pray, tremble, and rejoice in chapter 3.

Read: Habakkuk 2:18–20, Habakkuk 3:1–2

Habakkuk 3 · 2 questions

Who is “Your Anointed” in verse 13?

“You went forth for the salvation of Your people, for salvation with Your Anointed” (v. 13). The parallel lines suggest God’s anointed may be His people as a whole. It may also be the Davidic king who represented them. MTB leaves both open.

The main point is clear either way: God goes out to save His people and to strike down the wicked. That pattern reaches its fullness in Christ, the Anointed One. At the cross He defeated the powers of evil, and in His resurrection God vindicated Him (Colossians 2:13–15; Acts 2:23–24). The poem’s images do not each point to a single event.

Read: Habakkuk 3:3–15, Colossians 2:13–15, Acts 2:23–24

Do verses 17–19 promise escape from hardship? (vv. 16–19)

Habakkuk expects real loss: fig tree, vine, olive, fields, flock, and herd may all fail (v. 17). These are the ordinary means of living in a farming economy, not symbols. He trembles at the prospect (v. 16).

“Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation” (v. 18). MTB reads the climax as inward deliverance in the middle of outward loss. “He will make my feet like deer’s feet” (v. 19) pictures God giving steady footing on dangerous ground. It is strength to endure, not a promise of immediate escape.

As a spiritual application, MTB draws from these verses endurance in future tribulation and personal fruitfulness when outward supports fail. The passage gives no timetable for tribulation, and the crops are not code words.

Read: Habakkuk 3:16–19, Romans 8:28–29
