SCORNED - ( mishaq , Strong's H4890 )

Summary:

In Habakkuk 1:10, the Hebrew noun mishaq describes the absolute contempt and mocking derision that the invading Chaldeans (Babylonians) hold toward foreign rulers and fortifications. It refers to an object of laughter, a laughingstock, or a source of sport. Rather than fearing established earthly authorities or fortified cities, the Babylonians treat powerful kings and princes as trivial jokes, highlighting the unstoppable, divinely appointed judgment they bring, which sweeps away all human security and pride.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The noun mishaq is derived from the primary verbal root sachaq, which means to laugh, play, make merry, or mock.

• The underlying word picture is that of a spectator sport, a theatrical performance, or a game where the audience points and laughs in amusement. It conveys the image of someone being set up as a public laughingstock, completely stripped of dignity and power.

• In the ancient Near East, kings and princes represented the pinnacle of earthly glory, authority, and military defense. To reduce these exalted figures to mere "sport" or "playthings" depicts an overwhelming, mocking superiority that utterly devalues the target's status and strength.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The noun mishaq means an object of derision, a laughingstock, or a source of mockery.

• It denotes a state of being treated with utter contempt, where one's supposed power, status, or dignity is viewed as an insignificant joke.

• The term highlights the subjective attitude of the conqueror toward the conquered—viewing them not as formidable opponents to be respected, but as trivial amusements to be brushed aside.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The noun mishaq is a hapax legomenon, occurring only once in the Hebrew Bible (Habakkuk 1:10).

• Its closely related verbal root, sachaq, is used frequently to describe laughing in triumph, derision, or play (such as the wild donkey "scorning" the city tumult in Job 39:7, or God laughing at the rebellious nations in Psalm 2:4).

• The word picture in Habakkuk 1:10 is highly vivid: the Chaldeans do not merely defeat their enemies; they play with them. Earthly rulers, who once demanded absolute fear and reverence from their subjects, are transformed into objects of cheap entertainment for the pagan invaders.

4. Theological Implications

• The Ultimate Sovereignty of God: God raises up the Chaldeans as His "instrument of judgment" (Habakkuk 1:12). The ease with which they mock earthly rulers shows that human power is nothing before the sovereign decrees of God.

• The Emptiness of Human Pride: Earthly rulers and mighty fortresses, which men rely upon instead of God, are easily turned into objects of mockery.

• The Terrifying Nature of Divine Judgment: When God withdraws His protection, those who deemed themselves secure and high-minded become helpless laughingstocks to their enemies.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Habakkuk 1:1-4, the prophet laments the injustice and violence run rampant in Judah. God responds in verses 5-11 by announcing that He is raising up the Chaldeans—a bitter, hasty, and terrifying nation.

• In verse 10, the rapid, effortless conquest of the Babylonians is described. They "scoff at kings" and "princes are scorned (mishaq) by them."

• This context contrasts human confidence with the terrifying reality of God's judgment. The rulers of the earth believe they are secure in their royal dignity and walled cities, but to the Babylonians, they are a joke. The Babylonians simply "heap up dust" (build dirt siege ramps) and capture every stronghold. Mishaq underscores the psychological devastation of this judgment: it strips the leadership of Judah of all dignity, exposing their utter helplessness.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Because mishaq is used only once in the Old Testament, the following verses demonstrate the usage of its direct verbal root sachaq in the identical sense of mocking, scorning, or laughingstock:

• Habakkuk 1:10 – "They scoff at kings, And princes are scorned [mishaq] by them. They deride every stronghold, For they heap up dust and take it."

• Job 30:1 – "But now they mock [sachaq] me, men younger than I, Whose fathers I would have disdained to put with the dogs of my flock."

• Lamentations 1:7 – "The adversaries saw her and mocked [sachaq] her downfall."

• Proverbs 1:26 – "I also will laugh at your calamity; I will mock [sachaq] when your terror comes."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Greek Septuagint (LXX) translates mishaq in Habakkuk 1:10 with the noun paignion, which means a plaything, toy, or laughingstock. This reinforces the idea that the Babylonian military machine viewed these great rulers as mere toys to be played with and discarded.

• There is a tragic irony in this judgment: Judah had mocked God's laws, His temple, and His prophets; now, they themselves are delivered into the hands of a nation that mocks their own leaders, defenses, and dignity.
