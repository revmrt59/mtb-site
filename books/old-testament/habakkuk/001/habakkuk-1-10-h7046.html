SCOFF - ( qalas , Strong's H7046 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qalas refers to the act of mocking, deriding, or treating someone with utter contempt and ridicule. In the context of Habakkuk 1:10, it describes the fierce, arrogant pride of the invading Chaldeans (Babylonians), who look down upon earthly rulers and fortifications with absolute disdain. This word highlights the psychological terror of an unstoppable enemy who views human authorities and military defenses not as genuine obstacles, but as objects of amusement and mockery.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root qalas carries a physical and vocal picture of derision. It is not merely a quiet thought of superiority, but an active, outward expression of contempt. It involves gestures of mockery, jeering, and treating others as utterly worthless. In ancient Near Eastern warfare, mocking the rulers and gods of a besieged city was a psychological tactic designed to break the morale of the defenders before the physical assault even began. When the Babylonians scoffed at kings, they were demonstrating their belief that no human power could stand against them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb qalas occurs only in the Piel (intensive) and Hitpael (intensive/reflexive) stems in the Hebrew Bible.

• In the Piel stem, the verb means to mock, ridicule, or treat with insolence.

• In the Hitpael stem, which is the form used in Habakkuk 1:10 (yitqallas), the word carries the nuance of acting mockingly, making sport of, or continuously scoffing at someone.

• The semantic range encompasses scoffing, deriding, sneering, mocking, and treating with absolute scorn.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The term is used in Scripture to depict a deep-seated contempt that expresses itself openly. It is the attitude of those who believe they are completely invulnerable and superior to their peers or opponents.

• In military contexts, it portrays an invading army that does not even take the defensive measures of its enemies seriously. Rather than planning a cautious siege, they laugh at the fortresses, knowing they can easily conquer them.

• In social and spiritual contexts, it refers to the wicked mocking the righteous, prophets, or even God Himself, treating divine warnings and authority as a joke.

4. Theological Implications

• Arrogance and Self-Deification: The act of scoffing at earthly rulers is an extension of scoffing at the God who established those rulers. The Babylonians attribute their power to their own strength and their false gods, blind to the fact that they are merely an instrument of judgment in the hands of Yahweh.

• The Illusion of Human Security: The scoffing of the Chaldeans exposes the fragility of human power. The kings and princes of the earth, whom Israel might have trusted for political alliances, are easily swept away and ridiculed by the instrument of divine judgment.

• The Sovereignty of God: While the Babylonians scoff at all earthly authority, God remains sovereign. Their pride will eventually lead to their own destruction, as detailed in the subsequent woes of Habakkuk 2. The scoffer is ultimately subject to the supreme court of heaven.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Habakkuk 1:10, the prophet is processing God's shocking revelation that He is raising up the Chaldeans to punish Judah. God describes this enemy as "bitter and hasty" (1:6) and "terrible and dreadful" (1:7).

• The NKJV translates qalas as "sneer" in this verse: "They sneer at kings." This translation captures the facial expression and attitude of supreme superiority.

• Normal armies might respect the office of a king or the strength of a fortified city. To the Babylonians, however, these earthly rulers are a joke ("princes are a scorn to them").

• This creates a sense of dread for Habakkuk. The very systems of order and defense that Judah relies upon are laughed at by the invader. The text contrasts human anxiety with Babylonian amusement, emphasizing the futility of relying on military alliances or physical strongholds.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• 2 Kings 2:23 - "Then he went up from there to Bethel; and as he was going up the road, some youths came from the city and mocked him, and said to him, 'Go up, you baldhead! Go up, you baldhead!'"

• Ezekiel 16:31 - "You erected your shrine at the head of every road, and made your high place in every street. Yet you were not like a harlot, because you scorned payment."

• Ezekiel 22:5 - "Those near and those far from you will mock you, infamous as you are and full of confusion."

• Habakkuk 1:10 - "They sneer at kings, And princes are a scorn to them. They deride every stronghold, For they heap up dust and take it."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Hitpael stem used in Habakkuk 1:10 indicates continuous, self-indulgent action. The Babylonians make a habit and a hobby of scoffing. It is their default attitude toward others.

• This attitude of scoffing stands in stark contrast to the attitude of faith required of the righteous. In Habakkuk 2:4, God states that "the just shall live by his faith." The scoffer relies on his own power and mocks others; the righteous person relies on God and waits in quiet trust.

• Ultimately, those who scoff at God's authority will find the tables turned. God mocks the mockers (Proverbs 3:34) and laughs at the futile plotting of arrogant nations (Psalm 2:4).
