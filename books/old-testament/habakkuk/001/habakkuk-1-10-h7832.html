DERIDE - ( sahaq , Strong's H7832 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb sahaq carries the primary meaning of laughing, playing, or making sport. When used in a military or hostile context, it shifts from innocent amusement to contemptuous mockery and derision. In Habakkuk 1:10, it describes the supreme, arrogant confidence of the invading Babylonian army, who view the most formidable human fortifications not as obstacles, but as minor inconveniences to be laughed at and easily dismantled.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of sahaq is closely related to the verb tsahaq (to laugh), which is famously associated with the naming of Isaac. At its core, sahaq is an onomatopoeic word representing the physical sound of laughter or the joyful movements of play. In peaceful settings, it describes children playing safely in the streets or sea monsters frolicking in the ocean. However, when applied to warfare or confrontation, this "laughter" turns dark. It becomes the laughter of perceived invincibility. It depicts a dominant power looking down upon an opponent’s defenses with dismissive amusement, treating a serious fortification as nothing more than a toy or a joke.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb sahaq has a semantic range that transitions from joyful play to hostile contempt depending on the stem and context:

• Joyful Laughter / Play: In its basic sense, the word denotes rejoicing, celebrating, or playing.

• Contemptuous Derision: When followed by the preposition l (meaning "at" or "toward"), the Qal stem of the verb expresses mocking, deriding, or scoffing.

• Military Mockery: In historical narratives and poetic literature, it describes an attitude of utter fearlessness that laughs in the face of danger, armor, or defensive structures.

• In Habakkuk 1:10, the verb is used in the Qal imperfect stem, signifying an ongoing, characteristic attitude of the Chaldeans: they continuously look at every fortress and laugh.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture employs sahaq to paint vivid pictures of security, freedom, and triumph:

• The Wild Donkey's Freedom: The wild donkey is described as mocking (sahaq) the tumult of the city, completely indifferent to the shouts of human drivers because of its untamed freedom.

• The Warhorse's Courage: In description of the battle horse, the animal mocks (sahaq) fear, showing zero terror when charging into the clash of weapons.

• The Lord's Sovereign Mockery: When earthly rulers plot against the Almighty, the Lord sits in the heavens and laughs (sahaq) at them, recognizing the sheer futility of their rebellion.

4. Theological Implications

The use of sahaq in Habakkuk reveals a profound theological irony regarding human security. Judah had spent decades upgrading their physical defenses and trusting in their high walls. Yet, the instrument of God’s judgment—the Chaldeans—views these walls with absolute derision.

• The Futility of Carnal Security: This vocabulary choice warns the believer that any stronghold built apart from God is ultimately laughable. What humans deem impenetrable, God's agents of judgment deem a joke.

• The Trap of Arrogance: While the Chaldeans deride human strength, their laughter is rooted in self-deification. They credit their own power for these easy conquests, setting themselves up for the ultimate derision of God, who laughs at the wicked because He sees their day of reckoning is coming.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:10, God is describing the terrifying speed and ruthlessness of the incoming Chaldean army. The passage states: "They scoff at kings, And princes are a scorn to them. They deride every stronghold, For they heap up mounds of earth and take it."

• The word deride (sahaq) is paired with the action of "heaping up mounds of earth." The Babylonians do not spend months or years worrying about high stone walls. Instead, they quickly build dirt siege ramps, march over the defenses, and capture the city.

• To the Chaldeans, a fortress is not a military challenge; it is a comedy. Their derision is an expression of their supreme confidence. They know that no human barrier can prevent them from executing their conquests.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A structural parallel exists in Habakkuk 1:10. The prophet uses two synonymous concepts of mockery to describe the Chaldean attitude:

• They "scoff" (qalas) at kings.

• They "deride" (sahaq) strongholds.

This parallelism emphasizes that neither human political power (kings) nor human physical defense (strongholds) can withstand the judgment of God. What humanity relies on for protection becomes an object of ridicule when God withdraws His hand of blessing.
