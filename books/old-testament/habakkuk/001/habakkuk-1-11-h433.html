GOD - ( eloah , Strong's H433 )

Summary:

In Habakkuk 1:11, the word eloah refers to a false deity, representing the ultimate form of self-deification and pagan idolatry. As the Chaldeans sweep through the ancient Near East like a devastating wind, their military might becomes their god. Instead of acknowledging the true Creator who raised them up for judgment, they worship their own strength, committing a grave offense by attributing their sovereign victories to their false god.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• eloah is the singular form of the more common plural elohim. While elohim is used thousands of times in the Old Testament to refer to the true God (and occasionally pagan gods), eloah appears much less frequently, predominantly in poetic books like Job. The root meaning of eloah relates to power, strength, and fear or reverence. Historically, it points to a deity who is the object of worship, dread, and absolute allegiance.

In the ancient Near East, military conquest was deeply tied to theology. A nation's victory was seen as the victory of their god over the gods of the conquered. For the Babylonians (Chaldeans), their supreme god or their own military machinery became the object of their devotion.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• eloah means "a god" or "deity." When applied to the God of Israel, it emphasizes His singular majesty, power, and role as Creator. When applied to pagan idols or false gods, as in Habakkuk 1:11, it designates an object of worship, adoration, and ultimate trust that is fundamentally empty and powerless.

In this specific grammatical construction, with the third-person masculine singular pronominal suffix ("his god"), it highlights the personalized, localized nature of the Chaldeans' idolatry. They have fashioned a god in their own image—one that validates their cruelty and lust for power.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word eloah is used to contrast the true God of Israel with the false gods of the nations. In poetry, it evokes the awe and terror due to a divine being.

A striking word picture in Habakkuk 1:11 is the fluid, shifting nature of human devotion. The text describes the Chaldeans sweeping by "as a wind." Just as the wind shifts direction, their minds change, leading them to cross the line of human decency and sin against God by worshiping their own military power (koach) as their god (eloah). Their power is their temple, and their weapons are their idols. This is the tragic picture of self-deification: when humanity refuses to worship the true God, they do not worship nothing; they worship themselves and their ability to dominate others.

4. Theological Implications

• The Sovereignty of God vs. Human Pride: God sovereignly raises up the Chaldeans as an instrument of judgment against Judah (Habakkuk 1:5-6). Yet, the Chaldeans do not recognize this. They attribute their success to their own eloah, exposing the spiritual blindness of pride.

• The Anatomy of Idolatry: True idolatry is not merely bowing before wood and stone; it is trusting in human strength, technology, and military might as the ultimate source of security. The Chaldeans make their own power their god.

• The Certainty of Judgment: By deifying their own strength, the Chaldeans seal their doom. God cannot share His glory with another, nor will He allow those who make a god of their own power to escape justice. This sets up the dialogue in Chapter 2, where God promises to judge Babylon.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:11, the prophet is grieving the terrifying nature of the Babylonian invasion. God has just revealed that He is raising up this "bitter and hasty nation" (1:6). The climax of the description of their terrifying power is their ultimate spiritual failure: "He commits offense, ascribing this power to his god." The Hebrew phrase literally reads "this his power is his god."

This reveals the bitter irony of Habakkuk’s situation: God is using a nation whose very "god" is their own violent strength to punish His covenant people. It highlights the tension between God's holy purposes and the wicked instruments He temporarily employs.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition from elohim (the transcendent Creator) to eloah in poetic texts often heightens the personal nature of the deity being addressed or discussed. In Job, eloah is used to stress the sheer, unapproachable power of God in contrast to fragile humanity.

By using eloah for the Chaldeans' power, Habakkuk contrasts their self-made, fragile "god" with the eternal Eloah of Israel. The Babylonian empire, which seemed invincible, would collapse because their eloah was nothing more than their own passing strength. This serves as a timeless warning to modern empires, organizations, and individuals: whenever we treat our resources, intelligence, or strength as the ultimate guarantor of our success, we have recreated the sin of the Chaldeans, making our power our god.
