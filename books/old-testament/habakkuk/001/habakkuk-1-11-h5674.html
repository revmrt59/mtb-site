TRANSGRESSES - ( abar , Strong's H5674 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb abar literally means to "pass over," "cross over," or "go beyond." While frequently used in the Old Testament to describe physical movement across geographical boundaries, it functions metaphorically to describe "transgressing" or "overstepping" moral, legal, and divine boundaries. In Habakkuk 1:11, the term vividly depicts the unstoppable Babylonian empire sweeping through nations like a devastating wind and crossing the ultimate moral boundary by deifying their own military power.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of abar is deeply concrete, originating in the physical action of crossing a dividing line, such as traversing a river, passing through a land, or marching past a boundary marker. In the ancient Near East, boundaries were sacred markers of identity, ownership, and covenant safety. To cross a border without authorization was an act of hostility and violation. From this physical imagery, the Hebrew language developed the moral concept of transgression. To transgress is not merely to slip or make a mistake; it is to see a line that God has drawn and willfully step over it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of abar transitions from physical movement to moral accountability:

• Physical Movement: To pass over, cross through, march past, or sweep across (as an invading army crosses borders).

• Temporal/State Change: To pass away, vanish, or run its course.

• Covenantal and Moral Violation: To overstep, bypass, violate, or transgress a command, law, or covenant.

In Habakkuk 1:11, the word captures both the physical sweeping motion of the Chaldean army and their simultaneous moral overstepping of divine boundaries.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, abar creates powerful pictures of boundary crossings:

• The Crossing of the Jordan: The historic physical entry of Israel into the Promised Land is characterized by abar, where they "crossed over" the physical boundary to inherit God's promise.

• The Passing of the Wind: A wind that sweeps through and leaves nothing behind.

• The Overstepping of the Covenant: In legal contexts, abar represents a deliberate breach of contract. To transgress the covenant is to treat the protective boundaries of God's law as if they do not exist.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, abar teaches that sin is an aggressive disregard for God's boundaries. God establishes physical, moral, and spiritual borders for the preservation of order and the protection of His creation. When humanity operates in pride, it refuses to recognize these limits. The ultimate transgression is self-deification—crossing the line from creature to creator. When human beings overstep their creaturely boundaries, they inevitably slide into idolatry, attributing their achievements to their own power or false gods.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:11, God describes the coming Babylonian (Chaldean) invasion: "Then his mind changes, and he transgresses; He commits offense, attributing this power to his god."

The word abar functions as a brilliant double-entendre here. Physically, the Chaldeans sweep through the ancient world like a devastating wind, crossing national borders with impunity. However, this unchecked military success triggers a fatal internal shift. Their mind "changes" (literally, sweeps on like the wind), and they "transgress" (abar) morally. They lose all human restraint and overstep the boundary of sanity and righteousness by attributing their victorious power to their false gods rather than the sovereign Lord who raised them up. Their physical crossing of borders culminates in a spiritual transgression of the highest order.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Joshua 7:11 - "Israel has sinned, and they have also transgressed My covenant which I commanded them."

• Deuteronomy 17:2 - "If there is found among you, within any of your gates which the LORD your God gives you, a man or a woman who has been wicked in the sight of the LORD your God, in transgressing His covenant,"

• 1 Samuel 15:24 - "Then Saul said to Samuel, 'I have sinned, for I have transgressed the commandment of the LORD and your words, because I feared the people and obeyed their voice.'"

• Daniel 9:11 - "Yes, all Israel has transgressed Your law, and has departed so as not to obey Your voice; therefore the curse and the oath written in the Law of Moses the servant of God have been poured out on us, because we have sinned against Him."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The noun form of this root is eber, which means "the region beyond" or "the opposite side." It is the etymological root of the word "Hebrew" (Ibri), identifying Abraham and his descendants as "those from beyond the river." This serves as a redemptive contrast: while Israel was called to be a people who physically "crossed over" into covenant relationship with God, the Babylonians are characterized as those who "cross over" into pride, arrogance, and spiritual ruin.
