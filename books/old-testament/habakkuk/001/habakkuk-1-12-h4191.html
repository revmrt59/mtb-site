DIE - ( mut, Strong's H4191 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb mut fundamentally means to lose life, perish, or face physical death. In Habakkuk 1:12, the prophet uses this word in a declaration of covenant assurance: "We shall not die." Amidst the terrifying threat of the invading Chaldeans, this term transitions from a literal description of physical expiration to a profound theological claim of preservation. Because Yahweh is the everlasting and holy covenant God, His covenant people will not be utterly destroyed or extinguished from history.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root mut is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 800 times. It describes the cessation of physical life, whether of humans, animals, or metaphorically of nations and kingdoms. In the ancient Near Eastern context, death was the ultimate threat of chaos and the end of one's active relationship with the living. In Israelite thought, death represented a separation from the land of the living where God is actively praised. The word often carries covenantal weight throughout the Old Testament—disobedience leads to death, but God's mercy preserves a remnant from total annihilation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary meaning of mut is to die, perish, or be put to death.

• In the Qal stem (as used in Habakkuk 1:12), it denotes the natural or violent cessation of life.

• In the Hiphil stem (causative), it means to put to death, kill, or execute.

• In the Polel stem, it means to deal the death blow or slay.

• The term also encompasses national demise or destruction, referring to the ruin of a city, nation, or dynasty.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Cessation: It refers to the inevitable end of human life, exemplified by the patriarchal records where "he died" acts as a solemn refrain.

• Judicial Execution: It occurs in the legal codes demanding that certain offenses require the perpetrator to "surely be put to death."

• National and Spiritual Ruin: The prophets use the word metaphorically to describe a nation's spiritual demise through idolatry, where turning from God is equated to entering the realm of the dead.

• Contrast with the Divine: In poetic literature, the term stands in sharp contrast to the eternal nature of God. Humans are characterized by their subjection to mut, whereas God is the source of endless life.

4. Theological Implications

• God's Eternity vs. Human Mortality: Habakkuk contrasts the everlasting nature of Yahweh with the mortal vulnerability of His people. The prophet anchors Israel's hope not in their own strength, but in the immortal nature of their Creator.

• Covenant Faithfulness: The assertion "We shall not die" is not a claim of physical invulnerability for every individual Israelite, but a theological conviction of the preservation of the covenant line. The Chaldeans may chastise, but they cannot annihilate God's chosen remnant because God's character is bound to His people.

• Scribal Tradition (Tiqqune Sopherim): Ancient Jewish scribal traditions suggest a theological preservation of God's honor in this verse. Some early sources record an original reading of "You [God] do not die," which scribes changed to "We shall not die" to avoid the highly inappropriate association of the concept of death with the immortal Creator. Both readings underscore God’s absolute immortality and the security it provides to His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Habakkuk 1, the prophet is wrestling with the shocking revelation that God is raising up the ruthless Babylonians (Chaldeans) to judge Judah.

• Habakkuk asks how a holy God can use a nation more wicked than Judah to swallow up those more righteous.

• In verse 12, the prophet anchors his faith in God's attributes: Yahweh is "from everlasting," "my God," and "my Holy One."

• The phrase "We shall not die" represents the breakthrough of faith in the midst of terror. It is the realization that the Chaldean invasion is for "judgment" and "correction," not total annihilation. The purpose of the discipline is pedagogical and purgative, not terminal.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:17: "but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die."

• Genesis 35:18: "And so it was, as her soul was departing (for she died), that she called his name Ben-Oni; but his father called him Benjamin."

• Numbers 23:10: "Who can count the dust of Jacob, / Or number one-fourth of Israel? / Let me die the death of the righteous, / And let my end be like his!"

• Ezekiel 18:31: "Cast away from you all the transgressions which you have committed, and get yourselves a new heart and a new spirit. For why will you die, O house of Israel?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Qal imperfect first-person plural nāmût ("we shall die") with the negative particle lo ("not") serves as an emphatic declaration of hope.

• In the structure of Habakkuk's lament, this is the turning point of the first chapter. He moves from despair to an appeal to God's covenant nature.

• The juxtaposition of God's holiness ("my Holy One") with the preservation from death ("we shall not die") highlights the biblical concept that God's holiness is a consuming fire, yet to His covenant people, it is a protective shield.
