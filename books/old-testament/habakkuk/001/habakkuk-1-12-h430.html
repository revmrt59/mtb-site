GOD - ( elohim , Strong's H0430 )

Summary:

In Habakkuk 1:12, the word elohim (suffixed here as elohay, meaning "my God") reveals the sovereign, supreme, and covenant-keeping Creator in whom the prophet takes refuge during national crisis. While the plural form of this noun typically denotes majestic plenitude and supreme power, its grammatical use with singular verbs and adjectives throughout the Old Testament distinguishes the one true God of Israel from pagan deities. In this specific context of impending judgment by the Chaldeans, Habakkuk uses the personal possessive "my God" to appeal to the Lord's absolute sovereignty, moral perfection, and relational commitment to His covenant people, assuring the faithful that even in devastation, they will not be utterly destroyed.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun elohim is structurally plural, ending with the masculine plural suffix -im. In the ancient Near East, plural forms were often used to express intensification, magnitude, majesty, or the summation of all attributes. Rather than indicating polytheism when applied to the God of Israel, elohim functions as a plural of majesty (pluralis excellentiae). It represents the absolute fullness of divine power, glory, and authority concentrated in one supreme Being.

The etymological origin of the word points to two possible roots. The first is el, which conveys the concept of strength, power, and preeminence. The second is alah, an Arabic cognate root meaning to fear, worship, or be struck with awe. Combined, the word picture behind elohim is that of an infinitely strong Creator who is the ultimate object of human worship, reverence, and fear.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun elohim has a broad semantic range in the Old Testament, which is determined strictly by its grammatical context:

• The One True God: When referring to the Creator, it is paired with singular verbs, singular adjectives, and singular pronouns, designating Him as the unique, supreme Deity. This is its primary usage, occurring over 2,500 times.

• Pagan Deities: When referring to the gods of the nations, it is paired with plural verbs and represents plural foreign gods or goddesses.

• Angelic Beings: On rare occasions, it refers to heavenly messengers or supernatural beings in the spiritual realm.

• Human Judges or Rulers: Occasionally, it denotes earthly leaders, rulers, or judges who act as God’s representatives in executing justice.

In Habakkuk 1:12, the noun occurs with the first-person singular pronominal suffix (elohay), which translates to "my God." This suffix transitions the word from an abstract designation of supreme power to an intimate, personal, and relational title.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The biblical usage of elohim establishes several rich word pictures:

• The Sovereign Creator: In its very first biblical appearance (Genesis 1:1), elohim calls the universe into existence by His spoken word. This establishes a word picture of supreme cosmic authority; His voice alone shapes reality.

• The Supreme Judge: Throughout the Psalms and Prophets, elohim is depicted as a Ruler seated upon a high throne, presiding over the nations. He weighs the actions of empires, establishes justice, and cannot be thwarted by human armies.

• The Covenant Fortress: When joined with the covenant name of God (Yahweh), as seen in the phrase "the LORD my God," it fuses the infinite power of the Creator with the deeply personal, relational faithfulness of Israel's redeemer.

4. Theological Implications

The use of elohim in Habakkuk 1:12 carries profound theological weight:

• Absolute Sovereignty over History: Because God is elohim, He holds absolute dominion over geopolitical events. The rising Babylonian empire (the Chaldeans) is not an accidental historical anomaly or an indication that pagan gods have triumphed. The sovereign Creator is the one directing the course of empires.

• Covenant Preservation: By claiming elohim as "my God," the prophet appeals to the covenant. Because the almighty Creator has bound Himself to His people, their purification through judgment will never lead to their total destruction. The power of elohim guarantees the preservation of His covenant remnant.

• The Plurality of Majesty: The plural form of the name hints at the infinite, inexhaustible depths of the Godhead. It lays the Old Testament groundwork for the progressive revelation of the Trinity, showing that God's power and character are multi-dimensional, rich, and complete.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:12, the prophet is reeling from God's staggering announcement that He is raising up the ruthless, violent Chaldeans to execute judgment upon Judah (Habakkuk 1:5–11). This announcement creates a severe crisis of faith for Habakkuk.

To steady his soul, the prophet immediately appeals to the character of God, asking, "Are You not from everlasting, O LORD my God, my Holy One?"

• The Contrast of Power: The Babylonians are described as a people whose "own strength is their god" (Habakkuk 1:11). In direct contrast, Habakkuk lifts his eyes to elohim—the source of true, ultimate, and eternal power.

• The Assurance of Survival: Based on the character of elohim as the sovereign Creator and covenant Partner, Habakkuk declares with bold assurance: "We shall not die." The pagan army may act as God's instrument for temporary "judgment" and "correction," but they do not hold the ultimate power of life and death over God's people.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Habakkuk 1:12, the prophet stacks three distinct names and titles for God:

1. Yahweh (translated as "LORD") – The self-existent, unchanging covenant-maker.

2. Elohim (translated as "God") – The all-powerful, supreme Creator.

3. Qadosh (translated as "Holy One") – The morally perfect, pure, and transcendent One.

By combining these three titles, Habakkuk builds a theological fortress. Yahweh guarantees God's relational commitment, Elohim guarantees His sovereign capacity to act, and Qadosh guarantees that His actions—even when using a pagan nation—are perfectly pure, just, and holy.
