EVERLASTING - ( qedem , Strong's H6924 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun qedem fundamentally refers to what is "east," "front," or "of old." Temporally, it designates ancient times, antiquity, or primeval days, pointing back to the remote and unimaginable past. In Habakkuk 1:12, the prophet uses it to anchor his trembling faith in the eternal preexistence and unchanging character of Yahweh. Amidst the terrifying rise of the Chaldeans, qedem reminds the believer that God's sovereign plan and holy covenant predate any historical crisis, providing an unshakeable foundation of hope.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, geography and time were intimately linked through spatial orientation. The Hebrew people oriented themselves by facing the rising sun in the east. Therefore, qedem means "front" or "east." The west was "behind" (achor), the south was to the "right" (yamin), and the north was to the "left" (semol).

This spatial concept of "what is in front" naturally translated into a temporal concept. To the Hebrew mind, the past lies "in front" of us because it has already been lived and is visible in human memory and historical record. Conversely, the future lies "behind" us because it is unseen. Therefore, qedem represents the distant past, the ancient origin, or the primeval dawn of history. It carries the imagery of looking backward to the very beginning, past the horizon of human memory, to the eternal origin of all things.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word qedem functions primarily as a noun or adverb denoting antiquity, ancient times, or eternity past. When prefixed with the preposition min (appearing as miqqedem, as in Habakkuk 1:12), it translates as "from of old," "from ancient times," or "from everlasting."

The term does not always denote absolute, infinite eternity in a philosophical sense; it can refer to ancient human history or the early days of Israel's covenant relationship. However, when applied to Yahweh, qedem transcends creaturely time. It signifies His preexistence—that He is before all created things, standing outside the bounds of time as the uncreated Creator.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• qedem is utilized in several distinct ways across the Old Testament scriptures:

• The Geographic East: It describes the physical direction of the east, such as the location of the Garden of Eden (Genesis 2:8) or the "mountains of the east" (Numbers 23:7).

• The Days of Old: It references the historic eras of God's miraculous intervention, particularly the Exodus and the wilderness wanderings, which served as the anchor for Israel's faith during times of national distress.

• Ancient Decrees: It denotes the long-established purposes and plans of God, which were formulated in the distant past before they unfolded in human history (Isaiah 37:26).

• The Eternal Nature of God: It describes the self-existent, unoriginated nature of God, who existed before the mountains were brought forth.

4. Theological Implications

The use of qedem in relation to God carries profound theological weight:

• The Immutability of God: Because God is miqqedem (from everlasting), He is not subject to the chaotic fluctuations of time, culture, or geopolitics. He is the anchor who remains the same yesterday, today, and forever.

• Sovereignty Over History: The God of the ancient past is the author of history. Earthly empires rise, conquer, and crumble in a moment of time, but the eternal God reigns over their rise and fall.

• Covenant Security: The covenant Yahweh made with His people is rooted in His eternal character. His ancient promises are not fragile; they are backed by His everlasting existence. Because He is eternal, His commitment to redeem His people cannot be terminated by temporal threats.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1, the prophet is wrestling with a profound theological dilemma. God has just revealed that He is raising up the Chaldeans (Babylonians)—a ruthless, bitter, and unstoppable nation—to execute judgment on sinful Judah. The prospect of this brutal invasion threatens to shatter Habakkuk's faith.

In verse 12, Habakkuk turns to prayer, anchoring his soul in the character of God: "Are You not from everlasting (miqqedem), O Lord my God, my Holy One? We shall not die."

By invoking the word qedem, Habakkuk contrasts the sudden, terrifying rise of Babylon with the ancient, eternal presence of Yahweh. Babylon is a modern, passing phenomenon—a nation that only recently ascended to power and will soon vanish. Yahweh, however, is from everlasting. He was there before Babylon existed, and He will be there long after Babylon falls.

Furthermore, Habakkuk pairs qedem with the titles "O Lord [Yahweh] my God, my Holy One." This is the language of covenant. The eternal God entered into a binding relationship with Israel in the days of old. Because the God of the covenant is the eternal God, His covenant promises cannot fail. Therefore, Habakkuk can confidently declare, "We shall not die." God may use Babylon for temporary discipline, but the eternal God will not allow His covenant people to be utterly destroyed.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 33:27 - "The eternal God is your refuge, and underneath are the everlasting arms..." (Here, "eternal" translates qedem, highlighting God as the ancient, unchanging sanctuary for His people).

• Psalm 74:12 - "For God is my King from of old, working salvation in the midst of the earth." (In a time of national ruin, the psalmist appeals to God's ancient identity as King).

• Psalm 119:152 - "Concerning Your testimonies, I have known of old that You have founded them forever." (God's truth is not a recent innovation; it is rooted in His ancient, eternal character).

• Micah 5:2 - "But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah... Out of you shall come forth to Me the One to be Ruler in Israel, whose goings forth are from of old, from everlasting." (The promised Messiah's origins are rooted in the depths of eternity past).

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is helpful to contrast qedem with another common Hebrew word for eternity, olam (H5769). While olam is often forward-looking, pointing toward the vanishing point of an endless future, qedem is backward-looking, pointing toward the vanishing point of an endless past. Together, these terms encompass the fullness of God's eternity. He is the Alpha and the Omega, the One who is from qedem (everlasting past) and who reigns to olam (everlasting future). When the world around us is shaking, looking back to God's ancient faithfulness (qedem) gives us the confidence to face an uncertain future (olam).
