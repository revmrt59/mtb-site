DEVOURS - ( bala , Strong's H1104 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bala means to swallow down, engulf, or completely consume. While it can refer literally to swallowing food or liquid, its most frequent theological use is metaphorical, depicting total destruction, ruin, or being overwhelmed by a hostile force. In the context of Habakkuk 1:13, the word vividly describes the oppressive brutality of the Chaldeans (Babylonians), who ruthlessly sweep away and consume nations and individuals—specifically those more righteous than themselves—leaving nothing behind. This word underscores the horrifying totality of the enemy's violence and the agonizing question of why a holy God would permit such devastating injustice.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The physical imagery behind bala is that of an open throat or a gaping mouth gulping down an object until it completely disappears from sight. It evokes the terrifying reality of being swallowed alive, leaving no trace of existence. This word picture is used throughout the Old Testament to describe the earth opening its mouth to consume the rebellious (as in the case of Korah), a great sea creature swallowing a prophet, or the grave (Sheol) claiming its victims. The background of the word implies suddenness, helplessness of the victim, and an irreversible, devastating end.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Bala denotes the act of swallowing, consuming, engulfing, or bringing to utter ruin. In its basic active stem, it refers to swallowing greedily or gulping down. In its intensive and passive stems, it signifies being completely destroyed, wiped out, or ruined. The word does not merely suggest defeat or injury; it carries the weight of total assimilation or eradication. When an invading army "devours" a nation, it signifies that the victim's sovereignty, identity, and security are utterly consumed and erased by the predator.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Literal Picture of Swallowing: The word describes physical swallowing, such as Aaron's rod-turned-serpent swallowing the rods of the Egyptian magicians, or the great fish swallowing Jonah.

• The Picture of Sheol and Death: Sheol (the grave) is poetically described as having an insatiable appetite, opening its mouth wide to swallow the living whole.

• The Picture of Divine Judgment: God’s wrath is sometimes depicted as a consuming force that swallows up His enemies, completely removing them from the land of the living.

• The Picture of National Ruin: Israel's enemies are frequently described as beasts or monsters seeking to swallow up the covenant people, leaving them hollowed out and ruined.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of bala lies in its portrayal of unchecked wickedness and the ultimate triumph of God over destruction. When the wicked devour the righteous, it represents a direct assault on the moral order established by God. The act of devouring is an abuse of power that treats human lives as cheap and disposable. However, Scripture promises that this destructive swallowing is not the final word. The ultimate theological resolution of bala occurs when God promises to "swallow up" death itself forever, turning the ultimate consumer of humanity into the one who is eternally consumed.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:13, the prophet is wrestling with a profound divine paradox. God has just announced that He is raising up the ruthless Chaldeans to execute judgment on Judah. This declaration leaves Habakkuk in deep anguish. He appeals to God's holy character, noting that God's eyes are too pure to look upon evil. Habakkuk asks the agonizing question: why does God remain silent when the wicked Chaldean devours (using the intensive form of bala) a people more righteous than himself?

The nuance here is the absolute helplessness of Judah. The Chaldeans are not merely conquering; they are gulping down nations, assimilating them into their empire, and erasing their existence. The prophet is deeply troubled that a holy God would use a thoroughly pagan, predatory nation to completely consume a people who, despite their sins, still possess the covenant of the true God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 7:12 - "For every man threw down his rod, and they became serpents. But Aaron’s rod swallowed up their rods."

• Numbers 16:30 - "But if the Lord creates a new thing, and the earth opens its mouth and swallows them up with all that belongs to them..."

• Psalm 21:9 - "The Lord shall swallow them up in His wrath, And the fire shall devour them."

• Proverbs 1:12 - "Let us swallow them alive like Sheol, And whole, like those who go down to the Pit;"

• Isaiah 25:8 - "He will swallow up death forever, And the Lord God will wipe away tears from all faces;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The intensive grammatical form (Piel stem) of bala used in Habakkuk 1:13 emphasizes the active, violent, and thorough nature of the devouring. The Chaldeans do not accidentally or partially harm Judah; they act with premeditated, violent hunger to completely eradicate them. Furthermore, the contrast between the "wicked" and the "one more righteous than he" highlights the moral confusion Habakkuk faces. It is a striking picture of systemic injustice where God's timing and methods baffle human understanding, forcing the believer to live by faith rather than sight.
