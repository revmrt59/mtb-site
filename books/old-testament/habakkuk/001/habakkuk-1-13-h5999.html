WICKEDNESS - ( 'amal , Strong's H5999 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'amal represents a painful combination of exhausting labor, oppressive misery, and the mischievous wickedness that causes it. It is not merely abstract moral badness, but the tangible, agonizing trouble and grief inflicted upon the innocent by the corrupt. In Habakkuk 1:13, the prophet uses 'amal to describe the intolerable grievance of God's apparent silence while the treacherous Babylonians violently oppress those more righteous than themselves, highlighting the deep tension between God's absolute holiness and the reality of earthly suffering.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of 'amal carries the physical sensation of heavy, wearisome labor, exhausting toil, and the resulting physical pain or misery. It paints a picture of a laborer grinding under an oppressive burden or working to the point of utter depletion. Over time, this physical concept expanded to include the emotional and spiritual grief caused by unjust treatment. Thus, 'amal captures both the act of producing mischief and the painful product of that mischief—the sorrow, distress, and weariness felt by its victims. It is the heavy weight of sin experienced as a grueling, life-draining burden.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• ’Amal designates toil, troublesome labor, misery, pain, grievance, or mischief. The noun describes both the subjective feeling of misery or sorrow and the objective reality of trouble, mischief, or wickedness that produces that misery. In ethical and prophetic contexts, it emphasizes the injustice, oppression, and injury committed by the wicked against the vulnerable, resulting in social grievance and misery.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• ’Amal as burdensome labor: It is used of the wearisome toil of human life under the sun, emphasizing the vanity of labor without God.

• ’Amal as mischief/wickedness: It portrays the deliberate plotting of evil. Wicked people "conceive" 'amal under their tongues to bring forth harm to others.

• ’Amal as misery/trouble: It is the natural consequence of living in a fallen world, characterized by pain, grief, and physical or mental distress.

4. Theological Implications

The existence of 'amal presents a profound theological challenge for the righteous. Because God is holy and sovereign, His people expect Him to intervene and put an end to the wearying misery caused by wicked oppressors. Scripture reveals that while humanity is "born to trouble" ('amal) due to the Fall, God is not indifferent to this suffering. He notes the 'amal of His people, hears their cries, and ultimately promises to bring an end to all painful toil and grief. The ultimate redemption in Christ addresses the root of 'amal, offering rest to the heavy-laden and promising a future new creation where pain, sorrow, and exhausting toil are permanently removed.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:13, the prophet is wrestling with a crushing theological paradox. Having just been told that God will use the ruthless Babylonians to discipline Judah, Habakkuk asks how a holy God, whose eyes are too pure to approve of evil (ra'), can look upon "wickedness" ('amal) without immediate intervention. The choice of 'amal here highlights the excruciating distress, trouble, and raw misery that the Babylonian invasion will inflict upon the land. Habakkuk is not asking an abstract philosophical question; he is lamenting the physical and spiritual devastation of his people. For God to "look on" 'amal does not mean He is unaware, but that He appears to tolerate or look passively upon the active abuse, cruelty, and resulting misery of His people. This term bridges the moral corruption of the Babylonians with the agonizing experience of their victims.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Habakkuk 1:3 – "Why do You show me iniquity, And cause me to see trouble? For plundering and violence are before me; There is strife, and contention arises."

• Job 5:7 – "Yet man is born to trouble, As the sparks fly upward."

• Psalm 7:14 – "Behold, the wicked brings forth iniquity; Yes, he conceives trouble and brings forth falsehood."

• Psalm 10:7 – "His mouth is full of cursing and deceit and oppression; Under his tongue is trouble and iniquity."

• Psalm 90:10 – "The days of our lives are seventy years; And if by reason of strength they are eighty years, Yet their boast is only labor and sorrow; For it is soon cut off, and we fly away."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint, 'amal is often translated using terms like ponos (pain, labor, sorrow) or kopos (wearisome toil, labor). In Habakkuk 1:13, the Greek translation uses ponous (troubles, pains, grievances), highlighting the intense grief and suffering associated with the word. There is a powerful literary and thematic connection between Habakkuk 1:3 and 1:13. In verse 3, Habakkuk asks why God makes him look at 'amal (trouble) within Judah. In verse 13, he asks how God Himself can look at 'amal (wickedness/grievance) committed by a pagan nation. The problem of evil has expanded from local injustice to global crisis.
