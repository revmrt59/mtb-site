EVIL - ( ra' , Strong's H7451 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ra' denotes that which is bad, harmful, corrupt, or morally wicked. In Habakkuk 1:13, it highlights God's absolute holiness and moral purity, which prevents Him from looking upon or approving of moral evil, setting up the prophet's profound struggle with God's sovereign choice to use the wicked Babylonians to discipline Judah.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, concrete realities always undergird abstract theological terms. The word ra' carries a physical, sensory background of that which is broken, shattered, or spoiled. It refers to something that has been damaged and is therefore dysfunctional, harmful, or useless for its intended purpose. Just as a fractured vessel cannot hold water, or bad fruit cannot be eaten, anything designated as ra' is fundamentally ruined and brings ruin to others. It stands in direct contrast to tov (good, functional, beautiful, and life-giving).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word ra' possesses a wide semantic range in the Old Testament, categorized primarily into two dimensions: physical/circumstantial badness, and moral/ethical badness.

• Physical and Circumstantial: It denotes adversity, calamity, disaster, misery, or distress. In this sense, it describes difficult situations, harmful animals, or destructive plagues that disrupt human flourishing.

• Moral and Ethical: It refers to wickedness, depravity, sinfulness, and malicious intent. It describes thoughts, speech, and deeds that violate God’s covenant standards, representing that which is offensive to His holy character.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture employs ra' in several vivid ways to paint a picture of its destructive nature:

• Bad Quality: It describes low-quality, worthless things, such as the bad, sickly cows in Pharaoh’s dream or worthless, inedible figs.

• Calamity and Disaster: It refers to an "evil day" of trouble, judgment, or circumstantial ruin sent as a consequence of covenant unfaithfulness.

• Moral Corruption: It characterizes the wicked intentions of the human heart, which are described as continuously corrupt.

• Ethical Opposition: It represents active rebellion against the covenant commands of Yahweh, directly contrasting with His goodness.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, ra' reveals the absolute standard of God’s holiness. God is entirely devoid of ra'; in Him, there is no moral corruption, nor does He tempt anyone with it. Because God is the ultimate definition of tov (good), ra' is a direct assault on His sovereign rule and character. The existence of moral ra' in a world created good by God introduces the great biblical narrative of redemption. God hates ra' because it defaces His creation and harms His image-bearers. Yet, in His sovereignty, God can redirect the circumstantial consequences of ra' to accomplish His ultimate, good purposes.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:13, the prophet uses ra' to address a profound pastoral and theological crisis. Habakkuk is reeling from the revelation that God intends to use the ruthless, idolatrous Babylonians to judge the southern kingdom of Judah. The prophet appeals to God’s immutable nature: "You are of purer eyes than to behold evil."

Here, ra' refers specifically to the savage violence, rapacity, and moral degradation of the Chaldeans. The nuance of "beholding" (ra'ah) evil does not mean God is blind to it, but that He cannot look upon it with approval, pleasure, or indifference. Habakkuk’s dilemma is raw: If God’s holy character is so pure that He cannot tolerate the sight of ra', how can He passively "look on" while a violently wicked nation devours a people who, despite their sins, are more righteous than they are? The word heightens the contrast between God's perfect moral purity and the seemingly contradictory reality of His historical providence.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 6:5: "Then the LORD saw that the wickedness of man was great in the earth, and that every intent of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually."

• Psalm 23:4: "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; For You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me."

• Proverbs 8:13: "The fear of the LORD is to hate evil; Pride and arrogance and the evil way And the perverse mouth I hate."

• Isaiah 5:20: "Woe to those who call evil good, and good evil; Who put darkness for light, and light for darkness; Who put bitter for sweet, and sweet for bitter!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The parallel term in Habakkuk 1:13 is "wickedness" (amal), which carries the sense of misery, trouble, or painful labor resulting from sin. By pairing ra' with amal, the text emphasizes that moral evil inevitably produces human suffering and societal breakdown. Additionally, this passage provides comfort to believers experiencing injustice. Though God may temporarily tolerate the presence of ra' for His sovereign, inscrutable purposes, His holy nature guarantees that He will never ignore it permanently. His eyes see it, and His justice will ultimately deal with it.
