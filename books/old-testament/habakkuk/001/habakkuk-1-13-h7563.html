WICKED - ( rasha , Strong's H7563 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word rasha refers to someone who is guilty, lawless, or actively hostile to God's covenant and moral order. In the context of Habakkuk 1:13, it highlights the prophet's profound theological dilemma: how a holy and pure God can remain silent while an aggressively wicked, pagan empire (the Babylonians) consumes a people (Judah) who, though sinful, are relatively more righteous.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, rasha is not merely an abstract moral designation but a relational, legal, and concrete reality. Its conceptual opposite is tsaddiq (righteous). While the righteous person seeks to maintain the covenant community's peace (shalom) and lives in right relationship with God and neighbor, the rasha actively disrupts this order. The word carries an inherent background of turbulence and instability; the wicked are characterized by a restless, chaotic energy that expresses itself in hostility toward God and the exploitation of others.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term designates one who is guilty of a crime, condemned in a court of justice, or hostile to the law of God. The semantic range includes:

• Guilty of civil or criminal offenses.

• Actively wicked, godless, or acting impiously.

• Oppressors who abuse power and disregard human dignity.

• Those declared guilty under divine judgment.

It describes an active posture of rebellion against God's standards rather than a passive state of imperfection.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Courtroom Metaphor: In legal contexts, rasha refers to the guilty party in a dispute, the one whom the judge condemns. To "justify the wicked" (rasha) is an abomination because it perverts truth and justice.

• The Churning Sea: The wicked are compared to the troubled sea that cannot rest, whose waters cast up mire and dirt. They lack the quiet confidence of faith and are driven by chaotic desires.

• The Path of Darkness: The way of the wicked is depicted as deep darkness; they do not know what makes them stumble, contrasting sharply with the path of the righteous, which shines ever brighter.

4. Theological Implications

The existence of the rasha tests the faith of God's people. Because God is holy and His eyes are "purer than to behold evil," He must ultimately judge the wicked. The delayed execution of this judgment often leads to the theological crisis of theodicy—the questioning of God's justice. Scriptural theology resolves this by emphasizing that the prosperity of the wicked is temporary, and their ultimate end is destruction, while God's sovereign purposes are worked out even through their actions.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:13, the prophet uses rasha to refer to the Chaldeans (the Babylonians). God had just revealed that He was raising up this fierce and impetuous nation to execute judgment on the covenant people of Judah. This triggers Habakkuk's second complaint. He struggles to reconcile God's absolute holiness with the use of such an ungodly instrument. The Chaldeans are rasha because they are rapacious, violent, and self-deifying, worshiping their own military might. Habakkuk’s query centers on the moral asymmetry: how can God hold His tongue "when the wicked devours a person more righteous than he?" While Judah is guilty of violating the covenant, they are still relatively "righteous" compared to the sheer, unchecked wickedness of Babylon.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 11:6 – "Upon the wicked He will rain coals; Fire and brimstone and a burning wind shall be the portion of their cup."

• Proverbs 10:3 – "The LORD will not allow the righteous soul to famish, But He casts away the desire of the wicked."

• Isaiah 55:7 – "Let the wicked forsake his way, And the unrighteous man his thoughts; Let him return to the LORD, And He will have mercy on his soul; And to our God, For He will abundantly pardon."

• Ezekiel 18:23 – "'Do I have any pleasure at all that the wicked should die?' says the Lord GOD, 'and not that he should turn from his ways and live?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Habakkuk's dialogue teaches that God welcomes honest wrestling with hard theological realities. The tension of Habakkuk 1:13 is answered in Habakkuk 2, where God reassures the prophet that the rasha (Babylon) will indeed face their own catastrophic judgment in due time. The ultimate duty of the believer in the midst of seeming injustice is not to solve the mystery of divine timing, but to live by faithful trust: "the just shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4).
