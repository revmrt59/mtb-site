TREACHEROUSLY - ( bagad , Strong's H0898 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bagad refers to the act of dealing deceitfully, faithlessly, or treacherously, particularly by violating a covenant, marriage vow, or social contract. It denotes a deliberate breach of trust where loyalty is expected but betrayal is delivered instead. In Habakkuk 1:13, it describes the wicked Chaldeans (Babylonians), who ruthlessly conquer other nations without regard for justice or treaties, leaving the prophet to grapple with how a holy God can tolerate such faithless oppressors.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb bagad is closely related to the noun beged, which means "garment" or "clothing." This linguistic connection provides a vivid word picture: just as a garment covers or cloaks a person, dealing treacherously involves covering up one's true, malicious intentions under a cloak of deceit or friendship. To act treacherously is to wear a mask of reliability while planning betrayal, or to cover one's actions with treachery. In ancient Near Eastern culture, covenants and agreements were sacred bonds; violating them was not merely a breach of contract but a serious moral offense that stripped away the expected "covering" of mutual trust and security.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb bagad primarily means to act faithlessly, deal treacherously, or break faith. It represents a willful departure from a standard of loyalty or a formal agreement. The semantic range includes:

• To violate a covenant, treaty, or oath.

• To be unfaithful in marriage through adultery or desertion.

• To act deceitfully or practice duplicity in social or political relationships.

• To pillage, plunder, or betray trust through violent disregard for others.

It is an action of deceit that originates from a heart of rebellion and selfishness, choosing self-interest over established covenants and relationships.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs bagad in several distinct contexts to illustrate the devastating nature of faithlessness:

• Covenantal and Spiritual Infidelity: Israel’s departure from God is frequently described as acting treacherously. Instead of maintaining their covenant with Yahweh, they "dealt treacherously" by turning to idols, behaving like an unfaithful spouse.

• Marital Unfaithfulness: Malachi uses the term to condemn men who deal treacherously with the wives of their youth by divorcing them or breaking the marriage covenant.

• Political and Military Betrayal: In international relations, bagad characterizes nations that break treaties, raid unsuspecting neighbors, or plunder those with whom they should have peace. This is the primary imagery applied to the predatory Chaldeans.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, bagad reveals the severe offense of covenant-breaking in the eyes of God. Because Yahweh is entirely faithful ('emet), He detests faithlessness. Trust and loyalty are foundational to God's relationship with humanity and to human relationships with one another. When people or nations act treacherously, they assault the moral order established by God. The use of this word underscores that sin is not merely a violation of abstract laws, but a deeply personal betrayal of relationship, trust, and covenant responsibility.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:13, the prophet is in the midst of a profound theological crisis. God has revealed that He is raising up the Chaldeans (Babylonians) to punish Judah for her sins. Habakkuk is stunned by this divine strategy. He acknowledges God’s absolute holiness ("purer eyes than to behold evil") but cannot reconcile this with God's silence in the face of Babylonian cruelty.

By using bagad ("those who deal treacherously"), Habakkuk characterizes the Chaldeans as ruthless treaty-breakers who recognize no law but their own power. They are treacherous because they conquer nations with deceit and insatiable greed. The tension in Habakkuk's prayer is acute: How can a covenant-keeping, holy God use a notoriously covenant-breaking, treacherous nation to discipline a people who, despite their flaws, are still "more righteous" than their pagan captors? The word highlights the sheer injustice and horror of the Babylonian advance from a human perspective.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Jeremiah 3:20 - "Surely, as a wife treacherously departs from her husband, so have you dealt treacherously with Me, O house of Israel,” says the LORD."

• Malachi 2:14 - "Yet you say, 'For what reason?' Because the LORD has been witness between you and the wife of your youth, with whom you have dealt treacherously; yet she is your companion and your wife by covenant."

• Isaiah 21:2 - "A distressing vision is declared to me; the treacherous dealer deals treacherously, and the plunderer plunders. Go up, O Elam! Besiege, O Media! All its sighing I have made to cease."

• Isaiah 33:1 - "Woe to you who plunder, though you have not been plundered; and you who deal treacherously, though they have not dealt treacherously with you! When you cease plundering, you will be plundered; and when you make an end of dealing treacherously, they will deal treacherously with you."

• Hosea 5:7 - "They have dealt treacherously with the LORD, for they have begotten pagan children. Now a New Moon shall devour them and their heritage."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (LXX), bagad is often translated using terms related to contempt (kataphroneo) or rebellion, highlighting that treachery is ultimately an expression of arrogance. When a person or nation deals treacherously, they place themselves above the law and treat others with contempt. In Habakkuk 1:13, the Greek translation uses kataphronountas ("the contemptuous ones" or "those who despise"), which emphasizes the proud, dismissive attitude of the Babylonians toward both God and humanity. This connects back to the theme of pride versus faith that dominates the rest of Habakkuk’s prophecy.
