GLAD - ( giyl , Strong's H1523 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word giyl denotes a physical, visible manifestation of joy, often expressed through rejoicing, spinning around, or leaping for joy. In the context of Habakkuk 1:15, it describes the arrogant, self-satisfied celebration of the Chaldeans as they ruthlessly conquer nations, dragging them away like fish in a net. While joy is most frequently reserved for God's people exulting in His salvation, here it is perverted into a pagan triumph, revealing the wicked heart's tendency to rejoice in its own strength and exploits.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of giyl conveys the physical action of spinning or turning around in circles under the influence of intense emotion. It is a highly expressive, dynamic term representing outward joy, rather than a quiet, contemplative state of happiness. The word conjures up the image of a dancer whirling in excitement or a victor celebrating on the battlefield. It reflects a joy so profound that it cannot be contained internally but must express itself through visible movement and celebration.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb giyl means to rejoice, exult, be glad, or joy. Its primary semantic range involves outward exultation and gladness. It describes a deep-seated gladness that manifests in vocal celebration, physical dancing, or visible triumph. In Hebrew lexicography, the word indicates a state of high-spirited rejoicing. While it is frequently paired with the Hebrew verb samach (to rejoice, be glad), giyl carries a stronger emphasis on the physical and vocal expression of that joy.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Exultant Dance: The term evokes the motion of spinning around in a circle, depicting someone dancing with sheer delight.

• Harvest Rejoicing: It is used to describe the celebration of a bountiful crop, where laborers rejoice over the physical provision of their hard work.

• Military Victory: It portrays the euphoric shouting and dancing of soldiers who have conquered their enemies and are dividing the spoils of war.

• Natural Rejoicing: Poetically, creation itself is described as spinning or leaping with gladness under the blessing of God, such as the hills or the desert rejoicing.

4. Theological Implications

• The Object of Joy: Joy is not a morally neutral emotion in Scripture; its righteousness is determined by its object. Giyl is intended to find its ultimate fulfillment in Yahweh, His character, and His saving acts.

• The Perversion of Joy: When the object of rejoicing shifts from the Creator to the creature—or to one's own power—the joy becomes idolatrous.

• The Transience of Wicked Rejoicing: The exultation of the wicked is short-lived. Rejoicing in human strength or the oppression of others is an offense to God that invites divine judgment. True, lasting gladness is a gift of God's covenant relationship, whereas pagan gladness ends in ruin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:15, the prophet laments the devastating rise of the Chaldeans (Babylonians). He uses a vivid fishing metaphor, depicting the pagan invaders as fishermen casting hooks and nets to sweep up whole nations like helpless sea creatures.

• Self-Deification: The Babylonians "rejoice and are glad" (giyl) because of their easy conquests. Their gladness is a form of self-congratulation and hubris. They do not thank the sovereign God who permitted their success; instead, they worship their own military power, effectively sacrificing to their own "net."

• Contrast of Joys: This pagan, predatory gladness stands in sharp contrast to the prophet’s final resolution in Habakkuk 3:18. While the Babylonians rejoice in their temporary plunder, Habakkuk resolves that even in total economic ruin, "I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation." The worldly, exploitative giyl of chapter 1 is ultimately eclipsed by the faithful, spiritual giyl of chapter 3.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 2:11: "Serve the LORD with fear, / And rejoice with trembling."

• Psalm 9:14: "That I may tell of all Your praise / In the gates of the daughter of Zion. / I will rejoice in Your salvation."

• Psalm 118:24: "This is the day the LORD has made; / We will rejoice and be glad in it."

• Zechariah 9:9: "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion! / Shout, O daughter of Jerusalem! / Behold, your King is coming to you; / He is just and having salvation, / Lowly and riding on a donkey, / A colt, the foal of a donkey."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The word giyl is frequently paired in parallel poetic lines with samach (to be glad) or ranan (to shout). This doubling emphasizes the completeness and exuberance of the joy being described.

• In Habakkuk 1:15, the two verbs are samach ("rejoice") and giyl ("are glad"). The Babylonians are experiencing a total, unrestrained high of military triumph, celebrating with every fiber of their being. This heightens the tragedy of Judah's suffering but also underscores the magnitude of the pride that God will eventually bring down.
