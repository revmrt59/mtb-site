DRAGNET - ( mikmereth , Strong's H4365 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word mikmereth refers to a large fishing net or dragnet designed to sweep through a body of water and catch everything in its path. In the book of Habakkuk, it serves as a vivid metaphor for the unstoppable, all-consuming military campaigns of the Babylonians, who swept up entire nations like helpless fish. Rather than acknowledging God's sovereignty, these pagan conquerors deified their own military might, symbolically offering sacrifices to their fishing gear. For the pastor and Bible student, this study warns against the subtle trap of self-reliance and practical idolatry—the tendency to worship the tools, systems, and strategies of our success rather than the God who ordains all things.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, fishing was a vital industry, and the dragnet (mikmereth) was the largest and most effective tool available to fishermen. Unlike a small hand-net cast by a single fisherman, a dragnet was a massive net fitted with floats along the top edge and heavy sinkers along the bottom. It required a team of men, often utilizing boats and ropes, to pull the net in a wide semi-circle toward the shore. As the net was hauled in, it scraped the bottom of the water source, indiscriminately sweeping up everything in its path—fish of every size, debris, and marine life. The trapped fish were completely helpless, unable to escape the closing perimeter of the net. By using this imagery, the Old Testament writers presented a terrifying picture of overwhelming force, inescapable entrapment, and absolute vulnerability.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun mikmereth is a feminine noun derived from the root kamar, which carries the primary meaning of being warm, tender, or drawing together. In the context of netting, the root emphasizes the physical contraction or drawing together of the mesh as it traps its prey. Lexically, mikmereth denotes a "dragnet," "trawl-net," or "fishing net." It specifically refers to the large-scale harvesting net rather than smaller throw-nets or individual fishhooks. The term emphasizes scale, enclosure, and inescapable capture.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In prophetic literature, mikmereth is used to paint a picture of sweeping national judgment and imperial conquest. The word picture operates on several levels:

• Indiscriminate Captivity: Just as a dragnet does not select individual fish but captures the entire school, invading armies sweep across territories, capturing young and old, rich and poor, without distinction.

• Absolute Helplessness: Fish out of water gasp for air, completely stripped of their natural environment and power. Similarly, conquered peoples are displaced from their homelands, rendered powerless under the hand of their captors.

• The Pride of the Fisherman: The fisherman stands over the pile of flopping fish, boasting of his skill and the strength of his net. This represents the arrogance of empires that view conquered nations merely as a harvest for their own consumption and enrichment.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of mikmereth in Habakkuk centers on the danger of misplaced worship and the self-deification of human effort.

• The Sovereignty of God vs. Human Instruments: In the divine economy, the Babylonians were merely an instrument of judgment in God's hand (Habakkuk 1:6). However, because they lacked the knowledge of the true God, they attributed their victories to their own military strategies, weapons, and prowess—their metaphorical "dragnet."

• The Idolatry of the Instrument: To "sacrifice to their net" and "burn incense to their dragnet" is the ultimate expression of human hubris. It is the act of thanking the tool rather than the Creator who permitted its use. When humanity attributes success, security, or prosperity strictly to human systems, financial strategies, or technological tools, they commit the same idolatry as the Chaldeans.

• The Problem of Evil: The prophet Habakkuk wrestles with why a holy God would allow a wicked nation to treat human beings like worthless fish caught in a net. The metaphor highlights the raw cruelty of sin and the agonizing silence of God before His ultimate justice is revealed.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:15, the prophet uses three distinct fishing terms to describe the Chaldean invasion: the hook (chakkah), the throwing net (cherem), and the dragnet (mikmereth). This progression demonstrates the systematic and comprehensive nature of their conquest. They hook individual leaders, trap communities in their throwing nets, and sweep away entire nations with their dragnet.

In verse 16, the tragedy deepens. The Babylonians do not worship Yahweh, who gave them dominion; instead, they worship their own military machinery. The dragnet becomes their god because it brings them luxurious wealth ("their share is sumptuous") and material abundance ("their food plentiful"). The nuance here is a warning against "practical atheism," where God is ignored because our own "nets"—our jobs, intellect, and planning—seem to provide everything we need.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Habakkuk 1:15 - "They take up all of them with a hook, They catch them in their net, And gather them in their dragnet. Therefore they rejoice and are glad."

• Habakkuk 1:16 - "Therefore they sacrifice to their net, And burn incense to their dragnet; Because by them their share is sumptuous And their food plentiful."

• Isaiah 19:8 - "The fishermen also will mourn, And all those will lament who cast hooks into the River, And they will languish who spread nets on the waters."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), mikmereth is translated using the Greek word sagene, which also means a large dragnet. This directly connects the Old Testament imagery to the New Testament. In Matthew 13:47–50, Jesus uses the parable of the dragnet (sagene) to describe the kingdom of heaven gathering every kind of fish, which will eventually be sorted at the end of the age.

While the Babylonians used their dragnet to destroy and exploit for selfish gain, God uses His sovereign dragnet to gather humanity for ultimate accountability and redemption. Furthermore, when Jesus called His disciples to be "fishers of men" (Matthew 4:19), He turned the predatory, death-dealing military metaphor of the ancient world on its head. Instead of catching people in a net of judgment to destroy them, the apostles were commissioned to cast the net of the Gospel to save them and bring them into eternal life.
