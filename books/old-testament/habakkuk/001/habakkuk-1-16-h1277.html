PLENTIFUL - ( bari' , Strong's H1277 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bari' primarily denotes something that is fat, plump, healthy, or robust. In the agrarian culture of the ancient Near East, fatness was not viewed negatively; rather, it was a highly desirable symbol of health, fertility, prosperity, and the very best quality of life. In Habakkuk 1:16, this term is translated as "plentiful" to describe the rich, luxurious, and abundant food that the conquering Chaldeans (Babylonians) enjoyed as a result of their military conquests. Instead of thanking the true God for this abundance, they idolatrously worshiped their own military strength and weapons.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient world, physical fatness in livestock, crops, or people was the ultimate sign of well-being. Thinness represented scarcity, famine, and divine displeasure, while plumpness (bari') represented blessing, security, and the fullness of life. The word evokes a sensory picture of thick, well-fed cattle standing in green pastures, or heavy, bursting heads of grain ready for harvest. When applied to food, it refers to the richest, most nourishing portions. In Habakkuk, this word contrastingly illustrates how the Babylonians gorged themselves on the wealth of conquered nations, growing fat and robust on the spoils of war while leaving their victims depleted and devastated.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The adjective bari' means "fat," "plump," "healthy," or "plentiful." It derives from a root associated with being well-fed and robust.

• It describes physical fatness and health in animals, signifying that they are prime specimens fit for consumption or sacrifice.

• It refers to the fullness and thickness of agricultural crops, indicating a bountiful harvest.

• It denotes individuals who are well-nourished, stout, or physically heavy.

• It describes a portion of food or a lifestyle that is exceptionally rich, luxurious, and abundant.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In the dreams of Pharaoh, the word describes the "fat" cows and the "plump" heads of grain that came out of the Nile. This usage establishes bari' as the standard biblical picture of agricultural and economic prosperity.

• In pastoral contexts, the word describes the strongest, healthiest sheep in a flock. It represents those who have thrived and grown strong under good care—or, in cases of exploitation, those who have fed themselves at the expense of the weak.

• In Habakkuk, the word pictures the Babylonian war machine as a giant dragnet sweeping up human lives like fish. The result of this ruthless harvest is that their portion is made fat and their food "plentiful." They are living in bloated luxury because of their violent conquests.

4. Theological Implications

• The Spiritual Danger of Prosperity: Abundance often breeds spiritual amnesia. When humans experience extreme physical prosperity (bari'), they are uniquely tempted to forget God and credit their own strength.

• The Creation of Functional Idols: The Babylonians deified the tools of their success. Because their nets brought them "plentiful" food, they sacrificed to their nets. This warns against the human tendency to worship career, intelligence, or strategic planning rather than the God who provides the power to get wealth.

• The Sovereign Hand Behind Provision: Even though the Chaldeans acquired their wealth through wicked means, their abundance was ultimately permitted by God's sovereign decree for His own disciplinary purposes. True, lasting provision belongs to God, and those who abuse His material blessings will eventually face His justice.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1, the prophet wrestles with God’s decision to use the ruthless Babylonians to punish Judah. In verse 16, Habakkuk exposes the deep-seated pride of these invaders. The Babylonians did not acknowledge Yahweh as the source of their victories. Instead, they literally and figuratively bowed down to their own military might. The word "plentiful" (bari') emphasizes that their greed was fully satisfied; they lacked nothing materially. Their plates were piled high with the finest delicacies of the ancient world. Yet, this physical fatness masked a profound spiritual starvation. Their abundance became their indictment, proving that they were utterly unworthy of the blessings they enjoyed.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 41:5 - "Then he slept and dreamed a second time; and suddenly seven heads of grain came up on one stalk, plump and good."

• Judges 3:17 - "And he presented the tribute to Eglon king of Moab. (Now Eglon was a very fat man.)"

• Zechariah 11:16 - "For indeed I will raise up a shepherd in the land who will not care for those who are cut off, nor seek the young, nor heal those who are broken, nor feed those who stand still; but he will eat the flesh of the fat and tear their hooves in pieces."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Scripture frequently highlights the tragic irony of becoming "fat" (bari'). In the deuteronomic history, Israel is warned that when they eat and are full, they will turn to other gods. Prosperity acts as a spiritual anesthetic that numbs the soul to its need for God. The Babylonians believed their plentiful food was a sign of their supreme power, but in reality, their bloated self-sufficiency was setting them up for a sudden and catastrophic fall. The very wealth they worshiped became the weight that dragged them down to destruction.
