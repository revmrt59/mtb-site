SHARE - ( cheleq , Strong's H2506 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word cheleq refers to a portion, share, or allotment assigned to an individual, whether it be land, food, or spoil from war. In the context of Habakkuk 1:16, it denotes the rich and abundant portion of wealth and food that the ruthless Babylonians acquired through their military conquests. Instead of acknowledging God as the sovereign source of their bounty, they idolatrously worshiped their own military power, symbolized by their fishing nets, celebrating their sumptuous "share" as a product of their own strength.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun cheleq is derived from the root verb chalaq, which means to divide, share, distribute, or assign. The concrete word picture behind cheleq is the ancient practice of dividing land, inheritance, or the spoils of war among a group of people. In an agrarian society, a person's cheleq was their lifeline—the specific plot of ground allotted to them for farming, or the exact portion of the harvest or flock designated for their family's survival. To have a cheleq was to have a secured place, a designated provision, and a recognized standing within the community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, cheleq represents an allotment, a portion, a tract of land, or a share of booty. It defines a designated part of a larger whole.

• It refers to a physical inheritance or tract of land divided by lot among the tribes of Israel.

• It denotes a share of spoils or war booty divided among victorious soldiers.

• It signifies a portion of food allotted to priests or sacrificial participants.

• Figuratively, it refers to a person's ultimate destiny, reward, or spiritual "portion" assigned by God.

In Habakkuk 1:16, the word is used to describe the Babylonian conquerors' physical prosperity and luxurious lifestyle. Their "share" (the fruit of their conquests) is described as sumptuous, fat, and overflowing.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs cheleq in several vivid contexts:

• The Allotment of the Land: During the conquest of Canaan, Joshua divided the land so that every family had a physical cheleq—a tangible reminder of God's covenant faithfulness and provision.

• The Spoil of Battle: When Abraham returned from defeating the kings who captured Lot, he insisted that the men who fought with him receive their fair cheleq of the recovered goods.

• The Ultimate Portion: In a beautiful spiritual shift, the Levites were given no physical territory because Yahweh Himself was their cheleq. This established the profound theological concept that God is the believer's ultimate portion, exceeding any physical inheritance.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of cheleq highlights the source of human provision and the nature of worship. Scripture reveals that God is the ultimate divider of portions; He determines what is allotted to nations and individuals.

• The Sin of Self-Deification: When people receive a rich cheleq and attribute it to their own skill, intellect, or military might, they slide into idolatry. This is the sin of the Chaldeans.

• Worshiping the Instrument: Instead of thanking the God who allowed their conquests, the Babylonians sacrificed to their weapons (their "net" and "dragnet"). They worshiped the secondary causes of their prosperity rather than the Primary Cause.

• The Deception of Material Abundance: A sumptuous physical portion (cheleq) without a relationship with God is a spiritual trap. It leads to pride, self-sufficiency, and eventual judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1, the prophet is wrestling with why God would use a wicked nation like Babylon to judge Judah. God explains that He is raising up the Chaldeans, describing them as fierce, swift, and self-governing. Habakkuk responds by noting their extreme cruelty: they sweep up nations like fish in a net.

In verse 16, Habakkuk exposes the spiritual blindness of the Babylonians. Because their "net" brings them an abundant harvest of nations, their cheleq (their daily portion and overall wealth) is sumptuous, and their food is plentiful. Consequently, they deify their own military machinery. The tragedy of Habakkuk 1:16 is the tragedy of the self-made person who worships their career, bank account, or intellect because it has provided them with a comfortable, "sumptuous" life.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Habakkuk 1:16, the word "sumptuous" translates the Hebrew word shamen, which literally means "fat" or "oily." When combined with cheleq, the text paints a picture of a portion dripping with fatness—the absolute best, richest, and most luxurious food and wealth available.

This contrasts sharply with the righteous person described in Habakkuk 2:4, who "shall live by his faith." While the Babylonian's life is defined by a temporary, physical, "fat" portion acquired through violence and worshiped through idolatry, the believer's life is defined by trust in God, who remains their eternal portion even when the physical fields fail (Habakkuk 3:17-18).
