BURN INCENSE - ( qatar , Strong's H6999 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qatar refers to the act of making a sacrifice go up in smoke, specifically through burning incense or fat on an altar. While originally designed for the holy and fragrant worship of Yahweh in the tabernacle and temple, the term frequently describes idolatrous worship in the Old Testament. In Habakkuk 1:16, it highlights the ultimate absurdity of pagan self-deification, where the ruthless Chaldeans offer worship and sacrifice to their own military instruments—their nets and dragnets—crediting their own might and tools for their prosperity rather than the living God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of qatar denotes producing smoke, causing a sacrifice to steam, or burning aromatic substances to create a fragrant odor. In ancient Near Eastern culture, the rising smoke of incense or burnt offerings represented the ascent of prayers and homage to a deity, serving as a sensory demonstration of devotion. This physical image of a dense, sweet-smelling cloud carrying the worshiper's adoration upward was central to ancient worship. When done in accordance with God's law, it symbolized a pleasing aroma rising to heaven; when done in idolatry, it represented a fragrant betrayal.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb qatar in the Piel stem means to burn incense, offer sacrifices, or make offerings go up in smoke. In the Hiphil stem, it carries a similar causative meaning of turning a sacrifice into sweet-smelling smoke on an altar. The semantic range spans the preparation and burning of aromatic herbs, resinous gums (such as frankincense), or the fat portions of animal sacrifices. Lexically, it signifies a ritual act of devotion, dedication, and worshipful submission, wherein an offering is entirely consumed and surrendered to a higher power.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Holy Sanctuary: In the Pentateuch, qatar is used in a positive, prescribed sense for the Levitical priesthood burning holy incense on the golden altar or burning the fat of peace offerings on the bronze altar. It functions as a visual word picture of prayer, holy communion, and sensory-rich worship acceptable to Yahweh.

• Spiritual Adultery: In the Historical Books and the Prophets, qatar undergoes a tragic shift. It is predominantly used to describe Israel's apostasy, specifically burning incense to Baal, the Queen of Heaven, or on pagan high places (bamoth). The word picture changes from sacred devotion to spiritual treason, where the smoke of devotion is stolen from Yahweh and directed to worthless idols.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, qatar represents the innate human impulse to worship. Worship is never neutral; if it is not directed to the Creator, it will inevitably be misdirected to the creation. Burning incense is an act of total surrender and acknowledgment of dependency. By burning incense to an object, the worshiper declares that object to be the source of life, provision, protection, and security. When directed toward idols or self, qatar exposes the depth of human depravity and spiritual blindness, showing how humanity exchanges the glory of the incorruptible God for corruptible substitutes.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:16, the prophet laments the rise of the Chaldeans (Babylonians), whom God is raising up as an instrument of judgment against Judah. The prophet describes the Chaldeans as catching nations like fish in a net. The text states: "Therefore they sacrifice to their net, and burn incense (qatar) to their dragnet."

Here, qatar takes on a highly ironic and tragic nuance. The Babylonians do not worship the true God, nor do they even credit their own pagan pantheon in this moment; instead, they deify their own military might, technology, and strategy (the "net" and "dragnet"). To burn incense to a dragnet is to treat the tool of conquest as a god. The Chaldeans attribute their luxury, wealth ("sumptuous share"), and abundant food to their own power. It is the ultimate expression of human hubris—self-worship disguised as ritual devotion.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• 1 Kings 11:8: "And he did likewise for all his foreign wives, who burned incense and sacrificed to their gods."

• 2 Kings 22:17: "because they have forsaken Me and burned incense to other gods, that they might provoke Me to anger with all the works of their hands."

• Jeremiah 1:16: "I will utter My judgments against them concerning all their wickedness, because they have forsaken Me, burned incense to other gods, and worshiped the works of their own hands."

• Jeremiah 19:4: "because they have forsaken Me and made this place an alien place, because they have burned incense in it to other gods whom neither they, their fathers, nor the kings of Judah have known, and have filled this place with the blood of the innocents."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The parallel between Habakkuk 1:16 and Jeremiah 1:16 is striking. In Jeremiah, God's people are judged because they "burned incense to other gods, and worshiped the works of their own hands." In Habakkuk, the pagan conquerors do the exact same thing by burning incense to their physical fishing nets. Both passages expose the folly of idolatry: worshipping what one's own hands have made or achieved.

For the contemporary believer, burning incense to a net warns against the subtle danger of attributing our financial security, career success, intellect, or ministerial fruitfulness to our own systems, methods, and hard work, rather than giving the glory to God.
