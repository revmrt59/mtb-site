SPARE - ( chamal , Strong's H2550 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb chamal carries the rich meaning of having compassion, pitying, or sparing someone from judgment, destruction, or death. It describes an emotional response of pity that leads to an action of protection or the withholding of punishment. In the context of Habakkuk 1:17, it is used negatively to describe the ruthless Babylonian empire, which conquers and destroys nations continuously without showing any mercy, pity, or restraint.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root chamal is deeply tied to the emotional realm of pity and compassion, often resulting in sparing someone from harm or destruction. In the ancient Near East, sparing an enemy or an object of value was a conscious choice. The word-picture behind chamal is that of a person looking upon something or someone vulnerable, feeling a deep wave of sympathy, and actively choosing to stay their hand from inflicting harm. It is the opposite of cruelty, indifference, or ruthless execution. When a king spares a defeated rival, or a parent pities a child, chamal is at work.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb chamal means to spare, pity, have compassion, or look upon with mercy. It denotes a feeling of sympathy that manifests in protective action.

• To spare: Withholding harm, judgment, or destruction that one has the power to inflict.

• To pity or have compassion: Experiencing an inner stirring of sorrow for someone else's plight, leading to leniency.

• In negative contexts, failing to chamal indicates absolute ruthlessness, unyielding cruelty, and an unchecked desire to destroy.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Kingly Mercy and Restraint: The term frequently describes sparing lives in warfare. For instance, King Saul was commanded to utterly destroy the Amalekites but refused to chamal (spare) Agag and the best of the livestock, showing a misdirected application of the word where obedience was required.

• Parental Affection: It is used of a father sparing his son who serves him, or a mother having compassion on her child, such as when Pharaoh's daughter had pity on the infant Moses.

• Divine Forbearance: God Himself is the ultimate subject of chamal, choosing to spare His people from deserved judgment because of His covenant love, though He also warns that a time comes when He will not spare if wickedness continues.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, chamal represents the boundary line between judgment and grace. Because God is holy, His justice demands punishment for sin, but His chamal moves Him to spare a remnant. However, when God withdraws His pity, it signals the arrival of absolute, unmitigated judgment. In the prophetic literature, the suspension of divine chamal is one of the most terrifying declarations of doom. Conversely, when human beings fail to show chamal to one another, they reflect the image of the enemy rather than the Creator, revealing a heart hardened by pride and violence.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:17, the prophet is wrestling with the shocking reality of God using the Chaldeans (Babylonians) to punish Judah. Habakkuk describes the Babylonians as fishermen gathering nations like fish in a dragnet, rejoicing over their helpless prey, and worshiping their own military power. The prophet asks, "Shall they therefore empty their net, and not spare continually to slay nations?"

Here, chamal highlights the relentless, insatiable cruelty of the Babylonian empire. They have no moral brake, no internal compass of mercy, and no capacity for pity. Their conquest is a continuous, mechanical slaughter without any hesitation or sparing of the weak. By highlighting their lack of chamal, Habakkuk appeals to God's own character of justice: How can a merciful and holy God stand by and watch a nation that knows no mercy devour those more righteous than themselves?

• Malachi 3:17 – "'They shall be Mine,' says the LORD of hosts, 'On the day that I make them My jewels. And I will spare them As a man spares his own son who serves him.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

An important distinction exists between chamal and other Hebrew words for mercy, such as racham (womb-like compassion, deep emotional love) and chesed (covenant faithfulness/lovingkindness). While racham emphasizes the deep internal feeling of love and chesed emphasizes covenant loyalty, chamal specifically focuses on the action of sparing or showing leniency to someone who is at your mercy. It is the practical outworking of pity that stays a hand of violence or destruction.
