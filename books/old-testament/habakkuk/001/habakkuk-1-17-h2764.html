NET - (cherem, Strong's H2764)

Summary:

In Habakkuk 1:17, the word "net" (cherem) serves as a vivid, chilling metaphor for the relentless and systematic military conquest of the Chaldeans (Babylonians). Just as a fisherman casts a large dragnet into the sea to gather every fish in its path, leaving no means of escape, the Babylonian empire swept through the ancient Near East, capturing entire nations and treating human lives as mere catch. The prophet Habakkuk uses this terminology to express his deep pastoral and theological struggle: how can a holy God stand by while a wicked, pagan nation continuously empties its net of conquered peoples only to immediately cast it again to destroy more nations without mercy?

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, fishing was a vital industry, and the tools of the trade were familiar to everyone. The cherem was a large fishing net—either a casting net thrown by hand or a heavy dragnet swept through the water by boats. Once cast, the net was weighted at the bottom, trapping everything within its radius. It represents inescapable containment. To be caught in a cherem is to be rendered completely helpless, stripped of autonomy, and destined for processing at the whim of the fisherman. When applied to warfare, this imagery depicts an army that does not merely defeat its enemies in battle but completely rounds up, deports, and consumes entire populations, treating human beings like silent, helpless sea creatures.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew noun cherem refers to a net, specifically a net used for catching fish or, metaphorically, a snare used to trap people. It derives from a root meaning to shut in, perforate, or make net-like mesh. A distinct homonym sharing the same spelling refers to a ban, or something devoted to destruction. In Habakkuk 1:17 and its immediate context, the word refers strictly to the literal fishing implement used metaphorically. The term denotes an instrument of capture that leaves its victims with no avenue of retreat, emphasizing the total vulnerability of those caught within its mesh.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, cherem is used to paint pictures of entrapment, vulnerability, and moral decay.

• It is used of literal fishing nets being spread out to dry on bare rocks, symbolizing the complete desolation and ruin of once-mighty coastal cities.

• It is used metaphorically of wicked people who hunt their fellow citizens, treating society as a hunting ground where the weak are snared by the powerful.

• In Habakkuk, it is paired with the "dragnet" (mikmereth) to show a multi-layered system of capture. The Chaldeans do not rely on a single method; they have an entire arsenal of nets designed to ensure that no nation escapes their grasp.

4. Theological Implications

The theological crisis in Habakkuk revolves around the sovereignty of God and the problem of evil. The Babylonians did not recognize Yahweh; instead, they deified their own military strength and weapons. Habakkuk 1:16 states that they "sacrifice to their net," meaning they worshipped their own military apparatus, treating their tactical genius and destructive weapons as the source of their prosperity. This is the ultimate form of self-idolatry—worshipping the tool instead of the Creator. It poses a massive theological question: Will the sovereign God of Israel, who uses Babylon as a temporary instrument of divine discipline, allow this arrogant empire to continue treating human beings as worthless fish, endlessly glorifying their own violent instruments?

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:17, the prophet asks, "Shall they therefore empty their net, and not spare continually to slay the nations?" The phrase "empty their net" refers to the practice of hauling the catch ashore, dumping the fish out to be processed or consumed, and immediately returning to the water to catch more. Habakkuk is pointing out the insatiable appetite of the Babylonian war machine. Their conquest is not a one-time event or a localized judgment; it is a continuous, mechanical cycle of destruction. The nuance here is one of agonizing repetition. The prophet cries out on behalf of a suffering world, asking God when this endless cycle of violence, fueled by an idolized military complex, will finally be brought to an end by divine justice.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Habakkuk 1:15: "They take up all of them with the hook, They catch them in their net, And gather them in their dragnet. Therefore they rejoice and are glad."

• Habakkuk 1:16: "Therefore they sacrifice to their net, And burn incense to their dragnet; Because by them their share is sumptuous And their food plentiful."

• Micah 7:2: "The faithful man has perished from the earth, And there is no one upright among men. They all lie in wait for blood; Every man hunts his brother with a net."

• Ezekiel 26:5: "It shall be a place for spreading nets in the midst of the sea, for I have spoken,’ says the Lord GOD; ‘it shall become plunder for the nations."

• Ezekiel 47:10: "It shall be that fishermen will stand by it; from En Gedi to En Eglaim they will be places for spreading nets. Their fish will be of the same kinds as the fish of the Great Sea, exceedingly many."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The imagery of the cherem provides a stark contrast to the New Testament call of Jesus to His disciples. While the empires of this world cast their nets to capture, kill, and destroy human lives for their own enrichment, Jesus calls His followers to be "fishers of men" (Mark 1:17). In the kingdom of God, the "net" of the Gospel is cast not to bring people to slaughter, but to rescue them from the chaotic, dark waters of sin and death, drawing them into life, safety, and kingdom citizenship.
