EMPTY - ( ruq , Strong's H7324 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ruq carries the primary meaning of pouring out, emptying, or drawing out a weapon. It represents a transition from fullness to vacancy, whether referring to the emptying of sacks, the drawing of a sword from its scabbard, or the pouring out of divine blessings. In Habakkuk 1:17, the term depicts the relentless, insatiable greed of the Chaldeans, who continuously "empty" their dragnet of captured nations only to cast it again, illustrating a devastating cycle of ruthless conquest and plunder.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete imagery of ruq is rooted in ancient Near Eastern daily life, depicting the physical act of tipping over a container to completely drain its contents. This could refer to emptying grain sacks, pouring out liquid from a jar, or drawing a sword from its sheath (literally "emptying" the scabbard). The term conveys a thorough, unchecked action where nothing is left inside. In military contexts, drawing a sword (ruq) signals the preparation for immediate, unsparing destruction. In agricultural contexts, emptying vessels or sacks represents either preparation for replenishment or the sudden exposure of what was hidden inside.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ruq is a primitive root that functions primarily in the Hiphil (causative) and Hophal (passive) stems.

• In the Hiphil stem, it means to make empty, pour out, empty out, or draw out (such as a sword or lance).

• In the Hophal stem, it means to be poured out or emptied.

The lexical core of the word denotes the complete depletion or evacuation of a vessel's contents. It does not merely mean to leak or diminish, but to actively and completely transfer contents from one state or container to another.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs ruq in several vivid conceptual categories:

• The Draw of the Sword: In passages of military judgment, ruq describes drawing a sword. The sword is "emptied" from its sheath, signifying that restraint has ended and execution is imminent.

• The Pouring of Blessing: Conversely, the word is used to describe the overwhelming abundance of God's provision. To "pour out" a blessing suggests a vessel so thoroughly emptied by God that the recipient lacks the capacity to hold it all.

• The Emptying of Vessels: It describes the physical emptying of sacks or jars, which often leads to a moment of revelation or transition, such as Joseph's brothers finding their money in their emptied sacks.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, ruq demonstrates the absolute sovereignty of God in both judgment and grace. When God "empties" His storehouses, it results in either uncontainable blessing or unsparing judgment. The term reveals that human fullness is temporary and easily depleted by divine decree. Furthermore, the human tendency to "empty" things for selfish gain—such as the Chaldeans emptying their nets—stands in stark contrast to God's act of emptying Himself or His resources to give life and sustain His covenant people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:17, the prophet is wrestling with the shocking reality of God using the wicked Babylonians (Chaldeans) to punish Judah. Habakkuk uses the metaphor of a fisherman using a dragnet to sweep up fish (representing vulnerable nations).

The use of ruq ("Shall they therefore empty their net...?") functions as a cry for divine justice. The Chaldeans do not catch fish to satisfy a temporary hunger; instead, they "empty" their net—gathering the plunder, clearing the vessel—simply so they can cast it again to destroy more nations without pity. The word highlights their systematic, repetitive, and insatiable appetite for imperial expansion and violence. It implies a horrifying, endless cycle of devastation unless God steps in to stop them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 42:35: "Then it happened as they emptied their sacks, that surprisingly every man’s bundle of money was in his sack; and when both they and their father saw the bundles of money, they were afraid."

• Exodus 15:9: "The enemy said, ‘I will pursue, I will overtake, I will divide the spoil; My desire shall be satisfied on them. I will draw my sword, My hand shall destroy them.’"

• Ecclesiastes 11:3: "If the clouds are full of rain, They empty themselves upon the earth; And if a tree falls to the south or the north, In the place where the tree falls, there it shall be."

• Malachi 3:10: "Bring all the tithes into the storehouse, That there may be food in My house, And try Me now in this,” Says the Lord of hosts, “If I will not open for you the windows of heaven And pour out for you such blessing That there will not be room enough to receive it."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint or LXX), the Hebrew ruq in Habakkuk 1:17 is translated using the verb amphiballo, which refers to casting a net around or casting a dragnet. This highlights the action of casting rather than the emptying, focusing the Greek reader's attention on the endless casting of the net. However, the Hebrew text retains the profound psychological and systemic picture of "emptying" the net—showing that the Babylonians treat human souls as mere merchandise to be collected, dumped out into their storehouses, and pursued again.
