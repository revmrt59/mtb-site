SAVE - ( yasha , Strong's H3467 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yasha means to rescue, deliver, or save from danger, distress, or oppression. In its core sense, it denotes bringing someone out of a confined, suffocating space into a wide-open, spacious place of safety and freedom. In Habakkuk 1:2, the prophet cries out in agony because God seems inactive in the face of rampant injustice and violence, questioning why the covenant Lord has not stepped in to deliver His people from their internal moral decay and oppression.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of yasha is associated with being wide, spacious, or having room. In Hebrew thought, distress and trouble are frequently pictured as being in a tight, narrow, or restricted place—such as a deep pit, a strait, or a besieged city. To "save" (yasha) is the action of a champion who intervenes on behalf of the helpless, breaking their chains and bringing them out of that confined, suffocating space into a broad, open place where they can breathe and move freely. It is a word of active, decisive rescue, not passive assistance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• yasha is a primary root verb occurring over 200 times in the Old Testament, predominantly in the Hiphil (causative) form (hoshia).

• The verb denotes the act of rescuing, delivering, or preserving from physical threats, enemies, death, or moral ruin.

• It signifies a decisive intervention that radically alters the circumstances of the one in distress, moving them from jeopardy to safety, and from defeat to victory.

• It is closely linked with the concept of justice, as the savior or deliverer often has to execute justice against the oppressor to free the oppressed.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• yasha is frequently used of military deliverance where God or a human leader appointed by God rescues Israel from foreign oppressors. The judges in the book of Judges are called "deliverers" (moshia, a participle of yasha) who saved Israel from their enemies.

• The noun derivatives include yeshua (salvation/deliverance) and teshua (deliverance/victory), highlighting that salvation is not merely an abstract concept but a concrete, historical action of rescue.

• The term is often paired with terms for safety, help, and shield, illustrating a total package of preservation and security.

4. Theological Implications

• Salvation belongs fundamentally to Yahweh. He is the ultimate Savior (Moshia), and human help is ultimately vain without Him.

• The term spans both temporal-physical rescue (from war, sickness, or injustice) and spiritual deliverance (from sin, judgment, and exile).

• In the prophetic literature, yasha points forward to the ultimate, messianic deliverance where God will vindicate His righteousness, judge evil, and save His people eternally.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:2, the prophet is surrounded by systemic violence (chamas), injustice, and strife within Judah. The cry "And You will not save?" is a poignant expression of perceived divine silence and inaction. Habakkuk knows that Yahweh has the power to deliver and is covenantally bound to defend righteousness, yet He seems to stand idly by. yasha here highlights the physical, social, and spiritual rescue needed from the internal collapse of Judah's society before the Chaldean invasion is even threatened. It is a plea for God to act as the divine Deliverer of the righteous who are being swallowed up by the wicked.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 14:30 - "So the LORD saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians, and Israel saw the Egyptians dead on the seashore."

• Psalm 34:18 - "The LORD is near to those who have a broken heart, And saves such as have a contrite spirit."

• Isaiah 45:22 - "Look to Me, and be saved, All you ends of the earth! For I am God, and there is no other."

• Jeremiah 17:14 - "Heal me, O LORD, and I shall be healed; Save me, and I shall be saved, For You are my praise."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Greek equivalent in the Septuagint (LXX) is typically sozo, which carries the same rich meaning of physical rescue, healing, and spiritual preservation into the New Testament.

• The names Joshua (Yehoshua) and Jesus (Yeshua) are direct derivations of this root, meaning "Yahweh is salvation" or "Yahweh saves." Thus, Habakkuk's cry for salvation is ultimately answered in the person and work of Jesus Christ, who delivers His people from the ultimate violence of sin and death.
