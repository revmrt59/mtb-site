CRY - ( shawa` , Strong's H7768 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shawa` refers to a desperate, urgent cry for help, uttered by someone in extreme distress who has completely exhausted their own resources. Used almost exclusively in the context of prayer, this word captures the raw, guttural plea of a sufferer seeking divine intervention and deliverance. In Habakkuk 1:2, it reveals the prophet's agonizing frustration as he screams for help in the midst of rampant injustice, struggling to reconcile his deep covenant faith with God's puzzling silence.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word shawa` conveys the imagery of a person in a tight, suffocating space who cries out to be brought into a spacious, safe place.

• It is not a polite or formal request; it is the instinctive, high-pitched scream of someone facing imminent death, captivity, or ruin.

• Culturally, this term reflects the ancient Near Eastern practice of appealing to a king or a higher authority for justice when the local social systems have failed. It is the cry of the oppressed who has no other court of appeal.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• shawa` is a primary verb meaning to cry out for help, to shout for aid, or to appeal for deliverance.

• It occurs predominantly in the Piel stem (an intensive verb form in Hebrew), which underscores the repetitive, earnest, and agonizing nature of the plea. It is not a single shout but a prolonged, desperate pleading.

• The definition carries an inherent expectation of a savior. To utter a shawa` is to declare one's utter vulnerability and absolute dependence on the person being addressed.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• This term is highly prominent in the Psalms and the Book of Job, where it serves as the vocabulary of lament and severe suffering.

• It is often paired with physical imagery of sinking into deep mire, drowning in floods, or standing on the brink of Sheol, emphasizing that the speaker is at the end of their human limits.

• In the context of Israel's relationship with God, shawa` is the language of the covenant. It is the cry that God has historically promised to hear and act upon.

4. Theological Implications

• shawa` reveals that biblical faith accommodates raw, unfiltered lament. God does not demand polite or sanitized prayers; He invites His people to bring their deepest agony and desperation directly to Him.

• The word highlights the relational nature of God. A distress cry only makes sense if there is a personal, listening God who possesses both the compassion to care and the power to save.

• The ultimate theological crisis occurs when a servant of God utters this cry and experiences divine silence. Because God is defined as the One who hears the needy when they cry, an unanswered shawa` tests the very foundations of faith and covenant security.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Habakkuk 1:2, the prophet uses shawa` to expose his profound disillusionment: "O LORD, how long shall I cry, and You will not hear?"

• The phrase "how long" combined with shawa` indicates that Habakkuk's crying has been continuous, passionate, and agonizing. This is not his first prayer; it is the culmination of a long, unanswered season of intercession.

• Habakkuk is crying out because of the moral collapse, violence, and perversion of justice in Judah. The irony is agonizing: the prophet is crying out for God to rescue Judah from its own lawlessness, but God appears to be inactive.

• By using shawa`, Habakkuk holds God to His covenant promises, essentially asking why the Savior of Israel is acting like a distant, indifferent spectator to His prophet's desperate cries.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 18:6 - "In my distress I called upon the LORD, and cried out to my God; He heard my voice from His temple, and my cry came before Him, even into His ears."

• Psalm 30:2 - "O LORD my God, I cried out to You, and You healed me."

• Job 19:7 - "If I cry out, 'Violence!' I am not heard; If I cry aloud, there is no justice."

• Jonah 2:2 - "Out of the belly of Sheol I cried, and You heard my voice."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• It is helpful to distinguish shawa` from other Hebrew words for crying out. While za'aq (also used in Habakkuk 1:2) can be a general cry of grief or a public protest, shawa` is almost always directed vertically toward God with the specific hope of deliverance.

• In classical Hebrew, the noun form of this root (shawa) is closely related to the word for "salvation" (yeshua). Ethically and linguistically, the cry of distress (shawa`) is designed to be answered by the act of salvation (yeshua). The tragedy of Habakkuk 1:2 is the rupture of this linguistic and theological expectation.
