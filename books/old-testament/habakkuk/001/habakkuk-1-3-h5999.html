TROUBLE - ( amal , Strong's H5999 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun amal refers to wearisome toil, painful labor, misery, or the mischief that produces trouble. In Habakkuk 1:3, it describes the societal grievance and vexation that the prophet is forced to observe among his own people. This word highlights both the agonizing emotional weight of witnessing rampant injustice and the physical hardship endured by those who are oppressed by it.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of amal lies in the physical exertion and exhausting labor that leaves a person drained and weary. It is the kind of work that yields sweat, pain, and little lasting satisfaction. Over time, the word expanded from describing purely physical, backbreaking toil to encompassing the emotional and mental anguish that results from difficult circumstances. It captures the heavy, grinding burden of human existence under the weight of the Fall. In the ancient Near East, where physical survival was a daily struggle, amal represented the ultimate frustration of human effort—laboring exhaustingly only to meet sorrow, vanity, or oppression.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Amal functions primarily as a noun designating:

• Wearisome toil, labor, or effort.

• Misery, trouble, suffering, or sorrow resulting from such labor or from difficult life circumstances.

• Mischief, harm, or evil deeds planned by the wicked to cause distress to others.

• Grievance or vexation of spirit.

The term represents both the objective hardship (the difficult labor or unjust situation) and the subjective experience of that hardship (the grief, misery, and pain felt by the sufferer).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term is highly prominent in Wisdom literature, where it serves to describe the ultimate futility of human life apart from God:

• In Ecclesiastes, it is used repeatedly to describe the exhausting "toil" that humans perform under the sun, which ultimately yields no permanent profit.

• In the Book of Job, it portrays the sorrow and misery of a broken life, where days are filled with weariness and nights with sleepless grief.

• In the Psalms, amal frequently carries an ethical nuance, describing the "mischief" or "trouble" that wicked men devise in their hearts. The psalmists often picture this mischief as a boomerang that recoils on the head of the perpetrator.

• Visually, amal is a heavy yoke or a crushing load that bows a person down, draining their energy and filling their life with bitterness instead of joy.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, amal is deeply tied to the consequences of sin and the reality of a fallen world. Genesis 3 established that human labor and childbearing would be marked by pain and toil, and amal is a direct expression of that reality. It reminds God's people that life apart from the covenantal presence of God is a cycle of exhausting, empty effort ending in grief. However, Scripture also reveals that God is not indifferent to human amal. He observes the trouble of the helpless, remembers their sorrow, and promises a future state where His people will no longer labor in vain or experience wearisome sorrow. Ultimately, the presence of amal drives the believer to seek the divine rest and redemption that only Yahweh can provide.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:3, the prophet cries out, "Why do You show me iniquity, and cause me to see trouble?" Here, amal is paired parallel with awen (iniquity or vanity) and is surrounded by terms of social violence ("plundering and violence," "strife," and "contention"). In this specific setting, amal is the misery, grievance, and distress resulting from widespread injustice within the covenant community of Judah. Habakkuk is forced to look upon a society where the wicked devour the righteous, the law is powerless, and justice is perverted. The "trouble" is the exhausting, painful burden of witnessing systemic corruption and the suffering of the weak, while God appears to remain silent. The word emphasizes the prophet's emotional exhaustion and the sheer weariness of his soul as he beholds the spiritual and social decay of his nation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Job 3:10 - "Because it did not shut up the doors of my mother’s womb, Nor hide sorrow from my eyes."

• Psalm 7:16 - "His trouble shall return upon his own head, And his violent dealing shall come down on his own crown."

• Psalm 10:14 - "But You have seen, for You observe trouble and grief, To repay it with Your hand. The helpless commits himself to You; You are the helper of the fatherless."

• Ecclesiastes 2:11 - "Then I looked on all the works that my hands had done And on the labor in which I had toiled; And indeed all was vanity and grasping for the wind. There was no profit under the sun."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint or LXX), amal is frequently translated by the terms ponos (pain, distress, heavy labor) and kopos (intense, exhausting toil). These Greek equivalents emphasize the physical and psychological toll that trouble takes on an individual. This linguistic connection deepens our understanding of the New Testament promise where believers are encouraged that their "labor (kopos) is not in vain in the Lord" (1 Corinthians 15:58), showing that the exhausting, futile toil of the old covenant era is redeemed and given eternal purpose through the resurrection of Jesus Christ.
