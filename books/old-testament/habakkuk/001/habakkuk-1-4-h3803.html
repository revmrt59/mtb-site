SURROUND - ( katar , Strong's H3803 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb katar conveys the graphic imagery of encircling, crowning, or hemming in. In Habakkuk 1:4, it describes how the wicked aggressively hem in and choke out the righteous, paralyzing the legal and moral systems of Judah. Rather than representing a neutral grouping, this word indicates an oppressive, hostile siege that cuts off any avenue of justice, leaving the faithful feeling completely isolated and vulnerable.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of katar is intimately connected to the concept of a crown (keter), which wraps around or encircles the head. When applied to actions, it portrays a physical encirclement, much like a crown wrapping around a head or a hostile military force surrounding a city. In ancient Near Eastern warfare, to surround a city meant to lay siege to it—cutting off all supplies, communications, and escape routes. The word picture is one of complete confinement and suffocation, where the object in the center is entirely at the mercy of those who have closed the circle around it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb katar appears fifteen times in the Old Testament, functioning in several distinct senses depending on the stem. In the Pi'el stem, it can mean "to wait" or "to bear with" (as one waits for a crown to be placed). In the Hiphil stem—the form used in Habakkuk 1:4—it means "to surround" in a hostile sense, "to beset," or "to hem in." It carries the semantic weight of overwhelming pressure exerted from all sides, preventing escape, movement, or relief. It is the deliberate action of closing ranks around a target to neutralize its influence.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Hostile Siege: It depicts the closing of a circle around an enemy, leaving them trapped without hope of rescue (Judges 20:43).

• Demonic or Hostile Hemming In: In messianic suffering, it describes being surrounded by powerful, animalistic foes that close in on the righteous sufferer (Psalm 22:12).

• Coronation or Supportive Encirclement: In a positive sense, the term can relate to being crowned or surrounded with goodness, such as when the righteous encircle a person in celebration or support (Psalm 142:7; Proverbs 14:18).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the encircling of the righteous by the wicked represents the apparent triumph of chaos over God’s moral order. God’s covenantal design is that His favor should surround (sabab, a synonym) the righteous as a shield. However, under systemic corruption, the wicked mimic this divine protection with a demonic parody—encircling the righteous to choke out their voice. This inversion creates a profound theological crisis for the prophet. It raises the agonizing question of why a holy God allows the wicked to isolate, suppress, and silence His faithful remnant.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:4, the prophet laments the social and spiritual decay within Judah. He cries out that the law (torah) is paralyzed and justice (mishpat) never goes forth because "the wicked surround the righteous." The nuance here is one of judicial and societal strangulation. The righteous are outnumbered, politically blockaded, and legally paralyzed. Every avenue of legal recourse is blocked; wherever the righteous turn, they encounter corrupt adversaries. Consequently, "perverse judgment proceeds." The surrounding is so total that the godly are rendered powerless and silenced within their own covenant community.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Judges 20:43: "They surrounded the Benjamites, chased them, and easily trampled them down opposite Gibeah toward the east."

• Psalm 22:12: "Many bulls have surrounded me; Strong bulls of Bashan have encircled me."

• Psalm 142:7: "Bring my soul out of prison, That I may praise Your name; The righteous shall surround me, For You shall deal bountifully with me."

• Proverbs 14:18: "The simple inherit folly, But the prudent are crowned with knowledge."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) translates katar in Habakkuk 1:4 with the Greek verb katadynasteuo, which means "to oppress, tyrannize, or exploit." This translation demonstrates that the ancient translators understood this "surrounding" not merely as a passive physical position, but as an active, hostile exercise of abusive, systemic power. It was an encirclement specifically designed to subjugate, exploit, and silence the righteous.
