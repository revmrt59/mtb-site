WICKED - ( rasha` , Strong's H7563 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word rasha` denotes a person who is actively hostile to God's covenant, morally corrupt, and socially disruptive. In the Old Testament, it represents the absolute antithesis of the righteous person (tsaddiq). Rather than simply referring to a general state of badness, it carries a strong forensic or legal connotation of being "guilty" or "in the wrong" before a judge. In Habakkuk 1:4, the "wicked" refers to the lawless, covenant-breaking citizens of Judah whose systemic injustice and oppression of the godly prompted the prophet's agonizing cry to God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root r-sh-` relates to the concept of being loose, disjointed, or actively turbulent. A helpful word picture is that of a person who is unsettled, chaotic, and perpetually out of harmony with God’s established moral order. Unlike the righteous person who is "straight" (yashar) and firmly established, the wicked person is unstable, casting up trouble and strife. The rasha` is someone who actively pushes against God's boundaries, causing friction and ruin within the community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term rasha` means guilty of crime, acting wickedly, or being in the wrong in a legal dispute. It designates a person who has been tried and found guilty, or whose actions merit condemnation. In a theological sense, it describes those who live in willful rebellion against God’s covenant laws, displaying an ethical and spiritual hostility toward God and His people. The word describes not just an internal disposition, but a lifestyle of active, harmful wrongdoing.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Restless Sea: The Old Testament compares the wicked to the troubled sea that cannot rest, whose waters cast up mire and dirt (Isaiah 57:20).

• Chaff in the Wind: The wicked are depicted as weightless, worthless chaff driven away by the wind, lacking stability and substance (Psalm 1:4).

• The Snare: The wicked are frequently portrayed as setting traps and snares for the innocent, though they ultimately fall into their own pits (Psalm 9:15-16).

• The Courtroom: In judicial settings, rasha` is the legal status of the guilty party who stands condemned, contrasted with the innocent party (tsaddiq) who is vindicated.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of rasha` rests on the reality of the covenant. Wickedness is not merely a violation of abstract ethical rules; it is a relational rupture. The wicked person lives as if God does not exist, does not see, or will not hold them accountable. This practical atheism leads directly to the exploitation of the vulnerable. Ultimately, Scripture reveals that God’s holiness requires Him to condemn the rasha` and vindicate the tsaddiq, ensuring that the wicked will not stand in the final judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:4, the identity of the "wicked" is crucial for understanding the prophet's distress.

• Internal Oppression: The "wicked" here are not pagan enemies (like the Babylonians), but the corrupt, ruling class of Judah. They are the wealthy and powerful Judeans who have abandoned the Torah.

• Systemic Paralysis: Because these internal wicked rulers dominate society, the law (torah) has become "powerless" or paralyzed, and justice is never executed.

• The Siege of the Righteous: The phrase "the wicked surround the righteous" pictures a hostile army encircling a city. The godly minority in Judah is trapped, hemmed in, and helpless against the systemic corruption of their own countrymen. This perversion of justice within the covenant community is what drives Habakkuk to cry out to God for intervention.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 18:23 - "And Abraham came near and said, 'Would You also destroy the righteous with the wicked?'"

• Proverbs 10:25 - "When the whirlwind passes by, the wicked is no more, But the righteous has an everlasting foundation."

• Ezekiel 33:11 - "Say to them: ‘As I live,’ says the Lord God, ‘I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live. Turn, turn from your evil ways! For why should you die, O house of Israel?’"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A profound irony unfolds in Habakkuk chapter 1. When Habakkuk complains about the wicked (rasha`) Judeans in verse 4, God responds that He is raising up the Chaldeans (Babylonians) to punish them. This throws Habakkuk into an even greater crisis of faith. In Habakkuk 1:13, the prophet asks why God remains silent when the "wicked" (now referring to the brutal, pagan Chaldeans) devour a person "more righteous than he" (referring to Judah, which, despite its sins, still possessed the covenant). The progression of the book shows that while God uses one wicked instrument to judge another, both will ultimately face His absolute justice, while "the just shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4).
