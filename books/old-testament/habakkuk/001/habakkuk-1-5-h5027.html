WATCH - ( nabat , Strong's H5027 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nabat means to look intently, gaze with focused attention, scan the horizon, or regard something with deep mental and spiritual consideration. In Habakkuk 1:5, God uses this word as a powerful command to a doubting prophet and a drifting nation, telling them to stop looking only at their immediate circumstances and instead "watch" what He is doing on the grand stage of world history. It is an invitation to move from passive, despondent staring to active, expectant observation of divine sovereignty in action, even when the unfolding events seem terrifying or unbelievable.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root idea of nabat involves straining the eyes, scanning the distance, or focusing one's vision intensely on a specific object. Unlike a casual or fleeting glance, nabat carries a physical nuance of leaning forward, craning one's neck, or clearing away obstacles to see something clearly. It is the action of a watchman standing on a high tower scanning the horizon for an approaching messenger, or a father looking down the road with eager expectation. The word suggests a deliberate and purposeful direction of sight, born out of deep interest, concern, or expectation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• nabat is a Hebrew verb occurring primarily in the Hiphil stem. Its semantic range includes:

• To look intently or gaze upon something physical with sustained attention.

• To look with intellectual, moral, or spiritual comprehension—to truly behold and consider a reality rather than just seeing it.

• To look with favor, care, or regard (when used of God looking upon the humble, or humans looking to God for salvation).

• To look with disfavor or judgment, observing actions with a view to hold them accountable.

• To watch or observe events unfold with expectation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, nabat is used in rich, illustrative contexts:

• Abraham Counting the Stars: God brought Abraham outside and commanded him to "look" (nabat) toward heaven and count the stars (Genesis 15:5). This was a call to gaze in wonder at the physical display of God's limitless power and promise.

• Lot's Wife: When fleeing Sodom, Lot's wife "looked back" (nabat) from behind him and became a pillar of salt (Genesis 19:26). Her gaze was not a quick glance, but a lingering, longing look of attachment to what was being judged.

• The Bronze Serpent: In the wilderness, anyone bitten by a fiery serpent who "looked" (nabat) at the bronze serpent lived (Numbers 21:9). This look was an act of focused, obedient faith and expectant trust in God's provision.

• The Divine Gaze: God "looks" on the earth and it trembles (Psalm 104:32), demonstrating His active, all-powerful supervision over creation.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereignty Over History: When God commands His people to "watch," He asserts that history is not a chaotic series of random accidents. God is the sovereign author of history, directing the rise and fall of empires to accomplish His holy, redemptive purposes.

• Faith Versus Human Sight: Real biblical watching requires faith. Human sight often perceives only trouble, injustice, and divine silence. Godly watching (nabat) looks past the immediate crisis to see the hand of God moving in unexpected, even shocking ways.

• The Discipline of Expectant Patience: Watching implies a posture of waiting. It calls the believer out of paralyzing anxiety into a state of quiet, disciplined observation, waiting to see how God will resolve the tensions of this broken world.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1, the prophet is deeply distressed by the rampant injustice, violence, and lawlessness in Judah. He cries out to God, asking how long he must cry for help while God seemingly ignores the wickedness (Habakkuk 1:2-4). God's response in verse 5 begins with a series of urgent plural imperatives: "Look among the nations and watch—be utterly astounded!"

The use of nabat here is a direct remedy to Habakkuk's spiritual myopia. Habakkuk was focused entirely on his immediate, local surroundings. God commands him to lift his eyes and scan the broader international horizon. The Chaldeans (Babylonians) are rising, and God is actively raising them up as an instrument of discipline.

• nabat serves as a sharp transition from limited human perception to the expansive view of divine providence. God tells the prophet that what he is about to witness will require deep, sustained observation because it will completely shatter his preconceived notions of how God operates.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Dual Use of Sight in Habakkuk: There is a profound literary and spiritual irony in how nabat is used in this chapter. In Habakkuk 1:5, God commands the prophet to nabat (watch) His work among the nations. In verse 13, a bewildered Habakkuk throws this same word back at God, questioning how a holy God can "look on" (nabat) the treacherous Chaldeans and remain silent while they destroy people more righteous than themselves. This wordplay highlights the intense struggle between human logic and the mysterious timing of divine justice.

• The Septuagint (LXX) Translation: The ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament renders nabat in Habakkuk 1:5 with the verb epiblepsate (from epiblepo), which means to look upon with close attention, care, or regard. It is an urgent call to pay close, analytical heed to what is being pointed out.
