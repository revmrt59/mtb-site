BELIEVE - ( 'aman , Strong's H0539 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'aman carries the fundamental idea of firmness, reliability, and support. In the Hiphil stem, as used in Habakkuk 1:5, it denotes active trust, belief, and reliance on a message or person. In the context of Habakkuk's lament, God declares that He is about to perform an incredible work of judgment using the Chaldeans—a work so shocking and counterintuitive that the people of Judah would refuse to trust or accept it as true, even when explicitly told.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete root of 'aman is associated with things that are firm, stable, and dependable. It paints a picture of a structural pillar holding up a roof, providing unwavering support so the building does not collapse. A closely related noun refers to a guardian or nursing father ('omen) who securely carries an infant in his arms, providing absolute safety. From this root we also derive the word amen, which means "truly," "it is certain," or "so be it." Therefore, biblical belief is not a vague hope or intellectual speculation; it is the act of leaning one's entire weight upon something that is structurally sound and completely reliable.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In classical Hebrew, the meaning of 'aman depends heavily on its grammatical stem:

• Qal Stem: To support, nourish, or foster.

• Niphal Stem: To be established, faithful, reliable, permanent, or verified.

• Hiphil Stem (used in Habakkuk 1:5): To cause to trust, to believe, to stand firm, or to treat someone or something as completely trustworthy.

In the Hiphil, the verb does not merely mean "to think a statement is true." It denotes a deep, relational trust where the believer acts upon the truth of the speaker's word. To "not believe" means to reject the reliability of the speaker, treating their message as unstable, false, or impossible.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, 'aman depicts the posture of the heart toward God and His Word:

• The Pillar Metaphor: Just as a pillar supports a heavy overhang, believing is placing our life’s weight onto God's character.

• The Secure Step: Walking on solid, level ground rather than sinking sand. Unbelief is viewed as trying to stand on a crumbling ledge.

• The Nurse and Child: Finding total safety and rest in the arms of a faithful caretaker.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'aman is the primary Old Testament word for saving faith. It demonstrates that biblical faith is inherently relational. It requires a subject (the believer) and an object (the faithful God).

• Faith and Covenant: Faith is the only appropriate response to God's covenant promises. Without trust in His character, the covenant relationship is fractured.

• Faith and Stability: Isaiah 7:9 makes a wordplay on this root: "If you will not believe ('aman in the Hiphil), surely you shall not be established ('aman in the Niphal)." Spiritual stability is directly tied to active faith.

• Faith in the Unseen: Believing requires trusting God’s sovereignty even when His actions or methods seem contradictory to human reason.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:5, the Hiphil verb appears as ta'aminu in the negative: "Which you would not believe, though it were told you."

• The Shocking Work of God: Habakkuk has complained about the moral decay and injustice in Judah. God responds by revealing His instrument of discipline: the ruthless, pagan Chaldeans (Babylonians).

• The Crisis of Faith: This divine strategy violates Judah’s conventional theological expectations. They believed God would never use a more wicked nation to judge His own covenant people.

• Severe Mercy: God warns that His work of judgment will be so staggering that the people will refuse to trust the prophetic warnings. They would rather rely on false security than accept the sovereign, terrifying reality of God's imminent work.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 15:6 - "And he believed in the LORD, and He accounted it to him for righteousness."

• Exodus 4:31 - "So the people believed; and when they heard that the LORD had visited the children of Israel and that He had looked on their affliction, then they bowed their heads and worshiped."

• Isaiah 7:9 - "If you will not believe, Surely you shall not be established."

• Jonah 3:5 - "So the people of Nineveh believed God, proclaimed a fast, and put on sackcloth, from the greatest to the least of them."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• New Testament Connection: In Acts 13:41, the Apostle Paul quotes Habakkuk 1:5 in his sermon at Antioch of Pisidia. He uses this Old Testament warning to caution his Jewish listeners not to reject the work of Jesus Christ. Just as the Jews of Habakkuk’s day refused to believe God would use Babylon for judgment, many in Paul's day refused to believe God would accomplish salvation through a crucified Messiah.

• The Contrast of Habakkuk 2:4: The ultimate answer to the crisis of faith in Habakkuk 1:5 is found in Habakkuk 2:4: "But the just shall live by his faith." The noun for faith used there is 'emunah, which is directly derived from the root 'aman. While the unbelieving nation collapses in shock, the righteous person survives and flourishes by remaining steadfastly anchored in God.
