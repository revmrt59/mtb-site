UTTERLY - ( tamah , Strong's H8539 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb tamah means to be astonished, dumbfounded, or paralyzed with amazement. In Habakkuk 1:5, the word is repeated in a rare grammatical construction—combining the Hithpael and Qal stems—to multiply its force, translated in the NKJV as "be utterly astounded." God uses this intensive phrasing to warn Habakkuk that His impending work of judgment through the Chaldeans will shatter all conventional expectations, leaving the onlookers completely paralyzed with shock and wonder.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of tamah paints a vivid picture of someone frozen in place, staring with an open mouth in absolute bewilderment. It is not the expression of a pleasant or casual surprise; rather, it represents a deep, disorienting shock that halts normal human activity and speech. Historically, the word is associated with the reaction of seeing something so monumental, terrifying, or unexpected that the mind struggles to process it. It suggests a physical and emotional paralysis where one is rendered speechless by an extraordinary event.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, tamah signifies being amazed, astounded, or dumbfounded. Lexical analysis indicates a state of profound agitation or stupefaction arising from the sight of something marvelous or catastrophic. In Habakkuk 1:5, the Holy Spirit employs a rare double-verb construction (hittammahu temahu). By pairing the Hithpael imperative (which denotes intensive, self-reflective action: "astound yourselves") with the Qal imperative ("be astounded"), the Hebrew text commands a double portion of amazement. This rhetorical device operates as a superlative, demanding that the hearers be "utterly astounded." The shock is so intense that it cannot be captured by a single verb.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, tamah is used to describe the psychological collapse of those who witness God's raw power or unexpected judgments:

• The Shock of Kings: It describes powerful monarchs who march against Jerusalem, only to look upon the city, freeze in astonishment, and flee in panic (Psalm 48:5).

• Cosmic Awe: It represents the pillars of heaven shaking and standing dumbfounded at the rebuke of the Creator (Job 26:11).

• Spiritual Stupor: It is used as a command to freeze in blind wonder when God pours out a spirit of deep sleep upon a rebellious people (Isaiah 29:9).

In each case, tamah represents the point where human intellect and control reach their absolute limit, forced to stand in silent, fearful awe of a reality far larger than themselves.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, tamah highlights the utter transcendence of God's providence. God's ways and thoughts are so far above human designs that His actions frequently offend human logic and expectations. When God tells Habakkuk to "be utterly astounded," He establishes that His sovereignty is not bound by human sentimentality or political predictability. The lesson for the believer is profound: when God works to purify His people or judge wickedness, He often utilizes instruments and methods that seem completely counterintuitive. True faith must be prepared to stand in silent, trembling awe of a God who is both deeply mysterious and perfectly righteous.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the opening verses of the book, Habakkuk cries out to God regarding the rampant injustice, violence, and lawlessness within Judah. He asks how long God will remain silent and allow His covenant people to tear themselves apart. God's response in Habakkuk 1:5 is a shocking revelation: He is not silent; He is preparing a work. However, the solution is far more terrifying than the problem. God is raising up the ruthless, violent, and pagan Babylonians (Chaldeans) to execute His judgment upon Judah. God warns Habakkuk to look among the nations and "be utterly astounded," because this divine remedy will run so contrary to human expectations that the prophet would not believe it even if he were told. The double-verb imperative acts as a pastoral mercy, bracing Habakkuk for a sovereign action that will shatter his theological comfort zones.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Job 26:11 - "The pillars of heaven tremble, And are astonished at His rebuke."

• Psalm 48:5 - "They saw it, and so they marveled; They were troubled, they hastened away."

• Ecclesiastes 5:8 - "If you see the oppression of the poor, and the violent perversion of justice and righteousness in a province, do not marvel at the matter; for high official watches over high official, and higher officials are over them."

• Isaiah 29:9 - "Pause and wonder! Blind yourselves and be blind! They are drunk, but not with wine; They stagger, but not with intoxicating drink."

• Jeremiah 4:9 - "'And it shall come to pass in that day,' says the LORD, 'That the heart of the king shall perish, And the heart of the princes; The priests shall be astonished, And the prophets shall wonder.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the New Testament, the Apostle Paul quotes the Septuagint translation of Habakkuk 1:5 during his sermon at Pisidian Antioch (Acts 13:41). The Septuagint translates the Hebrew tamah using the Greek verb aphanizo (meaning to perish, vanish, or be wiped out) alongside thaumazo (to marvel). Paul applies this warning to his Jewish audience, urging them not to reject the work of Christ. Just as the ancient Judeans stood "utterly astounded" and perished because they refused to believe God would use the Babylonians, those who reject the cross of Christ will stand in shocked condemnation, marveling at their own destruction. This link demonstrates that God's greatest works—both in judgment and in salvation—frequently astound and offend the unbelieving heart.
