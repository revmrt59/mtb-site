BITTER - ( mar , Strong's H4751 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, the word mar denotes a physical taste of bitterness, a deep emotional state of grief, or a fierce, ruthless disposition in battle. In Habakkuk 1:6, God uses this term to describe the rise of the Chaldeans (Babylonians) as a "bitter and hasty nation." Here, the word does not refer to their emotional sorrow, but to their savage, merciless, and venomous hostility. It captures the terrifying reality of a ruthless military empire that is completely devoid of sweetness, pity, or moral restraint, serving as the sovereign instrument of divine discipline against a backsliding Judah.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The literal root of mar relates to a bitter taste, such as that of wild gourds, brackish water, or poison. In the ancient Near East, the sensation of bitterness was closely associated with death, suffering, and danger. Just as bitter water is unpalatable and life-threatening to a thirsty traveler in the wilderness, a "bitter" person or nation is toxic, destructive, and harsh to encounter. The word picture transitions from a physical taste that repels and harms the body to an psychological and military disposition that repels mercy and devastates societies. When applied to warriors, it describes an unrestrained, biting ferocity—minds saturated with venomous hostility, ready to strike without compassion.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The adjective mar stems from the verbal root marar, which means to trickle, drip, or be bitter. The semantic range of mar encompasses:

• Physical bitterness (as opposed to sweetness).

• Emotional anguish, grief, or distress of soul.

• Social or existential misery.

• Fierce, harsh, savage, and cruel behavior or temperament.

In the context of Habakkuk 1:6, the word designates a savage, fierce, and cruel disposition. The Chaldeans are not mourning; they are venomous, fierce, and predatory, characterized by an aggressive military force that shows no mercy to the conquered.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, mar is deployed in various ways to paint vivid pictures of distress and intensity:

• Physical Bitterness: It is used of undrinkable water at Marah, symbolizing physical hardship and the testing of faith.

• Emotional Anguish: It characterizes the cry of Esau after losing his birthright, the weeping of those mourning an only son, and the agonizing grief of Hannah praying for a child.

• Military Ferocity: It describes "enraged" or "angry" soldiers whose spirits are fierce and ready to destroy, comparable to a mother bear robbed of her cubs. The psychological state of these warriors is sharp, venomous, and unyielding.

4. Theological Implications

The description of the Chaldeans as "bitter" highlights a profound theological tension. God explicitly states, "I am raising up the Chaldeans." God in His sovereignty summons a ruthless, cruel, and pagan nation to execute His judgment upon His covenant people. This reveals that God's sovereignty extends over the most wicked and fierce empires of the earth. He can harness the natural, wicked, and "bitter" impulses of a pagan nation to accomplish His holy purposes of discipline, even while that nation remains fully accountable for its own cruelty and pride.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:6, the term mar is paired with nimhar ("hasty" or "impetuous"). This pairing of "bitter and hasty" describes a terrifying blitzkrieg. The Chaldeans are swift in their conquest and utterly devoid of human empathy when they arrive. This answers Habakkuk's initial complaint about the violence and injustice in Judah; God responds that He is bringing a discipline that is far more violent and severe. The nuance of mar here is the absolute absence of sweetness, justice, or pity. Their warfare is destructive and venomous, sweeping across the earth to seize lands that do not belong to them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 27:34: "When Esau heard the words of his father, he cried with an exceedingly great and bitter cry, and said to his father, 'Bless me—me also, O my father!'"

• Exodus 15:23: "Now when they came to Marah, they could not drink the waters of Marah, for they were bitter. Therefore the name of it was called Marah."

• Proverbs 31:6: "Give strong drink to him who is perishing, And wine to those who are bitter of heart."

• Isaiah 5:20: "Woe to those who call evil good, and good evil; Who put darkness for light, and light for darkness; Who put bitter for sweet, and sweet for bitter!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a striking literary and spiritual irony in the book of Habakkuk. Judah had made their own lives bitter through injustice, oppression, and ignoring the law. In return, God sends a literally "bitter" nation to devour them. The internal spiritual bitterness of Judah's sin is mirrored and punished by the external, physical bitterness of the Chaldean invasion. This serves as a warning that turning away from the sweetness of God's covenant law inevitably exposes a people to the bitter consequences of judgment.
