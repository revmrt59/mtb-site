BREADTH - ( merchab , Strong's H4800 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word merchab refers to a broad, wide, or spacious place. Derived from a root meaning to grow wide or expand, it denotes a vast physical expanse or, metaphorically, a state of freedom, relief, and deliverance from confinement. In Habakkuk 1:6, it highlights the terrifying, unchecked geographical reach of the invading Chaldean army as they sweep across the vast expanses of the known world under God's sovereign decree.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• merchab is a noun formed from the verbal root rachab, which means "to enlarge," "to make wide," or "to open broad."

• In the ancient Near East, confinement, narrow paths, and tight spaces symbolized distress, siege, adversity, and helplessness. Conversely, wide-open spaces, broad plains, and spacious places symbolized freedom, safety, victory, and dominion.

• When used of an invading army, it evokes the image of an unstoppable flood or a sweeping wind that covers every square inch of the landscape, leaving no place to hide or seek refuge.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• A broad, open space or wide expanse.

• A state or place of liberty, freedom, or safety, specifically in contrast to distress, restriction, and confinement.

• The vast extent, breadth, or full latitude of a territory or the entire earth.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical expanses: It is used to describe the immense stretches of land over which nations march, or where flocks graze without restriction.

• Metaphor for deliverance: In the poetic literature of the Old Testament, being brought out into a "broad place" represents God rescuing His servant from the tight, suffocating grip of enemies or circumstances.

• The theater of conquest: In the prophetic context, it represents the sweeping, global scale of geopolitical upheaval, where borders are dissolved by conquering forces.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereignty Over Geography: God’s plans are not localized. When He raises up a nation for judgment, their reach extends across the "breadth of the earth." The expansive geography of human history is fully under divine administration.

• The Scope of Judgment: The use of this word underscores that judgment is comprehensive. The Chaldeans will not merely raid a border town; they will march across the vast, open territories of the earth, demonstrating that no human boundary can halt the purposes of God.

• The Contrast of Spaciousness: While God's people find their "broad place" of safety in Him, the wicked attempt to seize the "broad places" of the earth for themselves through violence and greed. Ultimately, human expansion without God ends in ruin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Habakkuk 1:6, the Chaldeans (Babylonians) are described as a "bitter and hasty nation."

• The phrase "marches through the breadth of the earth" emphasizes their rapid, unhindered expansion. They do not navigate narrow, difficult paths with hesitation; they sweep majestically and terrifyingly across the broad plains of the world.

• The context is one of divine shock. Habakkuk has complained about injustice in Judah, and God answers by revealing an unbelievable instrument of judgment: a fierce empire that will conquer the entire known world (the "breadth of the earth") to claim dwelling places that do not belong to them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• "He also brought me out into a broad place; He delivered me because He delighted in me." (2 Samuel 22:20)

• "I called on the LORD in distress; The LORD answered me and set me in a broad place." (Psalm 118:5)

• "For Israel is stubborn Like a stubborn calf; Now the LORD will let them feed Like a lamb in a wide place." (Hosea 4:16)

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The root rachab is also found in the place name Rehoboth in Genesis 26:22. Isaac named the well Rehoboth (meaning "spaciousness" or "room"), saying, "For now the LORD has made room for us, and we shall be fruitful in the land."

• There is a powerful spiritual contrast between the claustrophobia of fear and the spaciousness of faith. The Chaldeans marched to conquer the physical "breadth" of the land, yet their souls were puffed up and narrow. Conversely, the righteous person, though physically besieged, walks in the spiritual "broad place" of God's sovereignty.
