TERRIBLE - ( ayom , Strong's H366 )

Summary:

The Hebrew adjective ayom denotes something that inspires deep fear, dread, terror, or overwhelming awe. Occurring only three times in the Old Testament, it describes an intensity of presence that leaves onlookers completely stunned or intimidated. While in the Song of Solomon it refers to the stunning, breathtaking majesty of the beloved—likened to an army with banners—in Habakkuk 1:7 it describes the terrifying, ruthless power of the invading Chaldean (Babylonian) army. This word highlights a force so formidable that it recognizes no external authority, operating entirely on its own cruel terms to execute judgment and strike paralyzing fear into the hearts of its enemies.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of ayom is linked to the noun eymah (Strong's H367), which means terror, dread, or horror. The word picture is one of overwhelming force or stunning presence that causes an immediate physical and emotional reaction of dread or astonishment. It is not merely "bad" or "unpleasant" in the modern sense of "terrible," but rather something so formidable, majestic, or frightening that it paralyzes the observer. It evokes the feeling of standing before a colossal, unstoppable power, whether that power is a dazzling, armored army marching in perfect formation or a ruthless military machine sweeping across the ancient Near East.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The adjective ayom means dread, terrible, formidable, or awesome. It describes an object or person that inflicts fear or commands an overwhelming sense of awe.

• The semantic range spans from militaristic terror to majestic, breathtaking beauty that commands absolute respect and submission.

• In a military context, it refers to an invading force that is ruthless, dread-inducing, and completely intimidating.

• In poetic literature, it characterizes an imposing, stunning beauty that possesses a powerful, almost conquering influence over the lover.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• ayom is used to capture the psychological impact of an entity upon those who behold it. It represents an external force that instantly penetrates the inner disposition of the witness, replacing security with fear or breathtaking astonishment.

• The word acts as a sensory descriptor of raw, unchecked power. In the ancient world, a "terrible" army was one that did not adhere to the standard rules of warfare or diplomacy, but instead crushed nations through sheer terror, psychological warfare, and overwhelming brutality.

4. Theological Implications

• The use of ayom in the context of Habakkuk reveals how God uses even the most wicked and terrifying human forces to accomplish His divine purposes. The Chaldeans are described as "terrible," yet they are raised up by God Himself (Habakkuk 1:6) as an instrument of discipline for Judah.

• It demonstrates the sovereign control of Yahweh over the nations. Even though the Chaldeans believe their own strength is their god (Habakkuk 1:11) and that they are completely autonomous ("their judgment and their dignity proceed from themselves"), they are ultimately bound to the sovereign decree of the Lord.

• The word highlights the terrifying reality of divine judgment when God withdraws His protection from a disobedient nation and allows the natural consequences of historical forces to take their course.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Habakkuk 1:7, ayom is paired with nora (dreadful/awesome, from the root yare, to fear). This parallel construction emphasizes the total psychological domination the Chaldeans exert over their victims.

• The context of Habakkuk 1 is the prophet’s complaint about the violence and injustice in Judah, followed by God’s shocking reply that He is raising up the Chaldeans—a "bitter and hasty nation" (Hab 1:6).

• Calling them ayom ("terrible") underscores their lawless nature. The text states that "their judgment and their dignity proceed from themselves." They do not answer to any international law, treaty, or god other than their own military might. Their terror is self-derived and self-justified, making them unpredictable and exceptionally dangerous.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because ayom occurs only three times in the Hebrew Old Testament, these are the only other occurrences, showing the contrast between military terror and poetic majesty:

• Song of Solomon 6:4: "O my love, you are as beautiful as Tirzah, Lovely as Jerusalem, Awesome as an army with banners!" (The word is translated "Awesome," describing the stunning, captivating, and intimidating power of the beloved's beauty.)

• Song of Solomon 6:10: "Who is she that looks forth as the morning, Fair as the moon, Clear as the sun, Awesome as an army with banners?" (Again translated "Awesome," expressing the overwhelming and formidable majesty of the bride.)

• Habakkuk 1:7: "They are terrible and dreadful; Their judgment and their dignity proceed from themselves." (Translated "terrible," describing the frightening and ruthless nature of the Babylonian empire.)

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The transition of meaning from the dazzling majesty of the bride in Song of Solomon to the horrific brutality of the Babylonian army in Habakkuk illustrates the richness of Hebrew word pictures. In both cases, the common thread is an irresistible, captivating power that completely dominates the scene, leaving the observer unable to look away or resist.

• The Greek Septuagint (LXX) translates ayom in Habakkuk 1:7 using the adjective phoberos, which means "fearful," "terrible," or "inspiring dread." This same Greek term is used in the New Testament to describe the terrifying prospect of falling into the hands of the living God (Hebrews 10:31).
