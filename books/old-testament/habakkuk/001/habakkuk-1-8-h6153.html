EVENING - ( 'ereb , Strong's H6153 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ereb refers to sunset, twilight, or nightfall, representing the transition from day to night. In Habakkuk 1:8, the phrase "evening wolves" vividly depicts the Chaldean invaders as stealthy, ferocious, and ravenous predators that hunt at dusk with desperate, daytime-starved hunger, symbolizing the swift and devastating nature of God's impending judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of 'ereb is closely associated with the ideas of mixing, growing dark, or the blending of light and shadow at sunset. In the ancient Near East, evening marked the end of the daily labor cycle and the return of workers to the safety of their homes. Conversely, it was also the time when security decreased and wild animals emerged from their dens to hunt. The "evening wolf" is a specific proverbial image of a predator that has hidden all day without food, emerging at twilight with a fierce, desperate hunger that makes it exceptionally dangerous and aggressive.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'ereb means sunset, evening, or nightfall. It designates the latter part of the day as the sun goes down and darkness begins to fall. In Hebrew thought, this transition is highly significant because the day is reckoned as starting at evening. Additionally, the term defines specific ritual times, such as the evening sacrifice, and serves as a boundary marker in Levitical law, where ceremonial uncleanness typically lasted "until evening."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Temporal Boundaries: 'ereb is used throughout the Old Testament to mark the transition of time, most famously in the creation narrative where evening and morning constitute a complete day.

  • Spiritual and Ritual Transition: The boundary of evening represents the end of a state of defilement. Entering evening marked the opportunity for cleansing and restoration to the community.

  • Danger and Judgment: Twilight is the season of the predator. Scriptures use the evening as a backdrop for danger, ambush, and spiritual blindness. The prowling of evening wolves represents rulers and nations who abuse their power under the cover of darkness, driven by insatiable greed.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'ereb highlights the dual nature of darkness in the biblical narrative. While God created the evening and rules over it, darkness is also the sphere where wickedness operates. When God warns Judah that their enemies are like "evening wolves," He is demonstrating that spiritual rebellion removes His protective light, leaving a people vulnerable to the terrifying forces of the night. The Babylonian invasion is a physical manifestation of the spiritual darkness the nation chose.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:8, the prophet describes the military might of the Chaldeans, noting that their horses are "more fierce than evening wolves." A wolf hunting at nightfall is motivated by an intense hunger that has built up all day, making it ruthless and efficient. By comparing the Chaldean cavalry to evening wolves, the text emphasizes that the invaders will not be casual or hesitant. They will strike with sudden, desperate, and absolute ferocity, leaving nothing behind.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:5 - "God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night. So the evening and the morning were the first day."

  • Joshua 10:26 - "And afterward Joshua struck them and killed them, and hanged them on five trees; and they were hanging on the trees until evening."

  • Zephaniah 3:3 - "Her princes in her midst are roaring lions; Her judges are evening wolves; They leave not a bone till morning."

  • Psalm 104:23 - "Man goes out to his work And to his labor until the evening."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Because the Hebrew calendar counts the day from evening to evening, the onset of darkness is not merely the end of life, but the preparation for a new beginning. In the context of prophecy, the "evening" of judgment always precedes the "morning" of redemption. Even as Habakkuk contemplates the terrifying twilight of the Babylonian captivity, the structure of biblical time offers a subtle hope: the dark night of judgment will eventually give way to a new day of God's faithfulness.
