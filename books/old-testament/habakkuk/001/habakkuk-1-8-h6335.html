CHARGE - ( push , Strong's H6335 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb push primarily means to spring, bound, leap, or spread out, capturing the energetic, unhindered movement of animals, such as horses galloping or calves skipping from a stall. In the prophetic text of Habakkuk 1:8, it depicts the fierce, expansive, and unstoppable advance of the Babylonian cavalry as they sweep across the Promised Land. This word highlights both the terrifying speed of divine judgment and the wild, unchecked arrogance of the instrument God uses to execute it, reminding believers of the ultimate sovereignty of God even over the chaotic movements of history.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The background of push is agrarian, rooted in the energetic and unrestrained movement of livestock or beasts of burden breaking free into open spaces. The word picture is that of young calves that have been kept in a dark, cramped winter stall. When they are finally released into the wide, sunlit pasture, they do not merely walk; they bound, leap, and frisk with explosive, joyful energy.

When applied to warhorses, the image shifts from joyful play to terrifying military power. It represents warhorses leaping forward, bursting with kinetic energy. This is not a disciplined, orderly march, but a wild, overwhelming, and rapid dispersion of forces. The horses charge and spread out over the landscape like a flood, leaving no corner untouched.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew word push is a primitive root verb. Its core semantic range includes "to leap," "to spring up," "to gallop," "to spread oneself," or "to be scattered." In poetic and prophetic contexts, it communicates rapid, unrestrained expansion or bounding motion.

While older translations occasionally associated this root with "growing fat" or "becoming heavy" (due to similarities with cognate languages), the primary meaning in contexts of physical movement is the vigorous, bounding action of animals or horsemen. In Habakkuk 1:8, it specifically denotes the aggressive galloping and spreading out of cavalry.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb occurs only a few times in the Hebrew Bible, each occurrence carrying a highly vivid physical word picture:

  • The Galloping Warhorse (Habakkuk 1:8): The Babylonian horsemen do not merely travel; they bound aggressively, charging forward and spreading out to cover the terrain with terrifying speed.

  • The Skipping Calf (Malachi 4:2): The righteous who fear the Lord are compared to calves let out of the stall, leaping and skipping (push) with joy and restored vitality.

  • The Threshing Heifer (Jeremiah 50:11): The Babylonians are described as frisking or leaping (push) like a heifer at grass, showcasing their arrogant, unbridled self-indulgence.

4. Theological Implications

  • Sovereignty in Judgment: God raises up the Babylonians as an instrument of discipline, yet their aggressive, bounding advance (push) displays their own savage autonomy and pride. God sovereignly uses even the wild, unrestrained passions of wicked nations to accomplish His holy purposes.

  • The Swiftness of Consequences: Sin brings rapid, overwhelming consequences. The description of the Chaldean horsemen charging highlights that when judgment comes, it is swift, expansive, and impossible to evade by human strength.

  • Contrast of Movement: There is a powerful biblical contrast in how this word is used. While the wicked charge forward to destroy (push in Habakkuk), the redeemed will eventually leap with joy (push in Malachi). The same energetic movement used for terror in judgment becomes the movement of ecstatic joy in salvation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1, the prophet cries out against the violence and injustice in Judah. God answers with an astonishing revelation: He is raising up the Chaldeans (Babylonians), a "bitter and hasty nation."

Verse 8 uses vivid animal imagery to depict this invading force: their horses are swifter than leopards, more fierce than evening wolves, and their cavalry "charge ahead" (push). The contextual nuance of push here emphasizes the vast, swarming nature of the invasion. They do not ride in a single, easily avoidable line; they fan out, galloping wildly over the hills, leaving no place of safety. The speed and dispersion make escape impossible, emphasizing the absolute dread that gripped Judah as they faced this divinely ordained chastisement.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Habakkuk 1:8 - "Their horses also are swifter than leopards, And more fierce than evening wolves. Their cavalry charge ahead; Their cavalry comes from afar; They fly as the eagle that hastens to eat."

  • Malachi 4:2 - "But to you who fear My name The Sun of Righteousness shall arise With healing in His wings; And you shall go out and grow fat like stall-fed calves."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) translates u-fashu in Habakkuk 1:8 using the Greek verb exippasontai, which means "to ride out" or "to charge on horseback." This ancient translation confirms that the early Jewish translators understood the Hebrew word to mean the swift, charging advance of horsemen.

The comparison to leopards, wolves, and eagles in the surrounding verses of Habakkuk 1:8 reinforces the predatory nature of the action. The verb push is the mechanical action of this predatory assault—the rapid bounding and fan-like spreading of the pack as they close in on their prey.
