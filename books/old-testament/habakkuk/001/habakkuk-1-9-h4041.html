SET - ( megammah , Strong's 4041 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun megammah is a rare term occurring only once in the Old Testament, representing a hapax legomenon in Habakkuk 1:9. Derived from a root meaning to gather, accumulate, or eagerly absorb, it conveys the ideas of intense direction, striving, or concentrated focus. In the context of the invading Babylonian army, it describes the fierce, unwavering, and destructive determination of the conquerors whose faces are set forward like a scorching desert wind, bent on absolute devastation and conquest.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The term megammah is etymologically linked to the concept of swallowing, absorbing, or gathering together. It shares a conceptual relationship with roots describing a horse eagerly swallowing the ground in its speed or drinking up moisture. The word picture portrays a massive, consuming force that sweeps over a region, absorbing everything in its path. Like a hot desert wind that dries up all moisture and life, the Chaldean army moves with an undivided, terrifying focus. Their faces are set with an unstoppable, consuming eagerness to devour nations, leaving no room for distraction, hesitation, or mercy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun megammah represents a striving, eager desire, direction, gathering, or accumulation. In its construct form (megammat), the term signifies "the striving of" or "the direction of." As a single-occurrence word, its precise nuance has been interpreted through several primary semantic avenues:

  • An association with the root meaning "to swallow up" or "consume," yielding translations such as "supping up" or "devouring."

  • The concept of "striving" or "eagerness," describing the intense, concentrated focus of the mind and body toward a single objective.

  • The physical orientation of "set direction" or "forward movement," indicating that the eyes and faces of the invaders are locked onto their target.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word is used exclusively in Habakkuk 1:9, where it is paired with "faces" (panim) and "east wind" (qadim).

  • The imagery of the "east wind" (the sirocco) is central to this word picture. In the ancient Near East, the east wind was a scorching, dry, and destructive blast blowing from the Arabian desert, ruining crops and drying up water sources.

  • To describe their faces as megammah like the east wind means their unified direction and intense eagerness act as a devastating desert blast. They do not look back or turn aside; their collective focus is a singular, burning drive toward conquest.

4. Theological Implications

  • Sovereignty and Judgment: God is the one raising up the Chaldeans as an instrument of discipline against Judah. The terrible, focused, and consuming nature of their march (megammah) underscores the severity and inevitability of divine judgment when a nation repeatedly rejects God's covenant.

  • The Brutality of Human Ambition: Left to their own desires, human empires display a ravenous, unchecked ambition. The Babylonian drive to "sup up" nations shows the terrifying extent of human pride and cruelty when used as a rod of chastisement.

  • The Contrast of the Focused Face: The unwavering direction of the wicked toward destruction stands in stark contrast to the godly focus of the pilgrim whose face is set toward Jerusalem, or the Messiah who set His face like flint to accomplish redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:9, the prophet receives a vision of the rising Babylonian power. God describes them as a bitter and hasty nation. The relentless onslaught of the Chaldeans is detailed with the words, "They all come for violence." The phrase megammat penehem qadimah (translated in the NKJV as "Their faces are set like the east wind") emphasizes that they do not look to the right or to the left. They have one singular, devastating purpose. Their faces do not show mercy; they are rigidly oriented forward, eager for conquest, just as the scorching east wind sweeps inexorably westward toward Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because megammah is a hapax legomenon, there are no other verses in the Hebrew Bible containing this exact word. However, the conceptual elements of the "east wind" and "setting one's face" for a specific purpose appear elsewhere:

  • Genesis 41:6 - "Then behold, seven thin heads of grain, blighted by the east wind, sprang up after them."

  • Jonah 4:8 - "And it happened, when the sun arose, that God prepared a vehement east wind; and the sun beat on Jonah’s head so that he grew faint."

  • Ezekiel 21:2 - "Son of man, set your face toward Jerusalem, preach against the holy places, and prophesy against the land of Israel;"

  • Luke 9:51 - "Now it came to pass, when the time had come for Him to be received up, that He steadfastly set His face to go to Jerusalem;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Translational variations across history highlight the depth of this rare word:

  • The Septuagint translates the phrase as "resisting with their faces opposite," capturing the hostile, confrontational stance of the invaders.

  • The Vulgate uses facies eorum ventus urens ("their face is a burning wind"), focusing on the scorching, destructive aspect of the east wind comparison.

  • The variety of translations—ranging from "supping up" to "eagerness" to "set direction"—does not weaken the text but enriches the understanding of the multi-layered threat: the Chaldeans are physically gathered (accumulation), mentally focused (striving/eagerness), and physically aligned (set direction) for destructive conquest.
