FACES - ( panim , Strong's H6440 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word panim literally means "faces" or "face," representing the front of a person, their presence, or their overall disposition. In Habakkuk 1:9, it depicts the terrifying, singular resolve of the invading Chaldean army, whose collective "faces" are set forward like a scorching desert wind. This word underscores the relentless, unyielding nature of the instrument of judgment that God is bringing upon His rebellious people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The face is the most expressive part of the human body, serving as the primary means of communication, recognition, and emotional revelation. In Hebrew thought, the word for face (panim) is always plural in form. This plural construction represents a "plural of composition" or "extension," reflecting the multifaceted nature of human expression and character. The face is the outer mirror of the inner soul; it communicates favor, anger, sorrow, or determination. When an individual or a collective group "sets" their face in a particular direction, it indicates an absolute, unbending focus of the will and a total commitment to a specific course of action.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun refers to the face of a human being, an animal, or even the surface or front of an object. It is frequently translated as "presence" because to be before someone's face is to be in their direct presence. The lexical range includes:

  • The physical face or countenance of a person.

  • The presence or front of someone or something (often prepended as liphne, meaning "before" or "in the presence of").

  • The surface of the earth, the water, or the sky.

  • The disposition, favor, or anger of a person or God, as indicated by their facial expression.

  • In idiomatic expressions, "setting the face" means directing one's attention, purpose, or hostility toward a specific target.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Intimacy and Presence: To see someone "face to face" (panim el-panim) denotes direct, intimate, and unhindered relationship, as seen in God's communication with Moses.

  • Favor and Blessing: A "shining" face represents favor, acceptance, and the bestowal of life, while "seeking the face" of a king or of God means seeking their goodwill and audience.

  • Anger and Rejection: Conversely, a "fallen" face indicates anger or displeasure, and "hiding the face" denotes the withdrawal of favor, presence, or protection.

  • Resolute Determination: To "set one's face" is a vivid word picture of stubborn, unyielding resolve. Depending on the context, this can be a holy determination (such as the Messiah setting His face like flint toward Jerusalem) or a wicked, destructive persistence in evil.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, panim lies at the very heart of the Old Testament experience of God. To be cast away from God's "face" is the ultimate tragedy of human existence, representing spiritual exile and death. The Aaronic Blessing centers on the face of Yahweh shining upon His people, which is the source of all peace (shalom). In prophetic literature, when God sets His face against a people, judgment is inevitable. The use of this word in judgment contexts reminds the reader that God's holy character is actively opposed to sin, and He can sovereignly direct the "faces" of foreign nations to execute His divine decrees.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1:9, the prophet receives a terrifying vision of the coming Chaldean (Babylonian) invasion. The text states: "Their faces are set like the east wind." The Hebrew phrase megammat penehem qadimah carries the idea of the collective direction or eagerness of their faces. The Chaldeans are not a disorganized mob; they are a highly disciplined, terrifyingly focused military machine. Their "faces"—their entire collective presence, intent, and energy—are oriented forward like the destructive qadim (the scorching, dry east wind of the Arabian desert). This wind instantly withers vegetation and chokes out life. By describing their faces this way, the passage emphasizes that the Babylonians will show no mercy, feel no hesitation, and cannot be turned aside by human defense. Their relentless focus is a physical manifestation of their cruel heart, serving as the sovereignly appointed broom of God's judgment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 32:30: "So Jacob called the name of the place Peniel: 'For I have seen God face to face, and my life is preserved.'"

  • Numbers 6:25: "The LORD make His face shine upon you, And be gracious to you;"

  • Jeremiah 21:10: "'For I have set My face against this city for adversity and not for good,' says the LORD. 'It shall be given into the hand of the king of Babylon, and he shall burn it with fire.'"

  • Ezekiel 3:8: "Behold, I have made your face strong against their faces, and your forehead strong against their foreheads."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Because the face is the index of the soul, the fixed gaze of the Chaldean army reveals a complete lack of fear and a total absence of compassion. There is no searching of the eyes, no negotiation, and no soft look of pity on their countenances. This aligns with the broader description of the Babylonians in Habakkuk as "bitter and hasty" (Habakkuk 1:6). Their facial expression alone strikes terror into the hearts of their enemies because it communicates a fixed, sovereignly permitted destiny of conquest.
