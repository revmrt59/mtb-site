ANSWER - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub is a foundational Old Testament word meaning "to turn," "to return," or "to turn back." When used in the causative Hiphil stem, as it is in Habakkuk 2:1, it literally means "to cause to return" or "to bring back." In the context of communication, this expression refers to "returning a word" or "giving an answer." In Habakkuk 2:1, the prophet stands upon his watchtower, waiting with intense anticipation to see what God will speak to him and what "answer" (literally, what "return" or "reply") he will make regarding his complaint or correction. It highlights a posture of humble accountability, where the prophet recognizes his duty to receive God's word and return a faithful, submissive response.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • shub is the twelfth most frequent verb in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 1,000 times across the Old Testament. Its primary, concrete, physical meaning is to turn back, retrace one's steps, or return to a point of origin.

  • It evokes the spatial movement of starting on a path, realizing a change of direction is needed, and turning 180 degrees to head back to where one belongs.

  • In the ancient Near Eastern context of communication, a reply was viewed as a physical object—a word sent out by a messenger that required another word to be "returned" or "brought back" to the sender to complete the loop of communication.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • In its basic Qal stem, shub means to turn back, return, go back, or retreat.

  • In the Hiphil (causative) stem used in Habakkuk 2:1 (ashib), the verb means to cause to return, bring back, restore, or pay back.

  • When used in a conversational or judicial context, the Hiphil of shub carries the idiomatic meaning of "to answer," "to reply," or "to bring back word." It denotes the act of returning an answer to an inquirer, an accuser, or a superior.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Physical Movement: Used of Jacob returning to the land of Canaan (Genesis 31:3), or Moses returning to Egypt (Exodus 4:18).

  • Spiritual Repentance: Often used for turning back to Yahweh with all one's heart (Deuteronomy 30:2) or turning away from wicked ways (Ezekiel 18:30).

  • Causative Restoration: Used of God bringing back the captives of Israel (Psalm 14:7) or restoring a person's life and soul (Psalm 23:3).

  • Interactive Response: Used when a subject returns a report to a king or when a servant brings back an answer to a master (Numbers 22:8).

4. Theological Implications

  • The Dialogical Nature of Faith: God is not a silent, distant force; He is a personal Creator who speaks to His people and expects a "return" (an answer). True biblical faith involves a reciprocal relationship where the human heart actively responds to the divine voice.

  • Divine Accountability: In Habakkuk, the prophet's "answer" is directly tied to his "correction" or "reproof." Humans are fully accountable to God's sovereign self-revelation. When God speaks or corrects, the only proper "return" is one of humble submission, confession, and alignment with His truth.

  • The Posture of Repentance: Because shub is the primary Old Testament term for repentance, its usage as "answer" in Habakkuk 2:1 subtly highlights the spiritual posture required of a believer. To answer God when corrected is to turn one's mind and heart back into alignment with Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 1, the prophet complained to God about the rampant injustice in Judah, and then expressed bewilderment when God announced He would use the ruthless Babylonians (Chaldeans) as an instrument of judgment.

  • In Habakkuk 2:1, the prophet takes the posture of a sentinel on a watchtower. He climbs the ramparts to wait in silent, expectant faith for God's reply.

  • The phrase "what I will answer when I am corrected" (literally, "what I will return concerning my reproof") reveals Habakkuk's deep self-awareness. He expects that God's reply will correct his short-sighted perspective. He knows his bold questioning of God's justice requires a divine response, and he prepares his own "return"—his submissive, faithful reply—the moment God corrects him.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 22:8: "And he said to them, 'Lodge here tonight, and I will bring back word to you, as the LORD speaks to me.'"

  • 2 Samuel 24:13: "...Now consider and see what answer I should take back to Him who sent me."

  • Job 20:2: "Therefore my anxious thoughts make me answer, And because of the turbulence within me."

  • Proverbs 22:21: "That I may make you know the certainty of the words of truth, That you may answer words of truth to those who send to you?"

  • Proverbs 27:11: "My son, be wise, and make my heart glad, That I may answer him who reproaches me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The ancient Greek translation (the Septuagint) translates the Hebrew ashib in Habakkuk 2:1 using the verb apokritho (from apokrinomai), which means "to answer" or "to reply." This reinforces the interpretive consistency of this passage as a dialogue of personal accountability.

  • The Hiphil form of shub is frequently used in ancient legal settings where a defendant must give an account or make restitution. When Habakkuk stands on the watchtower, he is preparing himself for a divine "courtroom" setting where he expects to be corrected, readying his heart to yield rather than to argue.
