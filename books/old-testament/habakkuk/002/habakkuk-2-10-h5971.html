PEOPLES - ( 'am , Strong's H5971 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'am refers to a group of people bound together by common blood, language, covenant, or political association. While often used of Israel as God's chosen people, in the plural it refers to the Gentile nations and ethnic groups of the world. In Habakkuk 2:10, it describes the numerous nations and populations that the conquering Babylonian empire ruthlessly cut off and destroyed, demonstrating how the pursuit of self-exaltation at the expense of others ultimately leads to self-ruin.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word 'am is derived from a root suggesting association, companionship, or being together. It is distinct from goy, which often emphasizes a political or national body. 'am carries a stronger connotation of kinship, family ties, and relational connection. It is the language of a tribe or a collective family. When used in the plural ('ammim), it encompasses the vast, diverse groupings of humanity throughout the earth, each with its own shared identity, heritage, and kinship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The noun 'am refers to a people, nation, or member of a people group.

  • In the singular, it most frequently denotes a specific, defined group, most notably Israel as the covenant people of God.

  • In the plural ('ammim), it refers to the various peoples, tribes, or Gentile nations of the world.

  • The term implies a shared relationship or commonality, whether through ancestry, geography, or political subjugation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses 'am over 1,800 times. It is used to contrast God's set-apart people (Israel) with the surrounding "peoples" of the earth. It can depict a military force or a collective assembly of citizens. The relational nature of the word is highlighted when God says, "I will be their God, and they shall be My people."

4. Theological Implications

  • God is the creator and sovereign ruler over all 'ammim (peoples). They are not merely political entities but human communities made in His image, for whom He has ultimate purposes of redemption and judgment.

  • The destruction of "many peoples" is an assault on God's creation. When human empires treat entire populations as disposable resources to be "cut off" for personal glory, they violate the sacredness of human life and invite divine justice.

  • History is not merely a clash of empires, but a theater where God holds nations accountable for how they treat other peoples.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 2:10, the prophet is pronouncing a series of "woes" against the Chaldeans (Babylonians).

  • The Babylonians sought to build their house (dynasty and empire) on the blood and ruins of others. "Cutting off many peoples" refers to their systematic campaign of violent conquest, deportation, and eradication of sovereign ethnic groups.

  • The bitter irony of the passage is that by seeking to secure their own house by destroying "many peoples," the Babylonians actually "shame" their own house and "sin" against their own souls. The violence they sowed against other peoples became the harvest of their own self-destruction.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 11:6 – "And the LORD said, 'Indeed the people are one and they all have one language...'"

  • Exodus 19:5 – "Now therefore, if you will indeed obey My voice and keep My covenant, then you shall be a special treasure to Me above all people; for all the earth is Mine."

  • Deuteronomy 7:6 – "For you are a holy people to the LORD your God; the LORD your God has chosen you to be a people for His treasured possession, above all peoples who are on the face of the earth."

  • Psalm 67:3 – "Let the peoples praise You, O God; Let all the peoples praise You."

  • Isaiah 2:4 – "He shall judge between the nations, And rebuke many people; They shall beat their swords into plowshares, And their spears into pruning hooks; Nation shall not lift up sword against nation, Neither shall they learn war anymore."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) translates 'am in Habakkuk 2:10 using the Greek word ethne (nations/peoples). This underscores that the Babylonian conquests were not merely local skirmishes, but the systematic dismantling of entire ethnic heritages and cultures. This text reminds the modern reader that human systems which prioritize institutional preservation or national expansion at the expense of human beings are fundamentally broken and under the judgment of God.
