BEAM - ( kapis , Strong's H3714 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word kapis refers to a wooden crossbeam, girder, or rafter used in building construction to connect walls or support ceilings. Occurring only once in the Old Testament, it functions within a powerful legal metaphor in Habakkuk 2:11. In this context, the physical materials of a fortress built through extortion, violence, and forced labor—specifically the stone in the wall and the wooden beam in the ceiling—are personified as eyewitnesses that cry out to expose the injustice of the builder.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • In ancient Near Eastern architecture, constructing grand houses, palaces, or fortresses required heavy stone foundations and walls reinforced by substantial wooden beams (kapis) spanning the top to support the roof or upper floors.

  • These beams were typically made of high-quality imported timber, such as cedar or cypress. For conquering empires like the Babylonians, such materials were highly valuable luxury items often acquired through conquest, plunder, and the forced labor of subjugated nations.

  • Etymologically, the word relates to the idea of binding, connecting, or holding fast, much like the palm of a hand (kaph). It represents the connective timber that ties the structural frame of a building together, making it a critical element that ensures the stability and unity of the entire house.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The noun kapis means a crossbeam, girder, rafter, or piece of timber used in joinery.

  • It denotes a structural wooden connector or joint-piece in a building's woodwork.

  • In the Greek Septuagint (LXX), the word is translated as kantharos, which can refer to a rafter, a connection piece, or a beetle (drawing a visual comparison to the shape of the joint or a insect chewing through wood).

  • Because it is a hapax legomenon (a word that occurs only once in the Hebrew Bible), its meaning is confirmed by its usage in rabbinic Hebrew and cognate Semitic languages, where it consistently refers to a wooden rafter or connecting beam.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The single biblical usage of kapis creates a vivid, personified courtroom scene where the very materials of a tyrant’s house testify against him.

  • The stone cries out from the wall, and the wooden beam (kapis) "answers" or echoes the charge.

  • This literary device of personification transforms inanimate objects into active, vocal witnesses. The wood and stone, though silent by nature, cannot contain the weight of the blood and oppression used to harvest, transport, and assemble them. They are depicted as shouting out the crimes committed in their gathering and construction.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Exposure of Sin: No sin is hidden from God. Even if a tyrant successfully silences human victims, the physical creation itself—even the timber in his private chambers—stands ready to testify against him.

  • Inherent Consequences of Injustice: Wealth and security built on the exploitation of others carry the seeds of their own destruction. The very structures built to provide safety and display glory become the primary witnesses of condemnation.

  • Creation's Voice: Creation is sensitive to human moral action. Just as the ground cried out with Abel's blood, the structural elements of a house cry out when stained by violence and greed.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 2:9–11, the prophet pronounces a "woe" against the Chaldean (Babylonian) Empire, which built its empire through ruthless conquest and plunder ("covets evil covetousness for his house").

  • The Babylonians sought to build an "impregnable nest" on high to escape the reach of disaster and secure their legacy.

  • However, the luxury and security they built were fundamentally corrupt. In verse 11, the kapis (beam) and the stone represent the inner security of their fortress. The very walls they trusted for protection are the ones that testify of their guilt. The connection between the stone wall and the wooden beam shows that every part of their grand enterprise—from foundation to roof—is unified in its testimony against them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Because kapis is a hapax legomenon, there are no other biblical verses containing this exact Hebrew word. Its sole occurrence is:

  • Habakkuk 2:11: "For the stone will cry out from the wall, / And the beam from the timbers will answer it."

  • To understand its theological equivalents, related scriptures employ similar imagery of inanimate objects testifying or being affected by sin:

  • Genesis 4:10: "And He said, 'What have you done? The voice of your brother’s blood cries out to Me from the ground.'"

  • Joshua 24:27: "And Joshua said to all the people, 'Behold, this stone shall be a witness to us, for it has heard all the words of the LORD which He spoke to us...'"

  • Luke 19:40: "But He answered and said to them, 'I tell you that if these should keep silent, the stones would immediately cry out.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The dynamic between the "stone" (Hebrew: eben) and the "beam" (kapis) highlights the two main building materials of ancient monumental architecture. Stone was used for walls, and wood was used for horizontal spans, ceilings, and roofs. Their joint testimony represents a complete, structural indictment.

  • Ancient Near Eastern builders often carved inscriptions into foundation stones or beams boasting of their conquests. The irony of Habakkuk's prophecy is that instead of praising the king, the stones and beams shout of his crimes.
