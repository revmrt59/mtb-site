VERY / FOR - ( day , Strong's H1767 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word day denotes sufficiency, abundance, or the exact measure needed to satisfy a demand. In the context of Habakkuk 2:13, it occurs in a construct idiom paired with the preposition be to convey that the exhaustive, prideful labor of godless nations is only "enough" to feed the consuming fire of judgment and result in absolute emptiness. Rather than building lasting legacies, their frantic human efforts serve only to satisfy the demands of destruction and futility.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of day lies in the ancient Near Eastern concept of measurement, portioning, and satisfying a specific need. In daily agrarian life, it referred to having a sufficient harvest, an adequate amount of food, or enough resources to sustain a household. It represents a boundary line—the point at which a vessel is full or a need is completely met. When prefixed with the preposition be (yielding bedey), the word shifts from a simple description of abundance to an idiomatic measurement of action. It describes labor or resources that are perfectly proportioned to achieve, satisfy, or fill up a specific destination. In a judgment context, the word picture becomes deeply ironic: the massive, sweating, straining efforts of empire-builders are measured out as being just enough to feed the ravenous appetite of a fire.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Day (noun): Sufficiency, enough, plenty, ability.

  • Bedey (prepositional/conjunctional construct): In the measure of, as often as, sufficient for, only to satisfy.

  • In Habakkuk 2:13, the double use of this word (bedey-es and bedey-riq) functions as an idiom of destiny and proportion. The labor of the peoples is bedey-es—meaning its ultimate measure and sufficiency is only to serve as fuel for the fire. Their weariness is bedey-riq—meaning all their exhaustive struggle is measured out to produce nothing but emptiness. The New King James Version translates these idiomatic expressions as "in the very [fire]" and "for [nothing]."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Measure of Need: The word frequently quantifies what is necessary to sustain life or fulfill an obligation. It is the standard of charity, ensuring a poor brother receives "sufficient for his need."

  • The Measure of Abundance: It describes a boundary-breaking supply. When God opens the windows of heaven, He pours out a blessing so great there is "not room enough" to contain it—meaning the blessing exceeds the boundaries of human capacity.

  • The Measure of Judgment: In prophetic literature, the word is used to mock human self-sufficiency. Empires exhaust whole populations to build monuments, walls, and cities. God looks at these massive architectural achievements and measures them: they are just enough to keep the flames of His judgment burning.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Sovereignty of God over Human Labor: God determines the ultimate value and outcome of human effort. If a society builds apart from God, He decrees that their labor is structurally destined for destruction.

  • The Illusion of Human Autonomy: Prideful empires believe they are building eternal monuments. However, without God, the "sufficiency" of their work is merely fuel for the fire. Human effort cannot generate lasting significance independent of the Creator.

  • The Folly of Worldly Exhaustion: The word exposes the tragic irony of godless ambition. Human beings will work themselves to the point of physical and mental collapse (viyafu, "weary themselves") only to satisfy emptiness. True rest and eternal sufficiency are found only in Yahweh.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2, God is answering the prophet’s complaint by pronouncing five "woes" against the proud Babylonian Empire. Babylon was notorious for conquering nations and forcing conscripted laborers to build its massive walls, temples, and palaces.

In verse 13, the prophet asks, "Behold, is it not of the LORD of hosts That the peoples labor in the very fire, And the nations weary themselves for nothing?"

The phrase bedey-esh (laboring "to satisfy the fire") reveals that Babylon’s glorious architecture is already evaluated by God as nothing but firewood. The labor of these enslaved nations is not building an eternal kingdom; it is merely preparing fuel for the fire of God's coming wrath. Similarly, their weariness is bedey-riq (to satisfy emptiness). The supreme Lord of hosts has sovereignly decreed that any labor expended on pride, greed, and violence will inevitably produce a net yield of absolute zero.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 15:8: "but you shall open your hand wide to him and willingly lend him sufficient for his need, whatever he needs."

  • Proverbs 25:16: "Have you found honey? Eat only as much as you need, Lest you be filled with it and vomit."

  • Jeremiah 51:58: "The broad walls of Babylon shall be utterly broken, And her high gates shall be burned with fire; The people will labor in vain, And the nations, because of the fire; And they shall be weary."

  • Malachi 3:10: "Bring all the tithes into the storehouse, That there may be food in My house, And try Me now in this," Says the LORD of hosts, "If I will not open for you the windows of heaven And pour out for you such blessing That there will not be room enough to receive it."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The parallel between Habakkuk 2:13 and Jeremiah 51:58 is exact, demonstrating a unified prophetic perspective on the fall of worldly superpowers. Both prophets utilize the word day in its construct form to expose the ultimate destiny of Babylon’s architectural achievements.

For pastors and teachers, this term serves as a powerful diagnostic tool for modern culture. It challenges congregations to evaluate their daily labors: Are we spending our lives, energy, and strength on kingdoms of clay that will only serve to feed the fire of judgment? Or are we laboring for the kingdom that cannot be shaken, investing our strength in that which yields eternal weight rather than absolute emptiness?
