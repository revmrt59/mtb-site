PEOPLES - ( 'am , Strong's H5971 )

Summary:

In Habakkuk 2:13, the word "peoples" refers to the collective groups of humanity, specifically those nations subjugated by the Babylonian Empire and forced to labor for its self-exalting projects. The text reveals the profound futility of human effort when divorced from God's purposes, illustrating that the exhaustive labor of the world's populations to build earthly kingdoms ultimately ends in smoke and nothingness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word 'am fundamentally denotes a group of people bound together by common ties, such as family, blood, language, or political structure. Unlike other terms for nations (such as goy), which often carry a political or territorial focus, 'am carries a strong relational and kinship undertone. It refers to a collective humanity viewed as a family or community. In the ancient Near East, empires like Babylon expanded by subjugating various 'ammîm (peoples), forcibly uniting them to build monumental cities, temples, and palaces to honor their false gods and human emperors.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'am means a people, nation, or populace. When used in the plural ('ammîm), as in Habakkuk 2:13, it refers to the various ethnic groups, tribes, or national populations of the earth. The term designates a collective gathering of individuals sharing a common identity. Lexically, it contrasts with goyim (nations as political bodies) by emphasizing the human element—the actual people who compose these nations. Its semantic range spans from a small localized clan to the entire human race, and most significantly, to Israel as God's special covenant possession ("My people").

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, 'am is used in several key ways:

  • Covenant Relationship: The singular 'am frequently designates Israel as God’s covenant people ("My people"), emphasizing familial-like intimacy, protection, and belonging.

  • The Broad Human Canvas: In the plural, 'ammîm represents the vast, diverse, and often rebellious populations of the earth who stand in contrast to the one true God.

  • Conscripted Labor: In contexts of imperial conquest, the word paints a picture of a massive, diverse workforce of subjugated peoples. They are depicted not as sovereign entities, but as a collective mass of human energy harnessed by ruthless rulers to construct monuments of self-glorification.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of 'am in Habakkuk 2:13 lies in the sovereignty of God over human history and the absolute futility of humanism. The "peoples" represent the peak of human strength, diversity, and collective effort. Yet, when human collectives unite to build an empire apart from God (reminiscent of the Tower of Babel), their labor is subject to divine judgment. God is the "LORD of hosts" (Yahweh of Armies), and He decrees that the collective energy of the world's peoples will burn. This underscores that any human endeavor not built upon the fear of the Lord is a tragic waste of God-given life and strength.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:13, the plural "peoples" ('ammîm) is paired poetically with "nations" (l'ummîm). The context is the series of five "woes" pronounced against the proud Chaldeans (Babylonians). Babylon had plundered many "peoples" to build its magnificent cities. Here, Habakkuk uses 'ammîm to show that the forced laborers from these conquered nations are working themselves to exhaustion. However, because Babylon's empire is built on bloodshed and violence, God has decreed its destruction. Thus, these peoples are literally "laboring for the very fire." Their monumental efforts will feed the flames of God's coming judgment, leaving them with nothing to show for their agony.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 20:26: "And you shall be holy to Me, for I the LORD am holy, and have separated you from the peoples, that you should be Mine."

  • Psalm 67:3: "Let the peoples praise You, O God; Let all the peoples praise You."

  • Psalm 96:5: "For all the gods of the peoples are idols, But the LORD made the heavens."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a powerful missionary undercurrent to the transformation of the 'ammîm in Scripture. While in Habakkuk 2:13 the "peoples" are exhausting themselves in futile, idolatrous service to a pagan empire, the ultimate destiny of the "peoples" in God's redemptive plan is to be freed from this vanity. In Habakkuk 2:14, the very next verse, the earth will be "filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea." This promises a day when the labor of the peoples will no longer be for the fire, but for the praise and worship of the living God.
