VANITY - ( riq , Strong's H7385 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun riq refers to emptiness, futility, or worthlessness, describing resources, efforts, or pursuits that are completely devoid of substance and yield no lasting value. In Habakkuk 2:13, it highlights the ultimate tragedy of godless ambition, illustrating how the exhausting labors of pagan nations to build empires and monuments result in absolute nothingness under the sovereign judgment of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun riq is derived from the verbal root ruq, which carries the physical meaning of emptying out, pouring forth, or drawing out a sword. The concrete word picture behind riq is that of a vessel that has been completely drained of its contents, leaving nothing but hollow space. It represents the spitting out of useless liquid or the scraping of a container until it is entirely bare. When applied metaphorically to human endeavors, riq evokes the imagery of a person working themselves to the point of collapse, only to open their hands and find them completely empty. It represents a total deficit of substance, purpose, or reward.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In the biblical text, riq functions as a noun, adjective, and adverb to denote emptiness, vanity, worthlessness, or that which is in vain. It specifies:

  • An empty, worthless endeavor that yields zero return.

  • A state of being devoid of moral or material value.

  • Idle, useless, or futile expenditures of strength and time.

  • It is closely linked with the concept of frustration—the exhausting of human potential for a result that vanishes like vapor.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, riq is used to contrast human self-sufficiency with divine reality. It is used to describe:

  • Agricultural futility, where planting and cultivating yield no harvest, representing a curse of empty storehouses.

  • Military and political alliances that prove to be broken reeds, offering empty promises of safety that result in sudden destruction.

  • Spiritual emptiness, where worship directed toward false idols leaves the soul completely destitute, as idols themselves are characterized as empty things.

  • In poetic parallelism, riq frequently pairs with words for "nothingness," "wind," and "fire," painting a vivid picture of human pride being consumed and leaving behind nothing but ashes.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, riq exposes the inherent limitation of the creature apart from the Creator. It demonstrates that any human achievement, no matter how monumental, is subject to the law of decay and divine frustration if it is built in opposition to God. This term highlights the doctrine of divine sovereignty in history. When humanity attempts to construct a kingdom for its own glory, God blows upon it, reducing their immense output to absolute emptiness. Ultimately, riq serves as a warning against the idolatry of self-determination, reminding believers that true substance, fullness, and security exist only in covenant relationship with Yahweh.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:13, the prophet addresses the rise of the Chaldean (Babylonian) Empire, which was rapidly expanding through military conquest, enslavement, and the construction of magnificent cities built on the blood of conquered peoples. The text asks rhetorical questions to emphasize that this grand empire-building is ultimately orchestrated by the Lord of hosts to fail. The Babylonians are described as laboring "for the very fire" and wearying themselves "for very vanity." The specific phrase day-riq ("for very vanity" or "for emptiness") means that their massive engineering projects, fortified walls, and lavish palaces are destined for destruction. Their intensive, backbreaking labor will produce nothing of permanent value; it is an exercise in ultimate futility because God has decreed its end.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 26:20: "And your strength shall be spent in vain; for your land shall not yield its produce, nor shall the trees of the land yield their fruit."

  • Psalm 2:1: "Why do the nations rage, And the people plot a vain thing?"

  • Isaiah 65:23: "They shall not labor in vain, Nor bring forth children for trouble; For they shall be the descendants of the blessed of the LORD, And their offspring with them."

  • Jeremiah 51:58: "Thus says the LORD of hosts: 'The broad walls of Babylon shall be utterly broken, And her high gates shall be burned with fire; The peoples will have labored in vain, And the nations because of the fire; And they shall be weary.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A profound literary and theological contrast exists between Habakkuk 2:13 and the verse that immediately follows it, Habakkuk 2:14. While the godless nations weary themselves for "very vanity" (riq), exhausting themselves to build an empire that will end in absolute emptiness, God’s ultimate plan is to fill the universe with His presence. Habakkuk 2:14 declares, "For the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea." The contrast is absolute: human pride results in emptiness (riq), but divine promise results in the earth being entirely filled (yimmale) with the heavy, substantial glory of God.
