WATERS - ( mayim , Strong's H4325 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word mayim refers literally to water, the fundamental element necessary for sustaining physical life, but it carries profound symbolic and theological weight throughout the Old Testament. In Habakkuk 2:14, mayim serves as a powerful metaphor for absolute abundance, complete coverage, and unstoppable force. Just as the waters of the ocean leave no part of the seabed untouched or dry, the future knowledge of the glory of God will thoroughly saturate, cover, and dominate the entire earth. This stands in stark contrast to the futile, dry, and fire-consumed achievements of wicked nations, pointing instead to a coming reality of refreshing, all-encompassing divine rule.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, water was viewed with both deep appreciation and profound awe. In a semi-arid climate like Israel's, water was the ultimate symbol of life, blessing, and divine favor. Springs, rain, and dew meant survival and prosperity. Conversely, large bodies of water—such as the seas and the deep—represented chaos, immense power, and the untamed aspects of creation that only God could control. The sea was seen as vast, deep, and unfathomable. To say that something "covers the sea" like waters implies an absolute, overwhelming density. The water does not merely sit on top of the sea; it constitutes the sea itself. It is the very substance that fills the vast, empty chasms of the earth, leaving no void.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun mayim is inherently dual or plural in form, reflecting a collective concept of flowing or abundant liquids.

  • It refers literally to natural water sources, including rain, springs, wells, rivers, and oceans.

  • It designates water used for drinking, washing, and ritual purification.

  • In figurative contexts, it represents overwhelming distress, sweeping armies, moral instability, or, conversely, abundant spiritual blessings, life, and the pouring out of the Holy Spirit.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs mayim in several key word pictures:

  • The Picture of Chaos and Judgment: In the flood narrative and in various psalms, waters represent chaotic forces and divine judgment that overwhelm the wicked and threaten the righteous.

  • The Picture of Spiritual Renewal: Prophets frequently use water to describe the future restoration of Israel, where dry ground becomes springs of water, symbolizing spiritual life and cleansing.

  • The Picture of Total Submersion: The specific imagery of waters covering the bed of the sea highlights the concept of complete, unchecked saturation. It represents a reality so pervasive that nothing else can coexist within that space without being completely submerged and characterized by it.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, mayim in prophetic literature often points to the eschatological renewal of the cosmos. In Genesis, the Spirit of God hovered over the face of the waters to bring order out of chaos. In the prophetic anticipation of the new creation, God's glory will perform a similar ordering work, filling the earth. The comparison in Habakkuk 2:14 demonstrates that the knowledge of God’s glory will not be a localized or temporary phenomenon. It will be global, permanent, and sovereign. Just as physical water is essential for physical life, the knowledge of God's glory is the ultimate source of spiritual life for the world.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2, the prophet is grappling with the rise of the violent and ruthless Babylonian empire. In verse 13, God reveals that the labor of these pagan nations is "for the very fire"—their massive cities and conquests will ultimately burn and turn to ash. Their efforts are empty and futile.

In verse 14, the contrast is drawn using mayim. While the achievements of proud humanity end in fire, smoke, and emptiness, God's kingdom ends in fullness. The "knowledge of the glory of the LORD" will cover the earth "as the waters cover the sea."

  • The sea basin represents the vast expanse of the world.

  • The waters represent the knowledge of God’s glory.

  • The coverage is absolute; just as there is no part of the sea that is not wet, there will be no part of the earth that is untouched by the majesty, sovereignty, and presence of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:2 - "The earth was without form, and void; and darkness was on the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters."

  • Exodus 15:19 - "For the horses of Pharaoh went with his chariots and his horsemen into the sea, and the LORD brought back the waters of the sea upon them. But the children of Israel went on dry land in the midst of the sea."

  • Psalm 23:2 - "He makes me to lie down in green pastures; He leads me beside the still waters."

  • Isaiah 11:9 - "They shall not hurt nor destroy in all My holy mountain, For the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD As the waters cover the sea."

  • Amos 5:24 - "But let justice run down like water, And righteousness like a mighty stream."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Grammatically, because mayim is always plural, it emphasizes the ideas of abundance, multiplicity, and flowing movement. In Hebrew thought, standing water could become stagnant and represent death, but "living water" (mayim chayim) was fresh, flowing, and life-giving. The comparison in Habakkuk utilizes this dynamic imagery. The glory of God is not static; it flows, floods, and saturates the earth, cleansing it from the bloodguilt and idolatry detailed in the preceding woes of Habakkuk 2.
