BOTTLE - ( chemah , Strong's H2573 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chemah, translated as "bottle" in the NKJV of Habakkuk 2:15, primarily carries the meaning of "heat," "burning anger," "wrath," or "poison." While some traditional English translations render it as "bottle" or "skin-bottle" (drawing a literary parallel to chemet, a leather vessel for liquids), the word fundamentally denotes a heated, toxic substance—either the burning venom of a serpent or the burning fury of wrath. In the context of Habakkuk 2:15, it paints a graphic picture of a wicked person (specifically representing the Babylonian empire) forcing an intoxicating, poisonous drink upon a neighbor to exploit, degrade, and shame them. This term emphasizes the destructive, venomous nature of the oppressor’s influence and the burning judgment that inevitably rebounds upon them.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word chemah derives from a root associated with heat, warmth, and feverish intensity. It communicates the physical sensation of burning, which is applied metaphorically to both internal anger (burning rage) and external toxins (burning poison or venom). The visual background is one of intense, consuming heat. When applied to anger, it represents a fury that has reached a boiling point, consuming the one who possesses it and threatening to destroy those upon whom it is poured out. When applied to venom, it represents the lethal, burning secretion of a serpent. The NKJV translation of "bottle" in Habakkuk 2:15 connects this concept to a vessel containing this burning, intoxicating mixture, suggesting a leather skin-bottle used to dispense a devastating, mind-altering substance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun chemah refers to heat, burning anger, wrath, poison, or venom. The semantic range spans:

  • Burning anger, rage, or fury, often of human rulers or of God's righteous indignation.

  • Poison or venom, particularly the burning venom of serpents or cobras.

  • A heated substance, metaphorically linked to a cup of staggering or wrath.

The translation "bottle" in the NKJV reflects a close association or wordplay with chemet (a leather skin-bottle used for water or wine), or represents the container from which this burning, toxic wrath is poured out. The core lexical idea, however, remains centered on the burning, destructive quality of the substance being offered.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently uses chemah to depict the execution of wrath and the effects of poison.

  • The Venom of Serpents: The word describes the burning poison of snakes, highlighting its hidden, lethal, and agonizing nature.

  • The Cup of Wrath: Wrath is often personified as a liquid poured into a cup or vessel, which God forces wicked nations to drink, leading to staggering, vulnerability, and ruin.

  • Outpoured Fury: The term is often paired with verbs meaning "to pour out" (shaphak), emphasizing that this burning anger acts like a liquid fire, flooding and consuming everything in its path.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, chemah underscores the reality of moral pollution and divine retribution.

  • The Sin of Degradation: Human wrath and malice are not passive; they actively seek to intoxicate and degrade others. Giving drink to a neighbor to look on their nakedness represents the abusive misuse of power, converting a gesture of hospitality into an act of violent humiliation.

  • The Principle of Retributive Justice: What a person pours out is what they will ultimately receive. The one who forces others to drink the burning poison of shame will be made to drink the cup of the Lord's right hand—a cup of ultimate wrath and public disgrace.

  • The Holiness of God: God's wrath is not an uncontrolled outburst but a settled, burning opposition to wickedness, cruelty, and the exploitation of the weak.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:15, the prophet pronounces a woe against the Babylonian empire (and by extension, any predatory power). The NKJV renders the phrase "pressing him to your bottle" (Hebrew: mispach chamateka).

  • The Action: The verb saphach means to attach, associate, or pour out.

  • The Object: The noun chemah (with the pronominal suffix "your") denotes "your poison" or "your wrath."

  • The Nuance: By translating this as "bottle," the text visualizes the oppressor forcing their victim to drink directly from a container of burning, toxic wine. The Babylonian empire did not merely conquer nations politically; they seduced, corrupted, and morally intoxicated them, stripping them of their dignity and strength. The "bottle" represents the source of this corrupting, toxic influence. The contextual focus is the deliberate, malicious intent to incapacitate a neighbor in order to expose and mock their vulnerability.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 32:24: "They shall be wasted with hunger, Devoured by pestilence and bitter destruction; I will also send against them the teeth of beasts, With the poison of serpents of the dust."

  • Psalm 58:4: "Their poison is like the poison of a serpent; They are like the deaf cobra that stops its ear,"

  • Psalm 79:6: "Pour out Your wrath on the nations that do not know You, And on the kingdoms that do not call on Your name."

  • Jeremiah 6:11: "Therefore I am full of the fury of the LORD. I am weary of holding it in. I will pour it out on the children outside, And on the assembly of young men together..."

  • Daniel 9:16: "O Lord, according to all Your righteousness, I pray, let Your anger and Your fury be turned away from Your city Jerusalem, Your holy mountain..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Structural Symmetry: This woe in Habakkuk 2:15 is directly answered by the judgment in Habakkuk 2:16. The oppressor who forced the "bottle" (burning poison/wrath) onto his neighbor will be forced to drink from the "cup of the LORD’s right hand," resulting in "utter shame" instead of glory. This is a classic biblical presentation of lex talionis (the law of retaliation).

  • Wordplay with Chemet: The phonetic and structural similarity between chemah (wrath/poison) and chemet (leather bottle/wineskin) creates a powerful literary double-meaning. The auditor hears both: the physical bottle of wine used to intoxicate, and the deadly, burning venom of political and moral hostility that destroys the soul.
