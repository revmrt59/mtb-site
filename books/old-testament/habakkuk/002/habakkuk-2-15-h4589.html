NAKEDNESS - ( ma'or , Strong's H4589 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ma'or refers to physical nakedness, bareness, and the exposure of the private parts. In Habakkuk 2:15, it describes the shameful exposure of a victim who has been aggressively intoxicated by an exploitative neighbor. This term represents not merely a lack of clothing, but a state of forced vulnerability, degradation, and stripped dignity used by oppressors to humiliate others. The passage warns of God’s impending judgment on those who abuse their power to strip away the honor and decency of their fellow human beings.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The root of ma'or is connected to the verb arah, which means to pour out, bare, make empty, or strip. The physical picture is of systematically removing coverings to leave someone completely bare and unprotected.

  • In ancient Near Eastern culture, public nakedness was the ultimate form of social humiliation and loss of honor. Clothing was a marker of status, identity, and protection. To strip someone or expose their nakedness was to rob them of their humanity, reduce them to a defenseless state, and subject them to public mockery.

  • This word picture contrasts the protective care expected toward a neighbor with the predatory violence of an enemy who delights in stripping away a neighbor's dignity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Ma'or (plural me'orim) is defined as a state of nakedness, bareness, or the exposed private parts (pudenda).

  • The word is closely related to ma'ar (nakedness, empty space) and erwah (nakedness, shame). It specifically denotes the physical exposure of the body in a context that elicits shame, vulnerability, and dishonor.

  • The term carries a strong moral and social connotation, representing a state where modesty has been stripped away, leaving a person exposed to the predatory gaze of others.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Ma'or occurs only in Habakkuk 2:15 in the plural form (me'orehem, "their nakedness"), making it a distinct noun form within the Hebrew Bible.

  • The singular form and its root are used to depict a state of utter vulnerability. The word picture is that of a host who deliberately intoxicates his guest, not out of hospitality, but to strip away their self-control and dignity so that he can mock and exploit their exposed, helpless state.

  • This physical degradation serves as a metaphor for national defeat and political humiliation. In the ancient world, conquering armies routinely stripped captives naked to march them into exile, signaling their complete subjugation and the loss of their national sovereignty.

4. Theological Implications

  • The exposure of nakedness recalls the original fall of humanity in Genesis, where nakedness was immediately associated with shame, vulnerability, and the need for covering. In Habakkuk, exposing another's nakedness is a direct assault on the image of God in man.

  • The act violates the biblical covenantal principle of protecting human dignity. Rather than covering a neighbor's shame (an act of love and righteousness), the oppressor actively causes and exploits it.

  • God's response to this exploitation is strict retributive justice. In the subsequent verse, the cup of wrath is turned back upon the oppressor, who will be filled with shame instead of glory and forced to expose their own uncircumcision (nakedness).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 2:15, the prophet pronounces the third of five "woes" against the Chaldeans (Babylonians). The immediate metaphor is a host who forces intoxicating drink upon his neighbor to gaze at his nakedness.

  • This serves as a double metaphor. Literally, it condemns the abusive, predatory behavior of individuals who use alcohol to sexually exploit or socially humiliate others.

  • Metaphorically, it describes the foreign policy of Babylon, which used political alliances, treaties, and military power (the metaphorical "bottle" or "poison") to render neighboring nations helpless, strip them of their wealth, and gloat over their national ruin and shame.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Habakkuk 2:15: "Woe to him who gives drink to his neighbor, pressing him to your bottle, even to make him drunk, that you may look on his nakedness!"

  • Nahum 3:5: "'Behold, I am against you,' says the LORD of hosts; 'I will lift your skirts over your face, I will show the nations your nakedness, and the kingdoms your shame.'" (Uses the closely related cognate ma'ar, Strong's H4587).

  • Lamentations 1:8: "Jerusalem has sinned greatly, therefore she has become vile. All who honored her despise her because they have seen her nakedness; yes, she sighs and turns away." (Uses the semantic equivalent erwah, Strong's H6172).

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The contrast between "glory" (kabod) and "shame" (qalon) is a major theme in Habakkuk. The predator seeks to turn their neighbor's glory into shame by exposing their ma'or (nakedness).

  • The judgment of God in verse 16 reverses this: "You are filled with shame instead of glory. You also drink, and be exposed as uncircumcised!" The word for "be exposed as uncircumcised" (he'arel) is a wordplay on the root arah (to strip/uncover) and arel (uncircumcised).

  • This indicates that those who delight in the vulnerability and shame of others will eventually be stripped of their own defenses, exposing their own spiritual and physical shame to the world.
