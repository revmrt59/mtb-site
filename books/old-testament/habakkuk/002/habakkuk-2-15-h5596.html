PRESSING - ( saphach , Strong's H5596 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb saphach primarily denotes the action of joining, attaching, associating, or binding one thing to another. In the intense poetic context of Habakkuk 2:15, the word describes an aggressive, forced attachment where an oppressor "presses" or binds a neighbor to a cup of intoxicating wrath. Rather than a gesture of hospitality, this is a coercive, hostile action designed to subjugate, incapacitate, and ultimately expose the victim to public shame and ruin.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, the primary imagery behind saphach is that of physical attachment or annexation. It is used to describe joining a group of people, attaching oneself to a piece of land, or clinging to an inheritance. It conveys the picture of bringing two distinct entities into an inseparable, tight-knit contact. In social settings, this joining could be voluntary, such as an outsider seeking to align with a community. However, in military and political contexts, it represents a hostile imposition where a powerful entity annexes a weaker one. In Habakkuk, this word picture is applied to the forced administration of wine, portraying an oppressor physically pinning a bottle to a victim's lips, leaving them no way to escape the incoming degradation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of saphach includes to join, to attach, to associate, to annex, or to cause to cling. In specific verb stems, it can also carry the nuance of pouring out or discharging, which closely links the concept of attaching a vessel to the act of pouring its contents. In Habakkuk 2:15, the word appears as a participle (mispiah), functioning to describe the continuous, deliberate action of the tormentor. The term emphasizes the forced connection between the victim and the toxic substance. The oppressor does not merely offer a drink; he aggressively attaches, pours out, or presses his wrath directly onto the neighbor.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb saphach occurs only a few times in the Hebrew Bible, but each instance highlights a close, binding connection:

  • Voluntary association: Foreigners who covenant with Yahweh will "cling" or attach themselves to the house of Jacob (Isaiah 14:1).

  • Desperate dependence: A destitute priest begs to be "put" or joined to a priestly office simply to secure food to eat (1 Samuel 2:36).

  • Political exile: David laments being driven out, which prevents him from "sharing" or joining in the physical inheritance of Israel (1 Samuel 26:19).

  • Coercive association: In Habakkuk 2:15, the word picture shifts dramatically to a hostile act. The oppressor binds his victim to the cup of venom, forcing an association that leads to physical and moral vulnerability.

4. Theological Implications

Theological truths regarding God's justice and the nature of sin emerge from this term:

  • Sin is aggressively coercive: Evil is rarely content to remain isolated; it actively seeks to "press" itself upon others, dragging them into participation and moral failure.

  • The abuse of power: God holds rulers and nations accountable not only for their direct violence, but also for how they seduce, corrupt, and degrade those under their influence.

  • The Law of Retribution (Lex Talionis): The theological climax of this passage shows that those who aggressively "press" the cup of shame onto others will have the cup of the Lord's right hand forced upon them in return (Habakkuk 2:16). The binder will be bound by God’s judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:15, the prophet is pronouncing a series of "woes" against the Chaldeans (Babylonians). The Babylonians conquered nations not only through physical weaponry but also through cultural and political destabilization. The metaphor of giving drink and "pressing" the bottle to a neighbor represents how Babylon intoxicated surrounding nations with treaties, false alliances, and cultural vices to strip them of their sovereignty and dignity ("that you may look on his nakedness"). The term saphach captures the inescapable grip of Babylonian dominance, where conquered peoples were forced into a destructive alliance that they could not shake off.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 2:36: "And it shall come to pass that everyone who is left in your house will come and bow down to him for a piece of silver and a morsel of bread, and say, 'Please put [saphach] me into one of the priestly offices, that I may eat a piece of bread.' "

  • 1 Samuel 26:19: "Now therefore, please, let my lord the king hear the words of his servant: If the LORD has stirred you up against me, let Him accept an offering. But if it is the children of men, may they be cursed before the LORD, for they have driven me out this day from sharing [saphach] in the inheritance of the LORD, saying, 'Go, serve other gods.'"

  • Isaiah 14:1: "For the LORD will have mercy on Jacob, and will still choose Israel, and settle them in their own land. The strangers will be joined with them, and they will cling [saphach] to the house of Jacob."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Some commentators and translators note a strong linguistic connection between saphach and the Hebrew word chemah, which can mean "wrath" or "poison/venom." Therefore, "pressing him to his bottle" carries the parallel sense of "pouring out your venom/wrath" upon him. This double meaning highlights the lethal nature of what is being forced upon the victim. It is not ordinary wine, but a poisonous mixture of political fury and moral corruption that completely destabilizes the recipient.
