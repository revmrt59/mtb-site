NEIGHBOR - ( rea , Strong's H7453 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word rea denotes a companion, friend, fellow, or neighbor—essentially another person with whom one has a social relationship, proximity, or covenantal bond. Within the community of Israel, this word carries profound ethical obligations of love, protection, honesty, and mutual respect. In the specific context of Habakkuk 2:15, the word appears in a solemn pronouncement of woe against the predatory Babylonian empire. Rather than offering neighborly hospitality and care, the oppressor metaphorically plies his neighbor with intoxicating drink to exploit, subjugate, and humiliate him.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun rea is derived from a root associated with associating with, tending (as a shepherd tends a flock), or being a companion. It represents the fundamental building block of horizontal relationships in Hebrew society. In the ancient Near East, a neighbor was not merely someone who lived nearby geographically, but someone with whom one shared a community, covenantal expectations, and a duty of protection. The word picture is one of proximity and mutual reliance; your neighbor is the one who stands beside you in daily life, whose welfare is intrinsically linked to your own.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • rea means associate, companion, friend, neighbor, or fellow. The semantic range spans from an intimate, trusted friend to a fellow citizen, to simply "another person" in a general relational sense. It defines any person within one’s circle of contact. The word carries an inherent moral weight, establishing that any human being encountered in the course of life is deserving of fair, honest, and compassionate treatment under God's law.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, rea is used to establish the boundaries of ethical behavior:

  • It is the object of covenantal love. The call to love one's neighbor as oneself summarizes the horizontal dimension of the law.

  • It is the touchstone of integrity. A person's character is judged by how they speak of, act toward, and treat their rea.

  • In wisdom literature, the rea is often the focus of social warnings. One is warned against betraying a neighbor, lying to a companion, or using false friendship for selfish gain. The tragedy of a broken relationship with a rea is a recurring theme in Israel's lamentations.

4. Theological Implications

The treatment of the rea is the ultimate indicator of a person's relationship with God. God's covenant law fiercely protects the neighbor. The Ten Commandments prohibit bearing false witness against, coveting the possessions of, or taking the wife of one's rea. To violate a neighbor is to violate the image of God in that person and to actively rebel against the Sovereign Lord who acts as the patron and protector of the vulnerable. True worship of Yahweh is always validated by justice and mercy extended to one's neighbor.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:15, the prophet delivers a powerful "woe" oracle against the proud, rapacious Chaldean (Babylonian) empire. The passage uses the vivid metaphor of a host offering drink to his rea (neighbor). In the ancient world, hospitality to a neighbor was a sacred duty. A host was expected to provide nourishment, safety, and honor.

Babylon, however, perverts this sacred duty. Instead of serving refreshing wine in a spirit of fellowship, the imperial predator forces its neighbor to drink a toxic, intoxicating cup ("pressing him to your bottle, even to make him drunk") with a malicious intent: "that you may look on his nakedness."

In this context, the rea represents the weaker neighboring nations surrounding Babylon. Instead of fostering peaceful, mutually beneficial neighborly relations, Babylon systematically stripped these nations of their wealth, sovereignty, and dignity, leaving them exposed, humiliated, and ruined.

  • Leviticus 19:18: "You shall not take vengeance, nor bear any grudge against the children of your people, but you shall love your neighbor as yourself: I am the LORD."

  • Proverbs 3:29: "Do not plan evil against your neighbor, for he dwells by you safely."

  • Proverbs 17:17: "A friend loves at all times, and a brother is born for adversity."

  • Jeremiah 22:13: "Woe to him who builds his house by unrighteousness and his chambers by injustice, who uses his neighbor's service without wages and gives him nothing for his work,"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) frequently translates rea using the Greek word plesion (neighbor) or philos (friend). This directly connects the Old Testament concept of the neighbor to Jesus’ teachings in the New Testament. When asked, "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29), Jesus responds with the Parable of the Good Samaritan, expanding the boundaries of rea beyond ethnic and national lines to encompass anyone in need.

Additionally, there is a terrible irony in Habakkuk 2:15-16. Because Babylon forced its "neighbor" to drink the cup of humiliation, Babylon itself will soon be forced to drink the cup of the Lord’s right hand, experiencing the very shame and exposure it inflicted on others.
