DRUNK - ( shakar , Strong's H7937 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shakar primarily means to become drunk, intoxicated, or to drink deeply to the point of satiation. In Habakkuk 2:15, it describes the deliberate act of inducing intoxication in another person to exploit and shame them. This term moves beyond physical drunkenness to serve as a powerful theological metaphor for God's judgment, where those who mockingly force others to drink the cup of humiliation are ultimately forced to drink the cup of divine wrath themselves.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, wine production and agriculture were central to daily life. Wine was celebrated as a gift that gladdens the heart, but Hebrew culture maintained strict boundaries regarding its consumption. The verb shakar captures the transition from drinking for gladness to losing cognitive control, physical stability, and moral restraint. In ancient societies, drunkenness was not merely a personal vice; it was a state of severe vulnerability. To be intoxicated was to be stripped of one's defenses, judgment, and honor, making the drunkard an easy target for mockery, exploitation, and public shame.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shakar signifies to drink deeply, to become intoxicated, or in its causative forms, to make someone drunk. Its semantic range extends from drinking abundantly without negative connotation to complete physical and mental incapacitation. The term emphasizes the loss of sober judgment, the exposure of vulnerability, and the state of being overcome by an external force. In the intensive causative stem (Piel), as used in Habakkuk 2:15, it means to actively make someone else drunk, indicating a deliberate, malicious intent to incapacitate another person.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs shakar in both literal and figurative contexts:

  • Literal Vulnerability: The word is used of Noah’s literal intoxication, which led to his exposure and subsequent familial dishonor. This established an early biblical association between physical drunkenness, nakedness, and shame.

  • The Cup of Wrath: Figuratively, the prophets frequently use shakar to depict divine judgment. God is shown handing a cup of wine—representing His wrath—to rebellious nations. They are forced to drink until they are "drunk," meaning they stagger, lose their strength, and fall helpless under His sovereign hand.

  • Sovereign Derision: When nations are made "drunk" by God's judgment, they lose their strategic wisdom and military might, stumbling like drunkards in their own vomit, completely unable to save themselves.

4. Theological Implications

Theological usage of shakar highlights the absolute moral accountability of humanity before God. Drunkenness represents a state of spiritual stupor and helplessness. When humans choose to intoxicate themselves, they surrender their God-given sober-mindedness.

More severely, the act of forcing others into a state of intoxication represents the ultimate violation of neighborly love. It is an abuse of power designed to strip another image-bearer of their dignity. The scriptures show that God is the defender of the vulnerable; those who use the "cup" of their influence, power, or literal wine to humiliate others will face a reciprocal judgment. They will be forced to drink the cup of the Lord’s right hand, resulting in eternal shame.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:15, the prophet pronounces a divine "woe" against Babylon (the Chaldeans). Babylon was a predatory empire that conquered, plundered, and humiliated neighboring nations.

The phrase "even to make him drunk" is used as a double-entendre. Literally, it condemns the malicious act of getting a neighbor drunk to gaze upon their nakedness—an act of predatory voyeurism and domination. Metaphorically, it describes Babylon’s foreign policy: she intoxicated surrounding nations with her political alliances, military might, and terrifying violence, stripping them of their sovereignty and dignity.

However, the context immediately reverses this dynamic in verse 16. Babylon, who forced others to become drunk and exposed, will herself be forced to drink from the cup of the Lord's right hand. She will stagger under God's wrath, and her own "nakedness" and ultimate shame will be exposed to the world.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 9:21: "Then he drank of the wine and was drunk, and became uncovered in his tent."

  • Isaiah 29:9: "Pause and wonder! Blind yourselves and be blind! They are drunk, but not with wine; They stagger, but not with intoxicating drink."

  • Jeremiah 51:7: "Babylon was a golden cup in the LORD’s hand, That made all the earth drunk. The nations drank her wine; Therefore the nations are deranged."

  • Lamentations 4:21: "Rejoice and be glad, O daughter of Edom, You who dwell in the land of Uz! The cup shall also pass over to you; You shall become drunk and make yourself naked."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The linguistic relationship between the verb shakar (to get drunk) and the noun shekar (strong drink/intoxicating liquor) underscores the danger of excess in biblical wisdom literature. While wine was a common table beverage, strong drink (shekar) was highly intoxicating and associated with mockery and brawling (Proverbs 20:1). The biblical warning is clear: to lose sobriety—whether physically through drink or metaphorically through pride and power—is to invite self-destruction and divine correction.
