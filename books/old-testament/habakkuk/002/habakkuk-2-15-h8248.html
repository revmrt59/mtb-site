DRINK - ( shaqah , Strong's H8248 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shaqah primarily means "to cause to drink," "to give drink to," or "to irrigate." In Habakkuk 2:15, it describes the calculated, malicious act of forcing or enticing a neighbor to drink intoxicating wine to expose their nakedness. Beyond literal hydration, shaqah carries deep metaphorical weight in Scripture, ranging from the life-giving provision of God watering the earth to the devastating cup of His wrath poured out in judgment. In the context of Habakkuk, it portrays a predatory violation of hospitality, representing the exploitation of the vulnerable by the wicked.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • In the arid climate of the ancient Near East, water and drink were matters of life and death. To "give drink" (shaqah) was normally an act of supreme hospitality, mercy, and life-sustenance. It described watering thirsty flocks, welcoming a weary traveler into a tent, or a river irrigating dry soil to produce life-giving crops.

  • The term is deeply associated with refreshment and survival. When Genesis describes the garden of Eden, it highlights a river that went out to "water" the garden.

  • In Habakkuk 2:15, this positive, life-giving action is subverted. Instead of refreshing the weary, the predator gives "drink" that is laced with venom and wrath, turning an act of hospitality into a weapon of subjugation, shame, and public humiliation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The verb shaqah is causative, meaning "to cause to drink," "to give drink to," "to water," or "to irrigate."

  • In the Hiphil (causative) stem, which is used in Habakkuk 2:15 as a participle (mashqeh), it functions as a substantive meaning "one who gives drink" or "he who makes his neighbor drink."

  • The semantic range spans from physical irrigation and life-giving provision to the metaphorical pouring out of poison, wrath, or intoxicating substances. It emphasizes the active agency of the one dispensing the liquid, placing the responsibility for the outcome entirely on the giver.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The River of Eden: The word is used to describe the primary source of life in the created order, where a river flowed to water (shaqah) the garden, symbolizing God's abundant, unsolicited grace and provision.

  • The Duty of Hospitality: Giving drink to the thirsty is a righteous act of compassion. Even an enemy is to be given water to drink (shaqah) if he is thirsty, reflecting the heart of God.

  • The Cup of Judgment: Metaphorically, the word is used when God forces the rebellious to drink the cup of His wrath or "water of gall" due to their persistent idolatry.

  • Subverted Hospitality: In Habakkuk 2:15, the act of giving drink becomes a predatory trap. The host pours out intoxicating wine mixed with "wrath" (or poison) to strip the neighbor of dignity and control, exposing their nakedness.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Subversion of God's Order: God designed the giving of drink to sustain life, but fallen humanity uses it to destroy life and dignity. The passage exposes the depth of human depravity, where tools of blessing are weaponized for abuse.

  • The Law of Retribution (Lex Talionis): The one who gives intoxicating drink to shame his neighbor will himself be forced to drink from the cup of the Lord's right hand. The "giver of drink" (mashqeh) eventually becomes the passive receiver of God's cup of fury, showing that God's judgment perfectly fits the crime.

  • Exploitation and Vulnerability: The theological warning in Habakkuk targets those who use their power, influence, or substance to make others vulnerable. God hates exploitation and will hold accountable those who lure others into moral and physical ruin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • Habakkuk 2:15 is the fourth "woe" pronounced against the proud, rapacious Babylonian empire (and by extension, any predatory power).

  • The Babylonians "gave drink" to neighboring nations—subjugating them, stripping them of their resources, and exposing their weakness (nakedness) to mock them on the world stage.

  • The literal act of enticing someone to get drunk to violate or humiliate them serves as a vivid metaphor for imperial conquest, political seduction, and moral degradation. The Babylonians used diplomatic allurements and intoxicating alliances to disarm and ultimately destroy their neighbors.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:10: "Now a river went out of Eden to water the garden, and from there it parted and became four riverheads."

  • Genesis 29:7: "Then he said, 'Look, it is still high day; it is not time for the cattle to be gathered together. Water the sheep, and go and feed them.'"

  • Psalm 104:13: "He waters the hills from His chambers; The earth is satisfied with the fruit of Your works."

  • Proverbs 25:21: "If your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat; And if he is thirsty, give him water to drink;"

  • Jeremiah 9:15: "therefore thus says the Lord of hosts, the God of Israel: 'Behold, I will feed them, this people, with wormwood, and give them water of gall to drink.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The noun derivative of shaqah is mashqeh, which refers to a butler or cupbearer (as seen in the story of Joseph and Pharaoh). A cupbearer's duty was to provide safe, pleasant, and life-sustaining drink to the king. In Habakkuk, the wicked acts as a corrupt cupbearer, serving poison instead of wine.

  • This Old Testament picture contrasts sharply with the New Testament revelation of Christ. While the wicked give drink to bring shame and exposure, Jesus offers living water so that those who drink will never thirst again, bringing restoration, clothing them in righteousness, and removing their shame.
