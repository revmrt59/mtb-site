TURNED - ( sabab , Strong's H5437 )

Summary:

In Habakkuk 2:16, the word "turned" (sabab) depicts a sudden, inescapable reversal of circumstances under divine judgment. While Babylon forced the cup of wrath and degradation upon neighboring nations, God declares that the cup of His right hand will be turned—or brought around—to them. The verb highlights the circular movement of divine retribution: the evil an oppressive empire pours out eventually circles back to become the very judgment they must drink, showcasing the poetic justice and absolute sovereignty of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb sabab literally means to turn around, go around, surround, or encompass. It evokes the spatial image of a circle, a turning wheel, or a dramatic change in direction. In ancient Near Eastern warfare, agricultural life, and nomadic journeys, sabab described physical movement, such as marching around a city wall, circling a mountain, or turning a chariot around. In the theological landscape of the Old Testament, this word picture is frequently employed to show how God redirects human history, alters the course of human hearts, or surrounds His people with protection or judgment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic semantic range of sabab includes to turn oneself, turn around, go around, surround, encompass, or cause to turn. In the causative active and passive stems, it means to bring around, direct, turn over, or transfer. In Habakkuk 2:16, the verb is used in the Niphal (passive) stem (yissob), meaning "will be turned," "will be brought around," or "will redirect itself." The lexical focus is on an inevitable, sovereignly directed transition: the cup of judgment, which has been circulating among the nations, is now being actively redirected toward Babylon.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, sabab is used in several distinct ways:

  • Physical turning or pivoting (such as a door turning on its hinges, or a traveler changing directions).

  • Encompassing or encircling (such as Israel marching around the walls of Jericho, or enemies surrounding a psalmist).

  • Changing or transferring ownership (such as a kingdom being turned over from one ruler to another).

  • Divine redirection (such as God physically or circumstantially turning people's hearts or changing the course of historical events).

4. Theological Implications

  • Sovereign Retribution: The "turning" of the cup demonstrates the law of divine retaliation. The exact suffering, exposure, and shame that Babylon inflicted on others is turned back upon them.

  • Inescapability of Divine Justice: Because the action of turning is decreed by God ("the cup of the LORD's right hand"), human power cannot alter its trajectory.

  • The Illusion of Power: Babylon believed they were the masters of history, pouring out wrath on others. The turning of the cup reveals that they are merely subjects under God’s ultimate authority. The cup of victory they thought they held is, by God's hand, turned into a cup of ruin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Habakkuk 2 consists of five "woes" directed against the Chaldeans (Babylonians). Verse 16 belongs to the fourth woe, targeting their shameful violence, degradation of others, and exploitation of nations. Babylon made others "drunk" to gaze on their nakedness and shame. Therefore, God uses the imagery of drinking to describe their judgment. The "turning" (sabab) of the cup signifies a dramatic reversal of fortunes. The cup of glory and triumph they thought they were holding is transformed; God turns the cup of His ultimate wrath directly to them, forcing them to drink until they are exposed and utterly shamed.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Joshua 6:3 - "You shall march around the city, all you men of war; you shall go all around the city once. This you shall do six days."

  • 1 Chronicles 10:14 - "...therefore He killed him, and turned the kingdom to David the son of Jesse."

  • Psalm 114:3 - "The sea saw it and fled; Jordan was turned back."

  • Ezekiel 47:2 - "He brought me out by way of the north gate, and led me around on the outside to the outer gateway that faces east..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The concept of the cup of God's wrath being passed around is a recurring prophetic motif (such as in Jeremiah 25:15-28 and Isaiah 51:17). The use of sabab in Habakkuk 2:16 underscores the systematic, orderly distribution of God's justice. No wicked nation is skipped; the cup makes its full round. For the modern believer, this serves as a sober warning against pride and exploitation, while simultaneously offering comfort. It assures the oppressed that God is not blind to injustice; the wheel of His righteous judgment will eventually turn to address every evil.
