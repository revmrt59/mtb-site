UTTER SHAME - ( qiqalon , Strong's H7022 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun qiqalon refers to extreme ignominy, disgraceful disgrace, or shameful spew. Appearing only in Habakkuk 2:16, it is a graphic term combining the ideas of "vomit" (qi) and "shame" (qalon) to depict the ultimate humiliation and degradation of the Babylonian empire. Instead of the enduring glory they sought through conquest, God promises that they will be utterly saturated with disgusting, shameful ruin, mirroring their own cruel treatment of other nations.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word qiqalon is an intensive compound or wordplay derived from two distinct elements: qi (meaning vomit) and qalon (meaning shame, dishonor, or disgrace). This creates a vivid, visceral word picture of "shameful vomit" or "disgraceful spew." In the ancient Near East, honor and shame were paramount values, and to be exposed in a state of drunken nakedness, covered in one's own vomit, was the absolute nadir of personal and national dignity.

The background is the grand banquet of Babylonian conquest. Babylon forced other nations to drink the cup of her wrath, exposing and humiliating them. God reverses this banquet, forcing Babylon to drink from the cup of His right hand, resulting in this "utter shame."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun qiqalon means extreme disgrace, great ignominy, shameful spew, or utter shame. It denotes a state of profound public humiliation and moral degradation where all prior honor is completely erased and replaced by something repulsive. The term emphasizes the graphic, physical nature of this disgrace, going beyond mere mental embarrassment to a state of absolute repulsiveness.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

This word is used as a poetic climax in the series of woes pronounced against Babylon. It stands in direct contrast to "glory" (kabod), which carries the weight of honor, beauty, and majesty.

The word picture is that of a drunken tyrant who, after forcing others into degradation, is forced to drink God's cup of judgment until he vomits on his own glory. The glory they built through violence becomes covered in the "shameful spew" of divine judgment.

4. Theological Implications

  • Divine Retribution (Lex Talionis): God’s judgment is perfectly fitted to the sin. Babylon made others drink to gaze on their nakedness; now Babylon must drink and be exposed.

  • The Futility of Human Glory: Human achievements and empires built on exploitation are inherently unstable. What looks like "glory" is quickly revealed to be nothing but "utter shame" when confronted by the holiness of God.

  • The Cup of Wrath: The cup of the Lord’s right hand is a frequent biblical metaphor for God’s stored-up anger against sin. To drink it completely leads to total loss of self-control and ultimate degradation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:16, the prophet addresses the Chaldeans (Babylonians), who have plundered nations to build their empire. The passage transitions from Babylon's active crime (forcing others to drink and exposing them) to their passive punishment (drinking the cup of the Lord’s right hand).

The phrase "utter shame will be on your glory" indicates that the very things Babylon boasted of—their grand palaces, secure walls, and vast conquests—will be utterly defiled. The Hebrew wordplay suggests that their "glory" (kabod) is literally covered in "shameful vomit" (qiqalon), transforming their pride into a disgusting spectacle.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because qiqalon (H7022) is a hapax legomenon (occurring only once in the Hebrew Bible in Habakkuk 2:16), the primary root word qalon (H7036), meaning shame, dishonor, or disgrace, must be examined to understand its usage.

  • Habakkuk 2:16 (Primary occurrence of H7022 and H7036): "You are filled with shame [qalon] instead of glory. You also—drink! And be exposed as uncircumcised! The cup of the LORD’s right hand will be turned against you, And utter shame [qiqalon] will be on your glory."

  • Proverbs 13:18: "Poverty and shame [qalon] will come to him who disdains correction, But he who regards a rebuke will be honored."

  • Hosea 4:7: "The more they increased, The more they sinned against Me; I will change their glory into shame [qalon]."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Dead Sea Scrolls (specifically the Habakkuk Commentary, 1QpHab), this passage is interpreted as referring to the "wicked priest" who pursued the Teacher of Righteousness and was ultimately struck down with illness and disgrace, reflecting the lasting impact of this vivid imagery on early Jewish interpretation.

The Septuagint (LXX) translates qiqalon as atimia (dishonor, disgrace), capturing the social and moral degradation, though losing the vivid "vomit" wordplay of the original Hebrew.
