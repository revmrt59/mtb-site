FILLED - ( saba , Strong's H7646 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word saba refers to being satisfied, sated, or filled to the point of excess. While it frequently describes the positive state of being physically satisfied with food and drink or spiritually fulfilled by God's goodness, it also carries a dark, ironic nuance. In contexts of judgment, it describes being gorged or oversated with something undesirable, such as shame, poverty, or wrath. In Habakkuk 2:16, the word highlights the tragic irony of the Chaldeans, who sought to satisfy their endless greed through conquest but instead became utterly bloated and drunk with their own public disgrace and God's impending judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of saba centers on the concept of fullness, satiety, and completion. In the ancient Near Eastern agrarian society, where hunger and famine were constant realities, being "filled" with food, water, or years of life was recognized as a direct sign of divine blessing, security, and favor. It is the feeling of having eaten a rich meal and wanting nothing more. However, when pushed to an extreme, the word picture shifts from healthy satisfaction to gluttony, bloating, and nausea. It evokes the graphic image of a person who has gorged themselves to the point of sickness. This negative trajectory is precisely what is utilized in prophetic literature to describe those who have overindulged in sin and are subsequently forced to gorge on judgment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb saba means to be sated, to be satisfied, or to have one's fill. The semantic range of this root includes:

  • Physical satiety: Eating and drinking to full satisfaction.

  • Spiritual or mental satisfaction: Being fully content with God's goodness, favor, or mercy.

  • Experiential fullness: Living a long, full life, often expressed idiomatically as being "full of days."

  • Excessive consumption or surfeit: Being gorged, overloaded, or overwhelmed with something negative, such as trouble, contempt, or shame.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs saba in three distinct theological and practical dimensions:

  • The Blessings of Covenant Obedience: God promises that if His people walk in His ways, they will eat and be satisfied. This physical satisfaction is linked directly to gratitude and worship.

  • The Danger of Self-Sufficiency: Satiety can lead to spiritual amnesia. When people are full, they are warned not to forget the Lord. Hosea warns that when the Israelites were fed, they became filled, and their hearts were exalted, leading them to forget their Creator.

  • The Cup of Wrath and Shame: In judgment passages, the wicked are forced to drink from the cup of God's wrath until they are "filled" or sated with humiliation. Instead of sweet wine, they gulp down ruin, resulting in spiritual and physical vomiting.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, saba reveals that human beings are designed with an inherent capacity for hunger and satisfaction. True satisfaction is a gift from God, found only in relationship with Him. When humanity attempts to satisfy this deep spiritual hunger with created things—such as wealth, power, or sensuality—the result is never true satisfaction. Instead, it leads to an unnatural, bloated state of greed that demands more but yields only corruption. Ultimately, God's holiness ensures that those who refuse to be satisfied with His righteousness will be forcefully satisfied with the consequences of their rebellion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:16, the prophet delivers a series of woes against the proud Babylonian empire. The Babylonians were insatiable; Habakkuk 2:5 notes that the arrogant nation "enlarges his desire as sheol, and he is like death, and cannot be satisfied." Because they refused to be satisfied through legitimate means and plundered nations to feed their greed, God pronounces a fitting judgment.

The phrase "You are filled with shame instead of glory" uses saba in the perfect tense, indicating a completed and settled state of ruin. The Babylonians sought glory through violent conquest. Instead of achieving lasting honor, they have gorged themselves on disgrace. God turns their self-indulgence upside down: they drank to excess at their victory feasts, so now they must drink the cup of the Lord's right hand—a metaphor for His inescapable judgment—until they are utterly drunk, exposed, and vomiting in shame. Their attempt to fill themselves with glory has left them bloated with eternal contempt.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 17:15: "As for me, I will see Your face in righteousness; I shall be satisfied when I awake in Your likeness."

  • Psalm 90:14: "Oh, satisfy us early with Your mercy, That we may rejoice and be glad all our days!"

  • Proverbs 14:14: "The backslider in heart will be filled with his own ways, And a good man will be satisfied from above."

  • Lamentations 3:30: "Let him give his cheek to the one who strikes him, And be full of reproach."

  • Hosea 13:6: "When they had pasture, they were filled; They were filled and their heart was exalted; Therefore they forgot Me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a powerful linguistic contrast in Habakkuk between the insatiable greed of the wicked and the ultimate destiny of the earth. While the proud oppressor cannot be satisfied (saba), Habakkuk 2:14 declares that "the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of the LORD." The wicked try to fill themselves with temporary riches and are filled with shame; conversely, the cosmos will be eternally filled with God's manifest presence. The passage also acts as a sobering warning to modern cultures driven by consumerism, showing that any attempt to find life's ultimate fulfillment apart from God will inevitably end in spiritual nausea.
