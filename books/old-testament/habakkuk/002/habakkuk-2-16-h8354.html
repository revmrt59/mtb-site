DRINK - ( shathah , Strong's H8354 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shathah literally means to imbibe a liquid, but in the context of prophetic judgment, it becomes a sobering metaphor for consuming God's wrath. In Habakkuk 2:16, the wicked Babylonian empire, which had forced other nations to drink the cup of its violent fury, is commanded by Yahweh to drink also. This acts as a divine turning of the tables, where the cup of the Lord’s right hand is passed to them, forcing them to ingest their own shame and receive righteous judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

Drinking is an essential human action for survival and a central feature of communal life, hospitality, and covenant-making in the ancient Near East. Sharing a drink signifies peace, acceptance, and fellowship. Conversely, in the context of conflict, forcing captives to drink excessive alcohol to expose their nakedness represents the ultimate form of degradation, conquest, and public humiliation. In the prophetic imagination, the act of drinking is elevated from a physical necessity to a spiritual and judicial reality, where the liquid consumed represents the consequences of one's ethical choices.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shathah is a primary Hebrew root meaning to drink, to swallow, to imbibe, or to participate in a banquet. It refers primarily to the physical consumption of liquids such as water, wine, or milk. Metaphorically, it describes absorbing or taking in abstract realities, such as suffering, violence, wisdom, or divine judgment. The word is used over 200 times in the Old Testament, spanning narrative, wisdom, and prophetic literature, and is frequently paired with the verb akal (to eat) to describe the fullness of human consumption and feasting.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Physical Sustenance: It designates the daily intake of fluids necessary for life, such as drinking water from a well or wine from a vineyard.

  • Feasting and Celebration: It is used to describe communal banquets, religious festivals, and covenant celebrations where drinking wine symbolizes joy, abundance, and divine favor.

  • Spiritual and Moral Ingestion: It represents deeply internalizing a reality. For example, the wicked are described as drinking iniquity like water, or drinking the "wine of violence," meaning they sustain their lives through exploitation and oppression.

  • The Cup of Wrath: The most intense metaphorical use involves drinking from the cup of God's fury. Here, the drinking is involuntary and leads to staggering, madness, and ruin.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of shathah in prophetic literature centers on the absolute sovereignty of God in judgment. God is depicted as the host of a feast, but for the wicked, the beverage He serves is the cup of His holy indignation. This represents the law of retribution (lex talionis)—what a nation sows, it must also reap. The cup of violence that Babylon forced upon others must now be drunk by Babylon itself. The theological truth is clear: no nation or individual can escape the moral consequences of their actions; they will eventually be made to drink the cup of their own making under the righteous judgment of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:16, the prophet addresses the prideful Babylonian empire under the fifth "woe" (verses 15–17). Babylon had shamelessly intoxicated neighboring nations, destabilizing them to gloat over their ruin and "nakedness." In a dramatic reversal of fortunes, Yahweh commands Babylon, "You also drink!" (shathah is used here in the imperative). The cup of God's right hand is turned toward them. Instead of drinking for pleasure or triumph, Babylon is forced to drink to their own shame. The physical act of drinking this cup of judgment results in their own shameful exposure, transforming their earthly glory into utter disgrace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 9:21 - "Then he drank of the wine and was drunk, and was uncovered in his tent."

  • Exodus 34:28 - "So he was there with the LORD forty days and forty nights; he neither ate bread nor drank water."

  • Proverbs 4:17 - "For they eat the bread of wickedness, And drink the wine of violence."

  • Isaiah 51:17 - "Awake, awake! Stand up, O Jerusalem, You who have drunk at the hand of the LORD The cup of His fury; You have drunk the dregs of the cup of trembling, And drained it out."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The prophetic image of drinking the cup of wrath in the Old Testament is the essential theological background for the New Testament's description of Christ's suffering. In the Garden of Gethsemane, Jesus agonized over the "cup" He was about to drink. On the cross, Jesus drank the full cup of God's righteous wrath against sin—the ultimate fulfillment of this metaphorical drinking—so that those who believe in Him might instead drink the cup of salvation and the water of eternal life.
