VIOLENCE - ( chamas , Strong's H2555 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chamas refers to cold-blooded, unethical, and destructive violence, ranging from physical cruelty and social oppression to moral injustice and systemic wrongdoing. In Habakkuk 2:17, it captures the devastating, ecological, and societal ruin brought by the Babylonian empire, warning that the very violence they inflicted on God's creation and His people will ultimately return to overwhelm and consume them under divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word chamas is not limited to mere physical force; it represents a deep violation of moral, legal, and covenantal boundaries. It is the raw, unrestrained exercise of power that crushes the weak and disrupts the divine order of shalom (peace, wholeness, and justice). The word evokes the image of a wild beast tearing its prey apart or a destructive storm sweeping away everything in its path. Historically, this term first appears globally in the narrative of Noah's flood, where the moral decay of humanity is summarized by the fact that the earth was "filled with violence." The background of the word indicates a willful, insolent disregard for God’s law, resulting in chaos, ruin, and the defilement of the land.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun chamas denotes wrong, violence, injustice, cruelty, damage, and ethical wrong. The semantic range spans from physical plunder and bloodguilt to judicial corruption and systemic social oppression. It is the direct antithesis of mishpat (justice) and tsedaqah (righteousness). When individuals or nations engage in chamas, they are actively tearing down the ethical fabric of society, replacing divine law with the law of the jungle, where might makes right.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, chamas is described through several vivid conceptual associations:

  • Physical Devastation: It is used of military invaders who strip lands, burn cities, and slaughter populations without mercy.

  • Systemic Oppression: It characterizes corrupt leaders, wealthy elites, and false witnesses who use legal and financial systems to exploit the poor, seize lands, and deny justice to the vulnerable.

  • Ethical and Spiritual Rebellion: The term is often paired with terms for deceit and falsehood, demonstrating that violence and lies are companion sins. It represents a heart attitude of self-deification, where human desire is elevated above God's commands.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, chamas is deeply offensive to God. As the Creator and Sustainer of life, He is the defender of the weak and the ultimate judge of systemic cruelty. Scripture establishes a clear principle of poetic justice regarding chamas: those who sow violence will inevitably reap violence. The law of retribution ensures that the wicked fall into the pit they dug for others. Furthermore, chamas has ecological consequences; it does not only damage human relationships but defiles the physical earth itself, causing the land to groan under the weight of human sin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:17, the word chamas is used twice: "For the violence done to Lebanon will cover you, and the plunder of beasts which made them afraid, because of men's blood and the violence to the land and the city, and all who dwell in it."

In this context, God is pronouncing a woe of judgment upon the Babylonian empire. The Babylonians were notorious for their scorched-earth military campaigns. To build their grand temples and palaces, they stripped the majestic cedar forests of Lebanon (ecological chamas), slaughtered the wild beasts for sport and resource, and brutally murdered the inhabitants of the cities they conquered (human chamas).

The specific nuance here is one of ironic, divine retribution. The "violence done to Lebanon" is personified; it will "cover" or overwhelm the Babylonians. The very destruction they brought upon the natural world and human societies will act as a flood that returns to drown them. Their prideful abuse of creation and humanity will become the instrument of their own ruin.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 6:11: "The earth also was corrupt before God, and the earth was filled with violence."

  • Psalm 11:5: "The Lord tests the righteous, But the wicked and the one who loves violence His soul hates."

  • Proverbs 4:17: "For they eat the bread of wickedness, And drink the wine of violence."

  • Ezekiel 8:17: "And He said to me, 'Have you seen this, O son of man? Is it a trivial thing to the house of Judah to commit the abominations which they commit here? For they have filled the land with violence; then they have returned to provoke Me to anger. Indeed they put the branch to their nose.'"

  • Habakkuk 1:2: "O Lord, how long shall I cry, And You will not hear? Even cry out to You, 'Violence!' And You will not save."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the ancient Near East, crying out "Violence!" (chamas) was a formal, legal appeal for help, similar to screaming "Murder!" or "Help!" in a modern city. When Habakkuk cries out "Violence!" in Habakkuk 1:2, he is bringing a covenant lawsuit before God against the injustice running rampant in Judah. By the time we reach Habakkuk 2:17, God reverses the focus. The global outcry of chamas against Babylon's imperial brutality has reached the courtroom of heaven. God's response guarantees that no act of systemic cruelty goes unnoticed or unpunished.
