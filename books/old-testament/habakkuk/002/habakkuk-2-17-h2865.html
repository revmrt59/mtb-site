AFRAID - ( chathath, Strong's H2865 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb chathath carries the primary meaning of being broken, shattered, or dismayed. In contexts of fear, it describes a deep, paralyzing terror that breaks a person's spirit or resolve, leaving them completely undone. In Habakkuk 2:17, it depicts the overwhelming panic and helplessness experienced by the beasts of Lebanon under the destructive onslaught of the Babylonian invaders, illustrating how the wicked bring devastating ruin not only upon humanity but upon God's entire creation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical background of chathath lies in the realm of shattering or cracking open. It is used in classical Hebrew to describe a physical object that is broken into pieces, such as a warrior’s bow that has been snapped, or soil that has cracked and dried up during a severe drought. When applied to the human mind and emotions, chathath functions as a vivid word picture of psychological collapse. Just as a hammer shatters a pottery vessel, traumatic events, looming danger, or the judgment of God can shatter a person's internal strength, leaving their courage, confidence, and resolve in ruins.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of chathath spans both physical destruction and emotional terror:

  • In the Qal stem, it means to be shattered, broken, dismayed, or terrified.

  • In the Niphal stem, it denotes being broken down, filled with dread, or discouraged.

  • In the Piel stem, it means to terrify, dismay, or cause panic.

  • In the Hiphil stem—the specific verbal form used in Habakkuk 2:17—it means to shatter, break, terrify, or put to flight.

The word goes beyond simple nervousness or respect. It denotes a paralyzing dread that robs the recipient of the ability to resist or flee constructively.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, chathath is frequently paired with other terms of fear, such as yare (to fear) or pachad (to dread).

  • It is most famously used in the negative imperative command, "do not be afraid, nor be dismayed," which was spoken to leaders like Joshua and the nation of Israel before battles. In those contexts, to be "dismayed" (chathath) is to have one's spirit broken before the battle even begins.

  • It is also used to describe the undoing of nations under divine judgment. When God marches out in judgment, the pagan armies do not merely lose; their hearts shatter within them, and their military structure collapses under the weight of supernatural panic.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, chathath reveals the true source of human strength and security. Human courage is fragile and easily shattered when confronted with the reality of sin, judgment, and cosmic upheaval.

  • Only those whose trust is anchored in Yahweh can remain unshattered when the earth gives way.

  • The term underscores the terrifying nature of God’s holiness and judgment. When God acts against wickedness, the proud and violent who once terrified others are themselves shattered by dread.

  • It demonstrates that sin has systemic, far-reaching consequences that shatter the peace (shalom) of the non-human creation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:17, the prophet pronounces a woe against the Babylonian Empire for its ruthless, imperialistic violence. The text states: "For the violence done to Lebanon will cover you, And the plunder of beasts which made them afraid..."

  • The Babylonians did not merely conquer cities; they committed ecological warfare. They cleared the majestic cedar forests of Lebanon for their building projects and slaughtered the wildlife for sport and resources.

  • The Hiphil form of chathath in this verse indicates that the plunder of the beasts actively "shattered" or "terrified" them. The wild animals of Lebanon were thrown into a state of absolute, traumatized panic as their habitats were demolished and their populations slaughtered.

  • The nuance here is one of innocent suffering. The violence of the wicked does not stop with human victims; it overflows to abuse and terrify the natural world. However, God takes notice of this ecological violence, and the very terror the Babylonians inflicted upon creation will return to shatter them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 1:21: "Look, the LORD your God has set the land before you; go up and possess it, as the LORD God of your fathers has spoken to you; do not fear or be dismayed."

  • Joshua 1:9: "Have I not commanded you? Be strong and of good courage; do not be afraid, nor be dismayed, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go."

  • 1 Samuel 2:4: "The bows of the mighty men are broken, And those who stumbled are girded with strength."

  • Jeremiah 1:17: "Therefore prepare yourself and arise, And speak to them all that I command you. Do not be dismayed before their faces, Lest I dismay you before them."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), chathath in Habakkuk 2:17 is translated using terms that emphasize deep, inner disturbance and frightening panic. This aligns with the biblical understanding that the outer world and the inner life are deeply connected. When a creature’s physical environment is ravaged, its internal world is shattered. This connection serves as a warning to pastors and teachers about the deep, lingering trauma that oppression and violence leave on the minds of those who survive it.
