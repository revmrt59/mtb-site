DWELL - ( yashab , Strong's H3427 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yashab primarily means to sit, remain, dwell, or inhabit. It conveys a sense of settled presence, permanence, and established residence rather than a temporary or nomadic stay. In the prophetic context of Habakkuk 2:17, it refers to the permanent inhabitants of the land and cities who suffer under the oppressive violence of the Chaldean empire. The word underscores the tragic disruption of peaceful, settled life by ruthless conquerors, highlighting God's ultimate judgment on those who violate the sacred order of human habitation and security.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical root of yashab means "to sit down," as on a chair, a seat of honor, or a throne. From this physical posture of sitting, the word expands semantically to sitting in a place permanently—hence, to dwell, inhabit, or settle down. It represents the transition from a nomadic, wandering lifestyle (like tent-dwelling) to an established, stable lifestyle (living in a house, city, or land). In the Ancient Near East, sitting also carried connotations of authority, as a king sits on his throne or a judge sits to render a verdict. Thus, the word picture moves from a physical posture of rest to an established state of peace, order, and authority within a specific territory.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb occurs over 1,000 times in the Old Testament, representing a broad semantic range centered on presence and duration:

  • To sit down or remain seated.

  • To dwell, abide, or inhabit a home, city, or country.

  • To remain, stay, or tarry in a place.

  • To be inhabited or populated (referring to cities or lands).

  • To sit enthroned (referring to kings, judges, or God Himself).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The term is used to contrast wandering with settling. For example, Israel’s wilderness wandering was a period of not "dwelling" in the Promised Land, whereas entering Canaan meant finally "dwelling" in safety.

  • It is used of God "dwelling" in the heavens or in the temple, demonstrating His settled covenant presence with His people.

  • It represents a state of peace and security, where one can "sit under his vine and under his fig tree" without fear.

  • Conversely, when a land is judged, it is described as "without inhabitant" (yashab), meaning its settled population has been destroyed, displaced, or exiled.

4. Theological Implications

Human habitation is part of God's design for creation. God did not create the earth to be a chaotic void, but formed it to be inhabited (yashab). Living or dwelling in a place implies stewardship, community, and relationship. The "dwellers" of the earth are accountable to God for how they treat the land and one another. God's covenant blessings include the promise of secure dwelling, while His judgment often targets the "dwellers" of the land because they have polluted it through violence and idolatry. When a foreign power violently uproots those who dwell in a land, they are actively dismantling God's established order of peace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:17, the prophet pronounces a woe against Babylon (the Chaldeans) for their devastating violence: "For the violence done to Lebanon will cover you, And the plunder of beasts which made them afraid, Because of men’s blood And the violence of the land and the city, And of all who dwell in it."

  • The word yashab here emphasizes the vulnerability of those who had established peaceful, settled lives in these regions. They were not combatants or invaders; they were the local, settled population who simply wished to live in quietness.

  • Babylon's sweeping violence shattered this stability. By targeting "all who dwell in it," the invaders violated the fundamental human right to peaceful habitation and disrupted the creation order.

  • God promises that the violence done to Lebanon and its peaceful inhabitants will return upon Babylon's own head, restoring justice to those whose settled lives were ruthlessly destroyed.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 13:12: "Abram dwelt in the land of Canaan, and Lot dwelt in the cities of the plain and pitched his tent even as far as Sodom."

  • Leviticus 25:18: "So you shall observe My statutes and keep My judgments, and perform them; and you will dwell in the land in safety."

  • Psalm 23:6: "Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me All the days of my life; And I will dwell in the house of the LORD Forever."

  • Psalm 91:1: "He who dwells in the secret place of the Most High Shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty."

  • Psalm 133:1: "Behold, how good and how pleasant it is For brethren to dwell together in unity!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The word yashab is often paired with verbs of movement (like halak, "to walk") to describe the totality of a person's life and conduct, such as in Psalm 1:1 ("nor sit [yashab] in the seat of the scornful").

  • In the Septuagint (LXX), yashab is frequently translated by oikeo (to dwell), katoikeo (to inhabit/settle), or kathemai (to sit). In Habakkuk 2:17, the LXX uses katoikountas ("those who dwell"), highlighting the permanent residence of the victims of Babylon's oppression.
