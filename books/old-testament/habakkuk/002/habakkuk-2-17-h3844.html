LEBANON - ( lebanon , Strong's H3844 )

Summary:

The proper noun lebanon refers to the famous mountain range on the northern border of Israel, renowned in the ancient world for its majestic snow-capped peaks and massive, valuable cedar forests. In biblical imagery, Lebanon represents glory, strength, pride, and natural abundance. In Habakkuk 2:17, "the violence done to Lebanon" refers to the ecological devastation and resource exploitation wrought by the Babylonian empire, which historically stripped the forests of Lebanon for its massive building projects. God declares that this rapacious exploitation of His creation, alongside the slaughter of humanity, will ultimately rebound upon the heads of the oppressors in divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The name lebanon is derived from the Semitic root lbn, meaning "white." This is a direct reference to the snow-capped summits of the Lebanon mountain range, which dominated the northern horizon of Israel, and the white limestone cliffs that characterize the geography.

  • Lebanon was famous throughout the Ancient Near East for its dense, towering forests of cedar trees (Cedrus libani). These trees grew up to 120 feet tall and were highly prized for their durability, fragrance, and resistance to decay and insects.

  • Because of these characteristics, Lebanon's cedars were the premier luxury building material of the ancient world. Kings of Assyria, Babylon, Egypt, and Israel (such as David and Solomon) regularly imported or plundered Lebanese cedar for palaces, temples, and shipbuilding.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Lebanon (transliterated lebanon) functions as a proper noun designating the twin mountain ranges (Lebanon and Anti-Lebanon) and the region they encompass north of Galilee.

  • The word is used consistently in the Old Testament to denote this specific geographical region, but it frequently carries deep symbolic weight. It represents the height of natural beauty, majesty, fertility, and wealth.

  • In poetic and prophetic literature, Lebanon is synonymous with human pride, royal grandeur, and the finest things the earth has to offer. Conversely, the destruction of Lebanon’s forests serves as a vivid picture of divine judgment, humiliation, and the stripping away of earthly glory.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • A Symbol of Pride and Power: Because of the towering height of its cedars, Lebanon represents lofty pride. Prophetic passages frequently depict God cutting down the "cedars of Lebanon" as an act of humbling arrogant rulers and nations who view themselves as untouchable.

  • A Source of Wealth and Temple Beauty: Solomon made extensive use of Lebanon's timber to construct the First Temple and his own royal palace, known as the "House of the Forest of Lebanon." Here, the word is associated with sacred beauty, divine presence, and royal splendor.

  • A Picture of Fragrance and Fertility: The prophets use the scent of Lebanon’s forests, its abundant streams, and its wine to illustrate the spiritual renewal, beauty, and fruitfulness of a restored Israel.

4. Theological Implications

  • Creation's Relationship to God: Creation is not a neutral stage; it belongs to Yahweh. The destruction of Lebanon’s ecosystem by greedy empires is treated by God as a moral offense. God holds human rulers accountable for how they treat the earth and its resources.

  • The Limit of Arrogant Empire: The exploitation of Lebanon's resources is a physical manifestation of imperial hubris. Empires believe they can strip-mine God's creation for their own self-glorification, but God's sovereign rule ensures that those who destroy the earth will themselves be judged.

  • The Law of Retribution (Lex Talionis): The violence done to Lebanon will "cover" or overwhelm the perpetrator. God's justice is symmetrical; the exact nature of the sin (violence and destruction) becomes the instrument of the sinner's ruin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 2:17, the prophet pronounces a woe against the Chaldeans (Babylonians). The verse contrasts the "violence done to Lebanon" and the "plunder of beasts" with "men’s blood" and "violence to the land."

  • The Chaldean military machine did not merely kill people; it devastated the environment. When the Babylonians invaded the Levant, they chopped down the pristine cedar forests of Lebanon to build siege works, military infrastructure, and lavish temples and palaces in Babylon.

  • The "violence to Lebanon" is both literal and figurative. Literally, it is the ecological rape of the land. Figuratively, Lebanon represents the glory of the region that Babylon stripped away. God declares that the very violence Babylon unleashed on these majestic forests and the animals dwelling in them will return to terrify and overwhelm them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 29:5 - "The voice of the LORD breaks the cedars; Yes, the LORD splinters the cedars of Lebanon."

  • Isaiah 2:13 - "Upon all the cedars of Lebanon that are high and lifted up, And upon all the oaks of Bashan;"

  • Isaiah 14:8 - "Indeed the cypress trees rejoice over you, And the cedars of Lebanon, saying, 'Since you were cut down, No woodsman has come up against us.'"

  • Hosea 14:6 - "His branches shall spread; His beauty shall be like an olive tree, And his fragrance like Lebanon."

  • Zechariah 11:1 - "Open your doors, O Lebanon, That fire may devour your cedars."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Historical records from antiquity confirm Habakkuk's description. In the famous East India House Inscription, Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon boasts of his exploits in Lebanon, detailing how he cut through the mountains to transport the massive cedars to Babylon to decorate his palace and the temple of Marduk.

  • What Nebuchadnezzar recorded as an act of supreme royal triumph, God's prophet recorded as an act of violent, criminal exploitation that demanded divine vengeance. This highlights the stark contrast between the world's standard of glory (conquest and consumption) and God's standard of justice (stewardship and respect for life).
