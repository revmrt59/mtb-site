BEASTS - ( behemah , Strong's H0929 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word behemah refers broadly to beasts, quadrupeds, cattle, or wild animals. In Habakkuk 2:17, it specifically denotes the wild animals of the Lebanon region that were violently displaced, terrorized, and decimated by the invading Babylonian military machine. The term highlights the ecological devastation wrought by human pride and greed, demonstrating that God's judgment extends to how nations treat His non-human creation and natural habitats.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • Behemah represents four-footed land animals as distinct from mankind, birds, or reptiles. In its singular form, it often denotes domestic cattle or livestock, but in its plural form (behemoth, as used in Habakkuk 2:17), it frequently represents large wild beasts of the forest and field. The background of Habakkuk 2:17 is the ruthless military campaign of the Neo-Babylonian (Chaldean) empire. In their insatiable quest for imperial dominance, the Babylonians did not merely slaughter human populations; they also stripped the famous cedar forests of Lebanon for their massive building projects in Babylon, utterly destroying the natural habitat of the wild beasts residing there. The word picture is one of helpless, innocent creatures fleeing in terror from the noise, axes, and weapons of the ruthless invaders.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Behemah refers to any large, four-footed land animal. The semantic range includes:

  • Domesticated animals, cattle, or livestock (as opposed to wild beasts).

  • Wild beasts of the earth (when contrasted with domestic animals or mankind).

  • Beasts in general, representing the animal kingdom apart from birds, fish, and creeping things.

In Habakkuk 2:17, the plural form denotes wild beasts that inhabited the forested regions of Lebanon.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • In the creation narrative, behemah is used for the land animals created on the sixth day alongside humanity, signifying their close connection to human stewardship.

  • In the context of judgment, the terrorization of behemah represents total societal and environmental collapse. The prophets often depict the land of a judged nation returning to a wild, untamed state where only wild beasts dwell, or conversely, as in Habakkuk, where even the wild beasts are destroyed by the senseless violence of human conquerors.

  • The physical devastation of these beasts serves as a physical witness to the moral depravity of the conquerors. Nature itself groans under the weight of human sin.

4. Theological Implications

  • God’s Concern for Creation: God holds empires accountable not only for human-on-human violence but also for the wanton destruction of His creation, including the animal kingdom.

  • The Ripple Effect of Sin: Human sin and hubris have devastating consequences that spill over into the physical environment and animal life. The suffering of the beasts of Lebanon is a direct result of Babylonian greed.

  • Cosmic Justice: The judgment of God is comprehensive. Because Babylon plundered the forests and terrorized the beasts, God’s retributive justice (lex talionis) would cause the very violence they inflicted to "cover" or overwhelm them.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:17, "the plunder of beasts which made them afraid" parallels "the violence done to Lebanon." The Babylonians logged the massive cedars of Lebanon to build palaces and temples, and in doing so, they slaughtered and scattered the animal population (behemah). The word "plunder" (sod) refers to violent destruction or devastation. The text indicates that the cruelty inflicted on these animals—terrifying and destroying them—has been noted by God. This environmental violence is placed on the same moral plane as "men's blood" and "violence to the land."

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:24: "Then God said, 'Let the earth bring forth the living creature according to its kind: cattle and creeping thing and beast of the earth, each according to its kind'; and it was so."

  • Genesis 6:7: "So the Lord said, 'I will destroy man whom I have created from the face of the earth, both man and beast, creeping thing and birds of the air, for I am sorry that I have made them.'"

  • Psalm 36:6: "Your righteousness is like the great mountains; Your judgments are a great deep; O Lord, You preserve man and beast."

  • Joel 1:18: "How the beasts groan! The herds of cattle are restless, Because they have no pasture; Even the flocks of sheep suffer punishment."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The plural form of behemah is behemoth, which is used in Job 40:15 as a proper noun to describe a primeval, colossal creature of unmatched power, showcasing God's absolute sovereignty over the grandest elements of creation.

  • Ancient Near Eastern kings frequently boasted on monumental inscriptions of their hunting prowess and their destruction of forests and wildlife as symbols of their total domination. Habakkuk’s prophecy serves as a divine polemic against these boasts, turning their acts of environmental vandalism into grounds for their own destruction.
