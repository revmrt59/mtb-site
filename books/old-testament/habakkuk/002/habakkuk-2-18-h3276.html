PROFIT - ( ya'al , Strong's H3276 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ya'al denotes the concept of yielding a profit, benefit, or useful result. It is used in the Old Testament to evaluate the functional worth of an action, alliance, or object. In prophetic literature, ya'al serves as a key term of contrast, demonstrating that while Yahweh actively saves and delivers His people, false idols and foreign alliances are entirely useless, yielding absolutely no benefit or deliverance in times of crisis.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of ya'al carries an ancient connection to ascending, gaining high ground, or reaching a place of advantage. In practical usage, it transitioned into a term of utility and transaction. It paints a picture of a traveler climbing to a high vantage point for safety, or a merchant realizing a tangible gain from an investment. When an action or an object is said to ya'al, it means it successfully lifts the person out of a deficit, provides security, or achieves its intended purpose. Conversely, when it fails to ya'al, it represents an utter waste of resources, leaving the person exposed, empty-handed, and disappointed.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • ya'al occurs exclusively in the Hiphil (causative) stem in the Old Testament.

  • It means to gain benefit, find utility, or achieve usefulness.

  • It describes the act of producing a positive, advantageous, or valuable outcome.

  • When negated with lo ("not"), it means to be entirely useless, unprofitable, or unable to deliver. It denotes a complete failure to assist or rescue when help is most needed.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • ya'al appears 33 times in the Hebrew Bible, clustered heavily in the prophetic books of Isaiah and Jeremiah. The prophets repeatedly employ the word to depict the spiritual tragedy of Israel's backsliding.

  • The Broken Transaction: The word is used to expose the foolish transaction of idolatry. Worshipers sacrifice their finest gold, silver, and energy to craft an idol, expecting protection and blessing in return. The prophets use ya'al to show that the ledger of idolatry always ends in bankruptcy; the dead idol cannot pay back the worshiper's investment.

  • The Worthless Ally: Israel frequently sought military alliances with Egypt or Assyria rather than trusting in God. The prophets declared that these nations would not ya'al (benefit or help) them in the day of judgment. They were broken reeds that would pierce the hand of anyone who leaned on them.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, ya'al establishes a clear boundary between the Creator and the created.

  • True profit, life, and deliverance belong solely to Yahweh. He is the only source of genuine benefit. He teaches His people to profit, guiding them in the way they should go.

  • Idolatry is not merely a theological error; it is a profound failure of wisdom. It is the pursuit of "empty things" that cannot save because they are nothing. The use of ya'al appeals to human rationality, asking the worshiper to look at the practical fruit of their devotion. If a god cannot hear, speak, or deliver, it is functionally worthless.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:18, the prophet pronounces a series of woes against the proud and ruthless Chaldeans (Babylonians). The Chaldeans conquered nations by their own military strength, which they attributed to their false gods.

Habakkuk asks a penetrating rhetorical question: "What profit is the image...?" The word ho'il (a form of ya'al) exposes the utter helplessness of the Babylonian pantheon.

  • The maker carves a piece of wood or molds metal, investing creative energy into a lifeless object, and then turns around to "trust in it."

  • Habakkuk mocks this dependency. The idol is a "teacher of lies" because it promises help, security, and "profit" that it cannot possibly deliver. When the sovereign judgment of Yahweh falls upon Babylon, their masterfully crafted idols will provide zero assistance. The passage reveals the ultimate irony: the maker is greater than the thing made, yet he worships it, expecting a dead thing to give him life and profit.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 11:4 - "Riches do not profit in the day of wrath, But righteousness delivers from death."

  • Isaiah 44:10 - "Who would mold a god or cast a carved image That profits him nothing?"

  • Jeremiah 2:11 - "Has a nation changed its gods, Which are not gods? But My people have changed their Glory For what does not profit."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The conceptual truth of ya'al is mirrored in the New Testament Greek word opheleo, which also means to profit or benefit. Jesus uses this same logical appeal in Mark 8:36: "For what will it profit a man if he gains the whole world, and loses his own soul?" Just as Habakkuk exposed the worthlessness of trusting in physical idols that cannot profit, Jesus exposes the ultimate futility of pursuing worldly security at the expense of one's eternal relationship with God. Both testaments urge the believer to invest their trust only in that which yields eternal, unchanging value.
