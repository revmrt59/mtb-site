MOLD - ( yetser , Strong's H3336 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word yetser refers to something formed, framed, or fashioned, representing both concrete physical objects and the abstract intentions, thoughts, or inclinations of the human heart. In the specific context of Habakkuk 2:18, it denotes the physical form, shape, or mold of a carved idol. The profound irony highlighted by the prophet is that the craftsman trusts in his own fabricated "mold"—a lifeless creation of his own hands—rather than trusting in the living Creator who formed him. This word exposes the self-deception of idolatry, where the creature attempts to play the role of the creator.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun yetser is derived from the root verb yatsar, which means to squeeze, mold, shape, or fashion. This verb is most commonly used for the work of a potter who takes raw, formless clay and patiently molds it into a vessel of his choosing. In the ancient Near East, the potter's wheel was a vivid illustration of sovereignty, design, and purposeful creation. Therefore, the noun yetser carries the inherent idea of a relationship between a designer and the design. It is the concrete outcome of artistic or functional shaping—the "thing formed" or the "mold" that gives structure to an object.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun represents both physical and mental concepts, depending on the context:

  • Physical/Concrete: It means a physical form, a molded object, or a framework. It is the literal structure or shape given to an item by a craftsman, such as a piece of pottery or a carved image.

  • Mental/Abstract: It refers to the inner framework of the mind, including thoughts, imaginations, purposes, intentions, and moral inclinations. Just as a potter molds clay, the human heart molds thoughts and plans.

  • In Habakkuk 2:18, the word is used in its concrete sense to denote the physical form or molded shape of an idol, specifically translated as "mold" in the phrase "the maker of its mold" (yotser yitsro).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament utilizes yetser to draw a powerful contrast between the physical and the spiritual:

  • The Potter and the Clay: The physical word picture is starkly drawn in passages where the clay pot represents human weakness and dependency. The shaped vessel (yetser) has no right to question the potter (yotsar) who gave it form.

  • The Inner Frame of Humanity: The word is used spiritually to describe the moral orientation of the human heart. After the Fall, the "intent" or "imagination" (yetser) of humanity's thoughts was only evil continually. God understands the very framework of our thinking, recognizing our human frailty and our propensity to drift away from Him.

  • The Steadfast Disposition: When a person's inner framework (yetser) is fixed or anchored upon Yahweh, it results in perfect, unshakeable peace.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of yetser rests on the Creator-creature distinction. God is the ultimate Yotser (the Potter/Former) who took dust and formed (yatsar) humanity. Thus, humanity is God's yetser (His formed creation).

Idolatry represents a catastrophic reversal of this divine order. In idolatry, the human being—who is merely formed clay—seeks to become the creator (yotser) of a god, giving it a physical form or "mold" (yetser). This is a theological absurdity. The creature attempts to breathe life into something he has manufactured, investing trust in a mute, helpless object. This highlights the foolishness of sin: it is the worship of the work of our own hands rather than the Creator who is blessed forever.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2, God pronounces five "woes" against the proud and rapacious Babylonian empire. The final woe (verses 18-20) exposes the ultimate folly of their national security: their trust in false gods.

In verse 18, Habakkuk asks, "What profit is the image, that its maker should carve it...?" The prophet then employs a brilliant Hebrew wordplay: yotser yitsro—"the fashioner of his fashioning" or "the maker of its mold."

By using yetser here, the text emphasizes that the idol is entirely dependent on its maker for its very shape and existence. The craftsman has to carve, solder, and mold the image. The nuance is bitingly sarcastic: the pagan worshiper is trusting in the very structure ("mold") that he himself designed and built. The maker is superior to the thing made, yet the maker bows down to his own "mold," expecting a mute, lifeless block of wood or stone to speak, guide, and deliver him.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In later post-biblical and Rabbinic theology, the word yetser became central to understanding human nature and ethics. Rabbinic literature developed the doctrine of the two inclinations within every human being: the yetser ha-ra (the evil inclination or impulse) and the yetser ha-tov (the good inclination or impulse). This theological framework suggests that human beings are constantly caught in a moral struggle between these two internal forces. While this fully developed dualism is not explicitly detailed in the Old Testament text, it grew directly out of the biblical usage of yetser as the internal, moral "framework" or "disposition" of the human heart that requires alignment with God's law.
