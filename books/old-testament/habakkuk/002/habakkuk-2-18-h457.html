IDOLS - ( 'elil , Strong's H0457 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun 'elil refers to an idol, false god, or something of utter worthlessness and insignificance. Derived from a root meaning weak, deficient, or nothing, the term serves as a scathing theological wordplay on 'el (the mighty God). While Yahweh is the living, all-powerful Creator, 'elilim are powerless, manufactured non-entities that cannot act or speak. In Habakkuk 2:18, the word highlights the tragic absurdity of human beings trusting in silent, hand-carved representations of nothingness rather than the sovereign Lord of the universe.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The background of 'elil lies in its deliberate, mocking contrast with the true God of Israel. In the ancient Near East, nations boasted of the grandeur and physical majesty of their pantheons, crafting massive, gold-covered statues to house the spirits of their deities. To counter this, the Hebrew Scriptures employ the term 'elil as a linguistic deconstruction of pagan religion.

The word sounds remarkably similar to 'el (the standard word for God or a mighty one), but it is likely connected to the negative particle 'al ("not" or "nothing") or a root meaning "weak" or "feeble." By calling these pagan gods 'elilim, Hebrew writers are engaging in a devastating pun: they are not "mighty ones" ('elim), but "worthless non-entities" ('elilim). They are literally "not-gods" or "un-gods." The visual background is that of a hollow shell—impressive on the outside due to human craft, but entirely devoid of substance, breath, or utility on the inside.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, 'elil denotes worthlessness, vanity, helplessness, and nothingness. When applied to pagan deities, it means "insignificant false gods" or "idols of no value." The term characterizes the objects of pagan worship not merely as false options, but as absolute vacuums of power and reality.

The plural form 'elilim represents the physical statues and the spiritual realities behind them as completely bankrupt. Rather than possessing independent divine power, these entities are defined by their utter inability to affect history, answer prayer, or provide guidance.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, the term is used to strip away the illusion of power from foreign deities. It is used in several vivid word pictures:

  • The Breathless Statue: It represents silver and gold shaped by human hands, requiring humans to carry it because it cannot walk.

  • The Worthless Physician / Shepherd: In its non-religious usage, the term describes anything that fails to perform its basic function. For example, job's friends are called "worthless ('elil) physicians" (Job 13:4), and a negligent leader is called a "worthless ('elil) shepherd" (Zechariah 11:17). The core picture is something that promises help but delivers absolute failure.

  • The Teacher of Lies: Idols are associated with falsehood. They act as "teachers of lies" because their very existence promises a divine presence and power that they simply do not possess.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'elil establishes a strict, uncompromising monotheism. It teaches that there is no middle ground between Yahweh and the gods of the nations; the gods of the nations are not lesser spiritual powers deserving of secondary honor, but are absolute non-entities.

This term exposes the spiritual pathology of idolatry. Humans are created in the image of God to worship the Creator. When they turn to 'elilim, they invert the created order: the creator (man) worships his own creation (the idol). This spiritual blindness results in the dehumanization of the worshiper, as those who make them and trust in them eventually become just like them—blind, deaf, and spiritually dead.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:18, the prophet delivers a series of divine "woes" against the proud, aggressive Babylonian empire. The Babylonians attributed their military success to their false gods. However, Habakkuk exposes the sheer foolishness of their theology.

The context of Habakkuk 2:18 pairs 'elilim with the adjective 'illemim ("mute" or "silent"). This is a deliberate, highly sarcastic rhyming wordplay: 'elilim 'illemim—"silent non-entities." The prophet asks, "What profit is the image, that its maker should carve it?" The maker trusts in his own creation to make "mute idols."

The nuance here is the absolute silence of the Babylonian gods. In times of crisis, they cannot speak, offer counsel, or provide comfort. They must be told to "Awake!" or "Arise!" (v. 19), yet they remain encased in gold and silver with "no breath at all in the midst of it." This stands in majestic contrast to verse 20: "But the LORD is in His holy temple. Let all the earth keep silence before Him." While the idols are silent because they are dead, the earth must be silent before Yahweh because He is terrifyingly alive, active, and speaking.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 19:4: "Do not turn to idols, nor make for yourselves molded gods: I am the LORD your God."

  • Psalm 96:5: "For all the gods of the peoples are idols, But the LORD made the heavens."

  • Isaiah 2:20: "In that day a man will cast away his idols of silver and his idols of gold, Which they made, each for himself to worship, To the moles and bats,"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A modern application of 'elil must recognize that contemporary idols are rarely made of wood, stone, or gold. Today, people craft 'elilim out of wealth, career, political power, human relationships, or technology. These modern idols function exactly like the ancient ones: they demand devotion, promise security and joy, but are ultimately "worthless things" that cannot speak peace to a broken soul, deliver a person from death, or provide ultimate meaning. Like the mute statues of Babylon, modern self-made gods always fail when trusted to do what only the living God can do.
