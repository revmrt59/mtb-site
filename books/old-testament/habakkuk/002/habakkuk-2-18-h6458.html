CARVE - ( pasal , Strong's H6458 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb pasal refers to the physical act of cutting, hewing, or carving stone, wood, or metal, specifically to shape an object. In the context of biblical theology, it is most frequently used to describe the manufacturing of idols—the deliberate crafting of a physical representation to be worshipped. In Habakkuk 2:18, it highlights the ultimate absurdity of idolatry: a human creator spending time, effort, and skill to "carve" a lifeless object, only to turn around and treat that crafted object as a source of divine help or truth.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The physical background of pasal lies in the ancient crafts of masonry and carpentry. It describes the physical exertion of a craftsman swinging an adze, chisel, or hammer to chip away at raw wood or stone until a specific, planned form emerges. In a positive or neutral context, this is how stone tablets were prepared for the Ten Commandments or how stones were squared in a quarry for building projects. In a negative context, it depicts a craftsman laboring over a block of wood or stone, carefully shaving away material to create an ear that cannot hear, an eye that cannot see, and a mouth that cannot speak. The word picture is one of human energy, ingenuity, and physical force acting upon passive, lifeless matter to impose a humanly devised shape upon it.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb pasal carries the basic meaning of "to cut," "to hew," or "to carve." It is a primary root that focuses on the shaping of raw materials by removing excess substance.

  • It is used of cutting stone tablets out of a mountain.

  • It is used of carving wood to make architectural features or decorative items.

  • It is used most prominently to denote the fabrication of physical images intended for religious worship (idols).

The noun form derived from this root, pesel, is the standard Old Testament term for a "graven image" or "carved image," which is explicitly forbidden in the Decalogue.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs pasal in two distinct categories of word pictures:

  • The Work of Obedience: Moses is commanded to "hew" (pasal) two stone tablets to replace the ones he broke. Here, the action of carving is neutral, representing human preparation to receive the divine revelation written by the finger of God.

  • The Folly of Self-Deification: When used of idol-making, the word pictures are heavy with irony. A person takes a tree, burns part of it to warm himself and cook his bread, and then uses his human skill to "carve" the remainder into a god. The word picture contrasts the raw, created material (which is subject to human control and decay) with the transcendent, uncreated Creator. The act of carving emphasizes that the idol is entirely a product of human hands; it is passive, subordinate to the tools of its maker, and completely devoid of independent life.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, pasal highlights the sharp dividing line between the true God and all false gods. The living God is the Creator; humanity is His creation, fashioned in His image. When a human "carves" an idol, this divine order is reversed. The human becomes the creator, and the "god" becomes the creation.

Furthermore, because the carved image is limited to the physical dimensions imposed by the craftsman's tools, it represents a theological attempt to domesticate, limit, and control the divine. To carve a representation of god is to deny His sovereign freedom, His spiritual nature, and His transcendence.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:18, the prophet is delivering a series of "woe" oracles against the proud, violent Chaldean (Babylonian) empire. The Babylonians were famous for their massive temples and elaborate, gold-plated idols. Habakkuk uses pasal to expose the spiritual bankruptcy and logical foolishness of the conquerors.

He asks, "What profit is the image, that its maker should carve (pasal) it?" The nuance here is one of absolute futility. The Babylonians trusted in their crafted gods for military victory, yet those gods were physically constructed by the very soldiers and craftsmen who feared defeat. By using pasal, Habakkuk forces the reader to look at the workshop: the dust, the wood shavings, the sweat of the craftsman, and the chisel. He exposes the tragic irony of a human being trusting in a "creation" that is entirely dependent on human labor to exist. The carved image is a "teacher of lies" because it promises power and divinity but is actually nothing more than carved wood or stone.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 34:1: "And the Lord said to Moses, 'Cut [pasal] two tablets of stone like the first ones, and I will write on these tablets the words that were on the first tablets which you broke.'"

  • Deuteronomy 10:1: "At that time the Lord said to me, 'Hew [pasal] for yourself two tablets of stone like the first, and come up to Me on the mountain and make yourself an ark of wood.'"

  • Habakkuk 2:18: "What profit is the image, that its maker should carve [pasal] it, the molded image, a teacher of lies, that the maker of its creation should trust in it, to make mute idols?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The relationship between the verb pasal (to carve) and the noun pesel (carved image) is central to understanding the Second Commandment (Exodus 20:4). When God commands His people not to make any pesel, He is forbidding them from engaging in the action of pasal for the purpose of worship. While other ancient nations believed that a deity would spiritually inhabit a carved image through magical rituals, Hebrew scripture relentlessly de-mystifies this process. By focusing on the physical verb—the dusty, earthly act of carving—Scripture strips away any illusion of divinity from the idol, leaving only a piece of hand-carved material.
