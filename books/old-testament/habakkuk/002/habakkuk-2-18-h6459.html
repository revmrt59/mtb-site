IMAGE - ( pesel , Strong's H6459 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun pesel refers to a carved or graven image, specifically an idol cut from stone, wood, or metal. It represents the ultimate expression of human self-deception: the attempt to fashion a god with one's own hands and then trust in it for deliverance. In Habakkuk 2:18, this word highlights the sheer absurdity of Babylon's reliance on lifeless, mute objects that require human protection and maintenance, contrasting sharply with the living, sovereign Lord who rules from His holy temple.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun pesel is derived from the root verb pasal, which means to cut, carve, or hew. Historically, this verb describes the physical work of a craftsman shaping raw materials—such as hewing stone tablets out of a quarry or carving a block of timber into a distinct shape.

The background of the term highlights the physical effort and human skill invested in creating the object. A craftsman takes an ordinary piece of wood or stone, cuts away at it, refines its shape, and suddenly designates this shaped material as a deity. The word picture emphasizes the dependency of the finished object. It is entirely passive, unable to form itself, and utterly reliant on human labor to come into existence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of pesel is a carved image or idol. It refers specifically to a three-dimensional representation of a false god.

  • A Carved Figure: An image sculpted from wood or stone.

  • A Cast Idol finished by carving: A metal image cast in a mold and then finished or detailed with a carving tool (such as a chisel or graving tool).

  • An Object of Prohibited Worship: Any physical representation of a deity used in religious worship, which is strictly forbidden under biblical law.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word pesel is used in the Old Testament to expose the futility of pagan worship. The prophets frequently paint vivid, almost satirical word pictures of the pesel to awaken the conscience of Israel:

  • The Dependent God: The craftsmen must secure the finished pesel with hammers and nails so it does not totter or fall over (Isaiah 41:7).

  • The Leftovers of Usefulness: A man cuts down a cedar, uses part of the wood to kindle a fire to bake his bread and keep himself warm, and then uses the leftover scrap to carve a pesel, bowing down to it and saying, "Deliver me, for you are my god" (Isaiah 44:14-17).

  • A Burden to be Carried: Instead of carrying its worshippers, the pesel is a heavy burden that must be carried on the backs of beasts of burden, unable to save itself or its captors (Isaiah 46:1-2).

4. Theological Implications

Theological study of pesel reveals the deep spiritual tragedy of idolatry:

  • Subverting the Creator-Creature Relationship: God created humanity in His own image (tselem). When humans carve a pesel, they attempt to reverse this relationship, creating a god in their own image.

  • The Source of Deception: In Habakkuk 2:18, the pesel is directly linked to a "teacher of lies." A carved image is an inherent lie because it represents the infinite, invisible, and self-existent God as a localized, visible, and helpless piece of wood or stone.

  • Spiritual Blindness: Trusting in a pesel requires a profound hardening of the intellect. It demonstrates the foolishness of the human heart when it rejects the true God, choosing to worship the work of its own hands rather than the Maker of heaven and earth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:18, the prophet delivers a series of five "woes" against the proud, violent Chaldeans (Babylonians). The fifth woe exposes the ultimate ground of their false confidence: their synthetic, human-made gods.

The context highlights a sharp contrast between the active verb "to carve" (pasal) and the static nature of the finished "image" (pesel). The prophet asks, "What profit is the image, that its maker should carve it?" This question exposes the irrationality of the Babylonians. They believed their military conquests were granted by their gods, yet those very gods were physically manufactured by their own craftsmen.

Habakkuk highlights the supreme irony: the maker of the mold must "trust in it," even though the object he made is a "mute idol" that cannot speak, breathe, or act. This sets up the magnificent climax in Habakkuk 2:20, where all the earth is commanded to keep silence before the true, living God who is in His holy temple.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 20:4 - "You shall not make for yourself a carved image—any likeness of anything that is in heaven above, or that is in the earth beneath, or that is in the water under the earth;"

  • Deuteronomy 27:15 - "‘Cursed is the one who makes a carved image or a molded image, an abomination to the LORD, the work of the hands of the craftsman, and sets it up in a secret place.’ And all the people shall answer and say, ‘Amen!’"

  • Isaiah 40:19 - "The workman molds a carved image, The goldsmith overspreads it with gold, And the silversmith casts silver chains."

  • Isaiah 44:15 - "Then it shall be for a man to burn, For he will take some of it and warm himself; Yes, he kindles it and bakes bread; Indeed he makes a god and worships it; He makes it a carved image, and falls down to it."

7. Other Relevant Insights

While modern believers rarely bow down to physical statues of wood or stone, the spiritual principle of the pesel remains highly relevant. An idol is anything created by human hands, intellect, or desires that is trusted to provide what only God can provide: security, identity, meaning, and deliverance. Whenever we trust in our own resources, systems, or achievements, we are trusting in a modern equivalent of a pesel—a crafted savior that is ultimately mute, helpless, and unable to profit us in the day of judgment.
