SILENT - ( dumam , Strong's H1748 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word dumam denotes a state of absolute silence, muteness, or stillness. In the Old Testament, it refers to the complete absence of speech, sound, or responsiveness, characterizing both the lifelessness of false idols and the reverent, submissive waiting of a soul before God. In Habakkuk 2:19, it highlights the utter absurdity of idolatry, contrasting a mute, lifeless stone carved by human hands with the living, speaking Creator who commands the entire earth to keep silence before Him.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The word dumam derives from the root damam, which means to be dumb, silent, still, or astonished. The root carries an onomatopoeic quality, resembling the closed-mouth hum of one who is speechless or stunned into silence.

  • It evokes the image of a sudden, heavy hush—either due to a lack of life, as with an inanimate stone, or due to overwhelming awe and dread in the presence of majesty.

  • Culturally, in the ancient Near East, gods were expected to speak, guide, and manifest power. A "silent" deity was a useless deity, exposed as nothing more than the physical material from which it was crafted.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Dumam functions semantically as a noun or an adverb, expressing the quality or state of silence.

  • It signifies a complete cessation of sound, voice, or responsiveness.

  • It describes inanimate objects that are inherently incapable of speech or communication.

  • It denotes a posture of quiet, patient expectation or submission, free from murmuring or self-assertion.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The term appears only three times in the Old Testament, but each occurrence paints a vivid portrait of silence:

  • Lifeless Silence: In Habakkuk, it describes a "silent stone" (NKJV). This is the silence of non-existence, death, and total powerlessness.

  • Shameful Silence: In Isaiah 47:5, the fallen Babylonian empire is told to "sit in silence" (NKJV). Here, the word depicts the stunned, speechless grief of a defeated tyrant, stripped of glory and reduced to dark obscurity.

  • Hopeful Silence: In Lamentations 3:26, the word is used for waiting "quietly" (NKJV) for Yahweh’s salvation. This is the silence of trust, where a suffering soul ceases its frantic strivings and rests in God's sovereign care.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Inadequacy of Idolatry: An idol of wood or stone cannot hear, move, or speak. Calling upon a "silent stone" to teach or guide is a tragic reversal of the created order, where the living human pleads with dead matter.

  • The Sovereignty of Yahweh: Unlike silent idols, Yahweh is the God who speaks. His silence is never due to weakness or non-existence; when He is silent, it is a purposeful call for humanity to wait on Him, or a prelude to His righteous judgment.

  • The Posture of the Believer: True faith does not demand constant noise or immediate answers. It is capable of dumam—a quiet, surrendered stillness that acknowledges God’s supreme authority.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 2:19, the prophet delivers a series of "woes" against the proud, wicked Chaldeans (Babylonians). The fifth woe exposes the foolishness of their religious security. They fashion elaborate idols of wood and stone, overlay them with gold and silver to make them look majestic, and then shout, "Awake!" or "Arise!" to them in times of crisis.

  • The word dumam is placed in sharp contrast to the commands "Awake!" and "Arise!" The idolater screams for action, but the stone remains in its inherent, unalterable state of silence. It cannot teach, guide, or breathe.

  • This dead silence prepares the reader for the climax in verse 20: "But the LORD is in His holy temple. Let all the earth keep silence before Him." While the pagan must beg a silent stone to speak, the true worshiper must be silent before the living God who reigns from heaven.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Isaiah 47:5: "Sit in silence, and go into darkness, O daughter of the Chaldeans; For you shall no longer be called The Lady of Kingdoms."

  • Lamentations 3:26: "It is good that one should hope and wait quietly for the salvation of the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Hebrew root damam also relates to physical death and destruction (e.g., being "silenced" in the grave). Thus, the "silent stone" in Habakkuk is not merely quiet; it is dead.

  • The gold and silver overlay mentioned in Habakkuk 2:19 represents a superficial attempt to hide the internal lifelessness of the idol. It mimics beauty and value, but cannot purchase a single breath of life.
