SILVER - ( kesep , Strong's H3701 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word kesep primarily refers to silver as a precious metal, which also functioned as the standard currency and measure of wealth in the ancient Near East. Throughout the Old Testament, silver represents value, purity, and beauty, but it also carries warnings regarding the corrupting influence of wealth and the folly of idolatry. In Habakkuk 2:19, the word illustrates the deceptive, superficial glory of man-made idols. Though adorned with shimmering, expensive silver to mimic divine majesty, these idols remain entirely lifeless, highlighting the contrast between dead material wealth and the living presence of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient world, silver was a highly prized commodity. Because it does not oxidize easily and retains a brilliant luster when polished, it was highly desired for jewelry, royal vessels, and religious artifacts. The Hebrew noun kesep is derived from a root verb meaning "to pale" (referring to the whitish color of the metal) or "to long for/desire" (reflecting the human longing for wealth and valuable possessions). In the biblical era, coins were not yet in general circulation; instead, silver was weighed out by shekels to conduct transactions. Therefore, kesep is also the standard Old Testament word for "money." To possess silver was to possess power, security, and social status.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Kesep refers to:

  • The raw, unrefined metal found in the earth.

  • Refined silver that has been purified in a furnace or crucible.

  • Manufactured items, such as cups, trumpets, jewelry, and idols.

  • Currency or money used in commercial transactions, wages, and tribute.

  • The material used to overlay less valuable materials (like wood or stone) to give them an appearance of beauty and value.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Refinement and Purity: Because silver must be melted down in a crucible to separate it from worthless dross, it serves as a powerful biblical metaphor for spiritual testing. God refines the hearts of His people just as a silversmith purifies silver, keeping the metal in the fire until He can see His own reflection in it.

  • Redemption Price: In the Law, silver was the currency of redemption. The "atonement money" required from every Israelite was paid in silver shekels, demonstrating that redemption carries a specific, costly price.

  • The Value of Truth: God's words are described as "silver tried in a furnace of earth, purified seven times," emphasizing their absolute purity, reliability, and immense value over any earthly treasure.

  • Deceptive Splendor: In prophetic literature, silver represents the outward veneer of human pride. It is the material used to dress up dead idols, masking their inner emptiness with a false display of wealth.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, kesep exposes the tension between material wealth and spiritual reality. God asserts His sovereign ownership over all creation by declaring, "The silver is Mine, and the gold is Mine" (Haggai 2:8). When human beings take God's silver and fashion it into idols, they commit a double sin: they steal His material glory to fabricate a rival god, and they trust in the created thing rather than the Creator. Furthermore, the Scriptures repeatedly warn that silver cannot ransom a soul in the day of judgment. It is a perishable commodity that ultimately fails to provide true security.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:19, the prophet pronounces a woe upon those who worship idols: "Woe to him who says to wood, 'Awake!' To silent stone, 'Arise! It shall teach!' Behold, it is overlaid with gold and silver, yet in it there is no breath at all."

The use of kesep here emphasizes the contrast between costly ornamentation and spiritual worthlessness. The idolater spares no expense, coating a worthless piece of wood or stone with shimmering silver to make it look majestic, powerful, and worthy of worship. The silver creates a visual illusion of divinity. However, the prophet strips away this illusion. The shiny silver exterior cannot breathe, speak, or save. The idol remains a dead, silent block of wood, exposing the tragic foolishness of replacing the living God with refined currency.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 13:2: "Abram was very rich in livestock, in silver, and in gold."

  • Exodus 20:23: "You shall not make anything to be with Me—gods of silver or gods of gold you shall not make for yourselves."

  • Psalm 12:6: "The words of the Lord are pure words, like silver tried in a furnace of earth, purified seven times."

  • Malachi 3:3: "He will sit as a refiner and a purifier of silver; He will purify the sons of Levi, and purge them as gold and silver, that they may offer to the Lord an offering in righteousness."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the construction of the Tabernacle, silver played a highly symbolic role. The sockets that formed the foundation for the sanctuary's wooden boards were made of silver, specifically melted down from the redemption shekels paid by the census of the congregation. This designed structure meant that the entire dwelling place of God's presence literally rested upon a foundation of silver redemption. This stands in stark, beautiful contrast to the silver in Habakkuk 2:19, which is used to construct a foundation-less, lifeless false god.
