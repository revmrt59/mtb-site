AWAKE - ( qits , Strong's H6974 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qits (H6974) refers to the act of waking up from sleep, returning to a state of consciousness, activity, and alertness. In the Old Testament, it describes both physical waking and metaphorically refers to spiritual awakening, resurrection, or divine intervention. In the context of Habakkuk 2:19, the word is used in a biting satirical sense, exposing the utter foolishness of idolatry. The idolater commands a lifeless piece of carved wood to "awake" and act on his behalf, highlighting the stark contrast between dead, unresponsive idols and the living, ever-watchful God of Israel who needs no such prompting.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Near Eastern life, sleep represented a period of vulnerability, darkness, and complete inactivity. To awake (qits) is to emerge into the light, regain strength, and resume purposeful, protective action. When applied to God in anthropomorphic language, it implies a transition from a state of apparent inactivity or silence to active engagement and protection of His people. However, when applied to idols, the concept becomes ironical. An idol is not sleeping; it is dead. The word picture of commanding an inanimate block of wood to "awake" highlights the desperate, irrational attempts of humanity to breathe life into things of their own creation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Qits is a root verb meaning "to awake" or "to rouse oneself." In the Hiphil stem (the form used in Habakkuk 2:19), it carries the causative sense of "to awaken" someone from sleep, "to arouse," or "to become active." The term denotes a transition from inactivity, slumber, or death to a state of life, vigilance, and conscious interaction with the environment. It stands in direct contrast to deep sleep (tardemah) or the permanent sleep of death.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Physical Awakening: The word frequently describes the transition from ordinary night sleep to daylight consciousness, such as when Jacob awoke from his dream at Bethel.

  • Awakening from Death: The word functions as a metaphor for resurrection, describing those who sleep in the dust of the earth awakening to eternal reality.

  • Divine Awakening: In times of national crisis, psalmist-leaders cried out to God to "awake" and defend His covenant people, petitioning Him to end His period of silent observation and execute justice.

  • Idolatrous Awakening: The word is used mockingly to expose the impotence of false gods. This is seen when Elijah suggested that Baal was asleep and needed to be awakened, and here in Habakkuk's woe oracle.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, qits underscores the fundamental difference between the living Creator and dead creations. Yahweh is the guardian of Israel who "neither slumbers nor sleeps" (Psalm 121:4). Therefore, human appeals for God to "awake" are poetic expressions of human distress, not statements of divine fatigue. Conversely, calling an idol to "awake" is a futile effort to infuse life and consciousness into dead matter. It highlights the vanity of human-centered religion, where man tries to create a god he can command, control, and wake up at his convenience.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:19, the prophet pronounces a series of woes against the proud and wicked Chaldeans. This specific woe targets their idolatry. The worshiper addresses a piece of carved wood, commanding it, "Awake!" and a silent stone, "Arise!" The parallel commands ("Awake!" and "Arise!") emphasize the desperate absurdity of the request. The wood cannot wake because it was never alive; it completely lacks the breath of life (ruach). By contrasting the "silent stone" with the "living Lord" who is in His holy temple (Habakkuk 2:20), Habakkuk shows that while idols remain perpetually asleep and unresponsive, the Lord of hosts reigns in active, sovereign silence, demanding the earth to be silent before Him.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 28:16 - "Then Jacob awoke from his sleep and said, 'Surely the Lord is in this place, and I did not know it.'"

  • 2 Kings 4:31 - "Now Gehazi went on ahead of them, and laid the staff on the face of the child; but there was neither voice nor hearing. Therefore he went back to meet him, and told him, saying, 'The child has not awakened.'"

  • Psalm 17:15 - "As for me, I will see Your face in righteousness; I shall be satisfied when I awake in Your likeness."

  • Daniel 12:2 - "And many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, Some to everlasting life, Some to shame and everlasting contempt."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The mockery of idols sleeping is a recurring theme in Old Testament polemics. The most famous parallel is 1 Kings 18:27, where Elijah taunts the prophets of Baal, suggesting that their god "is sleeping and must be awakened." In both instances, the underlying polemic is that a god who must be awakened is no god at all. True divinity possesses intrinsic, uninterrupted life and sovereignty. While false gods must be actively carried, gold-plated, and shouted at to wake up, the true God carries His people, needs no adornment, and answers before they even call.
