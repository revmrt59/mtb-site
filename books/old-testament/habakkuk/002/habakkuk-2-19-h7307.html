BREATH - ( ruach , Strong's H7307 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ruach encompasses breath, wind, and spirit, serving as the primary Old Testament term for the life-giving force and unseen power of God. In Habakkuk 2:19, it is translated "breath," highlighting the complete absence of life, vitality, and divine animation in hand-made idols. While God is the living Creator who breathes life into His creation, these silent, cold idols of wood and stone remain utterly devoid of the ruach necessary for life, exposing the profound folly of idolatry.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The phonetic sound of ruach is onomatopoeic, mimicking the breathy, rushing sound of a sudden gust of wind or a deep exhalation from the lungs. In the ancient world, where invisible forces were often mysterious, the wind and physical breath represented the most potent examples of invisible yet undeniable power. You cannot see the wind, but you can see its dramatic effects on the trees and seas; you cannot see physical breath, but its presence or absence marks the absolute boundary between a living being and a corpse. This word links the physical reality of breathing with the supernatural reality of spiritual existence and divine power.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Ruach possesses three primary avenues of meaning, depending on the context:

  • Wind: The natural, physical movement of air in the atmosphere, ranging from a gentle evening breeze to a violent, destructive tempest.

  • Breath: The physical inhalation and exhalation of air through the nose or mouth, which serves as the essential, visible indicator of life in both humans and animal life.

  • Spirit: The immaterial seat of human emotion, will, intellect, and character, as well as the personal, active presence of the Spirit of God (the Holy Spirit).

In Habakkuk 2:19, the word is used in its physiological sense of physical "breath." It indicates the absolute absence of any animating life-force inside the physical frame of the idol.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, ruach is used to draw vivid theological contrasts:

  • The Wind of God: God uses the ruach of the wind as an instrument of His sovereignty, such as parting the Red Sea or bringing the locust plague over Egypt.

  • The Breath of God: God breathes the ruach of life into physical matter, transforming dust into living, breathing souls.

  • The Borrowed Life: Humanity does not own its ruach. It is a divine loan. When God gathers His ruach back to Himself, all physical creatures immediately perish and return to the dust.

  • The Divine Spirit: The ruach of God comes upon individuals to equip them with extraordinary wisdom, prophetic speech, artistic skill, or physical strength to accomplish His covenant purposes.

4. Theological Implications

The theology of ruach emphasizes the absolute dependency of all creation upon the Creator. God is the self-existent Source of all life; He possesses ruach inherently. Conversely, humanity and animals are entirely dependent on Him for every single breath.

This creates a stark theological boundary between the living Creator and dead idols. To worship an idol is to reverse the natural order: a living, breathing human being (who possesses ruach from God) bows down to worship a block of wood or stone that has no ruach of its own. It is the ultimate spiritual tragedy—the living seeking life from the dead.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:19, the prophet delivers a biting "woe" oracle against the Chaldeans and all who trust in manufactured gods: "Woe to him who says to wood, 'Awake!' To silent stone, 'Arise! It shall teach!' Behold, it is overlaid with gold and silver, Yet in it there is no breath at all."

The contextual details are highly instructional:

  • The Contrast of Appearance vs. Reality: The idol is "overlaid with gold and silver," presenting a dazzling, expensive, and majestic exterior. It looks magnificent on the outside, yet its interior is completely empty. The Hebrew construction ên rûaḥ bəqirbōw literally means "there is no breath in its midst."

  • The Mockery of the Commands: The idolater commands the inanimate object to "awake" and "arise." But these physical objects cannot move because they lack the basic animating ruach that gives living things the capacity for movement and consciousness.

  • The Silent God vs. the Living God: This verse sets up a brilliant contrast with verse 20: "But the Lord is in His holy temple. Let all the earth keep silence before Him." While the dead idol must be desperately commanded to speak or wake up, the living God is so majestic and active that His very presence commands the entire earth to be silent.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:2 - "The earth was without form, and void; and darkness was on the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters."

  • Genesis 6:17 - "And behold, I Myself am bringing floodwaters on the earth, to destroy from under heaven all flesh in which is the breath of life; everything that is on the earth shall die."

  • Psalm 104:29 - "You hide Your face, they are troubled; You take away their breath, they die and return to their dust."

  • Ecclesiastes 12:7 - "Then the dust will return to the earth as it was, And the spirit will return to God who gave it."

  • Ezekiel 37:5 - "Thus says the Lord God to these bones: 'Surely I will cause breath to enter into you, and you shall live.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) translates ruach in Habakkuk 2:19 using the Greek word pneuma. This same Greek word is used throughout the New Testament to describe the wind, human breath, and the Holy Spirit.

This linguistic connection highlights a profound spiritual truth: just as physical life is impossible without physical ruach (breath), spiritual life is completely impossible without the divine ruach (the Holy Spirit) regenerating the human heart. The spiritual deadness of those who worship silent idols can only be remedied when the living God breathes His Holy Spirit into their dead hearts, making them alive in Christ.
