ANSWERED - ( 'anah , Strong's H6030 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'anah conveys the action of responding, testifying, or answering. It denotes more than just initiating speech; it is a relational, responsive word that implies a prior voice, cry, or situation that demands a reply. In the context of Habakkuk 2:2, it marks the pivotal moment when Yahweh breaks His silence to respond directly to the prophet’s agonizing lament, demonstrating that God is not indifferent to the cries of His people but speaks actively, authoritatively, and clearly in His perfect timing.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • 'anah primarily carries the physical or verbal picture of "turning toward" or "responding to" another. It represents an auditory transaction where one party cries out and the other leans in to speak back. It paints a physical picture of a relational bridge being crossed. When used of God, it highlights His condescension and grace—the transcendent Creator of the universe bending down to address the specific anxieties and questions of a finite, struggling prophet.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb 'anah refers to speaking in response to some stimulus, whether a question, a plea, a legal charge, or a physical situation. The semantic range includes:

  • To answer or reply to a question.

  • To respond to a prayer or cry for help (frequently used of God).

  • To testify or bear witness in a legal setting (giving a responsive testimony).

  • To sing responsively or shout.

In its relational aspect, it represents the completion of communication, transforming a monologue of human grief into a divine dialogue.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Responsive Dialogue: In Old Testament narrative, 'anah is the standard term for the back-and-forth of conversation. It shows that God does not merely issue cold decrees from afar but engages in real dialogue with His servants.

  • Legal Testimony: In covenantal contexts, the word can mean to testify. This nuances the word with a sense of truth, binding oath, and official declaration. When God responds, His words carry absolute judicial authority and truth; they are not mere opinions but sovereign decrees.

  • Sovereign Intervention: When God is the subject of 'anah, it often signals an act of deliverance. His verbal "answer" is frequently accompanied by historical action, showing that when God speaks, He acts.

4. Theological Implications

  • The God Who Hears: The use of 'anah reinforces the biblical truth that Yahweh is not like the silent, unresponsive idols of the nations. He has ears to hear and a voice to reply.

  • The Nature of Divine Revelation: God's revelation is often responsive to human distress. God does not reveal His plans in a vacuum; He speaks into the specific crises of faith experienced by His people.

  • Patience and Sovereignty: Although Habakkuk had to wait and stand watch, God's eventual answer ('anah) proves that delays in divine response are not denials. God's speech is perfectly timed to accomplish His purposes and sustain the faith of the righteous.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 1, the prophet cried out in deep distress over the violence in Judah and the shocking rise of the ruthless Chaldeans. In chapter 2, verse 1, Habakkuk takes his stand on the watchtower, waiting to see what God will say to him.

The word 'anah in verse 2 is the dramatic resolution to this tension. The LORD answered. The response is not an intellectual defense of His actions, but a command to "write the vision." God's answer shifts the prophet's focus from his immediate circumstances to the certain, future fulfillment of God's sovereign plan. The divine response is practical, authoritative, and designed to produce enduring faith.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 38:1 - "Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind, and said:"

  • Psalm 91:15 - "He shall call upon Me, and I will answer him; I will be with him in trouble; I will deliver him and honor him."

  • Isaiah 58:9 - "Then you shall call, and the LORD will answer; You shall cry, and He will say, 'Here I am.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The grammatical form of 'anah in Habakkuk 2:2 is a conversive imperfect (waw-consecutive), which expresses historical past action succeeding a previous event. It links Habakkuk's patient watching in verse 1 directly to God's immediate response in verse 2.

Furthermore, the command that follows God's answer—"Write the vision and make it plain on tablets"—underscores that God's answers are intended to be preserved for future generations. The answer transcends the immediate moment; it becomes a permanent source of comfort and instruction for all believers waiting on God.
