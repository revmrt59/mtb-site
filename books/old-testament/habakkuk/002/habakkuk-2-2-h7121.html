READS - ( qara , Strong's H7121 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qara primarily means to call out, proclaim, summon, or read aloud. In the context of Habakkuk 2:2, it refers to encountering a clearly written, publicly displayed divine message. To "read" this vision is not a passive mental exercise but an active engagement that demands an immediate response. The clarity of the written word is designed to grasp the reader's attention instantly, enabling them to "run"—either to escape impending judgment, to herald the message to others, or to walk in steady, active faith despite troubling circumstances.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, public announcements were often carved into stone, written on clay tablets, or painted on plaster walls and set up in high-traffic public squares. Because literacy was not universal, reading was rarely a private, silent activity. Instead, reading was inherently oral and communal. When a person encountered a public tablet, they would read it aloud so that others passing by could also hear. The word qara carries this vocal, dynamic quality. It is closely tied to the breath and the voice, representing the transformation of static, written characters into a living, spoken declaration that demands an audience and forces a reaction.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb qara is a primary Hebrew root occurring over 700 times in the Old Testament. Its primary semantic ranges include:

  • To call out, cry out, or raise one's voice.

  • To summon, invite, or call a person to a specific place or task.

  • To proclaim, herald, or announce public decrees.

  • To read aloud from a scroll, book, or tablet.

  • To name or designate a person, place, or object.

In Habakkuk 2:2, the word appears as a Qal active participle (qore), designating "the one who reads." In this form, it emphasizes the active, ongoing process of engaging with the written vision. The lexical focus is on the clarity of the text, which allows the reader to immediately comprehend and act upon the message without hesitation or confusion.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently uses qara to depict moments where God’s voice or His written Word breaks into human history to demand attention.

  • It is the word used when God "called" to Moses out of the burning bush, establishing a holy summons.

  • It describes the formal, public reading of the Law of Moses to the entire assembly of Israel, where the written covenant was brought to life through spoken proclamation.

  • It is the action of a watchman or herald crying out a warning to a city of approaching danger.

This background paints a vivid picture for Habakkuk 2:2. The written vision on the tablet is not meant for a private library; it is a public billboard. The reader is pictured as a herald who catches sight of the message, reads it aloud, and is immediately mobilized by the weight of its truth.

4. Theological Implications

The use of qara in Habakkuk 2:2 reveals several key aspects of God's character and His relationship with His people:

  • God desires His revelation to be clear and accessible. He commands the prophet to make the message plain. God does not hide His truth in obscure, unreachable mysteries; He publishes it clearly so that the reader can act.

  • Scripture demands an active, mobile response. Reading God's Word is never intended to end in mere intellectual assent. To truly "read" God's message is to be set in motion. Divine truth demands ethical, spiritual, and physical mobilization.

  • God’s Word is reliable and urgent. The written word remains stable ("write the vision"), while the reading of it generates urgent action ("that he may run"). The permanence of the text ensures that whenever it is read, it carries the same fresh authority to guide and warn.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:2, the prophet stands on his watchtower, waiting for God’s response to his agonizing questions about the rise of the ruthless Babylonians. God's answer begins with an instruction on how to handle the forthcoming vision: "Write the vision and make it plain on tablets, that he may run who reads it."

The phrase "that he may run who reads it" holds a dual, complementary nuance:

  • The Herald Nuance: The one who reads the tablet is so gripped by the clarity of the message that they immediately run to carry the news to others. The clarity of the text facilitates a rapid, accurate transmission of the warning.

  • The Passerby Nuance: The tablet is written in such large, clear characters that even a person running past it can read it on the move. The message is so striking and plain that it can be grasped instantly, advising the runner of the path of safety.

In both interpretations, qara serves as the vital bridge between God’s static written revelation and the urgent, active movement of the believer. In a time of national distress and impending judgment, God's Word provides clear, actionable direction.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 24:7 - "Then he took the Book of the Covenant and read in the hearing of the people. And they said, 'All that the LORD has said we will do, and be obedient.'"

  • Deuteronomy 31:11 - "...when all Israel comes to appear before the LORD your God in the place which He chooses, you shall read this law before all Israel in their hearing."

  • Jeremiah 36:6 - "You go, therefore, and read from the scroll which you have written at my instruction, the words of the LORD, in the hearing of the people in the LORD's house on the day of fasting. And you shall also read them in the hearing of all Judah who come from their cities."

  • Nehemiah 8:8 - "So they read distinctly from the book, in the Law of God; and they gave the sense, and helped them to understand the reading."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Hebrew Scriptures, there is no sharp division between hearing the Word of God and obeying it. The act of reading (qara) is functionally linked to hearing (shama) and doing (asah). When we read the Scriptures today, we must resist the modern tendency to treat reading as a passive, silent consumption of information. Instead, we must read the Bible with the ancient expectation: expecting the text to speak to us aloud, to arrest our attention, and to send us running into the world to live out and proclaim His truth.
