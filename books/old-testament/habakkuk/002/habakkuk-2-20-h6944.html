HOLY - ( qodesh , Strong's H6944 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun qodesh refers to holiness, sacredness, or a state of consecrated set-apartness. It designates someone, something, or some place separated from the common, profane, or unclean for divine ownership and exclusive use. In the context of Habakkuk 2:20, qodesh describes the temple of Yahweh—not merely as a physical structure, but as the earthly throne room of His transcendent majesty. This holiness demands absolute reverence, submission, and silent awe from all creation, standing in sharp contrast to the worthless, silent idols of the nations.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun qodesh derives from the root q-d-sh, which fundamentally carries the idea of cutting, dividing, or setting apart. It visualizes a boundary line drawn between the sacred and the profane, the clean and the unclean. In the ancient Near East, the world was divided into the common (ordinary, everyday life) and the sacred (that which belonged to the gods). While pagan nations designated places and objects as sacred to secure the favor of their capricious deities, Israel's understanding of qodesh was anchored in the unique character of Yahweh. The tabernacle, and later the temple, was "cut off" from ordinary human use because the living God, who is entirely distinct from His creation, chose to dwell there. To cross this boundary without authorization or proper purification was a grave sacrilege, as it treated the uniquely sacred as common.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

As a masculine noun, qodesh means "holiness," "sacredness," or "holy thing." It signifies a state of being dedicated and consecrated to God.

  • It is used to describe objects (such as priestly garments, vessels, and offerings), time (such as the Sabbath and feast days), and places (such as the tabernacle and temple mount) that are set apart for His service.

  • When associated with God Himself, qodesh denotes His absolute moral purity, His infinite majesty, and His total distinction from creation.

  • In Habakkuk 2:20, the phrase "His holy temple" utilizes a genitive construction (hekal qodso, literally "the temple of His holiness"). This grammatical structure emphasizes that the temple is not holy in and of itself, but is permeated with and characterized by God's own set-apart glory and presence.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses qodesh to establish clear distinctions within the covenant community:

  • The Boundary of Sinai: Mount Sinai was declared qodesh when the Lord descended upon it. Boundaries were set around the mountain to prevent the people from touching it and dying, illustrating the dangerous purity of God's presence.

  • The Holy of Holies: The innermost chamber of the tabernacle, the qodesh haqqodashim (literally, the "holy of holies" or "most holy place"), housed the Ark of the Covenant. It was separated by a thick veil, accessible only to the High Priest once a year on the Day of Atonement.

  • The Holy Garments: Aaronic priests wore garments made specifically for glory and beauty, designated as bigde qodesh (holy garments). These garments visually reminded Israel that the priests were set apart to mediate between a holy God and a sinful people.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, qodesh points to the core of God's identity. Holiness is not merely one attribute among many; it is the quality that qualifies all of His other attributes. His love is holy love; His wrath is holy wrath.

  • Ontological Transcendence: God is uncreated, self-existent, and entirely "other." He is infinitely above and beyond His creation.

  • Ethical Purity: God is completely free from the defilement of sin, compromise, and moral imperfection.

  • Derived Holiness: Created things, places, and people can only become qodesh by virtue of their relationship to Him. Holiness is contagious in the ritual law, but ultimately, it is God's indwelling presence that consecrates. Because God is holy, He demands that His covenant people reflect His character by being set apart from the sinful practices of the surrounding world.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2, the prophet wrestles with God's sovereign decision to use the ruthless, idolatrous Babylonians to discipline Judah. In response, God delivers a series of five "woes" against the proud, violent, and covetous Chaldeans (verses 5–19).

  • The climax of these woes exposes the sheer futility of idolatry (verses 18–19). Idolaters carve wood and stone, overlay them with gold and silver, and cry out to these breathless, speechless blocks of metal to "Awake!" and "Arise!"

  • Verse 20 provides a breathtaking contrast: "But the LORD is in His holy temple. Let all the earth keep silence before Him."

  • While idols are silent because they are dead, Yahweh is in His qodesh temple—active, reigning, and completely untouched by the chaos, violence, and vanity of the world. His holiness is the ultimate answer to the prophet's doubts. It assures Habakkuk that sovereign justice resides on the throne. The only proper human response to this transcendent reality is not anxious questioning or frantic pagan clamor, but a hushed, reverent silence.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 3:5 - "Then He said, 'Do not draw near this place. Take your sandals off your feet, for the place where you stand is holy ground.'"

  • Exodus 15:11 - "Who is like You, O LORD, among the gods? Who is like You, glorious in holiness, Fearful in praises, doing wonders?"

  • Leviticus 10:10 - "...that you may distinguish between holy and unholy, and between unclean and clean;"

  • Psalm 29:2 - "Give unto the LORD the glory due to His name; Worship the LORD in the beauty of holiness."

  • Psalm 93:5 - "Your testimonies are very sure; Holiness adorns Your house, O LORD, forever."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural and thematic tension in Habakkuk 2:18–20 highlights the spiritual bankruptcy of self-made religion. Idols are physically housed in humanly constructed shrines, yet they are empty. Conversely, Yahweh's "holy temple" (which refers to both His heavenly dwelling place and His earthly sanctuary) is filled with His living, active presence.

In the New Testament, this theology of qodesh undergoes a profound shift. Through the redemptive work of Jesus Christ, the veil of the temple has been torn. God no longer dwells in temples made with human hands. Instead, the local church corporately (1 Corinthians 3:16) and the believer individually (1 Corinthians 6:19) are designated as the naos—the holy sanctuary of God. The same holiness that demanded silence from the earth in Habakkuk's day now calls believers to lives of consecrated purity, set apart for the Master's use.
