TARRY - ( achar , Strong's H309 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb achar refers to delaying, lagging behind, or being late. In the context of biblical prophecy, specifically Habakkuk 2:3, it underscores the absolute certainty and precision of God's timing. Though the fulfillment of God's vision may seem to linger from a human perspective, it will not actually delay or arrive late. God's decrees are perfectly synchronized with His sovereign schedule, assuring believers that His deliverance is never tardy.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root achar fundamentally relates to the back, the rear, or that which comes behind. It carries the spatial picture of lagging at the end of a caravan, staying behind after others have departed, or dragging one’s feet. When applied to time, this spatial concept of "behind" shifts to the temporal concept of "delaying" or "being late." It evokes the imagery of someone who is overdue, moving slowly, or failing to arrive at the designated moment. In the ancient Near East, where travel was perilous and unpredictable, a delayed arrival (achar) often caused great anxiety and distress to those waiting.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In the Pi'el stem, achar means to delay, hesitate, tarry, or keep back. It denotes tarrying too long in a place or deferring an action that should be completed promptly. In its negated form, lo ye'acher ("it will not tarry"), it functions as a strong, absolute declaration that an event will not be postponed, deferred, or late by even a single moment. It signifies that the subject will arrive precisely at the predetermined and appointed time.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Hesitant Chariot: The word is used to describe the mother of Sisera anxiously looking out the window, asking why his chariot "is so long in coming" or delays (achar) its return (Judges 5:28). This depicts the painful suspense of waiting for someone who appears to be overdue.

  • Procrastinating with Vows: It is used to warn against delaying (achar) the payment of a vow to God (Deuteronomy 23:21; Ecclesiastes 5:4). Here, it paints a picture of holding back or dragging one's feet in performing a spiritual duty.

  • Lingering over Wine: It describes those who "tarry long" (achar) over wine, wasting time and lingering at the tavern (Proverbs 23:30).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, achar addresses the tension between human impatience and divine sovereignty. Human beings live under the tyranny of the urgent, often viewing waiting as a sign of God's absence or indifference. However, God's actions are never delayed in the ultimate sense. Because God is sovereign over time, what humans perceive as delay is actually the precise execution of His perfect counsel. The denial of delay (lo ye'acher) assures the believer that God's justice, salvation, and promises are mathematically precise. He is never slow as some count slowness, but acts exactly when the stage of history is perfectly set.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:3, the prophet is waiting in anguish for God's answer regarding the rise of the brutal Babylonians. God commands him to write the vision because it is for an "appointed time" (mo'ed). The beauty of this verse lies in the contrast between two different Hebrew words translated as "tarry."

  • The first is mahah ("Though it tarry, wait for it"). This represents the subjective experience of the believer. To Habakkuk, the vision seems to linger and move slowly. It feels like a delay.

  • The second is achar ("It will not tarry"). This represents the objective reality of God's decree. From God's perspective, the vision will not be late; it will not lag behind its appointed moment.

The pastoral comfort is profound: what feels like a painful delay (mahah) to the waiting saint is, in reality, an on-time arrival (achar) of God's perfect plan.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 34:19: "The young man did not delay to do the thing, because he delighted in Jacob’s daughter."

  • Deuteronomy 23:21: "When you make a vow to the Lord your God, you shall not delay to pay it; for the Lord your God will surely require it of you, and it would be sin to you."

  • Psalm 40:17: "But I am poor and needy; yet the Lord thinks upon me. You are my help and my deliverer; do not delay, O my God."

  • Daniel 9:19: "O Lord, hear! O Lord, forgive! O Lord, listen and act! Do not delay for Your own sake, my God, for Your city and Your people are called by Your name."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), achar in Habakkuk 2:3 is translated with the Greek verb chronizo ("to spend time, linger, delay"). In Hebrews 10:37, the New Testament writer quotes the Septuagint of Habakkuk 2:3, applying it directly to the second coming of Jesus Christ: "For yet a little while, and He who is coming will come and will not tarry (chronisei)." This links the historical deliverance of Israel from the Babylonians to the ultimate, final deliverance of the Church at the Parousia. The same law of divine timing applies: Christ's return may feel delayed to a suffering church, but He will not be a second late.
