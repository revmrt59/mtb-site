LIE - ( kazab , Strong's H3576 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb kazab denotes the act of lying, deceiving, failing, or proving false. In the context of prophetic revelation, it represents the absolute reliability of God's spoken word. Unlike human words that can disappoint, deceive, or fail to materialize due to weakness or deceit, God's revelation is fundamentally incapable of failing. When Habakkuk is told that the vision "will not lie," it is a divine guarantee that God’s promised judgment and redemption will certainly be fulfilled in history, demanding patient, faithful waiting from His people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete imagery of kazab often involves a dry spring or a deceptive brook that promises water to a thirsty traveler but ultimately fails to deliver. It is the picture of a false hope—something that looks promising from a distance but proves dry, disappointing, and empty upon closer inspection. In the arid climate of the ancient Near East, finding a dried-up water source was a matter of life and death; thus, a "lying" or "failing" thing is something that betrays crucial trust. When applied to God's word, kazab represents the exact opposite: His promises are not dry wells or deceptive brooks; they are completely reliable and will never betray the trust of the thirsty soul.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb kazab means to tell a lie, to act deceptively, to fail, or to disappoint expectations. In the Pi`el stem (the form used in Habakkuk 2:3), it carries the active meaning of telling a falsehood, acting deceptively, or proving false. It stands in direct contrast to truthfulness, stability, and the faithful execution of promises. Lexically, it describes both verbal deceit (uttering falsehoods) and existential failure (failing to perform what was expected or promised). It is the disappointment that occurs when reality fails to match the promise.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Deceptive Waters: The word is used metaphorically to describe physical features of the land that disappoint. A deceptive brook is one that flows during the winter rains but dries up completely in the summer heat when it is needed most.

  • Human Untrustworthiness: Scripture frequently contrasts the deceitfulness of mankind with the unchangeable truthfulness of God. Human speech and actions are often characterized by kazab because of human weakness, fear, or malice.

  • The Transition from Hope to Despair: To "lie" in the Old Testament sense is not merely to speak an untruth; it is to raise expectations only to shatter them, leaving the expectant one in shame and ruin.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of kazab centers on the character of God and the nature of His revelation. Because God is the source of absolute truth, His spoken and written words share His unshakeable character. God's promises cannot "lie" because His nature prevents Him from deceiving or failing. The non-lying vision in Habakkuk 2:3 establishes that biblical prophecy is not speculative human forecasting but historical reality pre-recorded. It assures believers that apparent delays in the fulfillment of God's promises are not a denial of fulfillment. The theological reality of a God who does not lie anchors the faith of the righteous, enabling them to live by faith even in times of suffering and silence.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:3, the prophet stands on his watchtower, waiting for a response to his complaint about the violence and apparent injustice of the rising Babylonian empire. God answers by commanding Habakkuk to write the vision plainly on tablets. The vision concerns the ultimate downfall of the proud Chaldeans and the preservation of the righteous. God assures the prophet that "at the end it will speak, and it will not lie." The word welo-yekazzeb ("and it will not lie/fail") serves as an absolute guarantee. Though the vision may seem to "tarry" (delay) from a human perspective, its fulfillment is fixed and certain. It will not disappoint the expectation of those who wait for it. It will not run dry like a deceptive desert brook. The contrast is between human perception of delay and the divine certainty of historical fulfillment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 23:19 - "God is not a man, that He should lie, Nor a son of man, that He should repent. Has He said, and will He not do? Or has He spoken, and will He not make it good?"

  • Psalm 89:35 - "Once I have sworn by My holiness; I will not lie to David:"

  • Proverbs 14:5 - "A faithful witness does not lie, But a false witness will utter lies."

  • Isaiah 58:11 - "The LORD will guide you continually, And satisfy your soul in drought, And strengthen your bones; You shall be like a watered garden, And like a spring of water, whose waters do not fail."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Greek Septuagint (LXX) translates the Hebrew phrase welo-yekazzeb in Habakkuk 2:3 using the negative particle and the verb pseudo (kai ou pseusetai, "and it will not lie"). The author of the New Testament Epistle to the Hebrews quotes this passage in Hebrews 10:37–38, applying the coming of the vision to the personal coming of the Messiah ("He who is coming will come and will not tarry"). This links the non-failing vision of Habakkuk directly to the ultimate, non-failing promise of Jesus Christ's return, demonstrating that the ultimate fulfillment of all God's promises is found in His Son, who is the Truth personified.
