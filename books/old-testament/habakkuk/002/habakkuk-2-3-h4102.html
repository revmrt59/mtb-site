TARRIES - ( mahah , Strong's H4102 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb mahah conveys the idea of lingering, hesitating, or delaying. In Habakkuk 2:3, it describes the human perception of God's timing. While the fulfillment of the divine vision may seem to drag on or delay to those waiting, the prophet is assured that God's sovereign plans are unfolding exactly on schedule. Believers are called to active, faithful waiting, confident that what looks like a delay is actually the precise, unhurried orchestration of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of mahah is closely tied to a sense of hesitation or questioning, as if a person is standing in place asking "What?" (from the Hebrew interrogative mah). It presents a vivid word picture of someone who is indecisive, stalling, or reluctant to move forward. In ancient cultural contexts, this verb depicted a traveler who delayed departure, or someone who lingered in a place of safety or danger instead of moving immediately. It represents an intentional or internal hesitation that causes a delay in progress.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb mahah is a Hithpalpel verb that means to delay, linger, tarry, or hesitate. Its semantic range emphasizes a state of lingering or holding back. Lexical analysis indicates the following aspects of its meaning:

  • To delay or linger in departure.

  • To hesitate or show reluctance in taking action.

  • To tarry or wait in a specific place.

In contrast to other Hebrew verbs for delay (such as achar, which means to lag behind or be late), mahah carries a psychological component of stalling or lingering, often describing how an event or person appears to slow down from an outside perspective.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Hebrew Bible, mahah is used to describe physical lingering or hesitation:

  • In the account of the destruction of Sodom, it describes Lot’s hesitation and reluctance to leave the doomed city, requiring the angels to physically seize his hand.

  • In the journey of Jacob’s sons to Egypt, it illustrates their delay in returning to purchase grain because of their fear and hesitation.

  • In military contexts, it describes soldiers or messengers who linger in the plains or delay their departure while waiting for crucial information.

In each case, the word picture is one of stalled movement, where an action that should be completed quickly is stretched out over time.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, mahah highlights the tension between human impatience and divine timing. Human beings exist in chronological time and easily become discouraged when God's promises are not immediately realized. From a human perspective, God often seems to linger (mahah), testing the endurance of His people. However, God does not hesitate out of indecision or weakness. His seeming delay is a purposeful interval designed to cultivate faith, refine character, and distinguish the righteous from the proud. The theological remedy for a seeming delay in God's promise is not despair, but active, trusting endurance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:3, the prophet is struggling with the apparent delay of God's judgment on the wicked and the deliverance of the righteous. God responds by commanding him to write down the vision, assuring him that it is for an "appointed time" (moed).

God says, "Though it tarries (mahah), wait for it; because it will surely come, it will not tarry (achar)."

This creates a powerful literary and conceptual contrast:

  • "Though it tarries (mahah)" addresses the prophet's subjective human experience. To Habakkuk and the faithful remnant, the vision will seem to linger and drag on.

  • "It will not tarry (achar)" declares the objective reality of God's timeline. In God's sovereign plan, the event will not be a single moment late; it will arrive precisely when scheduled.

Therefore, what feels like lingering (mahah) to humans is actually the perfect, uncompromised timing of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 19:16: "And while he lingered, the men took hold of his hand, his wife’s hand, and the hands of his two daughters, the Lord being merciful to him, and they brought him out and set him outside the city."

  • Genesis 43:10: "For if we had not lingered, surely by now we would have returned this second time."

  • Judges 19:8: "And they delayed until afternoon; and both of them ate."

  • 2 Samuel 15:28: "See, I will tarry in the plains of the wilderness until word comes from you to inform me."

  • Psalm 119:60: "I made haste, and did not delay to keep Your commandments."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) translates mahah in Habakkuk 2:3 using the Greek verb chronizo, which means to spend time, linger, or delay. In the New Testament, the writer of Hebrews quotes the Septuagint version of Habakkuk 2:3-4 in Hebrews 10:37-38: "For 'yet a little while, and He who is coming will come and will not tarry.'" The New Testament writer applies this directly to the second coming of Jesus Christ. Just as the Old Testament saints had to trust that the vision of judgment and deliverance would not ultimately stall, New Covenant believers are exhorted to endure through persecution, knowing that the ultimate return of Christ will occur precisely on God's perfect schedule.
