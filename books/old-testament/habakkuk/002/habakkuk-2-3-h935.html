SURELY - ( bo , Strong's H935 )

Summary:

The English word "surely" in Habakkuk 2:3 translates a highly emphatic Hebrew grammatical construction using the verb bo, which fundamentally means "to come," "to enter," or "to arrive." By repeating the verb in the infinitive absolute form (bo) alongside its finite imperfect form (yabo), the Hebrew text stresses the absolute certainty, inevitability, and unstoppability of God's promised action. Though the fulfillment of the divine vision may appear to linger from a human perspective, its arrival is guaranteed by the character of God Himself. It is not a matter of if the promise will arrive, but when, as it marches inexorably toward its appointed time.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb bo depicts movement toward a goal, destination, or person. In the everyday life of the ancient Near East, it was used to describe entering a tent, coming into a city, or the sun setting (entering its chamber) at the end of the day. It is a word of active transit, progression, and arrival.

In prophetic literature, this spatial movement translates into a temporal and theological certainty. The vision given to Habakkuk is personified as a traveler on an active journey. It has been dispatched from the heavenly throne and is steadily making its way toward the theater of human history. The background of bo implies that once God sets a word or an event in motion, it possesses an unstoppable momentum that cannot be thwarted, turned back, or canceled by human or demonic interference.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of bo is to come in, enter, go in, or bring. However, its specific grammatical construction in Habakkuk 2:3 (bo yabo) drastically sharpens this meaning. Combining the infinitive absolute with the imperfect finite verb of the same root serves as the strongest possible way to express emphasis in Hebrew syntax.

This construction denotes absolute certainty, necessity, or inevitability. It translates literally as "coming it will come" or "arriving it will arrive." In English translations, this grammatical intensity is captured by helper words like "surely," "indeed," or "without fail." The lexical range of this emphatic usage encompasses:

  • Inevitable occurrence: It will absolutely happen.

  • Unpreventable arrival: No obstacle can hinder its coming.

  • Absolute reliability: The event is sealed and guaranteed.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb bo is used across the Old Testament to capture transitions from potentiality or promise into physical reality. It serves as a word picture in several ways:

  • The Harvest: The physical bringing in of crops or sheaves to the storehouse after a long season of labor.

  • The Sunset: The inevitable arrival of evening as the sun "enters" its daily resting place.

  • The Messenger: A runner arriving at the gates of a city with critical news from the battlefield.

In prophetic contexts, when God's word comes, it does not merely hang in the air as abstract information; it enters human history as an objective reality that physically alters the world.

4. Theological Implications

The emphatic use of bo carries heavy theological weight, particularly regarding the doctrine of God's faithfulness and sovereignty:

  • The Integrity of God's Word: Because God is not a man that He should lie, His spoken decrees carry the weight of finished realities. The bo yabo construction highlights that a promise made by God is already "on its way" and must arrive.

  • The Dynamic of Faith and Patience: God's timing is distinct from human timing. The passage balances "though it tarries, wait for it" with "because it will surely come." The theological tension is resolved by trusting in the character of the Promiser.

  • The Basis for Justification by Faith: This absolute certainty serves as the immediate foundation for the landmark declaration in Habakkuk 2:4 ("the just shall live by his faith"). Saving faith is not wishful thinking; it is a settled conviction resting on the inevitable arrival (bo) of God's word.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2, the prophet stands upon his watchtower, waiting for a divine answer to his agonizing questions regarding why God is using the wicked Babylonians to judge Judah. God's response is to write the vision clearly on tablets so that a herald may run with it.

The prophet and the people of God are tempted to despair because of the apparent delay of God's justice. God directly answers this temptation with bo yabo. From a human perspective, the vision "tarries" (appears to linger sluggishly), but from the divine perspective, "it will not tarry" (it will not be late by even a second). The emphatic "surely come" assures the covenant community that God's justice and deliverance are actively marching toward them, fully synchronized with His perfect, predetermined schedule.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 126:6 - "He who continually goes forth weeping, bearing seed for sowing, Shall doubtless come again with rejoicing, Bringing his sheaves with him." (Uses the exact same emphatic bo yabo construction to guarantee the harvest of joy after a season of sorrow).

  • Exodus 20:24 - "An altar of earth you shall make for Me... In every place where I record My name I will come to you, and I will bless you." (Uses bo to show the certain, personal presence of God meeting His people in worship).

  • Zechariah 14:5 - "Thus the LORD my God will come, And all the saints with You." (Uses bo to describe the physical, certain return of Yahweh to establish His kingdom).

  • Malachi 3:1 - "And the Lord, whom you seek, Will suddenly come to His temple... Behold, He is coming,' Says the LORD of hosts." (Uses bo twice to promise the certain arrival of the Messiah).

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the New Testament, the author of Hebrews quotes Habakkuk 2:3–4 to encourage persecuted Jewish Christians to persevere (Hebrews 10:37–38). Under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, the author of Hebrews shifts the neuter "it" (the vision) to a masculine personal pronoun: "He who is coming will come and will not tarry."

The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) paved the way for this by translating the Hebrew bo with the Greek erchomenos ("the coming one"). This demonstrates that the ultimate fulfillment of the "vision" that would "surely come" is not merely an event, but a Person—Jesus Christ. His first coming fulfilled the historical promise of redemption, and His second coming will fulfill the ultimate promise of universal justice.
