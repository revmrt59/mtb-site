PROUD - ( apal , Strong's H6075 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb apal fundamentally refers to swelling, being puffed up, or lifting oneself up in a state of pride and self-presumption. In the context of Habakkuk 2:4, it describes a soul that is arrogant and self-reliant, standing in stark contrast to the righteous person who lives by faith and trust in God. It paints a picture of someone whose inner life is bloated with self-importance, leading them to act presumptuously rather than waiting submissively on the Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of apal carries a physical, concrete image of swelling or rising up. In its noun forms, it refers to a mound, hill, fort, or stronghold—a raised, elevated place. When applied metaphorically to human character and attitude, it depicts a person who is mentally and spiritually "puffed up." They swell with self-confidence, raising themselves up like a fortress of self-reliance. Instead of bowing before the Creator, the proud person elevates their own desires, intellect, and strength, creating an internal monument to their own ego.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb apal means to swell, be puffed up, or be arrogant. In the active causative sense, it carries the meaning of acting presumptuously, heedlessly, or headstrongly, pushing forward in self-will without divine authorization. It expresses a state of mind that is loftily self-sufficient, leading to reckless and defiant actions. This pride is not merely an emotion but an orientation of the heart that refuses to acknowledge dependence on God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The biblical usage of apal is rare but highly potent, illustrating two primary facets of pride:

  • The Puffed-Up Soul: It represents an inflated sense of self-worth and capability that lacks any true foundation. Like a bubble, it looks large and substantial but is entirely hollow and easily popped.

  • Presumptuous Action: In historical narrative, the word describes the reckless arrogance of acting apart from God's command. It is the attitude of those who push forward in their own strength, ignoring divine warnings because they trust in their own capability.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, apal exposes the root of all sin: the desire of the creature to usurp the place of the Creator. Pride is a spiritual inflation that distorts reality, making the individual believe they are self-sustaining. In Scripture, this self-elevation is the direct opposite of faith. While faith looks outward to God for life and justification, pride looks inward to self. Consequently, the proud soul is inherently unstable and crooked. God opposes the proud because their self-inflation is a direct assault on His sovereign glory.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:4, apal stands in direct opposition to faith (emunah). The prophet Habakkuk is waiting for God's answer in a time of national distress and impending judgment by the Chaldeans. God contrasts two ways of living: the way of the Chaldean conqueror (and any self-reliant person) whose soul is puffed up (apal) and not upright, and the way of the righteous person who lives by faithful trust in God. The proud person tries to secure their own life through power, wealth, and arrogance, but their soul is hollow and doomed to fail. True life is found not in self-inflation, but in humble, enduring faith.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 14:44 - "But they presumed to go up to the mountaintop; nevertheless, the ark of the covenant of the Lord and Moses did not depart from the camp."

  • Habakkuk 2:4 - "Behold the proud, his soul is not upright in him; but the just shall live by his faith."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The cognate noun ophel is used frequently in the Old Testament to describe a physical hill, citadel, or stronghold (such as the Ophel in Jerusalem). This physical connection reinforces the spiritual truth of Habakkuk 2:4: the proud person attempts to build their own internal "Ophel"—their own high fortress of defense and security. However, these human citadels are temporary and fragile. The only true stronghold for the soul is the Lord Himself, accessed through humble faith rather than self-exaltation.
