DESIRE - ( nephesh , Strong's H5315 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word nephesh fundamentally refers to the seat of vitality, breath, inner being, appetite, and desire. In Habakkuk 2:5, it highlights the insatiable craving and ravenous appetite of the proud, specifically referring to the Babylonian empire's predatory lust for conquest, which is compared to the bottomless hunger of Sheol (the grave) and death.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primary concrete meaning of nephesh relates to the throat, neck, or breath, which is the physical channel of life and nourishment. Historically, it traces back to the idea of breathing or gasping, representing the essential life force within a living being. Because the throat is the organ through which a person breathes, drinks, and eats, the word naturally expanded to symbolize appetite, longing, and intense desire. To "enlarge one's nephesh" is a vivid physical metaphor of opening one's throat wide, like a predatory beast or a gaping cavern, ready to consume everything in its path.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • nephesh possesses a broad semantic range that encompasses:

  • The breath of life, animal vitality, or the living being itself.

  • The inner self, including emotions, passions, and the will.

  • The physical throat or neck as the organ of breathing and swallowing.

  • Appetite, hunger, craving, or desire, whether physical (for food and drink) or metaphorical (for power, wealth, or conquest).

In the context of Habakkuk 2:5, nephesh refers specifically to this metaphorical appetite or greedy desire, expressing a relentless, unsatisfied hunger for more territory, wealth, and power.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Gaping Throat of Sheol: nephesh is frequently paired with Sheol or death to depict an unquenchable hunger. Just as the grave never says "enough" and continues to swallow up the living, the proud conqueror's nephesh expands to devour nations.

  • The Seat of Passion and Will: The word represents the center of a person’s driving ambitions. When a person sets their nephesh on something, they pursue it with their entire being.

  • The Vulnerable Life: It can also represent the fragile physical life that can be taken away, highlighting the contrast between the conqueror's self-proclaimed immortality and his ultimate mortality.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nephesh in Habakkuk 2:5 illustrates the destructive nature of unchecked human desire detached from God. When a person or nation replaces worship of the Creator with self-deification, their inner desire (nephesh) becomes a bottomless pit. This insatiable appetite is a form of idolatry that ultimately leads to death. God's judgment is designed to expose the futility of this pride, showing that what is acquired through ruthless greed can never satisfy the human soul, which was created to find its rest and fulfillment only in God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:5, the prophet is describing the arrogant Chaldean (Babylonian) empire. The passage contrasts the righteous person, who lives by faith, with the proud conqueror, whose "desire" is bloated and expansionist. The NKJV accurately translates nephesh here as "desire" because the context emphasizes the Babylonian appetite for conquest. By saying the proud man "enlarges his desire as hell [Sheol]," the text employs a terrifying hyperbole: the empire's lust for domination is as vast and unyielding as the realm of the dead. No matter how many nations Babylon subdues, its appetite is never satisfied, setting up the certainty of its eventual collapse under the weight of its own greed.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 21:10 – "The soul of the wicked desires evil; His neighbor finds no favor in his eyes."

  • Psalm 107:9 – "For He satisfies the longing soul, and fills the hungry soul with goodness."

  • Proverbs 27:7 – "A satisfied soul loathes the honeycomb, but to a hungry soul every bitter thing is sweet."

  • Micah 7:3 – "That they may successfully do evil with both hands— The prince asks for gifts, The judge seeks a bribe, And the great man utters his evil desire; So they scheme together."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The comparison of the Babylonian empire's desire to Sheol and death highlights the ironic law of retribution (lex talionis). Because the proud man behaves like Sheol—devouring peoples and nations without limit—he will eventually be swallowed up by Sheol himself. The very throat (nephesh) that he opened wide to consume others will be closed forever by the judgment of God. This serves as a pastoral warning against the deceitfulness of riches and the pride of self-sufficiency.
