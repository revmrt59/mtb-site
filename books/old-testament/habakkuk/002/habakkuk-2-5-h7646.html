SATISFIED - ( saba , Strong's H7646 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb saba primarily denotes being filled, sated, or completely satisfied, often in physical terms such as eating and drinking. In a theological and ethical context, however, it describes the human heart's capacity for contentment—or, in the case of the wicked, an insatiable greed that mimics the grave. In Habakkuk 2:5, saba exposes the tragic, self-destructive nature of pride and empire, showing that apart from God, human desire becomes an infinite void that can never be filled.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete background of saba is rooted in the agricultural and domestic spheres of ancient Israel. It depicts the sensory experience of reaching absolute capacity. It is the grazing animal that has eaten until it lies down content, the traveler who has quenched his thirst at a well, or the family enjoying the fullness of a harvest feast. The word carries no inherent negative or positive moral value in its root form; it simply describes the state of being completely full, where nothing more can be received, added, or desired. It represents the physical reality of "enough."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb saba means to be sated, satisfied, or filled to the point of excess. It functions in several distinct categories across the Old Testament:

  • Physical satiety: Eating to one's fill, being satisfied with bread, water, or agricultural harvest.

  • Experiential fullness: Being satisfied with years of life, honor, riches, or favor.

  • Spiritual contentment: Delighting in God’s goodness, mercy, and presence.

  • Negative satiety: Being gorged, weary, or filled to excess with trouble, contempt, or divine judgment.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, saba is frequently contrasted with hunger, emptiness, and scarcity. In the poetic and prophetic books, the word is often personified to emphasize the sheer scale of a situation:

  • The natural world: The earth is "satisfied" with the rain sent from heaven, and trees are "full of sap" (literally, satisfied with moisture).

  • Judgment: The sword of the Lord is poetically described as being "sated" or "satisfied" with the blood of His enemies.

  • Desire: The human eye and the grave (Sheol) are portrayed as entities with endless appetites that can never be saba.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, saba reveals that true satisfaction is a covenantal gift from God, found only in relationship with Him. Humans were created with a capacity for God that cannot be satisfied by created things.

  • When humans seek satisfaction in God, He fills their souls with good things, transforming their inner hunger into contentment and praise.

  • When humans seek satisfaction apart from God, they experience a tragic spiritual feedback loop. The appetite (nephesh, often translated as "soul" or "desire") expands but is never filled.

  • Consequently, the wicked are often described as being "filled" (saba) not with joy, but with their own ways, with shame, or with the consequences of their rebellion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:5, God is speaking to the prophet about the arrogant Chaldean (Babylonian) empire. The text states: "Because he enlarges his desire as hell, and he is like death, and cannot be satisfied."

Here, the inability to be satisfied (saba) is a spiritual diagnosis of pride. The Babylonians are drunk on conquest and power. Instead of finding peace in what they have, their pride "enlarges their desire" (literally, broadens their nephesh, their throat or appetite) to match Sheol (the realm of the dead). Just as death continuously swallows human lives and is never full, the Babylonian empire swallows up nations and is never sated.

This contextual use of saba highlights the self-destructive irony of greed. The conqueror believes that the next territory, the next victory, or the next sum of plunder will finally bring satisfaction. Yet, because his soul is detached from the only One who can truly satisfy, his capacity for hunger grows faster than his ability to consume. His insatiability is not just a sin; it is his ultimate torment and the very driver of his eventual collapse.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 17:15: "As for me, I will see Your face in righteousness; I shall be satisfied when I awake in Your likeness."

  • Psalm 103:5: "Who satisfies your mouth with good things, So that your youth is renewed like the eagle’s."

  • Proverbs 27:20: "Hell and Destruction are never full; So the eyes of man are never satisfied."

  • Isaiah 58:11: "The LORD will guide you continually, And satisfy your soul in drought, And strengthen your bones; You shall be like a watered garden, And like a spring of water, whose waters do not fail."

7. Other Relevant Insights

An intriguing linguistic connection exists between the root of saba (to satisfy) and the Hebrew word sheba (the number seven). In ancient Semitic thought, the number seven represents completeness, covenant oath-taking, and perfection. To be "satisfied" (saba) is conceptually related to being "sevened"—brought to a state of absolute completeness where nothing is lacking.

In the New Testament, this concept is fully realized in the ministry of Jesus Christ. He declares that those who hunger and thirst for righteousness "shall be filled" (Matthew 5:6), utilizing the Greek equivalent of saba. While the Babylonian model of Habakkuk 2:5 attempts to achieve fullness by conquering and taking, the Kingdom of God teaches that true satisfaction is received only by emptying oneself and trusting in the provision of the Savior.
