RIDDLE - ( chidah , Strong's H2420 )

Summary:

In Habakkuk 2:6, the word chidah denotes an enigmatic, mocking saying—a taunting riddle—used to expose the spiritual absurdity of the proud oppressor. This term describes a puzzle that requires spiritual discernment to understand. While the conquering Babylonians appear secure in their stolen wealth, the prophet uses a riddle to reveal that they are actually burying themselves in debt. Ultimately, the riddle serves as a tool of divine irony, demonstrating how God turns the apparent wisdom and power of the wicked into their own undoing.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun chidah derives from the root verb chud, which means to tie in knots, twist, or weave together. This root gives the noun chidah the literal sense of something knotted or intricate. Historically, a riddle in the ancient Near East was not merely a child's game; it was a serious test of wisdom, diplomatic skill, and intellectual prowess. It represented an indirect form of speech where the true meaning was wrapped in layers of metaphor, requiring the listener to untangle the "knots" to find the truth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun chidah refers to an enigma, a dark saying, a parable, or a perplexing question. It designates speech that is intentionally oblique or obscure rather than straightforward. The term does not imply a falsehood, but rather a profound truth wrapped in figurative language. In biblical poetry, it is closely associated with mashal (a proverb or ethical maxim) and melitsah (a mocking satire or figure of speech). It represents a communication that demands careful meditation and spiritual insight to comprehend.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • chidah appears 17 times in the Hebrew Bible, primarily in Wisdom Literature, prophetic poetry, and historical narratives.

  • It portrays the intellectual tests of foreign dignitaries, such as the Queen of Sheba testing Solomon's legendary wisdom.

  • It describes Samson's wager-riddle, which was woven out of his personal experience with the lion and the honey.

  • It represents prophetic allegories, such as the riddle of the two eagles and the vine in Ezekiel, where political realities are cloaked in vivid agricultural symbols.

  • It characterizes the mysterious, deep wisdom of God's redemptive history, which is disclosed to the faithful through poetic instruction.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Sovereignty of Divine Wisdom: God is the author of the ultimate riddles of history. What seems chaotic or triumphant to human eyes (such as the rise of a ruthless empire like Babylon) is actually a knot that God is weaving and will eventually untie in judgment.

  • The Blindness of Human Pride: The wicked are blind to the spiritual reality of their actions. They believe they are accumulating wealth, but the divine riddle exposes that they are actually accumulating judgment.

  • Wisdom as Spiritual Discernment: Understanding God's ways requires more than human intellect; it requires a heart aligned with God's covenant. God reveals His truth to the humble while leaving the proud ensnared in their own ignorance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:6, the word is paired with "taunting" (melitsah) to form "a taunting riddle against him." Habakkuk has complained about the violence and injustice of the Chaldeans (Babylonians). God responds by assuring the prophet that Babylon's triumph is temporary. The subject nations will eventually rise up and mock their former captors using a proverb and a riddle.

The riddle itself begins immediately in the text: "Woe to him who increases what is not his... And to him who loads himself with many pledges!" The irony (the "knot") of the riddle is that the extortioner is not actually becoming rich; he is simply accumulating an immense debt ("pledges" or collateral) that he must eventually pay back with interest. The plunderer is actually the debtor, and his victims will suddenly awake as creditors to collect.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint, the Greek translation of the Old Testament, chidah in Habakkuk 2:6 is translated by the word problema, which refers to a task, a question proposed, or a barrier thrown down. This underscores that the judgment of God is a moral problem that the wicked cannot solve. In the New Testament, this concept of indirect, wisdom-revealing speech is closely aligned with parabole (parable). Just as Jesus used parables to reveal truth to His disciples while hiding it from the hard-hearted, God uses the "taunting riddle" of Habakkuk to comfort the righteous while confounding the proud Chaldeans.
