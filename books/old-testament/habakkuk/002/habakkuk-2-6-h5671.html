PLEDGES - ( abtit , Strong's H5671 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word abtît refers to a heavy burden of debt, specifically goods or property taken as pledges or collateral for loans. Occurring only once in the Hebrew Bible (a hapax legomenon) in Habakkuk 2:6, it carries a double meaning. While it literally denotes the accumulation of pledges taken from debtors, it also functions as a wordplay. The King James Version famously translated it as "thick clay" by splitting the word into ab (thick) and tit (clay). In its context, the word describes the Babylonian empire as an aggressive, predatory creditor that extorts nations, stacking up stolen goods like a mountain of unpaid, heavy security deposits that will eventually collapse under the weight of its own greed.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word abtît is derived from the root ‘ābaṭ, which means to give a pledge, to lend on pledge, or to entangle. In the ancient Near East, a pledge was a piece of personal property—such as a cloak, millstone, or garment—given by a debtor to a creditor as security for a loan. This pledge was held as collateral to guarantee repayment.

Under Mosaic Law, taking pledges was strictly regulated to prevent the oppression of the poor. A creditor could not enter a debtor's house to seize a pledge, nor could they keep a poor man's cloak overnight, as it was his only warmth.

In the context of Habakkuk, Babylon is depicted as a ruthless, unlawful creditor. Instead of caring for the needy, Babylon pillages nations, accumulating massive piles of plundered wealth. The word picture is one of a greedy extortionist whose house is dangerously packed to the ceiling with stolen collateral, creating an unstable tower of debt that is destined to crash down.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun abtît denotes a mass of pledges, heavy debt, or items taken as security for loans. It represents the accumulation of debt-securities.

Historically, there is a famous linguistic ambiguity with this word. Ancient rabbinic and medieval commentators, followed by the King James translators, split the word into two components: ‘āb (thickness) and ṭîṭ (mud or clay), translating it as "thick clay." Modern lexicography, however, confirms that abtît is a single word—an intensive noun form derived from the root ‘ābaṭ (to take a pledge). Thus, the proper meaning is "pledges" or "heavy weight of debt."

The term represents not just a single pledge, but a massive collection of pledges, symbolizing compounding debt and systemic extortion.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In Habakkuk 2:6, the word is used in a taunt-song (or "woe" oracle) directed against the Chaldeans (Babylonians). The prophet uses the image of an insatiable, predatory lender who forces others into crushing debt.

The word picture is intensely ironic: Babylon thinks it is accumulating vast wealth, but in reality, it is only accumulating "pledges." These pledges are not true riches; they are symbols of debt that must eventually be settled.

This creates a picture of a man loading himself down with heavy bags of collateral. The heavier the bags, the more difficult it is to move, and the more certain his eventual collapse. The "wealth" of the oppressor is actually a crushing weight of moral and spiritual debt before God.

4. Theological Implications

  • Sovereign Justice and Retribution: The use of abtît demonstrates the biblical principle of lex talionis (the law of retaliation). Those who extort and enslave others through debt will themselves be plundered. The debtor nations will eventually rise up as "creditors" to demand payment from Babylon (Habakkuk 2:7).

  • The Illusion of Wealth: Earthly wealth gained through oppression is an illusion. It is not true asset accumulation but a massive liability. In God's economy, ill-gotten gains are recorded as debts that must be paid back with interest.

  • Ethics of Borrowing and Lending: God holds nations and individuals accountable for financial exploitation. Accumulating pledges through violence and theft violates the covenantal care for the vulnerable, drawing divine woes and judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:6, the prophet asks, "how long?" as he watches the proud Babylonian empire expand by crushing neighboring nations. The phrase "loads himself with pledges" highlights the sheer scale of Babylon's greed.

The nuance here is one of self-destruction. Babylon does not realize that every nation it plunders is another pledge added to its cargo. The cargo is becoming too heavy to bear.

The next verse (Habakkuk 2:7) immediately shifts the metaphor: "Will they not rise up suddenly who bite you? Will they not awaken who oppress you? And you will become boot to them." The debtors (the plundered nations) will suddenly awake, "bite" (a Hebrew play on the word našak, which means both "to bite" and "to charge interest"), and collect the debt by plundering Babylon. The "pledges" Babylon gathered will be reclaimed with violent interest.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because abtît (H5671) is a hapax legomenon (occurring only once in the Old Testament), there are no other biblical verses containing this exact word. However, its close cognate noun ‘ăbôṭ (H5667), meaning "pledge," and its root verb ‘ābaṭ (H5670), meaning "to take or give a pledge," establish the theological and legal context. These related forms appear in the following key passages:

  • Deuteronomy 24:10: "When you lend your brother anything, you shall not go into his house to get his pledge."

  • Deuteronomy 24:11: "You shall stand outside, and the man to whom you lend shall bring the pledge out to you."

  • Deuteronomy 24:12: "And if the man is poor, you shall not keep his pledge overnight."

  • Deuteronomy 24:13: "You shall surely deliver the pledge to him again when the sun goes down, that he may sleep in his own garment and bless you; and it will be righteousness to you before the LORD your God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The "Thick Clay" Wordplay: While modern scholarship agrees that "pledges" is the correct translation of abtît, the historical translation of "thick clay" (from ab + tit) offers an interesting metaphorical parallel. Clay is heavy, sticky, dirty, and difficult to wash off. A person covered in thick clay is weighed down and trapped. Even if philologically secondary, this wordplay vividly captures the dirty, encumbering nature of ill-gotten wealth.

  • The Economic Trap: In the ancient world, default on debt led to debt-slavery. Babylon was turning the known world into a massive camp of debt-slaves. God's judgment is tailored to fit the crime: the master of debt-slaves will be enslaved by his former debtors.
