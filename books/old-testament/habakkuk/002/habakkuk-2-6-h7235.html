INCREASES - ( rabah , Strong's H7235 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb rabah primarily means to multiply, increase, or make great. In Habakkuk 2:6, it appears in the causative (Hiphil) form, describing the aggressive, relentless accumulation of wealth and territory by the Chaldeans (Babylonians). The passage pronounces a "woe" upon those who greedily heap up possessions and power that do not rightfully belong to them. This term highlights the insatiable nature of sinful ambition, which seeks to expand its borders and increase its storehouses through exploitation and extortion, ultimately leading to divine judgment and ruin.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, wealth and security were closely tied to land, livestock, and descendants. The root rabah carries a concrete, physical picture of expansion—like a herd multiplying in a pasture or a family line growing numerous over generations. In the covenantal framework of the Old Testament, this kind of increase was understood as a direct blessing from God. However, when humans attempt to force this increase through violence, plunder, and conquest, the word picture changes from a beautiful scene of organic growth to a grotesque portrait of overreaching greed. In Habakkuk 2:6, the increase is artificial, violent, and predatory, resembling a debtor who endlessly piles up collateral that he must eventually pay back with interest.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root verb rabah signifies to become many, become great, multiply, or increase.

  • In the Qal stem, it refers to the natural process of growing numerically strong or physically great.

  • In the Hiphil (causative) stem used in Habakkuk 2:6 (hammarbeh), the verb means to make numerous, to heap up, to multiply, or to gather in abundance.

  • Lexically, the participle form functions as a substantive noun: "the one who multiplies" or "he who increases." It describes an ongoing, characteristic action. The offender makes a lifestyle out of enlarging his holdings by taking what is not his.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, rabah is used to depict both holy and unholy increases:

  • The Blessing of Multiplication: It is the primary word used for God's creation mandate to "be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:22).

  • The Arrogance of Accumulation: When used of kings or empires, it describes the forbidden accumulation of horses, wives, silver, and gold (Deuteronomy 17:16–17).

  • The Weight of Debt: In Habakkuk 2:6, this "increase" is immediately paired with the image of "loading himself with many pledges." The word picture is that of a ruthless lender piling up heavy, dusty pledges (debts) from poor borrowers. The conqueror's "increase" is actually an accumulation of massive spiritual and moral debt that will eventually crush him.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, rabah in Habakkuk 2:6 exposes the folly of self-assertion and covetousness.

  • The Sovereignty of Blessings: True increase is a gift of God's grace. When humanity attempts to bypass God's moral order to manufacture their own "increase," they invite divine judgment.

  • The Law of Retribution: God’s universe is morally balanced. Those who increase their wealth by stealing from others will find their own troubles multiplied. The very people they plundered will suddenly "awake" to plunder them.

  • The Illusion of Power: Material and territorial expansion can look like safety and success to the human eye, but in God's eyes, an unjust increase is simply a heap of condemnation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Habakkuk 2:6 sits at the beginning of five "woe" judgments pronounced against the Chaldean empire. Babylon was a military machine that swallowed up nations to "increase" its own boundaries. The prophet uses rabah to mock this imperial ambition. The phrase "How long?" breaks into the description, signaling that God sets a boundary to human greed. The Babylonian expansion is characterized as "increasing what is not his." This highlights the illegitimacy of their gain. The nuance is highly ironic: the Babylonians think they are increasing their security, but they are actually increasing their liability before the righteous Judge of the earth.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:22 - "And God blessed them, saying, 'Be fruitful and multiply, and fill the waters in the seas, and let birds multiply on the earth.'" (Here, rabah is used twice, showing its original design as a blessing of life and abundance.)

  • Deuteronomy 17:17 - "Neither shall he multiply wives for himself, lest his heart turn away; nor shall he greatly multiply silver and gold for himself." (Using rabah to warn Israel's leaders against the self-centered, excessive accumulation of wealth and power.)

  • Proverbs 28:8 - "One who increases his possessions by usury and extortion gathers it for him who will pity the poor." (Directly linking the unjust "increase" of wealth with exploitation, warning that such gain will ultimately be redistributed by God.)

  • Ezekiel 16:51 - "Samaria did not commit half of your sins; but you have multiplied your abominations more than they, and have justified your sisters by all the abominations which you have done." (Using the verb to describe the tragic, rapid multiplication of sin and rebellion.)

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Participle's Continuous Force: The grammatical form in Habakkuk 2:6 emphasizes a continuous, unchecked habit. The Chaldean is someone who cannot stop grabbing; his identity is defined by this compulsive desire to increase his footprint.

  • The Septuagint (LXX) Translation: The ancient Greek translation uses the verb plethuno (to multiply or increase), which underscores the sheer volume of the stolen goods.

  • Pastoral Application: This passage serves as a sobering reminder for modern believers. Any pursuit of "increase"—whether in finance, influence, or status—that comes at the expense of integrity, justice, and the love of neighbor is a spiritual trap. What we unjustly accumulate today will become the weight that drags us down tomorrow.
