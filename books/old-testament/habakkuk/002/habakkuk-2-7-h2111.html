OPPRESS - ( zua , Strong's H2111 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word zua primarily means to tremble, shake, or quake under pressure or terror. In its intensive form in Habakkuk 2:7, it refers to those who violently agitate, vex, or terrorize others, causing them to shake with fear. While translated as "oppress" in the NKJV, the word paints a vivid picture of sudden, violent shaking and destabilization. It describes the terrifying reversal of roles where the Babylonians, who once made the nations tremble, will themselves be violently shaken and plundered by their creditors.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root zua conveys physical movement born of fear, agitation, or awe. It is the bodily reaction of trembling or shaking when confronted by a superior, terrifying force. Historically, this term is associated with the physical manifestation of terror—knees knocking, limbs quivering, and the loss of stability. In the ancient Near East, to "shake" or "agitate" someone was to utterly disrupt their security, peace, and political stability. In Habakkuk 2:7, the intensive, reduplicated Pilpel form (za'aza') heightens this picture to denote a violent, repeated shaking. It represents an aggressive, destabilizing assault that leaves the victim helpless and trembling in terror.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb zua means to tremble, shake, or move in fear. In the Qal stem, it describes physical trembling or shaking, such as the keepers of a house trembling in old age or a person shaking in anger or fear. In the Pilpel stem, used in Habakkuk 2:7, the meaning shifts to an active, transitive force: to cause to tremble, to shake violently, to agitate, or to terrorize. The noun derivatives, zewa'ah and za'awah, refer to an object of terror, horror, or a cause of shuddering. Thus, the translation "oppress" captures the oppressive, terrifying action of enemies who violently shake the security of a nation, throwing its people into a state of trembling panic.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Hebrew scriptures use zua to paint several vivid pictures of shaking and instability:

  • Domestic Shaking: The physical trembling of the body due to aging or fear, representing the loss of strength and security.

  • Political and Social Convulsion: The violent shaking of a kingdom or nation under the threat of invasion, where the stability of the state is shattered.

  • Terror and Agitation: The mental and emotional panic that causes physical quaking when God's judgment or a sudden enemy attack falls upon a complacent people.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, zua demonstrates the ultimate instability of human power apart from God. Throughout the Old Testament, nations that pride themselves on their military might and oppress others are eventually subjected to a divine shaking. God is the ultimate Sovereign who shakes the heavens, the earth, and the nations. When human empires build their security on violence and plunder, God ordains a counter-force to shake them to their foundations. This highlights the biblical truth that human oppression is temporary; those who make others tremble will eventually tremble under the weight of divine justice.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:7, the prophet addresses the proud Babylonian empire. Babylon had ruthlessly plundered and shaken many nations, making them tremble. However, God pronounces a woe of sudden reversal. The creditors and victims of Babylon will rise up suddenly and awake. These attackers are described as those who will "oppress" or "violently shake" Babylon. The contextual nuance is a perfect poetic justice: the very empire that lived by terrorizing and shaking the world will suddenly be shaken, terrorized, and plundered by those they previously dominated. Their security will evaporate in an instant, proving that no earthly empire is immune to God's hand of judgment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because zua is a relatively rare verb, its occurrences and those of its direct nominal derivatives (zewa'ah / za'awah) illustrate the theme of terror and shaking:

  • Esther 5:9 – "Yet when Haman saw Mordecai in the king’s gate, and that he did not stand or tremble before him, he was filled with indignation against Mordecai." (Using zua to describe physical trembling out of fear or deference).

  • Ecclesiastes 12:3 – "In the day when the keepers of the house tremble, and the strong men bow down..." (Using zua to describe the trembling of the hands or limbs in old age).

  • Deuteronomy 28:25 – "The Lord will cause you to be defeated before your enemies... and you shall become troublesome to all the kingdoms of the earth." (The word "troublesome" is the noun za'awah, meaning an object of terror or shaking).

  • Jeremiah 15:4 – "I will hand them over to trouble, to all kingdoms of the earth, because of Manasseh the son of Hezekiah..." (The word "trouble" is the noun zewa'ah, an object of shuddering or horror).

7. Other Relevant Insights

The root also yields the noun zewa'ah, which is frequently used by the prophets to describe the covenant curses of Deuteronomy coming to pass. When Israel disobeyed God, they became a "shaking" or a "horror" to the surrounding nations. The transition from the active verb zua (to shake/oppress) to the noun zewa'ah (an object of horror) underscores the completeness of divine judgment. The one who causes others to tremble eventually becomes a public spectacle of terror and warning to the rest of the world.
