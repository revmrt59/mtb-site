AWAKE - ( yaqats , Strong's H3364 )

Summary:

In Habakkuk 2:7, the Hebrew word yaqats describes the sudden, terrifying arousal of Babylon’s conquerors. Used in a prophetic taunt-song, the word emphasizes that those who were dormant, oppressed, or seemingly helpless will suddenly awaken with full vigor to execute God's judgment, catching the proud empire completely off-guard.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The physical action of waking up (yaqats) is a transition from a state of vulnerability, inactivity, and helplessness (sleep) to a state of alertness, consciousness, and active power. In the ancient world, sleep symbolized safety, peace, or complacency, but it also represented extreme vulnerability. To awaken suddenly—especially in a military, judicial, or covenantal context—represents the swift, unexpected mobilization of a sleeping giant or a dormant predator. The word picture in Habakkuk is of a sleeping creditor or a dormant victim who suddenly springs to consciousness, fully alert and ready to exact vengeance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yaqats is a primitive root meaning to awake, awaken, or be aroused from sleep. It specifically describes the physical transition from slumber to conscious activity. Lexically, the term emphasizes the starting point of awakening rather than the ongoing state of being awake. It denotes waking up naturally, being startled out of sleep by an external force, or being metaphorically stirred to action after a long period of silence and dormancy.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Physical Awakening: The word frequently describes individuals waking up from natural sleep or prophetic dreams, such as Jacob awakening from his vision of the ladder at Bethel, or Pharaoh waking after his distressing dreams.

  • Metaphorical Awakening of Divine Judgment: It illustrates Yahweh "awaking" to defend His covenant people. When God appears silent to His suffering people, He is poetically described as "sleeping"; when He acts in judgment, He "awakes" to scatter His foes.

  • Sudden Reversal of Nations: In prophetic literature, the word depicts the sudden arousal of dormant political and military forces destined to execute divine retribution on proud empires that thought they were secure.

4. Theological Implications

  • Sovereign Retribution: The awakening of Babylon's destroyers demonstrates that human empires do not operate in a vacuum. God orchestrates the rise and fall of nations, using the oppressed as instruments of His sovereign justice.

  • The Illusion of Impunity: Proud oppressors assume their security is permanent because their victims are silent. This word warns that silent victims are merely sleeping, and their awakening will be devastating.

  • Divine Timing: God’s judgment may seem delayed, as though He or His instruments are asleep, but the awakening is sudden, absolute, and perfectly timed according to His sovereign schedule.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:7, the prophet pronounces a series of woes against Babylon for its rapacious greed, extortion, and violence. The Chaldeans had plundered many nations, gathering debt and creating enemies. The rhetorical question, "Will they not awake who distress you?" uses yaqats to shock the hearer. The victims of Babylon's usury will suddenly "awake" like a creditor demanding payment or a predator springing into action. The word highlights the terrifying speed of this reversal: Babylon went to sleep as the master of the world and woke up to find its debtors demanding their due with violent force.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 28:16: "Then Jacob awoke from his sleep and said, 'Surely the LORD is in this place, and I did not know it.'"

  • Genesis 41:4: "And the ugly and gaunt cows ate up the seven fine-looking and fat cows. So Pharaoh awoke."

  • Judges 16:20: "And she said, 'The Philistines are upon you, Samson!' So he awoke from his sleep, and said, 'I will go out as before, at other times, and shake myself free!' But he did not know that the LORD had departed from him."

  • Psalm 78:65: "Then the Lord awoke as from sleep, Like a mighty man who shouts because of wine."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The literary design of Habakkuk 2 utilizes brilliant double meanings. The "debtors" who will "bite" and "awake" create a vivid financial and predatory metaphor. In ancient Near Eastern economics, the word for interest on a debt literally meant "a bite." The debtors "bite" back when they "awake" to demand their collateral, stripping Babylon of its ill-gotten gains in a sudden, ironic act of poetic justice.
