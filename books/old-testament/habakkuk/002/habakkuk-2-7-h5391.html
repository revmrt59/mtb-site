CREDITORS - ( nashak , Strong's H5391 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word translated "creditors" in Habakkuk 2:7 contains a brilliant double meaning that transitions from the physical world of venomous predators to the economic world of predatory lending. Rooted in a verb meaning "to bite," the term depicts usurious creditors as toxic predators whose financial demands slowly drain the life out of their victims. In the context of Habakkuk’s prophecy, Babylon’s ruthless exploitation of other nations is exposed as a form of predatory debt accumulation. In a sudden, sovereign reversal, God warns that Babylon’s own "creditors"—the nations they have victimized—will suddenly arise to "bite" them back, demonstrating that those who build their lives on extortion will eventually be devoured by the very forces they unleashed.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew root nashak evokes the sharp, sudden strike of a serpent injecting venom into its prey. In the ancient Near East, this physical action became a powerful metaphor for charging interest on loans. Just as a snakebite seems minor at first but slowly poisons the entire bloodstream, predatory lending acts as a slow-release venom that gradually drains a debtor of their assets, freedom, and dignity. The ancient mind did not separate economic reality from physical survival; to be trapped in debt was to be slowly eaten alive by a creditor.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of nashak is to strike or wound with the teeth, specifically used of snakes. The secondary, commercial definition is to lend money at interest, to practice usury, or to act as a creditor who exacts heavy financial penalties. In Habakkuk 2:7, the plural masculine participle functions as a noun (noshekeyka), literally meaning "those who bite you" or "your debtors/creditors." It describes those who hold a claim over another and are ready to extract payment by force.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Literal Biting: The word is used to describe the physical strike of venomous snakes, emphasizing the suddenness and lethality of the attack (Gen 49:17; Num 21:6).

  • Metaphorical Poisoning: It describes the destructive, creeping effects of excessive alcohol consumption, which initially seems pleasant but ultimately "bites" like a serpent (Prov 23:32).

  • Economic Oppression: It represents the forbidden practice of charging interest to fellow covenant community members, which was viewed as a predatory act that consumed the poor (Deut 23:19).

4. Theological Implications

God is the ultimate guardian of the vulnerable and the righteous judge of economic systems. Under the Mosaic Law, charging interest to fellow Israelites was strictly forbidden because the covenant community was to be built on brotherly love, mutual support, and mercy, rather than financial exploitation (Exod 22:25; Lev 25:36). When individuals or nations ignore this moral boundary and accumulate wealth through extortion, they violate God’s character. This word study reveals that God views economic exploitation not merely as a white-collar offense, but as a violent, predatory act akin to releasing venomous serpents among the weak. Consequently, His justice demands a proportional retribution.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:7, the prophet pronounces a woe against the Babylonian Empire, which had amassed vast wealth by plundering and enslaving surrounding nations. Babylon viewed itself as an untouchable predator. However, Habakkuk uses a brilliant double entendre: the very nations Babylon had "bitten" and bled dry would suddenly awake as Babylon's "creditors" (noshekeyka). The tables would turn in a single night. The nations that were once passive victims would rise up like startled, venomous serpents to strike Babylon, turning the proud empire into helpless plunder.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 49:17 - "Dan shall be a serpent by the way, A viper by the path, That bites the horse’s heels So that its rider shall fall backward."

  • Numbers 21:6 - "So the LORD sent fiery serpents among the people, and they bit the people; and many of the people of Israel died."

  • Proverbs 23:32 - "At the last it bites like a serpent, And stings like a viper."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint translation of Habakkuk 2:7 preserves the raw, physical bite of the Hebrew root by employing the Greek verb dakno, which means "to bite with the teeth." Rather than softening the text with a polite financial term, the ancient Greek translators chose to highlight the violent, animalistic nature of the judgment. This emphasizes that Babylon's collapse would not be a orderly bankruptcy, but a fierce, painful, and sudden tearing apart by those they had previously oppressed.
