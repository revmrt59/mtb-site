COVETS - ( batsa , Strong's H1214 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb batsa fundamentally means to cut off, break off, or tear away, which developed metaphorically into acquiring dishonest gain through violence, plunder, or greed. In Habakkuk 2:9, it describes a rapacious and destructive desire to amass wealth and security at the expense of others. This "evil gain" represents a false, self-centered security built on oppression, which ultimately invites God's swift judgment rather than providing the safety it promises.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of batsa is found in the physical action of cutting, breaking, or shearing. It was used in ancient times to describe a weaver cutting a finished piece of cloth from the loom, or a metalworker breaking off pieces of silver and gold. Over time, this concrete image became a vivid metaphor for greedily "cutting off" a portion of wealth for oneself. It pictures someone aggressively tearing away a piece of another person's livelihood, livestock, or land to satisfy their own hunger for security and power. It is not a passive wish but an active, tearing grab.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb batsa carries a primary semantic range of cutting off, breaking off, wounding, or bringing to an end. In its active, intensive forms, it denotes the act of gaining by violence, greedily pursuing unjust gain, or coveting. The word refers to the active, aggressive acquisition of wealth through illicit, deceptive, or oppressive means. It combines the inward sin of covetousness with the outward action of exploitation. It does not merely mean to desire what another has; it means to actively plunder or slice away another's portion for personal enrichment.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Weaving and Ending: The verb describes cutting off life itself, comparing a person's final days to a weaver cutting a thread from the loom (Job 6:9; Isaiah 38:12).

  • Violent Plunder: It illustrates a predator tearing its prey, representing thieves, bandits, or oppressive rulers who violently seize what is not theirs (Proverbs 1:19).

  • Unjust Gain: The noun cognate betsa frequently pairs with this verb to emphasize the dirty, ill-gotten nature of the acquired wealth (Jeremiah 6:13).

4. Theological Implications

  • Idolatry of Self-Sufficiency: Covetousness (batsa) seeks to establish security apart from God. In the prophetic books, nations and rulers attempt to build their "nest on high" to be delivered from the power of disaster, placing their faith in physical fortifications funded by blood and stolen goods.

  • The Law of Retribution: Scripture consistently warns that dishonest gain brings trouble to one’s own house (Proverbs 15:27). What is cut off from others is ultimately cut off from the covetous person by God's judgment.

  • Incompatibility with God's Character: God abhors the covetous (Psalm 10:3) because greed directly violates the love, justice, and mercy that characterize His covenant.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:9, the prophet pronounces the first of five "woes" against the Chaldeans (Babylonians). The phrase "covets evil gain" (botsea betsa ra) uses both the verb and its cognate noun together for maximum emphasis ("covets a covetousness of evil"). The Chaldeans were pillaging nations to fortify Babylon, hoping their wealth and high walls would keep them safe from ruin. The nuance here is the sheer futility of violent greed: the very stones and timber of their built houses will cry out in testimony against them. Their pursuit of security through exploitation is the very thing that seals their doom.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 27:8: "For what is the hope of the hypocrite, Though he may gain, If God takes away his life?"

  • Psalm 10:3: "For the wicked boasts of his heart's desire; He blesses the covetous whom the LORD abhors."

  • Proverbs 1:19: "So are the ways of everyone who is greedy for gain; It takes away the life of its owners."

  • Proverbs 15:27: "He who is greedy for gain troubles his own house, But he who hates bribes will live."

  • Jeremiah 6:13: "Because from the least of them even to the greatest of them, Everyone is given to covetousness; And from the prophet even to the priest, Everyone deals falsely."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Illusion of the Nest: The "nest on high" (Habakkuk 2:9) is an allusion to the eagle. It represents an ancient desire for impenetrable security. However, secure housing built on the exploitation of others has a structurally unsound foundation in the economy of God.

  • New Testament Parallel: This Old Testament concept of violent greed and self-security strongly parallels Jesus' parable of the Rich Fool in Luke 12:13–21, who tore down his barns to build larger ones, only for his soul to be required of him that very night.
