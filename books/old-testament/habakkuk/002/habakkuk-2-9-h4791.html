HIGH - ( marom , Strong's H4791 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun marom refers to a high place, height, or elevation, frequently used in scripture to depict physical altitudes, majestic celestial heights, the transcendent dwelling of God, or the haughty posture of human pride. In Habakkuk 2:9, the word describes the Chaldean empire’s self-destructive ambition to build an impregnable, elevated fortress—likened to an eagle's nest—in a futile attempt to secure itself against divine judgment. The passage exposes the ultimate failure of human self-sufficiency, showing that heights scaled through injustice provide no safety from the sovereign hand of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The imagery behind marom is rooted in the topography of the ancient Near East, where mountain ridges and high rocky cliffs served as natural military strongholds. To be on the "heights" was to be physically secure, militarily dominant, and out of reach from attacking forces. Birds of prey, particularly eagles, naturally build their nests in these inaccessible places to protect their young from predators. Ancient rulers co-opted this natural defense mechanism by constructing royal palaces, fortresses, and capitals on elevated ground. For a nation like Babylon, the physical height of their city walls and the geographic elevation of their strongholds created a psychological sense of absolute invincibility and separation from the vulnerabilities of the common plains.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun marom is derived from the root verb rum, which means to be high, exalted, or lifted up.

  • Literal meaning: A physical height, summit, mountain elevation, or the high heavens.

  • Metaphorical meaning: A position of political dominance, social superiority, haughtiness, or arrogant self-exaltation.

  • Spiritual meaning: The elevated, transcendent, and holy sphere where God dwells, far above earthly limitations.

In the syntax of Habakkuk 2:9, the word functions as an adverbial accusative of place, indicating the specific destination where the proud ruler intends to establish his dwelling: "on high."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture employs marom in several distinct ways to construct vivid theological pictures:

  • God's Transcendent Throne: The heights of heaven are described as God's sanctuary, demonstrating His absolute sovereignty and distance from human corruption.

  • Human Pride: The word represents the lofty eyes, speech, and attitude of the wicked who look down on others and believe they are unaccountable to God.

  • Natural Fortresses: The word illustrates the nests of eagles built on high cliffs, representing an attempt to create a perfect, impenetrable shelter.

4. Theological Implications

The desire to "set one's nest on high" represents the ancient sin of Babel—the pursuit of autonomy, security, and significance apart from God. When humanity seeks to elevate itself through its own strength, wealth, and violence, it attempts to usurp the place of the Almighty, who alone dwells legitimately on high. The scriptures consistently teach that those who exalt themselves will be brought low, while those who humble themselves will be exalted. Seeking security in self-made heights is an act of spiritual rebellion that mistakes physical elevation for immunity from divine moral laws.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:9, the prophet pronounces the first of five "woes" against the invading Chaldean (Babylonian) empire. The Chaldeans were systematically plundering surrounding nations to enrich their own empire, attempting to establish a dynasty that was secure from collapse. The phrase "set his nest on high" highlights the sheer arrogance of their geopolitical strategy. By acquiring "evil gain," they sought to fortify their cities and palaces to such an extent that they would be "delivered from the power of disaster." However, the verse exposes a profound spiritual blindness: a stronghold built on the blood of others is structurally hollow. God reveals that the very stones and timber of these high fortresses will cry out in testimony against their builders, proving that physical height cannot protect against moral and spiritual ruin.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Obadiah 1:3 - "The pride of your heart has deceived you, You who dwell in the clefts of the rock, Whose habitation is high; You who say in your heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?'"

  • Micah 6:6 - "With what shall I come before the LORD, And bow myself before the High God? Shall I come before Him with burnt offerings, With calves a year old?"

  • Psalm 93:4 - "The LORD on high is mightier Than the noise of many waters, Than the mighty waves of the sea."

  • Psalm 18:16 - "He sent from above, He took me; He drew me out of many waters."

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a powerful literary contrast in the book of Habakkuk between the false heights of the wicked and the true heights of the righteous. While the proud Chaldean seeks to build a nest "on high" (marom) through violence and greed, the prophet Habakkuk ends his book with a song of triumph, declaring that God is his strength, who "will make my feet like deer’s feet, and He will make me walk on my high hills [bamah]" (Habakkuk 3:19). The wicked attempt to climb to safety by crushing others, only to fall; the righteous are lifted to high places of spiritual security by the grace and power of God.
