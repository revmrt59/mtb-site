DELIVERED - ( natsal , Strong's H5337 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb natsal fundamentally means to snatch away, rescue, or deliver from danger, captivity, or ruin. In Habakkuk 2:9, it is used to expose the foolishness of human self-reliance. While God is the ultimate agent of true deliverance throughout Scripture, the greedy and prideful try to deliver themselves from disaster by accumulating dishonest wealth and building secure, high fortresses. This highlights the deception of seeking security and salvation through earthly power and injustice rather than through trust in God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical background of natsal involves the action of snatching something away, stripping, or tearing something out of danger. It carries the image of a shepherd snatching a lamb out of the jaws of a predator, or pulling a burning branch out of a fire. It implies a situation of imminent peril where the subject is helpless unless an outside force actively intervenes to pluck them out. In the history of Israel, the word is deeply connected to the Exodus, where God powerfully snatched His people out of the crushing grip of Egypt.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In its basic form, natsal means to snatch away, draw out, or rescue. It occurs over 200 times in the Old Testament, most frequently in the Hiphil stem, where it means to deliver, rescue, or save. In Habakkuk 2:9, the word is used in the Niphal infinitive construct form (hinnatsel), meaning "to be delivered," "to rescue oneself," or "to find escape." The lexical range of the word includes:

  • To rescue from physical enemies or mortal danger.

  • To deliver from spiritual ruin, guilt, or the consequences of sin.

  • To snatch away property or plunder (stripping an enemy).

  • To escape safely from a threatening situation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Shepherd's Rescue: A vivid picture of natsal is found in the prophets, where it describes a shepherd rescuing a remnant of a sheep—perhaps only two legs or a piece of an ear—from the mouth of a lion. This emphasizes that deliverance is often an urgent, rescue-from-the-brink action.

  • Divine Intervention: The term is heavily used in the Psalms to petition God for rescue from distress, persecutors, and the grave. It positions God as the active rescuer who reaches down to snatch His people from deep waters.

  • The Failure of Self-Deliverance: In contrast to divine rescue, Scripture often uses natsal to describe the complete inability of idols, riches, or military power to deliver a person in the day of God's wrath.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, natsal reinforces the truth that true deliverance is a divine prerogative. Salvation and safety belong to the Lord alone. When humanity attempts to achieve natsal through their own strength, wealth, or political alliances, they commit idolatry. The prideful attempt at self-deliverance is a rejection of God's sovereignty. Habakkuk 2:9 illustrates this tragic spiritual reality: the wicked try to bypass God's moral order and secure their own lives through extortion, only to find that their self-made fortress offers no escape from divine judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 2:9, the prophet pronounces a woe against the Babylonian Empire (and by extension, anyone who operates in the same spirit). The Babylonians sought to "set [their] nest on high." This is a metaphor comparing the empire to an eagle building its nest on an inaccessible cliff, out of reach of predators. The explicit goal of this prideful height is "that he may be delivered from the power of disaster" (literally, "the hand of evil").

The irony is sharp: the Chaldeans used violence and plunder to build a fortress they believed would make them untouchable. However, because their security was built on sin, the very stones of their walls would cry out against them. The passage reveals that seeking security through injustice is a self-defeating illusion; the very means used to escape disaster actually guarantee it.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 32:11 - "Deliver me, I pray, from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau; for I fear him, lest he come and attack me and the mother with the children."

  • Exodus 3:8 - "So I have come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians, and to bring them up from that land to a good and large land..."

  • Psalm 33:19 - "To deliver their soul from death, And to keep them alive in famine."

  • Proverbs 10:2 - "Treasures of wickedness profit nothing, But righteousness delivers from death."

  • Jeremiah 1:8 - "Do not be afraid of their faces, For I am with you to deliver you," says the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The phrase "power of disaster" in Habakkuk 2:9 uses the Hebrew word yad (hand). Throughout the Old Testament, natsal is almost always paired with escaping from "the hand" of an enemy. In this verse, "disaster" or "evil" (ra) is personified as a powerful enemy with a reaching hand. The wicked believe their high fortress keeps them out of evil's reach, but apart from God, no height is high enough.

  • This verse stands in stark contrast to Habakkuk 2:4, which declares that "the just shall live by his faith." The righteous person finds safety by trusting in God's faithfulness, while the wicked person tries to grasp safety through greed and self-exaltation.
