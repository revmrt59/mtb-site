PRAYER - ( tepillah , Strong's H8605 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun tepillah refers to an earnest prayer, petition, or intercessory plea directed to God, often set within a communal, liturgical, or poetic context. Derived from a root verb meaning to intervene, mediate, or arbitrate, tepillah is not a casual wish list but a solemn appeal to the sovereign Judge of the universe. In Habakkuk 3:1, this word introduces a highly intense, musical intercession where the prophet pleads for covenant mercy amidst impending national judgment, demonstrating that true biblical prayer shifts the focus of the believer from earthly terror to God's historic salvation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The background of tepillah is closely tied to its root verb, palal, which conveys the action of mediating, arbitrating, or executing judgment.

  • When a petitioner offers a tepillah, they are metaphorically standing in a courtroom before the supreme Judge, presenting a case that they cannot resolve on their own strength.

  • This word picture evokes a posture of deep humility, absolute dependence, and submission. It represents an appeal to a higher authority to intervene and set wrong things right.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The noun tepillah denotes prayer, entreaty, or supplication.

  • It designates both private, emotional outpourings of the soul and formal, structured liturgical petitions or hymns of praise.

  • The term encompasses the entire range of communication with God, emphasizing an earnest seeking of His favor, intervention, and alignment with His sovereign will.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Tepillah occurs over 70 times in the Old Testament, predominantly in the book of Psalms, where it frequently functions as a title or superscript for specific prayers of David, Moses, and others.

  • It is poetically described as "rising up" to God's holy temple, symbolizing that God hears and receives the prayers of His people.

  • It is associated with the pouring out of the soul, depicting a desperate, unfiltered release of grief, anxiety, or hope before the Lord.

  • It is frequently paired with crying out, showing that biblical tepillah is often accompanied by vocal, emotional intensity rather than silent, detached contemplation.

4. Theological Implications

  • Covenant Foundation: Tepillah is inherently relational. It is not an attempt to appease an unpredictable deity, but an appeal to the covenant-making God of Israel who has committed Himself to His people.

  • God's Attentiveness: The use of this word carries the strong theological conviction that God is not distant. He is a God who hears and regards the prayer of the destitute.

  • Divine Intervention: Because of its connection to arbitration, tepillah underscores that God is the final arbiter of history, human suffering, and national destiny.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 3:1, tepillah introduces a remarkable literary shift; the book transitions from a dialogue between the prophet and God into a formal, sung liturgy.

  • The prayer is set "on Shigionoth," which indicates a highly emotional, erratic, or passionate musical rhythm, showing that this tepillah is an intense, heart-wrenching song of worship.

  • Given the context of the Babylonian invasion, Habakkuk's prayer is not for escape, but for mercy during judgment ("In wrath remember mercy").

  • This usage shows that tepillah can be a vehicle for wrestling with terror, remembering God's past works, and ultimately arriving at absolute joy in God's salvation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Kings 8:28 – "Yet regard the prayer of Your servant and his supplication, O Lord my God, to listen to the cry and the prayer which Your servant is praying before You today:"

  • Psalm 102:17 – "He shall regard the prayer of the destitute, And shall not despise their prayer."

  • Isaiah 56:7 – "Even them I will bring to My holy mountain, And make them joyful in My house of prayer. Their burnt offerings and their sacrifices Will be accepted on My altar; For My house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations."

  • Jonah 2:7 – "When my soul fainted within me, I remembered the Lord; And my prayer went up to You, Into Your holy temple."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • In the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), tepillah is translated by the word proseuche, which became the primary word for prayer in the New Testament.

  • The plural form of this word, tepillot, is used in Psalm 72:20 to summarize the entire collection of David's prayers ("The prayers of David the son of Jesse are ended"), showing that a "prayer" in the Hebrew mind was often synonymous with a sung psalm.
