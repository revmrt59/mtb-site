UTTERED - ( nathan , Strong's H5414 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nathan is a fundamental and highly versatile term meaning "to give," "to put," or "to set." In Habakkuk 3:10, where it is translated as "uttered," it is paired with the noun for "voice" to form a vivid idiomatic expression meaning to sound, thunder, or cry out. In this majestic description of God’s sovereign march, the deep abyss is personified as a submissive subject, giving forth its roaring cry and lifting its watery waves like hands in involuntary, awe-struck surrender to the Creator.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb nathan is one of the most frequently used words in the Hebrew Bible, appearing over 2,000 times. At its core, it represents the act of transfer—handing something over, placing something in a location, or assigning a status. When applied to speech or sound, nathan represents "giving" or "issuing" a sound from the throat.

In ancient Near Eastern cosmology, the "deep" (tehom) represented the untamed, chaotic, and terrifying forces of nature. For the deep to "give its voice" (natan qolo) is a powerful word picture. Rather than acting as a rival power or an uncontrollable entity, the terrifying depths of the ocean act as a servant delivering a submissive cry. The sound of the roaring sea is cast not as a threat, but as an involuntary shout of reverence to its true Master.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of nathan is "to give," "to yield," "to bestow," or "to commit." When the verb is grammatically joined with qol (voice or sound), the idiom means:

  • To utter a sound or cry aloud.

  • To thunder (when referring to God's voice).

  • To roar or shout (when referring to nature or beasts).

In Habakkuk 3:10, the phrase natan tehom qolo translates literally as "the deep gave its voice." The lexical force of the word here emphasizes that the sound made by the ocean is a yielded response. The sea is not merely making noise; it is rendering a forced acknowledgment of God’s supreme power.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, nathan is used to construct vivid sensory descriptions of both divine and earthly actions:

  • The Divine Voice: When God nathan His voice, it is a metaphor for thunder, depicting His absolute power over the universe, shaking the heavens and the earth.

  • The Roar of Beasts: When young lions nathan their voice, it describes the terrifying, booming roar they produce when securing their prey.

  • The Cry of the Suffering: It is used to describe weeping aloud or crying out in distress, where the individual "gives" their voice to grief.

In Habakkuk's poetic vision, the ocean does not simply crash; it speaks. The roaring of the waves is translated into a submissive cry of terror and adoration, showing that even the most lawless parts of creation obey the divine command.

4. Theological Implications

The use of nathan in this context carries profound theological truths for the believer:

  • The Absolute Sovereignty of God: There is no element of creation, no matter how wild or chaotic, that can stand independent of God. The deep must "give" its voice to Him. What humans fear as uncontrollable chaos is to God merely a submissive servant.

  • The Response of Creation to Theophany: When God reveals Himself, creation cannot remain indifferent. The mountains writhe, the skies pour down water, and the ocean depths cry out. This underscores the holiness and majesty of Yahweh.

  • Comfort in the Chaos: For a community facing Babylonian invasion (the historical context of Habakkuk), this imagery provides deep comfort. If the terrifying primeval deep must submit to and praise God, then the chaotic rise and fall of pagan empires are also completely under His sovereign hand.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Habakkuk 3 is a highly poetic prayer styled as a liturgical psalm of deliverance. The prophet looks back at God’s historical redemptive acts—such as the Exodus and the crossing of the Red Sea—and projects them into a future expectation of salvation.

In verse 10, the natural world reacts violently to the presence of the warrior-God. The mountains "see" and "tremble." The overflowing waters "pass by." Then, the "deep uttered (nathan) its voice, and lifted its hands on high." The parallel actions of uttering a voice and lifting hands portray the sea as a worshiper overwhelmed by the manifest glory of God. The ocean waves crashing upward are seen as hands raised in desperate surrender. The deep gives up its voice because it cannot contain its awe.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 46:6 - "The nations raged, the kingdoms were moved; He uttered His voice, the earth melted."

  • Psalm 18:13 - "The LORD thundered from heaven, And the Most High uttered His voice, Hailstones and coals of fire."

  • Joel 3:16 - "The LORD also will roar from Zion, And utter His voice from Jerusalem; The heavens and earth will shake; But the LORD will be a shelter for His people, And the strength of the children of Israel."

  • Jeremiah 2:15 - "The young lions roared at him, and paced; They uttered their voice; They made his land waste; His cities are burned, without inhabitant."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The personification of the deep in Habakkuk 3:10 is an exceptional literary device. By combining nathan (uttering/giving a voice) with the lifting up of its "hands" (the lifting of the waves), the prophet presents a complete picture of bodily and vocal submission. In Hebrew thought, the "hands" (yadayim) represent power and activity. By lifting its hands and giving its voice, the deep surrenders both its strength and its sovereignty to Yahweh.
