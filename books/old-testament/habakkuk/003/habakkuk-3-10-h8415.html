DEEP - ( tehom , Strong's H8415 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word tehom refers to the vast, primeval depths of the ocean, the abyss, or subterranean water sources, carrying a sense of untamed power that is utterly beyond human control. In the biblical narrative, the "deep" represents both the raw material of creation under God's sovereign design and a symbol of potential chaos. When God manifests His presence in judgment or deliverance, even these fathomless and terrifying depths recognize His absolute authority. Personified in scripture, the deep cries out and submits to its Creator, demonstrating that there is no power in the universe—no matter how dark, chaotic, or deep—that does not bow to the sovereign hand of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Near Eastern cultures, the deep ocean was viewed with terror. It was seen as a wild, unpredictable, and hostile force of chaos that threatened to overwhelm the order of the dry land. While neighboring pagan mythologies spoke of gods fighting battles against personified sea monsters or ocean deities to bring order, the Old Testament presents a radically different picture. The Hebrew word tehom is not a rival god or a mythological beast; it is simply a part of God's created order. It is an inanimate, massive reservoir of water that is completely under the command of Yahweh. The word picture of tehom evokes the image of a vast, dark, bottomless ocean—fathomless to human investigation and terrifying to human strength, yet fully contained within the palm of God's hand.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term tehom is a noun denoting the "deep," "depths," "abyss," or "primeval ocean." It designates:

  • The massive body of water that covered the earth before the separation of land and sea at creation.

  • The subterranean ocean waters that feed springs, wells, and fountains.

  • The deep, dark recesses of the seas.

Grammatically, tehom is almost always used in the Old Testament without the Hebrew definite article (ha). This omission indicates its ancient, poetic, and quasi-proper name character in biblical literature, emphasizing its vast and singular nature.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs tehom to convey several powerful concepts:

  • The Primeval Deep: In the creation account, tehom represents the unformed and empty earth, shrouded in darkness and covered by water, awaiting the ordering word of God.

  • The Instrument of Judgment: During the Flood, the boundaries of creation were temporarily suspended as the fountains of the "great deep" burst forth to cleanse the earth.

  • The Pathway of Deliverance: At the Red Sea, the Lord dried up the "great deep" to make a way for the redeemed to pass over, while using those same deep waters to swallow Pharaoh's army.

  • Personified Submission: In poetic and prophetic literature, the deep is often personified. It is described as seeing God and trembling, roaring in fear, or lifting its waves like hands in absolute surrender to His authority.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of tehom centers on the absolute sovereignty of God over chaos.

  • Creator over Chaos: The deep is the ultimate physical representation of disorder, yet it has no power to resist God. It must retreat when He speaks, and it must stay within the boundaries He sets.

  • Redemption in the Depths: The most terrifying places of human experience—symbolized by the deep ocean—are not outside the reach of God's saving power. God routinely goes into the "deep" to rescue His people, transforming a place of death into a highway of redemption.

  • Creation's Sovereign Ruler: The natural world is acutely sensitive to the presence of its Maker. When God appears to execute justice or bring salvation, the deep acknowledges its King.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:10, the prophet is describing a majestic theophany—a visible manifestation of God marching in power to deliver His people. Habakkuk recalls God's historic wonders at the Red Sea, Sinai, and the Jordan River, projecting this power into his present crisis.

In this verse, the prophet writes, "The mountains saw You and trembled; the overflowing of the water passed by. The deep uttered its voice, and lifted its hands on high."

The contextual nuances here are deeply moving:

  • The Voice of the Deep: The roaring sound of the ocean depths (tehom) is not a meaningless noise; it is poetically described as a "voice" crying out in terror and awe at the presence of the Creator.

  • The Lifted Hands: The surging, cresting waves of the ocean rising toward the heavens are pictured as the "hands" of the deep. In the ancient world, lifting one's hands was a gesture of complete surrender, submission, and worship.

  • The Pastoral Comfort: For Habakkuk, who was facing the terrifying prospect of the Babylonian invasion, this imagery served as a profound comfort. If the fathomless, untamable "deep" surrenders and lifts its hands to God in absolute submission, then the chaotic rise of hostile nations is also entirely under God's sovereign control.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:2 - "The earth was without form, and void; and darkness was on the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters."

  • Genesis 7:11 - "In the six hundredth year of Noah’s life, in the second month, the seventeenth day of the month, on that day all the fountains of the great deep were broken up, and the windows of heaven were opened."

  • Exodus 15:5 - "The depths have covered them; They sank to the bottom like a stone."

  • Psalm 42:7 - "Deep calls unto deep at the noise of Your waterfalls; All Your waves and billows have gone over me."

  • Proverbs 3:20 - "By His knowledge the depths were broken up, And clouds drop down the dew."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), tehom is translated using the Greek word abyssos, from which we get the English word "abyss." In the New Testament, this term shifts from a physical description of ocean depths to a spiritual description of the prison house of demonic spirits and the bottomless pit. This linguistic connection reminds us that whether the "deep" represents physical chaos, emotional despair, or spiritual darkness, the Lord Jesus Christ holds the keys and exercises absolute dominion over it all.
