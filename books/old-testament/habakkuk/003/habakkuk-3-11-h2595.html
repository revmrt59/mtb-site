SPEAR - ( chanith , Strong's H2595 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chanith refers to a spear or lance, a primary weapon of warfare and personal defense in the ancient Near East. In the Old Testament, it represents physical military power, royal authority, and protection. In prophetic and poetic literature, specifically Habakkuk 3:11, the spear is anthropomorphically attributed to God, symbolizing His cosmic power, divine judgment, and lightning-like swiftness as He marches forth as a Divine Warrior to deliver His people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The chanith was a standard infantry weapon consisting of a wooden shaft fitted with a pointed metal head made of bronze or iron. Unlike the lighter kidon (javelin), which was designed primarily to be thrown, the chanith was a heavier thrusting weapon used in close combat, though it could be cast in moments of extreme urgency. In the ancient world, the spear was a ubiquitous symbol of military status. Kings and commanders kept their spears close at hand; a spear driven into the ground outside a tent door signaled the presence of the chieftain. Saul’s constant possession of his spear throughout the narrative of 1 Samuel highlights both his royal office and his growing paranoia.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun chanith designates a spear or lance. It is derived from the verbal root chanah, which means to decline, bend down, or pitch a tent (encamp). The semantic connection lies in the physical action of planting or driving something into the ground—just as tent pegs are driven down to establish a camp, a spear is driven into the earth when a soldier rests. The term occurs 47 times in the Hebrew Bible, almost exclusively referring to the literal weapon of war, though it occasionally functions as a vivid metaphor for destructive forces or natural phenomena.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Military and Physical Might: The word is used to describe the terrifying armaments of giants and champions. Goliath's spear shaft is famously compared to a weaver's beam, illustrating insurmountable human strength that is ultimately useless against the power of God.

  • Royal Authority and Instability: In the narrative of King Saul, the spear becomes an extension of his unstable rule. He repeatedly hurls his spear at David and Jonathan, turning a symbol of royal protection into an instrument of lawless violence.

  • Metaphor for Piercing Words: Poetically, the spear describes the lethal impact of human malice. The teeth and tongues of the wicked are described as sharp spears and arrows, emphasizing how slander can pierce and destroy a person's life.

  • Heavenly Lightning: In poetic descriptions of God's judgments, natural elements are cast as divine armaments. The rapid, piercing flashes of lightning during a storm are depicted as the glittering spears of Yahweh.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of the spear in Habakkuk 3 belongs to the theological motif of the Divine Warrior. Throughout the Old Testament, Yahweh is revealed not as an abstract force, but as an active protector of His covenant people. When Israel's enemies threaten to overwhelm them, God Himself takes up weapons of war. The spear represents the finality and precision of divine judgment. God's warfare is never chaotic or arbitrary; it is a calculated, righteous strike against wickedness to establish cosmic order and bring salvation to His covenant people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:11, the prophet reflects on a majestic, terrifying theophany—a visible manifestation of God. The verse reads: "The sun and moon stood still in their habitation; At the light of Your arrows they went, At the shining of Your glittering spear." Here, the "glittering spear" (bəraq chănîṯeḵā, literally "the lightning of Your spear") is a brilliant metaphor for lightning bolts slicing through the tempest.

The context is one of supreme cosmic disruption. The sun and moon withdraw their light, standing still in their heavenly dwellings, because the brilliant, flashing splendor of God's lightning-spear completely outshines them. The prophet uses this imagery to assure God's trembling people that the same sovereign power that shook the earth during the Exodus and the conquest of Canaan is active on their behalf. Even as the Babylonian invasion looms, God's glittering spear remains ready to execute final justice.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 18:11 - "And Saul cast the spear, for he said, 'I will pin David to the wall!' But David escaped from his presence twice."

  • Psalm 35:3 - "Draw also the spear, And stop those who pursue me. Say to my soul, 'I am your salvation.'"

  • Psalm 46:9 - "He makes wars cease to the end of the earth; He breaks the bow and cuts the spear in two; He burns the chariot in the fire."

  • Isaiah 2:4 - "He shall judge between the nations, And rebuke many people; They shall beat their swords into plowshares, And their spears into pruning hooks; Nation shall not lift up sword against nation, Neither shall they learn war anymore."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The ultimate destiny of the spear in biblical prophecy is disarmament. In the messianic peace of the kingdom of God, weapons of destruction are refashioned into tools of cultivation. The transformation of spears (chanith) into pruning hooks (mazmeroth) in Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3 indicates that when Christ establishes His perfect rule, the need for defense, offense, and royal enforcement via violence will be permanently swallowed up in His righteousness. Conversely, Joel 3:10 reverses this imagery to summon the nations to final judgment, commanding them to beat their pruning hooks back into spears, showing that apart from Christ, humanity remains in a state of futile warfare against God.
