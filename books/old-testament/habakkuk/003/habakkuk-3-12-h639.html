ANGER - ( 'ap , Strong's H0639 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ap fundamentally refers to the nose, nostrils, or face, but is most frequently used metaphorically in the Old Testament to describe anger, wrath, or heat of passion. This concrete word picture stems from the physical manifestation of rapid, heavy breathing or snorting through the nostrils when a person or animal is intensely angry. In the context of Habakkuk 3:12, 'ap denotes the intense, righteous indignation of Yahweh as He marches forth in cosmic judgment to trample the hostile nations and rescue His covenant people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • Concrete Hebrew Thought: Ancient Hebrew thinking is highly concrete, describing abstract concepts through physical anatomy. The nose ('ap) is viewed as the seat of anger because of the heat and heavy breathing associated with a person who is enraged.

  • The Burning Nose: When anger rises, the nostrils flare, and the breath becomes hot. Thus, the common Hebrew idiom for "anger burned" literally translates as "his nose became hot."

  • Slow to Anger: The Hebrew expression for being patient or longsuffering is 'arek 'appayim, which literally means "long of nostrils" or "long of nose." This implies that it takes a long time for the heat of anger to travel and manifest. Conversely, a quick-tempered person is "short of nostrils."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Literal Meaning: Nose, nostril, face, or countenance. In its dual form ('appayim), it often refers to the nostrils or the face (particularly when bowing to the ground in worship or respect).

  • Figurative Meaning: Anger, wrath, vexation, or hot displeasure.

  • Semantic Range: The term spans from mild human irritation to the fierce, consuming, holy wrath of God. In contexts of divine judgment, it represents God's active, righteous opposition to evil.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Breath of Life: The literal use appears in descriptions of breathing, such as God breathing the breath of life into Adam's nostrils (Genesis 2:7).

  • The Heat of Wrath: The figurative use is often paired with verbs meaning "to burn" (charah). When God's anger burns, it is pictured as a consuming fire or smoke rising from His nostrils (Psalm 18:8).

  • Anthropomorphic Representation: Applying 'ap to God is an anthropomorphism that communicates the reality and intensity of His holy reaction to sin. It shows that God is not a cold, detached force, but a personal Being who feels deeply and acts decisively against wickedness.

4. Theological Implications

  • Holy Opposition to Sin: Divine 'ap is never an uncontrolled, irrational temper tantrum like human anger. It is the calculated, holy, and necessary reaction of a perfectly righteous God against sin, rebellion, and injustice.

  • Covenant Protection: God's anger is deeply connected to His covenant. While His anger can burn against His own people when they are unfaithful, it also burns against pagan nations who oppress His people.

  • Redemptive Wrath: In prophetic literature, God's wrath and His salvation are often two sides of the same coin. He unleashes His anger against the wicked to deliver and vindicate the righteous.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • Context of Habakkuk 3: Habakkuk 3 is a poetic prayer and a dramatic description of a cosmic theophany. God is envisioned as a victorious warrior marching from the south to rescue His people.

  • Parallelism in Verse 12: The prophet pairs "indignation" (za'am) with "anger" ('ap). This poetic parallelism reinforces the intensity and totality of God's judgment.

  • Threshing the Nations: God is depicted as marching through the earth and "trampling" or threshing the nations. The anger ('ap) of God here is not destructive for destruction's sake; it is a harvesting process, separating the wicked oppressors from those who trust in Yahweh. It highlights His absolute sovereignty over geopolitical powers.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 11:1: "Now when the people complained, it displeased the LORD; for the LORD heard it, and His anger was aroused..."

  • Psalm 85:3: "You have taken away all Your wrath; You have turned from the fierceness of Your anger."

  • Proverbs 16:32: "He who is slow to anger is better than the mighty, And he who rules his spirit than he who takes a city."

  • Micah 7:18: "Who is a God like You, Pardoning iniquity And passing over the transgression of the remnant of His heritage? He does not retain His anger forever, Because He delights in mercy."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Greek Equivalents: In the Septuagint (LXX), 'ap is most frequently translated by the Greek words thumos (burning, passionate anger) and orgē (settled, controlled, and righteous wrath).

  • Ultimate Resolution: The New Testament reveals that the ultimate satisfaction of God's righteous 'ap (wrath) took place on the cross. Jesus Christ absorbed the full, burning judgment of God on behalf of believers, turning God's face toward us in eternal favor and peace.
