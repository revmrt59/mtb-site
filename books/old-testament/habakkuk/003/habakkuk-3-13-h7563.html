WICKED - ( rasha` , Strong's H7563 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word rasha` refers to those who are actively hostile to God's covenant, morally corrupt, and guilty of violating divine law. In the context of Habakkuk 3:13, it denotes the representative head of the wicked forces—specifically the Babylonian oppressor—who opposes God's people. God's judgment shatters this "house of the wicked" from foundation to neck, demonstrating that evil is ultimately unstable and destined for total destruction by the hand of the sovereign Warrior-King.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb of rasha` carries the fundamental concept of being loose, disjointed, or out of bounds. It is the direct opposite of tsaddiq (righteous), which represents alignment with a straight, true standard. While the righteous person walks in a straight path of covenant fidelity, the wicked person is unregulated, unstable, and actively chaotic. In the ancient Near Eastern context, wickedness was not merely a private moral failing; it was a public disruption of the cosmic and social order established by God. The word picture of rasha` is that of a restless wave or a building with a faulty foundation—constantly shifting, causing harm to those around it, and destined to collapse under the weight of its own lawlessness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term rasha` is an adjective and a noun that means wicked, criminal, guilty, or hostile to God. In a forensic or legal context, it denotes someone who is in the wrong, who has been tried and found guilty of a crime, or who stands condemned before a judge. Culturally and spiritually, it refers to those who live in open defiance of God's moral law, showing utter disregard for the rights, safety, and dignity of their neighbors.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, the rasha` is described through vivid metaphors:

  • The wicked are like the restless sea that cannot rest, whose waters cast up mire and dirt.

  • They are compared to chaff that the wind blows away, lacking substance, permanence, or value.

  • They are depicted as hunters setting traps for the innocent, using their mouths as deadly weapons.

  • In wisdom literature, the path of the wicked is dark and leads to self-destruction, contrasted sharply with the path of the righteous, which shines brighter and brighter until the full day.

4. Theological Implications

Theological study of rasha` reveals that wickedness is ultimately an assault on God's sovereignty. The wicked person lives as if God does not see, does not care, or does not exist. This word exposes the deep-seated rebellion of the human heart that rejects divine authority to pursue self-exaltation. Because God is holy and just, He cannot remain passive in the face of the rasha`. Divine judgment is not an arbitrary fit of anger, but a necessary, righteous re-establishment of order and justice. The existence of the wicked necessitates the intervention of God as Judge and Deliverer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:13, the prophet recounts a powerful, poetic vision of God marching forth as a divine warrior to save His covenant people: "You went forth for the salvation of Your people, / For salvation with Your Anointed. / You struck the head of the house of the wicked, / By laying bare from foundation to neck."

  • In this specific context, "the wicked" refers to the proud, violent Babylonian empire (and its king) that God used as a temporary instrument of chastisement but now holds guilty for their excessive cruelty and idolatry.

  • The "house of the wicked" represents the entire dynasty, empire, and structural system of Babylonian power.

  • The destruction is complete and graphic: God strikes the "head" (the leader or top of the structure) and "lays it bare from foundation to neck." This indicates a total, systematic demolition from the ground up, leaving nothing standing. It comforts the suffering believer that no matter how imposing a wicked empire appears, its foundation is a lie, and God's judgment will thoroughly dismantle it.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 10:7: "The memory of the righteous is blessed, / But the name of the wicked will rot."

  • Isaiah 55:7: "Let the wicked forsake his way, / And the unrighteous man his thoughts; / Let him return to the LORD, / And He will have mercy on him; / And to our God, / For He will abundantly pardon."

  • Ezekiel 18:21: "But if a wicked man turns from all his sins which he has committed, keeps all My statutes, and does what is lawful and right, he shall surely live; he shall not die."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Habakkuk 2:4, a crucial thematic contrast is established: "Behold the proud, / His soul is not upright in him; / But the just shall live by his faith." The proud soul of the Babylonian oppressor is the epitome of the rasha`. In contrast, the righteous (tsaddiq) person lives by faithful trust in Yahweh. Even when the wicked seem to prosper and swallow up those more righteous than themselves (Habakkuk 1:13), the prophet is assured that the triumph of the wicked is short-lived. God's sovereign justice is the guarantee that the righteous will endure, while the house of the wicked will be utterly demolished.
