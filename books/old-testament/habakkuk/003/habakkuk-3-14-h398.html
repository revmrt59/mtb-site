FEASTING - ( akal , Strong's H398 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb akal fundamentally means to eat, devour, or consume. In Habakkuk 3:14, the NKJV translates this root as "feasting" to capture the vivid, predatory imagery of Israel's enemies who took sadistic pleasure in secretly exploiting and destroying the helpless. This word highlights the rapacious appetite of wicked oppressors who view the vulnerable not as fellow image-bearers of God, but as prey to be consumed for their own selfish gain and satisfaction.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root akal is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Bible, appearing hundreds of times to describe the physical act of eating food, grazing, or consuming nutrients. Beyond literal ingestion, the word carries a powerful metaphorical background of complete consumption and destruction. In ancient Near Eastern imagery, enemies, wild beasts, fire, and the sword are frequently described as "eating" or "devouring" their targets. The word picture is primal and visceral: it represents the total absorption of one entity by another, leaving nothing behind. In a hostile context, it depicts a predator hunting, tearing apart, and feeding upon its prey.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary lexical definition of akal is to eat, consume, devour, or burn up. Depending on the grammatical stem and context, its meanings include:

  • To eat food for sustenance (literal consumption).

  • To consume or lay waste (used of fire, sword, pestilence, or famine).

  • To enjoy, participate in, or experience (metaphorical tasting or taking in).

  • To devour or oppress (used of ruthless leaders, nations, or adversaries destroying the poor).

In Habakkuk 3:14, the infinitive form functions as a gerund ("eating" or "feasting"), conveying a continuous, eager, and highly satisfying act of consuming the weak.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture employs akal in several highly descriptive theological and metaphorical word pictures:

  • The Devouring Fire: God's holiness and judgment are frequently compared to a "consuming fire" (esh okelah) that burns up wickedness and disobedience.

  • The Devouring Sword: War and military conquest are personified as a sword that "eats" or "devours" flesh.

  • The Exploitation of the Poor: Wicked rulers and oppressors are described as "eating my people like they eat bread." They do not merely govern harshly; they feed upon the resources, livelihoods, and very lives of the vulnerable.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, akal in predatory contexts reveals the depth of human depravity and the tragic inversion of God's design. God created humans to shepherd and care for one another; sin transforms humans into beasts of prey who find their joy ("rejoicing") in "feasting" on the helpless. This word underscores the reality of systemic injustice and the demonic cruelty that often characterizes worldly empires, such as the Chaldeans in Habakkuk's day. It also demonstrates that God notes this predatory behavior. His ultimate judgment is a holy reversal, where those who devoured others will themselves be consumed by the fire of His wrath.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3, the prophet prays and remembers God's past mighty acts of salvation, looking forward to how God will march through the earth in indignation to rescue His anointed. In Habakkuk 3:14, the prophet describes the arrogant assault of the invading Chaldeans (Babylonians). They came out like a whirlwind to scatter God's people, gloating and rejoicing "like feasting on the poor in secret."

The nuance here is the sheer joy and secrecy of their malice. They did not just conquer out of geopolitical necessity; they took sadistic pleasure in the exploitation and destruction of the Judean remnant. The word akal shows that to these ruthless conquerors, the "poor" (the defenseless covenant people) were nothing more than a meal to be consumed in a hidden corner, helpless to defend themselves.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 14:4 – "Have all the workers of iniquity no knowledge, Who eat up my people as they eat bread, And do not call on the Lord?"

  • Proverbs 30:14 – "There is a generation whose teeth are like swords, And whose fangs are like knives, To devour the poor from off the earth, And the needy from among men."

  • Ezekiel 22:25 – "The conspiracy of her prophets in her midst is like a roaring lion tearing the prey; they have devoured souls; they have taken treasure and precious things; they have made many widows in her midst."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The use of akal in Habakkuk 3:14 provides a striking contrast to the lifestyle of the righteous. While the wicked find their rejoicing in eating (devouring) the poor, the prophet Habakkuk concludes his prayer with a different kind of joy. Even if the fields produce "no food" (using okal, a derivative noun of akal in Habakkuk 3:17) and starvation threatens the land, the righteous will not resort to predatory behavior. Instead, they will "rejoice in the Lord" (Habakkuk 3:18). True spiritual nourishment and joy come from trusting Yahweh, whereas the wicked seek temporary, cursed satisfaction by consuming others.
