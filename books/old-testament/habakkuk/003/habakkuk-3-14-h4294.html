ARROWS - ( matteh , Strong's H4294 )

Summary:

In Habakkuk 3:14, the Hebrew noun matteh represents a multi-faceted word whose primary meaning is a "staff," "rod," or "shaft." While most frequently used in the Old Testament to denote a tribal staff or a symbol of authority (and by extension, a "tribe"), in poetic and military contexts it refers to a weapon of war, such as a staff, spear, or the shaft of an arrow. In the dramatic depiction of God's judgment in Habakkuk 3, the NKJV translates this term as "arrows," highlighting how God turns the very weapons of the enemy against them. The word emphasizes that the instruments of power the wicked use to oppress others become the very means of their own destruction under divine justice.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word matteh derives from the root natah, which means to stretch out, spread out, or bend. A matteh is literally something stretched out or a branch cut from a tree to serve as a staff. In ancient Near Eastern culture, a staff served multiple vital purposes. It was a walking support for travelers, a defensive weapon for shepherds against predators, a scepter representing royal or judicial authority, and a tribal standard symbolizing lineage and identity. In a military context, the staff or rod could refer to a spear shaft, a club, or potentially the shaft of an arrow, representing offensive military power.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun matteh has three primary branches of meaning:

  • A physical rod, staff, or shaft used for walking, support, beating, or as a weapon of authority (e.g., Moses' staff, Aaron's rod).

  • A tribal division or clan, stemming from the concept of the staff as a symbol of tribal leadership and authority (used extensively in Numbers and Joshua).

  • A weapon of war, specifically a staff, lance, spear-shaft, or by extension, an arrow, used to strike an enemy. In Habakkuk 3:14, the term refers to the instruments of violence belonging to the adversaries of God's people.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently employs matteh to paint vivid pictures of divine authority and human vulnerability:

  • The Rod of God: The staff of Moses and Aaron (matteh) is the physical instrument through which God unleashes the plagues upon Egypt, parts the Red Sea, and brings water from the rock, demonstrating supreme authority over nature and empires.

  • The Rod of Judgment: Isaiah refers to the Assyrians as the "rod" (matteh) of God's anger, indicating that God uses foreign empires as temporary instruments of discipline.

  • The Tribal Connection: Because each tribal leader carried a representative staff, the word matteh became synonymous with the tribes of Israel themselves, representing the structure and identity of God’s covenant people.

  • In poetic warfare, as seen in Habakkuk, the "staff" or "arrows" of the enemy represent their proud military might, which is ultimately fragile and easily turned against them by the sovereignty of God.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, matteh in the context of Habakkuk 3:14 illustrates the supreme irony of divine judgment: lex talionis (the law of retribution). God does not always need to forge new weapons to destroy the enemies of His people; He frequently causes the wicked to fall into the pit they dug for others and to be pierced by their own weapons. The ultimate sovereignty of God is revealed when the very instruments of oppression are redirected by God's hand to execute His justice. This reassures the believer that human power, no matter how menacing, carries the seeds of its own destruction when aligned against the Almighty.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3, the prophet recounts a spectacular, poetic vision of God marching forth as a warrior to deliver His anointed people. Verse 14 describes the chaotic retreat of the enemy who came out like a whirlwind to scatter the righteous. The NKJV reads, "You thrust through with his own arrows the head of his villages." The term matteh here (appearing as the plural suffix mattav, meaning "with his own staffs/rods") refers to the weapons of the enemy leader. Whether visualized as heavy staffs, lances, or arrows, the nuance is clear: God seizes the enemy's own instruments of violence and turns them back upon their own leaders. The power they trusted to obliterate the poor and helpless becomes the instrument of their own execution.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), matteh is translated using several Greek words depending on context. In administrative and genealogical passages, it is translated as phyle (tribe). In passages referring to a physical rod or staff, it is translated as rabdos (staff, rod, or scepter). In Habakkuk 3:14, the Septuagint translates the phrase with a focus on confusion and division, reflecting the difficulty ancient translators had with the poetic military terminology, yet the Hebrew text consistently maintains the physical image of the matteh as an instrument of striking.
