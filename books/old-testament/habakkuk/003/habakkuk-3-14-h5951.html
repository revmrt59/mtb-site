REJOICING - ( alitsuth , Strong's H5951 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun alitsuth refers to an intense, exultant joy, a triumphant rejoicing, or a boisterous exultation. Occurring only once in the Old Testament as a hapax legomenon in Habakkuk 3:14, it derives from a root meaning to jump for joy or triumphantly exult. In its specific contextual setting, alitsuth does not describe the holy, worshipful joy of God’s people, but rather the arrogant, sadistic, and predatory exultation of ruthless invaders who delight in crushing the helpless and devouring the poor in secret.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The noun alitsuth is built on the root verb alats (Strong's H5937), which communicates the physical or emotional expression of being puffed up with joy, leaping, or triumphing. It conveys a highly expressive, loud, and visible form of rejoicing rather than a quiet, internal happiness.

  • Historically and culturally, this word family describes the behavior of a victorious army or a ruler who has successfully subdued an enemy and celebrates with unbridled, arrogant boasting.

  • In the prophetic vision of Habakkuk, the prophet uses this word picture to describe the Babylonian (Chaldean) invaders. They are depicted not merely as soldiers executing a military campaign, but as sadistic predators who derive deep, perverse pleasure from the terror and destruction they inflict on the vulnerable.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • alitsuth means "exultation," "rejoicing," or "triumphant joy."

  • It is a feminine abstract noun derived from the verb alats ("to exult, rejoice, triumph").

  • The semantic range of this root denotes a high-spirited, energetic celebration of triumph, often associated with victory over enemies or the attainment of a long-sought goal. In this unique nominal form, it specifically denotes the state of exultant, arrogant glee felt by oppressors who believe they are completely unstoppable.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Because alitsuth occurs only once in the Hebrew Bible, its usage is defined by its immediate poetic context in Habakkuk 3:14 and its relationship to its verbal root.

  • The word picture in Habakkuk 3:14 is exceptionally vivid. The oppressors are compared to bandits or wild beasts lying in wait, whose "rejoicing" (alitsuth) is likened to "feasting on the poor in secret."

  • This imagery evokes a group of brigands devouring their stolen plunder in a hidden place, laughing and celebrating their successful prey. Their joy is derived directly from the misery and exploitation of others.

  • While the verbal root alats is often used in positive contexts (such as Hannah's prayer in 1 Samuel 2:1), when substantivized here to describe the wicked, it depicts a prideful, self-glorifying boastfulness that sets itself against God and His people.

4. Theological Implications

  • This term highlights the moral depravity of sin, demonstrating how human joy can become thoroughly corrupted. Instead of finding joy in God and His righteousness, fallen humanity can reach a state of sadistic exultation, taking pleasure in wickedness, violence, and oppression.

  • It underscores the doctrine of divine justice. God observes the arrogant "rejoicing" of the wicked. Though they celebrate their apparent victories over the helpless, their exultation is short-lived. Habakkuk 3:14 reveals that God Himself strikes through the head of these proud warriors, turning their wicked rejoicing into sudden destruction.

  • It contrasts the fleeting, cruel joy of the world with the enduring, righteous joy of the believer. While the enemy rejoices in devouring the poor, the prophet Habakkuk concludes his prayer by declaring that he will "rejoice in the LORD" (using the Hebrew roots gool and samach in Habakkuk 3:18) even in the midst of total earthly devastation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 3:14, the prophet describes God's dramatic, redemptive intervention as a divine warrior marching through the earth to save His covenant people.

  • The phrase "They came out like a whirlwind to scatter me" describes the sudden, chaotic, and terrifying onslaught of the Chaldeans against Judah.

  • The clause "Their rejoicing was like feasting on the poor in secret" reveals the inner motivation of the invaders. They were not merely passive instruments of divine judgment; they were ravenous oppressors who took delight in the clandestine destruction of the vulnerable ("the poor").

  • The contextual contrast is sharp: the Babylonians’ arrogant, predatory joy (alitsuth) is met by the sovereign wrath of God, who pierces the enemy's head with his own spears, completely overturning their sadistic triumph.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because alitsuth (Strong's H5951) is a hapax legomenon, its sole occurrence is:

  • Habakkuk 3:14 – "You thrust through with his own arrows the head of his villages. They came out like a whirlwind to scatter me; Their rejoicing was like feasting on the poor in secret."

To understand the semantic range of the underlying root, the closely related verb alats (Strong's H5937, "to rejoice, exult") appears in the following passages:

  • 1 Samuel 2:1 – "And Hannah prayed and said: 'My heart rejoices in the LORD; My horn is exalted in the LORD. I smile at my enemies, Because I rejoice in Your salvation.'"

  • Psalm 9:2 – "I will be glad and rejoice in You; I will sing praise to Your name, O Most High."

  • Psalm 68:3 – "But let the righteous be glad; Let them rejoice before God; Yes, let them exceedingly rejoice."

  • Proverbs 11:10 – "When it goes well with the righteous, the city rejoices; And when the wicked perish, there is gladness."

  • Proverbs 28:12 – "When the righteous rejoice, there is great glory; But when the wicked arise, men hide themselves."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The contrast between alitsuth (wicked, predatory joy) in Habakkuk 3:14 and alats (godly, worshipful joy) in other passages shows that Hebrew words for joy are highly dependent on the moral state of the subject.

  • The concept of "feasting on the poor in secret" parallels Psalm 10:8-10, where the wicked lie in wait in secret places to murder the innocent and catch the poor. Both passages highlight the stealthy, predatory nature of those who oppose God's justice.
