SCATTER - ( puts , Strong's H6327 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb puts conveys a violent, chaotic dispersal, like a flock of sheep routed by a predator or a stone shattered into flying fragments. In Habakkuk 3:14, it describes the terrifying intent of Israel's enemies who sweep down like a fierce whirlwind to shatter, disperse, and erase the covenant people. Yet, this word reveals a profound theological truth: while the forces of darkness plot to scatter the righteous, God is the sovereign protector who turns the enemy's violence back upon themselves, preserving His chosen remnant and scattering the proud.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root puts evokes the vivid physical imagery of fragmentation and sudden, forced separation. It is the picture of a vessel struck with such force that its pieces fly in every direction, or a pile of chaff caught in a sudden, violent gale and swept away. In the ancient Near East, where community, land, and flock represented survival, to be scattered was equivalent to destruction. Sheep scattered on the hillside were defenseless against wild beasts; a nation scattered from its homeland lost its identity, its protection, and its inheritance. The word captures the panic of retreat and the total loss of cohesion under overwhelming pressure.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb puts means to disperse, scatter, overflow, or drive asunder. In its active and causative stems, it denotes the aggressive action of breaking up a unified group and forcing its members in different directions. The semantic range spans from the physical distribution of people across the earth to the military routing of an army. In the context of Habakkuk 3:14, the verb appears in the Hiphil (causative) infinitive, expressing the purposeful, destructive objective of the adversary: to cause the complete dispersal and ruin of the prophet's people.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Judgment at Babel: The word is famously used to describe God’s judgment on human pride at the Tower of Babel, where He scattered humanity across the face of the earth, breaking their rebellious unity.

  • The Scattered Flock: It frequently depicts the tragic state of Israel when leadership fails, leaving the people scattered like sheep without a shepherd, vulnerable to predators.

  • Military Route: It describes the chaotic flight of soldiers when a battle is lost, fleeing in panic in every direction to escape the sword.

  • The Splitting of Rock: It is used poetically of God’s voice or presence shattering the mountains, demonstrating His absolute sovereignty over creation.

4. Theological Implications

Throughout Scripture, scattering is a primary consequence of covenant unfaithfulness. To be scattered among the nations is the ultimate curse of the law. However, when the enemies of God attempt to execute this scattering on their own terms out of malice, God rises as the Defender of His flock. The theological tension of puts lies in who is doing the scattering. When God scatters, it is a holy act of judgment against human pride or covenant rebellion. When the wicked attempt to scatter God's people, God intervenes to scatter the enemy instead, demonstrating that the ultimate destiny of His people is gathering and restoration, not permanent dispersion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:14, the prophet describes a dramatic, warlike theophany where God marches forth for the salvation of His people. The invading enemy is depicted as a furious whirlwind (sa'ar) coming to "scatter me." The shift to the first-person singular ("me") personalizes the threat; the enemy’s goal is not just land, but the total annihilation and dispersal of the covenant representative. Their rejoicing is described as "like feasting on the poor in secret," highlighting their ruthless, predatory nature. Yet, the nuance of the passage turns on a divine irony: the very weapons of the enemy are turned against them. While they charge to scatter the righteous, it is God who rides upon His horses to trample the sea, scattering the wicked and rescuing His anointed.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 11:4 - "And they said, 'Come, let us build ourselves a city, and a tower whose top is in the heavens; let us make a name for ourselves, lest we be scattered abroad over the face of the whole earth.'"

  • Genesis 11:9 - "Therefore its name is called Babel, because there the LORD confused the language of all the earth; and from there the LORD scattered them abroad over the face of all the earth."

  • Ezekiel 34:5 - "So they were scattered because there was no shepherd; and they became food for all the beasts of the field when they were scattered."

  • Zechariah 13:7 - "'Awake, O sword, against My Shepherd, against the Man who is My Companion,' says the LORD of hosts. 'Strike the Shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered; then I will turn My hand against the little ones.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (LXX), puts is frequently translated by the Greek verb diaskorpizo, which carries the same meaning of scattering or dispersing. This Greek term is used by Jesus in Matthew 26:31 when He quotes Zechariah 13:7 on the eve of His crucifixion: "I will strike the Shepherd, and the sheep of the flock will be scattered." This NT connection demonstrates that while the ultimate "scattering" occurred at the cross when the disciples fled, God used that very moment of dispersion to inaugurate the New Covenant, gathering a global flock that no enemy could ever scatter again.
