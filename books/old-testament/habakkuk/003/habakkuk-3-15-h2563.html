HEAP - ( chomer , Strong's H2563 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chomer carries the vivid imagery of churning, bubbling, or piling up, referring variously to foaming clay, mortar, a dry measure, or a surging heap of waters. In Habakkuk 3:15, it describes the turbulent, foaming masses of the Red Sea during the Exodus. Rather than being a barrier to God, this chaotic heap of great waters becomes the very highway upon which Yahweh rides His war-chariot of salvation, demonstrating His absolute sovereignty over chaos and His unyielding commitment to deliver His people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb chamar means to boil up, foam, trouble, or smear. It conveys turbulent motion, such as the churning of water, the fermentation of wine, or the thick boiling of pitch and mud. Out of this root comes the noun chomer, which paints a physical picture of materials that are thick, bubbling, or piled up. Whether it is the sticky mud used for building mortar, the malleable clay on a potter’s wheel, or the surging, foaming heaps of the ocean, the word always points to something dynamic, thick, and highly tactile. It represents the raw material of creation and history, entirely subject to the shaping hands and treading feet of the Almighty.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun has four primary lexical meanings based on its context of surging or piling up:

  • Clay or mire: The wet, moldable earth used by a potter to shape vessels or found in the streets as mud.

  • Mortar or cement: The binding material used in ancient bricklaying and construction.

  • A dry measure (homer): A unit of volume equivalent to a heap of grain, roughly equal to ten ephahs (about five to six bushels).

  • A foaming surge or heap: The massive, piling waves of the deep sea.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Potter's Clay: It depicts human frailty, dependence, and the absolute sovereignty of the Creator. God is the potter, and humanity is the chomer, possessing no right to question the Maker's design.

  • Building Mortar: It represents human effort and labor, such as the building of the Tower of Babel, where men used chomer to construct their own monument of rebellion.

  • The Foaming Heap of Waters: It pictures the untamed, chaotic power of the sea. Ancient literature often viewed the deep sea as an untamable, hostile monster, but in the Scriptures, these roaring waters are reduced to a helpless heap beneath the Creator's feet.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of chomer in historical poetic passages is its depiction of God's supremacy over chaos. In ancient Near Eastern cosmology, the sea symbolized chaos, death, and uncontrollable forces. By describing the Red Sea as a "heap of great waters" through which God walks, the text strips the sea of its terror. The foaming, surging depths do not swallow God; rather, God tramples them. This reveals that what appears to human eyes as an insurmountable, chaotic barrier is merely a submissive pathway for God's redemptive purposes.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:15, the prophet is engaged in a dramatic prayer of praise, recalling the historical deliverance of Israel from Egypt to find hope amid the impending Babylonian invasion. The "heap of great waters" refers directly to the Red Sea. While the narrative in Exodus describes the waters being divided and standing like a wall, Habakkuk's poetic description focuses on the churning, foaming majesty of the sea as God marches through it. The term highlights that even when the geopolitical situation of Habakkuk’s day is churning with Babylonian fury, God remains the warrior-king who effortlessly treads upon the foaming heaps of historical chaos.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 8:14: "They gathered them together in heaps, and the land stank."

  • Job 10:9: "Remember, I pray, that You have made me like clay, And will You turn me into dust again?"

  • Isaiah 45:9: "Woe to him who strives with his Maker! Let the potsherd strive with the potsherds of the earth. Shall the clay say to him who makes it, 'What are you making?' Or shall your handiwork say, 'He has no hands'?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

This Old Testament imagery provides a profound background for New Testament accounts where Jesus walks on the water (Matthew 14:22–33) and calms the storm (Mark 4:35–41). By treading upon the wind and the waves, Jesus was doing what only Yahweh can do: walking through the foaming "heap" of great waters. For the believer, this word study is a reminder that the foaming, chaotic, and muddy trials of life are not out of God's control. He does not bypass our storms; He walks through them, establishing His path in the midst of the churning deep.
