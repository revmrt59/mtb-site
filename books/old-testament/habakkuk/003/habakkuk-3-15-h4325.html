WATERS - ( mayim , Strong's H4325 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word mayim refers broadly to water, but in the context of Habakkuk 3:15, it represents the vast, chaotic, and seemingly impassable barriers of the deep. In this poetic theophany, "great waters" depict the cosmic and historical forces of chaos and opposition that Yahweh effortlessly treads underfoot to deliver His covenant people. Rather than a barrier to God, the mighty waters serve as His highway, demonstrating His absolute sovereignty over creation and history.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Near Eastern culture and Hebrew cosmology, large bodies of water were viewed with a mixture of awe and terror. Unlike modern societies with advanced seafaring technology, ancient Israel perceived the deep sea as a place of absolute chaos, danger, and untamed power. Water was essential for life in agriculture, yet in mass, it was an unpredictable force that could swallow up ships, flood lands, and destroy life.

Throughout the Old Testament, the "deep" and "great waters" symbolize the ultimate forces of chaos, national enemies, and spiritual opposition to God's order. By invoking the image of God treading through these great waters, Habakkuk draws upon a rich theological background: the God of Israel is not threatened by the chaotic forces of the world. Instead, He masterfully subdues them, turning the terrifying depths into a stable path for His divine purposes.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun mayim is inherently plural or dual in form, reflecting an abundance or mass of liquid. It is used over 580 times in the Hebrew Bible. The semantic range of mayim includes:

  • Natural, life-sustaining drinking water from wells or springs.

  • Rain falling from the heavens to nourish the earth.

  • Large bodies of water, such as seas, rivers, lakes, and floods.

  • Metaphorical waters representing distress, danger, divine judgment, or spiritual renewal.

In Habakkuk 3:15, the word is paired with rabbim ("great" or "many") to form the phrase mayim rabbim ("great waters" or "mighty waters"). The term chomer (heap, churning mass, or surging foam) precedes it. Therefore, the lexical focus here is not on quiet, refreshing water, but on the violent, surging, and deep mass of the sea.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently employs mayim in vivid poetic word pictures:

  • The Churning Abyss: In creation and the flood, the waters represent the primordial state of chaos that must be bound and governed by the word of God.

  • The Highway of the Lord: When God is depicted as walking on, treading, or rebuking the waters, it portrays supreme kingly authority. Job 9:8 states that God "treads on the waves of the sea."

  • Overwhelming Affliction: To be in "deep waters" is a common Hebrew idiom for experiencing overwhelming trial, sorrow, or persecution. To be saved from these waters is to experience God's deliverance.

In Habakkuk 3:15, the word picture is highly active and dramatic. God is depicted as a warrior riding His horses and chariots of salvation directly through the foaming, heaped-up waves of the sea. The very element that would drown any human army becomes a paved highway for the Commander of heaven’s hosts.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of mayim in Habakkuk 3:15 centers on the doctrines of sovereignty and redemption:

  • Absolute Sovereign Dominion: Nothing in creation is independent of God's control. The "great waters"—the most unstable and uncontrollable element known to ancient man—are completely subject to the feet of Yahweh.

  • Redemption Through Historical Judgment: Habakkuk 3:15 directly echoes the historical Exodus, where God parted the Red Sea (the heap of waters) to deliver Israel and destroy Pharaoh’s army. The theology of this passage declares that God's past redemptive power is the guarantee of His future deliverance.

  • The Subjugation of Chaos: God does not merely bypass obstacles; He marches through them. The hostile forces of the world (symbolized by the waters) are forced to serve as the staging ground for His glory and His people’s salvation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Habakkuk 3 is a prayer of faith set to music, written during a time of imminent national ruin. The fierce Babylonian (Chaldean) army is poised to invade Judah. Habakkuk is deeply afraid, but he deliberately shifts his gaze from the terrifying present to the glorious past.

In verse 15, the prophet recalls the climax of the Exodus. The "sea" (yam) and the "heap of great waters" (mayim rabbim) were insurmountable barriers to the fleeing Israelites. To human eyes, defeat was certain. Yet, Yahweh "walked through" them. The grammatical structure emphasizes the ease of His march; the surging waters posed no resistance to His horses.

The contextual nuance is clear: just as the mighty waters of the Red Sea could not stop God from saving His people from Egypt, the surging, chaotic flood of the Babylonian empire will not be able to thwart God's ultimate plan of salvation for His remnant. The historical reality of God treading the waters provides the theological foundation for Habakkuk's eventual declaration of joy in the midst of devastation (Habakkuk 3:17-19).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:2 - "The earth was without form, and void; and darkness was on the face of the deep. And the Spirit of God was hovering over the face of the waters."

  • Exodus 15:8 - "And with the blast of Your nostrils The waters were gathered together; The floods stood upright like a heap; The depths congealed in the heart of the sea."

  • Psalm 18:16 - "He sent from above, He took me; He drew me out of many waters."

  • Psalm 77:19 - "Your way was in the sea, Your path in the great waters, And Your footsteps were not known."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the New Testament, this Old Testament imagery of Yahweh treading the great waters finds its ultimate christological fulfillment when Jesus walks on the stormy Sea of Galilee (Matthew 14:22-33; Mark 6:45-52).

When Jesus walked upon the churning mayim, He was not merely performing a spectacular miracle; He was revealing His divine identity as the Yahweh of Habakkuk 3:15. By treading the wind and the waves under His feet, Jesus demonstrated that the same sovereign power that delivered Israel at the Red Sea was present in bodily form to deliver His disciples from the storms of life.
