QUIVERED - ( tsalal , Strong's H6750 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb tsalal captures a violent, involuntary physical vibration or tingling triggered by overwhelming dread, awe, or shocking news. In Habakkuk 3:16, it describes the prophet’s lips rapidly quivering or trembling when confronted with the terrifying majesty of Yahweh and the impending Babylonian invasion. This word highlights how deeply the physical body reacts to spiritual realities, illustrating that a genuine encounter with God’s voice and holiness shatters human self-sufficiency before it builds up saving faith.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of tsalal relates to vibration, oscillation, or the production of a sharp, resonant sound. It is closely connected to nouns meaning "cymbals" (tselatsal) because of the rapid, vibrating contact that produces a ringing noise. When applied to the human body, the word evokes the image of a sudden, uncontrollable shivering or shaking. Just as a metal instrument vibrates violently when struck, the human nervous system and physical body quiver in response to a sudden impact of terror, awe, or divine revelation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb tsalal means to tingle, quiver, or shake. It is used in two main contexts in the Hebrew Bible: the "tingling" of ears upon hearing devastating news of judgment, and the "quivering" of lips due to severe dread and fear. Lexically, the term signifies an involuntary physiological spasm or vibration caused by an external, overwhelming stimulus. It represents a physical manifestation of deep inner panic and reverence, showing that the body cannot remain unaffected when the spirit is shaken.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament limits the use of tsalal to four specific occurrences, each painting a picture of human vulnerability in the face of divine judgment:

  • The Tingling Ears: When God announces a catastrophe so severe that it defies comprehension, the hearer’s ears are said to "tingle" (tsalal). This is not a mild itch, but a sharp, vibrating shockwave in the ears, like the reverberation of a loud clash of cymbals.

  • The Quivering Lips: In Habakkuk, the vibration moves from the ears to the lips. The prophet’s lips shake and flutter uncontrollably, rendering him unable to speak or control his breathing. It represents the utter silencing of human arguments and strength in the presence of the Almighty.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of tsalal lies in its demonstration of the fear of the Lord. In modern theology, "fear" is often diluted to mean mere "respect" or "reverence." However, the physical reaction of tsalal demonstrates that an encounter with the living God involves a holy, physical dread. When God reveals His power and his righteous indignation against sin, even His chosen prophets experience a total collapse of physical strength.

Furthermore, this quivering is a necessary stage in the life of faith. Before Habakkuk can declare his triumphant joy in the God of his salvation (Habakkuk 3:17–19), he must first be brought to absolute, trembling brokenness. True biblical peace does not bypass the trembling of the flesh; it transcends it.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:16, the prophet reacts to a vision of Yahweh marching through the earth in fury to thresh the nations (Habakkuk 3:12). Upon hearing this divine report, Habakkuk suffers a total physical collapse. He describes a chain reaction of physical terror: his stomach churns ("my belly trembled"), his lips vibrate uncontrollably ("my lips quivered at the voice"), his skeletal structure fails ("rottenness entered into my bones"), and his legs shake beneath him ("I trembled in myself").

The specific nuance of tsalal applied to the lips shows that Habakkuk is stripped of his previous complaints. Throughout chapters 1 and 2, the prophet questioned God's justice and methods. Now, confronted with the reality of God's presence, his questioning lips can only quiver in silent submission. This physical undoing prepares him to "wait quietly" for the day of trouble.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because tsalal appears only four times in the Hebrew Bible, we can examine every parallel use to see how the word consistently points to the terrifying reality of divine judgment:

  • 1 Samuel 3:11 – "Then the LORD said to Samuel: 'Behold, I will do something in Israel at which both ears of everyone who hears it will tingle.'"

  • 2 Kings 21:12 – "therefore thus says the LORD God of Israel: 'Behold, I am bringing such calamity upon Jerusalem and Judah, that whoever hears of it, both his ears will tingle.'"

  • Jeremiah 19:3 – "and say, 'Hear the word of the LORD, O kings of Judah and inhabitants of Jerusalem. Thus says the LORD of hosts, the God of Israel: "Behold, I will bring such a catastrophe on this place, that whoever hears of it, his ears will tingle."'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is worth noting the pastoral progression of Habakkuk’s posture. He begins his book crying out with a loud voice of complaint (Habakkuk 1:2) and ends his book with quivering lips that are hushed in awe. This mirrors the biblical pattern seen in other saints: Isaiah cried "Woe is me!" (Isaiah 6:5), Job laid his hand over his mouth (Job 40:4), and John fell at His feet as dead (Revelation 1:17). For the pastor or teacher, tsalal serves as a powerful reminder that we must not rush to give cheap comfort to those who are trembling under the conviction of God’s word. The trembling of the lips is often the very ground where genuine, unshakeable trust is born.
