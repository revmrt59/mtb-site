VOICE - ( qol , Strong's H6963 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word qol refers to a sound, voice, noise, or report. In Habakkuk 3:16, it represents the terrifying sound of God’s voice and the report of His impending judgment. As the prophet listens to the divine declaration of coming wrath against both Judah and her enemies, the sheer weight of this "voice" shatters his physical composure, driving him to a place of profound, reverent fear and ultimately, quiet trust in God's sovereignty.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun qol is an onomatopoeic word, likely originating from a root that mimics a sharp, resonant sound. In ancient Hebrew culture, a voice was not merely a carrier of abstract information; it was an extension of the speaker’s personal presence, authority, and power. To hear someone's qol was to experience their active presence. In the natural world, the term is used to describe the crash of thunder, the roar of the ocean, the blast of a trumpet, or the rustling of leaves. When applied to God, His qol is the ultimate creative and judicial force—the same voice that spoke the universe into existence and the voice that shakes the wilderness in judgment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of qol is a sound, voice, or noise. Its semantic range is broad and is determined entirely by its context:

  • An audible sound made by humans, including weeping, singing, shouting, or speaking.

  • The noise produced by inanimate objects or instruments, such as the sound of a chariot or a trumpet.

  • The sounds of nature, specifically thunder, which the Old Testament frequently calls "the voice of the LORD."

  • A report, rumor, or message that is heard and spread among a group of people.

In Habakkuk 3:16, the word occurs with the preposition l- (l'qol), translating literally to "at the sound" or "at the voice." It refers directly to the divine proclamation of judgment and the thunderous majesty of God's self-revelation that the prophet has just witnessed in his vision.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, qol is used to paint vivid pictures of divine majesty and human response. In the Garden of Eden, Adam and Eve heard the qol of the Lord walking in the cool of the day and hid in terror. At Mount Sinai, the qol of the trumpet sounded long and grew louder, accompanied by thunder, establishing a boundary of holy dread between God and Israel. The poets of Israel used qol to describe thunder as God’s voice stripping the forests bare and making the deer give birth. In every case, when the qol of God is unleashed, the creation must respond; nothing can remain passive or indifferent in its presence.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, qol reminds us that our God is a speaking God who does not leave Himself without a witness.

  • The Voice of Revelation: God's voice is the medium of His self-revelation. He speaks so that humanity may know His character, His law, and His covenant promises.

  • The Voice of Sovereign Power: The voice of God carries the full weight of His divine decree. When God speaks, history moves, empires fall, and the earth melts.

  • The Necessity of Holy Trembling: When a holy God speaks in judgment, the appropriate spiritual posture for fallen man is holy fear. A lack of trembling in the presence of God's voice reveals a hardened and self-righteous heart.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:16, the prophet has just finished describing a glorious and terrifying theophany (Habakkuk 3:3-15)—a vision of God marching through the earth in indignation to crush His enemies and rescue His covenant people.

When Habakkuk says, "My lips quivered at the voice," he is experiencing the physical and spiritual aftermath of hearing this divine report. The qol of God acts as a devastating wave of holiness. The prophet's body trembles, his lips shake so much he can barely speak, and a sense of decay and weakness enters his bones.

This physical collapse, however, is the beginning of his spiritual triumph. By utterly collapsing before the qol of God, Habakkuk lets go of his demands for explanations and his frustration over Babylon's impending invasion. Trembling at the voice of God prepares him to "rest in the day of trouble." It is only when we are thoroughly humbled by God's voice that we can stand firm in the midst of worldly shaking.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 29:3 - "The voice of the LORD is over the waters; The God of glory thunders; The LORD is over many waters."

  • Psalm 46:6 - "The nations raged, the kingdoms were moved; He uttered His voice, the earth melted."

  • Isaiah 40:3 - "The voice of one crying in the wilderness: 'Prepare the way of the LORD; Make straight in the desert A highway for our God.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew thought, hearing and obeying are deeply intertwined. The Hebrew language does not have a distinct, single word for "obey." Instead, the writers of Scripture used the idiom shama l'qol—literally, "to hear to the voice" or "to listen to the voice." To truly hear God's qol is to submit to it. Habakkuk’s trembling response in chapter 3 is the ultimate proof that he has truly "heard" God. He has stopped arguing with the Lord's providence and has yielded his life, his nation, and his future to the sovereign voice of the Creator.
