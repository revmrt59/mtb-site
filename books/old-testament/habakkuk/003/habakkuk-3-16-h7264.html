TREMBLED - ( ragaz , Strong's H7264 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ragaz denotes a deep physical and emotional agitation, manifesting as shaking, quivering, or trembling. In Habakkuk 3:16, it describes the prophet’s visceral reaction to the terrifying revelation of God's impending judgment upon Judah, combined with a profound awe of the Lord's majestic power. Rather than representing a paralyzing fear of defeat, this trembling signifies a holy, reverent dread that ultimately leads to quiet trust and surrender to God's sovereign will.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word ragaz is rooted in the physical reality of shaking, quaking, or vibration. It describes the bodily response of an individual who is completely overcome by an external power or an internal emotional storm. It is the shaking of the earth during an earthquake, the quivering of leaves in a violent wind, or the uncontrolled knocking of knees in the presence of terror. In the ancient Near East, emotions were not viewed as abstract concepts but as physical sensations felt in the organs. When a person experienced ragaz, their entire physical being was destabilized. It is a word of complete undoing, where self-control is lost and the body yields to the physical reality of fear, anger, or awe.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ragaz means to tremble, quake, shake, or be agitated. Depending on the grammatical context, it expresses a wide semantic range of intense emotional and physical states:

  • In the Qal stem, it refers to physical shaking—such as the earth quaking—or the emotional trembling of people seized by fear, awe, or grief.

  • In the Hiphil (causative) stem, it means to cause to tremble, to provoke to anger, or to disturb.

  • In the Hithpael (reflexive) stem, it denotes raging or showing oneself angry and agitated.

In Habakkuk 3:16, the verb is used in the Qal stem to describe the involuntary, violent physical shaking of the prophet’s body and inner being when confronted with the voice and vision of Yahweh.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs ragaz to paint vivid pictures of power and vulnerability:

  • Cosmic Convulsion: The word describes the physical creation reacting to the presence of its Creator. When Yahweh marches forth, the mountains and the earth literally shake (ragaz) in submission to His majesty.

  • National Panic: It depicts the overwhelming dread that paralyzes pagan nations when they hear of God’s redemptive and judgment-executing power.

  • Deep Personal Grief: It is used to describe King David’s violent emotional shaking upon hearing of the death of his rebellious son Absalom, illustrating that the word carries the weight of profound emotional devastation.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, ragaz represents the only appropriate creaturely response to the manifest holiness and sovereign power of God. When God speaks or reveals His judgment, human self-reliance is completely shattered. This holy trembling is the physical counterpart to the spiritual "fear of the Lord." It demonstrates that God’s presence is too immense to be processed by human intellect alone; it must be felt in the very bones. Yet, in the economy of grace, this trembling is not meant to drive the believer to despair. Instead, it is the necessary precursor to true faith. To tremble before God’s Word is to abandon self-assertion, allowing the believer to find true security in the midst of worldly shaking.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:16, the prophet has just witnessed a terrifying vision of God riding on horses and chariots of salvation, threshing the nations in anger to save His people. The immediate prospect, however, is the brutal invasion of Judah by the Chaldeans.

Habakkuk uses ragaz twice in this single verse to frame his reaction: "my body trembled" (literally, "my belly shook") and "I trembled in myself" (literally, "I shook in my place"). This repetition highlights the absolute totality of his collapse. His lips quiver, rottenness enters his bones, and his legs fail him.

However, this trembling is a vital spiritual pivot. By trembling (ragaz) at the voice of God rather than merely fearing the power of the Babylonians, Habakkuk is repositioned. His physical shaking empties him of his previous doubts and complaints. It prepares him to "rest in the day of trouble." The physical agitation of ragaz yields the spiritual quietness of absolute trust.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 2:25 - "This day I will begin to put the dread and fear of you upon the nations under the whole heaven, who shall hear the report of you, and shall tremble and be in anguish because of you."

  • Psalm 18:7 - "Then the earth shook and trembled; The foundations of the hills also quaked and were shaken, Because He was angry."

  • Isaiah 64:2 - "As fire burns brushwood, As fire causes water to boil— To make Your name known to Your adversaries, That the nations may tremble at Your presence!"

  • Joel 2:1 - "Blow the trumpet in Zion, And sound an alarm in My holy mountain! Let all the inhabitants of the land tremble; For the day of the LORD is coming, For it is at hand:"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek Septuagint (LXX), the translators rendered ragaz in Habakkuk 3:16 using the Greek verbs ptoeo (to be terrified, startled) and tremo (to tremble, quake), while the noun form is rendered as tromos (trembling, shivering). This Greek terminology carries over into the New Testament to describe the "fear and trembling" (phobos kai tromos) with which believers are to work out their salvation (Philippians 2:12). It underscores that a healthy, trembling awe of God's sovereign power is a continuous, vital component of faithful discipleship in both Testaments.
