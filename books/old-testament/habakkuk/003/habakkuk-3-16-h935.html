ENTERED - ( bo' , Strong's H0935 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bo' is a foundational Old Testament word meaning "to come in," "enter," or "go." In Habakkuk 3:16, it describes the terrifying, invasive impact of God's voice upon the prophet's physical being, where decay and weakness "entered" his very bones. This graphic expression depicts a holy dread and complete undoing of the self when confronted with the presence and imminent judgment of Almighty God, shifting the prophet from self-reliance to absolute dependence on God's mercy.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb bo' occurs over 2,500 times in the Hebrew Bible, making it one of the most common verbs in the Old Testament. Its primary spatial meaning involves movement from one place to another, specifically crossing a boundary to come inside. It serves as the natural antonym to yatsa' ("to go out"). In its most concrete sense, it describes entering a tent, a home, a city, or a specific territory.

In Habakkuk 3:16, the word is used in a highly dramatic, metaphorical sense. Rather than a person entering a physical space, "rottenness" (raqab) enters the prophet's "bones" (atsamim). The boundary crossed is not geographical, but physiological and psychological. The sheer terror of God's impending judgment crosses the threshold of Habakkuk’s physical body, invading his innermost framework and paralyzing his strength.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The core meaning of bo' is to come, enter, go in, or come upon.

  • In the Qal stem (as found in Habakkuk 3:16), the verb means to go in, enter, or come. It describes an active entry of an object or force into a container or space.

  • In the Hiphil (causative) stem, it means to bring in, lead in, or introduce.

  • In the Hophal (passive) stem, it means to be brought in.

In this poetic context, the Qal stem portrays an unstoppable, passive vulnerability on the part of the prophet. An outside force—decay and trembling—actively penetrates his physical frame. The word conveys not just a superficial feeling of fear, but a deep, structural penetration of dread that compromises his core stability.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, bo' is employed to illustrate several key concepts:

  • Physical and Territorial Entry: Abraham entering Egypt (Genesis 12:11) or Israel entering the Promised Land (Deuteronomy 6:18).

  • Military Invasion: An enemy army entering a land or city to besiege and conquer it (2 Kings 19:32).

  • Natural and Temporal Transitions: The setting of the sun (Genesis 15:12) or the coming of a specific day or season.

  • Spiritual Penetration: The experience of fear, dread, or blessings coming upon a person (Job 3:25).

The word picture in Habakkuk 3:16 is an internal military invasion. Just as the Chaldeans are about to invade Judah's physical borders, the dread of God's voice has already invaded the borders of Habakkuk's physical body, conquering his strength and leaving him completely undone.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Holy Dread of God's Presence: True encounters with the holiness and sovereign judgment of God produce a profound physical and emotional shattering. This aligns with other biblical accounts where prophets are completely undone by theophanies, such as Isaiah in the temple (Isaiah 6) or Daniel by the Tigris River (Daniel 10).

  • The Emptying of Self: Before Habakkuk can sing his great song of faith and triumph in the face of destruction (Habakkuk 3:17-19), he must first be emptied of all human strength. The "entry" of rottenness into his bones represents the death of his self-reliance, which is necessary for him to rely solely on "the God of my salvation."

  • Sovereign Submission: By allowing this dread to enter him, Habakkuk demonstrates a posture of total surrender to God’s painful decree. He does not rage against the sovereign plan; instead, he trembles in wait for the day of trouble.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Habakkuk 3 is a majestic prayer and a recording of a divine theophany where God marches forth in judgment and salvation. In verses 3-15, the prophet witnesses a vision of God's cosmic power shaking the earth.

In verse 16, the focus shifts to the prophet's immediate physical response to this revelation. The noise of God's voice makes his lips quiver and his belly tremble. The climax of this physical undoing is that rottenness entered his bones. In Hebrew thought, the bones represent the seat of physical strength, health, and life-force. For rottenness to enter the bones means the absolute collapse of his vital power. This internal invasion of weakness is a literary and experiential precursor to the physical invasion of the Babylonian troops described at the end of the verse.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Septuagint (LXX) Translation: The ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament translates ba' in this verse using the verb eiselthen (from eiserchomai, meaning "to enter" or "go into"). This is the same verb used in the New Testament to describe sin "entering" the world (Romans 5:12) or believers "entering" into God's eternal rest (Hebrews 4:11).

  • Structural Parallelism: There is a striking parallel in Habakkuk 3:16 between the rottenness that entered (bo') the prophet’s bones and the invaders who come up (alah) against the people. The internal invasion of holy fear prepares the prophet's heart to endure the external historical invasion of the enemy.
