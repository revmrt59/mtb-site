FRUIT - ( yebul , Strong's H2981 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word yebul refers to the produce, yield, or harvest of the soil, representing the tangible fruit of agricultural labor. In Habakkuk 3:17, it captures the devastating reality of complete agricultural failure in an agrarian society. Even when the fields and vines yield absolutely no fruit, the prophet resolves to rejoice in Yahweh, demonstrating a profound, mature faith that is grounded entirely in the character of God rather than material blessings.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word yebul is derived from the root yabal, which carries the primary meaning of bringing, carrying, or conducting, often in a solemn or formal procession. The word picture of yebul is that of the earth "bringing forth" or "bearing" its harvest as a rich cargo. It depicts the land presenting its wealth to those who cultivate it, a physical manifestation of divine favor and the natural cycle of life. It implies that harvest is not merely a mechanical result of human effort, but the earth actively yielding its God-given increase.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yebul refers to agricultural produce, crops, grain, or the fruit of trees and vines. It encompasses the collective yield of the ground that sustains human and animal life. In a covenantal context, yebul represents the material goods that flow from God’s blessing upon the rain and the soil. Conversely, the absence or withholding of yebul signifies famine, economic devastation, and the withdrawal of divine favor due to covenant disobedience.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, yebul is used to measure the spiritual and material state of the nation. It appears in several key word pictures:

  • Covenant Blessing: The earth yielding its yebul is a primary sign of God's covenantal peace and blessing.

  • Sovereign Judgment: God shutting up the heavens so that the soil cannot produce its yebul acts as a vivid picture of divine discipline and the fragility of human security.

  • Fruit of Labor: It contrasts human toil with divine enablement, reminding the farmer that while they plant, it is God who causes the earth to bring forth its wealth.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yebul highlights the doctrine of divine providence. Humanity is utterly dependent upon God for daily sustenance. The presence of agricultural produce is not guaranteed by human ingenuity but is a gift from the Creator. When God removes the yebul, He strips away the false security of material wealth, forcing His people to confront the state of their relationship with Him. The ultimate theological lesson of yebul is that God Himself is more valuable than the material blessings He provides.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:17, the prophet describes a worst-case scenario for an agrarian economy. The fig tree fails, the olive crop fails, the fields produce no food, the stalls are empty of livestock, and there is no yebul (fruit) on the vines. The grape harvest was not only essential for nutrition but was also the biblical symbol of joy, celebration, and gladness. The total absence of yebul on the vines means a life stripped of comfort, joy, and basic sustenance. By choosing this word, Habakkuk emphasizes that even when the most visible signs of God's physical blessing are completely cut off, the believer's true source of joy remains untouched in the person of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 26:4: "then I will give you rain in its season, the land shall yield its produce, and the trees of the field shall yield their fruit."

  • Deuteronomy 11:17: "then the Lord’s anger be aroused against you, and He shut up the heavens so that there be no rain, and the land yield no produce, and you perish quickly from the good land which the Lord is giving you."

  • Psalm 67:6: "Then the earth shall yield her increase; God, our own God, shall bless us."

  • Psalm 85:12: "Yes, the Lord will give what is good; And our land will yield its increase."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) frequently translates yebul using words like genema (produce/fruit). This links the Old Testament concept of physical produce directly to the New Testament teachings on spiritual fruitfulness. Just as the physical land must yield its yebul to sustain bodily life, the believer must yield spiritual fruit to honor God. Habakkuk’s response to the lack of physical yebul is the ultimate spiritual fruit—unshakeable faith and joy in the midst of suffering.
