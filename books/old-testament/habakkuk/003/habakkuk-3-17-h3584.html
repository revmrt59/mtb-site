FAIL - ( kachash , Strong's H3584 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb kachash fundamentally communicates the act of deception, denial, or disappointment. When applied to agricultural failure, as in the labor of the olive tree, it paints a vivid picture of a crop that "lies" or "deceives" by failing to yield its expected fruit despite the farmer's hard work. In the face of such devastating disappointment, where circumstances mock human effort and survival is threatened, the believer is called to find absolute joy and strength in God alone.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of kachash involves deception, untruthfulness, or disappointment. In a physical or relational sense, it refers to lying, denying a claim, or showing unfaithfulness. When applied to nature or agriculture, the word represents a metaphorical lie. An olive tree that produces leaves but no olives "lies" to its cultivator. It promises life and sustenance but delivers emptiness. This agricultural deception was devastating in the ancient Near East, where olives were essential for food, cooking, lighting, and trade.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Kachash carries a primary meaning of lying, deceiving, or denying.

  • In the context of physical health or material conditions, it means to waste away, grow lean, or fail.

  • In covenantal or legal contexts, it denotes dealing falsely, breaking faith, or shrinking back in feigned submission.

  • In Habakkuk 3:17, the term means to fail, dry up, or disappoint expectations, describing a total collapse of the olive crop.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Relational Deception: The word is frequently used to describe a person lying to another or denying a known truth, such as Sarah denying she laughed at God's promise.

  • Covenantal Unfaithfulness: It describes Israel's false dealing with God, where they offer feigned obedience or live in outright deceit.

  • Physical Emaciation: It depicts the body shrinking or wasting away under suffering or fasting, where the flesh "fails" of its fatness.

  • Agricultural Failure: The crop "deceives" the farmer. The labor expended on the olive tree yields nothing, leaving the community in economic and physical ruin.

4. Theological Implications

The use of kachash in Habakkuk highlights the fallen, unpredictable nature of the material world. Earthly resources are ultimately unreliable; they can "lie" to us by promising security and failing to deliver. This contrasts sharply with the character of God, who cannot lie and whose faithfulness never fails. When the creation "fails" (deceives), the believer's faith is shifted from the gift to the Giver. Ultimate security is found not in the harvest, but in the God of salvation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Habakkuk 3:17-19 is a supreme expression of faith amidst absolute desolation. The prophet lists the failure of the most vital ancient crops and livestock: figs, grapes, olives, grain, sheep, and cattle. The "labor of the olive" refers to the extensive work required to cultivate olive trees, which takes years to mature. For this labor to "fail" (deceive) meant the loss of a multi-year investment and a primary source of life. Despite this total loss of earthly security, Habakkuk resolves to "rejoice in the LORD." The failure of the olive tree does not mean the failure of God's covenant promises.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 18:15: "But Sarah denied it, saying, 'I did not laugh,' for she was afraid."

  • Leviticus 6:2: "If a person sins and commits a trespass against the Lord by lying to his neighbor about what was delivered to him to keep..."

  • Leviticus 19:11: "You shall not steal, nor deal falsely, nor lie to one another."

  • Hosea 4:2: "By swearing and lying, killing and stealing and committing adultery..."

  • Hosea 9:2: "The threshing floor and the winepress shall not feed them, and the new wine shall fail her."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The olive tree was a symbol of peace, prosperity, and divine blessing in Israel. The failure of the olive crop was often associated with divine judgment or covenant curses. Habakkuk’s song moves from a fearful recognition of God’s coming judgment on the nation to a triumphant declaration of trust. The prophet accepts that even if the land suffers under covenant curses and the crops "lie," his joy is anchored in the unchanging character of Yahweh.
