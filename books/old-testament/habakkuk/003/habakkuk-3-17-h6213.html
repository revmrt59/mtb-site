YIELD - ( asah , Strong's H6213 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, the Hebrew verb asah is a foundational and highly versatile word meaning "to make," "to do," "to fashion," or "to produce." When applied to agricultural contexts like Habakkuk 3:17, it refers to the fields "yielding" or producing food. Rather than describing a passive process of growth, asah conveys an active, generative labor of bringing something into functional existence. In the face of total agricultural failure—where the fields fail to "make" or yield sustenance—the believer's faith is tested and refined, shifting from the gifts of the earth to the Giver of salvation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew verb asah is one of the most frequently used words in the Old Testament, occurring over 2,600 times. It carries the physical concept of labor, craftsmanship, and active construction. While the Hebrew word bara (to create) is reserved exclusively for God's divine action of bringing something out of nothing, asah describes forming, shaping, or producing something out of existing materials. In agrarian life, the land is seen as an active participant under God's sovereign care, "working" or "manufacturing" food for human survival. When the fields "yield" no food, it indicates a complete cessation of this vital, life-sustaining labor of the earth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of asah is to do, fashion, accomplish, make, or produce. In agricultural contexts, it specifically means to bear fruit, yield a crop, or produce grain. The word implies an active output or consequence of labor. In Habakkuk 3:17, it is translated as "yield" (NKJV), representing the earth's failure to produce its expected harvest. The lexical range spans from physical manufacturing to ethical behavior (doing good or evil) and divine actions in history.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Yielding of Nature: Scripture frequently depicts plants and trees as active agents that "make" (asah) fruit. In Genesis 1:11, the fruit tree is designed to "yield" (asah) fruit according to its kind.

  • The Industry of Creation: The word presents a picture of nature as an industrious household or factory designed by God to continuously manufacture blessings for humanity.

  • The Cessation of Productivity: When the prophet declares that the fields "yield no food," the word picture is one of sudden unemployment in nature. The "workers" (the fields) have gone silent; the machinery of agricultural provision has ground to a halt.

4. Theological Implications

  • Sovereignty Over Provision: God is the ultimate authority behind whether the creation "yields" or fails to yield. Natural productivity is not an inherent guarantee but a gift of divine grace.

  • The Trial of Faith: Habakkuk's joy is not anchored in the "yielding" of the fields but in the Lord Himself. True faith persists even when God's material provisions cease, demonstrating that the Giver is greater than the gift.

  • Covenant Consequences: In the Deuteronomic covenant, obedience brings agricultural abundance (asah), while disobedience brings barrenness. The failure of the fields to yield in Habakkuk's day reflects the spiritual decay and impending judgment on Judah, yet the prophet responds with transcendent praise despite the covenant curses being realized.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:17, the prophet contemplates the terrifying, impending invasion of the Babylonians (Chaldeans). An invading army would systematically destroy the crops, cut down trees, and ruin the fields. The prophet lists a sequence of devastating failures: the fig tree does not blossom, the vines have no fruit, the olive labor fails, the fields yield no food, and the flocks and herds are cut off. The specific use of asah regarding the "fields" emphasizes that despite the farmer's hard work in tilling, sowing, and watering, the soil itself refuses to perform its expected duty. It is a state of absolute, life-threatening deprivation. Yet, this total lack of physical "yield" sets up the supreme triumph of faith in verse 18: "Yet I will rejoice in the Lord."

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:11 - "Then God said, 'Let the earth bring forth grass, the herb that yields seed, and the fruit tree that yields fruit according to its kind, whose seed is in itself, on the earth'; and it was so."

  • Genesis 18:8 - "So he took butter and milk and the calf which he had prepared, and set it before them; and he stood by them under the tree as they ate."

  • Psalm 104:19 - "He appointed the moon for seasons; The sun knows its going down."

  • Hosea 8:7 - "They sow the wind, And reap the whirlwind. The stalk has no bud; It shall yield no meal. If it should yield, Aliens would swallow it up."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Unlike the English word "yield," which can sometimes mean to surrender or give way, the Hebrew asah never carries the passive sense of submission. It is always active, productive, and operational.

  • The contrast between bara (creation out of nothing) and asah (working with existing materials) highlights that when God prevents the fields from "yielding," He is suspending the natural order of the materials He has already created.

  • Pastorally, this serves as a reminder that when our earthly labors fail to "yield" visible results—whether in finance, health, or ministry—God remains our constant source of strength and salvation.
