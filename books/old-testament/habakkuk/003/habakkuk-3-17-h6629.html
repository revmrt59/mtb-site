FLOCK - ( tso'n , Strong's H6629 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word tso'n refers collectively to small livestock, specifically sheep and goats, which constituted the economic and spiritual lifeblood of ancient Israel. In the Old Testament, the flock represents physical sustenance, pastoral wealth, and vulnerability, serving as a primary metaphor for God’s people under the care of their divine Shepherd. In Habakkuk 3:17, the sudden disappearance of the flock from the fold represents the ultimate extremity of agricultural and economic devastation under divine judgment. Yet, this bleak portrait of an empty fold serves as the launchpad for one of the greatest declarations of faith in Scripture, demonstrating that even when the earthly flock is entirely lost, the believer's joy remains secure in the God of salvation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, pastoral life was central to daily survival. The collective noun tso'n encompasses both sheep and goats, which were typically herded together. Unlike larger cattle, which required lush pastures, these small livestock could thrive in the rugged, semi-arid terrain of Judea. They provided families with milk, meat, wool for clothing, skins for containers, and were the primary animals used in the sacrificial system. Because sheep and goats are defenseless, easily lost, and entirely dependent on human guidance, the image of the flock naturally evoked a deep sense of vulnerability, requiring constant protection, provision, and leading from a dedicated shepherd.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term tso'n designates a collective flock of sheep and goats. It refers strictly to small cattle, distinguishing them from baqar, which refers to larger cattle or herds of oxen. Lexically, tso'n denotes wealth in a pastoral economy, a source of domestic provision, and the raw material for religious offerings. The word emphasizes the corporate nature of the group; it is rarely used to speak of an individual animal, but rather points to the collective unit that moves, feeds, and finds safety together.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Literal Wealth and Provision: Abraham, Jacob, and Job possessed vast quantities of tso'n, which served as the primary measure of their earthly prosperity.

  • Metaphor for God's People: Scripture repeatedly describes Israel as the flock (tso'n) of Yahweh's pasture. This highlights their election, their need for guidance, and God's tender, protective ownership over them.

  • Leaderless Vulnerability: When the scriptures depict a nation without godly leadership, they are described as "sheep (tso'n) having no shepherd," emphasizing confusion, exposure to predators, and aimless wandering.

  • Sacrifice: The tso'n provided the daily burnt offerings (olat) and the Passover lambs, painting a picture of innocent life given to cover the sins of the people.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of tso'n lies in its portrayal of humanity's relationship with God. By identifying His people as a flock, God underscores their inherent limitations: they cannot defend themselves against spiritual predators, they cannot find green pastures on their own, and they easily go astray. This necessitates the role of Yahweh as Shepherd (ro'eh). Furthermore, the flock represents the covenant community. God does not merely care for isolated individuals; He shepherds a corporate flock. In times of national judgment, the removal of the flock is a physical manifestation of covenant curses, showing that disobedience disrupts both the land's fertility and the people's security.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:17, the prophet contemplates the impending Chaldean invasion, describing a scene of total agricultural collapse. The climax of this devastation is that "the flock [tso'n] be cut off from the fold." The "fold" (miklah) was the stone enclosure where sheep were gathered at night for safety from predators and thieves. To have the flock "cut off" (gazar, meaning to divide, exclude, or violently sever) means the pens are empty—either because the invading armies have slaughtered the sheep for food, driven them off as plunder, or because drought has killed them. In a pastoral economy, an empty fold signifies total bankruptcy, starvation, and the loss of spiritual means for sacrifice. Yet, the nuance here is transitional: Habakkuk gazes at the empty folds and dry fields, yet resolves to rejoice in Yahweh. The loss of the physical flock drives the prophet to find his ultimate security in the spiritual Shepherd who cannot be plundered.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 100:3: "Know that the LORD, He is God; It is He who has made us, and not we ourselves; We are His people and the sheep of His pasture."

  • Isaiah 53:6: "All we like sheep have gone astray; We have turned, every one, to his own way; And the LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all."

  • 1 Kings 22:17: "Then he said, 'I saw all Israel scattered on the mountains, as sheep that have no shepherd. And the LORD said, "These have no master. Let each return to his house in peace."'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

While the loss of the tso'n in Habakkuk 3:17 points to temporal devastation, it serves as a profound contrast to the New Testament fulfillment in Jesus Christ. Jesus identifies Himself as the "Good Shepherd" who lays down His life for the sheep (probaton, the Greek equivalent of tso'n). When physical provisions fail and the earthly "flocks" of our security are cut off, the believer is reminded that our ultimate Shepherd has already secured our eternal fold. The empty stalls of Habakkuk challenge modern believers to ask whether their joy is rooted in the flock (the gifts) or in the Shepherd (the Giver).
