STALLS - ( repheth , Strong's H7517 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word repheth refers to an enclosure, pen, or stall specifically built for housing domesticated cattle or herds. Occurring only once in the Hebrew Bible (a hapax legomenon) in Habakkuk 3:17, it represents the ultimate place of agricultural security and livestock storage. In the ancient Near East, an empty stall indicated severe economic devastation, famine, or divine judgment. Yet, in the context of Habakkuk’s prayer, the empty stall becomes the ultimate backdrop for a triumphant confession of faith—demonstrating that even when the most basic sources of earthly sustenance and wealth are completely swept away, the believer's joy and strength remain fully secure in the person of Yahweh.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the agrarian economy of ancient Israel, livestock represented capital, food, labor, and sacrificial worship. A repheth was not merely a decorative barn but a sturdy, protective shelter designed to safeguard valuable oxen and cows from predators, harsh weather, and theft. These stalls were often constructed from stone or integrated into the lower levels of domestic dwellings to share warmth and security. An empty stall was a catastrophic sight, signaling that the primary means of plowing fields, transporting goods, and producing milk and meat had perished.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun repheth means a stall, stable, or enclosure for cattle. It is derived from an unused root meaning to gather or to plaster/build. In the plural form repathim, as found in Habakkuk 3:17, it refers to the stalls or pens where herds of cattle are kept. The term denotes physical structures of confinement and safety for larger domesticated animals, distinct from smaller sheepfolds.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Because repheth occurs only once in the Old Testament, its word picture is defined entirely by its stark contrast with abundance. A healthy, occupied stall hummed with life, signifying wealth, preparation, and divine blessing. Conversely, the empty stall in Habakkuk's vision paints a grim picture of absolute desolation, silence, and death. It symbolizes the grinding halt of daily agricultural life and the total collapse of the domestic economy.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the empty stall serves as a crucible for genuine faith. In the Old Covenant, material prosperity and overflowing stalls were frequently linked to covenant blessings, while drought and empty stalls indicated covenant curses. By declaring praise in the face of empty stalls, the prophet transcends a transactional relationship with God. The passage teaches that God Himself, rather than His material blessings, is the ultimate source of the believer’s joy, salvation, and strength. True biblical faith worships the Giver even when the gifts are entirely absent.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:17, the term repathim occurs at the climax of a six-fold description of total agricultural ruin (figs, vines, olives, fields, flocks, and herds). The progression moves from luxuries (figs and wine) to essentials (olives and grain) to livestock (sheep and cattle). The "herd in the stalls" represents the most valuable agricultural asset. The loss of the herd from the stalls indicates that the devastation is complete; there is no reserve left for the future. Yet, this extreme deprivation sets up the dramatic "Yet I will rejoice" of verse 18, showing that the prophet's faith is not anchored in economic security but in the sovereign Lord.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because repheth is a hapax legomenon in the Hebrew Scriptures, its direct equivalent in the Greek Septuagint (LXX), phatne (meaning stall or manger), provides the linguistic link to other biblical passages.

  • Luke 2:7 - "And she brought forth her firstborn Son, and wrapped Him in swaddling cloths, and laid Him in a manger (phatne), because there was no room for them in the inn."

  • Luke 2:12 - "And this will be the sign to you: You will find a Babe wrapped in swaddling cloths, lying in a manger (phatne)."

  • Luke 2:16 - "And they came with haste and found Mary and Joseph, and the Babe lying in a manger (phatne)."

  • Luke 13:15 - "The Lord then answered him and said, 'Hypocrite! Does not each one of you on the Sabbath loose his ox or donkey from the stall (phatne), and lead it away to water?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Ancient Near Eastern parallels show that the loss of cattle stalls was often associated with military invasions and scorched-earth tactics. When the Babylonian army invaded, they would confiscate or slaughter all livestock, leaving the stalls empty and ruined.

  • While Proverbs 14:4 states that "Where no oxen are, the trough is clean; but much increase comes by the strength of an ox," Habakkuk 3:17 describes a state far worse than a clean trough—it describes the complete absence of the herd itself due to national judgment.

  • The Septuagint uses the plural phatnais (mangers/stalls) to translate repathim, highlighting that the place where the animals fed and rested was completely abandoned.
