FIELDS - ( shedemah , Strong's H7709 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun shedemah refers to cultivated fields, agricultural terraces, or crop-producing lands, specifically those used for growing grain or vines. In the context of ancient Near Eastern agriculture, these fields represented the primary source of life-sustaining sustenance and economic security. In Habakkuk 3:17, the failure of these fields to yield food paints a vivid picture of total economic collapse, physical starvation, and the absolute removal of earthly security, setting the stage for one of the most profound expressions of faith in the entire Old Testament.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The word shedemah (most often occurring in its plural form, shedemot) paints a picture of terraced hillsides and fertile valleys meticulously prepared for cultivation. In the rugged topography of ancient Israel, farming required building stone terraces on hillsides to retain precious soil and water.

  • These cultivated fields or terraces were highly valued because they required intense, backbreaking human labor to construct and maintain. They represented the lifeblood of the agrarian community.

  • Historically, a barren shedemah was not merely an inconvenience; it was a national catastrophe. Without the yield of these fields, the community faced immediate famine, as there were no modern supply chains to import food.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The noun shedemah refers to a cultivated field, agricultural terrace, or arable land used for sowing crops or planting vineyards. It describes land that has been worked by human hands to produce food, distinct from wild pastureland or desert.

  • The semantic range spans from grain-producing fields to terraced vineyards on sloping hills. The term emphasizes productivity and the human labor invested in securing a harvest.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • In the Old Testament, shedemah often appears in contexts of agricultural devastation, military conquest, or divine judgment. When God's judgment falls on a nation, these cultivated fields languish, dry up, or are burned by invading armies.

  • The word is used as a metaphor for national vitality and divine blessing. Conversely, barren fields depict spiritual desertion and the removal of God's hand of favor. In prophetic poetry, the transformation of these fields from fertile to barren highlights the fragility of human effort apart from God's blessing.

4. Theological Implications

  • The theological weight of shedemah in Habakkuk lies in the stripping away of common grace and covenant blessings. Under the Mosaic Covenant, agricultural abundance was a sign of obedience, while crop failure was a sign of judgment.

  • By highlighting the failure of the shedemot, the text shifts the believer's focus from the gifts of God (the harvest) to the Giver Himself. It challenges God's people to find their ultimate joy and security not in the temporal blessings of the land, but in the eternal character of Yahweh.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 3:17, the prophet describes a worst-case scenario. The failure of the fields to yield food is part of a comprehensive list of agricultural disasters (figs, grapes, olives, grains, flocks, and herds). This represents the complete destruction of the nation’s food supply, likely due to an invading army practicing scorched-earth warfare.

  • Even if the cultivated fields fail to produce anything, the prophet resolves to rejoice in the Lord. The word emphasizes that even when the most basic, dependable sources of physical nourishment completely fail, God remains a sufficient source of strength and salvation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 32:32: "For their vine is of the vine of Sodom And of the fields of Gomorrah..."

  • 2 Kings 23:4: "...and burned them outside Jerusalem in the fields of Kidron, and carried their ashes to Bethel."

  • Isaiah 16:8: "For the fields of Heshbon languish, And the vine of Sibmah..."

  • Jeremiah 31:40: "...and all the fields as far as the Brook Kidron, to the corner of the Horse Gate toward the east, shall be holy to the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Septuagint (LXX) often translates shedemah with terms related to fields or plains, such as pedion. In Habakkuk 3:17, the Greek translation focuses on the lack of crop yield on the land.

  • The contrast between the "labor of the olive" failing and the "fields yielding no food" emphasizes that both the luxury items (oil, wine) and the absolute necessities (bread and grain from the fields) are completely gone, leaving the believer utterly dependent on divine provision.
