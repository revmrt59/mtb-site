HIGH HILLS - ( bamah , Strong's H1116 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bamah refers to a high place, ridge, mountain height, or elevated summit. While it frequently carries a negative connotation in the Old Testament when associated with pagan cultic shrines ("high places"), its use in Habakkuk 3:19 represents a place of security, triumph, and spiritual elevation. Amidst economic devastation, national crisis, and overwhelming fear, God empowers the believer to transcend earthly trials, navigating dangerous, rocky peaks with the agility, stability, and confidence of a deer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word bamah designates physical elevations such as mountain ridges, hills, or the high backbones of the land. In the ancient Near East, high places were highly prized for both military strategy and spiritual significance. Geographically, high hills provided an advantageous vantage point, safety from attackers, and a position of dominance over the surrounding valleys. Metaphorically, walking on these high hills represents conquering obstacles, moving above danger, and sharing in God's sovereign domain.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The noun bamah denotes an eminence, high place, or mountain ridge.

  • In a physical sense, it refers to the heights of the earth, hills, or even the towering waves of the sea.

  • In a political or military sense, it denotes the fortified heights of a conquered land, signifying victory and ownership.

  • In a cultic sense, it refers to elevated platforms or sanctuaries used for worship, which were often illegitimate altars dedicated to foreign deities or unauthorized worship of Yahweh.

  • In poetic and prophetic literature, it signifies a place of triumph, security, and intimate spiritual communion with God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • bamah occurs over 100 times in the Hebrew Bible, most frequently translated as "high places" in the historical books in reference to idolatry.

  • The word picture in Habakkuk 3:19 draws on the imagery of the female deer or gazelle (ayyalah). To "walk on my high hills" means to tread upon dangerous, elevated terrain with absolute surefootedness. It is a picture of God-given grace that transforms a precarious path into a secure platform of victory.

  • Rather than being trapped in the low valleys of despair, defeat, or enemy occupation, the righteous are elevated to a place of safety where the enemy cannot reach them.

4. Theological Implications

  • True security is not found in favorable earthly circumstances but in the sovereign character of God.

  • The elevation to "high hills" is an act of divine enablement. It is not the believer's own strength that scales the heights, but God who makes the feet like deer's feet.

  • This term illustrates the ultimate triumph of faith. Habakkuk begins the book in the low valley of confusion and lament, but ends on the heights of praise and confidence, demonstrating that faith lifts the believer above the circumstances of judgment and suffering.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 3:19, bamah appears in the plural with a personal pronominal suffix (bamotay, "my high places" or "my high hills").

  • This concluding verse follows a terrifying description of impending Babylonian invasion and total economic devastation (no fruit on the vine, no herd in the stalls).

  • The nuance here is intensely personal. By calling them "my" high hills, Habakkuk claims the secure, elevated places God has prepared for him. Despite the physical desolation of the land, the prophet's spiritual standing remains secure, lofty, and unshakeable. It is a confession of radical joy in the midst of sovereign judgment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 32:13: "He made him ride on the high places of the earth, That he might eat the produce of the fields; He made him draw honey from the rock, And oil from the flinty rock;"

  • Deuteronomy 33:29: "Happy are you, O Israel! Who is like you, a people saved by the LORD, The shield of your help And the sword of your majesty! Your enemies shall submit to you, And you shall tread down their high places."

  • 2 Samuel 22:34: "He makes my feet like the feet of deer, And sets me on my high places."

  • Psalm 18:33: "He makes my feet like the feet of deer, And sets me on my high places."

  • Amos 4:13: "For behold, He who forms mountains and creates the wind, Who declares to man what his thought is, And makes the morning darkness, Who treads the high places of the earth—The LORD God of hosts is His name."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The parallel between Habakkuk 3:19, 2 Samuel 22:34, and Psalm 18:33 is striking. All three passages pair the agility of the deer's feet with the scaling of the bamah. This suggests a well-known poetic motif in Israel's liturgy representing salvation, deliverance, and divine empowerment.

  • While kings like Hezekiah and Josiah sought to tear down the idolatrous bamot (high places) of the land, God preserves a spiritual bamah for His people—a high place of pure worship, refuge, and intimacy that no earthly enemy can tear down.
