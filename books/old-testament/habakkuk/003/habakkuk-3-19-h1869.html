WALK - ( darak , Strong's H1869 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb darak fundamentally means to tread, march, bend a bow, or walk. In Habakkuk 3:19, it denotes being guided or enabled to walk securely on high places. Rather than representing a routine or passive stroll, this walking represents a sovereignly empowered, confident, and victorious progression through treacherous spiritual terrain.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of darak is to tread down, stamp, or step heavily. It is used in agricultural contexts for treading out grapes in a winepress, where the feet press down with force to extract the juice. It is also used in military contexts for treading a bow, which describes the physical act of stepping on a large wooden bow to bend it and secure the bowstring for battle. This concrete physical background conveys action, pressure, direction, and step-by-step progress. When applied to walking, it is not merely about moving the legs but about placing the feet firmly, making a path, and asserting dominion or progress over the ground.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary verbal stems of darak mean to tread, march, or bend a bow. In the Hiphil (causative) stem—as used in Habakkuk 3:19—it means to cause to tread, to lead, to guide, or to make one walk. The lexical range encompasses:

  • Literally stepping or treading upon something (such as a winepress or a path).

  • Directing, leading, or making someone walk along a path.

  • Bending a bow by placing the foot upon it for leverage.

  • In poetic and prophetic texts, it denotes God enabling a believer to march triumphantly or step securely over hazardous heights.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Winepress: Treading grapes represents productivity, abundance, and joy, but it also serves as a picture of divine judgment, where enemies are crushed underfoot.

  • The Bow: Bending a bow represents preparation for warfare, utilizing the full leverage of the foot to draw back the string with power.

  • The Path: To tread a path is to establish a way of life. When God causes someone to tread, He establishes their steps, making an otherwise impassable or dangerous road traversable.

4. Theological Implications

The use of darak highlights the sovereign enabling of God. Left to themselves, human feet slip, stumble, and fall, especially in times of crisis, fear, or judgment. When God becomes His servant's strength, He transforms the nature of their walk. Walking is no longer a human struggle but a divinely assisted march of victory. God does not necessarily change the rugged terrain; instead, He changes the believer's capability to navigate it safely.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Habakkuk 3 is a prayer of triumph in the face of imminent national devastation. The prophet anticipates the Babylonian invasion, which will bring famine, economic ruin, and physical destruction. In the climax of the book (verse 19), Habakkuk confesses that the Sovereign Lord is his strength. The phrase "He will make me walk on my high hills" uses the causative form of darak. The "high hills" represent treacherous, elevated places where a single slip means death, but they also represent places of safety and victory. By making his feet like "deer's feet" (sure-footed, swift, and secure), God enables Habakkuk to walk—to march securely and triumphantly—above the devastation of his circumstances. The walk is characterized by faith, joy, and spiritual elevation despite earthly collapse.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 1:36: "...except Caleb the son of Jephunneh; he shall see it, and to him and his children I am giving the land on which he walked, because he wholly followed the LORD."

  • Psalm 25:5: "Lead me in Your truth and teach me, For You are the God of my salvation; On You I wait all the day."

  • Psalm 25:9: "The humble He guides in justice, And the humble He teaches His way."

  • Psalm 119:35: "Make me walk in the path of Your commandments, For I delight in it."

  • Micah 6:15: "You shall sow, but not reap; You shall tread the olives, but not anoint yourselves with oil, And make sweet wine, but not drink wine."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Ancient Near East, high places were often the strongholds of enemies or sites of pagan worship. To "tread" or "walk" on these high places was a military idiom for conquest, victory, and taking possession of the land. Therefore, when Habakkuk speaks of God making him walk on high hills, he is not merely surviving; he is occupying the high ground, exercising spiritual dominion over fear, doubt, and calamity through faith.
