LORD - ( Yehovih , Strong's H3069 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word Yehovih (Strong's H3069) is a specialized vocalization of the Tetragrammaton, the sacred covenant name of God (YHWH). It is employed almost exclusively in compound titles alongside Adonai (Lord) to prevent the verbal duplication of "Lord Lord" during public reading. Scribes pointed the consonants of the covenant name with the vowels of Elohim (God), signaling the reader to pronounce it as Elohim. This name emphasizes God's absolute sovereignty, supreme authority, and covenant-keeping faithfulness. In Habakkuk 3:19, this name acts as the prophet’s theological anchor, transforming his fear of national collapse into absolute confidence in the sovereign Lord who is his strength.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • Yehovih is a morphological variant of the covenant name of God, YHWH, which means "the self-existent or eternal One."

  • In ancient Hebrew, the biblical text was written without vowels. Out of deep reverence for the command not to take the Lord’s name in vain, Jewish readers substituted the word Adonai (Lord) when reading the covenant name aloud. Scribes later placed the vowel points of Adonai under the consonants Y-H-W-H to guide the reader.

  • In cases where the text already contained the actual word Adonai immediately preceding or following the covenant name, reading it literally would produce the redundant phrase "Adonai Adonai" (Lord Lord).

  • To prevent this, scribes pointed the covenant name with the vowel markings of Elohim (God), producing the form Yehovih. When encountering this combination, the reader would speak the words as Adonai Elohim ("Lord God").

  • In Habakkuk 3:19, the phrase Yehovih Adonai is rendered "The LORD God" in the NKJV, highlighting both the majestic authority of the Creator and His personal relationship with His prophet.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Yehovih shares the identical semantic root and meaning as the standard Tetragrammaton, YHWH. It derives from the verb hayah (to be) or havah (to become).

  • It designates the self-existent, self-sufficient, and eternal God who causes all things to exist. It represents the unchangeable Ruler who remains faithful to His covenant promises.

  • When vocalized as Yehovih, the word specifically functions to highlight God’s ultimate lordship and supreme dominion. It carries the weight of Elohim (the omnipotent Creator) combined with the personal, redemptive character of YHWH.

  • Lexically, the word represents the supreme Master who possesses absolute authority to command, judge, and deliver His creation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Yehovih occurs over 300 times in the Old Testament, predominantly in the prophetic books such as Ezekiel, Amos, Isaiah, and Jeremiah, where the prophets declare the sovereign decrees of God.

  • The word picture is that of an absolute, unmatched Sovereign who sits enthroned over history. Unlike pagan deities who were limited by geography or function, Yehovih is the master of all nations, directing the rise and fall of empires according to His divine decree.

  • Addressing God as Yehovih paints a picture of a servant standing before an all-powerful King, submitting fully to His will while resting in His absolute capacity to protect and sustain.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Sovereignty of God: The name is a direct challenge to human pride and earthly powers. It declares that God is the ultimate authority, and no human ruler or nation can thwart His plans.

  • Covenant Loyalty: Because it is a form of the Tetragrammaton, Yehovih is deeply connected to God's redemptive relationship with His people. It reassures believers that the omnipotent Creator of the universe is personally committed to their ultimate good.

  • The Basis of Trust: By combining the concepts of supreme Lordship and covenant love, the name provides an immovable foundation for faith. It teaches that God is both willing (as the covenant Partner) and able (as the supreme Lord) to save.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 3:19, the prophet has just surveyed the impending invasion of the Chaldeans. The fields will be barren, the vines empty, and the stalls without cattle.

  • In the face of total economic and national devastation, Habakkuk does not despair. Instead, he declares, "The LORD God [Yehovih Adonai] is my strength."

  • By invoking Yehovih, Habakkuk recognizes that the approaching Babylonians are merely instruments in the hands of the sovereign Lord. Babylon is not supreme; Yehovih is.

  • This specific name shifts the prophet's focus from his circumstances to the ultimate source of power. Because his Master is the eternal, sovereign God, Habakkuk can trust Him to supply supernatural strength, enabling him to navigate the treacherous, high places of suffering with the sure-footedness of a deer.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 15:2 - "But Abram said, 'Lord GOD, what will You give me, seeing I go childless, and the heir of my house is Eliezer of Damascus?'"

  • Deuteronomy 3:24 - "O Lord GOD, You have begun to show Your servant Your greatness and Your mighty hand, for what god is there in heaven or on earth who can do anything like Your works and Your mighty deeds?"

  • 2 Samuel 7:18 - "Then King David went in and sat before the LORD; and he said: 'Who am I, O Lord GOD? And what is my house, that You have brought me this far?'"

  • Amos 3:7 - "Surely the Lord GOD does nothing, Unless He reveals His secret to His servants the prophets."

  • Ezekiel 37:3 - "And He said to me, 'Son of man, can these bones live?' So I answered, 'O Lord GOD, You know.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The use of Yehovih underscores the grammatical practice of Qere (what is read) and Ketiv (what is written) in the Hebrew Bible, reflecting a deep-seated reverence for God's holy name.

  • This scribal practice serves as a practical reminder for modern believers that our language concerning God should reflect both intimate boldness and profound holy fear.

  • In pastoral ministry, the compound title reminds us that when we preach or counsel, we do not point people to a weak or passive deity, but to the supreme, covenant-keeping Master of the universe.
