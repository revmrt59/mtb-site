INSTRUMENTS - ( neginah , Strong's H5058 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word neginah refers to music played on stringed instruments, the instruments themselves, or a song accompanied by strings. In the context of Habakkuk 3:19, it denotes the stringed instruments used in temple worship to accompany the prophet’s song of triumphant faith. By dedicating this prayer to the chief musician to be played on "my stringed instruments," Habakkuk transforms a personal cry of distress into a public corporate hymn of praise, demonstrating that God's strength enables believers to sing even in the midst of devastation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word neginah comes from the root verb nagan, which means "to touch" or "to strike" the strings of an instrument. It conveys the action of playing a stringed instrument (such as a lyre, harp, or lute) with the fingers or a plectrum. In ancient Israel, stringed instruments were highly valued for their sweet, resonant tones. They were used to express deep emotional realities, ranging from profound grief to ecstatic joy. The word evokes a picture of skilled fingers striking taut strings to produce a melody. This is not a chaotic noise, but a structured, beautiful, and deliberate expression of faith that requires preparation, skill, and focus. Just as a stringed instrument must be brought under tension to play in tune, so the prophet's faith was tensioned by trial to produce a song of exquisite praise.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Neginah carries a few closely related meanings depending on the context:

  • Music produced by stringed instruments (instrumental music).

  • The stringed instruments themselves (lyres, harps, ten-stringed instruments).

  • A song or melody sung with stringed accompaniment.

  • A mocking song or taunt (when used in a negative context, as in the book of Job or Lamentations).

In Habakkuk 3:19, the word appears in its plural form with a first-person pronominal suffix (bin'ginotai), literally translated as "with my stringed instruments" or "upon my stringed instruments." It refers to the physical instruments designated for accompanying this prophetic song in the temple liturgy.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word neginah is frequently found in the superscriptions of the Psalms (such as Psalms 4, 6, 54, 55, 67, and 76), where it is rendered "On stringed instruments" (Neginoth). This indicates that these psalms were specifically arranged for stringed accompaniment in the temple.

The word is also used to describe personal songs born out of deep trial. In Psalm 77:6, the psalmist says, "I call to remembrance my song [neginah] in the night." The word picture here is of a melody playing in the darkness of the soul, a quiet song of faith when outward circumstances are bleak. Conversely, it can picture mockers making a song out of someone's misery, as in Lamentations 3:14 ("I have become the ridicule of all my people—their taunting song [neginah] all the day").

4. Theological Implications

  • Corporate Worship and Liturgy: The presence of neginah in the final verse of Habakkuk shows that personal, prophetic revelation is meant to be shared and integrated into the corporate worship of God’s people. The prophet’s private wrestling with God ends in a liturgical song designed for public performance.

  • Faith Amidst Devastation: Habakkuk’s song begins with a vision of agricultural and economic collapse (Habakkuk 3:17-18). Yet, the book ends with an instruction for stringed instruments. The theological implication is stunning: faith does not wait for the harvest to return before it begins to sing; it prepares the instruments while the fields are still barren.

  • God as the Source of Joy: Playing stringed instruments in the wake of judgment signifies that God Himself is the believer's portion and exceeding joy, independent of earthly circumstances.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Habakkuk 3 is a prayer cast in the form of a psalm. It recounts God's historical deliverance of His people and expresses an unshakeable trust in Yahweh despite the impending Babylonian invasion. In verse 19, the prophet declares that God is his strength and will make his feet like deer's feet to walk on high places. The sudden transition to the liturgical postscript—"To the Chief Musician. With my stringed instruments"—shows that this confession is not a private sigh of relief, but a public anthem of victory.

The suffix "my" (my stringed instruments) suggests that Habakkuk may have been a Levite or temple musician himself, or that he personally owned and played these instruments. He is personally leading the praise, actively engaging his own resources and talents to glorify God in a time of national crisis.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 61:1 — "To the Chief Musician. On a stringed instrument. A Psalm of David. Hear my cry, O God; Attend to my prayer." (Used to designate the musical accompaniment for David's cry of distress).

  • Psalm 77:6 — "I call to remembrance my song in the night; I meditate within my heart, And my spirit makes diligent search." (Refers to a personal, reflective song of faith during a time of deep soul-searching).

  • Isaiah 38:20 — "The LORD was ready to save me; Therefore we will sing my songs with stringed instruments All the days of our life, in the house of the LORD." (Hezekiah's praise after being healed, pledging lifelong praise using stringed instruments).

  • Lamentations 5:14 — "The elders have ceased gathering at the gate, The young men from their music." (In the tragedy of Jerusalem's fall, the silencing of their stringed music represents the loss of joy).

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • From Lament to Liturgy: Habakkuk begins his book with a passionate complaint: "O LORD, how long shall I cry, and You will not hear?" (Habakkuk 1:2). He ends with neginah (stringed instruments). This trajectory—moving from agonizing questions to a structured song of praise—illustrates the classic biblical pattern of lament. The presence of musical instruments proves that God does not merely want us to survive our trials; He intends to bring us to a place of expressive, triumphant worship.

  • The Prophetic Role of Music: In the Old Testament, music was closely tied to prophecy (e.g., 1 Samuel 10:5; 2 Kings 3:15). Elisha called for a harpist, and as the harp was played, the hand of the Lord came upon him. Habakkuk's use of neginah underscores the reality that sacred music is not merely entertainment or a prelude to a message; it is a vehicle for prophetic truth and spiritual warfare.
