REMEMBER - ( zakar, Strong's H2142 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb zakar goes far beyond simple cognitive recall; it signifies an intentional direction of the mind that moves a person to decisive, covenant-keeping action. In the Old Testament, when God "remembers," He acts mercifully and faithfully on behalf of His people, honoring His promises even in the midst of judgment. In the context of Habakkuk's prayer, the plea for God to "remember mercy" is not a request for God to retrieve a forgotten thought, but a bold appeal for Him to actively manifest His covenant-keeping compassion while executing His righteous anger.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, memory is fundamentally linked to action. The Western concept of remembering is often passive and intellectual—a mental retrieval of stored information. In contrast, the Hebrew understanding of zakar is dynamic and functional. To remember is to act on behalf of the person or promise being remembered. Conversely, to "forget" (shakach) is not a slip of the mind, but a failure to act, a refusal to honor an obligation, or a departure from a relationship. When the biblical writers speak of remembering, they visualize a turning of the face, an engagement of the will, and the physical steps taken to fulfill a commitment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb zakar occurs over two hundred times in the Old Testament. Its primary meaning is to call to mind, recall, bring to remembrance, mention, or commemorate.

  • In the Qal stem (as used in Habakkuk 3:2), the verb denotes the active mental process of focusing attention on someone or something, which inevitably leads to a corresponding action.

  • In the Hiphil stem, it carries the causative sense of "causing to remember," "making mention of," or "bringing a memorial offering."

  • The word is deeply rooted in covenantal language. When applied to God, it indicates His sovereign decision to intervene in human history based on His pre-existing oaths and character. It is an act of divine grace whereby God brings the past promises of His covenant into the present reality of His people.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs zakar as a catalyst for redemption and salvation. The word picture is one of a king who, upon reviewing his records or hearing a petition, actively steps into a crisis to deliver his subjects.

  • The Ark in the Flood: When the waters prevailed upon the earth, the text records that God "remembered" Noah. This did not mean Noah had slipped God's mind; rather, it marked the precise moment God acted to make the winds blow and the waters recede.

  • The Exodus from Egypt: God heard the groaning of the Israelites in bondage and "remembered" His covenant. This remembering was the direct trigger for the call of Moses and the plagues of deliverance.

  • The Womb Opened: Hannah, in her deep distress, prayed that God would "remember" her. God's remembering of Hannah resulted in the conception and birth of Samuel.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, zakar teaches us that God’s memory is the anchor of human hope. God does not suffer from amnesia, nor is He distracted by the passage of time. His "remembering" is the faithful execution of His eternal decrees.

  • Covenant Faithfulness: God’s memory is bound to His covenant (berit). He remembers His promises because He is faithful to His own character (hesed—steadfast love).

  • The Basis of Prayer: Believers are repeatedly invited to "remind" God of His promises. This is not because God is forgetful, but because praying His promises back to Him aligns the believer's heart with His sovereign will and displays trust in His unchanging character.

  • Judgment and Grace: While God remembers His covenant to show mercy, He also "remembers" sin when executing judgment. However, for those under His covenant, He promises to "remember their sins no more" (Jeremiah 31:34)—meaning He will never again bring those sins to mind as a basis for condemnation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:2, the prophet stands in awe of God’s impending judgment. He has heard the report of God's majesty and the terrifying reality of the coming Chaldean invasion. In this state of trembling fear, Habakkuk cries out: "In wrath remember mercy."

  • The Tension of Wrath and Mercy: The word for wrath (rogez) indicates agitation, trembling, and raging anger. Habakkuk acknowledges that God's wrath against Judah's sin is entirely justified. Yet, he petitions God to let zakar (remembering) bridge the gap.

  • The Prophet's Appeal: Habakkuk is asking God to allow His mercy (rahamim—deep, womb-like compassion) to be the active, defining force that limits and shapes the execution of His wrath. He does not ask God to cancel the judgment, but to remember His covenant relationship with Israel during the dark days of captivity, ensuring that judgment does not lead to utter annihilation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 8:1: "Then God remembered Noah, and every living thing, and all the animals that were with him in the ark. And God made a wind to pass over the earth, and the waters subsided."

  • Exodus 2:24: "So God heard their groaning, and God remembered His covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob."

  • Psalm 25:6: "Remember, O Lord, Your tender mercies and Your lovingkindnesses, For they are from of old."

  • Psalm 136:23: "Who remembered us in our lowly state, For His mercy endures forever;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A powerful contrast exists between human memory and divine memory. Human memory is fragile, subjective, and prone to decay. Humans "remember" grievances and "forget" benefits. God, however, remembers His mercy eternally and chooses to forget the confessed sins of His people. In the economy of grace, when we ask God to "remember" us, we are asking Him to look at us through the lens of His covenant sacrifice, transforming our present suffering into an occasion for His redemptive intervention.
