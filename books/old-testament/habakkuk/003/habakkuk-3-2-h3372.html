AFRAID - ( yare , Strong's H3372 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yare expresses a range of emotions and attitudes starting from raw, physical dread and extending to a holy, reverent awe before a majestic God. In Habakkuk 3:2, it marks the transition of the prophet from a state of questioning and perplexity to one of quiet surrender and worshipful submission. Confronted with the hearing of God's impending judgment and historical acts of redemption, Habakkuk does not despair; rather, his fear of God's sovereign power inspires a deep prayer for revival and mercy. This fear is not a paralyzing terror that drives the believer away from God, but a shaking reverence that humbles the soul and draws it closer to the only Source of salvation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root yare is deeply embedded in the ancient Near Eastern context of power dynamics, where a subject responds to a sovereign ruler. It carries the physical sensation of trembling, shaking, or a rapid heartbeat in the presence of someone or something vastly superior in power. In the nomadic and agrarian life of ancient Israel, this word was used to describe the instinctual reaction to encountering a roaring lion, a violent storm, or an armed enemy. When applied to God, this physiological response of trembling is elevated. It becomes the appropriate posture of a finite creature standing before the infinite, holy Creator. The background of yare is not psychological pathology but theological reality—it is the alignment of human perspective with the sheer weight of God’s glory.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yare is a primary root meaning to fear, to revere, to stand in awe, or to be afraid. In its Qal stem, it denotes the emotional state of fear (such as terror or dread) as well as the ethical and religious attitude of reverence and piety. In the Niphal stem, it acts as an adjective meaning "fearful," "awesome," or "terrible," describing God's marvelous deeds. In the Piel stem, it means to terrify or make afraid. In Habakkuk 3:2, the verb is in the Qal perfect form (yareti), indicating a completed and settled state of reverent fear that has taken hold of the prophet upon hearing the divine report.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word yare is used over 300 times in the Old Testament, showcasing a rich semantic development:

  • Physical Terror: Jacob was "greatly afraid" (Genesis 32:7) when preparing to meet his brother Esau.

  • Reverence for Authority: Leviticus 19:3 commands every person to "revere" (yare) his mother and father, showing that the term carries an element of respect and obedience within established relationships.

  • Covenant Faithfulness: To "fear the LORD" is synonymous with serving Him, walking in His ways, and keeping His commandments (Deuteronomy 10:12).

  • The "Awesome" God: God is described as a "great and awesome (nora, from yare) God" (Daniel 9:4), indicating that His holiness and power demand a respectful distance and profound worship.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yare acts as the foundation of true biblical wisdom and worship. It is the beginning of knowledge and guards the soul against presumption. When a believer fears God, they no longer need to fear worldly circumstances, rulers, or disasters. The fear of God swallows up all other human fears. In the prophetic literature, this holy fear is the necessary prelude to receiving God's comfort and salvation. It humbles human pride so that divine grace can be received. It establishes the Creator-creature distinction, ensuring that humanity approaches the holy God with clean hands, a pure heart, and a trembling spirit.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:2, the prophet is reacting to the divine disclosure of the Chaldean invasion and God's sovereign plan of judgment. Initially, Habakkuk was filled with confusion and complaint (Habakkuk 1:2-4, 12-17). However, after standing on his watchtower and hearing God's response, he realizes the terrifying scale of God’s holy judgment. The statement "I have heard Your speech and was afraid" is not a confession of faithless cowardice. It is the shaking of a holy man who has glimpsed the reality of divine wrath against sin. This fear immediately shifts into intercession: "revive Your work... in wrath remember mercy." His fear is the catalyst for his prayer. It is the realization that Israel deserves judgment, but can only hope in God's covenant mercy.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 15:1: "After these things the word of the LORD came to Abram in a vision, saying, 'Do not be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward.'"

  • Exodus 14:31: "Thus Israel saw the great work which the LORD had done in Egypt; so the people feared the LORD, and believed the LORD and His servant Moses."

  • Psalm 23:4: "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; For You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me."

  • Jonah 1:9: "So he said to them, 'I am a Hebrew; and I fear the LORD, the God of heaven, who made the sea and the dry land.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound connection between the Old Testament yare and the New Testament Greek equivalent phobeo. In Philippians 2:12, Paul exhorts believers to "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling." This matches the theological movement in Habakkuk. The prophet's fear leads to a deeper surrender, showing that biblical fear is not the opposite of love, but its companion in recognizing the absolute holiness of the one who loves us.
