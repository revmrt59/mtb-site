WRATH - ( rogez , Strong's H7267 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun rogez refers to agitation, excitement, turmoil, trembling, or raging. While translated as "wrath" when applied to God in Habakkuk 3:2, its root meaning describes a state of violent shaking, disturbance, or emotional turmoil. In the context of Habakkuk's prayer, it represents the terrifying storm of divine judgment and holy agitation against sin, prompting the prophet to plead that amidst this overwhelming display of righteous anger, Yahweh would remember His covenant mercy.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The root verb ragaz means to tremble, shake, quake, or be agitated.

  • The noun rogez carries the vivid imagery of a violent shaking or churning. It is used in the Old Testament to describe the raging of a storm, the trembling of the earth, or the internal quaking of a person seized with terror or anger.

  • It is not a cold, detached judicial ruling, but an active, turbulent, and earth-shaking commotion. It depicts anger as an agitating storm that disrupts and displaces everything in its path.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The noun rogez means agitation, trouble, turmoil, rage, or wrath.

  • In physical contexts, it denotes a state of excitement, shaking, or raging, such as the raging of a horse or the rumbling of thunder.

  • In emotional and relational contexts, it refers to internal turmoil, trouble, disquiet, or human anger.

  • When applied to God, it refers to His holy indignation and the turbulent, disruptive execution of His judgment upon wickedness.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • In Job 3:17, the word describes the "trouble" or "turmoil" of life on earth, from which the weary are at rest in the grave.

  • In Job 37:2, it describes the "rumbling" or "thunder" of God’s voice, depicting a storm.

  • In Job 39:24, it describes a warhorse swallowing the ground with "fierceness" and rage (agitation).

  • In Isaiah 14:3, it describes the "hard bondage" or "turmoil" Israel suffered in Babylon.

  • In Habakkuk 3:2, the word is used of God's active, shaking wrath. This is the only place in the Old Testament where rogez is translated as "wrath" of God in the NKJV, highlighting the specific nuance of divine judgment manifesting as an earth-shaking, terrifying storm of holiness.

4. Theological Implications

  • Divine wrath (rogez) is not an unguided, irrational burst of temper. It is the active, holy agitation of a righteous God reacting against sin. God's holiness cannot remain passive in the presence of evil; it must shake, disrupt, and consume it.

  • The tension between wrath (rogez) and mercy (rechem): Habakkuk's prayer reveals that God's covenant love and mercy are not incompatible with His wrath. Indeed, mercy is most desperately needed and most clearly seen against the backdrop of the shaking storm of divine judgment.

  • Prophetic intercession: The prophet does not ask God to cancel His righteous judgment, acknowledging that Israel's sin deserves the shaking of rogez. Instead, he asks that within that very storm of agitation, God would remember His covenantal compassion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 3:2, Habakkuk has just heard the report of God's coming judgment through the ruthless Babylonians (Chapters 1-2). He is filled with fear. He prays, "O LORD, revive Your work in the midst of the years... In wrath remember mercy."

  • The use of rogez here emphasizes the sheer terror and cosmic shaking of the impending Babylonian invasion, which is God's instrument of judgment. The land, the temple, and the people will be violently agitated and disrupted. Habakkuk realizes that human strength cannot survive this divine storm, so he appeals to God's character: in the midst of this violent shaking and wrath, remember mercy.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 3:17 - "There the wicked cease from troubling, And there the weary are at rest."

  • Job 3:26 - "I am not at ease, nor am I quiet; I have no rest, but trouble comes."

  • Job 14:1 - "Man who is born of woman Is of few days and full of trouble."

  • Job 37:2 - "Hear attentively the thunder of His voice, And the rumbling that comes from His mouth."

  • Isaiah 14:3 - "It shall come to pass in the day the LORD gives you rest from your sorrow, and from your fear and the hard bondage in which you were made to serve."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Septuagint (LXX) translates rogez in Habakkuk 3:2 using the Greek word orge (wrath, anger). Orge is the standard New Testament word for the settled, righteous anger of God against sin (e.g., Romans 1:18). This links Habakkuk's Old Testament cry directly to the New Testament theology of God's wrath being satisfied at the cross, where in the ultimate "wrath" (orge), God remembered and poured out His greatest "mercy."
