MERCY - ( racham , Strong's H7355 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word racham refers to a deep, visceral, and tender compassion, closely linked to the maternal womb. It describes an intense emotional movement of love and pity toward those who are vulnerable, helpless, or in distress. In Habakkuk 3:2, as the prophet contemplates the terrifying reality of divine judgment through the Chaldean invasion, he pleads with God to remember this tender, womb-like compassion even in the midst of His righteous wrath.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of the verb racham is intimately connected to the noun rechem, which means "womb." This etymological link provides a vivid somatic word picture. It represents the instinctual, protective, and unconditional love that a mother feels for the child of her womb. In ancient Near Eastern thought, the internal organs—specifically the womb and the bowels—were viewed as the seat of deep emotions. To experience racham is to feel a physical, yearning ache of compassion for someone who is helpless or suffering. It depicts a deep, involuntary pulling of the inward parts on behalf of another.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb racham means to show compassion, have mercy, or love deeply. It denotes an intense feeling of pity or affection that moves a person to act on behalf of another to relieve their suffering. While the closely related word chesed represents God's loyal, covenantal love based on commitment and faithfulness, racham emphasizes the emotional warmth, spontaneous tenderness, and heartfelt sympathy of a superior toward an inferior who has no claim to favor. It is a mercy triggered not by the merit of the recipient, but by the deep-seated goodness of the giver.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Maternal Compassion: The word pictures the absolute devotion of a mother. It illustrates a love that cannot easily be broken, showing that even if a human mother could forget the child of her womb, God’s racham toward His people remains unshakeable.

  • Paternal Pity: It is used to describe the protective care of a father who looks upon his children with tenderness, understanding their weaknesses and vulnerabilities, and treating them with gentle forbearance.

  • Sovereignty of Mercy: The term is used to declare the absolute freedom of God to bestow His tender mercy upon whom He chooses, independent of human efforts or works.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Character of God: Racham reveals that God’s justice and holiness are balanced by His deep capacity for tender emotion. He is not a distant, impassive deity, but one whose heart is deeply moved by the plight of His people.

  • Covenant Restoration: In the history of redemption, racham is the driving force behind God's restoration of His rebellious people. Even when Israel's sin demands judgment, God's womb-like compassion is stirred, prompting Him to limit His anger and promise future renewal.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:2, the prophet is overwhelmed by the vision of God’s impending judgment on Judah through the cruel Chaldeans. He cries out, "In wrath remember mercy."

  • The word for "wrath" (rogez) refers to agitation, trembling, or the violent shaking of war. Habakkuk acknowledges that God’s anger is justified and that the shaking of the nation must come.

  • However, Habakkuk asks God to "remember" (zakar—to act on covenant promises) His racham. The prophet is not asking God to bypass justice, but to let His maternal-like compassion frame the judgment, preserving a remnant and ensuring that His wrath does not end in total annihilation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 103:13 - "As a father pities his children, So the LORD pities those who fear Him."

  • Isaiah 55:7 - "Let the wicked forsake his way, And the unrighteous man his thoughts; Let him return to the LORD, And He will have mercy on him; And to our God, For He will abundantly pardon."

  • Jeremiah 31:20 - "Is Ephraim My dear son? Is he a pleasant child? For though I spoke against him, I still remember him eagerly; Therefore My heart yearns for him; I will surely have mercy on him, says the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), racham is frequently translated using oikteiro (to have pity) or eleeo (to show mercy). In the New Testament, this concept is beautifully carried over in the Greek verb splanchnizomai, which also refers to being moved in one's inner parts (the bowels) with compassion. This is the very word used to describe Jesus' emotional response when He saw the helpless crowds, sick individuals, and grieving families, demonstrating that Jesus is the ultimate embodiment of the Father's racham.
