SPEECH - ( shemuah , Strong's H8088 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shemuah refers to a message, tidings, or a report that is heard. Rooted in the verb shama (to hear), it describes the objective content of a proclamation or revelation. In Habakkuk 3:2, it represents the prophet's reception of God's sovereign decree regarding judgment and salvation. Upon hearing this divine "speech" or "report," Habakkuk is filled with holy reverence, prompting his famous prayer for mercy in the midst of wrath.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • shemuah is built upon the foundational Hebrew verbal root shama, which means to hear, listen with attention, or obey. In the ancient Near Eastern world, where oral communication was the primary means of transmitting news, history, and official decrees, a shemuah was the physical message that arrived via a messenger. It represents the sound waves that hit the ear and the intellectual content received by the mind. It is not merely passive noise; it is an active announcement that demands a response from the hearer, whether it brings joy or strikes fear into the heart.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • shemuah is a feminine noun that denotes a report, news, rumor, tidings, or a divine revelation. It points to the specific substance of what has been heard. It ranges semantically from common, everyday news (such as the report of a military defeat or a rumor of war) to the exalted, prophetic reception of Yahweh's own word. Rather than referring to the act of speaking, it emphasizes the perspective of the listener receiving the message.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses shemuah in both secular and sacred contexts:

  • Secular News: It describes the report of the death of Eli's sons (1 Samuel 4:19) or the rumors of coming military invasions (Jeremiah 51:46). In these instances, the word carries the weight of shocking or distressing news.

  • Prophetic Proclamation: In the prophets, the word is elevated to describe the divine message received from God. It is the official "report" of Yahweh's decrees concerning the nations or His people. In this context, the prophet acts as the hearer who must faithfully transmit this divine report to others.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shemuah highlights that biblical faith is fundamentally auditory. Yahweh is not a silent deity but a speaking God who makes His character, plans, and judgments known. Humans do not discover God through speculative philosophy; they receive His "speech" or "report." This establishes a relationship based on hearing and responding. Furthermore, because shemuah is rooted in shama (which includes obedience), hearing God's report necessitates a transformation of heart and action. To hear His speech is to be confronted with His absolute sovereignty.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:2, the prophet declares, "O LORD, I have heard Your speech and was afraid." Throughout chapters 1 and 2, Habakkuk struggled with the moral dilemma of God using the wicked Chaldeans to punish Judah. God answered by revealing His sovereign plan to judge both Judah and the Chaldeans, asserting His holiness and ultimate justice. In chapter 3, Habakkuk reacts to this divine disclosure. The "speech" (shemuah) is this terrifying yet comforting revelation of God's holy anger and eventual deliverance. The nuance here is one of awe-inspiring majesty; the report of God's cosmic power and impending judgment leaves the prophet trembling, shifting his stance from questioning God to praying for His mercy.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Isaiah 53:1: "Who has believed our report? And to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed?"

  • Jeremiah 49:14: "I have heard a report from the LORD, And an ambassador has been sent to the nations: 'Gather together, come against her, And rise up to battle!'"

  • Obadiah 1:1: "The vision of Obadiah. Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom (We have heard a report from the LORD, And an ambassador has been sent among the nations, saying, 'Arise, and let us rise up against her for battle'):"

  • 1 Samuel 2:24: "No, my sons! For it is no good report that I hear. You make the LORD's people transgress."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), shemuah is frequently translated using the Greek noun akoe. This direct connection bridges the Old and New Testaments. In Romans 10:16, the Apostle Paul quotes Isaiah 53:1 using akoe ("Who has believed our report?"), and immediately concludes in verse 17 that "faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God." Thus, the Old Testament shemuah is the conceptual foundation for the New Testament proclamation of the Gospel—it is the divine report that must be heard and believed for salvation.
