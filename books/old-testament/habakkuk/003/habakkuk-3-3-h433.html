GOD - ( eloah , Strong's H0433 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word eloah is a singular noun for God, used predominantly in poetic and majestic biblical texts to emphasize the absolute, singular, and transcendent nature of the Creator. Unlike its plural counterpart elohim, eloah draws attention to the personal, majestic, and singular reality of God as the sole object of adoration, trust, and holy fear. In Habakkuk 3:3, this name highlights God's awesome, sovereign arrival from the south to deliver His people, reassuring a trembling prophet that the same God who acted in history is marching once again to save.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word eloah has an ancient, lofty, and deeply poetic heritage in the Hebrew Scriptures. It is closely related to the more common plural noun elohim, but as a singular noun, it possesses a distinct focus. It is used heavily in poetry—appearing over forty times in the Book of Job alone—where it highlights the vast chasm between finite human beings and their infinite Creator. The background of the term evokes the picture of a singular, towering, and absolute Ruler who stands entirely alone in His power, completely distinct from the created order. It is the name used when the biblical writers wish to emphasize God's absolute uniqueness, His supreme authority, and His role as the ultimate refuge and object of human worship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term eloah means God, deity, or the one true God. It is derived from a root that points to the concepts of fear, awe, and strength, or alternatively, to the idea of being the supreme object of dread and adoration. In its singular form, it refers specifically to the true God of Israel, though in rare, non-capitalized poetic contexts, it can refer to a foreign deity. When applied to Yahweh, it denotes the supreme, self-existent Being, the sovereign Lord of the cosmos, and the singular, personal focus of all true worship.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Out of its 57 occurrences in the Old Testament, the word is used to paint several distinct spiritual pictures:

  • The Majestic Creator: In the poetical debates of Job, eloah is the majestic Creator who controls the rain, commands the stars, and cannot be questioned by human wisdom.

  • The Living Rock: In Deuteronomy and the Psalms, eloah is depicted as a rock—an immovable, reliable foundation of salvation for those who trust in Him.

  • The True Object of Worship: The term contrasts sharply with worthless idols. He is the living God who acts, speaks, and saves, while false gods are lifeless fabrications.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, eloah is a powerful testament to strict monotheism. By using the singular noun, the scriptures highlight that there is only one supreme God; He has no rivals, no equals, and no partners in His sovereignty. Furthermore, the word balances God's transcendence with His immanence. He is the infinitely high God who dwells in unapproachable light, yet He is also the personal God who hears the prayers of His people, establishes covenants, and enters human history to execute justice and bring salvation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:3, the prophet is engaged in a passionate, liturgical prayer. Facing the terrifying prospect of a Babylonian invasion, Habakkuk looks backward to Israel's history to find hope for the future. He declares, "God (eloah) came from Teman, The Holy One from Mount Paran."

By choosing the singular eloah rather than elohim, Habakkuk emphasizes the personal, majestic singularity of the God who marched out of the southern deserts during the Exodus and the Sinai covenant. This is not a localized, weak tribal deity, but the sovereign Eloah whose glory covers the heavens and whose praise fills the earth. The contextual nuance is one of absolute assurance: the very same God who acted with singular, catastrophic power to redeem Israel from Egypt is still on the throne and is marching once again to deliver His covenant people.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Linguistic Connections: The Hebrew eloah is linguistically cognate to the Aramaic elah (used extensively in the books of Ezra and Daniel) and the Arabic ilah (which forms the basis of the word Allah, the term used for God by Arabic-speaking Jews and Christians).

  • Contrast with Elohim: While elohim can occasionally be used of human judges or pagan pantheons, eloah is almost exclusively reserved in poetic literature for the one true God, making it an exceptionally intimate and focused title in the prayer language of the Old Testament.
