FEVER - ( resheph , Strong's H7565 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word resheph refers to a flame, spark, or burning heat, often associated with fever, pestilence, and divine judgment. In the context of biblical imagery, it depicts the intense, consuming heat of God’s holiness and wrath as He marches forth in judgment. In Habakkuk 3:5, it is personified alongside pestilence as an attendant of Yahweh, emphasizing the devastating, inescapable power of His sovereign presence.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb rashaph is not found in the Hebrew Bible, but cognate languages indicate it carries the meaning of catching fire, glowing, or burning. The noun resheph paints a vivid word picture of flying sparks, darting flames, or burning arrows. In the Ancient Near East, Resheph was also the name of a Canaanite deity of plague, war, and the underworld, who shot arrows of pestilence. However, the biblical writers demythologize this concept. Rather than representing an independent, hostile pagan deity, resheph in the Old Testament is entirely subject to Yahweh. It is a mere tool or servant in His hand, representing the physical realities of fever and heat that accompany His holy judgment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Resheph carries a range of meanings related to intense heat and rapid movement:

  • A flame, spark, or burning coal.

  • A burning pestilence, plague, or consuming fever.

  • Flashing arrows or firebolts shot from a bow.

  • An intense, consuming passion or flash of love.

  • In the medical and judgment-oriented contexts of the Old Testament, the word describes a wasting fever that inflames the body and leads to death.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The semantic range of resheph displays a fascinating transition from literal sparks to metaphorical fire:

  • Literal Sparks: Job 5:7 describes "sparks" (bene-resheph, literally "sons of flame") flying upward, capturing the rapid, dancing nature of fire.

  • Flaming Arrows: Psalm 76:3 uses the phrase rishphe-qashet ("flashing arrows of the bow") to describe God breaking the weapons of war. Here, the word pictures arrows flying like streaks of fire.

  • Intense Passion: Song of Solomon 8:6 describes the jealousy and power of love, stating that its "flames" (reshpeha) are flames of fire, representing a blazing, unquenchable heat.

  • Consuming Plague: Deuteronomy 32:24 lists resheph as a consequence of covenant unfaithfulness, where the rebellious are devoured by "pestilence" or "burning fever."

4. Theological Implications

The use of resheph reveals critical theological truths about the nature of God and His judgment:

  • The Sovereignty of God over Disease: Epidemics, fevers, and pestilences are not wild, uncontrolled forces of nature, nor are they the work of autonomous evil spirits. They are entirely under the sovereign command of Yahweh. He unleashes and restrains them according to His holy purposes.

  • The Consuming Fire of God’s Holiness: Hebrews 12:29 declares that "our God is a consuming fire." The burning fever of resheph symbolizes how the raw purity of God's presence naturally consumes and destroys sin and rebellion.

  • Demythologization of Pagan Fears: By utilizing a term associated with a Canaanite god of plague, the biblical authors show that what the nations feared as an angry god is actually just an obedient servant walking "at the feet" of the one true God of Israel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:5, the prophet is describing a glorious, terrifying theophany—a visible manifestation of God coming from Teman and Mount Paran to deliver His people and execute judgment on His enemies. The verse states: "Before Him went pestilence, And fever followed at His feet."

The word resheph (translated "fever" in the NKJV) is poetically paired with deber ("pestilence"). Together, they act as royal bodyguards or vanguard soldiers marching with King Yahweh. Pestilence clears the way in front of Him, and burning fever follows in His footsteps. The nuance here is that God’s presence is so intensely holy and powerful that the natural world, along with sinful humanity, is scorched in His wake. The passage reassures Habakkuk that the same terrifying power that brought Egypt to its knees during the Exodus is currently active on behalf of His covenant people.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 32:24: "They shall be wasted with hunger, Devoured by pestilence and bitter destruction; I will also send against them the teeth of beasts, With the poison of dust-crawlers." (Here, resheph is translated as "pestilence.")

  • Job 5:7: "Yet man is born to trouble, As the sparks fly upward." (Here, resheph is translated as "sparks.")

  • Psalm 76:3: "There He broke the flashing arrows, The shield and the sword and the battle." (Here, resheph is translated as "flashing.")

  • Song of Solomon 8:6: "Set me as a seal upon your heart, As a seal upon your arm; For love is as strong as death, Jealousy as cruel as the grave; Its flames are flames of fire, A most vehement flame." (Here, resheph is translated as "flames.")

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Israel, a fever was not merely understood as an elevated body temperature, but as an internal fire consuming a person from the inside out. When applied to God’s judgment, it highlights how His wrath is not only external (like an invading army) but internal and inescapable, affecting the very life-force of those who oppose Him. Additionally, while the Greek Septuagint (LXX) occasionally translates this word with terms relating to birds or wings (due to the idea of flying sparks), the parallel with pestilence in Habakkuk 3:5 firmly establishes the context of burning plague and divine, consuming heat.
