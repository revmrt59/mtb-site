SCATTERED - ( patsats , Strong's H6327 )

Summary:

In Habakkuk 3:6, the word "scattered" (patsats) describes the violent shattering and dispersion of the earth's most permanent geographical features—the everlasting mountains—at the presence of Yahweh. Rather than a gentle dispersion, this term denotes a forceful disintegration, illustrating that the elements of creation most trusted by humanity for their stability are fragile and transient when confronted by the sovereign glory and judgment of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew verb patsats carries the physical imagery of a violent impact. The root picture is that of a heavy hammer striking a solid object, such as a rock or a stone wall, causing it to shatter instantly into flying fragments. In the ancient Near East, mountains were viewed as the pillars of the earth, representing the ultimate symbols of permanence, age, and cosmic stability. By depicting these ancient, unyielding mountains as being shattered or scattered, the writer paints a dramatic picture of cosmic dissolution. At the mere look of Yahweh, the very foundation stones of the physical universe break apart and disperse like dust.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The lexical definition of patsats is to break, shatter, smash, or disperse through breaking. In the passive, reflexive, or intensive verbal stems (specifically the Hitpolel form used in Habakkuk 3:6), the word means to be shattered to pieces, to be violently dispersed, or to fly apart. It signifies the complete destruction of physical integrity. Instead of a group of entities being gently distributed, patsats describes a single, solid mass being violently pulverized and scattered in every direction.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Hammer and the Rock: The word describes the action of a hammer shattering a rock into fragments, demonstrating irresistible power over the hardest substances.

  • The Shattered Soul: It is used to describe a person being gripped by the neck and shaken or broken to pieces, illustrating complete personal ruin and physical or emotional disintegration.

  • The Disintegration of Solid Structures: In every context, patsats refers to the breaking down of things that seem firm, cohesive, and unbreakable, turning them into fragmented, scattered debris.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of patsats in the prophetic text centers on the supremacy of the Creator over His creation. Earthly structures, no matter how ancient or seemingly permanent, possess no inherent stability of their own. The "everlasting mountains" are revealed to be temporary and fragile. This convicts the human heart of the folly of placing trust in the created order, pagan mountain-deities, or human empires that appear as stable as mountains. True eternity and unshakeable permanence belong to Yahweh alone, whose ways are truly everlasting.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3, the prophet is recalling God’s historical redemptive acts to find hope amidst the terrifying prospect of the Babylonian invasion. In verse 6, God’s arrival convulsed the earth: "He stood and measured the earth; He looked and startled the nations." In response to His presence, "the everlasting mountains were scattered." The contextual nuance of patsats here highlights the absurdity of fearing the Babylonian empire. If the literal, primeval mountains—the oldest and strongest structures on earth—shatter and fly apart before God, then the pagan nations and their military might will easily be broken. The passage sets up a deliberate contrast: the mountains are "everlasting" (ad) in human estimation but are shattered (patsats), whereas God’s ways are truly "everlasting" (olam).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 16:12 - "I was at ease, but He has shattered me; He also took me by my neck and shook me to pieces; He has set me up for His target."

  • Jeremiah 23:29 - "Is not My word like a fire?" says the LORD, "And like a hammer that breaks the rock in pieces?"

  • Habakkuk 3:6 - "He stood and measured the earth; He looked and startled the nations. And the everlasting mountains were scattered, The perpetual hills bowed. His ways are everlasting."

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is helpful to distinguish patsats from other Hebrew verbs translated as "scatter." While zarah refers to the winnowing of grain or the gentle dispersal of seeds, and puts refers to the scattering of a flock or a population, patsats is intrinsically violent. It requires an impact or a shattering force. The use of this specific term in Habakkuk 3:6 underscores that nature does not simply step aside for God; it is forcibly broken by His majesty. This serves as a pastoral comfort: the chaotic, shattering events of history are entirely under the sovereign hand of the God who commands the cosmos.
