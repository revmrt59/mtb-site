TENTS - ( 'ohel , Strong's H0168 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ohel refers to a tent, a portable dwelling structure typical of nomadic life in the ancient Near East. Beyond its literal meaning of a temporary shelter made of animal skins or woven goat hair, it represents home, family, security, and a people's way of life. In prophetic and poetic literature, "tents" often stands as a synecdoche for the inhabitants themselves or their entire civilization. In Habakkuk 3:7, the shaking and affliction of the "tents of Cushan" and the "curtains of Midian" depict the overwhelming terror and disruption felt by Israel's desert-dwelling neighbors as Yahweh marches forth in glorious, redemptive judgment. It contrasts the fragile, temporary shelters of earthly kingdoms with the unshakeable majesty of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The 'ohel is the quintessential nomadic dwelling. Typically constructed from dark goat hair woven into long strips, these tents were designed to be quickly pitched, taken down, and transported as pastoralists moved their flocks in search of water and pasture. The structure relied on wooden poles and tent pegs secured by cords. Inside, the tent was often divided into sections for privacy and daily activities. In the ancient Near East, a tent was not a recreational item but a home. It represented a family's primary sanctuary, their legacy, and their defense against the harsh desert environment. To strike a tent or see it collapse was synonymous with the disruption or destruction of the entire household.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'ohel denotes a tent, nomadic habitation, or temporary dwelling place. It refers to:

  • A literal tent used by patriarchs (such as Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob) and nomadic tribes (like Midian and Cushan).

  • The Tabernacle of the Lord, frequently designated as the "tent of meeting" ('ohel mo'ed), where God met with His people.

  • A figurative expression for a household, family line, or a nation (e.g., "the tents of Judah").

  • The physical human body or temporary earthly existence in poetic contexts.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Patriarchal Pilgrimage: The patriarchs are consistently described as dwelling in tents. This visualizes their status as strangers and sojourners on the earth, looking forward to a permanent city built by God.

  • The Tabernacle ('ohel mo'ed): God chose to dwell in a tent among His nomadic people during the wilderness wanderings. This emphasized His accessibility, His willingness to journey with His people, and His temporary accommodation of His glory before the construction of the permanent Temple.

  • Fragility and Impermanence: Poetic texts use the collapsing or pulling up of tent cords to depict death, national ruin, or the fleeting nature of human strength.

4. Theological Implications

  • God’s Sovereignty Over Earthly Powers: When God manifests His presence, even the most established nomadic nations—who pride themselves on their mobility, adaptability, and survival skills in the harsh desert—are utterly undone. Their tents offer no protection against the Creator.

  • The Temporal vs. The Eternal: Tents represent the temporal and fragile nature of human security. In contrast, God is described as an eternal dwelling place.

  • Judgment and Salvation: The shaking of Cushan and Midian's tents in Habakkuk 3 recalls the Exodus and Sinai events, demonstrating that God's cosmic march to save His covenant people strikes terror into those who oppose Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:7, the prophet is engaged in a poetic prayer, recalling Yahweh's historic, saving acts on behalf of Israel. Cushan and Midian represent the nomadic tribes living in the desert regions bordering the Sinai Peninsula and Transjordan. These peoples lived almost exclusively in tents. The parallelism of the verse pairs "tents of Cushan" with "curtains of the land of Midian." To see these tents "in affliction" (meaning trouble, sorrow, or distress) and their curtains "trembling" (meaning to shake or quiver) indicates that the arrival of God in judgment throws these desert nations into absolute panic. The very structures that represent their security, home, and survival are shaking at the presence of the Lord. Their nomadic agility cannot save them from divine scrutiny.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 12:8 - "And he moved from there to the mountain east of Bethel, and he pitched his tent, having Bethel on the west and Ai on the east; there he built an altar to the Lord and called on the name of the Lord."

  • Numbers 24:5 - "How lovely are your tents, O Jacob! Your dwellings, O Israel!"

  • Isaiah 54:2 - "Enlarge the place of your tent, And let them stretch out the curtains of your dwellings; Do not spare; Lengthen your cords, And strengthen your stakes."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Imagery of Curtains: The "curtains" mentioned in parallel with tents in Habakkuk 3:7 refer to the woven goat-hair fabrics that formed the tent's canopy and walls. When the curtains "trembled," it painted a vivid picture of a desert windstorm, but here caused by the breath of God's wrath.

  • Historical Echoes: Cushan may refer to Cushan-Rishathaim, an early oppressor of Israel, or a southern nomadic tribe. Midian was a persistent adversary during the time of Moses and Gideon. God shaking their tents reminds Israel of past victories where God easily dismantled the power of their oppressors.
