DISPLEASED - ( charah , Strong's H2734 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb charah literally means to burn, glow, or grow hot, and is most frequently used to describe the kindling of anger. In Habakkuk 3:8, the prophet uses this vivid term to ask whether God’s cosmic display of power was fueled by a burning irritation or displeasure against the physical creation itself, ultimately revealing that God's fiery zeal is not a temperamental outburst against nature, but a targeted, redeeming anger directed at the enemies of His people to secure salvation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of charah paints a vivid, sensory picture of heat and fire. It refers to the kindling of a flame, the glowing of embers, or the heat that radiates from a sudden blaze. In the ancient Near East, where fires were essential for survival but also highly destructive when unchecked, this word captured the sudden, consuming power of intense emotion. In Hebrew thought, deep anger is not viewed as a cold, calculating state of mind, but as a physical burning within a person. When someone becomes angry, their nose or face is said to "burn hot." Thus, charah represents a dynamic, active ignition of displeasure that refuses to remain dormant.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb charah means to burn, wax hot, or be kindled with anger. In its active Qal stem, it denotes the process of growing hot or becoming angry. When paired with the preposition le (literally, "to burn to [someone]"), it functions idiomatically to mean "it displeased" or "made angry." The semantic range includes:

  • To burn with anger or heat.

  • To be deeply vexed, grieved, or highly displeased.

  • To burn with zeal or passionate intensity.

In Habakkuk 3:8, the grammatical structure asks if the Lord’s anger "burned" against the natural elements, using this term to convey a burning, active displeasure.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, charah appears over 90 times, serving as the standard term for the kindling of both human and divine anger.

  • The Ignition of Anger: The word is frequently paired with aph (nose or face), indicating a visible, physical expression of wrath, where the "nose burns" with heat.

  • The Contrast of Human and Divine Burning: When used of humans, charah often denotes an irrational, sinful, or frustrated rage, such as Cain's jealousy or Jonah's petulance. When used of God, it represents His holy, consuming opposition to covenant faithlessness and oppression. It is not a volatile loss of control, but a righteous, burning zeal for His name and His people.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of charah lies in what it reveals about God's character. He is not a distant, unfeeling deity who looks upon the world with passive indifference. He is passionately involved. His holiness is a consuming fire, and when His covenant promises are threatened or His righteousness is compromised, His displeasure burns hot. This divine "burning" is the active execution of His justice. Because God is holy, He cannot tolerate evil; His charah is the inevitable, righteous reaction of perfect holiness encountering sin and rebellion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:8, the prophet is caught in a majestic, terrifying vision of Yahweh marching from the south to deliver His people. Habakkuk witnesses God's power shaking the earth, causing the mountains to tremble and the waters to overflow.

The prophet asks: "Was the LORD displeased with the rivers? Was Your anger against the rivers, Was Your wrath against the sea...?"

By using charah, Habakkuk asks if God was throwing a cosmic tantrum against inanimate nature. Did the Red Sea and the Jordan River experience the "burning heat" of God’s personal irritation?

The implied answer is a resounding "No." God’s burning displeasure was not directed at the water, but at the wicked oppressors of His people. The shaking of the natural world was simply the byproduct of His glorious march. The "horses" and "chariots of salvation" reveal that His burning zeal is fundamentally redemptive. He shakes the earth not to destroy creation, but to deliver His covenant people.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 4:5 - "but He did not respect Cain and his offering. And Cain was very angry, and his countenance fell."

  • Exodus 32:11 - "Then Moses pleaded with the LORD his God, and said: 'LORD, why does Your wrath burn hot against Your people, whom You have brought out of the land of Egypt with great power and with a mighty hand?'"

  • Numbers 11:1 - "Now when the people complained, it displeased the LORD; for the LORD heard it, and His anger was aroused. So the fire of the LORD burned among them, and consumed some in the outskirts of the camp."

  • Jonah 4:9 - "Then God said to Jonah, 'Is it right for you to be angry for the plant?' And he said, 'It is right for me to be angry, even to death!'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

For the preacher and teacher, this word provides a powerful corrective to modern misconceptions about God's wrath. Pagan nations viewed natural disasters as the arbitrary, chaotic rage of temperamental gods who needed to be appeased. Habakkuk shows that Yahweh's power over nature is structured, purposeful, and covenantally directed. When the structures of this world shake, it is not because God has lost His temper with the world He made. His burning displeasure (charah) is reserved for sin, injustice, and evil. For the believer, the "heat" of God's power is never a threat, but the ultimate guarantee of their salvation and deliverance.
