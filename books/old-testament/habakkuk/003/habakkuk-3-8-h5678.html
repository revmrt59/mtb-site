WRATH - ( ebrah , Strong's H5678 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun ebrah refers to an overflowing, swelling, or boundless anger. Derived from a root meaning "to cross over" or "to overflow," the word vividly depicts wrath that has broken through normal boundaries, much like a river flooding its banks. In Habakkuk 3:8, it highlights the unstoppable, overwhelming force of God's holy indignation. This divine fury is not a temper tantrum but a majestic, targeted display of sovereign power over creation, aimed ultimately at defeating His enemies and delivering His covenant people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The word ebrah is derived from the root verb abar, which means "to pass over," "to cross over," or "to overflow."

  • This root is often used to describe crossing a river or passing through a land.

  • Applied to emotion, the noun ebrah paints the picture of a flood. It is an anger that cannot be contained, swelling until it bursts past its normal boundaries and sweeps everything away in its path.

  • In the ancient Near East, where flash floods could instantly transform dry ravines into roaring torrents of destruction, this word picture carried immense weight and terror.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Lexically, ebrah denotes an outburst of anger, wrath, rage, or fury.

  • It represents the extreme manifestation of anger, emphasizing its overflowing, excessive, or uncontainable nature.

  • When applied to humanity, it often carries a negative connotation of unrestrained, sinful rage or arrogance.

  • When applied to Yahweh, it refers to His intense, righteous, and judicial indignation against sin, covenant faithfulness violations, and cosmic rebellion. It is a holy wrath that is fully expressed and visible in history.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Old Testament frequently pairs ebrah with other words for anger, such as ap (nostrils/anger) or hemah (heat/fury), to construct a comprehensive picture of divine judgment.

  • In prophetic literature, it is a key term used to describe the "day of the Lord"—a day characterized by the overflowing flood of divine justice against a wicked world.

  • The physical manifestation of this wrath is often described using natural disturbances—earthquakes, dark clouds, and raging waters—showing that creation itself reacts to the overflowing fury of its Creator.

4. Theological Implications

  • Divine ebrah is never an irrational, capricious, or malicious loss of temper. God's anger is always principled, holy, and perfectly aligned with His justice.

  • The concept of "overflowing" wrath demonstrates that God's patience, while immense, has a boundary. When His judgment is finally unleashed, it is complete, thorough, and irresistible.

  • This term reveals that God is actively involved in history. He is not a distant, indifferent deity; He is deeply grieved and angered by sin, oppression, and rebellion, and He will eventually act to sweep them away.

  • Crucially, in redemptive history, the terrifying manifestation of God's wrath against His enemies is the exact mechanism by which He rescues His people. God's wrath and His salvation are two sides of the same sovereign intervention.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Habakkuk 3:8, the prophet is engaged in a poetic prayer, recalling God’s past mighty acts of deliverance (such as the Exodus and the conquering of the Promised Land) to find hope for the dark days of the impending Babylonian invasion.

  • The verse poses three rhetorical questions: "Were You displeased with the rivers, O LORD? Was Your anger against the rivers, Was Your wrath (ebrah) against the sea, That You rode on Your horses, Your chariots of salvation?"

  • The obvious answer to these questions is "No." God was not angry at the inanimate waters of the Red Sea or the Jordan River.

  • Instead, God unleashed His overflowing, boundary-breaking power (ebrah) upon nature to dismantle every obstacle standing in the way of His covenant people.

  • The ultimate target of His wrath was the oppressive powers of Egypt and Canaan. The overflowing of His power over the physical boundaries of the sea served as a visible sign of His sovereign capability to save His people.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 11:4: "Riches do not profit in the day of wrath, But righteousness delivers from death."

  • Isaiah 13:9: "Behold, the day of the LORD comes, Cruel, with both wrath and fierce anger, To lay the land desolate; And He will destroy its sinners from it."

  • Zephaniah 1:15: "That day is a day of wrath, A day of trouble and distress, A day of devastation and desolation, A day of darkness and gloominess, A day of clouds and thick darkness,"

  • Ezekiel 7:19: "They will throw their silver into the streets, And their gold will be like refuse; Their silver and their gold will not be able to deliver them In the day of the wrath of the LORD; They will not satisfy their souls, Nor fill their stomachs, Because it became their stumbling block of iniquity."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • There is a striking structural contrast in Habakkuk 3. In verse 2, Habakkuk prays, "In wrath remember mercy." The word for "wrath" there is rugez, meaning turmoil, trouble, or shaking. But by verse 8, when God appears in His glory, the word shifts to ebrah—the overflowing flood of wrath.

  • This progression shows that while the earth experiences the shaking (rugez) of God's judgment, the actual flood of His ultimate wrath (ebrah) is reserved for His enemies, resulting in "salvation" for those who trust Him.

  • Ultimately, the New Testament teaches that believers are delivered from this overflowing wrath. The cup of God's unmitigated ebrah was poured out upon Jesus Christ on the cross. He absorbed the flood of divine judgment so that those who are in Him might only experience the overflowing river of His grace.
