ANGER - ( 'aph , Strong's H0639 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'aph primarily refers to the nose or nostrils, but is used frequently in the Old Testament as a vivid anthropomorphic metaphor for anger, capturing the physical flare of the nostrils and rapid breathing associated with intense emotion. In Habakkuk 3:8, the prophet uses 'aph to describe Yahweh's holy, active indignation. Rather than pointing to a chaotic, uncontrolled rage, this divine anger represents God’s targeted, righteous execution of judgment over creation and the enemies of His covenant people to bring about salvation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical root of 'aph is the nose or nostril, derived from the verb 'anaph, which means to breathe heavily or snort. In the ancient Hebrew mindset, abstract concepts like anger were expressed through physical, tangible realities. When a person becomes angry, their breathing quickens, their body heat rises, and their nostrils flare. Thus, to the Hebrew writer, anger was not just a mental state; it was a physical burning in the nose. This physiological word picture carries over into descriptions of God. When God is described as angry, scripture poetically portrays Him as breathing out fire or smoke from His nostrils, symbolizing the intense, consuming heat of His holy displeasure.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'aph carries a few distinct meanings within its semantic range, determined strictly by context:

  • The physical nose or nostrils: The organ of breathing or smelling (e.g., God breathing the breath of life into Adam’s nostrils).

  • The face or countenance: Often used in the dual form ('appayim) to describe bowing down one's face to the ground in honor or worship.

  • Anger, wrath, or displeasure: The most common figurative usage, describing both human irritation and divine, judicial indignation. When applied to God, it refers to His intense opposition to sin, rebellion, and injustice.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The physical association with the nose leads to several profound idiomatic expressions in the Old Testament:

  • "Slow to anger" ('arek 'appayim): Literally translated as "long of nostrils." This beautiful picture implies that it takes a long time for the heat of God’s nose to build up. He has a "long nose," meaning He is patient, long-suffering, and restrains His wrath.

  • "Fierce anger" (charon 'aph): Literally the "burning of the nose." This refers to wrath that has reached its peak execution, ready to consume that which is unholy.

  • "Quick-tempered" (qetsar 'appayim): Literally "short of nostrils." This refers to someone who has a "short nose," meaning they lose their temper rapidly and easily.

4. Theological Implications

Divine 'aph is a vital attribute of God's holiness. Unlike human anger, which is often tainted by selfishness, pride, and lack of control, God’s anger is always a controlled, principled, and judicial response to evil. It is His holy love reacting against that which destroys and corrupts. Without divine 'aph, God would be indifferent to wickedness, oppression, and sin. His wrath is the necessary friction between perfect holiness and human rebellion. Yet, because God is "long of nose" (slow to anger), His wrath is always preceded by warnings, calls to repentance, and opportunities for mercy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:8, the prophet is engaged in a poetic, terrifying prayer (a shiggaion) recounting God’s historical acts of deliverance, likely recalling the Exodus, the crossing of the Red Sea, and the Jordan River. Habakkuk asks: "O LORD, were You displeased with the rivers? Was Your anger ['aph] against the rivers, Was Your wrath against the sea...?"

The rhetorical answer is a resounding no. Yahweh’s 'aph was not an arbitrary, destructive fury directed at the inanimate elements of creation. God was not throwing a cosmic tantrum against the waters. Rather, His majestic power shook the rivers and divided the sea to execute judgment upon Egypt and deliver His covenant people. Nature was merely the stage upon which God rode His "chariots of salvation." This teaches the pastor and student that God’s anger is never chaotic or wasteful; it is always laser-focused on overcoming evil and establishing His redemptive purposes.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalm 30:5 - "For His anger is but for a moment, His favor is for life; Weeping may endure for a night, But joy comes in the morning."

  • Proverbs 15:1 - "A soft answer turns away wrath, But a harsh word stirs up anger."

  • Deuteronomy 29:24 - "Even all nations would say, 'Why has the LORD done so to this land? What does the heat of this great anger mean?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

For the expositor, it is crucial to contrast human anger with divine 'aph. James 1:20 reminds us that "the wrath of man does not produce the righteousness of God." Human anger is typically "short-nosed," driven by injured pride. God's 'aph is "long-nosed," driven by covenant love and justice. Ultimately, the burning 'aph of God against sin was fully satisfied (propitiated) at the cross. Jesus Christ took the "heat of the nose" of divine judgment upon Himself so that those who trust in Him might only experience God's eternal favor.
