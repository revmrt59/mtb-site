DIVIDED - ( baqa , Strong's H1234 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb baqa fundamentally means to cleave, break open, rip open, or divide. In Habakkuk 3:9, it depicts God's sovereign, majestic power over creation, where His voice or action splits the very earth to release rivers. This term emphasizes the sheer force of divine intervention, reshaping the physical landscape to accomplish His redemptive purposes and care for His people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The ancient Hebrew language is highly concrete and functional. The root baqa evokes the physical action of splitting wood, breaking open a city wall, tearing a garment, or hatching an egg. It is a forceful, decisive action that ruptures an existing barrier. In cosmological and geographical contexts, it describes the dramatic parting of the Red Sea or the gushing of water from a split rock. It is not a gentle parting but a dramatic, powerful cleavage of matter.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb baqa means to cleave, split, breach, break through, or divide. It denotes a violent or highly energetic action of separating something into parts. In its various verbal stems, it ranges from breaking open a town wall during a siege to hatching eggs, ripping open pregnant women in warfare, or splitting rock to yield water. In the active causative or intensive stems, it highlights God as the supreme agent who breaks open barriers, whether they are physical rocks, bodies of water, or the crust of the earth itself.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Splitting the Sea: The most famous theological usage occurs in the Exodus narrative, where God splits the waters of the Red Sea to create a dry path for Israel.

  • Water from the Rock: God cleaves the rock in the wilderness to provide life-giving water for His thirsty people.

  • Breaching Walls: Military contexts use the term to describe breaching enemy walls to allow armies to flood into a city.

  • Creation and Flood: The term is used to describe the bursting forth of the fountains of the great deep during the Noahic flood, indicating a massive disruption of the earth's crust.

4. Theological Implications

  • Sovereignty Over Nature: Nature is not an autonomous system; it responds instantly to the command and presence of its Creator.

  • Judgment and Deliverance: The splitting of the earth or sea serves a dual purpose. It brings terror and judgment to God's enemies while simultaneously creating paths of deliverance and provision (rivers in the desert) for His covenant people.

  • Divine Re-creation: Like the splitting of the deep in Genesis, God's splitting of the earth in Habakkuk shows Him ordering and re-ordering the cosmos.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:9, the prophet experiences a majestic, terrifying vision of God marching forth for the salvation of His people. The phrase "You divided the earth with rivers" paints a vivid picture of the earth splitting open under the weight of God's presence, causing rivers to burst forth. This refers both to the historical memories of God's wilderness provisions (splitting the rock) and a cosmic description of the earth trembling and cracking open at His arrival. The rivers are not gentle streams but torrents unleashed by God’s raw, sovereign power to reshape the terrain as He marches in victory.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 7:11: "In the six hundredth year of Noah’s life, in the second month, the seventeenth day of the month, on that day all the fountains of the great deep were broken up, and the windows of heaven were opened."

  • Exodus 14:21: "Then Moses stretched out his hand over the sea; and the LORD caused the sea to go back by a strong east wind all that night, and made the sea into dry land, and the waters were divided."

  • Psalm 78:15: "He split the rocks in the wilderness, and gave them drink in abundance like the depths."

  • Isaiah 35:6: "Then the lame shall leap like a deer, and the tongue of the dumb sing. For waters shall burst forth in the wilderness, and streams in the desert."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint translates baqa in Habakkuk 3:9 using rhegnumi, meaning to break, burst, or rend. This Greek equivalent reinforces the sudden, explosive force associated with the Hebrew word. When the New Testament speaks of the veil of the temple being torn, or the rocks splitting at the crucifixion of Jesus Christ (Matthew 27:51), it echoes this profound Old Testament imagery of God violently breaking through physical structures to reveal His presence and inaugurate a new era of redemption.
