RIVERS - ( nahar , Strong's H5104 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun nahar refers to a natural, continuously flowing stream of water, distinct from seasonal wadis that dry up in the summer months. In biblical poetry and prophecy, it represents life, blessing, divine judgment, and the overwhelming power of God's presence. In Habakkuk 3:9, "rivers" are the dramatic results of God's sovereign reshaping of the physical earth. As the Lord marches to deliver His covenant people, His presence fractures the terrain, releasing torrents of water and demonstrating His absolute authority over creation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The word nahar comes from a root meaning to flow or to shine.

  • In the ancient Near East, rivers were the lifeblood of civilizations. They provided irrigation, drinking water, and trade routes through otherwise arid landscapes.

  • Unlike seasonal streams (nahal) that were notorious for running dry when water was most desperately needed, a nahar was dependable and perennial.

  • Because rivers could flood violently, carving deep canyons and sweeping away obstacles, they also served as a vivid picture of unstoppable force and majestic power. In biblical literature, God’s ability to control, direct, or divide these massive bodies of water is a primary proof of His omnipotence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • A nahar is a stream, river, or canal. It designates major historical rivers such as the Euphrates (often referred to simply as "the River"), the Nile, or the Tigris.

  • The term signifies a constant, flowing body of water.

  • In the plural form (neharot), it can refer to the currents of the sea, the ocean depths, or multiple streams carving through a landscape.

  • Figuratively, nahar represents prosperity, peace, and the abundance of divine blessings, as well as overwhelming distress or national invasion when depicted as a sweeping flood.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Perennial Flow: A nahar represents consistency and permanent supply. It is used to picture the unending flow of God’s peace (Isaiah 66:12) and the continuous nature of true righteousness (Amos 5:24).

  • The River of God: In Psalm 46:4, a nahar glad-dens the city of God. This symbolizes the constant, refreshing stream of divine presence, grace, and protection that keeps God's people secure while the kingdoms of the world are shaken.

  • Cosmic Control: God founded the world upon the seas and established it upon the waters and rivers (Psalm 24:2). His sovereign control over these powerful elements demonstrates His absolute kingship.

4. Theological Implications

  • Absolute Dominion over Nature: The dividing, drying up, or releasing of rivers shows that physical creation yields instantly to its Creator's voice. Nature is not an autonomous force; it is God's instrument.

  • Judgment and Redemption: Rivers serve a dual purpose in God's redemptive plan. They can be split to make a way of escape for His people (as at the Jordan), or they can overflow to sweep away His enemies in judgment.

  • Covenant Faithfulness: God's power to split the earth and bring forth rivers in dry places highlights His promise to sustain His church through barren wilderness seasons.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • Habakkuk 3 is a prophetic prayer written in the form of a majestic theophany, recalling God's historical, saving acts during the Exodus, Sinai, and the conquest of Canaan.

  • In Habakkuk 3:9, the prophet states: "You divided the earth with rivers." The carving of rivers represents the earth fracturing under the sheer weight of God’s arrival.

  • The immediate literary context is highly active and military. God prepares His bow, readies His arrows, and marches in indignation. As He moves, the solid ground splits open, releasing torrents of water.

  • This imagery recalls the miraculous wilderness provisions where God split rocks to give water to His thirsty people. It reassures the prophet that even when the political and social landscape is in chaos, God can violently reshape the physical and spiritual world to deliver and sustain His remnant.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:10: "Now a river went out of Eden to water the garden, and from there it parted and became four riverheads."

  • Psalm 46:4: "There is a river whose streams shall make glad the city of God, The holy place of the tabernacle of the Most High."

  • Isaiah 48:18: "Oh, that you had heeded My commandments! Then your peace would have been like a river, And your righteousness like the waves of the sea."

  • Jonah 2:3: "For You cast me into the deep, Into the heart of the seas, And the floods surrounded me; All Your billows and Your waves passed over me."

  • Joshua 24:2: "And Joshua said to all the people, 'Thus says the Lord God of Israel: "Your fathers, including Terah, the father of Abraham and the father of Nahor, dwelt on the other side of the River in old times; and they served other gods."'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The plural form neharot used in Habakkuk 3:9 highlights the global and multi-directional impact of God's majesty. The earth is fractured systematically, showing that His power is not localized.

  • Ancient Near Eastern mythologies often personified the sea and rivers (Yam and Nahar) as chaotic, rebellious deities that fought against the pagan gods. Habakkuk 3:8-9 demythologizes these elements. The rivers are not rival deities; they are mere servants that obey Yahweh's commands.
