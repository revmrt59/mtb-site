SWORN - ( omer , Strong's H0562 )

Summary:

In Habakkuk 3:9, the English term "sworn" in the NKJV phrase "Sworn oaths of the tribes" translates a highly condensed Hebrew phrase, shebu'ot mattot omer. The Hebrew word omer (Strong's H0562) fundamentally means "word," "speech," "promise," or "utterance." While the preceding Hebrew word shebu'ot refers directly to "oaths" or "sworn statements," omer provides the divine guarantee—it is the spoken, authoritative word of Yahweh that establishes these oaths. Thus, "sworn" in this context points to the absolute reliability of God's covenant promises. When God speaks, His word carries the weight of a solemn oath; it is an irrevocable decree of deliverance for His people and judgment upon their enemies.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word omer comes from the primitive root amar, which means "to say," "speak," or "command." In the ancient Near East, a king's spoken word was not merely a statement of intent; it was an active force that carried the full weight of royal authority. Once a king uttered an omer, it became law—unchangeable, binding, and self-fulfilling. In the biblical context, when the Creator of the universe speaks, His omer acts as an unstoppable decree. In Habakkuk 3:9, this word is set against the backdrop of God as a cosmic warrior. Yahweh rides His chariots of salvation, readies His bow, and executes His sworn word. This imagery draws on God's historical covenant faithfulness; His spoken promises to the patriarchs and the tribes of Israel are as sure as a sworn oath, backed by His very character and holy name.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun omer denotes a word, an utterance, a speech, or a promise. It refers to that which is spoken aloud. The semantic range of omer spans from general human speech to the highly specialized, authoritative decrees of God. While related terms like dabar can mean both "word" and "thing" or "event," omer focuses specifically on the vocalized utterance—the actual speech act. In the poetic and prophetic literature of the Old Testament, omer frequently signifies a divine promise or command that stands forever. In Habakkuk 3:9, it qualifies the oaths (shebu'ot) made to the tribes, serving as the verbal, spoken foundation of God's covenant loyalty.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Old Testament poetry, omer is used to paint vivid pictures of communication, decree, and cosmic order:

  • In creation and nature, omer describes the silent yet powerful testimony of the cosmos. The heavens "utter speech" (omer) day after day, proclaiming the glory of God without a vocalized human sound, yet their message reaches the ends of the earth (Psalm 19:2-3).

  • In divine revelation, omer is the powerful command of God that mobilizes His hosts. When the Lord gives the "word" (omer), a great company of heralds publishes the good news of victory (Psalm 68:11).

  • In personal devotion, omer represents the sweet, life-giving promises of God that the believer treasures. It is a source of comfort, direction, and spiritual sustenance.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, omer underscores the absolute truthfulness and immutability of God's word. Human words can be empty, deceptive, or subject to failure. God’s omer, however, is inherently powerful, creative, and active. When God speaks a promise, it is identical to an oath because God cannot lie; He is the ultimate standard of truth. The integration of omer with shebu'ot (oaths) in Habakkuk 3:9 shows that God's covenant promises to His people are doubly secured. His spoken word is His sworn bond. This highlights the doctrine of God's sovereignty and faithfulness: He remembers His word forever, and He will shake the heavens and the earth to fulfill what His mouth has spoken.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3, the prophet is praying in the midst of national distress, remembering God’s mighty deeds of old. In verse 9, the prophet describes God preparing for battle: "Your bow was made quite ready; Sworn oaths of the tribes." The Hebrew text shebu'ot mattot omer is exceptionally terse and has challenged translators for centuries. A literal rendering points to "the oaths of the rods [or tribes] of the word." Here, omer (word or promise) is the ultimate source of the "sworn oaths." The nuance is that God’s judgment of the nations and deliverance of His people is not a random act of violence, but the precise execution of His sworn covenant word. The bow is bent, and the arrows are ready because God's spoken promise to the tribes must be fulfilled. His word is the blueprint of His historical intervention.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 22:28 - "You will also declare a thing [omer], and it will be established for you; So light will shine on your ways."

  • Psalm 19:2 - "Day unto day utters speech [omer], And night unto night reveals knowledge."

  • Psalm 19:3 - "There is no speech [omer] nor language Where their voice is not heard."

  • Psalm 68:11 - "The Lord gave the word [omer]; Great was the company of those who proclaimed it:"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase in Habakkuk 3:9 contains a wordplay that would not be lost on an ancient Hebrew listener. The word for "tribes" (mattot) can also mean "rods" or "scepters," which are instruments of chastisement or symbols of authority. Thus, the "sworn oaths of the tribes/rods of the word" suggests that God's spoken word (omer) itself acts as a rod of authority and judgment. When God speaks, His word acts as a weapon of warfare, shattering the oppressors of His people and bringing about the salvation He promised of old.
