NAKED - ( ur , Strong's H5783 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ur refers to the action of stripping away a covering, exposing, or making completely bare. In Habakkuk 3:9, it is used in a vivid poetic description of Yahweh as a warrior preparing for battle, drawing His bow out of its leather sheath. This action signifies God's active, visible, and unmistakable intervention in human history to deliver His people and execute judgment, transitioning from a state of hidden restraint to sudden, powerful revelation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, an archer's bow was highly valued and sensitive weapon. To protect the wood, sinew, and taut bowstring from moisture, dust, heat, and structural damage, the bow was kept in a form-fitting leather case or sleeve. When a warrior prepared for battle, the final act before engaging the enemy was to slide the bow out of this protective sleeve. To make the bow "naked" was to expose it, notch an arrow, and prepare to shoot. This word picture conveys imminent, active combat. When applied to God, it indicates that His period of patience has ended, and He is about to act openly in the sight of all nations.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ur carries the primary lexical meaning of "to expose," "to strip," "to make bare," or "to lay completely open." It is closely related to the root arah (to bare or uncover) and the root arar (to strip bare). In this specific active verbal form, it denotes the deliberate action of stripping away a covering to reveal what is underneath. It is a term of exposure, indicating that whatever was concealed is now brought into full, undeniable view.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The use of ur in the Hebrew Bible creates an intense, dramatic word picture of divine warfare. In Habakkuk 3:9, the prophet uses a double-emphasis construction in Hebrew: eriyah te'or qashteka, which literally translates to "in nakedness your bow is made naked." This poetic repetition emphasizes the absolute readiness and sudden visibility of God’s weapon. The bow is not merely held; it is completely stripped of its cover. The judgment of God is no longer passive, hidden, or delayed; it is drawn, aimed, and ready to fly.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Divine Warrior: This term reinforces the biblical portrait of Yahweh as the Divine Warrior who fights on behalf of His covenant people. God is not an indifferent observer of history; He actively enters the fray to execute justice.

  • The Revelation of Justice: God’s judgment is often hidden or slow from a human perspective. The unsheathing of the bow represents the moment God's silent tolerance gives way to visible, historical intervention.

  • Covenant Faithfulness: The uncovering of the bow is directly linked to the "oaths sworn over Your arrows." God bares His weapon not in capricious anger, but to fulfill His sworn, immutable promises to redeem His people and crush their oppressors.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Habakkuk 3:9, the prophet is engaged in a dramatic prayer-hymn recalling God's past acts of deliverance, particularly the Exodus and the conquest of Canaan. Habakkuk is struggling with the terrifying prospect of the impending Babylonian invasion. By describing God's bow as being "made quite naked," Habakkuk comforts himself and the faithful remnant. The Babylonian empire is not the ultimate sovereign power; Yahweh is. Though God seems silent, His bow is already uncovered, and He will march through the earth in indignation to save His anointed.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because ur (H5783) is a rare term in this specific verbal form, the ancient Greek Septuagint (LXX) translates it using the verb enteino (meaning "to bend" or "to draw" a bow). This same Greek equivalent appears in the following passages depicting God's preparation for judgment:

  • Psalm 7:12: "If he does not turn back, He will sharpen His sword; He bends His bow and makes it ready."

  • Psalm 11:2: "For look! The wicked bend their bow; They make ready their arrow on the string, That they may shoot in secret at the upright in heart."

  • Lamentations 2:4: "Standing with His right hand like an adversary; He has bent His bow like an enemy; He has slain all that were pleasing to the eye;"

  • Lamentations 3:12: "He has bent His bow And set me as a mark for the arrow."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase eriyah te'or in Habakkuk 3:9 contains a brilliant poetic wordplay (paranomasia). The repetition of the sharp consonant sounds (r and t) mimics the sudden, snapping sound of a leather case being ripped open and a bowstring being drawn taut. This poetic device would have gripped the ancient Hebrew listener, conveying the terrifying beauty and suddenness of theophany—the manifest presence of God.
