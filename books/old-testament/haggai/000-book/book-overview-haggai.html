Book Overview

At a Glance

Haggai
Author

Haggai the prophet, through whom “the word of the LORD came” (1:1). The book speaks of him in the third person and gives no family line or personal details. It does not say who put the messages and their dated settings into their final form.

Audience

Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel, governor of Judah; Joshua son of Jehozadak, the high priest; and “the remnant of the people” (1:1, 12; 2:2). The final word is spoken to Zerubbabel alone (2:20–23).

Date

The second year of King Darius (Darius I), 520 BC. The messages run from the first day of the sixth month to the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month, so the whole book covers less than four months.

Historical Setting

Judah is a province under Persian rule, led by a governor and a high priest, and the LORD’s house still lies in ruins (1:4, 9). Ezra 3–6 tells the wider story of the returned community. Foundation work had begun years earlier, opposition halted it, and the building resumed when Haggai and Zechariah prophesied. Ezra also records that the temple was eventually completed.

Purpose

Haggai confronts a people who have put their own houses ahead of the LORD’s house. He calls them to build it “that I may take pleasure in it and be glorified” (1:8). Once they begin, he strengthens the builders with the LORD’s presence and with promises of glory, blessing, and a chosen servant.

Central Message

Through his prophetic word, the LORD of hosts restores his people’s obedience and worship. He calls them to “Consider your ways!” He exposes their neglect of his house and names their failed harvests as his own doing. When the leaders and the remnant obey, he answers, “I am with you,” stirs their spirits to work, and assures discouraged builders, “My Spirit remains among you; do not fear!” He then makes promises that their own labor could never produce: glory and peace for his house, blessing for a people whose work could not make them holy, and a chosen servant from David’s line. Read alongside the rest of Scripture, the promised glory, peace, and chosen King reach their fullest expression in Christ.

Key Verse

“Yet now be strong, Zerubbabel,” says the LORD; “and be strong, Joshua, son of Jehozadak, the high priest; and be strong, all you people of the land,” says the LORD, “and work; for I am with you,” says the LORD of hosts (Haggai 2:4).

Key Themes

Considering your ways (1:5, 7; 2:15, 18); the LORD’s presence and Spirit (1:13; 2:4–5); the LORD stirring his people to obey (1:12, 14); God’s hand in harvest and blessing (1:9–11; 2:17, 19); holiness that does not pass to other things by touch, while uncleanness spreads (2:11–14); the glory of the LORD’s house (1:8; 2:3, 7, 9); the shaking of heaven, earth, and nations (2:6–7, 21–22); and the LORD’s chosen servant from David’s line (2:23).

Book Flow

  • I. Consider Your Ways: The Call to Rebuild (Haggai 1:1–11): neglect of the LORD’s house exposed, and drought sent by the LORD

  • II. The Remnant Obeys and the LORD Stirs Them (Haggai 1:12–15): “I am with you,” and the work begins

  • III. The Promise of Greater Glory (Haggai 2:1–9): courage for discouraged builders

  • IV. From Defilement to Blessing (Haggai 2:10–19): the priests’ rulings, and “from this day I will bless you”

  • V. Zerubbabel, the LORD's Chosen Signet (Haggai 2:20–23): kingdoms overthrown, and a chosen servant

Go Deeper

Background

Author

Haggai is the prophet through whom the LORD speaks in this book. Again and again the word of the LORD “came by Haggai the prophet” (1:1, 3; 2:1, 10), and once it “came to Haggai” (2:20). He is called “the LORD’s messenger,” who “spoke the LORD’s message to the people” (1:13). That description keeps attention on the One who is speaking. “Thus says the LORD of hosts” and “says the LORD” run through every message (for example 1:5, 7; 2:4, 6–9, 23). Haggai carries a word that is not his own, and so when the people obeyed “the words of Haggai the prophet, as the LORD their God had sent him,” they were obeying the voice of the LORD (1:12).

The book tells us almost nothing about Haggai himself. It gives no father’s name, no hometown, and no account of his call. It also speaks of him in the third person and places his messages within a short narrative frame: the dates, the people’s response (1:12–15), and the priests’ answers to his questions (2:12–13). The book does not say whether Haggai or someone close to his ministry gave it its final form.

Audience

Haggai’s messages were first spoken to two named leaders and the people with them. Zerubbabel son of Shealtiel was “governor of Judah,” and Joshua son of Jehozadak was “the high priest” (1:1). With them was “the remnant of the people” (1:12, 14), whom the LORD also calls “This people” (1:2) and “all you people of the land” (2:4). The governor, the high priest, and the community all hear the same word, and all three obey and are stirred together (1:12, 14). Rebuilding the LORD’s house was never left to the officials alone.

Some in this audience were old enough to remember the earlier temple. The LORD asks, “Who is left among you who saw this temple in its former glory?” and acknowledges that the new work seemed “as nothing” in their eyes (2:3). These hearers were also farmers whose harvests had failed (1:6, 10–11), so the LORD’s word reached their daily bread as well as their worship. Particular groups within the audience receive particular words. The priests are asked questions about the law (2:11–13), and the book’s final message is for Zerubbabel alone (2:20–23). Haggai never uses the words “exile” or “return,” but the wider account in Ezra presents this community as people who had come back to Judah from exile.

Date

Haggai is unusually precise about time. Every message is tied to a day in “the second year of King Darius.” The word first came on the first day of the sixth month (1:1), and the people began work on the twenty-fourth day of the sixth month (1:15). Further messages came on the twenty-first day of the seventh month (2:1) and on the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month (2:10), with a second message on that same day (2:20). These dates also give the book its structure, marking where each new message begins.

The king is Darius I of Persia, and his second year was 520 BC. By the book’s own dates, everything from the first rebuke to the promise to Zerubbabel took place in less than four months. Only twenty-three days passed between the first message and the day the leaders and people began working on the LORD’s house, which shows how quickly they responded. Because Haggai does not mention the temple’s completion, the book was likely put into its final form soon after 520 BC.

Historical Setting

Judah was a small province under Persian rule. Its leader was a “governor” (1:1), and the book dates its messages by the reign of “King Darius,” a foreign ruler, not a king from David’s line. That detail becomes important at the book’s close. Zerubbabel belonged to David’s royal family, and Matthew 1:11–13 records him as a link in that royal genealogy. Generations earlier, Jeremiah 22:24 had used the image of a signet ring when the LORD rejected a king of that line. When the LORD calls Zerubbabel his signet in Haggai 2:23, he is speaking into a world where no son of David sat on a throne.

Haggai also assumes that a house of the LORD once stood in glory and now lay “in ruins” (1:4, 9). He sets “this latter temple” beside “the former” (2:9), and that contrast runs through the book. The former temple had been destroyed when Babylon conquered Jerusalem. Haggai does not retell that history, but his hearers knew it well.

The Book of Ezra fills in the wider story (Ezra 3–6). After the people returned to Judah, they restored the altar, resumed worship, and began foundation work on the temple. Opposition from outside Judah then halted the building. The work resumed when Haggai and his contemporary Zechariah prophesied, and the temple was eventually completed. This background explains two features of Haggai. First, it explains how foundation work could already have been done while the LORD’s house still lay unfinished. Second, it explains why offerings were already being made at the site, even though the LORD declares that “what they offer there is unclean” (2:14). Ezra does not settle exactly how its earlier foundation work relates to the foundation day Haggai marks in 2:18.

Life in Judah depended on the land. Haggai speaks of dew withheld from the heavens (1:10), of drought on “the grain and the new wine and the oil” (1:11), and of the vine, the fig tree, the pomegranate, and the olive tree that had not yet borne fruit (2:19). For people living from one harvest to the next, drought affected every household. That is why the LORD’s promise, “But from this day I will bless you” (2:19), meant so much to them.

Occasion

The first message answers a specific claim. The people were saying, “The time has not come, the time that the LORD’s house should be built” (1:2), while they themselves lived in “paneled houses” (1:4). The LORD sets the two kinds of houses side by side: his house lies in ruins “while every one of you runs to his own house” (1:9). The problem Haggai confronts is what the people had put first.

Haggai never explains why the people thought it was too early to build. Ezra records outside opposition that had halted the work, but Haggai leaves the people’s reasoning unstated and speaks to their present priorities instead.

Hard times gave the message its urgency. The people had “sown much” and brought in little (1:6), and the LORD tells them plainly why: “Because of My house that is in ruins… For I called for a drought” (1:9–11). Haggai presents the drought as the LORD’s own action, and the charge comes as the LORD’s word through his prophet, calling the people to rebuild.

Each later message meets a new situation. Once the leaders and people had obeyed and begun to work (1:12–15), the builders who compared the new house with the former glory saw it as “nothing,” and the LORD answered their discouragement (2:1–9). About two months later, the people needed to learn why their work and offerings were unclean and to hear the day the LORD marked for blessing (2:10–19). On that same day, Zerubbabel received a personal promise (2:20–23). Haggai spoke to people in the middle of the work and addressed each need as it arose.

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Haggai is a short book of dated words from the LORD. It holds four messages delivered through Haggai and one brief account of how the leaders and people responded, all within a few months of King Darius’s second year. Each message meets the community at a particular point in a real rebuilding effort, so the book reads like a record of what God said, when he said it, and what happened next.

The book’s first concern is what the people put first. They were saying, “The time has not come, the time that the LORD’s house should be built” (1:2), while they themselves lived in “paneled houses” (1:4). The LORD sets those homes beside his own house lying in ruins and observes that “every one of you runs to his own house” (1:9). Haggai traces the unfinished temple to the people’s priorities rather than to a shortage of stone or timber.

Haggai also refuses to treat the people’s hardship as bad luck. The LORD says of their meager harvests, “I blew it away,” and declares, “For I called for a drought” (1:9, 11). Months later he still speaks of crop failure as his own doing: “I struck you with blight and mildew and hail,” and adds, “yet you did not turn to Me” (2:17). In this book God speaks to his people through their daily bread and uses their empty barns to call them back to himself.

The book speaks to discouragement as well. After the work began, some among the people could remember the temple “in its former glory,” and the modest structure in front of them looked “as nothing” by comparison (2:3). The LORD answers that disappointment with his presence and his promises, and he does not scold them for it.

Restoration in Haggai reaches deeper than the building. Offerings were already being made at the site, yet the LORD declares that “every work of their hands” and “what they offer there is unclean” (2:14). Restarting construction and continuing religious activity were not enough. The people needed renewed obedience and a cleansing that only God could give.

The book ends looking forward. The LORD promises to “fill this temple with glory” and to give peace in that place (2:7, 9), to bless his people “from this day” (2:19), and to make Zerubbabel “like a signet ring” because he has chosen him (2:23). These promises reach beyond the building project to what God himself intends to do among his people.

Why This Book Matters

Haggai shows what happens when God’s people actually listen to his word. The first message came on the first day of the sixth month, and before that month ended the leaders and “all the remnant of the people” had “obeyed the voice of the LORD their God” and begun working on his house (1:1, 12, 14–15). Scripture records the response as carefully as the message: “the people feared the presence of the LORD,” and the LORD answered their obedience with a promise, “I am with you, says the LORD” (1:12–13). In Haggai, God meets obedience with his nearness.

The book also shows that God’s presence, not the size or splendor of a building, gives the work its worth. To builders who saw their work “as nothing,” the LORD’s first answer was himself. He commanded them to “be strong” and to “work; for I am with you,” and he assured them, “My Spirit remains among you; do not fear!” (2:3–5). Haggai reveals a God who meets small and discouraged beginnings with his own presence.

Haggai’s questions to the priests carry a lesson about holiness for every generation. Holy meat carried in a garment did not make other food holy, but uncleanness from contact with a dead body did spread (2:12–13). Haggai applies that ruling to the people, their work, and their offerings (2:14). Good works cannot make you holy. Even rebuilding the temple could not cleanse the builders inwardly.

Haggai’s words also reach well beyond the builders who first heard them. Hebrews 12:26–28 takes up the LORD’s promise, “Once more (it is a little while) I will shake heaven and earth” (2:6), and applies it to a kingdom that cannot be shaken. The closing promise to Zerubbabel, a descendant of David serving as governor under a Persian king, keeps hope for David’s royal line alive. Its signet image reverses the picture of rejection in Jeremiah 22:24.

Book Flow

I. Consider Your Ways: The Call to Rebuild (Haggai 1:1–11)

On the first day of the sixth month, the LORD quotes the people’s own claim, “The time has not come,” and sets their “paneled houses” beside his house lying in ruins. Twice he commands, “Consider your ways!” (1:5, 7). Their sowing and earning have come to little, like wages put “into a bag with holes” (1:6). He calls them to “bring wood and build the temple, that I may take pleasure in it and be glorified” (1:8). He then explains the poor yields and the drought as his own response to their neglect of his house (1:9–11).

II. The Remnant Obeys and the LORD Stirs Them (Haggai 1:12–15)

Zerubbabel, Joshua, and all the remnant “obeyed the voice of the LORD their God,” and “the people feared the presence of the LORD” (1:12). Haggai, “the LORD’s messenger,” brings a single promise: “I am with you, says the LORD” (1:13). The LORD stirs up the spirit of the leaders and the people, and on the twenty-fourth day of the sixth month they come and work on his house. This short account of the people’s response is the hinge of the book. From here on, the LORD’s words encourage, instruct, and promise rather than rebuke.

III. The Promise of Greater Glory (Haggai 2:1–9)

On the twenty-first day of the seventh month, the LORD speaks to builders who see the new house “as nothing” beside the former one. He tells Zerubbabel, Joshua, and the people of the land to “be strong” three times, then adds, “and work; for I am with you” (2:4). He grounds their courage in “the word that I covenanted with you when you came out of Egypt” and in the assurance that “My Spirit remains among you” (2:5). He promises to shake heaven, earth, and the nations and to “fill this temple with glory” (2:6–7). Then comes the promise at the center of the movement: “The glory of this latter temple shall be greater than the former,” and “in this place I will give peace” (2:9).

IV. From Defilement to Blessing (Haggai 2:10–19)

On the twenty-fourth day of the ninth month, Haggai puts two questions to the priests. Their answers show that holy meat carried in a garment does not make other food holy when the garment’s edge touches it, while uncleanness from contact with a dead body does spread (2:12–13). Haggai applies the contrast directly: “so is every work of their hands; and what they offer there is unclean” (2:14). The LORD then recalls the shrunken harvests and the blight he sent, calls the people to “consider” the day he is marking, and promises, “But from this day I will bless you” (2:19).

V. Zerubbabel, the LORD's Chosen Signet (Haggai 2:20–23)

On that same twenty-fourth day, a second word comes for Zerubbabel alone. The LORD announces that he will shake heaven and earth, “overthrow the throne of kingdoms,” and bring down chariots and their riders (2:21–22). “In that day” he will take “Zerubbabel My servant” and make him “like a signet ring; for I have chosen you” (2:23). The book closes on God’s sovereign choice and a renewed hope for David’s royal line.

Key Themes to Watch For

Consider your ways. The LORD calls his people to look honestly at their priorities, at their harvests, and at the turning point he marks (1:5, 7; 2:15, 18).

The LORD’s presence. “I am with you” and “My Spirit remains among you” sustain both obedience and courage (1:13; 2:4–5).

Stirring and obedience. The LORD stirs up the spirit of leaders and people, and they come and work on his house (1:12, 14).

God’s hand in harvest and blessing. The LORD withholds the harvest while his house lies neglected, then promises blessing “from this day” (1:6, 9–11; 2:16–17, 19).

The glory of the LORD’s house. God wants to “take pleasure in it and be glorified,” and he promises a glory and peace greater than the former house knew (1:8; 2:3, 7, 9).

Holiness and defilement. Holiness does not pass to other things by touch, but uncleanness does, and that truth reaches the people’s work and offerings (2:11–14).

Shaking. The LORD shakes heaven, earth, and nations, once with the glory of his house in view and once with the overthrow of kingdoms in view (2:6–7, 21–22).

The chosen servant. The book’s closing word names Zerubbabel the LORD’s servant, his signet, and his chosen one (2:23).

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

The LORD’s house. In Haggai, “the LORD’s house” and “this temple” mean the temple in Jerusalem. The “former” temple was the one Solomon built, which the Babylonians destroyed long before Haggai’s day. Haggai assumes that history without retelling it. “This latter temple” (2:9) is the house the people are now rebuilding. Knowing this background lets the reader feel the comparison in 2:3, where some among the people still remember the house “in its former glory.”

Joshua, also called Jeshua. Haggai calls the high priest Joshua son of Jehozadak, and the book of Ezra spells the same names Jeshua and Jozadak. Readers who move between the two books are meeting the same man. He stood beside Zerubbabel the governor, so the civil leader and the priestly leader answered the LORD’s word together (1:12, 14).

“The remnant of the people.” Haggai uses this phrase, along with “this people” and “all you people of the land,” for the community in Judah that heard his messages (1:2, 12, 14; 2:2, 4). A remnant is what remains of a larger whole. Ezra’s account shows that this community had come back from exile in Babylon, although Haggai himself never uses the word exile.

“LORD of hosts.” This title for God runs throughout Haggai (for example, 1:2, 5, 7; 2:4, 6–9, 23). “Hosts” refers to vast companies or armies, and the title presents the LORD as sovereign over every power. That is why his words carry such confidence: the God who speaks can shake heaven and earth and “overthrow the throne of kingdoms” (2:22).

Dated messages. Haggai dates his messages the way Scripture often does: by the year of the reigning king, here Darius I of Persia, and by the numbered months of the Israelite year. These dates let the reader see how quickly events unfold and where the book turns.

Holy and unclean. Under the law of Moses, “holy” described what was set apart for God, like the “holy meat” in 2:12. “Unclean” described a condition that kept a person or thing from taking part in worship until it was made clean, and touching a dead body made a person unclean. Haggai’s questions in 2:11–13 rely on these rules: holiness did not pass along by touch, but uncleanness did.

Drought and the covenant warnings. The withheld dew and failed crops in Haggai echo Deuteronomy 28. In that chapter, God’s covenant with Israel sets blessing on the land beside drought and loss as the outcomes of obedience and disobedience (Deut. 28:1–12, 22–24, 38–42). This background explains why Haggai reads the harvest as the LORD’s response to his people. It also explains why the promised blessing concerns Judah’s fields, vines, and trees.

“The Desire of All Nations” (2:7). The NKJV capitalizes this phrase. The capitals reflect a traditional reading that took the phrase as a title for a person, and that reading has influenced how many Christians have heard the verse. The phrase is better understood as the treasures of the nations. The Hebrew word behind “Desire” can refer to things of value. The Hebrew verb behind “shall come” is plural, which fits many valuable things being brought in better than a single person arriving. The next verse points the same way: “The silver is Mine, and the gold is Mine” (2:8).

Signet ring. A signet was a ring bearing its owner’s seal, used to mark documents with the owner’s authority. When the LORD makes Zerubbabel “like a signet ring” (2:23), the image marks him as chosen by God and gives him royal significance.

Shaking. Haggai’s language of shaking heaven, earth, and the nations is prophetic imagery for God acting with power over creation and the nations (2:6–7, 21–22).

Christ and Life

When the builders compared their work with the temple’s former glory, some saw it “as nothing” (2:3). The LORD did not answer by promising them a grander building. He promised his own glory and peace: “I will fill this temple with glory,” and “The glory of this latter temple shall be greater than the former… And in this place I will give peace” (2:7, 9). Haggai is speaking about the house those builders were raising in Jerusalem. Read alongside the New Testament, that promise reaches its fullest expression in Jesus Christ. John tells how the Word became flesh, lived among his people, and revealed the glory of God (John 1:14). Jesus came into that Second Temple, carried there as an infant and later cleansing its courts (Luke 2:22–32; John 2:13–22). His reconciling work brings near those who were far off and makes peace with God, the fullest form of the peace promised “in this place” (Eph. 2:13–18). This connection rests on the glory and peace of 2:9. It does not depend on the treasures the nations bring in 2:7, and its measure is not how splendid the building later became.

Behind that promise stands the gift that carried the builders from the start. The LORD told them, “I am with you” (1:13; 2:4), and “My Spirit remains among you” (2:5). Haggai’s deepest encouragement is the LORD himself among his people, not the stones they were setting in place. In Christ that same presence took on flesh and came to live among us.

The New Testament makes one of Haggai’s connections explicit. In 2:6 the LORD says, “Once more (it is a little while) I will shake heaven and earth.” Hebrews 12:26–28 takes up that “once more” and applies it to God’s final shaking of created things. Those things are removed, and what cannot be shaken remains. Believers, Hebrews says, are receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken. A word first spoken to a small company of builders under a Persian king now gives believers a settled confidence: God’s kingdom in Christ will stand when everything else gives way. Haggai gives no timetable for this shaking, and the point of the promise lies in the stability of God’s kingdom rather than in a schedule of events.

The book closes with a personal word to Zerubbabel, governor of Judah. The LORD calls him “My servant” and promises to “make you like a signet ring; for I have chosen you” (2:23). In Jeremiah 22:24 the LORD had used the signet image to describe rejecting a king of David’s line. Here the same image turns toward choice and renewed hope for that line. The reversal concerns the signet image itself and does not cancel every judgment in Jeremiah 22. Zerubbabel remained a governor under Persia, and the oracle gives no account of him overthrowing kingdoms. He stands in history as a figure who points ahead and as a link in the royal line Matthew traces to Jesus (Matt. 1:11–13). The overthrow of “the throne of kingdoms” announced in 2:22 and the LORD’s chosen servant of 2:23 find their final fulfillment in Christ alone, the last King from David’s line and God’s chosen Servant (Luke 1:32–33; Matt. 12:18). Revelation 11:15 announces the day when the kingdom of the world becomes the kingdom of the Lord and of his Christ. God kept faith with David’s line through an obscure governor in a small province, and he carried that promise forward until it reached its King.

Haggai’s questions to the priests lead to Christ by another road. The priests’ answers showed that holiness does not pass to other things by contact, while uncleanness does (2:12–13). Haggai applied that ruling to the people, their work, and their offerings: “what they offer there is unclean” (2:14). Good works cannot make you holy. Even rebuilding the temple could not cleanse the builders inwardly. Yet on that same day the LORD promised, “But from this day I will bless you” (2:19). Haggai does not explain how an unclean people would be made clean. The New Testament supplies that answer in Christ, through whom believers have peace with God by faith (Rom. 5:1) and bear fruit by remaining in him like branches in a vine (John 15:4–5). The blessing Haggai announced concerned Judah’s harvests. For believers, the corresponding gifts are spiritual fruitfulness, peace with God, and the Father’s provision for those who give themselves to his kingdom (Matt. 6:25–34). In that same passage Jesus calls his followers to seek first the kingdom of God (Matt. 6:33). His call sets right the order Haggai exposed, when the people said “The time has not come” while every one of them ran to his own house (1:2, 9).

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Haggai’s words were first spoken to real builders about Jerusalem’s temple, on dated days in Darius’s second year. Each promise means something first for those builders and for Zerubbabel, and only then opens toward its fulfillment in Christ. Holding both together keeps the book from becoming an allegory detached from history or a piece of history with nothing to say about Christ.

The drought and the promised blessing concerned Judah’s farming life under the covenant God gave through Moses (Deut. 28). The LORD withheld dew and harvest from that community and then promised to bless its fields. Judah’s crop yields supply no promise of material prosperity for believers today.

The book contains two shakings with two different aims. The shaking in 2:6–7 serves the glory of the LORD’s house. The shaking in 2:21–22 overthrows kingdoms and leads to the LORD’s choice of Zerubbabel. Both describe God acting in creation and among the nations, and neither gives a timetable or a map of particular political events.

Every step of restoration in Haggai begins with God. One teaching summary puts it this way: Build Up, Stir Up, Cheer Up. Through his prophetic word the LORD calls the people to build (1:8), stirs up their spirit (1:14), and strengthens discouraged builders with his presence (2:4–5). The people’s obedience is real, but it answers God’s word and his stirring.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

The foundation in 2:18. Ezra 3:8–13 records foundation work on the temple years before Haggai spoke. Haggai 2:18 refers to “the day that the foundation of the LORD’s temple was laid” and uses it to mark a turning point, after looking back to the time “before stone was laid upon stone” (2:15). Readers who compare the two books may wonder whether they disagree. A renewed foundation stage at the time of Haggai’s message is plausible, but the exact construction event Haggai has in view is uncertain. Haggai’s point does not depend on settling it: the LORD marks that day as the start of his blessing.

Where 1:15 belongs. The date in 1:15 sits at the chapter break. Here it is read as marking the day the work began after the LORD stirred up the people (1:14). Some read the verse instead as the opening of the message dated in 2:1. Either reading leaves the book’s movement from rebuke to encouragement unchanged.

The temple’s completion. Haggai does not report the finished temple. The book ends with the work under way and the LORD’s promises still ahead. Ezra’s account of this period (Ezra 3–6) records that the rebuilding was eventually completed.
