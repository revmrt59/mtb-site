Book Questions

Is Haggai about a building, or about believers as God’s temple?

Haggai is first about a real building. In 520 BC the LORD told the returned exiles in Judah to finish rebuilding His temple in Jerusalem, which had stood unfinished since opposition halted the work years before (Ezra 4:1–5, 24; 5:1–2). The commands, the dates, and the people named are historical.

MTB reads the book as God restoring His people’s worship and obedience through His word and His presence. The command to rebuild was about Jerusalem’s temple. It is not a command about believers. But the promise of a greater glory reaches further. Believers are now God’s dwelling (1 Corinthians 3:16–17; Ephesians 2:19–22), and MTB sees them as part of the glory of the latter temple promised in 2:9. Other uses of the temple picture, such as our lives or bodies as God’s house (1 Corinthians 6:19), are applications with their own New Testament grounds, not the meaning of Haggai’s command.

Read: Haggai 1:1–8, Haggai 2:9, Ezra 4:1–5, Ezra 5:1–2, 1 Corinthians 3:16–17, Ephesians 2:19–22 · Related: How is the glory of the latter temple greater than the former?

Does Haggai promise prosperity to people who put God first? (Haggai 1:5–11; 2:15–19)

Haggai links Judah’s failed harvests to their neglect of God’s house (1:9–11), and he promises, “from this day I will bless you” (2:19). These words belong to Judah’s covenant with God, in which rain and harvest were tied to faithfulness (Deuteronomy 28:1–12, 22–24, 38–42).

They are not a promise of material wealth to believers today, and they are not a tool for raising money. The lasting principle is the one Jesus gives: “seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things shall be added to you” (Matthew 6:33). For believers, God’s blessing is fruitfulness in Christ, peace with God, and His provision for what He calls us to do (John 15:4–5; Romans 5:1; Matthew 6:25–34).

Read: Haggai 1:5–11, Haggai 2:15–19, Deuteronomy 28:1–12, Matthew 6:25–34 · Related: Did God send the drought, and does that explain our own hardships?

Haggai 1 · 1 question

Did God send the drought, and does that explain our own hardships? (vv. 9–11)

Yes, in this case God says so plainly: “I called for a drought on the land” (v. 11), “because of My house that is in ruins” (v. 9). This fits the covenant warnings God had given Israel (Deuteronomy 28:22–24, 38–42).

That explanation came through a prophet, about Judah, in that moment. It gives no rule for deciding that someone’s money trouble or suffering is God’s punishment for a particular sin. As a spiritual application, Haggai’s “Consider your ways!” (vv. 5, 7) still calls us to examine our priorities before God.

Read: Haggai 1:5–11, Deuteronomy 28:22–24, 38–42, John 9:1–3 · Related: Does Haggai promise prosperity to people who put God first?

Haggai 2 · 4 questions

Is “the Desire of All Nations” a title for the Messiah? (v. 7)

The NKJV reads, “they shall come to the Desire of All Nations,” capitalizing it as a title for the Messiah. That reading has a long history in the church. But the Hebrew phrase can mean the desirable things or treasures of the nations, and the verb is plural, which fits “treasures.” The next verse supports it: “The silver is Mine, and the gold is Mine” (v. 8). Isaiah 60:5 pictures the wealth of nations coming to Zion in the same way.

MTB understands the treasures of the nations as the most likely meaning of verse 7. MTB still finds Christ here, but through the promise of verse 9 rather than by making verse 7 a title.

Read: Haggai 2:6–9, Isaiah 60:5 · Related: How is the glory of the latter temple greater than the former?

How is the glory of the latter temple greater than the former? (v. 9)

The builders who remembered Solomon’s temple thought the new one was “as nothing” (v. 3). God answered, “The glory of this latter temple shall be greater than the former... And in this place I will give peace” (v. 9).

The second temple never matched Solomon’s in splendor. MTB understands the promise to reach its fullest meaning in Jesus. God’s glory took on flesh in Him (John 1:14). He came into that very temple as an infant and as a man (Luke 2:22–32; John 2:13–22). He made peace through His blood (Ephesians 2:13–18). Hebrews 12:26–28 also applies God’s “once more” shaking in verse 6 to the unshakable kingdom believers receive.

MTB also sees believers in this greater glory. The temple Christ builds is His people. They are “being built together for a dwelling place of God in the Spirit” (Ephesians 2:22), and as living stones they “are being built up a spiritual house” (1 Peter 2:5). God’s glory now rests in them: “Christ in you, the hope of glory” (Colossians 1:27). A temple of living people, filled with God’s Spirit and being changed “from glory to glory” (2 Corinthians 3:18), holds a glory Solomon’s house of gold and cedar never had. That fullness comes first in Christ and then in His people, who are joined to Him. Some readers expect the promise to be fulfilled in a future temple (compare Ezekiel 40–48). MTB sees its fullest meaning in Christ and in the people He builds into God’s dwelling.

Read: Haggai 2:3–9, John 1:14, Luke 2:22–32, John 2:13–22, Ephesians 2:13–22, 1 Peter 2:4–5, Colossians 1:27, 2 Corinthians 3:18, Hebrews 12:26–28, Ezekiel 40–48 · Related: Is “the Desire of All Nations” a title for the Messiah?, Was Zerubbabel the promised King?; Is Haggai about a building, or about believers as God’s temple?

Why couldn’t rebuilding the temple make the people holy? (vv. 10–14)

Haggai asks the priests two questions from the law. Holy meat carried in a garment does not make other food holy when the garment touches it. But a person made unclean by a dead body does make food unclean by touching it (vv. 12–13). Holiness does not pass to other things by outward contact, but uncleanness does.

Then Haggai applies it: “So is this people, and so is this nation before Me… and so is every work of their hands; and what they offer there is unclean” (v. 14). Working on God’s house and standing near His altar could not make the people holy, while their own uncleanness defiled what they did and offered.

Good works cannot make you holy. Holiness comes from God, and only the blood of Christ cleanses the conscience “from dead works to serve the living God” (Hebrews 9:14).

Read: Haggai 2:10–14, Hebrews 9:13–14

Was Zerubbabel the promised King? (vv. 20–23)

God tells Zerubbabel, the governor of Judah, “I will... make you like a signet ring; for I have chosen you” (v. 23). A signet ring carried a king’s authority. The promise reverses Jeremiah 22:24, where God said that even if Zerubbabel’s ancestor Coniah were His signet ring, He would pull him off. David’s royal line was not finished.

Zerubbabel himself stayed a governor. He did not overthrow kingdoms. MTB sees him as a historical picture of the King to come and a link in Jesus’ family line (Matthew 1:11–13). The shaking of heaven and earth and the overthrow of thrones find their final fulfillment in Christ alone, the Son of David who will reign forever (Luke 1:32–33; Revelation 11:15). The text gives no timetable.

Read: Haggai 2:20–23, Jeremiah 22:24, Matthew 1:11–13, Luke 1:32–33, Hebrews 12:26–28, Revelation 11:15 · Related: How is the glory of the latter temple greater than the former?
