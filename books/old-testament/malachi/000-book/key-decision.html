Book Questions

Why does Malachi begin with God’s love? (Malachi 1:2; 3:6)

Malachi opens with the LORD’s word, “I have loved you” (1:2). Only then does He ask the priests, “If then I am the Father, Where is My honor?” (1:6). MTB reads the whole book in that order. The LORD’s covenant love comes first, and the disputes that follow call a people He already loves to honor Him with wholehearted reverence.

That reverence reaches far beyond the altar. Malachi confronts careless worship, corrupt teaching, broken marriages, harsh words about God, injustice toward workers, widows, orphans, and strangers, and withheld tithes (1:6–2:16; 3:5, 8, 13–15). The LORD who loves them does not change, and that is why Jacob’s children are not consumed (3:6).

These words were first spoken to Israel after the return from exile, under Persian rule. Believers today hear them through Christ and the new covenant. Hard times in someone’s life are not a reliable sign of that person’s sin.

Read: Malachi 1:2–6, Malachi 2:10–16, Malachi 3:5–8, Malachi 3:13–15

Malachi 1 · 2 questions

What does “Jacob I have loved; But Esau I have hated” mean? (vv. 2–5)

When Israel asked, “In what way have You loved us?” the LORD pointed to two brothers: “Yet Jacob I have loved; But Esau I have hated” (vv. 2–3). In Malachi the brothers stand for the peoples who came from them, Israel and Edom. Israel’s place in the covenant rested on God’s choice, not on anything Israel had earned. Edom faced real judgment: its land lay waste, and God would throw down whatever it rebuilt (vv. 3–4).

“Hated” is strong language for God’s real opposition to Edom. Paul quotes these words in Romans 9:10–13 as part of his own argument about God’s purpose in choosing. Malachi itself speaks of nations and their covenant history, and it does not set out a full teaching on the eternal destiny of individuals.

Read: Malachi 1:2–5, Genesis 25:23, Romans 9:10–13

What is the “pure offering” offered among the nations? (v. 11)

The priests were offering blind, lame, and sick animals and calling the LORD’s table contemptible (vv. 7–8, 12–13). Against that dishonor, God declares, “My name shall be great among the Gentiles; In every place incense shall be offered to My name, And a pure offering” (v. 11).

MTB reads this as a promise that people from every nation will worship the LORD with the honor His own priests withheld. The promise finds its fulfillment as the nations worship God through Christ, offering “the sacrifice of praise” and doing good (Hebrews 13:15–16). Some Christian traditions apply the verse to the Lord’s Supper. Malachi itself does not describe what form that worship takes.

Read: Malachi 1:6–14, Hebrews 13:15–16

Malachi 2 · 3 questions

Do the warnings to the priests apply to pastors and teachers today? (vv. 1–9)

God made a covenant with Levi, “one of life and peace.” Levi revered God, “The law of truth was in his mouth,” and he “turned many away from iniquity” (vv. 5–6). The priests of Malachi’s day “caused many to stumble at the law” and showed partiality in teaching it, so God warned that He would curse their blessings and make them contemptible before the people (vv. 2, 8–9).

These warnings belong to Israel’s Levitical priesthood, and a pastor is not a Levitical priest. The principle still reaches everyone who teaches God’s word: teachers answer to God for teaching truthfully, living with integrity, and leading people toward Him rather than away from Him (James 3:1).

Read: Malachi 2:1–9, James 3:1

Was the problem with marrying “the daughter of a foreign god” a matter of race? (vv. 10–14)

No. Judah “has married the daughter of a foreign god” (v. 11). The problem was covenant allegiance. Marriages that brought the worship of other gods into Judah’s homes profaned “the LORD’s holy institution which He loves.” God was guarding His people’s undivided loyalty to Him.

Malachi exposes a second betrayal as well. Men were dealing treacherously with “the wife of your youth,” and the LORD had been “witness” to that covenant (v. 14). He refused their offerings, however much they wept at the altar (vv. 13–14). The two charges are likely connected: men were putting away their wives and marrying women who worshiped other gods. Disloyalty to God and betrayal at home both reach the altar.

Read: Malachi 2:10–14 · Related: Does God hate divorce, or divorced people?

Does God hate divorce, or divorced people? (vv. 15–16)

The NKJV reads, “For the LORD God of Israel says That He hates divorce, For it covers one’s garment with violence” (v. 16). God’s hatred is aimed at treacherous divorce, the betrayal and harm a husband inflicts when he breaks faith with “the wife of his youth” (v. 15). God sees the spouse who has been wronged and holds the betrayer responsible. Twice He says, “take heed to your spirit” (vv. 15–16): guard your heart and stay faithful.

Verse 15 is hard to translate. MTB’s preferred reading follows the NKJV: God made husband and wife one, and He seeks godly offspring from that union. Some interpreters connect the “one” with Abraham or with Israel’s unity as God’s people.

God’s anger here falls on treachery, not on the person who has been betrayed. A spouse who has been wronged may have legitimate grounds for divorce, and the wider teaching of Scripture addresses those grounds (Matthew 19:3–9; 1 Corinthians 7:10–15).

Read: Malachi 2:13–16, Matthew 19:3–9, 1 Corinthians 7:10–15 · Related: Was the problem with marrying “the daughter of a foreign god” a matter of race?

Malachi 3 · 4 questions

Who are “My messenger,” “the Lord,” and “the Messenger of the covenant”? (v. 1)

“Behold, I send My messenger, And he will prepare the way before Me. And the Lord, whom you seek, Will suddenly come to His temple, Even the Messenger of the covenant, In whom you delight” (v. 1).

The Gospels identify the preparing messenger as John the Baptist (Matthew 11:10; Mark 1:2; Luke 7:27). John prepared the way for Jesus. MTB reads the Lord who comes to His temple, the Messenger of the covenant, as Jesus Himself. His coming answers the people’s complaint, “Where is the God of justice?” (2:17). He purifies the sons of Levi so that their offerings please the LORD, and He comes as a swift witness against those who exploit and deceive (vv. 2–5).

The word “messenger” names three different figures in Malachi: the prophet, whose name can mean my messenger (1:1), the forerunner, and the Messenger of the covenant. John is the forerunner, and Jesus is the One he prepares for.

Read: Malachi 2:17–3:6, Matthew 11:10, Mark 1:2, Luke 7:27 · Related: Who is the Elijah who comes before the day of the LORD?

Should Christians tithe? (vv. 7–12)

In Malachi, returning to the LORD meant bringing the tithes and offerings Israel had withheld: “Will a man rob God? Yet you have robbed Me! … In tithes and offerings” (v. 8). The storehouse supplied food for God’s house, and God’s promise concerned Israel’s harvests, vines, and land (vv. 10–12).

MTB teaches believers to give the full tithe, one tenth of their increase, as belonging to the Lord. The tithe helps sustain the work of ministry and those who serve in it, and offerings are gifts beyond the tithe. Abraham gave a tenth before the law, and Jesus affirmed tithing (Genesis 14:20; Matthew 23:23). The New Testament grounds support for gospel ministry and willing, generous giving (1 Corinthians 9:13–14; 2 Corinthians 9:6–8).

Giving is an act of obedience and trust in God, and His blessing can take forms beyond money. Tithing is not a formula for getting rich, and a person’s hardship does not tell us whether that person has tithed.

Read: Malachi 3:7–12, Genesis 14:20, Matthew 23:23, 1 Corinthians 9:13–14, 2 Corinthians 9:6–8

Does “try Me now in this” mean we can test God, or that tithing guarantees wealth? (vv. 9–12)

God tells Israel, “You are cursed with a curse, For you have robbed Me, Even this whole nation” (v. 9). Then He invites them to bring all the tithes into the storehouse, “And try Me now in this,” to see “If I will not open for you the windows of heaven And pour out for you such blessing That there will not be room enough to receive it” (v. 10).

Scripture forbids testing God through presumption: “You shall not tempt the LORD your God” (Deuteronomy 6:16; Matthew 4:7). That kind of testing demands proof of His care or takes reckless risks to force His hand, and it is unbelief. Here the LORD Himself invites His people to test His faithfulness by obeying what He has already commanded. That is trust in His word.

The curse in verse 9 was the loss Israel brought on itself under the covenant by refusing to put God first. Returning to Him meant rain, abundant harvests, and protected crops: “I will rebuke the devourer for your sakes” (v. 11). Christ has redeemed believers from the curse of the law (Galatians 3:13), so Christians give out of willing trust, not fear of a curse. God supplies His people’s needs (2 Corinthians 9:6–8; Philippians 4:19). Giving is not a formula for wealth, and financial hardship is not proof that someone has failed to tithe.

Read: Malachi 3:8–12, Deuteronomy 6:16, Matthew 4:7, Galatians 3:13, 2 Corinthians 9:6–8, Philippians 4:19 · Related: Should Christians tithe?

Is it wrong to ask God hard questions when the wicked seem to prosper? (vv. 13–18)

The LORD says, “Your words have been harsh against Me” (v. 13). The people had said, “It is useless to serve God,” and “So now we call the proud blessed” (vv. 14–15).

Scripture welcomes honest lament from people who grieve over injustice and cry out to God in faith (Psalm 13:1–2; Habakkuk 1:2–4). Asaph struggled with the prosperity of the wicked until he went into God’s sanctuary and “understood their end” (Psalm 73:12–17). What God rebukes in Malachi is cynical accusation that treats obedience as a failed bargain and uses delayed judgment as a reason to stop serving Him.

Beside those harsh words, Malachi records a second group: “Then those who feared the LORD spoke to one another, And the LORD listened and heard them; So a book of remembrance was written before Him” (v. 16). The picture recalls a king’s book of records (Esther 6:1–2). God notices quiet faithfulness and mutual encouragement when the proud appear to win. On the coming day He will spare His own and make the difference between the righteous and the wicked plain to see (vv. 17–18).

Read: Malachi 2:17, Malachi 3:13–18, Psalm 13:1–2, Habakkuk 1:2–4, Psalm 73:12–17, Esther 6:1–2 · Related: Who is “the Sun of Righteousness”?

Malachi 4 · 4 questions

Who is “the Sun of Righteousness”? (v. 2)

The coming day will burn the proud and the wicked like stubble (v. 1). But God says, “to you who fear My name The Sun of Righteousness shall arise With healing in His wings” (v. 2). For those who fear Him, the same day rises like the dawn, bringing healing, freedom, and joy, like calves let out of the stall.

MTB’s preferred reading sees the Sun of Righteousness as a picture of Jesus bringing God’s saving righteousness. At the birth of John the Baptist, Zechariah moves from John preparing the Lord’s way to “the Dayspring from on high,” who comes “To give light to those who sit in darkness” (Luke 1:76–79). Christ’s first coming brought that light, and His return will bring full restoration, including the redemption of our bodies (Romans 8:23; Philippians 3:20–21).

As a spiritual application, the woman healed when she touched the hem of Jesus’ garment pictures the healing He brings (Matthew 9:20–22).

Read: Malachi 3:16–4:3, Luke 1:76–79, Romans 8:23, Philippians 3:20–21, Matthew 9:20–22 · Related: Who is the Elijah who comes before the day of the LORD?

Does “You shall trample the wicked” give believers permission to take revenge? (v. 3)

God says, “You shall trample the wicked, For they shall be ashes under the soles of your feet On the day that I do this” (v. 3).

No. In Malachi’s picture of the coming day, God Himself burns up the proud and the wicked like stubble (v. 1), and the trampling happens “On the day that I do this.” The image describes the complete triumph of God’s justice and the vindication of those who fear His name. It gives no one permission for private revenge or violence.

The New Testament holds the same balance. Believers must not avenge themselves; they overcome evil with good and leave judgment to God (Romans 12:19–21). They trust that “the God of peace will crush Satan under your feet shortly” (Romans 16:20).

Read: Malachi 4:1–3, Romans 12:19–21, Romans 16:20 · Related: Who is “the Sun of Righteousness”?

Why does Malachi end with “Remember the Law of Moses”? (v. 4)

“Remember the Law of Moses, My servant, Which I commanded him in Horeb for all Israel, With the statutes and judgments” (v. 4). The command stands between the promise of the Sun of Righteousness (v. 2) and the promise of Elijah (vv. 5–6). For Israel after the exile, waiting for the coming Lord did not mean setting aside what God had already spoken. They were to hold fast to His covenant word until He came.

MTB follows the historic Reformed understanding of the law. The ceremonial laws were shadows that pointed to Christ, and He has fulfilled them (Matthew 5:17; Hebrews 8:6–13). The civil laws governed Israel as a nation. God’s moral law, summed up in the Ten Commandments, still reveals His character and guides believers, who keep it out of love and not to earn salvation (Romans 13:8–10). Readiness for Christ’s coming is never separated from reverent obedience to God’s word (John 14:15).

Read: Malachi 4:4–6, Matthew 5:17, Hebrews 8:6–13, Romans 13:8–10, John 14:15 · Related: Who is the Elijah who comes before the day of the LORD?

Who is the Elijah who comes before the day of the LORD? (vv. 5–6)

God promises, “Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet Before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD. And he will turn The hearts of the fathers to the children, And the hearts of the children to their fathers” (vv. 5–6).

John the Baptist fulfilled this promise as the forerunner of Jesus’ first coming. He came “in the spirit and power of Elijah” to turn hearts back to God (Luke 1:17), and Jesus said of John, “he is Elijah who is to come” (Matthew 11:14; see 17:10–13).

Because the promise looks to “the great and dreadful day of the LORD,” MTB also expects a further Elijah-like ministry of repentance and reconciliation before Christ returns. Scripture does not name that messenger or give a timetable.

Read: Malachi 4:4–6, Luke 1:16–17, Matthew 11:14, Matthew 17:10–13 · Related: Who are “My messenger,” “the Lord,” and “the Messenger of the covenant”?, Who is “the Sun of Righteousness”?
