Obadiah

Obadiah - Book Overview

Background

Author

Obadiah the prophet. His name means “servant of the LORD.” Scripture gives us almost no personal information about him, and he cannot be confidently identified with any of the other men named Obadiah in the Old Testament.

There is something fitting about that. Obadiah means “servant of the LORD,” and that is really all we need to know about him. He faithfully delivered the word God gave him and then disappeared from the pages of Scripture. It reminds us of the heart of a true servant: “He must increase, but I must decrease” (John 3:30). The goal is not that people remember the servant, but that they hear the what God is saying.

Audience

The message concerns Edom, but it was also meant for God’s people in Judah, who had experienced the devastation of Jerusalem.

Judah had suffered not only at the hands of an invading enemy, but also from Edom, a neighboring people with deep family ties to Israel. Obadiah assures them that the Lord had seen what happened and that Edom would be held accountable.

Date

The date of Obadiah is uncertain, but the book appears to have been written after the destruction of Jerusalem, most likely shortly after the Babylonian conquest in 586 BC.

Obadiah describes Jerusalem being invaded, plundered, and its people carried away, along with Edom's response to Judah's disaster. Edom stood at a distance, rejoiced over Jerusalem's fall, took advantage of the destruction, and helped prevent survivors from escaping.

Some place Obadiah earlier, around 845 BC during the reign of Jehoram. While that remains possible, the destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC seems to fit the events described in the book especially well.

Historical Setting

The Edomites were descendants of Esau, while Israel descended from Esau’s twin brother Jacob. This family relationship is important to the entire book. Edom was not simply another enemy nation. They were Israel’s relatives.

Edom lived southeast of Judah in the mountainous region of Seir. The area was marked by high cliffs and difficult terrain, which provided significant natural protection. Edom was also located along important trade routes, bringing wealth and commercial opportunity. Their location, wealth, alliances, wisdom, and military strength all contributed to their sense of security.

The history between Israel and Edom had often been difficult. During the Exodus, Edom refused to allow Israel to pass through its territory. Later there were repeated conflicts between the two nations. Yet God specifically told Israel, “You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother” (Deuteronomy 23:7).

When Jerusalem was attacked, Edom did not treat Judah as a brother. They stood at a distance and watched. They rejoiced over Judah’s destruction, participated in the plunder, blocked escape routes, and handed survivors over to the enemy.

Occasion

Jerusalem had been devastated, and Edom had taken advantage of Judah's suffering. Rather than coming to the aid of their brother nation, the Edomites stood at a distance, rejoiced over Jerusalem's fall, joined in the plunder, and helped prevent survivors from escaping.

The Lord gave Obadiah this message in response to what Edom had done. Their actions against Judah, together with the pride behind them, would not go unanswered..

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Obadiah is the shortest book in the Old Testament, but it deals with serious matters. It is about the Lord's judgment of Edom for its pride and for what it did to Judah in the day of Jerusalem's distress.

At the heart of the book is Edom's treatment of “your brother Jacob.” When Judah suffered, Edom stood aloof. They watched, rejoiced, boasted, plundered, blocked those trying to escape, and handed survivors over to the enemy. What began with standing at a distance eventually became active participation in Judah's suffering.

Behind Edom's actions was pride. Their mountainous location made them feel secure. Their wealth, wisdom, alliances, and strength added to that confidence. But the Lord identifies the real problem: “The pride of your heart has deceived you” (v. 3).

Edom thought no one could bring them down. They were wrong.

The book then moves beyond Edom to “the day of the LORD upon all the nations” (v. 15). The principle is clear: “As you have done, it shall be done to you.” Edom's judgment becomes part of the larger truth that nations and people ultimately answer to God.

But Obadiah does not end with judgment. It ends with deliverance on Mount Zion, restoration for God's people, and one of the strongest closing statements in the Minor Prophets: “And the kingdom shall be the LORD's.”

Central Message

The Lord will bring down proud Edom for its violence and betrayal of its brother, restore His people, and establish His rule.

Edom may appear secure and Judah may appear defeated, but neither situation determines the final outcome. The Lord does.

Why This Book Matters

Obadiah exposes the deception of pride. Edom's confidence was not based on imagination. They really did have formidable mountain strongholds. They had wealth, wisdom, alliances, and strength. The deception was believing those things made them secure.

Pride can do the same thing in us. It can blind us to our true state. We can think we are fine and not even see that there is a problem. We can begin trusting our money, our position, our gifting, our wisdom, or what we have built instead of trusting the Lord.

Obadiah also confronts how we respond to the suffering of others. Edom did not begin by attacking Jerusalem. They first “stood on the other side.” Then they looked. Then they rejoiced. Then they entered. Then they took advantage. Finally, they helped prevent survivors from escaping. The progression is sobering. A heart that is comfortable standing at a distance can eventually become involved in the very thing it once only watched.

The book also exposes something that can remain hidden in the heart: rejoicing over another person's misfortune. Edom gloated when Judah fell. Scripture calls that response wrong even when the person suffering is an enemy (Proverbs 24:17–18). What happens inside us when someone else fails, suffers, or is brought down matters to the Lord.

For those who have suffered injustice, Obadiah gives hope. God's apparent silence does not mean He has not seen. Edom appeared to prosper while Judah suffered, but that was not the end of the story. The Lord saw it all.

Key Themes to Watch For

  • The deception of pride - Edom's pride did more than make them arrogant. It blinded them to their true condition and convinced them they were secure when they were not.

  • False security - Edom trusted its geography, wealth, alliances, wisdom, and strength. Watch how each of the things they trusted is stripped away.

  • Brotherhood and betrayal - The repeated reference to “your brother Jacob” gives Edom's actions additional weight. This is not simply hostility between two unrelated nations.

  • Standing aloof - Edom's sin develops from watching Judah's suffering to actively participating in it. Obadiah shows that indifference can become a doorway to something worse.

  • Gloating over another's misfortune - Edom rejoiced when Judah fell. The Lord exposes not only what they did with their hands, but what was happening in their hearts.

  • Reaping what is sown - “As you have done, it shall be done to you.” Edom's treatment of others returns upon them.

  • Deliverance, holiness, and restoration - Judgment is not the final movement of the book. Mount Zion becomes a place of deliverance, holiness, and restored possession.

  • The kingdom of the LORD - Everything moves toward the closing declaration that the kingdom belongs to the Lord.

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

Edom and Esau - Edom is the nation descended from Esau. Genesis 36 repeatedly connects the two and states plainly, “Esau is Edom.” When Obadiah moves between the names Edom and Esau, he is speaking about the same people and their ancestry.

Jacob and Esau - Jacob and Esau were twin brothers. Their struggle began before birth, continued through the birthright and blessing, and later continued in the relationship between the nations that descended from them. Israel came through Jacob; Edom came through Esau.

Mount Seir - This was Edom's mountainous homeland. Its difficult terrain contributed to Edom's sense that it could not be conquered. Understanding this helps explain the language about living “in the clefts of the rock” and asking, “Who will bring me down to the ground?”

“Your brother Jacob” - This is one of the keys to understanding Obadiah. God had instructed Israel not to despise Edom because Edom was their brother. Edom's actions during Jerusalem's suffering therefore represented a serious betrayal of that relationship.

The Day of the LORD - In verse 15, the prophecy moves beyond Edom to “all the nations.” The Day of the LORD speaks of the time when God acts openly in judgment and brings nations to account.

Mount Zion - Zion stands in deliberate contrast to the mountain of Esau. Edom's mountain represents the security in which they trusted; Zion becomes the place of deliverance and holiness under the Lord's rule.

Book Flow

Obadiah 1–4 - Edom's Pride Will Be Brought Down

Edom trusts in its mountain strongholds and asks, “Who will bring me down to the ground?” The Lord answers that even if Edom makes its nest among the stars, He will bring it down.

Obadiah 5–9 - Everything Edom Trusts Will Fail

Edom's treasures will be searched out. Its allies will turn against it. Its wisdom will fail, and its mighty men will be dismayed. Geography, wealth, relationships, wisdom, and strength will not be enough.

Obadiah 10–14 - Edom's Sin Against Its Brother

The Lord explains why judgment is coming. Edom stood aloof during Judah's calamity, gloated over its destruction, entered its gates, took its possessions, blocked those trying to escape, and handed survivors over.

Obadiah 15–16 - The Day of the LORD

The message widens beyond Edom to all nations. “As you have done, it shall be done to you.” Edom will reap what it has sown.

Obadiah 17–20 - Deliverance and Restoration

The focus shifts from Mount Seir to Mount Zion. There will be deliverance and holiness, and God's people will again possess what had been lost.

Obadiah 21 - The Kingdom Belongs to the LORD

The book reaches its final destination. Edom's kingdom does not last. Human power does not have the last word. “And the kingdom shall be the LORD's.”

Christ and Life

Obadiah is first about a real nation called Edom and God's dealings with that nation. That historical meaning should not be lost. Yet the relationship between Esau and Jacob also develops a larger biblical pattern that gives Obadiah a strong spiritual application.

Esau repeatedly represents life governed by immediate natural desire rather than spiritual hunger. He sold his birthright for a meal and is later called “profane” in Hebrews 12:16. Jacob had serious character problems of his own, but he valued the birthright and belonged to the line God had chosen by grace.

Against that larger biblical background, the closing contrast in Obadiah is striking. The mountain of Esau is judged, while on Mount Zion there is deliverance and holiness. The house of Esau does not prevail. “The kingdom shall be the LORD's.”

This gives us a picture of a truth made much clearer in the New Testament: the flesh does not win in the end. The flesh and the Spirit are opposed to one another (Galatians 5:16–17), and what is born of the flesh cannot produce the life of the Spirit.

The answer is found in Christ. At the cross, Jesus dealt decisively with sin and the rule of the flesh. Those who belong to Christ are called to “crucify the flesh with its passions and desires” (Galatians 5:24) and learn to walk by the Spirit.

Obadiah therefore leaves us with more than the destruction of an ancient enemy. It points toward the certainty that everything opposed to God's rule will eventually come down. Pride will not win. The flesh will not win. The kingdom belongs to the Lord.

Reading Guide

One-Sentence Summary

God brings down proud Edom for its betrayal of its brother, restores His people, and declares that the kingdom ultimately belongs to Him.

Read This Book Remembering...

Remember that Edom is not simply Judah's enemy. Edom is Judah's brother.

That relationship gives the middle of Obadiah much of its weight. Edom did not merely watch the suffering of another nation. They watched the suffering of their own relatives, rejoiced over it, benefited from it, and eventually participated in it.

Also watch what happens to everything Edom trusts. Their location, wealth, allies, wisdom, and strength all fail. The Lord is exposing not only Edom's cruelty but the pride and false security underneath it.

And do not stop reading at judgment. Obadiah is moving somewhere. It moves from Mount Esau to Mount Zion, from pride to holiness, from destruction to deliverance, and finally to the declaration: “The kingdom shall be the LORD's.”

Helpful Notes

  • Obadiah's date is debated. An earlier setting during the reign of Jehoram and a later setting connected with Babylon's destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC have both been proposed. The later setting is used in this overview because it fits the devastation described in verses 10–14 particularly well.

  • Edom is called by several related names in Scripture. Edom, Esau, Mount Seir, and the mountain of Esau can all refer to the people, their ancestor, or their territory depending on the passage. The later Greek/Roman form is Idumea.

  • Edom had a long history with Israel. Edom refused Israel passage during the Exodus (Numbers 20:14–21), fought against Israel at various times, and is condemned elsewhere for long-standing hostility and vengeance against Judah (Ezekiel 25:12; 35:5).

  • Psalm 137:7 provides an important parallel to Obadiah. It remembers Edom's response when Jerusalem fell: “Raze it, raze it, to its very foundation!” This helps explain the severity of Obadiah's condemnation of Edom's rejoicing over Jerusalem's destruction.

  • The Esau/flesh connection should be handled as spiritual application, not as a replacement for the historical meaning of Obadiah. Obadiah is first speaking about the historical Edomites. The broader application grows out of the biblical portrait of Esau and the New Testament contrast between flesh and Spirit.

  • Obadiah is the shortest Old Testament book. Its twenty-one verses move quickly, but they form a complete message: pride exposed, sin named, judgment announced, God's people restored, and the Lord's kingdom established.
