Book Overview

At a Glance

Obadiah
Author

The book is “the vision of Obadiah” (v. 1). Nothing else is known of him. His name is commonly understood to mean “servant of the LORD.” Several men in the Old Testament bear the name, but no identification with any of them is established.

Audience

The oracle is addressed to Edom, the nation descended from Esau, Jacob’s brother. It was preserved for Judah, and its message of judgment on Edom and restoration for Zion was meant for God’s people who had suffered at Edom’s hands.

Date

The book gives no date. The events Obadiah describes in verses 10–14, when strangers entered Jerusalem’s gates and cast lots for the city and Edom is charged with joining them, best fit Babylon’s destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC, so the prophecy likely follows that event. Some date it earlier, near 845 BC, when Edom revolted against King Jehoram of Judah and Philistines and Arabians invaded Judah (2 Kings 8:20–22; 2 Chronicles 21:8–17). The message of the book does not depend on settling the date.

Historical Setting

Edom lay south and east of the Dead Sea in a rugged, mountainous region known as Mount Seir. Its people lived among high rock strongholds that seemed beyond the reach of any enemy (v. 3). Edom was descended from Esau, and Israel from Jacob, so the two nations were brothers (Genesis 25:23–26; 36:1, 8). Their history included long hostility, from Edom’s refusal to let Israel pass through its land (Numbers 20:14–21) to repeated wars under Israel’s kings.

Purpose

Obadiah charges that in the day of Jerusalem’s calamity, Edom stood aloof and then joined the invaders (vv. 10–14). Other Scriptures speak of Edom’s hostility at Jerusalem’s fall in the same terms (Psalm 137:7; Lamentations 4:21–22; Ezekiel 35:5). The LORD answers that betrayal with this vision: Edom will be brought down, and Zion will be delivered.

Central Message

The LORD will bring down proud Edom for its violence against its brother Jacob in the day of Jerusalem’s calamity, because the Day of the LORD upon all the nations is near: “As you have done, it shall be done to you.” On Mount Zion there will be deliverance and holiness, the house of Jacob will possess their possessions, and the kingdom shall be the LORD’s.

Key Themes

  • Pride and false security. Edom trusts its heights, wealth, allies, and wisdom, and the LORD announces that all of them will fail (vv. 3–9).

  • Betrayal of a brother. Edom’s sin is sharpened by kinship; Jacob is “your brother” (vv. 10, 12).

  • The Day of the LORD. Edom’s judgment belongs to God’s wider judgment on all nations (v. 15).

  • Reciprocal justice. What Edom did returns on its own head (v. 15).

  • Deliverance and holiness. Zion’s rescue is joined to its holiness (v. 17).

  • Restored inheritance. God’s people possess their possessions (vv. 17, 19–20).

  • The LORD’s kingdom. The book ends with God’s reign, not Edom’s fall (v. 21).

Book Flow

Obadiah 1:1–9: The LORD Will Bring Edom Down

The vision opens with a report from the LORD: a messenger has been sent among the nations to rise up against Edom. Edom dwells “in the clefts of the rock,” but “the pride of your heart has deceived you” (v. 3). Though Edom sets its nest among the stars, the LORD says, “From there I will bring you down” (v. 4). Its hidden treasures will be searched out, its allies will deceive it, and its wise men and mighty men will fail.

Obadiah 1:10–14: Violence Against Your Brother Jacob

The LORD names the reason: “For violence against your brother Jacob, shame shall cover you” (v. 10). In the day strangers entered Jerusalem’s gates, Edom “stood on the other side” and was “as one of them” (v. 11). The repeated “you should not have” presses the charge from gazing and rejoicing to plundering and handing over those who escaped (vv. 12–14).

Obadiah 1:15–21: The Day of the LORD and the Kingdom

Verse 15 is the hinge of the book. “The day of the LORD upon all the nations is near,” and Edom becomes a representative case of God’s justice: “As you have done, it shall be done to you.” Then the oracle turns: “But on Mount Zion there shall be deliverance, and there shall be holiness” (v. 17). The house of Jacob will be a fire and the house of Esau stubble, the captives will possess their inheritance, and “saviors shall come to Mount Zion to judge the mountains of Esau, and the kingdom shall be the LORD’s” (v. 21).

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Obadiah is a short prophetic oracle against Edom. It opens with the LORD’s announcement that proud Edom, secure in its mountain heights, will be brought down, stripped of its treasures, betrayed by its allies, and left without wisdom or strength. It then names the reason: Edom’s violence against its brother Jacob in the day of Jerusalem’s distress. Finally, it widens to the Day of the LORD upon all the nations, in which Edom’s deeds return on its own head, while Mount Zion receives deliverance and holiness, God’s people possess their inheritance, and the kingdom becomes the LORD’s.

Why This Book Matters

Obadiah shows that God sees how people treat His people in their day of distress. Edom’s sin was not only open violence but also standing by, gloating, and taking advantage of a brother’s ruin (vv. 11–14). The book exposes the cruelty that can grow from pride and old resentment, and it shows that God holds nations and people accountable for it.

Obadiah also reveals that God’s justice is fitting and sure: “As you have done, it shall be done to you” (v. 15). For a people who had lost their city, the book’s closing promise, “the kingdom shall be the LORD’s” (v. 21), declared that the last word belongs to God.

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

  • Edom, Esau, Mount Seir, and Teman. These names are related but not interchangeable. Esau is the ancestor and Edom the nation descended from him (Genesis 36:1, 8). Obadiah often uses “Esau” and “the mountains of Esau” for the nation, which keeps the family relationship in view. Mount Seir is Edom’s highland region, though Obadiah does not name it. Teman (v. 9) is a place or district in Edom whose exact location is uncertain.

  • Jacob, Joseph, and Zion. Names for God’s people and their city. “The house of Jacob” and “the house of Joseph” (v. 18) appear to encompass Judah and the northern tribes together.

  • An oracle against a nation. A prophetic message about a foreign nation, delivered in the hearing of God’s own people (compare Isaiah 13–23; Jeremiah 46–51; Amos 1–2).

  • The Day of the LORD. A time when God acts decisively to judge and to save (compare Joel 2:31–32; Amos 5:18–20; Zephaniah 1:14–18). In Obadiah it brings judgment on Edom and on all nations, and deliverance on Mount Zion.

  • Drinking on My holy mountain (v. 16). Who “drank on My holy mountain” is debated. Some read Edom and Judah’s enemies celebrating their victory there; others read Judah drinking the cup of judgment first. Either way, the nations will drink the cup of the LORD’s judgment (compare Jeremiah 25:15–16).

  • Saviors (v. 21). Plural deliverers who come to Mount Zion to judge the mountains of Esau. The same word is used of the deliverers God raised up in the days of the judges (Judges 3:9, 15). Obadiah does not identify them.

  • Possess (vv. 17, 19–20). To take and hold as an inheritance, the language used of Israel’s entry into the land.

Christ and Life

Obadiah’s final words, “the kingdom shall be the LORD’s” (v. 21), find their fullest expression when “the kingdoms of this world have become the kingdoms of our Lord and of His Christ, and He shall reign forever and ever” (Revelation 11:15). The small oracle against Edom ends where all of Scripture ends, with God’s reign over all.

The Day of the LORD in Obadiah joins judgment on the proud to deliverance for God’s people. The New Testament places the same twofold day at Christ’s return, when He brings relief to those who are troubled and judgment on those who do not know God (2 Thessalonians 1:6–10). Those who are His can wait for that day with hope rather than dread.

Obadiah joins deliverance with holiness: “On Mount Zion there shall be deliverance, and there shall be holiness” (v. 17). Christ redeems His people to make them holy. He “gave Himself for us, that He might redeem us from every lawless deed and purify for Himself His own special people, zealous for good works” (Titus 2:14). Rescue in Christ leads to a life set apart for God.

Read prophetically, the saviors of verse 21 point to an end-time generation of empowered, victorious believers whom God raises up to take part as His kingdom advances and becomes visible. Scripture speaks of the saints receiving the kingdom and reigning with Christ (Daniel 7:18, 27; Luke 22:29–30; Revelation 5:10). Their victory comes from Christ and remains under His authority, for the kingdom is the LORD’s. Obadiah itself does not name the saviors; this prophetic reading develops the verse in light of the rest of Scripture.

Edom’s story also warns against pride and the bitterness that gloats over another’s fall. God’s people are not to take vengeance for themselves (Romans 12:19). They entrust judgment to Him and, in Christ, learn humility toward the God who brings the proud down and lifts up the lowly.

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Read Obadiah as God’s answer to His people’s suffering. It is a word of justice for those who were wronged, not permission for personal revenge, and Edom is not a symbol to attach to any person or group a reader dislikes.

Notice where the oracle widens at verse 15, and read the whole book toward its final line.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

  • Obadiah and Jeremiah 49. Several lines closely parallel Jeremiah 49:7–22 (compare Obadiah 1–4 with Jeremiah 49:14–16, and Obadiah 5–6 with Jeremiah 49:9–10). Whether both prophets drew on an earlier oracle, one used the other, or another explanation applies remains uncertain.

  • Sela. The rock imagery of verse 3 recalls Edom’s strongholds. Sela, meaning “rock,” is named as an Edomite stronghold in 2 Kings 14:7, but Obadiah does not mention it, and its location is uncertain.

  • Verses 19–20. The restored inheritance is described by region: the South, the Lowland, Ephraim and Samaria, Gilead, Zarephath, and Sepharad. Sepharad’s location is uncertain. The emphasis is restoration in every direction.

  • Translation differences. The NKJV keeps “Esau” and “the mountains of Esau” (vv. 8–9, 19, 21), while the NLT often uses “Edom,” as in verse 21: “to rule over the mountains of Edom.” The NLT renders the saviors of verse 21 as “those who have been rescued.”

  • Edom’s later history. Edomites were later displaced from their homeland, and those who settled in southern Judah became known as Idumeans. This is background to the book, not a one-to-one fulfillment of any verse.

  • References. Obadiah has one chapter, so verses may be cited as Obadiah 1:3 or simply as verse 3.
