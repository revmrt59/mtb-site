Important questions in the text, and how MTB understands them.

Book Questions

When was Obadiah written, and what attack is it describing? (Obadiah 1:10–14)

Obadiah does not name the invasion. He says only that strangers “carried captive his forces,” that foreigners “entered his gates and cast lots for Jerusalem,” and that Edom stood by “as one of them” (v. 11).

MTB understands this as Babylon’s destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC. That event fits the language best, and other Scripture remembers Edom’s part in it: Psalm 137:7 recalls the sons of Edom crying, “Raze it, raze it,” and Lamentations 4:21–22 and Ezekiel 35:5 also charge Edom for that day. Some place the book earlier, around 845 BC, when Philistines and Arabians raided Jerusalem under King Jehoram (2 Chronicles 21:16–17).

MTB holds the 586 BC setting as the strongest reading, not as a settled date. The message does not depend on it: God saw what Edom did to its brother, and He will answer it.

Read: Obadiah 1:10–14, Psalm 137:7, Lamentations 4:21–22, Ezekiel 35:5, 2 Chronicles 21:16–17

Why do Obadiah and Jeremiah 49 sound so much alike? (Obadiah 1:1–9)

Several lines in Obadiah 1:1–9 closely match Jeremiah 49:7–22, Jeremiah’s own prophecy against Edom. Some think one prophet drew on the other. Others think both used an earlier prophecy about Edom that was already known.

MTB does not claim to know which explanation is right, and it does not treat the two passages as separate, unrelated revelations. Together they form a strong prophetic witness that God would bring proud Edom down.

Read: Obadiah 1:1–9, Jeremiah 49:7–22

Does Obadiah permit us to rejoice when our enemies fall? (Obadiah 1:12, 15)

No. Obadiah condemns Edom for doing exactly that. “Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah in the day of their destruction” (v. 12). God judges Edom for gloating over a brother’s ruin, not to give anyone else permission to gloat.

Edom’s judgment is God’s work, announced by His prophet. Even where Obadiah pictures the house of Jacob as a fire and the house of Esau as stubble (v. 18), this is God’s judgment carried out in His day. It gives believers no warrant for personal revenge or violence, and Edom is not a stand-in for any person or group we dislike. Vengeance belongs to the Lord (Romans 12:19).

As a spiritual application, Obadiah exposes the pride that enjoys someone else’s fall. The cure is humility, and leaving judgment with God.

Read: Obadiah 1:12–15, Proverbs 24:17–18, Romans 12:17–21, Obadiah 1:18 · Related: How does Edom’s judgment relate to the Day of the LORD upon all the nations?

Obadiah 1:10–14 · Violence Against Your Brother Jacob

Is God judging Esau the man, or Edom the nation? (vv. 10–14)

Obadiah uses “Esau” for the nation of Edom, which descended from Esau (Genesis 36:1, 8). The charge is about what the nation did: “violence against your brother Jacob” (v. 10). Edom stood aloof, rejoiced, plundered, and cut off survivors (vv. 11–14).

The name keeps the family tie in view. Edom’s sin was worse because Judah was its brother. But the book judges a people for their conduct, not a man for who he was. Hebrews 12:16–17, about Esau selling his birthright, is a separate matter.

Read: Obadiah 1:10–14, Genesis 36:1, 8, Hebrews 12:16–17

Obadiah 1:15–16 · The Day of the LORD upon All the Nations

How does Edom’s judgment relate to the Day of the LORD upon all the nations? (v. 15)

Verse 15 is the turning point of the book: “For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near; as you have done, it shall be done to you; your reprisal shall return upon your own head.” Edom is the immediate example of God’s reciprocal justice. He answers Edom’s specific sins against its brother in kind, gathering up the charges of verses 10–14.

Then the prophecy widens. Edom’s judgment becomes part of the Day of the LORD upon all the nations, and from here Edom stands as one case within that larger judgment (v. 16). The rest of Scripture carries the Day of the LORD forward to Christ’s return, when He will both judge and deliver His people (2 Thessalonians 1:6–10). Obadiah gives no timetable for it.

“As you have done, it shall be done to you” reveals God’s justice in this judgment. It is not a formula for explaining every hardship in a person’s life, as if all suffering were payback (John 9:1–3).

Read: Obadiah 1:10–16, Joel 3:14–16, 2 Thessalonians 1:6–10, John 9:1–3 · Related: Does Obadiah permit us to rejoice when our enemies fall?, Who “drank on My holy mountain”?

Who “drank on My holy mountain”? (v. 16)

“For as you drank on My holy mountain, so shall all the nations drink continually” (v. 16). The Hebrew “you” here is plural, which raises a question. It may still be Edom, celebrating on Mount Zion after Jerusalem fell. Or it may now be Judah, who drank the cup of God’s judgment in that fall, so that the nations will drink it next.

MTB does not settle this. Both readings lead to the same point: God’s judgment does not stop with Judah or with Edom. It reaches “all the nations.”

Read: Obadiah 1:16, Jeremiah 25:15–17, Lamentations 4:21

Obadiah 1:17–18 · Deliverance and Holiness on Mount Zion

Was verse 18 fulfilled when Edom disappeared from history? (vv. 10, 18)

Obadiah announces Edom’s complete judgment: “no survivor shall remain of the house of Esau” (v. 18). Edom did later lose its land and its place as a nation, and that is significant.

But Edom’s disappearance as a nation does not by itself establish that verse 18 is completely fulfilled. The end of a nation is not the same as the death of every descendant of Esau.

Read: Obadiah 1:10, Obadiah 1:18, Malachi 1:2–4

Are the promises of verses 17–20 only spiritual? (vv. 17–20)

No. God promises more than Edom’s fall. Mount Zion will have “deliverance” and “holiness,” and the house of Jacob “shall possess their possessions” (v. 17). Verses 19–20 name real places: the mountains of Esau, Philistia, Ephraim, Samaria, Gilead, Zarephath.

MTB holds to the plain meaning of these promises to Israel, Zion, and the named lands. They are not only spiritual blessings. The text gives no timetable for their complete fulfillment.

Some place names are uncertain, such as Sepharad in verse 20. The meaning does not rest on locating them.

Read: Obadiah 1:17–20 · Related: Who are the “saviors” who come to Mount Zion?

Obadiah 1:19–21 · The Kingdom Shall Be the LORD’s

Who are the “saviors” who come to Mount Zion? (v. 21)

“Then saviors shall come to Mount Zion to judge the mountains of Esau, and the kingdom shall be the LORD’s” (v. 21). In the verse’s plain sense, these are plural deliverers who act with God’s authority, much like the deliverers God raised up for Israel in the Book of Judges. Obadiah does not name them.

MTB also reads verse 21 as a prophetic picture of an end-time generation of empowered, victorious believers whom God raises up to share in the advance and display of His kingdom. Other passages show God’s people sharing in Christ’s rule (Daniel 7:18, 27; Luke 22:29–30; Revelation 2:26–27; 5:10). Those passages support the theme; they do not directly identify Obadiah’s saviors. Many interpreters stop at the immediate historical meaning.

The NKJV’s “saviors” follows the usual reading of the Hebrew. The ancient Greek translation reads the same word as “those who have been saved,” and a few English Bibles follow it. Either way, God’s people come to Mount Zion, and the kingdom belongs to the LORD.

The plural saviors are not Jesus Himself, and Romans 11:26, which speaks of one Deliverer, draws on Isaiah, not Obadiah. Yet every deliverer serves under Christ, the source of all victory. The verse ends where the whole book ends: “the kingdom shall be the LORD’s,” a kingdom fully revealed when “the kingdoms of this world have become the kingdoms of our Lord and of His Christ” (Revelation 11:15).

Read: Obadiah 1:21, Judges 2:16–18, Daniel 7:18, 27, Luke 22:29–30, Revelation 2:26–27, Revelation 5:10, Revelation 11:15 · Related: Are the promises of verses 17–20 only spiritual?
