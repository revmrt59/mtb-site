GOD - ( ye.ho.vih , Strong's H3069 )

Summary:

The proper covenant name of God (Yahweh), vocalized in Hebrew tradition with the vowels of Elohim when paired with the divine title Adonai (Strong's H136) to avoid duplicating title pronunciations, typically translated in English as "GOD" in the compound phrase "Lord GOD." In Obadiah 1:1, yhwh functions within the title Adonai YHWH ("Lord GOD") to establish the ultimate divine authority behind the prophet's message. It demonstrates that the impending judgment against Edom is not the product of human political animosity, but the authoritative decree of Israel's sovereign covenant Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In Hebrew lexicography, Strong's H3069 represents the divine name yhwh specifically when pointed by the Masoretes with the vowels for Elohim ("God"). This occurs whenever yhwh directly follows the divine title Adonai (H136, "Lord" or "Master"). Rather than reading the combination as "Adonai Yahweh" (which would be rendered phonetically as "Lord Lord" under traditional substitution practices), ancient scribes vocalized the second word as Elohim, resulting in the translation "Lord GOD" in the New King James Version.

The historical setting of Obadiah centers on the bitter conflict between Israel (Jacob) and Edom (Esau). Edom had gloated over Judah's downfall and assisted enemy invaders. In the ancient Near East, national conflicts were often viewed as rivalries between local deities. By invoking the supreme compound title Adonai YHWH at the very opening of this prophetic vision, Obadiah sets the historic dispute in its proper cosmic framework: Yahweh is not merely a regional deity, but the sovereign Ruler over all nations.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yhwh is the personal, proper name of the God of Israel, rooted in the concept of self-existent, uncaused, and eternal reality. Grammatically, the target occurrence in Obadiah 1:1 is a proper noun, masculine singular, in the absolute state.

When paired with Adonai, the word carries the standard lexical sense of the covenant Lord, emphasized alongside His supreme lordship. It designates the personal, covenant-making God who possesses absolute authority over creation and human history. The term does not denote an abstract theological concept, but identifies the living God who acts, speaks, and executes righteousness.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the prophetic books, the phrase Adonai YHWH ("Lord GOD") serves as a formal heraldic formula. Prophets used this exact title to introduce royal decrees, solemn covenant warnings, and binding declarations of judgment or salvation.

The combination unites two complementary aspects of God's character:

  • Adonai emphasizes His mastership, ownership, sovereignty, and supreme command over all created entities.

  • YHWH emphasizes His personal identity, unchangeable nature, faithfulness, and covenant relationship with His chosen people.

In prophetic discourse, when a messenger says, "Thus says the Lord GOD," it signals that the words that follow carry the unalterable weight of the heavenly court.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of yhwh in Obadiah 1:1 carries significant theological weight:

  • Sovereignty Over History: Edom trusted in its mountain strongholds and strategic alliances. However, the title Adonai YHWH asserts that human fortresses are subject to the supreme Master of the universe.

  • Covenant Vindication: Although Israel was currently suffering and disgraced, God’s personal covenant name (yhwh) assured them that their God had not abandoned His commitments. He remains the covenant defender of His people.

  • Moral Accountability: The God of Israel is not indifferent to international injustice or arrogance. His absolute sovereignty entails universal moral accountability for all nations.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:1 begins with a dramatic double invocation of divine revelation: "The vision of Obadiah. Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom..." The immediate syntax links the divine title Adonai YHWH directly to the messenger formula "Thus says" (koh 'amar).

The usage of yhwh in this opening verse performs several contextual functions:

  • It grounds the entire prophecy in divine authorship. Obadiah presents himself not as an author of political commentary, but as an accurate herald recording what the Sovereign Lord has decreed.

  • The masculine singular absolute proper noun underscores the singular focus of divine authority. Edom is not dealing with human armies alone, but with the one true Lord.

  • It creates an immediate contrast between the proud arrogance of Edom (described in verses 3–4) and the absolute, unquestionable majesty of Adonai YHWH. While Edom asks in pride, "Who will bring me down to the ground?", the opening verse has already established that the sovereign Master of creation has spoken his sentence.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In times of national disaster, exile, or persecution, Israel's prophets repeatedly turned to the compound title Adonai YHWH. The usage reminded God's people that the God who bound Himself to them by covenant (yhwh) is the exact same God who exercises absolute control over the pagan nations that oppress them (Adonai).

This title provided immediate assurance to Obadiah's original audience: despite Edom's pride and Judah's temporary humiliation, history remains under the direct, active governance of their covenant God.
