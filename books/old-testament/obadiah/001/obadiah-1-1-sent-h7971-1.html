SENT - ( sha.lach , Strong's H7971 )

shalach - ( shalach , H7971 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shalach fundamentally means to send, dispatch, release, or extend. It describes the deliberate act of directing a person, message, object, or force from one location or authority toward a specific destination or purpose. In Obadiah 1:1, the verb appears in a passive construction ("a messenger has been sent among the nations") to indicate that the herald mobilizing international action against Edom is operating under divine dispatch. The term highlights Yahweh's sovereign governance over international affairs, demonstrating that the gathering of nations against Edom's pride is initiated and authorized by God Himself.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root shalach is one of the primary verbs of motion and mission in the Hebrew Bible. In ancient Near Eastern diplomatic, military, and legal contexts, sending an envoy or messenger (mal'akh or tsir) involved conferring authority from the sender to the bearer of the message. The envoy did not speak on his own authority; rather, his dispatch signified that the full power and weight of the commissioning authority stood behind the words delivered.

In the opening vision of Obadiah, the prophet addresses the impending judgment of Edom, a nation nestled in the rocky fortresses of Mount Seir that felt secure against invasion. By introducing the divine decree with the news that a messenger "has been sent among the nations," the text establishes that Edom's destruction is not an arbitrary twist of political fortune, but an orchestrated execution of divine justice originating from God's heavenly throne.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shalach encompasses a spectrum of related actions involving outward motion initiated by an agent:

  • To send or dispatch a person (such as a prophet, envoy, or military leader) with a specific mandate.

  • To release, let go, or dismiss someone from custody, obligation, or service.

  • To extend or stretch out an object or body part (such as reaching out a hand, a rod, or a weapon).

  • To unleash or cast forth natural or supernatural forces (such as judgment, pestilence, or fire).

When modified by specific verbal stems, shalach carries distinct operational nuances. In the Qal stem, it denotes active sending or stretching out; in the Piel stem, it frequently carries the intensive sense of driving out, letting go, or dismissing; and in the Pual stem—the specific form used in Obadiah 1:1—it presents the passive focus: the entity or messenger has been dispatched by an implicit higher power.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Hebrew Scriptures, shalach serves as a key theological term regarding divine initiative and commission. It appears in several distinct biblical contexts:

  • Divine Commissioning: Yahweh sends human instruments (such as Moses, judges, and prophets) to deliver His people or declare His word.

  • Geopolitical & Diplomatic Dispatches: Earthly monarchs send messengers, letters, or armies to negotiate treaties, issue challenges, or forge military alliances.

  • Sovereign Execution of Judgment: God sends plagues, destructive forces, or foreign armies as instruments to discipline Israel or punish arrogant pagan nations.

  • Freedom and Release: The verb describes setting captives free, releasing servants in Sabbath or Jubilee years, or letting birds loose into the open air.

In contexts concerning divine judgment, shalach emphasizes that earthly history moves under God's active supervision. Nothing happens outside the scope of His divine dispatch.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shalach reveals the dynamic, proactive nature of God's reign. Yahweh does not remain a passive observer of human events; He dispatches His word, His messengers, and His judgments into the world to accomplish His redemptive and righteous purposes.

The passive construction in prophetic announcements underscores divine sovereignty. When a messenger "has been sent," the focus shifts from the identity of the human messenger to the sovereign authority behind the mission. God uses both willing and unwilling agents among the nations to fulfill His holy decrees. For arrogant nations that rely on geography, wealth, or military might, the divine shalach signals that human defense mechanisms are entirely ineffective against God's sovereign summons.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:1, shalach is parsed in the Pual stem, perfect form, 3rd person, masculine, singular.

The choice of the Pual stem (passive) alongside the perfect form carries significant contextual weight:

  • Passive Action (Pual): The messenger is presented as having been acted upon—dispatched by a higher authority. While the diplomatic messenger moves among the heathen nations, the opening parallel clause ("We have heard a report from the LORD") establishes Yahweh as the ultimate Sender.

  • Completed Reality (Perfect): The perfect form portrays the dispatch as a accomplished divine decision. In the council of God, the decree is already active, making the historic downfall of Edom certain.

  • Contextual Contrast: Edom trusted in its impregnable position in the cliffs, asking in pride, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" (v. 3). The context contrasts Edom's false security with the active reality that Yahweh's messenger has already been sent among the nations to incite them to battle against her.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In prophetic literature, the sending of an envoy (tsir) to gather nations is a recognized literary feature marking divine warfare. The verb shalach links the heavenly decree with earthly execution. When Yahweh dispatches a word or a herald, that command carries sovereign momentum that cannot be recalled or thwarted by human resistance. Obadiah 1:1 uses this verb to assure God's covenant people that despite the apparent triumph and cruelty of Edom, God's sovereign command has already set in motion the historical events that will vindicate His righteousness.
