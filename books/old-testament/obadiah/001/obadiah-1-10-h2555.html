VIOLENCE - ( chamas , Strong's H2555 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chamas refers to cold-blooded, non-accidental violence, injustice, and physical or moral cruelty. It is not merely physical harm, but a flagrant violation of moral law, covenant obligations, and human rights. In Obadiah 1:10, chamas describes the malicious, unprovoked hostility and betrayal committed by Edom against their brother nation, Israel (Jacob), during Jerusalem's destruction. This cruel violation of family ties invited divine judgment, demonstrating that God holds nations accountable for how they treat the vulnerable and their kin.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun chamas comes from a root verb meaning to treat violently, wrong, or strip bare. In the ancient Near East, life was governed by tribal alliances, treaties, and family obligations. To commit chamas was to break these established boundaries, resulting in social chaos and the oppression of the weak. It is not a word for accidental manslaughter or justified warfare; it describes calculated, predatory aggression. It evokes the image of a societal fabric being violently torn apart by greed, hatred, and ruthless self-interest. Historically, Edom's chamas against Judah was particularly egregious because they were brother nations, descended from the twin brothers Jacob and Esau. Instead of offering brotherly protection during the Babylonian invasion, Edom chose predatory exploitation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Chamas means physical violence, social injustice, ethical wrong, or cruel damage. The semantic range of the word encompasses:

  • Physical brutality and bloodshed.

  • Legal injustice, false testimony, and the abuse of power to strip others of their rights.

  • Ethical devastation, where moral boundaries are intentionally crossed to harm another.

  • A cry for help, where a victim screams "Violence!" (chamas) to summon legal or divine intervention.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Old Testament, chamas is often paired with terms like deceit, blood, lies, and robbery, showing that physical violence is the natural fruit of moral decay. Scripture uses vivid word pictures to describe how deeply chamas can corrupt a person or nation:

  • A Garment: The wicked are described as wearing violence like a decorative chain or garment, showing that cruelty is their public identity (Psalm 73:6).

  • Food and Drink: The wicked "eat the bread of wickedness, and drink the wine of violence," indicating that cruelty is what sustains and satisfies them (Proverbs 4:17).

  • A Harvest: Those who sow wickedness will reap a harvest of violence. It is an active force that eventually turns back upon the head of the one who unleashed it.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, chamas is the antithesis of God's character and His design for creation.

  • God is the defender of the victim of chamas. Scripture declares that God hates the one who loves violence (Psalm 11:5).

  • The presence of unchecked chamas historically triggers global divine judgment. It was the specific sin that grieved God and filled the earth prior to the Flood in Genesis.

  • For Edom, committing chamas against "your brother Jacob" violated the natural family bond and covenant background. This escalated their sin from a geopolitical conflict to a severe moral betrayal of the natural law written on human hearts. God's holiness demands that such structural and relational cruelty be answered with perfect justice.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:10, chamas is qualified by the phrase "against your brother Jacob." The use of "brother" (ach) intensifies the moral weight of Edom's crime.

  • During the Babylonian siege of Jerusalem in 586 B.C., Edom did not merely stand aloof. They actively rejoiced in Judah's ruin, looted their treasures, and stood at the crossroads to intercept fleeing Judean refugees, handing them over to the enemy (Obadiah 1:11-14).

  • This active betrayal and cruelty is what God labels chamas.

  • The context shows that God applies the lex talionis (the law of retribution): because Edom participated in the violent destruction of Jacob, they would experience poetic justice. Shame would cover them, and they would be "cut off forever." Edom's prideful, violent self-sufficiency would lead to their absolute national extinction.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 6:11: "The earth also was corrupt before God, and the earth was filled with violence."

  • Psalm 11:5: "The LORD tests the righteous, But the wicked and the one who loves violence His soul hates."

  • Proverbs 4:17: "For they eat the bread of wickedness, And drink the wine of violence."

  • Habakkuk 1:2: "O LORD, how long shall I cry, And You will not hear? Even cry out to You, 'Violence!' And You will not save."

  • Micah 6:12: "For her rich men are full of violence, Her inhabitants have spoken lies, And their tongue is deceitful in their mouth."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Phonetic Significance: Though linguistically unrelated, the Hebrew chamas sounds identical to the modern Arabic word "Hamas" (which means "zeal" or "enthusiasm"). For modern readers, this homophonic similarity serves as a sobering, memorable bridge to understand the ancient biblical text's description of destructive, terrorizing violence.

  • Pastoral Warning: Obadiah's indictment of Edom's chamas serves as a warning against passive indifference or active exploitation of others in their time of trial. To profit from, gloat over, or participate in the suffering of others is to align oneself with chamas, which God promises to judge.
