JACOB - ( ya'aqob , Strong's H3290 )

Summary:

The name Jacob (ya'aqob) literally means "heel-catcher" or "supplanter," originating from the historical patriarch who wrestled for his birthright and blessing. In Obadiah 1:10, however, "Jacob" is used corporately and covenantally to represent the nation of Israel—specifically the southern kingdom of Judah—standing in stark contrast to "Esau" (Edom). By calling the victims of Edom's violence "your brother Jacob," the text highlights the horrific betrayal of family bonds, emphasizing that Edom's aggression was not merely a geopolitical conflict, but a violation of fraternal duty and a direct assault on God's covenant lineage.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The name ya'aqob is deeply rooted in the historical narrative of Genesis. At his birth, Jacob emerged from the womb holding onto his twin brother Esau’s heel (aqeb). This physical action prompted his naming as "one who takes by the heel," which idiomatically came to mean "supplanter" or "overreacher." This name characterized his early life of striving, scheming, and grasping for blessings.

However, God transformed Jacob's character, renaming him Israel ("he struggles with God"). In the prophetic books, both names are preserved, but "Jacob" is frequently used to evoke the historic, human reality of the family line. In Obadiah, the name is used to personify the nation of Israel as a single brother. It takes the reader back to the shared womb of Rebekah, reminding the audience that the conflict between Edom and Judah is not a war between strangers, but a bitter feud between twin brothers.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew noun ya'aqob is derived from the verb aqab, which means to seize by the heel, circumvent, overreach, or betray.

• As a proper noun, it refers to the younger son of Isaac and Rebekah, the father of the twelve tribes of Israel.

• As a collective singular noun, it refers to the descendants of the patriarch, representing the nation of Israel as a whole, or specifically the southern kingdom of Judah after the division of the monarchy.

• In a theological and covenantal sense, it designates the chosen people of Yahweh who inherit the Abrahamic promises, contrasted with the non-covenant nations (like Edom/Esau).

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The name "Jacob" occurs over 350 times in the Old Testament. In Hebrew poetry, it is frequently paired with "Israel" in a synonymous parallelism (e.g., "Hear this, O house of Jacob, / Who are called by the name of Israel").

The primary word picture in Obadiah 1:10 is that of family and kinship. By referring to Judah as "your brother Jacob," the prophet utilizes a powerful literary foil. Esau (the father of the Edomites) and Jacob (the father of the Israelites) were twin brothers. By referring to the suffering nation of Judah as "Jacob," Obadiah strips away the impersonal nature of international warfare and reframes it as a domestic tragedy: brother turning against brother.

### 4. Theological Implications

The use of "Jacob" in this context carries profound theological weight:

• The Faithfulness of God's Election: Jacob was chosen by God before birth, not because of his own merit, but because of God's sovereign grace. Calling the nation "Jacob" reminds the readers that despite Israel's failures, they remain God’s chosen covenant people.

• The Sacredness of Sibling Bonds: God values family relationships and holds nations accountable for violating natural familial obligations. Edom's hostility was not just a political sin; it was a violation of the created order of brotherhood.

• The Certainty of Divine Justice: Because Jacob is God's covenant partner, an attack on Jacob is an attack on Yahweh Himself. God rises up as the defender of His family. The violence shown to Jacob will return upon Edom's own head.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:10, the phrase "your brother Jacob" (achika ya'aqob) is the emotional and moral center of the indictment against Edom. During the Babylonian invasion and destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC, Edom did not merely stand aloof. They rejoiced in Judah's ruin, cut off their escaping refugees, and handed survivors over to the enemy.

The prophet uses the name "Jacob" to expose the unnatural cruelty of these actions. It was not "violence against a neighboring state," but "violence against your brother Jacob." The term "violence" (chamas) refers to cold-blooded, unjust ethical violations. The contrast is sharp: Esau, who once wept and embraced Jacob in reconciliation (Genesis 33), has now, through his descendants, completely abandoned brotherly love to seek Jacob's total destruction.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The historic struggle between Jacob and Esau spans the entire canon of Scripture. In the New Testament, Paul quotes Malachi 1:2-3 in Romans 9:13 ("Jacob I have loved, but Esau I have hated") to illustrate God's sovereign choice in election.

While Esau chose the immediate gratification of a single meal and despised his birthright, Jacob, for all his early manipulation, valued the spiritual inheritance of the covenant. Edom’s ultimate destruction and Jacob's eventual restoration (Obadiah 1:17-21) demonstrate that those who align themselves against God's covenant purposes will perish, while those who belong to the house of Jacob will ultimately possess their promised inheritance.
