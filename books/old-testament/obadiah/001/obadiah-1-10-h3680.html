COVER - ( kasah , Strong's H3680 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb kasah means to cover, clothe, conceal, or overwhelm. In the context of Obadiah 1:10, it depicts the overwhelming and public engulfing of Edom by shame as a direct consequence of their pride and violence against Israel. This covering is not a protective concealing of sin, but a complete, visible drowning in disgrace that marks their permanent ruin.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical background of the root kasah refers to placing a lid, garment, or canopy over an object to hide it from sight or protect it from the elements. It describes physical acts such as water covering the earth during the flood, clouds covering the sky, or a person putting on clothing. In the ancient Near East, garments and coverings carried deep social significance. To cover someone with a cloak could symbolize protection or marriage, whereas being stripped of a covering signified exposure and vulnerability. When used metaphorically of abstract concepts like shame or confusion, the word evokes the image of a person completely submerged, overwhelmed, or clothed in an inescapable layer of disgrace.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb kasah occurs frequently in the Old Testament, predominantly in the intensive Piel stem. Its core lexical meaning encompasses:

• To clothe or drape a physical object or person.

• To conceal or keep a matter secret.

• To overwhelm, flood, or submerge (as water covers the sea).

• To forgive or overlook offenses (conceptually "covering" sin so it is no longer in view).

In Obadiah 1:10, the verb is in the Piel imperfect stem, indicating an intensive, thorough, and active engulfing. The shame that covers Edom is not a light dusting but a total, suffocating immersion in defeat and ignominy.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs kasah in several vivid theological word pictures:

• The Flood of Judgment: The waters of the flood covered (kasah) the high hills, leaving no place of escape (Genesis 7:19). This spatial usage underlies the judgment metaphors, where calamity or shame floods over a rebellious nation.

• The Covering of Sin: In the sacrificial and relational realm, kasah denotes the covering of transgressions (Psalm 32:1). When God covers sin, He removes it from His sight. When love covers offenses (Proverbs 10:12), it restrains strife.

• Clothed in Shame: When applied to judgment, the word pictures shame, terror, or confusion acting as a garment. The individual or nation is so identified with their disgrace that it is the only thing visible to onlookers.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of kasah in Obadiah 1:10 highlights the law of divine retribution (lex talionis). Edom gloated over the exposure and vulnerability of Jerusalem during the Babylonian invasion. They watched as God’s people were stripped of their glory and dignity. Because Edom actively participated in and celebrated this exposure, God declares that they themselves will be covered—not with glory, protection, or mercy, but with inescapable shame.

Furthermore, this judgment demonstrates that human pride cannot build a fortress high enough to escape God's reach. Edom dwelt high in the clefts of the rocks, feeling secure and uncovered by threat. Yet, God's sovereign judgment would rise like a flood to cover them completely.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:10, the prophet states: "For violence against your brother Jacob, shame shall cover you, and you shall be cut off forever."

The context is the historical betrayal of Israel by their brother nation, Edom (descendants of Esau). During Jerusalem's crisis, Edom did not merely stand aloof; they joined the looters and cut off Jewish fugitives.

The choice of kasah here creates a stark contrast with Edom's self-perception. Edom perceived themselves as wise, strong, and highly exalted. God reverses this: the very shame they sought to inflict on Israel will backfire and thoroughly cover them. It denotes a public, historical reversal where their boasting is permanently silenced under the heavy shroud of defeat. Unlike Israel, whose shame was temporary and whose sins God would eventually cover with mercy, Edom's covering of shame leads to permanent destruction ("cut off forever").

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 7:19 – "And the waters prevailed exceedingly on the earth, and all the high hills under the whole heaven were covered."

• Psalm 32:1 – "Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, Whose sin is covered."

• Proverbs 10:12 – "Hatred stirs up strife, But love covers all sins."

• Micah 7:10 – "Then she who is my enemy will see, And shame will cover her who said to me, 'Where is the LORD your God?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew poetry, being "covered in shame" is the antithesis of being "clothed in salvation" or "girded with gladness." The passive experience of being covered by shame (bosheth) implies an absolute loss of control. Edom cannot remove this garment of disgrace because it is woven by their own violent deeds and secured by divine decree.
