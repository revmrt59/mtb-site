OFF - (kârath, Strong's H3772)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb karat primarily means to cut, sever, eliminate, or destroy, and it is frequently used in the context of cutting a covenant or executing judgment by cutting off transgressors. In Obadiah 1:10, the word communicates God's absolute, irreversible decree of destruction against the nation of Edom. By declaring that Edom will be "cut off forever" because of its brutal violence against its brother Jacob, the prophet signals that Edom will suffer total physical and historical extinction under divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israel and the broader Ancient Near East, the verb karat referred fundamentally to physical cutting or severance, such as felling trees, severing limbs, or cutting up objects. It also held a central position in covenantal life, where making a covenant was idiomatic for "cutting a covenant" (karat berit), derived from the practice of dividing animal sacrifices during covenant ratification ceremonies.

In prophetic discourse, karat frequently functions as a term of judicial sentencing. When targeted at hostile nations or covenant violators, it denotes divine expulsion, destruction, or extermination from the community or the earth. In Obadiah 1:10, the term is directed at Edom, the neighboring nation descended from Esau. Following the Babylonian invasion of Jerusalem, Edom gloated over Judah's ruin, assisted the invaders, and ambushed Judean refugees. The prophetic context of Obadiah announces divine retribution: Edom, who sought the severance and end of Israel, will itself be severed from existence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, karat denotes the act of severing or removing something by cutting. Its core lexical range includes:

  • To cut down or cut off (e.g., trees, objects, or body parts).

  • To eliminate, destroy, or exterminate (people, names, or dynasties).

  • To conclude or establish a covenant (when paired with berit, literally "to cut a covenant").

When used in the Qal stem, the verb generally carries an active sense (e.g., cutting wood, cutting off an enemy, or cutting a covenant). In the Niphal stem, as found in Obadiah 1:10, the verb functions in a passive or reflexive voice, meaning "to be cut off," "to be eliminated," or "to be destroyed." Lexically, the word itself simply describes the act of cutting or being cut off; the specific theological, judicial, or covenantal meaning depends on the syntax and immediate context.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, karat exhibits three primary usages:

  • Literal Severance: Describing physical cutting, such as felling trees, destroying idols, or physically severing an object.

  • Covenantal Ratification: Formulating solemn agreements, treaties, or divine covenants (karat berit). In these contexts, the physical cutting of animals symbolized the binding nature of the oath and the solemn consequences of covenant breach.

  • Judicial Exclusion and Destruction: Serving as a technical legal and prophetic term for divine judgment. In the Pentateuch, individuals who intentionally broke divine commands were sentenced to be "cut off" from among their people, indicating banishment, capital punishment, or divine eradication. In prophetic literature, this nuance expands to entire nations whose wickedness incurs ultimate divine judgment.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of karat in divine judgment underscores key biblical doctrines regarding God's righteousness and sovereignty:

  • Divine Retributive Justice: God's moral ordering of the universe ensures that wicked actions return upon the perpetrator. Edom sought to see Jacob cut off from the land; consequently, God decrees that Edom will be cut off.

  • Sovereignty over Nations: The God of Israel is not merely a local deity, but the sovereign Ruler of all nations. He possesses the authority to raise up kingdoms and to cut them off when they cross the threshold of divine forbearance.

  • Protection of Covenant People: Although God allowed Judah to undergo severe chastisement, He promised that their enemies would not succeed in wiping them out. By cutting off Edom, God preserves His covenant line and demonstrates His enduring fidelity to His promises.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:10, the prophet charges Edom with a specific crime: "For violence against your brother Jacob, shame shall cover you, and you shall be cut off forever."

Several contextual factors clarify the target word in this verse:

  • Brotherly Obligation: The reference to "your brother Jacob" highlights the egregious nature of Edom's crime. Edom (Esau) and Judah (Jacob) shared a common ancestry. Edom's violence was not merely international warfare, but family betrayal.

  • Grammatical Nuance: The verb appears in the Niphal stem, perfect form, second-person masculine singular. The Niphal stem presents Edom as the subject who undergoes this complete destruction ("you shall be cut off"). The second-person masculine singular form addresses Edom directly and personally, ensuring that the nation cannot evade the divine indictment. The perfect form, within prophetic discourse, views the future judgment as so certain that it is spoken of as a established decree.

  • Irreversible Extinction: The verb is qualified by the temporal phrase "forever" ('ad-'olam). Unlike Israel, who experienced temporary judgment followed by restoration, Edom's judicial severance is absolute and permanent. Edom will never rise again as a sovereign nation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 26:28 – "But they said, 'We have certainly seen that the LORD is with you. So we said, "Let there now be an oath between us, between you and us, and let us make [cut] a covenant with you..."'"

  • Psalms 89:3 – "'I have made [cut] a covenant with My chosen, I have sworn to My servant David...'"

  • Ezekiel 21:4 – "'Because I will cut off both the righteous and the wicked from you, therefore My sword shall go out of its sheath against all flesh from south to north...'"

  • Nahum 3:15 – "There the fire will devour you, the sword will cut you off; it will eat you up like a locust."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A stark theological contrast exists within the canonical usage of karat. While God graciously "cuts" (karat) covenants to bring sinful human beings into relation with Himself (as seen with Abraham, Israel, and David), those who arrogantly reject His righteousness or persecute His people ultimately end up being "cut off" (karat) by His judgment.

In Obadiah 1:10, Edom's pride led them to rejoice over Jerusalem's fall. They assumed that by aligning with the dominant world powers, they could exploit Jacob's destruction for their own gain. However, the prophetic word turns their expectation upside down: human arrogance cannot resist divine sovereignty, and those who attempt to eradicate God's people will themselves be cut off from history.
