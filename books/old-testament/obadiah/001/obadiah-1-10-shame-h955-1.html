SHAME - (bûwshâh, Strong's H955)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun bushah denotes public shame, disgraceful humiliation, and the catastrophic loss of honor, particularly as an objective state brought on by divine judgment. In Obadiah 1:10, bushah describes the overwhelming dishonor that will completely envelop Edom as a direct divine penalty for their malicious violence against their brother nation, Jacob. The word functions in the passage to mark the total moral and physical reversal of Edom's proud gloating, exposing their arrogance to public ignominy and signal destruction.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The noun bushah derives from the primary verbal root bosh, which fundamentally signifies coming to shame, being put to shame, or suffering utter disappointment through disgraced defeat. In the ancient Near East, honor and shame were not merely internal psychological experiences; they were foundational social realities that defined a person's or nation's standing, security, and standing before God and the community.

The book of Obadiah addresses the nation of Edom, descendants of Esau, regarding their conduct during the destruction and plunder of Jerusalem, the inheritance of Jacob. Despite their shared fraternal origin, Edom acted with gloating, hostility, and active participation in the ruin of Judah. In this prophetic setting, bushah is declared as God's divine sentence against Edom's unbrotherly cruelty and pride.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, bushah refers to:

  • Public shame and ignominy

  • Extreme humiliation and dishonor

  • The state of disgraced ruin resulting from defeated confidence or divine judgment

In contrast to modern internalized notions of shame as mere emotion or self-consciousness, bushah represents an objective condition. It is the visible, exposed condition of one whose cause has failed, whose security has proven false, and whose shameful deeds are laid open to public contempt. The noun appears in the target passage in the feminine singular absolute form.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The noun bushah appears in prophetic and poetic literature to describe the public humiliation that overtakes those who rely on false gods, indulge in arrogant violence, or mock the covenant family of Yahweh.

In Old Testament usage, the concept of shame operates as the antithesis of honor, glory, and divine protection. When God's blessing rests upon a people, their head is lifted high; when divine judgment falls, they are clothed in bushah. Metaphorically, shame is frequently depicted as a garment or a physical covering—something that completely envelops the recipient so that their dishonor is visible to all observers.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of bushah in divine judgment reveals several key Old Testament theological principles:

  • Divine Lex Talionis (Retributive Justice): Those who take pleasure in exposing others to humiliation and defeat will themselves be enveloped in humiliation by the righteous judgment of God.

  • The Ruin of Human Pride: Pride and arrogant self-sufficiency inevitably result in utter disgrace when confronted by the sovereign holiness of God.

  • Covenant Accountability: God evaluates moral conduct within relational structures. Edom's violence was particularly egregious because it was committed against "your brother Jacob." Betrayal of fraternal obligations under God invites severe dishonor.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:10, the Prophet writes: "For your violence against your brother Jacob, shame shall cover you, and you shall be cut off forever."

The immediate context emphasizes Edom's prideful self-exaltation (vv. 3–4) and their malicious behavior during Jerusalem's crisis (vv. 11–14). While Edom looked down on Judah's disaster and joined in their devastation, God reverses their fortunes.

The phrase "shame shall cover you" employs vivid poetic personification. The noun bushah (in the feminine singular absolute) acts as the covering that will coat Edom entirely. Rather than standing tall in their mountain strongholds, Edom will be utterly draped in public disgrace. This total covering of shame is immediately paired with the declaration "and you shall be cut off forever," establishing that Edom's public dishonor is not a temporary embarrassment, but the herald of permanent historical removal.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ezekiel 7:18 – "They will also gird themselves with sackcloth; horror will cover them; shame will be on all faces, and baldness on all their heads."

  • Psalms 89:45 – "The days of his youth You have shortened; You have covered him with shame."

  • Micah 7:10 – "Then she who is my enemy will see, and shame will cover her who said to me, 'Where is the LORD your God?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The thematic connection between Micah 7:10 and Obadiah 1:10 is striking. In both prophetic passages, the exact phrase "shame will cover" (tekhasseh bushah) is used to describe the fate of arrogant enemies who gloated over the suffering of God's people. In Micah, the enemy mocked Yahweh's covenant presence; in Obadiah, Edom betrayed fraternal ties and gloated over Judah's downfall. In both instances, the public dishonor of bushah serves as Yahweh's visible vindication of His justice.
