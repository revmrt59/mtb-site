VIOLENCE - (châmâç, Strong's H2555)

Summary:

Chamas denotes severe violence, wrong, lawless injustice, or malicious cruelty that breaches moral, social, or covenantal boundaries. In Obadiah 1:10, chamas captures Edom's treacherous, unbrotherly hostility against Jacob (Israel) during Jerusalem's calamity, identifying Edom's opportunistic cruelty and indifference as a flagrant breach of brotherly obligation that invites inescapable divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew noun chamas signifies illegal force, injury, destructive malice, or systemic oppression. In the ancient Near East, society recognized chamas as a violation of fundamental human rights and social order, representing the breakdown of law where power and force supersede justice. Across the Old Testament, the term appears in wisdom literature, law, psalmody, and prophetic indictments. It frequently describes the ruthless exploitation of the vulnerable, physical brutality, corrupt testimony, or military arrogance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, chamas encompasses a range of severe wrongdoing, including:

  • Physical violence, slaughter, and destruction.

  • Injustice, extortion, and legal cruelty (e.g., a "violent witness" who destroys another through perjury).

  • Malicious cruelty and cold-hearted exploitation.

Hebrew lexicography (BDB, TWOT) highlights that chamas is not limited to sudden outbursts of physical assault; it characterizes any unrighteous action whereby one person or nation unlawfully mistreats, oppresses, or plunders another.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In prophetic literature, chamas often serves as a primary marker of a society or nation ripe for divine judgment. When human relationships or international conduct become governed by chamas, God intervenes as the righteous Judge.

The term carries distinct shades of meaning depending on context:

  • Interpersonal Oppression: Malicious speech, judicial corruption, or physical extortion between individuals.

  • Civil and Corporate Corruption: Widespread lawlessness where violence becomes the routine method of securing wealth or power.

  • International Brutality: The destructive aggression, looting, and cruelty displayed by conquering empires or hostile neighbors against weaker peoples.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, chamas stands in direct opposition to God’s character of righteousness (tsedaqah) and justice (mishpat). Where God calls His people to protect the weak and honor covenant commitments, chamas tramples the weak and disregards sacred bonds.

Crucially, Scripture reveals a principle of divine retribution regarding chamas: God turns the violence of the wicked back upon their own heads. The violence that nations inflict on others ultimately consumes them under God's holy governance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:10, the Prophet declares God's verdict against the nation of Edom: "For your violence (chamas) against your brother Jacob, shame shall cover you, and you shall be cut off forever."

The grammatical form of the target word in this verse is a masculine singular noun in the construct state. The construct state grammatically ties chamas directly to "your brother Jacob." This syntactical connection highlights the heinous nature of Edom's offense: it was not merely violence against a stranger, but violent wrong committed against a brother. Edom descended from Esau, while Israel descended from Jacob.

The surrounding narrative (Obadiah 1:11–14) clarifies what constituted this chamas. When foreign invaders breached Jerusalem, Edom did not merely watch with malicious indifference; Edom actively joined in the spoils, rejoiced over Judah’s ruin, looted their wealth, and stood at the crossroads to cut down Judah's fugitives. The prophetic message classifies this tragic mix of passive betrayal, opportunistic plundering, and active malice under the single devastating term chamas.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 16:5 – "Then Sarai said to Abram, 'My wrong (chamas) be upon you!'"

  • Psalms 27:12 – "Do not deliver me over to the will of my adversaries; for false witnesses have risen against me, and such as breathe out violence (chamas)."

  • Ezekiel 12:19 – "They shall eat their bread with anxiety... that her land may be stripped of all it contains, because of the violence (chamas) of all those who dwell in it."

  • Habakkuk 2:17 – "For the violence (chamas) done to Lebanon will cover you, and the plunder of beasts which made them afraid..."

  • Jonah 3:8 – "But let man and beast be covered with sackcloth, and cry mightily to God; yes, let every one turn from his evil way and from the violence (chamas) that is in his hands."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Obadiah, God’s accounting of chamas establishes that silence, opportunistic exploitation, and gloating over a brother's distress carry the same moral culpability as direct assault. Edom’s chamas was fundamentally a failure of kinship and covenant obligation. Consequently, the poetic justice of the passage promises that the terror Edom inflicted upon Jacob will return upon Edom: as they cut off their brother, so they will be "cut off forever."
