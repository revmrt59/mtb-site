CAPTIVE - (shâbâh, Strong's H7617)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shavah fundamentally means "to take captive," "to lead away," or "to carry off," describing the forcible capture and removal of people, livestock, or property during military conflict or raids. In Obadiah 1:11, shavah denotes the tragic conquest of Judah when foreign enemies took her forces and assets captive. By establishing this temporal backdrop, the word highlights the central moral indictment of the prophecy: as invaders stripped Jerusalem and carried her people into captivity, Edom stood aloof on the other side, acting as one of the hostile oppressors rather than a brotherly ally.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the Ancient Near East, warfare regularly involved the practice of plunder and deportation. Victory in combat was not merely defined by holding territory, but by seizing property, livestock, and human captives (shavah). Prisoners of war were taken to serve as forced labor, to be integrated into host populations, or to be held for ransom.

Within the Old Testament, shavah functions as the primary verbal root for capturing personnel or goods in military encounters, nomadic raids, and large-scale national exiles. The term encompasses both local tactical seizures and full-scale imperial deportations.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shavah signifies the act of taking individuals or property by force under authority or constraint, preventing escape and relocating them under foreign control. Its core meanings include:

  • To take captive or lead away captive (referring to people or armies).

  • To drive away or carry off (referring to herds, flocks, or plunder).

  • To deport or lead into exile (referring to populations).

In Obadiah 1:11, the grammatical form is a Qal stem, infinitive construct, appearing in the construct state. The infinitive construct operates as a verbal noun bound within a temporal clause ("in the day of taking captive"), specifying the precise moment of Judah's national trauma when foreign adversaries overpowered her forces and carried them away.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, shavah appears in a variety of settings:

  • Military Raids and Local Conflict: In nomadic and local warfare, the verb describes raiders taking women, children, and livestock while burning settlements (e.g., 1 Samuel 30:2).

  • Property and Livestock Disputes: In legal contexts, the term addresses cases where livestock under a neighbor's care is injured, dies, or is driven away/taken captive (e.g., Exodus 22:10).

  • National Deportation and Exile: In covenantal history and prophetic texts, shavah describes whole tribes or nations being forcibly removed by conquerors (e.g., 1 Kings 8:50; Psalms 137:3).

The nuance of the word depends largely on its direct object. When applied to herds, it emphasizes the loss of economic wealth; when applied to people, it emphasizes subjugation, loss of freedom, and forced removal from the covenant land.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of being taken captive (shavah) carries substantial theological weight within Israel's covenant history:

  • Covenant Sanction: Deuteronomy warned that persistence in covenant unfaithfulness would result in Israel being carried away captive before her enemies. Captivity was understood not merely as military defeat, but as divine discipline executed through human instrumentality.

  • Divine Sovereignty over Pagan Nations: Though God used foreign armies to discipline His people, those foreign entities—and surrounding nations who gloated over Judah's fall—remained fully responsible for their cruelty and arrogance.

  • The Theme of Restoration: Counterbalancing the judgment of captivity is God's covenant mercy. Old Testament theology frequently promises that God will remember His people in the land of their captivity, show them compassion, and ultimately restore them.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:11 forms the heart of the prophet's indictment against the nation of Edom. The verse uses structured temporal clauses ("In the day that...") to set the scene: "In the day that you stood on the other side—in the day that strangers carried away captive his forces, when foreigners entered his gates and cast lots for Jerusalem—even you were as one of them."

The contextual power of shavah in this verse relies on several crucial factors:

  • The Identity of the Victims and Captors: The ones carrying away Judah's forces into captivity are called "strangers" and "foreigners." These pagan invaders acted according to expected Gentile hostilitiy.

  • The Position of Edom: Edom was not an unfamiliar pagan nation; Edom descended from Esau, making them the fraternal neighbor ("brother") of Israel (Jacob).

  • Moral Complicity: While foreign invaders actively engaged in taking Judah captive (shavah), Edom "stood on the other side." Obadiah asserts that by remaining passive, gloating over Israel's demise, and eventually sharing in the spoils, Edom became morally indistinguishable from the violent captors ("even you were as one of them").

  • Syntax and Event Marking: The Qal infinitive construct of shavah marks the exact temporal turning point when Jerusalem was stripped of her defense and dignity. Edom's standing aloof during this specific act of forced captivity turned fraternal obligation into treasonable betrayal.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 30:2 – "And had taken captive the women and those who were there, from small to great; they did not kill anyone, but carried them away and went their way."

  • 1 Kings 8:50 – "And forgive Your people who have sinned against You, and all their transgressions which they have transgressed against You; and grant them compassion before those who took them captive, that they may have compassion on them;"

  • Psalms 137:3 – "For there those who carried us away captive asked us a song, and those who tormented us asked for mirth, saying, 'Sing us one of the songs of Zion!'"

  • Jeremiah 41:14 – "Then all the people whom Ishmael had carried away captive from Mizpah turned around and came back, and went to Johanan the son of Kareah."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Lex Talionis (Retributive Justice): The indictment of Edom in Obadiah 1:11 sets up the divine sentence pronounced in Obadiah 1:15: "As you have done, it shall be done to you; your reprisal shall return upon your own head." Because Edom approved of and participated in Judah being taken captive, Edom itself would eventually face subjugation and ruin.

  • Contrast with Divine Deliverance: While Obadiah begins with the tragedy of Judah being carried away captive while Edom gloats, the vision concludes with the complete restoration of God's kingdom on Mount Zion (Obadiah 1:17, 21), where the former captives are restored and the Lord's kingdom is established.
