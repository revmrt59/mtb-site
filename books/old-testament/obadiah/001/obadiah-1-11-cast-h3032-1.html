CAST - (yâdad, Strong's H3032)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yadad means "to cast" or "to throw," used specifically in prophetic literature for the casting of lots over captives and plunder. In Obadiah 1:11, yadad highlights the callous, cold-hearted complicity of Edom as foreign conquerors cast lots over Jerusalem's fallen property and population. It underscores Edom's moral guilt in celebrating and sharing in the subjugation of God's covenant people as if Jerusalem were mere gambling spoils.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb yadad is an ancient Hebrew root denoting the act of throwing or casting items, particularly in the context of lot-casting. In the ancient Near East, casting lots was a standard procedure used not only for seeking divine guidance or making administrative decisions, but also for distributing plunder, dividing territory, and allocating human captives following a successful military siege.

In the prophetic texts of the Old Testament, yadad surfaces within narratives of tragic warfare and divine judgment. Rather than describing a neutral civic or sacred decision, the word carries a somber cultural backdrop: victorious foreign armies treating defeated populations and sacred possessions as cheap commodities to be divided by chance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yadad signifies "to cast" or "to throw" (specifically in the idiom "to cast lots").

While general Hebrew verbs for throwing (such as shalak or ramah) cover a wide spectrum of physical actions, yadad functions in Biblical Hebrew as a specific term for throwing lots to determine ownership of spoil.

Maintaining lexical restraint is essential: the word itself simply describes the physical throwing or casting of lots. The moral degradation, tragic tone, and divine condemnation connected to the act are not inherent in the mechanical definition of the verb itself, but stem from the specific context of plundering God's people.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In prophetic literature, yadad is reserved for scenes of national devastation and exile. The action describes conquerors reducing human beings and covenant inheritances to mere gambling stakes.

When invaders cast lots over people, they strip away the dignity, freedom, and covenant identity of the defeated. The nuance of yadad in these settings exposes the utter objectification of the vulnerable: elders, children, and holy items become common spoils distributed by the toss of a lot.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of yadad rests in Yahweh's deep concern for justice and His defense of His covenant community. The casting of lots over Jerusalem represents an arrogant defiance of God's sovereignty over His land and people.

Furthermore, the prophetic exposure of this act demonstrates that God holds nations accountable not only for direct violence, but also for passive alignment, malicious joy, and predatory exploitation during a neighbor's downfall. Edom’s willingness to stand by and identify with those who cast lots over Jerusalem exposed a profound betrayal of brotherly covenant obligations (tracing back to the shared heritage of Esau and Jacob), provoking divine retribution.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the prophet brings a formal indictment against Edom. The verse recalls the day when strangers carried away Judah's wealth and foreigners entered her gates. The text states that these enemies "cast lots for Jerusalem," and immediately adds the piercing charge against Edom: "even you were as one of them."

The verb appears in the Qal stem, perfect form, 3rd person plural. This grammatical construction presents the action of casting lots as a completed historical event carried out by the foreign invaders.

The word yadad captures the cruel indifference of the scene. The pagan invaders did not merely conquer Jerusalem; they casually rolled lots to divide her treasures and people. Obadiah’s argument hinges on Edom's presence and attitude during this event. Though Edom may not have been the primary foreign army breaking down the gates, by approving of the lot-casting and joining in the plunder, Edom became fully complicit in the eyes of Yahweh. The use of yadad highlights the tragic reality that Israel's own kin stood with pagan conquerors, treating God's chosen city like cheap loot.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Joel 3:3 — "They have cast lots for My people, have given a boy as payment for a harlot, and sold a girl for wine, that they might drink."

  • Nahum 3:10 — "Yet she was carried away, she went into captivity; her young children also were dashed to pieces at the head of every street; they cast lots for her honorable men, and all her great men were bound in chains."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Across the prophetic books where yadad appears, a clear canonical pattern emerges: the act of casting lots over human beings is viewed by Yahweh as a peak expression of human cruelty that demands divine judgment.

In Joel 3:3, casting lots over God's people is directly linked to the horrific devaluation of human life, where children are traded for temporary worldly indulgence. In Nahum 3:10, the same verb describes the historical fall of Nineveh's enemy, No-Amon (Thebes), where nobles were divided by lot, serving as a warning that arrogant oppressors will ultimately face the same fate they inflicted on others.

In Obadiah 1:11, yadad serves as the moral turning point of the oracle. By linking Edom to the foreign conquerors who cast lots over Jerusalem, Obadiah establishes the absolute righteousness of God's coming judgment against Edom detailed in the remainder of the prophecy.
