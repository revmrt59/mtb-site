DAY - (yôwm, Strong's H3117)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun yom fundamentally denotes a day, a specific point in time, or a defined period. In Obadiah 1:11, yom designates the specific, disastrous historical moment when Jerusalem was invaded and looted, highlighting Edom's treacherous indifference and complicity in the downfall of their brother Jacob.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The prophet Obadiah brings divine judgment against the nation of Edom for their opportunistic betrayal of Judah. Edom descended from Esau, making them a brother nation to Israel, who descended from Jacob. Rather than offering aid when foreign invaders attacked Jerusalem, Edom stood aloof, rejoiced over Judah’s ruin, and even participated in the plundering.

The word yom serves as a critical structural marker in Obadiah's prophecy. By repeatedly drawing attention to "the day" of Israel's crisis, the prophet stresses that God marks specific moments in human history. Edom's conduct on that singular, dark day exposed the moral condition of their heart and incurred divine retribution.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yom refers to a solar day (daytime as opposed to night), a 24-hour period, or more broadly, an appointed time, season, or historical occasion. Standard Old Testament lexicons (such as BDB and TWOT) identify yom as the foundational Hebrew term for temporal designation, ranging from a single calendar day to an epoch.

When combined with specific events, yom often functions idiomatically to designate a moment of decisive significance, such as a "day of distress," a "day of battle," or a "day of victory." In Obadiah 1:11, the word is used in a specific temporal sense to pinpoint the exact time when Judah was overrun by enemies.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament Scriptures, yom carries a wide variety of nuances depending on context:

  • Literal Time: Denoting a literal day or cycle of light and darkness.

  • Historical Moment: Indicating a defined period marked by specific human actions or divine intervention.

  • Day of Calamity: Referring to times of severe national distress, judgment, or warfare, where God's people undergo intense testing.

  • Eschatological Judgment: Appearing in the prophetic phrase "the Day of the LORD" (yom YHWH), pointing to God's ultimate cosmic accountability and justice.

In prophetic literature, human "days" of violence and rebellion are set in direct contrast to God's sovereign "day" of ultimate vindication and judgment.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of yom in Obadiah resides in the reality that human action occurs within God's ordered temporal realm, and every "day" of human conduct is observed by the Almighty.

Edom presumed that Judah’s day of trouble was merely an isolated political catastrophe that could be exploited for gain. However, Scripture demonstrates that how a nation or individual acts during a brother's "day of distress" carries eternal weight. Neutrality during injustice is framed by God as active rebellion; standing aloof while evil transpires constitutes covenant treason in God's sight.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, yom appears in a masculine singular form within the temporal clause: "In the day that you stood on the other side—In the day that strangers carried away captive his forces..."

The syntax constructs a vivid indictment. Obadiah uses yom repeatedly across verses 11–14 to construct a detailed catalog of Edom's transgressions on that specific occasion:

  • Standing aloof ("on the other side") while foreigners invaded.

  • Rejoicing over the children of Judah in the day of their destruction.

  • Speaking proudly in the day of distress.

  • Entering the gate and looting Judah's substance in the day of their calamity.

The repeated use of yom highlights that Edom’s failure was not an abstract attitude, but concrete action (and inaction) committed at a real moment in history. The prophet explicitly states the divine verdict on Edom’s passive stance: "Even you were as one of them." By doing nothing to help their brother on that day, Edom made themselves full partners with the foreign plunderers.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Kings 19:3 – "And they said to him, 'Thus says Hezekiah: "This day is a day of trouble, and rebuke, and blasphemy..."'" Here the same Hebrew word describes a specific historical moment of national crisis under enemy threat.

  • Lamentations 1:7 – "In the days of her affliction and roaming, Jerusalem remembered all her pleasant things that she had in days of old..." The noun reflects a period of intense historical suffering following Jerusalem's fall.

  • Zechariah 4:10 – "For who has despised the day of small things?" The word marks a specific time period characterized by humble, initial efforts in God's service.

  • 2 Samuel 3:39 – "And I am weak today, though anointed king..." The term denotes a specific present moment marked by personal limitation and circumstance.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A profound literary and theological connection exists between the "day" (yom) of Judah's calamity in Obadiah 1:11–14 and "the Day of the LORD" (yom YHWH) in Obadiah 1:15.

Edom watched with malicious satisfaction during Judah's dark "day," but Obadiah immediately transitions from Edom's historical opportunity to God's ultimate temporal reckoning: "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near; as you have done, it shall be done to you." The measure Edom used on Judah's yom became the standard by which God would judge Edom on His yom.
