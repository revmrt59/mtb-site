ENTERED - (bôwʼ, Strong's H935)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb boʾ basic meaning is to enter, come, or arrive. In Obadiah 1:11, the word describes the hostile breach of Jerusalem's city gates by foreign invaders who entered to plunder her wealth and subjugate her people. Its presence in this verse establishes the physical violation of Judah's capital and exposes Edom's horrific moral failure—watching and aligning with foreign enemies as they breached the gates of Edom's own brother.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root boʾ is one of the primary verbs of motion in Hebrew, denoting movement toward a specific goal, destination, or boundary. It is used throughout the Old Testament to describe physical entry into spaces such as houses, tents, lands, or cities, as well as the temporal arrival of events, judgments, or appointed times. In ancient Near Eastern culture, a city's gates represented its primary line of defense, civil court, market, and symbol of security. To "enter" through a city's gates by force meant that defenses had failed, sovereignty was lost, and the population stood exposed to foreign domination and pillage.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, boʾ expresses the motion of coming, arriving, or entering into a space or state.

  • Physical Movement: To come in, go in, enter, or pass through a boundary (e.g., entering a room, a city, or a territory).

  • Directional Arrival: To arrive at a destination or draw near to a person or location.

  • Ingress/Invasion: In military contexts, it signifies penetrating enemy defenses, entering a besieged city, or invading a nation.

  • Temporal/Metaphorical Use: To come upon someone (as trouble, fear, or blessing) or the arrival of a designated time or day.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The verb boʾ spans a broad context across the Old Testament:

  • Covenant and Land: It frequently describes Israel's entry into the Promised Land under God's guidance, emphasizing the fulfillment of divine promises.

  • Worship and Sanctuary: It designates approaching God's presence, entering the tabernacle or temple, or bringing offerings before the Lord.

  • Judgment and Warfare: In prophetic texts, boʾ often describes invading armies entering sovereign territory or the sovereign arrival of divine judgment upon rebellious nations.

4. Theological Implications

In the prophetic witness, the forcible entry of hostile nations into Jerusalem carries deep covenantal implications. When foreign nations "entered" Jerusalem's gates, it signified the execution of covenantal warnings resulting from Judah's unfaithfulness. However, Obadiah focuses on the accountability of neighboring nations—specifically Edom. Edom's crime was not merely passive observation; by standing aloof and approving as invaders entered Jerusalem, Edom cast off fraternal obligation (as descendants of Esau) and participated in Judah's ruin. Theologically, boʾ in Obadiah 1:11 illustrates how human actions during times of crisis reveal the true posture of the heart before God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the target word is parsed in the Qal stem, perfect form, 3rd person plural. The Qal stem expresses simple active motion, while the perfect form presents the entry of the invaders as a completed historical reality. The 3rd person plural identifies the "foreigners" who breached Jerusalem's defenses.

The immediate context sets up a indictment against Edom: "In the day that you stood on the other side—In the day that strangers carried away captive his forces, When foreigners entered his gates And cast lots for Jerusalem—Even you were as one of them." The repetition of "in the day" emphasizes the critical moment of calamity. As the foreign troops crossed through the gates of Jerusalem, Edom chose to identify with the plunderers rather than offer aid to their brother nation. The verb boʾ marks the exact moment the threshold of safety was crossed, heightening the severity of Edom's betrayal.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The breach described in Obadiah 1:11 highlights the contrast between earthly security and divine protection. Jerusalem's physical gates could not keep out invaders when the Lord withdrew His protective hand. Furthermore, Edom's failure to defend Judah violated the explicit divine command in Deuteronomy 23:7 ("You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother"). Edom's willingness to watch enemies enter Jerusalem's gates ultimately sealed Edom's own judgment, demonstrating that God holds nations accountable for how they treat His covenant people in times of distress.
