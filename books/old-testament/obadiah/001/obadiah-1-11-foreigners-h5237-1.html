FOREIGNERS - (nokrîy, Strong's H5237)

Summary:

The Hebrew word nokhri fundamentally means foreign, alien, or strange. It describes someone or something that originates outside of a specific family, tribe, nation, or covenant community. In Obadiah 1:11, nokhri refers to the invading Babylonian army that breached the gates of Jerusalem. The use of this word in the passage creates a devastating contrast: while "foreigners" (who had no kinship or covenant loyalty to Judah) were destroying the city, Edom—Judah's brother nation—stood by and actively participated in the plunder, effectively acting like a foreigner rather than a brother.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Near East, identity, protection, and obligation were deeply tied to kinship and covenant. A person's primary loyalties were to their family, tribe, and nation. A nokhri was an outsider who did not belong to these concentric circles of belonging.

Because a foreigner lacked the natural bonds of blood or the legal bonds of a covenant, they were not bound by the same societal obligations, nor did they inherently enjoy the same protections as a native-born citizen. Depending on the context, a nokhri could be a vulnerable alien needing hospitality, a hostile invader threatening the community, or a cultural outsider introducing foreign practices and idolatry.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic lexical meaning of nokhri is "foreign," "alien," or "strange." It is an adjective that often functions as a noun to describe a foreigner or a stranger.

The term identifies someone who is fundamentally separate from the group in question. It denotes a lack of shared origin, shared land, or shared covenant. When applied to people, it means an alien or outsider; when applied to objects or concepts (such as gods or customs), it means something imported from outside the community of Israel that is alien to Yahweh's covenant.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The biblical usage of nokhri spans several distinct categories:

  • Literal Outsiders: It frequently describes people from other nations who are not part of ethnic or covenantal Israel.

  • Personal Alienation: It can describe an Israelite's own experience of living outside their homeland, feeling like a stranger in a foreign land.

  • Covenantal Threat: The word is often used negatively to describe "foreign women" or "foreign gods" that lead the Israelites away from exclusive devotion to Yahweh.

  • Metaphorical Estrangement: In wisdom literature, particularly Proverbs, the term is used for the "strange woman" or "adulteress"—someone who operates outside the bounds of the marriage covenant.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nokhri helps define the boundaries of God's covenant people. Israel was called to be holy and distinct from foreign nations, particularly regarding worship and ethics. The intrusion of foreign gods or foreign alliances often marked a failure in Israel's covenant fidelity.

Conversely, the presence of foreigners in the land also served as a test of Israel's justice and a testimony to God's global purposes, as the temple was designed to be a place where even the nokhri could come and pray to Yahweh. However, in the context of divine judgment, God frequently used hostile foreigners as instruments of discipline, fulfilling the covenant curses by allowing alien nations to conquer and exile His rebellious people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the prophet condemns Edom for their actions during the fall of Jerusalem. The verse states: "In the day that you stood on the other side—In the day that strangers carried captive his forces, When foreigners [nokhri] entered his gates And cast lots for Jerusalem—Even you were as one of them."

The grammatical morphology of nokhri here is masculine, plural, and absolute, simply identifying these invaders as a distinct, multiple group of outsiders acting against the city.

The pastoral and theological weight of the word lies in its contrast with Edom's identity. Edom was descended from Esau, the twin brother of Jacob (Israel). They shared blood. When foreigners—an invading army with no shared history, no brotherhood, and no covenant loyalty—attacked Jerusalem, Edom's brotherly obligation should have compelled them to help Judah. Instead, Edom stood aloof, watched the destruction, and ultimately joined in the looting. By highlighting that it was "foreigners" who entered the gates, Obadiah underscores the unnatural and treacherous cruelty of Edom. Edom abandoned their brother to aliens and, in doing so, became indistinguishable from the nokhri themselves.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 2:22 – "And she bore him a son, and he called his name Gershom; for he said, 'I have been a stranger in a foreign land.'"

  • Nuance: Here, the word describes the geographical and social reality of Moses living in Midian, separated from his people and his homeland.

  • 1 Kings 8:41 – "Moreover, concerning a foreigner, who is not of Your people Israel, but has come from a far country for Your name’s sake..."

  • Nuance: Solomon's temple dedication prayer explicitly makes theological provision for the outsider who recognizes Yahweh's power and seeks to worship Him, showing God's grace extending beyond ethnic Israel.

  • Proverbs 5:10 – "Lest aliens be filled with your wealth, And your labors go to the house of a foreigner;"

  • Nuance: In wisdom literature, the word is used to warn against the destructive consequences of adultery, where a person's strength and resources are squandered on those outside their own household.

  • Ezra 10:2 – "And Shechaniah the son of Jehiel, one of the sons of Elam, spoke up and said to Ezra, 'We have trespassed against our God, and have taken pagan [foreign] women from the peoples of the land...'"

  • Nuance: The term highlights the covenantal crisis of intermarriage with outsiders, which threatened the spiritual purity and exclusive worship of the post-exilic community.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The prophetic literature often portrays the invasion of the nokhri as the ultimate desecration of sacred space. For foreigners to enter the gates of Jerusalem and cast lots for its spoils meant that the protective presence of Yahweh had been withdrawn. Obadiah's use of the term captures the horror of this violation, making Edom's complicity in the face of such a tragedy a profound betrayal of both kinship and basic human decency.
