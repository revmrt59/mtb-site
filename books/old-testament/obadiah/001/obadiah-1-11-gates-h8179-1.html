GATES - (shaʻar, Strong's H8179)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun shaʿar fundamentally denotes a "gate," referring to the physical structural entry or barrier to a tent, enclosure, courtyard, fortress, or city. In ancient Israel and neighboring cultures, city gates were the primary military checkpoints, commercial hubs, and formal sites of judicial administration. In Obadiah 1:11, shaʿar appears in the masculine plural absolute form to depict the entryways of Jerusalem breached by hostile armies. In this prophetic indictment, the word serves to underscore the utter violation of Jerusalem's security and sovereignty, emphasizing the moral atrocity of Edom's indifference and eventual alignment with pagan invaders during the tragic collapse of their brother nation's defenses.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the Ancient Near East, a city’s gate (shaʿar) was its most vital architectural and functional feature. Because ancient cities were encircled by formidable defensive walls, the gate represented both the primary point of vulnerability and the centerpiece of municipal strength. Massive wooden doors reinforced with metal bars were mounted within fortified gatehouses, guarded continuously against military incursions.

Beyond its military function, the city gate served as the heart of public life. Civic leaders, elders, and judges gathered at the gate to hold court, settle disputes, and finalize binding legal transactions. Merchants established markets in the spacious plazas adjacent to the entryways. Consequently, to possess or control the gate of a city was to hold dominion over its people, commerce, and governance. In prophetic literature, foreign armies entering or destroying a city's gates signified the total breakdown of civic order and defensive security.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shaʿar refers to an opening or gate designated for passage through a wall, fence, or boundary enclosure. Standard Hebrew lexicons classify its senses across three primary categories:

  • Physical Architecture: The actual entryway or gateway structure of a compound, court, tabernacle, or city wall (e.g., Exodus 35:17).

  • Legal and Civic Sphere: The formal assembly place at the city entrance where legal cases were argued, official deeds witnessed, and public proclamations declared (e.g., Genesis 23:18; Amos 5:15).

  • Metonymy for the City or Nation: A representative term referring to the city as a whole, its population, or its internal security (e.g., Deuteronomy 17:2).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, shaʿar is employed to describe varied administrative, spatial, and military contexts. In administrative contexts, the phrase "in the gate" consistently denotes a place of legal accountability and civic leadership, as seen in Proverbs 31:31 where community recognition occurs at the gate.

In military narrative and prophecy, the state of a city's gates reflects its national status before God. Open and secure gates symbolize peace and divine favor, whereas breached, burned, or captured gates signify divine judgment and national vulnerability. The morphology of shaʿar in Obadiah 1:11 presents the word in the masculine plural absolute, denoting the multiple entryways and fortified gate complexes of Jerusalem that were breached simultaneously as hostile forces swept into the capital.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the gates of Jerusalem held unique covenantal weight. Jerusalem was the chosen city where Yahweh placed His name, and its gates represented the border between the holy sanctuary-city and the surrounding nations. When God permitted foreign forces to enter Jerusalem's gates, it was an execution of covenant warnings resulting from persistent national unfaithfulness.

However, Obadiah's prophecy focuses on the covenant responsibility of neighboring nations—specifically Edom, descendants of Esau—toward Israel, the descendants of Jacob. The entry of pagan invaders through Jerusalem's gates exposed the betrayal of Edom. Instead of aiding their kin or lamenting the breach of Yahweh's city, Edom stood aloof and approved of the destruction. The breach of the gates thus serves as a theological baseline in Obadiah: human distress and judgment on God's people do not grant license for neighbors to act with malice, opportunistic greed, or gloating indifference.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:11 forms part of a formal divine indictment against Edom. The prophet reminds Edom of their conduct on "the day that you stood on the other side"—a reference to the time when foreign armies invaded Judah, carried off its wealth, and cast lots for Jerusalem.

In this verse, the clause "When foreigners entered his gates" marks the turning point of Jerusalem's downfall. The entrance of pagan strangers through the gates meant that the city's outer defenses had failed, military resistance had ended, and the sacred precinct was completely exposed to pillage. Obadiah brings the charge home with the severe conclusion: "Even you were as one of them." By watching passively as hostile forces crossed the threshold of Jerusalem's gates—and subsequently taking advantage of the ruin—Edom rendered themselves morally equivalent to the foreign invaders. The word shaʿar highlights the exact spatial context where Jerusalem's defense gave way to tragedy, framing Edom's treachery in stark relief.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 23:18 — "to Abraham as a possession in the presence of the sons of Heth, before all who went in at the gate of his city."

This legal passage illustrates the gate (shaʿar) as the recognized public setting for binding civil transactions and witness testimonies in the early patriarchal period.

  • Deuteronomy 17:2 — "If there is found among you, within any of your gates which the LORD your God gives you, a man or a woman who has been wicked in the sight of the LORD your God..."

Here, the term is used metonymically to refer to any Israelite town or civic settlement under Yahweh's covenant governance.

  • Amos 5:15 — "Hate evil, love good; Establish justice in the gate. It may be that the LORD God of hosts Will be gracious to the remnant of Joseph."

Amos employs the term to designate the place of judicial ruling, calling Israel to reform the legal corruption occurring within their municipal court systems at the gate.

  • Proverbs 31:31 — "Give her of the fruit of her hands, And let her own works praise her in the gates."

In wisdom literature, the gate represents the public sphere of community evaluation and praise where civic leaders gather.

  • Micah 1:9 — "For her wounds are incurable. For it has come to Judah; It has come to the gate of my people—To Jerusalem."

Micah uses the same Hebrew word prophetically to portray invading judgment reaching the very threshold and entrance of the capital city.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The betrayal at the gates in Obadiah 1:11 stands in stark thematic contrast to prophetic promises regarding the ultimate restoration of God's people. While Obadiah details a dark historical moment when hostile foreigners breached Jerusalem's gates while Edom watched, subsequent prophetic literature anticipates a day when Zion's gates will no longer fall to hostile entry or treachery. The breach described by the target word highlights the fragility of human security and earthly alliances, reinforcing that true protection for the city of God rests not on physical gateways or human brotherhood, but on Yahweh's sovereign covenant faithfulness.
