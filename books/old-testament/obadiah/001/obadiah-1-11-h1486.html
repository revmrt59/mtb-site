LOTS - ( goral , Strong's H1486 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun goral refers to a small stone, pebble, or tablet used to cast lots to determine the divine will, distribute property, or decide a course of action. In a broader sense, it came to mean one's assigned portion, destiny, or inheritance. In Obadiah 1:11, it describes foreign invaders dividing the plunder and captive population of Jerusalem as though they were mere spoils of war, highlighting the tragic degradation of God's covenant people and Edom's wicked complicity in their humiliation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, casting lots was a widespread method of decision-making. The goral was a physical object—a pebble, piece of wood, or small stone clay tablet—that was marked, colored, or inscribed. These objects were placed into a vessel, shaken, and then cast or drawn out. The physical properties of the stone determined the choice, but the ancient mind understood that the outcome was directed by a higher power. In Israel, the casting of lots was not viewed as a matter of blind chance, but as a mechanism through which God revealed His sovereign will. Over time, the physical object (goral) became a metaphor for the portion or destiny that the lot determined, much like the English word "lot" can refer to a piece of land or one's destiny in life.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Goral denotes a pebble used for casting lots. The word carries two primary definitions across its Old Testament occurrences:

• The literal object used in casting lots to make a decision, distribute land, or choose individuals for specific tasks.

• The figurative allotment, share, portion, or destiny assigned to a person or group, whether beneficial (such as an inheritance of land) or detrimental (such as a portion of judgment).

The action of casting lots is frequently paired with verbs meaning "to throw" (yarah), "to cast" (naphal), or "to cast down" (yadad). In Obadiah 1:11, the verb yadad is used, depicting the aggressive throwing down of the stones to divide the spoils.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs goral in several distinct contexts to paint vivid theological pictures:

• The Day of Atonement: Two lots were cast over two goats to determine which would be sacrificed to Yahweh and which would be the scapegoat (azazel) driven into the wilderness. This demonstrated God's direct selection of the animal that would bear the sins of the nation.

• The Division of the Promised Land: Joshua divided the land of Canaan among the tribes of Israel by lot. Here, goral transitions from the physical stone cast in Shiloh to the actual geographic territory assigned to each tribe.

• The Destiny of the Righteous and Wicked: The word pictures one’s ultimate portion in life. For the believer, God Himself is the portion and maintains their "lot." For the wicked, their "lot" is a cup of judgment and destruction.

• The Spoils of War: Conquering soldiers routinely cast lots to divide plundered wealth, garments, and enslaved captives. This usage conveys a cold, dehumanizing process where human lives are treated as worthless game pieces.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of goral rests on the absolute sovereignty of God. Proverbs 16:33 establishes that while the lot is cast by human hands, its decision belongs entirely to Yahweh. Therefore, when the land of promise was distributed by lot, it was received not as a human conquest, but as a divine gift. Conversely, when foreign nations cast lots over Jerusalem, it represented a profound theological crisis. God’s holy city and His covenant people—His own special possession—were being treated as common plunder by pagan empires. This represents the temporary suspension of Israel's physical inheritance due to covenant unfaithfulness, allowing the Gentiles to trample their "lot."

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the prophet confronts Edom for their treacherous behavior during a foreign invasion of Jerusalem. Edom, though descended from Jacob's brother Esau, stood aloof as passive spectators while "foreigners entered his gates and cast lots for Jerusalem." The casting of lots here refers to the systematic partitioning of Jerusalem's riches and the enslavement of its citizens. The conquering soldiers threw down their stones to see who would claim which captive and which treasure. Edom's guilt was sealed because they watched this pagan desecration and approved of it; they "were as one of them." Instead of acting as a brotherly protector, Edom shared the predatory mindset of the invaders, gloating over the dismantling of Judah's divine allotment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Leviticus 16:8 - "Then Aaron shall cast lots for the two goats: one lot for the LORD and the other lot for the scapegoat."

• Joshua 18:10 - "Then Joshua cast lots for them in Shiloh before the LORD, and there Joshua divided the land to the children of Israel according to their divisions."

• Psalm 16:5 - "O LORD, You are the portion of my inheritance and my cup; You maintain my lot."

• Proverbs 16:33 - "The lot is cast into the lap, But its every decision is from the LORD."

• Joel 3:3 - "They have cast lots for My people, They have given a boy as payment for a harlot, And sold a girl for wine, that they may drink."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The casting of lots over human beings is a recurring motif of extreme conquest and humiliation in the Minor Prophets. Nahum 3:10 describes the fall of No-Amon (Thebes), where "they cast lots for her honorable men, and all her great men were bound in chains." This pagan practice stands in stark contrast to the biblical ideal of human dignity. For Edom, the tragedy of Obadiah 1:11 is compounded by the law of divine retribution. Because Edom rejoiced when Jerusalem’s lot was cast away, Edom would face a similar fate. Their pride would be brought low, and their own inheritance would eventually be possessed by others, proving that God remains the ultimate arbiter of every nation's destiny.
