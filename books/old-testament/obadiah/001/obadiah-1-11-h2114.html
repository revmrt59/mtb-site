STRANGERS - ( zar , Strong's H2114 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word zar refers to someone who is an outsider, foreigner, or alien, often carrying the hostile connotation of an enemy, intruder, or illegitimate claimant. In the context of Obadiah 1:11, it describes the foreign Babylonian forces who plundered Jerusalem's wealth while Edom, Israel's brother nation, stood by in passive and active betrayal.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word zar is derived from the root zur, which carries the primary concept of turning aside, turning away, or becoming estranged.

• In ancient Near Eastern culture, kinship, boundary lines, and covenant loyalties were paramount to survival and identity. A zar is not merely someone from another town; it is someone who stands outside the boundaries of family, covenant, and nation. Their presence often signals danger, pollution, or hostile invasion.

• This term stands in stark contrast to ger (the resident alien who respects the host community and seeks shelter under its laws). The zar is an outsider who does not respect boundaries, entering instead to usurp, desecrate, or plunder.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The word functions as an adjective or noun meaning "foreign," "strange," "unauthorized," "outsider," or "enemy."

• In ritual and legal contexts, it denotes an "unauthorized person" who is barred from sacred spaces or duties. For instance, it describes the "strange" (unauthorized) fire offered by Nadab and Abihu.

• In relational and marital contexts, it refers to an adulterous spouse or a seductive outsider who violates the marriage covenant.

• In national and military contexts, it refers to hostile foreign invaders who ransack a city and strip it of its resources.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Hostile Conqueror: In the prophets, the plural form zarim regularly depicts foreign nations (such as Babylon or Assyria) sent as instruments of judgment. They ruthlessly strip the land of its harvest and heritage.

• The Unauthorized Offerer: In the Pentateuch, the term designates anyone who is not of the line of Aaron attempting to perform priestly duties. They are "strangers" to the altar.

• The Strange Woman: In the Wisdom Literature of Proverbs, the feminine form zarah refers to the forbidden, immoral woman who coaxes a young man away from his covenant commitments.

4. Theological Implications

• God established clear boundaries for His people, His land, and His sanctuary. The intrusion of the zar represents a violent breach of these divinely ordained boundaries.

• In Obadiah, the theological tragedy is not simply that zarim (strangers) plundered Jerusalem, but that Edom (descendants of Esau, Jacob’s brother) behaved exactly like these zarim. Instead of acting as family, Edom chose the status of a hostile outsider.

• This highlights the gravity of covenant betrayal. When God's people or their close relatives align with "strangers" against the covenant family, they participate in the desecration of God's inheritance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:11, the prophet condemns Edom’s behavior during the Babylonian siege of Jerusalem in 586 BC.

• The passage states: "In the day that you stood on the other side—In the day that strangers carried captive his forces, and foreigners entered his gates..."

• The "strangers" here are the Babylonians. They had no covenant relationship with Yahweh or Israel. Their plunder of Jerusalem, while tragic, was expected of foreign enemies.

• However, Edom’s sin was that they "stood on the other side," acting as passive spectators and eventual accomplices. By doing so, Edom, who should have acted as a brother, became "like one of them" (Obadiah 1:11b). The contrast between the natural "stranger" (the Babylonian invader) and the brother who acts like a stranger (Edom) intensifies the moral outrage of Edom’s betrayal.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Job 19:15 - "Those who dwell in my house, and my maidservants, count me as a stranger; I am an alien in their sight."

• Psalm 54:3 - "For strangers have risen up against me, And oppressors seek after my life; They have not set God before them. Selah"

• Isaiah 1:7 - "Your country is desolate, Your cities are burned with fire; Strangers devour your land in your presence; And it is desolate, as overthrown by strangers."

• Hosea 7:9 - "Strangers have devoured his strength, But he does not know it; Yes, gray hairs are here and there on him, Yet he does not know it."

• Lamentations 5:2 - "Our inheritance has been turned over to aliens, And our houses to strangers."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The root zur also has a homonym meaning "to press or squeeze" (used of binding a wound), but the noun zar (stranger) is firmly linked to the concept of turning aside and becoming estranged.

• In the ancient world, to be a zar meant possessing no legal rights or protections within a foreign community unless specifically granted by a host. Thus, a zar entering a city with force was inherently viewed as an invading enemy rather than a peaceful traveler.

• Edom’s alignment with these zarim shows the ultimate breakdown of fraternal obligations (the "brotherly covenant" mentioned in Amos 1:9). Edom traded their brotherhood with Jacob for a partnership with destructive outsiders, resulting in their own ultimate judgment.
