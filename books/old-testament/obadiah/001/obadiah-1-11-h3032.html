CAST - ( yadad , Strong's H3032 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yadad carries the primary meaning of throwing, hurling, or casting. In the context of Obadiah 1:11, it specifically refers to the casual, indifferent casting of lots by foreign invaders to divide the spoils, wealth, and captive inhabitants of Jerusalem. This term underscores the devastating humiliation of God's covenant people, whose lives and sacred heritage were treated as mere commodities. Edom's silent, approving presence during this act of desecration condemned them as active accomplices in the eyes of God, providing a sobering pastoral warning about the sin of passive indifference in the face of injustice.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew root yadad paints a physical picture of forcefully throwing, pitching, or hurling an object. In ancient warfare and cultural practices, "casting" was often associated with throwing small pebbles, pieces of wood, or marked stones—known as lots—onto the ground or into a helmet to make decisions or divide spoils. The physical action of shaking and throwing these items was believed to reveal divine will or fate. In the context of military conquest, casting lots was the final act of subduing a city, where victors gathered to divide the surviving population and the wealth of the land like mere objects.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yadad means to cast, throw, or hurl. It is a rare root in the Hebrew Bible, appearing only three times, and is exclusively paired with the Hebrew noun goral (lot) to form the expression "to cast lots" (yaddû gôrāl). It denotes a deliberate, physical action of throwing or tossing markers to determine ownership or destiny. The semantic range of this root emphasizes the physical motion of throwing, which contrasts with other Hebrew terms for casting lots that focus more on the falling or choosing of the lot itself.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Dividing Human Lives: The primary word picture associated with yadad is the treating of human beings as property. When conquerors cast lots, they reduced honorable men, women, and children to numbers, throwing dice to decide who would become whose slave.

• The Toss of Indifference: The physical motion of tossing the stones implies an attitude of cheapness and trivialization. The sacred heritage of God’s people, which was of infinite value to Him, was treated as nothing more than a casual prize of war to be distributed by a random throw.

• The Desecration of Holy Space: Casting lots over Jerusalem represents the violent partitioning of the city where God placed His name. The pagan conquerors threw their markers over the holy gates, claiming ownership of what belonged to Yahweh.

4. Theological Implications

• The Grief of God Over His People: The use of yadad reveals how deeply the Lord feels the humiliation of His people. God notes the exact manner in which the nations mistreated Israel, recording that they treated His chosen inheritance as plunder.

• The Guilt of the Silent Onlooker: Edom did not physically throw the lots, yet Obadiah declares that because they stood by and watched, they were "as one of them." In the economy of God's justice, silent approval of evil makes one a partner in that evil. Believers must realize that passive indifference in the presence of suffering is a grave sin.

• God's Ultimate Sovereignty: While pagan soldiers threw their stones, believing chance or their false gods governed the outcome, Scripture demonstrates that God remains the ultimate judge. The very book of Obadiah is a declaration of judgment against those who cast lots, proving that those who mock God’s heritage will ultimately face His righteous hand.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the prophet vividly reconstructs the tragic day when foreign invaders entered the gates of Jerusalem. As the Babylonians (or other hostile forces) "cast lots for Jerusalem," they were physically dividing the city's nobility and wealth. Edom, who should have acted as a brotherly protector due to their descent from Esau (Jacob's brother), stood aloof. The nuance of yadad in this specific verse is the extreme contrast between the frantic, desperate suffering of Judah and the cold, calculated, almost playful action of the invaders throwing lots. Edom’s quiet satisfaction as they watched this disrespectful division of their brother's house cemented their spiritual betrayal and guaranteed their impending judgment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Joel 3:3 - "They have cast lots for My people, Have given a boy as payment for a harlot, And sold a girl for wine, that they may drink."

• Nahum 3:10 - "Yet she was carried away, she went into captivity; Her young children also were dashed to pieces at the head of every street; They cast lots for her honorable men, And all her great men were bound in chains."

• Obadiah 1:11 - "In the day that you stood on the other side—In the day that strangers carried captive his forces, When foreigners entered his gates And cast lots for Jerusalem—Even you were as one of them."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• An underlying linguistic tension exists between yadad (to cast) and the noun yad (hand), suggesting a thematic connection. The hands that should have been lifted in prayer or extended to help a brother were instead used to hurl the lots of destruction.

• Ancient Near Eastern historical records confirm that the division of captives by lot was a standard, brutal practice of victorious armies. By utilizing this specific verb, the biblical writers depict the raw, historical reality of captive exile, stripping away any romanticized view of ancient warfare.

• This passage serves as a powerful homiletical reminder of the contrast between the world's standard of survival of the fittest (where the weak are cast lots for) and the kingdom of God (where the weak are protected and defended).
