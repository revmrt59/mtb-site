DAY - ( yom , Strong's H3117 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word yom (day) in Obadiah 1:11 refers to a specific, historical period of crisis, calamity, and judgment. Rather than a literal twenty-four-hour cycle, it denotes a momentous, defining time of visitation—specifically, the tragic fall of Jerusalem to foreign invaders. Edom’s failure to act and their active betrayal of their brother Jacob during this critical "day" of distress highlights the severe breach of covenant brotherliness, marking a pivotal moment of divine indictment against them.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primary concrete word picture behind yom is the cycle of light, beginning with the warmth of the rising sun and ending with its setting. It is the basic unit of lived time in the ancient agricultural world, representing the space of opportunity, labor, and life. Culturally and historically, however, yom expanded beyond chronological time to represent a qualitative season of destiny. In ancient Near Eastern thought, a "day" of battle or a "day" of a city was the defining moment when its fate was decided. In the prophetic mind, a "day" is the appointed hour when God enters human history to execute judgment or bring deliverance, transforming human time into an arena of divine encounter.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew noun yom possesses a flexible semantic range determined strictly by its context:

• The daylight hours (as opposed to night).

• A literal twenty-four-hour cycle (evening and morning).

• A generic, undefined period of time, season, or era (e.g., "in the day of old").

• A specific, designated point in history marked by a significant event, particularly an event of battle, defeat, or judgment (e.g., "the day of Jerusalem").

In Obadiah 1:11, yom falls squarely into the fourth category. It identifies the historical moment of Jerusalem’s catastrophic breach and plunder by the Babylonians.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, yom is frequently paired with qualifying terms to paint vivid theological pictures:

• The Day of Trouble / Distress: A metaphor for a time of intense pressure, confinement, and helplessness.

• The Day of Midian: Used in Isaiah to evoke the memory of a sudden, divinely orchestrated victory.

• The Day of Jerusalem: A tragic designation for the historical moment when the holy city was exposed, plundered, and leveled by foreign adversaries.

In these contexts, "day" ceases to be a mere calendar date; it becomes a monument of memory—either of God's saving power or of the crushing consequences of sin.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yom in Obadiah serves as a local precursor to the ultimate yom YHWH (the Day of the LORD) introduced in Obadiah 1:15. The historical "day" of Judah’s judgment at the hands of the Babylonians reveals God’s holiness and His refusal to spare even His own covenant people when they persist in rebellion. However, Edom’s malicious joy and active exploitation of Judah during this "day" guarantees their own future "day" of reckoning. The theological principle is clear: God observes how nations and individuals behave during the "day" of their neighbor's vulnerability, and He will render a perfectly mirrored justice on the great Day of the Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, yom is used twice in rapid succession: "In the day that you stood on the other side—In the day that strangers carried captive his forces..." This repetition functions as a courtroom indictment. It establishes the precise timing of Edom’s guilt. Edom did not merely drift away during a time of peace; they actively chose to stand aloof and join the plunderers at the exact moment of Jacob’s greatest agony. The use of yom emphasizes that Edom's sin was an act of deliberate, opportunistic treachery during a highly visible, critical crisis.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:4 - "This is the history of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens..." (Representing an epoch of creation).

• Psalm 137:7 - "Remember, O LORD, against the sons of Edom The day of Jerusalem, Who said, 'Raze it, raze it, To its very foundation!'" (A direct historical parallel to the crisis of Obadiah 1:11).

• Obadiah 1:12 - "But you should not have gazed on the day of your brother In the day of his captivity; Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah In the day of their destruction; Nor should you have spoken proudly In the day of distress." (A relentless rhetorical repetition of yom highlighting Edom's specific crimes).

• Joel 1:15 - "Alas for the day! For the day of the LORD is at hand; It shall come as destruction from the Almighty." (The eschatological development of the term).

7. Other Relevant Insights

The literary structure of Obadiah 1:11-14 is built upon a rhythmic, haunting drumbeat of the word yom. It occurs ten times in these four verses. This poetic repetition forces the reader to feel the agonizing, unfolding duration of Judah's tragedy. It systematically dismantles any excuse of ignorance or passivity on Edom's part. Each mention of the "day" of calamity corresponds to a specific command that Edom violated, demonstrating that God keeps a precise, detailed record of how the vulnerable are treated during their moments of trial.
