JERUSALEM - ( Yerushalaim , Strong's H3389 )

Summary:

Jerusalem, meaning "foundation of peace" or "city of peace," is the covenant capital of Judah, the earthly dwelling place of God's presence, and the focal point of redemptive history. In Obadiah 1:11, the city represents the ultimate target of foreign invasion and Edom's treacherous betrayal. Rather than acting as a brotherly neighbor, Edom stood aloof while pagan invaders cast lots for the city's spoils. This turning point highlights Jerusalem not just as a political capital, but as the sanctuary of God's chosen people, making Edom’s apathy and opportunism an offense against God Himself.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Yerushalaim is ancient, with roots likely combining the elements yarah (meaning to lay a foundation, cast, or establish) and shalem (meaning peace, completeness, or wholeness). Thus, the name paints a beautiful word picture: "founded in peace" or "the possession of peace." Geographically, it was situated in the rugged Judean mountains, flanked by deep valleys (Kidron, Hinnom, and Tyropoeon), which provided excellent natural defenses. Historically, it was initially a Jebusite stronghold known as Jebus. King David conquered the city, established it as his royal capital, and brought the Ark of the Covenant there, transforming it into the spiritual and political heart of Israel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper noun refers specifically to the historical city of Jerusalem. In the Hebrew Scriptures, it functions as more than a mere geographical marker. It carries a heavy metonymic and theological weight:

• It denotes the physical capital of the southern kingdom of Judah and the seat of the Davidic dynasty.

• It represents the entire covenant community of God's people, often addressed collectively in prophetic warnings and promises.

• It identifies the earthly sanctuary of Yahweh, localized in the Temple on Mount Moriah, where God chose to put His name.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, Jerusalem is depicted with various vivid anthropomorphic and symbolic descriptions:

• The Daughter of Zion: This portrait emphasizes God's tender, protective relationship with the city and her inhabitants, casting her as a daughter or a bride.

• A Sanctuary of Safety: The prophets frequently picture Jerusalem as a secure refuge, a "quiet home," or a mountain that cannot be moved, symbolizing the absolute security of those who trust in Yahweh.

• A Cup of Drunkenness / A Heavy Stone: In late prophetic literature, Jerusalem is portrayed as an instrument of divine judgment against hostile nations who attempt to manipulate or destroy her.

4. Theological Implications

Jerusalem sits at the absolute center of biblical theology. It is the earthly copy of the heavenly assembly, the place where God's holiness met human sin through the sacrificial system. The Davidic Covenant tied the eternal dynasty of the Messiah directly to this city. Therefore, the fate of Jerusalem is never a matter of secular geopolitical tragedy; it is always a matter of divine covenantal discipline or redemption. When Jerusalem is attacked, God’s own honor and covenant promises are brought into public question among the pagan nations.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, Jerusalem is the victim of brutal violation. The prophet describes a historical crisis where "foreigners entered his gates and cast lots for Jerusalem." Casting lots was a pagan practice used to divide captive populations, strip the city's wealth, and partition the land as spoils of war.

• Edom's sin in this context is egregious because of their kinship. Edom was descended from Esau, the twin brother of Jacob (Judah's ancestor).

• Instead of acting as a protective brother, Edom "stood on the other side" in cold apathy, gloated over the disaster, and eventually joined in the plunder.

• By casting lots for Jerusalem, the invaders desecrated the very city God had claimed as His own. Edom's alignment with these pagan invaders ("Even you were as one of them") constituted an act of treason not only against their brother Jacob but against Yahweh Himself.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 122:6 - "Pray for the peace of Jerusalem: 'May they prosper who love you.'"

• Isaiah 52:1 - "Awake, awake! Put on your strength, O Zion; Put on your beautiful garments, O Jerusalem, the holy city! For the uncircumcised and the unclean Shall no longer come to you."

• Joel 3:17 - "So you shall know that I am the LORD your God, Dwelling in Zion My holy mountain. Then Jerusalem shall be holy, And no aliens shall ever pass through her again."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The tragic irony of Edom’s betrayal of Jerusalem is highlighted by subsequent biblical history. While Edom celebrated the fall of Jerusalem and sought to occupy the vacant lands of Judah, God promised that Jerusalem would be rebuilt and restored, whereas Edom would face permanent desolation. Obadiah's prophecy demonstrates that God keeps a careful ledger of how the world treats His covenant people and His holy city. The modern believer is reminded that God is intensely jealous for His church, the spiritual temple, and will vindicate His people against all who gloat over their trials.
