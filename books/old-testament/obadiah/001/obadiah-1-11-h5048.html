ON THE OTHER SIDE - ( neged , Strong's H5048 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word neged (frequently occurring with the preposition min as minneged) denotes a spatial position directly opposite, in front of, or at a distance. In Obadiah 1:11, it describes Edom's choice to stand aloof on "the other side" while invaders plundered Jerusalem. This word highlights the sin of passive indifference, illustrating how standing by as a silent spectator during a brother's crisis is viewed by God as active complicity with the enemy.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew term neged originates from a root meaning to stand out, be conspicuous, or bring to the front. Physically, it describes a spatial relationship where one object is directly in front of, opposite to, or facing another. It implies a clear line of sight; to be neged someone is to be positioned where you can see them clearly and they can see you. When prefixed with the preposition min (yielding minneged), the focus shifts to standing at a distance or on the opposite side while still remaining an eyewitness. The word picture is not of someone who is absent or ignorant of a situation, but of someone who deliberately positions themselves at a distance to watch events unfold without getting physically involved.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Neged functions as a noun meaning "front" or "counterpart," and as a preposition meaning "before," "in the presence of," or "opposite to." In its compound form minneged, it carries the lexical meaning of "from opposite," "at a distance," "aloof," or "on the other side." It denotes a state of separation that is deliberate and visual. The semantic range spans from physical proximity (standing directly opposite) to relational alienation (remaining aloof during a crisis). It does not mean mere physical distance, but a conscious decision to remain apart from a specific occurrence or person while maintaining a direct view of them.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs neged to convey deep relational dynamics through spatial descriptions:

• In Genesis 2:18, God resolves to make a helper "comparable to" (kenegdo, literally "as opposite him" or "corresponding to him") Adam, representing a perfect, face-to-face counterpart.

• In Genesis 21:16, Hagar leaves her starving child and sits "opposite him" (minneged) at a distance of a bowshot. She cannot bear to watch him die, yet her position "opposite" him shows her eyes are still fixed on his plight.

• In 2 Kings 2:7, the sons of the prophets stand "facing them at a distance" (minneged) to watch Elijah and Elisha cross the Jordan.

These usages demonstrate that neged refers to a deliberate posture of observation. You are close enough to comprehend the reality of the situation, but you choose to maintain a boundary.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, neged reveals God’s standard for relational accountability. In the covenant community, there is no category for benign neutrality. To stand minneged—on the other side or aloof—when a brother is suffering is not a neutral act; it is a hostile choice. God judges passivity in the face of injustice as a sin of omission that aligns the spectator with the oppressor. This anticipates the ethical teachings of the New Testament, where walking by "on the other side" (as the priest and Levite did in Luke 10:31-32) is condemned. God demands active, interventionist love rather than distant, comfortable observation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the prophet uses minneged to expose the deep betrayal of Edom during the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem. Edom was descended from Esau, making them the brother nation to Israel (descended from Jacob). When the Babylonians breached the walls of Jerusalem, Edom "stood on the other side." They did not march with Babylon initially, but they positioned themselves close enough to watch the carnage with malicious glee. Their "standing aloof" was a calculated refusal to offer aid to their brother. This passive stance quickly deteriorated into active malice as they celebrated Judah's ruin and eventually intercepted fleeing Judeans to hand them over to the enemy (v. 14). The term convicts Edom of violating fraternal duty by choosing the posture of a distant, uncaring spectator.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 21:16 - "Then she went and sat down opposite him, a great way off, about a bowshot; for she said to herself, 'Let me not see the death of the boy.' So she sat opposite him, and lifted up her voice and wept."

• Psalm 38:11 - "My loved ones and my friends stand aloof from my plague, And my relatives stand afar off."

• 2 Kings 2:7 - "And fifty men of the sons of the prophets went and stood facing them at a distance, while the two of them stood by the Jordan."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint translates minneged in Obadiah 1:11 using the Greek phrase ex enantias, which means "from the opposite side" or "against." This translation highlights that in the ancient Greek understanding, standing on the opposite side of someone in distress is functionally equivalent to standing "against" them. There is a grammatical link between neged and nagid (a ruler or prince—one who stands in front of the people). This reinforces the concept of prominent, public positioning. Edom's refusal to help was not done in secret; it was a public declaration of abandonment, visible to both God and the victims.
