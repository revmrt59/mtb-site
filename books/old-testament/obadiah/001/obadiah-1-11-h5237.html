FOREIGNERS - ( nokri , Strong's H5237 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word nokri refers to someone who is foreign, alien, or belonging to another nation, culture, or family. It stands in contrast to a native-born Israelite and carries connotations of unfamiliarity and alienation from the covenant community. In Obadiah 1:11, nokri describes the pagan invaders who breached the gates of Jerusalem while Edom, Judah's brother nation, stood by and actively participated in their brother's downfall. The word highlights the tragic irony that Edom acted like these distant, hostile foreigners rather than family.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word nokri is derived from the verbal root nakar, which carrying a fascinating double meaning. On one hand, it means "to recognize" or "to regard." On the other hand, it can mean "to act as a stranger" or "to be foreign." Thus, a nokri is literally "the unrecognized one" or "the one who is treated as an outsider."

In ancient Near Eastern society, a foreigner lacked the civic and religious rights of a native citizen. Unlike the ger (the resident alien who sought to assimilate, respect Israel's laws, and often worship Israel's God), the nokri retained allegiance to their own foreign gods, customs, and nations. Because of this, they were viewed with a degree of caution, representing a potential threat to Israel's spiritual purity and national security.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term nokri functions as an adjective or a noun meaning "foreign," "alien," "strange," or "foreigner." It designates someone who belongs to a different land, race, or family.

  • National and Ethnic: It refers to someone from a foreign country (e.g., Egypt, Assyria, or Babylon) who does not belong to the covenant people of Israel.

  • Domestic and Familial: In wisdom literature, it denotes someone outside of one's household or immediate family circle.

  • Spiritual and Religious: It characterizes those who worship foreign gods and remain outside the covenant promises of Yahweh.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture uses nokri to create several vivid word pictures:

  • The Alienated Brother: When a person is alienated from their own family, they are described as becoming a nokri to their flesh and blood, illustrating a profound and painful relational breach.

  • The Seductive Path: In the Book of Proverbs, the "immoral woman" or adulteress is often called the nokriyah (the feminine form of nokri). She is "strange" because she is outside the marriage covenant. Embracing her represents a departure from wisdom and covenant faithfulness.

  • The Corrupting Influence: Foreign gods are often associated with the nokri. To worship these gods is to bring the "foreign" and "unrecognized" into the sacred space reserved exclusively for Yahweh.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nokri represents those who are outside the covenantal boundary of Israel. While God demanded that Israel show kindness to the vulnerable ger (resident alien), the nokri was subject to different civil regulations (such as being required to pay back debts during the Sabbath year) because they did not share the same covenant obligations.

However, the presence of the nokri in the land was often a barometer of Israel’s spiritual health. When Israel was faithful, foreigners came to marvel at God's wisdom and Temple. When Israel was unfaithful, God used nokrim (foreigners) as instruments of judgment, allowing them to invade, conquer, and rule over the promised land. Ultimately, God's redemptive plan contains a promise that even the nokri who binds himself to Yahweh will be welcomed into His house of prayer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the prophet delivers a stinging indictment against Edom. During the Babylonian invasion and the subsequent fall of Jerusalem, hostile pagan forces—the nokrim—breached the gates of Jerusalem, plundered its wealth, and cast lots for its captive citizens.

The contextual sting of this passage lies in the contrast between these nokrim and Edom. Edom was descended from Esau, the twin brother of Jacob (Israel). Nationally and ethnically, Edom was family, not nokri. Yet, instead of defending their brother, Edom "stood on the other side." They gloated over Judah's calamity and actively helped the invaders.

By using the word nokri, Obadiah highlights Edom's utter betrayal. The pagan invaders acted like foreigners because they were foreigners. Edom, however, had no excuse. Yet, the Lord declares to Edom: "Even you were as one of them." Edom’s treacherous behavior erased their family bond and aligned them spiritually and morally with the godless nokrim.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Danger of Passivity: Edom’s descent into acting like a nokri began with passive indifference. By simply standing "on the other side" and watching their brother suffer, they crossed a moral line. Passivity in the face of a brother's suffering is, in the eyes of God, equivalent to active hostility.

  • A New Testament Reversal: The spiritual alienation represented by nokri is answered in the New Testament. In Ephesians 2:19, Paul tells Gentile believers—who were once "strangers and foreigners"—that through the blood of Christ they are "no longer strangers and foreigners, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God." What Edom threw away by choice, the global Church has been granted by grace.
