STOOD - ( 'amad, Strong's H5975 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amad fundamentally means to stand, remain, endure, or take one's stand. In the context of Obadiah 1:11, it describes Edom’s passive and hostile posture of standing aloof while their brother Jacob (Israel) was being plundered by foreign invaders. Rather than acting as a brotherly protector, Edom chose to stand on the "other side," a posture of cold indifference that God equated with active participation in the violence.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb 'amad conveys the physical act of standing upright, as opposed to sitting, lying down, or falling. It suggests stability, presence, and positioning. Historically and culturally, where and how one "stood" in the ancient Near East was a public declaration of allegiance, readiness, or opposition. To "stand on the other side" ('amad minneged) is a spatial idiom denoting deliberate distance, neutrality, or hostility when action is required. It conjures the image of a relative watching a family member get assaulted, refusing to help, and waiting to see how they can profit from the tragedy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of 'amad is to take a stand, stand still, stop, or remain standing. In its various verbal stems, it can mean to establish, appoint, maintain, or cause to stand. In the Qal stem, which is used in Obadiah 1:11, it indicates a physical position or a state of remaining in a certain place. It carries the nuance of taking up a specific position, whether for defense, judgment, or, in this case, silent, complicit observation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• 'amad is used throughout the Old Testament to describe several postures:

• Standing in a physical location to witness an event (Exodus 3:5, standing on holy ground).

• Standing before a superior or king to minister, serve, and wait for orders (1 Kings 17:1, "before whom I stand").

• Standing firm in battle, resisting an enemy, or holding one's ground (Joshua 10:8).

• Standing in judgment or accusation (Psalm 109:6).

In Obadiah 1:11, the word picture is of a spectator standing at a safe, calculated distance, watching a tragedy unfold without intervening. This passive "standing" is contrasted with the active "entering," "looting," and "destroying" done by the foreigners, yet God holds the spectator to the same level of guilt.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of 'amad in Obadiah is profound: God holds His people accountable not only for the evil they commit but also for the good they fail to do. Passive indifference in the face of a brother's suffering is a sin of omission that God judges as a sin of commission. By "standing" on the other side, Edom became "as one of them" (the pagan invaders). Neutrality in the face of injustice is, in God's eyes, complicity with the oppressor.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the prophet addresses Edom, the descendants of Esau, who stood by when Babylon plundered Jerusalem. The phrase "stood on the other side" ('amodka minneged) highlights their proximity and their relationship—they were close enough to help and bound by blood to care, yet they chose to stand aloof. The context contrasts their brotherly obligation with their actual behavior: they watched their brother's captivity, rejoiced over their destruction, and even intercepted fugitives. Their "standing" was not neutral; it was a malicious, silent endorsement of Israel's destruction.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 18:22: "Then the men turned away from there and went toward Sodom, but Abraham still stood before the Lord."

• Exodus 14:13: "And Moses said to the people, 'Do not be afraid. Stand still, and see the salvation of the Lord, which He will accomplish for you today.'"

• 1 Kings 17:1: "And Elijah the Tishbite, of the inhabitants of Gilead, said to Ahab, 'As the Lord God of Israel lives, before whom I stand, there shall not be dew nor rain these years, except at my word.'"

• Psalm 1:5: "Therefore the ungodly shall not stand in the judgment, nor sinners in the congregation of the righteous."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The root 'amad is contrasted in Scripture with naphal (to fall). To "stand" is to survive or endure. In the prophetic literature, those who stand against God's purposes will ultimately fall. Edom stood in haughty judgment over Israel, but God declares that Edom will not stand when His day of judgment comes.
