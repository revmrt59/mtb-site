CAPTIVE - ( shabah , Strong's H7617 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shabah means to take captive, deport, or lead away prisoners of war. In the context of Obadiah 1:11, it describes the tragic subjugation of Jerusalem's forces by foreign invaders, highlighting the passive and treacherous complicity of Edom, who stood by and gloated while their brother Jacob was carried away into exile.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, warfare was absolute and devastating. When a city fell, the victorious army did not merely seize territory; they took the surviving population captive. This practice, represented by the word shabah, served multiple purposes: it provided forced labor for the conquering empire, neutralized future military threats, and served as a visible display of the conqueror’s supreme power. To be carried captive was to be stripped of home, inheritance, family, and personal agency. It was a living death, a state of complete vulnerability and deep humiliation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shabah refers to the act of taking captives, leading away prisoners, or driving away spoil (both human and livestock). In its basic active verbal form, it means to take captive or carry away captive. In the passive form, it means to be taken captive or carried away. The term focuses on the forced removal of people from their native land, placing them under the absolute dominion of a foreign power. It is distinct from simple exile in that shabah emphasizes the physical subjugation, capture, and restraint by an enemy force.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• shabah is consistently used in military contexts to describe the aftermath of a raid or battle.

• The word portrays a picture of bound prisoners being marched away in chains, often stripped of their clothing to maximize their shame and prevent escape.

• It is used to describe the plunder of entire households, where women, children, and livestock are swept up as war booty.

• In poetic and prophetic literature, it represents the ultimate breakdown of societal security, where the strong are brought low and the free are reduced to servitude.

4. Theological Implications

Captivity is the ultimate covenant curse outlined in the Law of Moses. It represents the loss of the Promised Land due to persistent idolatry and disobedience. Despite the tragedy of captivity, Yahweh retains absolute sovereignty over the captors. He uses foreign nations to discipline His people, but He also promises to break the yoke of captivity. The concept of captivity sets the stage for the biblical theme of redemption. The restoration of Israel is frequently described as God "turning back" or "restoring" the captives, symbolizing spiritual liberation and the restoration of covenant blessings.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the prophet condemns Edom for standing aloof "in the day that strangers carried captive his forces." The term "forces" can refer to Jerusalem's wealth, substance, or army. The invaders stripped the city of its strength and led its people away. The contextual sting lies in Edom's relation to Judah. As descendants of Esau, the Edomites were brothers to the descendants of Jacob (Judah). Instead of defending their kin or mourning their captivity, Edom stood by as indifferent spectators, effectively joining the ranks of the hostile invaders. The use of shabah highlights the severe tragedy of Judah's displacement, making Edom’s betrayal all the more egregious.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 14:14: "Now when Abram heard that his brother was taken captive, he armed his three hundred and eighteen trained servants born in his own house, and went in pursuit as far as Dan."

• 1 Samuel 30:2: "and had taken captive the women and those who were there, from small to great; they did not kill anyone, but carried them away and went their way."

• 2 Kings 5:2: "And the Syrians had gone out on raids, and had brought back captive a young girl from the land of Israel, who waited on Naaman’s wife."

• Psalm 137:3: "For there those who carried us away captive asked of us a song, And those who plundered us requested mirth, Saying, 'Sing us one of the songs of Zion!'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound theological and linguistic wordplay in the Hebrew Scriptures between shabah (to take captive) and shub (to return or repent). When God's people return to Him in repentance, He promises to return their captivity, demonstrating that spiritual repentance is the key to physical and existential liberation.
