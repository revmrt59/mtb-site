GATES - ( sha'ar , Strong's H8179 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sha'ar refers to the gate of a city, camp, temple, or palace. In ancient Near Eastern culture, the gate was far more than a mere entry point; it was the vital hub of civic, judicial, economic, and military life. In Obadiah 1:11, the breach of Jerusalem's gates represents the total collapse of her physical defenses, the violation of her national sovereignty, and the exposure of her people to foreign plunder—a tragedy in which Edom stood by as a malicious accomplice rather than a brother.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient world, cities were protected by massive stone walls designed to keep out invaders. The weakest points in these fortifications were the entrances, making the gate (sha'ar) the most heavily fortified, engineered, and defended part of the city. These gates were not simple doors; they were complex, multi-chambered stone structures, often featuring towers, massive wooden doors reinforced with bronze or iron, and internal guardrooms.

Because the gate was the primary point of entry and exit, it naturally became the public square. It was the place where elders gathered to administer justice, where merchants established markets, and where kings addressed their subjects. To control the gate was to control the city itself.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun sha'ar refers to an opening, gate, or entrance of a city, town, palace, temple, or tent. It is derived from a root verb meaning to split open, break through, or calculate.

Lexically, the term represents:

• The literal physical structure of a city gateway, including its posts, bars, and chambers.

• The civic, legislative, and judicial assembly of a city (often synonymous with the leadership of the city).

• Metaphorically, the entrance to the spiritual realms, such as the gates of heaven or the gates of Sheol.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, sha'ar is used in several vivid word pictures:

• The Seat of Justice: Court was held "at the gate." To have a case heard at the gate meant to seek public, legal arbitration before the city's recognized elders.

• Market and Commerce: The gate was the center of economic life. Grain, livestock, and goods were traded at the gate, where transactions could be witnessed and regulated.

• A Symbol of Security: Safe gates meant a secure city. When the bars of a gate were locked, the inhabitants slept in peace; when they were burned or broken, the community was entirely defenseless.

• Metonymy for the City's Population: Sometimes "the gate" refers to the citizens themselves. To say "all the gate of my people" means the entire community.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, gates represent covenant security, divine protection, and spiritual access. God's covenant promise to Abraham included the guarantee that his descendants would "possess the gate of their enemies" (Genesis 22:17), signifying total victory and dominion over those who opposed them.

Conversely, when Israel violated her covenant with Yahweh, the vulnerability of her gates was the direct result of divine judgment. Without God's protective presence, the physical gates, regardless of how thick their stone walls or iron bars were, would easily crumble. In the Psalms, the "gates of Zion" are loved by God because they lead to His holy habitation, representing the place where humanity meets the holy presence of God.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the prophet details the indictment against Edom during the Babylonian invasion of Jerusalem in 586 B.C. The phrase "When foreigners entered his gates" marks the catastrophic climax of Judah's defense. The penetration of the gates meant the enemy had breached the outer walls, rendering the city completely vulnerable to slaughter, captivity, and looting.

For Edom—Judah’s brother nation descended from Esau—to stand "on the other side" and watch the gates fall without offering assistance was an act of treacherous betrayal. By refusing to defend the gates of their brother, and by subsequently entering those same breached gates to participate in the plunder (as noted in verse 13), Edom became morally identical to the pagan conquerors. The fall of Jerusalem's gates exposed not only Judah's vulnerability but also the profound wickedness of Edom's heart.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

Archaeological excavations in Israel (such as at Megiddo, Gezer, and Hazor) have uncovered massive Solomonic-era six-chambered gates. These ruins show that gates were constructed with multiple chambers on either side of a central corridor. In times of peace, these chambers acted as offices, shops, or administrative rooms. In times of war, they were filled with soldiers who would rain arrows, spears, and boiling oil down upon any enemy forces attempting to force their way through the passage. Understanding this architectural reality underscores how devastating it was when the "foreigners entered his gates."
