ENTERED - ( bo' , Strong's H0935 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bo' is a fundamental and highly versatile word meaning "to come," "to enter," or "to go in." In the context of Obadiah 1:11, it denotes the aggressive, unwanted invasion of foreign adversaries breaching the gates of Jerusalem. This entry represents a total violation of covenant security. Rather than standing as a defender of his brother Jacob, Edom watched with passive, gloating approval—and later participated in the plunder—as foreign enemies marched directly into the heart of God's chosen city.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root bo' occurs over 2,500 times in the Old Testament, making it one of the most common verbs. It describes physical movement from one location into another, crossing a boundary or threshold. It is used for the sun setting ("going down"), people entering a tent or house, and armies entering a city. In an ancient Near Eastern context, a city's gates were its primary line of defense, the hub of civic, judicial, and commercial life. For an enemy to "enter" (bo') these gates meant the complete collapse of sovereignty, a devastating breach of safety, and the vulnerability of the city's inhabitants to plunder and captivity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of bo' is to go or come in, to enter. Depending on the conjugation and context, it carries several shades of meaning:

  • In the Qal stem (as in Obadiah 1:11), it means to come in, enter, or go into a specific place. It can refer to entering a land, a house, or a city gate.

  • It expresses the arrival of a specific time, event, or judgment (e.g., "the day comes").

  • In the Hiphil (causative) stem (hebi'), it means to bring in, lead in, or present.

  • In the Hophal (passive) stem, it means to be brought in or introduced.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The word is frequently paired with yatsa' ("to go out") to describe the full range of human activity, leadership, or military campaigns (e.g., "going out and coming in").

  • It is used metaphorically for entering into covenant relationship with God or entering into rest.

  • In judgment contexts, it depicts the arrival of disasters, plagues, or invading armies sent by God as a consequence of covenant unfaithfulness.

4. Theological Implications

The gates of Jerusalem were not merely physical structures; they represented the security of the Davidic kingdom and the earthly dwelling place of God’s presence. When foreign nations "entered" these gates, it signaled divine judgment. God allowed the pagan nations to breach the sacred boundaries because His people had breached His covenant boundaries. For Edom (descendants of Esau), watching their brother Jacob's territory be violated and "entered" by foreigners without offering aid was a severe betrayal. It demonstrated a complete lack of brotherly love (chesed) and a proud, rebellious heart that aligned itself with God's enemies.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the verb ba'u (perfect tense, "they entered") captures a decisive, tragic historical event. The context is one of extreme crisis: Jerusalem is being ransacked, strangers are carrying away its wealth, and foreigners are entering its very gates to cast lots for its spoils. The focus is on Edom's moral failure during this crisis. The passage charges Edom with standing aloof "on the other side" while this hostile entry took place. By doing nothing to stop the invaders—and indeed, rejoicing in the destruction—Edom became morally complicit. The text states, "Even you were as one of them." Edom’s passive observation of the enemies' "entering" was equivalent to active participation in the assault.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 7:7: "So Noah, with his sons, his wife, and his sons’ wives, went into the ark because of the waters of the flood."

  • Joshua 6:19: "But all the silver and gold, and vessels of bronze and iron, are consecrated to the LORD; they shall come into the treasury of the LORD."

  • Psalm 24:7: "Lift up your heads, O you gates! And be lifted up, you everlasting doors! And the King of glory shall come in."

  • Psalm 100:4: "Enter into His gates with thanksgiving, And into His courts with praise. Be thankful to Him, and bless His name."

  • Jeremiah 17:25: "then there shall enter in the gates of this city kings and princes sitting on the throne of David..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between entering gates for worship versus entering gates for destruction is a powerful biblical motif. Psalm 100:4 commands God's people to "enter His gates with thanksgiving." However, due to sin, those same gates are opened to enemies who "entered" to destroy (Obadiah 1:11). This serves as a sobering warning to the modern believer and the church. God values covenant loyalty and holiness over physical structures. When the church compromises its devotion, it loses its divine protection, allowing spiritual enemies to enter and pillage its strength. Furthermore, it reminds us that indifference to the suffering of our brothers and sisters in Christ is viewed by God as active alignment with the enemy.
