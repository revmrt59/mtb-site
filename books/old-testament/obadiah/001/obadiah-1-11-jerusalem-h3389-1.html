JERUSALEM - (Yᵉrûwshâlaim, Strong's H3389)

Summary:

The proper noun yerushalami identifies the ancient city of Jerusalem, the royal capital of the United Kingdom and later the Southern Kingdom of Judah, location of the Solomonic temple, and central geographic symbol of God's covenantal presence with His people. Lexically, it designates this specific historic city situated in the Judean hill country. In Obadiah 1:11, yerushalami refers to the fallen capital under brutal foreign assault and plunder, emphasizing that when foreign invaders cast lots to divide the spoils of God's chosen royal city, Edom—Judah's fraternal neighbor—stood by as a hostile accomplice, thereby violating brotherly duty and spurning Yahweh's covenant center.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The proper noun yerushalami refers to the historic city of Jerusalem, located on the ridge of the Judean mountains between the Mediterranean and Dead Seas. Early in biblical history, the site appears as Salem under King Melchizedek and later as Jebus prior to its capture by David. Following David's conquest of the stronghold of Zion, the city was established as the political capital of the unified monarchy and the central sanctuary of Israel when the Ark of the Covenant was brought within its walls.

In the prophetical literature, Jerusalem represents more than mere political topography. It functions as the earthly residence of Yahweh's named presence, the central locus of Davidic kingship, and the primary earthly administration of God's covenant with Israel. In the vision of the prophet Obadiah, the background is marked by the tragedy of Jerusalem’s conquest and sack by pagan forces, during which the nation of Edom—descendants of Esau, brother of Jacob—complicitly participated in Judah's devastation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yerushalami functions strictly as a proper geographic noun denoting the city of Jerusalem. Etymologically, ancient Semitic roots combined in the name suggest "foundation of Peace" or "foundation of [the god] Shalem," though in canonical usage the name is consistently treated as the proper name of the covenant capital.

Morphologically in Obadiah 1:11, yerushalami appears in the singular number and absolute state. The form identifies the target of the enemy's aggressive act: the physical city and community of Jerusalem upon which foreign forces cast lots. The lexical definition remains firmly rooted in its identification of this specific historical city; its rich theological depth derives not from speculative etymological components, but from the extensive canonical framework attached to Yahweh’s choice of Jerusalem as His dwelling place and throne.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, yerushalami appears across historical accounts, poetic literature, and prophetic books. Its usage spans several key dimensions:

  • Political Capital: As the seat of the Davidic throne, it represents the ruling centre of Judah.

  • Cultic Center: As the location of the Temple, it is the designated place for Israel's annual feasts, sacrifices, and divine encounters.

  • Theological Symbol: In prophetic discourse, Jerusalem embodies the collective spiritual state of the covenant nation, undergoing judgment for idolatry or receiving promises of ultimate restoration.

In contexts of warfare and siege, yerushalami frequently acts as the definitive marker of Judah's political and national survival. When enemies reach or breach Jerusalem, the nation's core security and religious identity are directly threatened.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Jerusalem serves as the earthly footstool of Yahweh's kingdom. God’s choice of Jerusalem was an act of divine election, tying the city to the Davidic Covenant and the dwelling of His glory in the Holy of Holies.

Consequently, an assault upon Jerusalem by foreign nations was an assault against the place where God chose to put His name. When pagan nations conquered Jerusalem, it was not owing to Yahweh's weakness, but was His righteous judgment upon covenant unfaithfulness. However, neighboring nations that gloated over, assisted in, or exploited Jerusalem's collapse incurred severe divine wrath. Because Yahweh pledged covenant love to Zion, the betrayal of Jerusalem by a brother nation like Edom constituted a direct offense against Yahweh Himself.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, the prophet indicts Edom for its treacherous stance during a catastrophic breach of Jerusalem. The text explicitly depicts a scene of foreign invasion: "In the day that strangers carried away captive his forces, When foreigners entered his gates And cast lots for Jerusalem—Even you were as one of them."

The phrase "cast lots for Jerusalem" pictures foreign soldiers casting lots to divide the conquered city's booty, captives, or territories among themselves, reducing the holy city to common plunder. The occurrence of yerushalami at the culmination of this verse heightens the atrocity of Edom's conduct. Edom did not merely remain neutral; by standing on the "other side" and approving the division of Jerusalem's spoils, Edom aligned itself with pagan invaders against its own brother, Jacob. The context demonstrates that Edom’s passive spectator stance morphed into active moral alignment with the destroyers of Yahweh's holy city.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The mention of casting lots over Jerusalem in Obadiah 1:11 highlights the tragic reversal of Israel's history. Whereas Israel originally received the Promised Land divided by lot under Joshua, here pagan strangers cast lots over Israel's most sacred city. Edom's failure to intervene—and subsequent participation in the plunder—serves as the main thematic justification for Obadiah's pronouncement of total doom upon Esau's descendants, proving that contempt for Yahweh's city brings divine retribution.
