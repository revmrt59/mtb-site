STRANGERS - (zûwr, Strong's H2114)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun/adjective zar denotes an outsider, stranger, alien, foreign enemy, or unauthorized person who stands outside a defined covenantal, familial, or legal boundary. Unlike a resident alien (ger) who seeks dwelling and protection under Israel's laws, a zar is typically categorized by non-belonging, unfamiliarity, or active hostility. In Obadiah 1:11, the word describes the foreign military invaders who overran Jerusalem and plundered its wealth. By using zar, Obadiah highlights the tragic contrast between the hostile foreign conquerors and Edom, Israel's brother nation, who stood passively by and joined in the spirit of these enemy outsiders.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root underlying zar carries the core idea of turning aside, departing, or being an outsider. In ancient Near Eastern societies, social and legal status depended heavily on lineage, covenant membership, and tribal affiliation. Anyone outside these established bonds was deemed an outsider.

In Old Testament Hebrew usage, zar functions across several relational contexts. It can denote an alien from a foreign nation, an unauthorized person lacking priestly qualifications to approach holy things, or an outsider to a specific household or marriage covenant. When applied nationally and militarily, the term emphasizes the hostile, invasive nature of foreign powers who possess no rightful claim to Israel's God, land, or sacred heritage.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, zar refers to a "stranger," "foreigner," "alien," or "unauthorized person." Its semantic range encompasses three primary nuances:

  • Foreign Invaders or Nations: Enemies from outside Israel who attack, conquer, or plunder (e.g., hostile foreign armies).

  • Unauthorized Persons: Individuals who do not belong to a specific group, particularly non-Levites or non-priests attempting to handle holy items reserved exclusively for Aaron's line.

  • Estranged or Outside Persons: Individuals who are strangers to a particular household, marriage bond, or intimate relationship.

A crucial distinction exists between zar and ger. While a ger is a foreign resident who commits to live peacefully within Israelite society under covenant law, a zar remains outside covenant obligations, often appearing as a hostile plunderer or an illegitimate claimant when encroaching upon Israel's land or worship.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Hebrew Scriptures, zar reflects various levels of boundary-crossing depending on context:

  • Military and Political Conquest: In prophetic literature, zar frequently describes foreign troops executing judgment by stripping the land, overrunning defenses, and pillaging wealth. Here, the word highlights the harsh reality of foreign oppression.

  • Cultic and Sacred Boundaries: In the Pentateuch, the word identifies anyone outside the designated priesthood who attempts to consume sacred offerings or perform priestly duties. In this sphere, zar underscores the holy boundaries God established to preserve pure worship.

  • Domestic and Personal Relationships: Wisdom and poetic texts use zar to designate outsiders who threaten the integrity of marriage or family property, as well as friends who have grown distant and estranged.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, zar illustrates the distinction between those within God's covenant structure and those outside it. When foreign zarim overrun Israel, it signifies the terrible consequence of covenant unfaithfulness—God allowing foreign nations to breach the land He granted His people.

Furthermore, the word underscores the breach of kinship obligations. In prophetic discourse, foreign enemies (zarim) act according to their nature as pagan conquerors; however, when covenant relatives act like zarim, a profound moral failure occurs. God holds nations accountable not only for foreign oppression but also for covenantal betrayals between kindred peoples.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11, zar appears in the masculine plural absolute form (zarim), referring directly to the pagan foreign invaders who broke through Jerusalem's defenses, captured its forces, and plundered its treasures.

The immediate context sets up a poignant and devastating indictment against Edom. Verse 11 contrasts the actions of these foreign zarim with the conduct of Edom. Edom was descended from Esau, making them the fraternal nation to Israel (descended from Jacob). When foreign enemies—complete strangers to the covenant—invaded Jerusalem and cast lots for its spoils, Edom "stood on the other side" and observed passively, ultimately celebrating Judah's ruin.

Obadiah concludes the verse with the scathing declaration: "Even you were as one of them." By using zarim for the foreign conquerors, Obadiah demonstrates that while the invaders were actual foreign strangers acting as enemies, Edom acted like a stranger and an enemy toward their own brother. The lexical choice exposes Edom's severe breach of brotherly duty, demonstrating that by their neutrality and malicious joy, Edom aligned themselves completely with the pagan plunderers.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 29:33 — "They shall eat those things with which the atonement was made, to atone and to sanctify them; but a stranger shall not eat them, because they are holy."

  • Isaiah 1:7 — "Your country is desolate, Your cities are burned with fire; Strangers devour your land in your presence; And it is desolate, as overthrown by strangers."

  • Ezekiel 28:7 — "Behold, therefore, I will bring strangers against you, The most terrible of the nations; And they shall draw their swords against the beauty of your wisdom, And defile your splendor."

  • Hosea 8:7 — "They sow the wind, And reap the whirlwind. The stalk has no bud; It shall never produce meal. If it should produce, Strangers would swallow it up."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The stark contrast between blood relations and foreign invaders is central to the message of Obadiah. The prophet explicitly reminds Edom of their shared lineage elsewhere in the vision, calling Judah "your brother." When foreign enemies (zarim) strip a nation, it is an expected tragedy of ancient warfare; but when a brother nation stands by and identifies with those foreign invaders, it represents a collapse of natural affection and covenantal responsibility. This makes Edom's apathy and eventual complicity equal to the hostility of the pagan conquerors themselves.
