CHILDREN - (bên, Strong's H1121)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun ben primarily signifies a male child, son, direct offspring, or descendant. Beyond immediate biological parentage, it is widely used in collective contexts to designate members of a family, tribe, or nation sharing common lineage, as well as individuals characterized by a specific relationship or status. In Obadiah 1:12, the word appears in the masculine plural construct form (children of Judah), pointing specifically to the living covenant descendants of Jacob. In this passage, its use heightens the moral gravity of Edom's sin: Edom callously gloated and rejoiced over the tragic calamity and downfall of their own brotherly kin.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The noun ben is one of the most fundamental social and relational terms in the Old Testament text. In the patriarchal culture of the ancient Near East, lineage, inheritance, and the continuation of the family name were tied directly to one's offspring. A ben was not merely an individual family member, but the carrier of the father's name, land rights, covenant identity, and ancestral heritage.

Throughout Israel's history, individual identity was defined in relation to household and tribal origins. Consequently, identifying an individual or group as the "sons" or "children" of a patriarch (such as Israel or Judah) anchored that group within a specific covenantal line and historical community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, ben carries a straightforward primary meaning that expands into well-defined contextual categories:

  • Literal Offspring: A direct male child born to a father or mother (a biological son).

  • Descendants / Posterity: In the plural, "sons" or "children" refers to successive generations or descendants originating from a common ancestor (e.g., descendants of Jacob or Judah).

  • Corporate or National Membership: Used in construct phrases to designate members of a specific nation, city, or community (e.g., "children of Israel").

  • Relational or Character Categorization: Used idiomatically to indicate affiliation, age, or a defining characteristic (e.g., "son of strength" for a warrior, or "son of death" for one doomed to die).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament narrative, law, poetry, and prophecy, ben serves as a primary marker of continuity and community. In historical narratives and genealogies, the term establishes legal lineage, royal succession, and priestly qualification. In legal passages, it defines covenant obligations passed down through family units.

In prophetic literature, ben frequently appears in the plural construct state to address the covenant nation collectively. When prophets speak of the "children of Israel" or "children of Judah," the term invokes the enduring corporate bond between God and the lineage of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The term emphasizes that God's dealings with His people span across generations, binding contemporary listeners to the obligations and promises given to their ancestors.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, ben highlights the generational transmission of God's covenant promises. When God swore an oath to Abraham, He pledged to establish His covenant not only with Abraham individually, but with his offspring after him. Thus, every "son" born within the covenant line was a living recipient and steward of divine promise.

Furthermore, ben underscores corporate solidarity. Scripture views the individual as deeply embedded within the family and covenant nation. The actions of one generation impact the next, and God's judgments or blessings often encompass the entire corporate body of "children." In prophetic contexts, targeting or oppressing the "children" of God's chosen people constitutes an attack on the covenant line through which God revealed His redemptive purpose.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:12, the target word occurs in the masculine plural construct state, directly bound to "Judah" (children of Judah). The short prophecy of Obadiah is directed against the nation of Edom, the descendants of Esau. The overarching theme of the prophecy is the divine condemnation of Edom for their brutal, malicious behavior toward Jerusalem during its time of siege and destruction.

The immediate context of verse 12 sets up a sharp moral rebuke through a series of negative commands: "you should not have gazed... nor should you have rejoiced... nor should you have spoken proudly." The target word ben appears in the second clause: "nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah in the day of their destruction."

By referring to the fallen residents of Jerusalem specifically as the "children of Judah," the text emphasizes three vital realities:

  • Familial Kinship: Obadiah repeatedly reminds Edom of their ancestral ties, calling Judah "your brother" (Obadiah 1:10, 1:12). Edom was descended from Esau, while Judah was descended from Jacob. The "children of Judah" were literally the extended family of the Edomites. Edom's gloating was an act of brotherly betrayal.

  • Covenant Identity: The people suffering defeat were not an anonymous foreign army; they were the living covenant descendants of Judah.

  • Inhumanity of Gloating: Instead of showing compassion to their kin in their darkest hour ("the day of their destruction"), Edom felt malicious joy at the ruin of Jacob's line. The construct form explicitly ties the victims' identity to their honorable heritage, contrasting Judah's covenant status with Edom's cruel gloating.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 46:11 — "The sons of Levi were Gershon, Kohath, and Merari."

  • Here the word denotes direct, immediate biological male offspring within a patriarchal genealogy.

  • Exodus 14:15 — "And the Lord said to Moses, 'Why do you cry to Me? Tell the children of Israel to go forward.'"

  • Here the word refers collectively to the entire covenant nation as descendants of Jacob, highlighting their corporate identity during the Exodus.

  • Exodus 25:22 — "And there I will meet with you, and I will speak with you from above the mercy seat, from between the two cherubim which are on the ark of the Testimony, about everything which I will give you in commandment to the children of Israel."

  • Here the word identifies the recipient community of God's holy law and covenant instruction.

  • 2 Chronicles 20:14 — "Then the Spirit of the Lord came upon Jahaziel the son of Zechariah, the son of Benaiah, the son of Jeiel, the son of Mattaniah, a Levite of the sons of Asaph, in the midst of the assembly."

  • Here the word appears repeatedly to trace ancestral lineage and priestly service across multiple generations.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The prophetic indictment in Obadiah demonstrates that God holds nations accountable for how they treat His covenant people, particularly during times of divine chastisement. While God brought judgment upon the "children of Judah" through foreign invaders due to Judah's sins, Edom had no right to celebrate Judah's agony or participate in their plunder.

Edom mistakenly interpreted Judah's temporal downfall as permanent rejection by God and an opportunity for personal gain. However, God's covenant with the "children of Judah" remained intact. By gloating over the suffering of Jacob's offspring, Edom brought swift divine judgment upon themselves, illustrating the enduring principle that God takes notice when malice is directed toward His chosen lineage.
