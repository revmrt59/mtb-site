CHILDREN - ( ben , Strong's H1121 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ben fundamentally denotes a son, child, or descendant, but its semantic range in Scripture extends far beyond biological lineage to encompass covenant identity, corporate solidarity, and national inheritance. It represents the continuation of a family line, a member of a specific nation or group, and a recipient of a covenantal heritage. In Obadiah 1:12, the "children of Judah" designates the covenant community of God's people suffering under foreign invasion. Edom’s failure to show familial compassion to their "brother" Judah during their destruction highlights a grievous breach of both natural affection and covenantal solidarity, bringing swift divine judgment upon Edom for gloating over their suffering.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near Eastern world, family, lineage, and legacy were central to identity and survival. A son or child (ben) was not merely an individual but the physical representation of the father’s house, carrying forward the family name, property, and spiritual inheritance. The term is etymologically related to the Hebrew verb banah, which means "to build." Children were understood to be the "building blocks" of the household and, by extension, the nation. In a tribal society, to have banim (children) was to have strength, security, and a future. When Obadiah addresses the "children of Judah," he is not referring to toddlers, but to the entire generation of descendants who formed the living house of Judah. Furthermore, because Edom descended from Esau and Judah from Jacob, the children of Judah were the literal kin—the nieces and nephews—of the Edomites, making Edom’s cruelty a profound betrayal of family bonds.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun ben refers primarily to a male offspring or son. In the plural form (banim or the construct benei), it frequently carries a wider, collective meaning:

• Offspring or Descendants: Children of both genders, referring to the posterity of a patriarch (e.g., the "children of Israel" or "children of Judah").

• Member of a Class or Guild: Individuals sharing a common characteristic, profession, or condition (e.g., "sons of the prophets" or "children of affliction").

• Age Designation: Used idiomatically to express age, such as a "son of ninety years" meaning ninety years old.

In Obadiah 1:12, the plural construct benei yehudah ("children of Judah") refers to the citizens and descendants of the Southern Kingdom of Judah, emphasizing their shared covenant origin and their collective vulnerability as the offspring of Jacob.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture frequently employs ben to convey deep spiritual and relational truths. Because the word shares a root with "to build" (banah), the family is pictured as a structure built by God Himself. When the term is applied to a nation, it pictures the citizens as a single household.

• The Household of Faith: By calling the nation the "children of Judah," the text frames the political entity of the Southern Kingdom as a family unit. They are the offspring of promise, heirs to the Davidic covenant.

• The Son as the Father's Representative: In Hebrew thought, a son represents the father. To honor the son is to honor the father; to mistreat the son is to insult the father. Edom’s abuse of the benei yehudah was an insult to Judah’s father, Jacob, and ultimately a rebellion against Judah's God.

### 4. Theological Implications

The usage of ben in the prophets underscores several major theological themes:

• The Continuity of the Covenant: God's promises are not merely to individuals but to their children. The "children of Judah" were heirs to the promises made to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Even when God disciplined them through foreign captivity, they remained His covenant children.

• The Sin of Kinship Betrayal: God holds families accountable for how they treat one another. Edom (Esau) and Judah (Jacob) were twin brothers. By rejoicing over the destruction of the children of Judah, Edom was violating the natural order of brotherly love (brotherhood) and celebrating the ruin of their own flesh and blood.

• Divine Fatherhood and Protection: God views the oppression of His children as a personal attack. Just as a human father rises to defend his children, Yahweh promises to vindicate the "children of Judah" and bring judgment upon those who exploited their day of calamity.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:12, the prophet delivers a series of stinging rebukes to Edom: "But you should not have gazed on the day of your brother in the day of his captivity; nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah in the day of their destruction."

• The use of "children of Judah" alongside "your brother" intensifies the indictment. Edom did not merely watch a foreign empire fall; they gloated over the slaughter and captivity of their own relatives.

• The term highlights the vulnerability of the victims. In the day of their "destruction," the children of Judah were helpless, stripped of their defenses. Instead of acting as a protective older brother, Edom acted as a predator, standing at the crossroads to cut off survivors. The contrast between Edom's arrogant pride and the vulnerability of the "children" of their brother heightens the moral outrage of Edom's sin.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 1:7 - "But the children of Israel were fruitful and increased abundantly, multiplied and grew exceedingly mighty; and the land was filled with them." (Here, benei yisrael refers to the growing family-nation under God's blessing).

• Joshua 15:63 - "As for the Jebusites, the inhabitants of Jerusalem, the children of Judah could not drive them out; but the Jebusites dwell with the children of Judah at Jerusalem to this day." (This verse highlights the historical struggles of the same group, the benei yehudah, in possessing their inheritance).

• Psalm 103:13 - "As a father pities his children, So the LORD pities those who fear Him." (Using the plural banim, this passage reveals the tender, fatherly heart of God toward His weak and failing children).

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The corporate identity embedded in the word ben is essential for understanding biblical prophecy. In Hebrew thought, the individual and the community are deeply intertwined. What Edom did to the "children of Judah" in their day of distress was recorded by God as an act against the entire lineage of His people. This explains why the judgment on Edom was corporate and generational. Because Edom sought to cut off the survivors of the children of Judah, Edom’s own descendants would be cut off forever (Obadiah 1:10). The law of sowing and reaping applies directly to how nations treat the "children" of God's covenant.
