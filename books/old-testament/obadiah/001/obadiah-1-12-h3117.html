DAY - (yôm, Strong's H3117)

Summary:

The Hebrew word yôm refers to a literal twenty-four-hour day, a daylight period, or metaphorically to a specific epoch, season, or historical event characterized by certain conditions. In Obadiah 1:12, it is used repeatedly to mark the specific, tragic time of Jerusalem's fall, representing a day of calamity, judgment, and distress for Judah.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The ancient Near Eastern concept of a "day" (yôm) extends far beyond a mere marking of twenty-four hours on a sundial. A "day" often represented a focal point in time where the true nature of a person, nation, or relationship was brought to light. It is frequently associated with battle, harvest, or covenant reckoning. In poetic and prophetic literature, "the day of" an individual or nation is an idiom for their time of visitation—either in terms of supreme blessing or devastating judgment. It marks a designated, critical season of destiny when normal daily routines halt, and a definitive historical crisis takes center stage.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of yôm is broad but well-defined by its structural context:

  • Literal Daylight: The period of light in contrast to the night.

  • Literal 24-Hour Period: A complete cycle of evening and morning.

  • A Indefinite Period of Time: An era, year, or lifespan (often translated as "days" or "years" in the plural yamim).

  • A Point in Time: A specific day on which a particular event occurs.

  • A Day of Doom or Judgment: A specific, historically bounded period of tragedy, defeat, or divine wrath. In Obadiah 1:12, the word is modified by genitive constructs ("day of your brother," "day of his captivity," "day of their destruction," "day of distress"), indicating a specific, agonizing historical period of judgment experienced by the southern kingdom of Judah.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In Hebrew thought, the "day" of an enemy or a brother is a word picture for their hour of vulnerability. In Obadiah 1:12, yôm functions as a metonymy for the actual events of defeat, deportation, and slaughter occurring during that time. The repetition of yôm creates a rhythmic, driving drumbeat of tragedy. By linking yôm with "captivity," "destruction," and "distress," the prophet portrays the day not as a neutral passing of time, but as an active, hostile environment of suffering. The day is pictured as a stage upon which Edom's treachery is laid bare before God.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, every human yôm of judgment points forward to the ultimate yôm YHWH (the Day of the Lord). The historical "day" of Judah’s captivity at the hands of the Babylonians—which Edom celebrated—was a local, historical day of divine chastisement upon God's covenant people. However, this event sets the stage for a theological reversal. The minor prophets routinely warn that those who gloat over the "day" of God's people will face their own "day" of reckoning. Edom’s malicious joy during Judah’s day of distress guaranteed that Edom would eventually experience its own day of inescapable judgment when the sovereign Lord rights all wrongs.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:12, yôm is repeated four times (and continues into verses 13 and 14, totaling ten occurrences of the word in this brief passage). This rhetorical saturation emphasizes the relentless nature of Judah’s suffering and the systematic nature of Edom's betrayal.

  • "The day of your brother" emphasizes the familial covenant bond that Edom violated; Jacob and Esau were twin brothers, yet Edom stood aloof.

  • "The day of his captivity" highlights the physical displacement and exile of the people.

  • "The day of their destruction" marks the violent end of their political and religious security.

  • "The day of distress" points to the inner anguish and terror of the survivors.

The contextual nuance reveals that Edom did not merely watch a historical event; they actively feasted their eyes upon, rejoiced in, and profited from their brother's defined window of catastrophic ruin.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:5: "God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night."

  • Joel 2:1: "For the day of the LORD is coming, / For it is at hand:"

  • Amos 5:18: "Woe to you who desire the day of the LORD! / For what good is the day of the LORD to you? / It will be darkness, and not light."

  • Obadiah 1:15: "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Hebrew text, the phrase "day of your brother" (yôm ahika) is highly unusual and deeply poignant. Normally, Hebrew writers would refer to the "day of Jerusalem" or the "day of Judah." By calling it the "day of your brother," the text strips away political terminology and appeals to familial obligation. This highlights the ethical dimension of time: how one treats a brother during his "day" of vulnerability is the ultimate measure of one's righteousness and covenant faithfulness.
