NOT - ( 'al , Strong's H408 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle 'al functions as a subjective, deprecatory, or prohibitive "not," distinct from the absolute, objective negation of lo'. While lo' expresses an absolute "no" or permanent prohibition, 'al conveys a passionate, urgent, or emotional plea, often translated as "do not," "let not," or in the context of retrospective regret, "should not." In Obadiah 1:12–14, 'al is repeated eight times in a series of urgent rhetorical prohibitions, warning Edom against gloating over, exploiting, and participating in the destruction of their brother Jacob (Judah). It serves as a powerful divine indictment, highlighting the moral tragedy of sibling betrayal and God's absolute intolerance of prideful malice.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The particle 'al is a primitive word of negation. Unlike a cold, clinical, or legalistic negation, 'al is deeply tied to immediate circumstances, expressing a deprecation, a wish, or an emotional appeal. It is the language of petition ("do not do this!") and urgent warning.

  • In the ancient Near Eastern context of family obligations, sibling nations (such as Edom, descended from Esau, and Judah, descended from Jacob) were bound by a covenant of brotherhood. The background of Obadiah is the Babylonian sack of Jerusalem, where Edom did not merely stand aloof but actively rejoiced and participated in Judah's ruin. The emotional force of 'al carries the weight of shattered family bonds and divine grief over heartless betrayal.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The particle 'al is an adverb of negation used to express deprecation, prohibition, or a negative wish.

  • It negates the jussive or imperfect aspect of Hebrew verbs, creating a subjective prohibition ("let not," "do not").

  • In retrospective or rhetorical contexts, it indicates what ought not to have occurred, translated in the NKJV as "should not."

  • Unlike lo', which represents absolute, objective, and permanent negation ("you shall not"), 'al represents immediate, situational, and emotionally charged prohibition.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • In Hebrew syntax, 'al creates a vivid picture of a hand raised to stop an action in progress or to prevent an imminent moral disaster. It is an active barrier.

  • When paired with verbs of sight (ra'ah), joy (samach), or speech (gadal), 'al acts as a spiritual brake. It demands that the eyes stop gazing with malicious pleasure, the heart stop rejoicing over calamity, and the mouth stop boasting.

  • The repetition of 'al eight times in Obadiah 1:12-14 creates a cascading wave of divine disapproval. It functions like a repeated, echoing "Stop!" or "You must not!" shouted over the scene of plunder and betrayal.

4. Theological Implications

  • God’s Moral Standard for Relationships: The use of 'al demonstrates that God does not merely judge outward actions but the inward posture of the heart. Gloating over a brother's fall is as offensive to God as physical violence.

  • The Retributive Justice of God: God’s use of prohibitive warnings after the fact (rhetorical retrospect) shows that human choices are recorded in heaven. Edom’s failure to heed the moral obligation of brotherhood invited the very destruction they celebrated in Judah.

  • The Character of God as Defender of the Oppressed: God identifies with the suffering of His people. To gloat over Judah is to mock the God who chastises Judah.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Obadiah 1:12, the particle 'al occurs three times (and eight times total in verses 12-14). The NKJV translates these occurrences as "should not have..."

  • Although the events had already taken place, the prophet uses the imperfect tense with 'al to place the reader directly into the moment of decision. It paints the scene as if Edom is standing on the ridge watching Jerusalem fall, and God is urgently pleading, "Do not look! Do not rejoice! Do not speak proudly!"

  • The nuance is not a mere historical record of what they did wrong, but a dramatic, living reenactment that exposes the raw malice of Edom's heart. It highlights that their actions were a direct violation of natural affection and covenantal proximity.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 13:8: "So Abram said to Lot, 'Please let there be no strife between you and me, and between my herdsmen and your herdsmen; for we are brethren.'"

  • Genesis 37:22: "And Reuben said to them, 'Shed no blood, but cast him into this pit which is in the wilderness, and do not lay a hand on him'—that he might deliver him out of their hands, and bring him back to his father."

  • Ruth 1:16: "But Ruth said: 'Entreat me not to leave you, Or to turn back from following after you; For wherever you go, I will go; And wherever you lodge, I will lodge; Your people shall be my people, And your God, my God.'"

  • Psalm 51:11: "Do not cast me away from Your presence, And do not take Your Holy Spirit from me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The structural beauty of Obadiah 1:12-14 relies on the rhythmic cadence of 'al. This is a literary device known as anaphora (the repetition of a word or phrase at the beginning of successive clauses). This poetic repetition builds tension, mimicking a parent desperately shouting warnings to a child heading toward a cliff.

  • In the Septuagint (LXX), 'al in Obadiah 1:12 is translated using the Greek negative particle mede ("neither/nor") with the subjunctive, capturing the subjective and prohibitive force of the Hebrew original.
