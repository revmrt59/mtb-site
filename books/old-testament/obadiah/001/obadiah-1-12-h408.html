NOT - ( 'al , Strong's H0408 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle 'al is an urgent, deprecatory adverb used to express an immediate warning, prohibition, or plea. Unlike the absolute, permanent negative lo' (found in the Ten Commandments), 'al is intensely personal, situational, and emotional—carrying the force of "do not," "let not," or "should not." In Obadiah's severe indictment of Edom, this word functions as a divine veto and a moral barrier. God stands in the path of Edom’s malicious joy over Judah's fall, pleading with and commanding them to stop their betrayal. It reveals that God places active boundaries against human cruelty, particularly when directed toward a brother in distress.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew, 'al functions not merely as an abstract grammatical negative, but as an active intervention. It is the language of a parent warning a child away from danger, or a friend pleading with another to stop a ruinous course of action. It possesses an immediate, situational quality. In the historical and poetic context of Obadiah, it serves as a passionate divine warning. Although Edom's treachery had already occurred historically, the prophet uses 'al repeatedly in a series of eight prohibitions to vividly recreate the scenes of Edom's sin. It paints a picture of God standing in the path of Edom's cruelty, raising His hand and crying out, "Stop! Do not proceed any further down this path of malice!"

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The particle 'al is a subjective negative adverb indicating deprecation, prohibition, or wishful negation. It is used primarily with jussive or imperfect verbs to express a situational "do not," "let not," or "should not." While the alternative negative lo' establishes absolute, objective, and permanent prohibitions, 'al expresses an immediate, emotional appeal. It denotes a strong desire to prevent an action from occurring or continuing, carrying the semantic force of "refrain from," "spare," "desist," or "do not allow."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The particle occurs frequently across the Old Testament to convey urgent, personal appeals:

• Pleas of desperation: When Abraham pleads for the sparing of Sodom, he says, "Let not the Lord be angry" (Genesis 18:30). Here, 'al conveys deep reverence and a plea for restraint.

• Warnings of wisdom: In the wisdom literature, it cautions against foolish paths: "Do not enter the path of the wicked" (Proverbs 4:14).

• Restraint in anger: It is used by the psalmist to beg for mercy: "O LORD, do not rebuke me in Your anger" (Psalm 6:1).

The word picture is that of a boundary line drawn in the sand—an emotional and moral fence designed to halt destructive behavior before it crosses into an irreparable offense.

4. Theological Implications

The repetitive use of 'al in Obadiah reveals the heart of God regarding brotherly love and covenant loyalty. Edom was descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother. God's standard of holiness does not tolerate passive pleasure in another's pain, especially when that person is a brother. The theological reality of 'al is that God sets active boundaries for human behavior. Gloating over a brother’s ruin is an offense to the Creator. When God says "should not," He is declaring that human actions are not morally neutral; taking joy in the suffering of others draws the immediate gaze and judgment of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:12, 'al is repeated eight times in rapid succession (verses 12 to 14) to build a powerful rhetorical crescendo. The NKJV translates this sequence of 'al with the imperfect verb as "you should not have..." because the prophet is looking back at Edom’s actual conduct during the Babylonian conquest of Jerusalem (586 BC) while projecting a present, living warning. The nuance here is one of profound moral outrage. God outlines exactly where Edom crossed the line: gazing on Judah's disaster, rejoicing over their destruction, speaking proudly in their distress, entering their gates to plunder, and standing at the crossroads to cut off survivors. The use of 'al turns a historical record into a vivid, courtroom-style prosecution, making it clear that Edom's actions were deliberate violations of divine expectations.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 18:30 - "Then he said, 'Oh, let not the Lord be angry, and I will speak...'"

• Ruth 1:16 - "But Ruth said: 'Entreat me not to leave you, Or to turn back from following after you...'"

• Psalm 6:1 - "O LORD, do not rebuke me in Your anger, Nor chasten me in Your hot displeasure."

• Proverbs 3:27 - "Do not withhold good from those to whom it is due, When it is in the power of your hand to do so."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The distinction between lo' and 'al is vital for expositors. While lo' represents the objective, immutable standard of divine law (the "shall not" of cosmic order), 'al represents the personal, relational appeal of the divine heart. In Obadiah, God is not merely citing an abstract statute; He is appealing to the familial relationship that should have existed between Edom (Esau) and Judah (Jacob). The tragedy of Edom was their refusal to heed this relational "do not," choosing instead to actively participate in their brother’s annihilation, which ultimately sealed their own national doom.
