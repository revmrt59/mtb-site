CAPTIVITY - ( neker, Strong's H5235 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun neker in Obadiah 1:12, rendered as "captivity" in the NKJV, fundamentally denotes a foreign, alien, or disastrous event—specifically a sudden calamity or misfortune that renders someone a stranger in their own land or subjects them to foreign invaders. In the context of Obadiah, it highlights the tragic day of Judah's downfall when they were treated as foreigners, subjected to ruin, and exiled, while their brother nation Edom watched with malicious glee.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root n-k-r carries the primary idea of being foreign, unrecognizable, or alienated. It is the same root from which we get nekar (stranger or foreigner) and nokri (alien).

• When applied as a noun (neker), the word paints a vivid picture of a sudden, catastrophic shift where everything familiar is swept away. It represents a day of estrangement—a time when a person or nation is treated as an alien, cast out of their home, or subjected to a foreign power's cruelty.

• Historically, this refers to the Babylonian invasion and destruction of Jerusalem (586 BC), where the citizens of Judah were alienated from their land, stripped of their covenant privileges temporarily, and dragged into exile.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The term neker refers to a calamity, disaster, misfortune, or an alien fate.

• While many English translations render it as "calamity," "misfortune," or "disaster," the NKJV translates it as "captivity" to emphasize the historical reality of Judah being carried away by foreigners.

• Lexically, the word denotes a strange, adverse occurrence or an extraordinary judgment that alienates the sufferer from their normal state of peace and security.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In the Old Testament, the root is used to describe things that are foreign or strange, such as foreign gods or foreign lands.

• As a noun meaning calamity, neker represents the ultimate "alien experience" for God's covenant people. To be in a state of neker is to be alienated from God’s blessings, the Promised Land, and covenant security, experiencing instead the harsh reality of foreign oppression.

• It serves as a stark word picture of sudden reversal: the heirs of the promise becoming strangers and captives in a foreign land.

4. Theological Implications

• Neker underscores the reality of divine judgment. When God’s people repeatedly violated the covenant, God allowed them to experience the "strange" and "alien" fate of exile.

• The word exposes the sin of Edom. Edom, descended from Esau, was Jacob’s (Judah’s) brother. Instead of showing brotherly compassion during Judah's neker (calamity/captivity), Edom gloated. This highlights the theological principle of brotherly obligation and the gravity of rejoicing over another's ruin. God fiercely judges those who gloat over the chastisement of His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:12, the prophet delivers a series of rhetorical prohibitions to Edom: "But you should not have gazed... In the day of his captivity [neker]."

• The parallel expressions in verse 12 clarify the nuance of neker: it is synonymous with "the day of their destruction" and "the day of distress."

• By translating neker as "captivity," the NKJV highlights the physical consequence of the calamity: the Judeans being taken as captives by foreign invaders. Edom stood by, gloating at the sight of their brother being treated as a foreign captive, failing to recognize that they shared a common ancestry.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Obadiah 1:12 – "But you should not have gazed on the day of your brother / In the day of his captivity; / Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah / In the day of their destruction; / Nor should you have spoken proudly / In the day of distress."

• Job 31:3 – "Is it not destruction for the wicked, / And disaster for the workers of iniquity?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Septuagint (LXX) translates neker in Obadiah 1:12 using the Greek word allotrion, which literally means "belonging to another" or "foreigners." This confirms the core Hebrew concept of alienation and foreignness—Judah was experiencing "the day of his becoming a foreigner/stranger" through exile and captivity.

• The tragedy of the "day of his captivity" was compounded by Edom's proximity and shared bloodline. Edom’s joy at Judah’s alienation from their land was a betrayal of the fraternal relationship that God had commanded Israel to respect (Deuteronomy 23:7).
