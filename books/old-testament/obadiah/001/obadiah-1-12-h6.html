DESTRUCTION - ( 'obed , Strong's H6 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun 'obed signifies utter ruin, perishing, and catastrophic destruction. In Obadiah 1:12, it refers to the tragic collapse and historical downfall of Judah and Jerusalem under the Babylonian invasion. Instead of showing brotherly compassion during this dark hour, the nation of Edom rejoiced over Judah’s ruin, demonstrating a wicked pride that ultimately invited God's righteous wrath upon themselves.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The noun 'obed originates from the active Hebrew verb 'abad, which carries the graphic image of wandering away, losing oneself, perishing, or being utterly destroyed.

• In the ancient Near Eastern pastoral context, the root word evokes the picture of a sheep that has wandered away from the flock, lost its path, and is left to perish in the wilderness, vulnerable to wild beasts and the elements.

• When applied to cities, nations, or peoples, the word picture shifts from a lost sheep to a state of complete, irreversible ruin. It represents a physical collapse where structures are reduced to rubble and a population's historical existence is severely threatened or ended.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The noun 'obed means destruction, ruin, or a state of perishing. It refers to the physical and political annihilation of a group or nation.

• As a rare noun derived from the verb 'abad (which means to perish, die, or be lost), 'obed isolates the final, tragic state of ruin itself rather than just the process of decaying or wandering.

• The term denotes a catastrophic end of prosperity, power, and security, leaving behind nothing but devastation and loss.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Because 'obed is an extremely rare noun in the Hebrew Bible, its occurrences carry significant prophetic weight. It is reserved for major, historical turning points where nations face complete devastation.

• The word portrays a finality that excludes any immediate hope of self-restoration. It is a dry, barren wasteland of defeat where once there was life, culture, and strength.

• The word picture in prophetic literature is often associated with the sovereign judgment of Yahweh, who brings about the 'obed of proud and rebellious nations by using foreign armies as His instruments of wrath.

4. Theological Implications

• Theological focus in Obadiah centers on the sin of schadenfreude—rejoicing in the misfortune, ruin, and 'obed of others. Edom and Judah shared a common ancestry through Jacob and Esau, making them brother nations. Edom’s joy at Judah’s destruction was a direct violation of brotherly covenant obligations.

• God's judgment is sovereign, but He holds neighboring nations accountable for how they respond to the discipline of His people. When God disciplines Judah, Edom should have reacted with fear, sobriety, and mercy. Instead, their malicious joy exposed their own deep-seated pride and hatred.

• The passage demonstrates that God defends the dignity of His people even in their moments of divine discipline. Those who gloat over the 'obed of God's people will eventually experience an even greater, final 'obed themselves.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:12, 'obed describes the Babylonian sack of Jerusalem in 586 B.C. The city was burned, the temple destroyed, and the citizens slaughtered or carried into exile.

• The prophet uses a series of prohibitions ("you should not have...") to condemn Edom's specific actions on that day. Edom did not merely stand aloof; they actively gloated, rejoiced, and even participated in cutting off survivors.

• The phrase "in the day of their destruction" highlights the extremity of Judah's suffering. By rejoicing on this specific day, Edom treated the horrific ruin of their brother as a spectator sport and a political victory, sealing their own doom under the same standard of divine justice.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Numbers 24:20: "Then he looked on Amalek, and he took up his oracle and said: 'Amalek was first among the nations, But his latter end shall be destruction.'"

• Numbers 24:24: "But ships shall come from the coast of Cyprus, And they shall afflict Asshur and afflict Eber, And so shall he perish forever." [Note: The Hebrew phrase translated "perish forever" literally reads "unto destruction" ('adey 'obed), indicating a state of permanent ruin.]

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The lexical link between Obadiah 1:12 and Balaam's prophecies in Numbers 24 is highly significant. In both cases, 'obed is used to describe the absolute, historical end of a rebellious, anti-God nation (Amalek and Edom).

• The Greek Septuagint (LXX) translates 'obed in Obadiah 1:12 using the noun apoleia, which means ruin, waste, or perdition. This same Greek word is used in the New Testament to describe the eternal destruction of the wicked (as in Matthew 7:13 and Philippians 3:19), underscoring the ultimate, spiritual gravity of turning away from God.
