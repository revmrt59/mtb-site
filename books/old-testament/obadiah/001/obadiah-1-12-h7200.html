GAZED - ( ra'ah , Strong's H7200 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ra'ah refers to the physical act of seeing, looking, or beholding. While it is the standard Old Testament term for physical sight, its specific usage in Obadiah 1:12 conveys a deep, malicious quality. In this context, it describes looking upon another's misfortune with smug satisfaction, gloating, or voyeuristic pleasure. Rather than turning away in grief or stepping in to offer assistance, the Edomites stood by and feasted their eyes on the destruction of their brother nation, Judah.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete root of ra'ah originates with the physical function of the eye focusing on an external object. In ancient Semitic thought, looking was rarely considered a passive or detached sensory experience. Instead, looking was deeply connected to the heart, representing an active engagement of the mind, will, and emotions. To gaze upon someone in distress without offering aid was to participate actively in their humiliation. In the ancient Near East, staring at a defeated enemy was a formal gesture of dominance and contempt. In Obadiah, this word paints a picture of Edom acting as a cold, approving spectator to Judah's tragedy, transforming a basic sensory action into an act of active betrayal.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ra'ah possesses a broad semantic range, spanning from simple sensory perception to deep spiritual insight. At its core, it means to perceive with the eyes, to observe, or to look at. When paired with the Hebrew preposition be (looking upon or at), the verb frequently shifts to mean looking with gloating, satisfaction, or triumphant joy at an enemy's ruin. It can also signify to inspect, to consider, to choose, or to experience. In Obadiah 1:12, the word is used in a prohibition ("you should not have gazed"), indicating a malicious, celebratory observation of Jerusalem's downfall.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament scripture, ra'ah is employed to illustrate various postures of the human and divine heart:

  • The Look of Compassion: God "seeing" the affliction of His people in bondage and being moved to deliver them.

  • The Look of Lust or Covetousness: Seeing an object of desire, allowing the eyes to linger, and then taking it.

  • The Look of Gloating: Standing at a distance to watch an opponent fall with a sense of superior pride and vindication.

  • The Look of Provision: God "seeing" or "foreseeing" a need and providing for it, as seen in the naming of Jehovah-Jireh (The Lord Will Provide/See to It).

4. Theological Implications

This term exposes the severe spiritual danger of schadenfreude—finding sinful joy or satisfaction in the calamity of others. God holds Edom morally accountable not merely for their active violence, but for their passive, approving gaze. In divine law, indifference to a brother's suffering is treated as a hostile act. Because God is the ultimate Judge who sees all things, He closely monitors the visual posture of our hearts. This passage warns that God observes how we look at those who fall, and He will execute judgment on those who treat the suffering of others as entertainment or self-validation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:12, the prophet delivers a series of rhetorical prohibitions ("should not have") to expose Edom’s moral failures during the Babylonian invasion of Jerusalem. The pairing of ra'ah with "the day of your brother" intensifies the gravity of their sin. Edom and Judah shared a ancestral bond as descendants of Jacob and Esau. Family obligations demanded grief and intervention. Instead, Edom stood aloof, "gazing" on Judah's disaster. This gaze was not one of shock, but of cold, deliberate pleasure that validated the Babylonian destruction and set the stage for Edom's subsequent looting and capturing of Jewish fugitives.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 3:7 - "And the Lord said: 'I have surely seen the oppression of My people who are in Egypt, and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters, for I know their sorrows;'"

  • Psalm 22:17 - "I can count all my bones. They look and stare at me."

  • Psalm 118:7 - "The Lord is for me among those who help me; Therefore I shall see my desire on those who hate me."

  • Obadiah 1:13 - "You should not have entered the gate of My people In the day of their calamity. Indeed, you should not have looked on their affliction In the day of their calamity, Nor laid hands on their substance In the day of their calamity;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The visual terminology in the book of Obadiah contrasts human sight with divine oversight. While Edom smugly gazed at Judah’s ruin, they failed to realize that God's eyes were fixed on them. Scripture establishes a poetic justice regarding this verb: those who look upon the fallen with pride will eventually become a spectacle of judgment themselves, gazed upon by others in their own shame and defeat.
