REJOICED - ( samach , Strong's H8055 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb samach refers to a deep, spontaneous, and often outwardly expressed joy, gladness, or exultation that affects the entire person—heart, face, and voice. In the context of Obadiah 1:12, it describes the malicious pleasure and gloating (Schadenfreude) that the Edomites displayed over the catastrophic defeat and destruction of their brother nation, Judah. Instead of showing sibling compassion, Edom actively rejoiced in Judah's ruin, prompting God's severe warning and subsequent judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew root samach is associated with brightness, light, and swelling or expanding. Physically, it describes a person whose face lights up, whose eyes shine, or whose heart swells with gladness. It is the opposite of a fallen, dark, or sorrowful countenance. In ancient Near Eastern societies, emotional responses were communal and public. Joy was shared, and grief was accompanied by corporate lamentation. To experience samach was to participate in a visible, external celebration of life, favor, or victory.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb samach means to rejoice, be glad, exult, or be joyful. It describes a state of mind and body that rejoices in positive circumstances, relationships, or divine actions. The semantic range spans from simple contentment to ecstatic, festive celebration. In contexts where it is paired with the preposition be (in/over) or el (at/over) and directed toward another person's misfortune, it shifts semantically from positive gladness to malicious satisfaction, mockery, and gloating.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Heart's Gladness: The heart is the primary seat of samach. This indicates that true Hebrew joy is not a superficial mask but originates deep within the inner man before manifesting externally.

• Cultic and Festival Joy: It is frequently used for Israel's annual feasts and assemblies before Yahweh, where rejoicing was a commanded act of worship in response to God's covenant blessings.

• Malicious Rejoicing: In wisdom literature and the prophets, samach is sometimes used negatively to depict the wicked celebrating the downfall of the righteous or enemies mocking God's people.

4. Theological Implications

Joy is not morally neutral in Scripture; its righteousness depends entirely on its object. God is the ultimate source and object of healthy joy. When humans rejoice in God's salvation, justice, and character, they align with His design. Conversely, rejoicing in evil, injustice, or the suffering of others is a severe sin. It demonstrates an alignment with the forces of chaos and rebellion. God holds humans accountable for their emotional attitudes toward their neighbors. Malicious joy (Schadenfreude) invites divine justice, as it treats another's tragedy as a personal triumph.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:12, the prophet addresses Edom, the sibling nation of Judah. When Babylon invaded Jerusalem, Edom did not merely stand by; they actively participated in and celebrated Judah's downfall. The use of samach in "Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah" highlights the moral depravity of Edom's joy. Their gladness was a cruel celebration of a brother's ruin, violating the familial bond established since the days of Jacob and Esau. God states that this gloating is unacceptable, warning them that their wicked joy will be answered by their own absolute destruction.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 4:14: "Is not Aaron the Levite your brother? I know that he can speak well. And look, he is also coming out to meet you. When he sees you, he will be glad in his heart."

• Psalm 35:24: "Vindicate me, O Lord my God, according to Your righteousness; And let them not rejoice over me."

• Psalm 122:1: "I was glad when they said to me, 'Let us go into the house of the Lord.'"

• Proverbs 24:17: "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls, And do not let your heart be glad when he stumbles;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound spiritual contrast between godly grief and ungodly joy. Edom's laughter during Judah's tears represents a temporary, hollow triumph. The New Testament counterpart of this warning is found in the definition of love in 1 Corinthians 13:6, which does not rejoice in iniquity, but rejoices in the truth. When a believer observes the fall of an enemy or a brother, the biblical response is not samach, but grief and intercession, knowing that the same hand of judgment can easily turn toward those who gloat.
