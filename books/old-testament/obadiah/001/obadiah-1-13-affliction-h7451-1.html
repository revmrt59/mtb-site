AFFLICTION - (raʻ, Strong's H7451)

Summary:

, The Hebrew word raʿ possesses a broad semantic range that encompasses both moral evil (wickedness, badness, malice) and severe physical or circumstantial distress (calamity, harm, affliction, disaster). In Obadiah 1:13, raʿ refers to the profound calamity and suffering that engulfed Jerusalem during its destruction. Within the prophet's argument, the word exposes Edom's callous cruelty; rather than showing brotherly compassion toward Jacob during their time of overwhelming affliction, Edom gloated over Judah's downfall and sought to exploit their ruin for personal gain.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The prophecy of Obadiah addresses the historical animosity between the nation of Edom and the nation of Israel. As descendants of Esau and Jacob respectively, Edom and Israel shared ancient fraternal bonds. However, when foreign invaders laid waste to Jerusalem, Edom failed to come to their brother's aid. Instead, Edom stood aloof, rejoiced over Jerusalem’s devastation, intercepted fugitives, and pillaged the city’s remaining resources.

Obadiah 1:10–14 forms a poetic indictment outlining Edom’s transgressions. In verse 13, the prophet uses a series of negative commands ("You should not...") to condemn Edom’s actions on the day of Jerusalem's fall. The word raʿ appears in this setting to describe the severe crisis and suffering that had overtaken God's covenant people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, raʿ serves as the primary Old Testament term for expressing that which falls short of the ideal, divine order, or natural wholeness. Its lexical range includes two major categories:

  • Circumstantial or Physical Distress: Calamity, disaster, injury, adversity, affliction, misery, or hardship.

  • Moral or Ethical Wrong: Evil, wickedness, viciousness, or malicious intent.

Context determines which dimension of meaning is emphasized. When applied to circumstances, cities, or human experiences, raʿ designates a state of ruin, distress, or severe affliction. In Obadiah 1:13, raʿ carries this circumstantial sense, denoting the catastrophic affliction and distress suffered by Jerusalem under foreign assault.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Hebrew Scriptures, raʿ is used in varied contexts to highlight the realities of brokenness in a fallen world.

  • Functional and Experiential Harm: The term describes dangerous beasts, bad water, ruined land, or grievous illness. It represents that which brings harm or destruction to human well-being.

  • Moral Malice: When applied to actions, intentions, or human heart motives, raʿ indicates rebellion against God’s covenant standards, ethical corruption, or malicious behavior toward others.

  • Sovereign Judgment and Calamity: In prophetic literature, raʿ frequently refers to national disasters or military defeats sent under divine providence as covenant warnings or judgments.

In poetic and prophetic texts, raʿ is often paired with terms for ruin, distress, and day of trouble, highlighting the helpless condition of those under severe trial.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of raʿ in Obadiah centers on divine justice, human accountability, and the obligations of covenant brotherhood.

  • God's Entrustment of Compassionate Duty: God holds nations, particularly those bound by historical or covenant ties, accountable for how they respond to the suffering of others. To gloat over another's raʿ (affliction) is to display a callous disregard for divine image-bearers.

  • The Repression of Covetousness in Times of Tragedy: Exploiting a neighbor’s affliction for material gain transforms a spectator into an accomplice in violence. Edom's gloating and looting in the day of Judah's raʿ incurred the same divine retribution meant for Jerusalem's destroyers.

  • Sovereign Reversal: The prophetic message of Obadiah demonstrates that God observes those who take pleasure in the affliction of His people. The harm and destruction (raʿ) that Edom inflicted upon Judah would ultimately recoil upon Edom's own head under God's righteous judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:13, the prophet uses a rhythmic repetition of the phrase "in the day of their calamity" alongside the word raʿ ("their affliction"). This literary structure intensifies the gravity of Edom's offense:

  • "You should not have entered the gate of My people in the day of their calamity."

  • "Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction (raʿ) in the day of their calamity."

  • "Nor laid hands on their substance in the day of their calamity."

The phrase "gazed on their affliction" points to a malicious, gloating gaze. Rather than turning away in sorrow or stepping in to help, Edom stared with cruel delight at Judah’s devastation. The target word raʿ underscores the severe distress and ruin that Jerusalem was enduring. By gazing on their raʿ with triumph, Edom manifested an unbrotherly hatred that violated natural affection and covenant obligation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Kings 5:4 – "But now the LORD my God has given me rest on every side; there is neither adversary nor evil occurrence."

In this passage, the word describes external harm or disastrous circumstances that disrupt peace and stability.

  • Jeremiah 48:16 – "The calamity of Moab is near at hand, and his affliction comes quickly."

Here, the term is used in a prophetic warning to denote the catastrophic disaster and distress coming upon a nation.

  • Lamentations 1:22 – "Let all their wickedness come before You, and do to them as You have done to me for all my transgressions..."

In this lament over Jerusalem's fall, the word highlights the moral evil and harmful deeds committed by adversaries.

  • Psalms 34:16 – "The face of the LORD is against those who do evil, to cut off the remembrance of them from the earth."

This psalm uses the term to describe those who actively practice moral wrongdoing and inflict harm on others.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The prophet Obadiah highlights a striking irony regarding raʿ. Edom viewed Jerusalem’s affliction as an opportunity for self-aggrandizement, believing that Judah's ruin marked Edom's ultimate security and elevation. However, God’s word through Obadiah reveals that Edom’s malicious joy over Judah’s raʿ was the very catalyst for Edom’s own complete downfall. The evil that Edom celebrated against their brother became the moral grounds for Edom’s own permanent judgment.
