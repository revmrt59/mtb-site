DAY - (yôwm, Strong's H3117)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun yom primarily denotes the daylight hours, a full twenty-four-hour period, or a designated time, season, or appointed occasion. In Obadiah 1:13, yom specifies the precise historical occasion and period of Judah's downfall ("the day of their calamity"). The term contributes to Obadiah's argument by directly linking Edom's opportunistic, predatory acts—entering Jerusalem, gloating over her suffering, and seizing her wealth—to the explicit moment of Israel's deepest crisis. By marking the time of Israel's helplessness, yom underscores the calloused treachery of Edom and establishes the legal and moral grounds for God's impending judgment upon them.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root underlying yom is the foundational Hebrew term used throughout the Old Testament to mark the passage of time. From the creation narrative in Genesis, where light is separated from darkness, yom serves as the basic building block for biblical chronology, corporate memory, and covenantal calendars. In ancient Israelite thought, time was not merely an abstract sequence of moments; specific "days" were characterized by the significant events that occurred within them. Consequently, yom frequently functions beyond its literal twenty-four-hour sense to signify designated seasons, decisive historical events, or appointed times of divine intervention and human crisis.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yom possesses a clear and well-attested semantic range in Biblical Hebrew:

  • Daylight: The period of natural light between sunrise and sunset, as distinct from night.

  • A Twenty-Four-Hour Day: The complete cycle of evening and morning.

  • An Appointed Time or Season: A generalized period marked by specific conditions, activities, or historical circumstances (e.g., "in the day of battle" or "in the day of distress").

  • A Specific Occasion of Crisis or Triumph: A particular historical moment defined by an event, such as a "day of calamity" or "day of salvation."

The lexical meaning itself is straightforward; its conceptual depth in Scripture arises from how the biblical authors qualify yom within specific syntactical constructs and narrative settings.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament narrative, poetry, and prophecy, yom functions as a primary temporal anchor. In historical narratives, it establishes chronology and records historical sequences. In prophetic literature, yom frequently pairs with genitival qualifiers to denote thematic periods of profound theological weight.

When linked to concepts of adversity—such as trouble, ruin, or distress—yom isolates the season of vulnerability experienced by a person or nation. The term does not inherently carry moral evaluation or emotional weight on its own; rather, it provides the temporal frame within which human decisions and divine actions are measured. Whether describing a literal solar day or a broader time of trial, yom identifies the specific reality of an event within human history.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yom highlights God's absolute sovereignty over history and time. Human lives and national fortunes unfold within bounded "days" that are observed and evaluated by the LORD.

In the prophetic books, human "days" of suffering or pride are set against the ultimate horizon of "the Day of the LORD" (yom YHWH). When nations or individuals act wickedly during a neighbor's "day of calamity," they do not act in a vacuum; God registers their conduct within that specific timeframe. Edom's betrayal of Judah during her "day" of crisis reveals the moral responsibility human beings possess toward one another in times of affliction. Scripture demonstrates that how one treats the vulnerable in their "day" of distress directly determines how God will deal with them in His "day" of universal reckoning.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:13, yom is used three times within a single verse, forming part of a repeated, rhythmic indictment that spans verses 12 through 14. The prophet presents a series of prohibitions regarding Edom's conduct when Jerusalem fell: "You should not have entered the gate of My people in the day of their calamity. Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction in the day of their calamity, nor laid hands on their substance in the day of their calamity."

Lexically, yom functions here to define the temporal scope of Edom's transgression. It identifies the moment as "their calamity" (edam)—Judah's period of utter ruin and vulnerability. The repetition of yom does not alter the basic lexical definition of the word, but contextually it creates a relentless focus on the precise timing of Edom's malice. Edom did not merely invade or plunder; they specifically chose to do so in the day when their brother Judah was brought low.

The moral weight of the passage derives from the contrast between Judah's helpless condition during this "day" and Edom's shameless exploitation of it. By anchoring every predatory act (entering, gazing, looting) directly to "the day of their calamity," the text demonstrates that Edom's actions were characterized by extreme treachery and total lack of brotherly covenant love.

  • Jeremiah 16:14 – "'Therefore behold, the days are coming,' says the LORD, 'that it shall no more be said, "The LORD lives who brought up the children of Israel from the land of Egypt..."'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A profound literary and theological connection exists within the short prophecy of Obadiah between the human "day" of crisis and the divine "day" of judgment. In Obadiah 1:13, Edom exploits Judah's "day of calamity" (yom edam). Only two verses later, in Obadiah 1:15, the prophet declares: "For the day of the LORD (yom YHWH) upon all the nations is near; as you have done, it shall be done to you; your reprisal shall return upon your own head."

This explicit thematic link relies on the double use of yom. Edom seized a temporal "day" of advantage to oppress God's covenant people, but that temporal act directly summons the cosmic "Day" of divine justice, wherein God reverses the triumph of the proud and vindicates the afflicted.
