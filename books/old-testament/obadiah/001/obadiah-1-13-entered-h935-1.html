ENTERED - (bôwʼ, Strong's H935)

Summary:

The primary lexical meaning of the Hebrew verb bo' is to enter, come, or arrive, describing physical movement across a threshold or the arrival of a condition or time. In Obadiah 1:13, the word carries a grave indictment, describing Edom's brazen crossing of the gate into God's vulnerable covenant people during Jerusalem's catastrophic fall. Here, bo' highlights how Edom transitioned from an unbrotherly bystander into an active trespasser and participant in Judah's ruin, incurring divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Book of Obadiah delivers a divine indictment against the nation of Edom, the descendants of Esau. Despite their ancient fraternal ties to Israel (descendants of Jacob), Edom repeatedly demonstrated hostility toward their brother nation. This animosity reached its peak during a time of grave crisis for Jerusalem, likely during the Babylonian siege and conquest of the city.

In Obadiah 1:10–14, the prophet delivers a rhythmic series of divine prohibitions ("You should not..."), exposing Edom's moral failures during Judah's collapse. The verb bo' appears in Obadiah 1:13 to mark a critical turning point in Edom's transgression—moving from passive delight over Judah's downfall to physical intrusion into their space and property.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, bo' denotes basic spatial movement: to go in, to enter, to come, or to arrive. It is one of the most common motion verbs in the Hebrew Bible, expressing the movement of a subject from one domain or space into another.

Depending on context, the semantic range of bo' includes:

  • Physical entry into a city, building, home, or boundary.

  • The arrival or onset of a non-physical reality (such as time, terror, blessing, or judgment).

  • Approaching a person or authority figure.

In the Qal stem, the verb simply expresses the basic subject action of coming or entering. In Obadiah 1:13, the Qal imperfect form describes Edom's prohibited action of setting foot inside the gates of Jerusalem during her destruction.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, bo' functions neutrally to describe physical relocation, but its theological and moral nuance is dictated by the domain being entered and the intent of the person entering.

When bo' is paired with spatial boundaries such as a "gate" (sha'ar), it signifies passing through the primary line of civic defense and community life. To enter a peaceful city gate is normal civic interaction; however, to enter the gate of a defeated or besieged city signifies conquest, intrusion, and plunder. Thus, bo' can signify normal arrival, sacred entrance into God's presence, or hostile breach into a vulnerable community.

4. Theological Implications

In the broader canonical context, Edom’s entry into Jerusalem violates both natural family obligation and covenantal order:

  • The Violation of Brotherhood: Scripture repeatedly underscores the shared lineage of Israel and Edom (Deuteronomy 23:7). By entering Jerusalem during her calamity, Edom treated a brother as an enemy, violating the natural obligation of kinship.

  • Trespassing God's Inheritance: In Obadiah 1:13, Yahweh refers to Jerusalem as the gate of "My people" (‘ammi). Edom did not merely cross an earthly boundary; they breached the city belonging to Yahweh's covenant people. Intrusion against God's people is viewed by God as intrusion against Himself.

  • Moral Progression of Sin: The passage displays how sin metastasizes. Edom began by looking on Judah's disaster with approval, escalated to physically entering (bo') their gate, and culminated in plundering their wealth and capturing their fugitives.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:12–14 structures Edom's guilt in a carefully built progression. In verse 12, Edom stands afar, watching and rejoicing over Judah's ruin. In verse 13, the verb bo' signals the physical transition from distant malice to direct invasion: "You should not have entered the gate of My people in the day of their calamity."

The Qal imperfect form functions here in a prohibited past sense (often rendered as "You should not have entered"), expressing Yahweh's retroactive prohibition and moral condemnation of Edom's choice. By passing through the gate, Edom crossed a literal and moral threshold. Once inside the breach, they proceeded to gaze maliciously upon Judah's affliction and stretch out their hands against Judah's substance. The entry (bo') served as the key that unlocked Edom's subsequent violent acts.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The physical act of entering the gate highlights the irony of Edom's self-confidence. Later in Obadiah, God promises that the very pride of heart that emboldened Edom to enter Jerusalem's gates will end in Edom's own gates being overrun. The measure Edom meted out by entering their brother's breach would ultimately come back upon their own head when the day of the LORD arrives upon all nations (Obadiah 1:15).
