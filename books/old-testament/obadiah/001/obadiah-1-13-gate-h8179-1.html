GATE - (shaʻar, Strong's H8179)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun shaʿar primarily refers to a gate—most notably the fortified entrance to a city, court, building, or encampment. Beyond its basic structural sense as an entry point in a defensive wall, the city gate in ancient Israel served as the central hub for public assembly, judicial trials, commercial trading, and civic administration. In Obadiah 1:13, shaʿar designates the entry point of Jerusalem ("the gate of My people"). In this context, the term highlights Edom's shameful breach of covenantal obligations and brotherly duty. Rather than standing as an ally to Judah, Edom physically intruded through Jerusalem's gate during the city's collapse to participate in plundering Yahweh's judgment-stricken people.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Near Eastern culture, walled cities relied heavily upon their gateway systems for military defense, structural stability, and civil order. A shaʿar was rarely a simple doorway; it was typically a complex architectural structure consisting of heavy wooden doors reinforced with metal, flanked by defensive towers, and containing inner guard chambers or alcoves.

Because the space inside and immediately surrounding the gate provided a wide, accessible public area, the shaʿar naturally evolved into the social, legal, and economic center of the community. Kings held court at the gate, elders rendered legal decisions there, merchants traded goods, and prophets delivered public proclamations. Consequently, to control or enter the gate of a city meant gaining access to its life, governance, and resources.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shaʿar denotes an opening or portal through an enclosure wall. Standard Old Testament lexicography establishes its primary senses:

  • An architectural city gate or town portal through which traffic passes and defenses are maintained.

  • An entry portal to a sacred structure, such as the court of the tabernacle or the temple complex.

  • A metonymy for the city itself or the local civil assembly convened at the entry portal.

The word designates the physical location where public boundary, defense, and administration converge.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, shaʿar carries several distinct contextual nuances depending on the environment in which the entrance is depicted:

  • Judicial and Administrative Hub: The gate functioned as the formal legal court where elder councils rendered justice and verified property transactions (e.g., Genesis 23:18). Israel was commanded to execute righteous judgment at the gate (Amos 5:15).

  • Defensive and Military Barrier: As the primary point of vulnerability in a city's fortifications, the gate represented security. Enemies aimed to break through the gate, while defenders strove to hold it (Micah 1:9).

  • Sacred Boundary: In sanctuary and temple settings, the gate marked the transition between common space and consecrated space, regulating access to divine worship (Exodus 35:17; Ezekiel 11:1).

  • Civic Honor and Visibility: Public recognition and community praise were bestowed upon virtuous individuals at the gate (Proverbs 31:31).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the shaʿar symbolizes both divine protection and covenantal accountability. God's oversight extends to the gates of His people, making the gate a place where covenant ethics must be upheld. When justice fails at the gate, the moral integrity of the nation crumbles.

Furthermore, when God permits an enemy to cross through the gate of His covenant city, it signifies severe covenantal judgment. However, those who breach the gate opportunistically to inflict unauthorized cruelty upon God's people incur divine retribution. God guards the boundary of His heritage; invading the gate of His people is viewed not merely as a military maneuver, but as an attack on Yahweh Himself.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:13, the prophet delivers Yahweh's indictment against Edom following the tragic fall of Jerusalem. The target word shaʿar occurs in the phrase "the gate of My people" (shaʿar ʿammi). Morphologically, the noun carries a masculine designation.

The context of Obadiah 1:10–14 contains a rhythmic series of prohibitions addressing Edom's opportunistic betrayals during Judah's ruin ("In the day of their calamity"). Edom, linked historically to Israel through Esau's kinship with Jacob, had a brotherly obligation to aid or at least pity Judah. Instead, Edom stood aloof, gloated over Judah's downfall, and finally crossed the threshold into the city.

The word shaʿar marks the spatial boundary between passive stance and active aggression. By passing through "the gate of My people," Edom actively joined the invader in violating Jerusalem's sanctuary and plundering her wealth. Yahweh specifically labels Jerusalem "My people," underscoring that although Judah was undergoing divine discipline, the city's gates still belonged to God's chosen covenant nation. Edom's trespass through the shaʿar was therefore an arrogant intrusion into Yahweh's covenant domain.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 23:18 – "to Abraham as a possession in the presence of the sons of Heth, before all who went in at the gate of his city."

  • Amos 5:15 – "Hate evil, love good; Establish justice in the gate."

  • Micah 1:9 – "For her wounds are incurable; For it has come to Judah; It has come to the gate of my people—even to Jerusalem."

  • Proverbs 31:31 – "Give her of the fruit of her hands, And let her own works praise her in the gates."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The literary structure of Obadiah 1:11–14 depicts a moral descent that reaches its turning point at the shaʿar. The sequence moves from internal attitude to open violation:

1. Standing on the other side as a stranger (v. 11)

2. Gazing and rejoicing over Judah's affliction (v. 12)

3. Entering the gate of God's people to seize goods (v. 13)

4. Standing at the crossroads to cut off fugitives (v. 14)

Entering the shaʿar represents the decisive shift where Edom's heart malice materialized into physical trespass and violence. The gate, designed to protect God's community, became the precise location where Edom's treachery was fully manifested.
