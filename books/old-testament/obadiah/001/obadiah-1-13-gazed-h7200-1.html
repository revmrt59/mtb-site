GAZED - (râʼâh, Strong's H7200)

Summary:

The basic lexical meaning of ra'ah is to see, observe, look at, or perceive visually. In Obadiah 1:13, the verb describes Edom’s malicious, gloating gaze directed at Judah during Jerusalem's destruction. Rather than responding with brotherly grief or assistance, Edom watched Israel's devastation with contemptuous delight. In the prophet's argument, this verb identifies heart-level hostility and sinister passive participation, establishing Edom's moral guilt prior to their active plunder and violence.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew root ra'ah is the standard Old Testament verb for physical vision and sensory perception. It functions across the full spectrum of sight, ranging from simple visual recognition to deep mental inspection, discernment, and experiential observation.

In the historical and prophetic setting of Obadiah, the word appears within an oracle of judgment against the nation of Edom. As descendants of Esau, the Edomites shared a genealogical brotherhood with the descendants of Jacob (Israel). When Jerusalem fell under divine judgment at the hands of foreign invaders, Edom did not merely remain neutral; they watched the catastrophe with arrogant satisfaction and eventually participated in the hostility. The prophet uses ra'ah to highlight the cruel spectating that marked Edom's attitude during Israel's tragedy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, ra'ah refers to the physical act of seeing or perceiving with the eyes. Lexically, its primary senses include:

  • To view, observe, or behold physical objects or events.

  • To perceive mentally, understand, or consider a matter.

  • To inspect, examine, or evaluate (such as God inspecting creation or human actions).

  • To look upon with a specific disposition—such as favor, grief, or malicious pleasure—when joined with particular prepositions and contextual markers.

In the Qal stem, ra'ah expresses the direct verbal action of seeing or looking. While the verb itself often denotes simple observation, its context determines whether the gaze is neutral, compassionate, or hostile. When directed toward an enemy's disaster or a brother's ruin, ra'ah frequently implies gloating or taking pleasure in another's downfall.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, ra'ah spans various contexts:

  • Divine Sight: God "sees" His creation and pronounces it good, or He "sees" the affliction of His covenant people and moves to deliver them.

  • Human Sight: Individuals "see" physical objects, leaders "inspect" situations, and prophets "behold" visions.

  • Emotional and Moral Gaze: Sight in Hebrew thought is rarely detached or strictly objective. To look upon suffering can express deep sympathy, but to "gaze upon" a fallen adversary often carries the nuance of schadenfreude—finding malicious delight in another's judgment.

The semantic range of ra'ah must not be artificially restricted to negative gloating in every context; rather, the specific emotion or moral evaluation attached to the sight is supplied by the surrounding narrative and prepositional phrases.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of ra'ah in prophetic judgment highlights key theological truths regarding divine holiness and human accountability:

  • The Morality of the Eyes: God holds humanity accountable not only for outward physical violence, but for the disposition of the heart expressed through the eyes. Malicious spectating over another's suffering is a sin against God.

  • God’s All-Seeing Omniscience: While Edom was gazing upon Judah's calamity with approval, God was observing Edom's arrogance. Human gaze is finite and biased, but divine sight is comprehensive and perfectly just.

  • Covenantal Solidarity: Edom’s failure to mourn Judah’s affliction violated the relational obligations of brotherhood. God takes notice of how neighboring nations treat His chastened covenant people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:10-14 forms a poetic sequence of divine indictments, structured around a series of negative commands ("You should not have..."). This passage traces a clear escalation in Edom's wicked response to Judah's crisis:

1. Standing aloof as an onlooker (v. 11).

2. Rejoicing and speaking proudly over their downfall (v. 12).

3. Entering the gate and "gazing" on their affliction (v. 13).

4. Laying hands on their substance and cutting off escapees (vv. 13-14).

In verse 13, ra'ah is rendered "gazed" in the NKJV ("gazed on their affliction"). Morphologically, the verb appears in the Qal stem, representing the simple act of looking upon the distress of Jerusalem. However, the surrounding syntax and rhetorical structure infuse this looking with heavy moral condemnation. Edom entered the conquered city not to help, but to feed their pride by spectating Judah's humiliation. The prophet demonstrates that this malicious visual indulgence directly preceded and fueled Edom's overt acts of looting and violence.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The following passages illustrate the range of ra'ah across different biblical contexts:

  • Genesis 1:25 – "And God made the beast of the earth according to its kind, cattle according to its kind, and everything that creeps on the earth according to its kind. And God saw that it was good."

  • Genesis 26:8 – "Now it came to pass, when he had been there a long time, that Abimelech king of the Philistines looked through a window, and saw, and there was Isaac, showing endearment to Rebekah his wife."

  • Job 34:21 – "For His eyes are on the ways of man, and He sees all his steps."

  • Isaiah 14:16 – "Those who see you will gaze at you, and consider you, saying: 'Is this the man who made the earth tremble, who shook kingdoms...'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew poetry, parallel clauses often expand and clarify an initial action. In Obadiah 1:12-13, ra'ah is placed in direct parallelism with "rejoiced," "spoken proudly," and "laid hands on their substance." This literary arrangement reveals that in prophetic rhetoric, malicious gazing is not viewed as passive or harmless; it is classified alongside active gloating and theft.

Furthermore, a subtle contrast exists throughout the Old Testament between God seeing His people's affliction to save them, and enemies seeing that same affliction to mock them. Where divine sight leads to mercy, Edom's sight led to hardened cruelty, sealing their own impending judgment under the law of retaliation (lex talionis).
