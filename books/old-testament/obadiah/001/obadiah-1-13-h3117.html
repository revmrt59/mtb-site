DAY - ( yom , Strong's H3117 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun yom primarily denotes a literal twenty-four-hour day, the daylight portion of a day, or more broadly, an appointed season, epoch, or specific point in historical time characterized by a particular event. In the prophetic context of Obadiah 1:13, yom is used repeatedly in the phrase "the day of their calamity" to point to a specific, tragic moment in Israel's history—the Babylonian siege and destruction of Jerusalem in 586 B.C. Here, yom transcends a simple calendar date to signify a devastating season of divine chastisement upon Judah, during which their brother nation, Edom, maliciously participated in their downfall rather than offering brotherly aid.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word-picture of yom is rooted in the regular cycle of light breaking through darkness, marking out time. In the ancient Near East, time was viewed not merely as a quantitative measurement (ticking minutes) but qualitatively, characterized by what happens during that time. Therefore, a "day" of something describes the atmosphere, events, and divine significance of that specific period (such as a "day of trouble," "day of wrath," or "day of salvation"). It represents a window of opportunity, a period of judgment, or a designated epoch where God's hand is visible.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun yom has a broad semantic range defined by its grammatical context:

• The period of light in contrast to night or darkness.

• A literal twenty-four-hour period, consisting of an evening and a morning.

• A generic, indefinite period of time, such as an era, lifetime, or year.

• A specific, historically significant point in time marked by a major event, often characterized by judgment, battle, or deliverance.

In Obadiah 1:13, yom refers specifically to this last category—a designated, historic period of crisis and catastrophe.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word yom often pairs with descriptive genitives to create vivid thematic expressions throughout the Old Testament:

• The Day of the Lord (Yom Yahweh): The ultimate period of God's direct intervention in human history for judgment and salvation.

• Day of Calamity / Day of Trouble: A metaphor for sudden, overwhelming disaster where human strength fails and divine sovereignty is starkly felt.

• In poetic and prophetic literature, yom serves to condense a prolonged historical catastrophe (like the siege of Jerusalem) into a singular, memorable "moment of reckoning" that demands a response from onlookers.

4. Theological Implications

• Divine Sovereignty over Time: yom reminds us that history is not cyclical or meaningless; God ordains specific days of testing, judgment, and redemption.

• The Ethical Accountability of Onlookers: Edom's sin was committed in the day of Judah's calamity. God holds nations accountable for how they treat His covenant people during their times of divine discipline.

• Chastisement vs. Hostility: While Judah's "day" was a day of paternal discipline from Yahweh, Edom viewed it as an opportunity for opportunistic exploitation. God distinguishes between His refining judgment and the wicked malice of human instruments.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:13, the word yom is repeated three times in a rhythmic, indictment-style cadence: "the day of their calamity."

• This triple repetition intensifies the gravity of Edom’s betrayal. It emphasizes that Edom did not merely make a passive mistake; they repeatedly chose active, systematic cruelty during Judah's absolute darkest hour.

• The "day of calamity" refers to the fall of Jerusalem to the Babylonians in 586 B.C. Edom stood by, gloated, entered the gates to plunder, and even intercepted survivors. yom marks this specific window of moral failure that sealed Edom's own doom.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:5 - "God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night. So the evening and the morning were the first day."

• Psalm 20:1 - "May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble; May the name of the God of Jacob defend you;"

• Obadiah 1:15 - "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near; As you have done, it shall be done to you; Your reprisal shall return upon your own head."

• Jeremiah 46:21 - "Also her mercenaries are in her midst like fatted bulls, For they also have turned back, They have fled away together. They did not stand, For the day of their calamity had come upon them, The time of their punishment."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The poetic structure of Obadiah uses the word yom overall ten times (verses 11, 12 [three times], 13 [three times], 14, 15). This literary focus shifts from the "day" of Jacob's disaster (vv. 11–14) to the "Day of the LORD" (v. 15). This structural shift delivers a powerful pastoral lesson: those who exploit the "day" of others' suffering will ultimately face the "Day" of God's supreme justice.
