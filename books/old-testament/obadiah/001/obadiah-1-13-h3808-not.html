NOT - ( lo , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle lo represents absolute, objective, and permanent negation, translating as "not," "no," or "never." Unlike the situational or deprecative negative 'al (Strong's H408)—which appeals to the will and prohibits temporary actions—lo establishes an unalterable boundary, a factual state of non-existence, or an unconditional command. In the prophetic indictment of Obadiah, this absolute negation serves as a profound theological anchor. While Edom active-aggressively crossed covenant boundaries to gloat over and plunder Judah, God's sovereign decree of absolute negation ultimately ensures that those who seek the destruction of His people will themselves face a permanent end, becoming as though they had "not" been.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In biblical Hebrew, negation is not merely a grammatical tool but a conceptual boundary marker. The language employs two primary negative adverbs: lo (H3808) and 'al (H408). While 'al is subjective and immediate—used for emotional appeals, temporary warnings, or jussive requests ("do not do this right now")—lo is the objective, absolute negative. It represents a permanent, unchanging reality. The word picture behind lo is that of an unyielding wall or an iron barrier. When paired with an imperfect verb, it transforms a command into an eternal, categorical imperative. It is the word used to establish cosmic order, setting limits on what can and cannot occur in God’s creation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• lo is an adverb of absolute negation. It functions in several distinct ways:

• Absolute Denial: It denies a fact or states a definitive non-reality.

• Permanent Prohibition: When paired with an imperfect verb, it expresses an unconditional, permanent prohibition (e.g., "you shall never").

• Substantive Negation: When joined with nouns, it indicates complete absence or non-existence, turning a concept into its absolute opposite (such as "non-people" or "not-pitied").

• Unconditional Boundary: It defines the outer limits of covenant law and divine decree, marking out behaviors and states of being that are completely excluded from God's order.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The semantic weight of lo is most vividly seen in covenantal and legal contexts. In the Decalogue (the Ten Commandments), lo is the standard negative particle used to issue the absolute moral boundaries of the community. It does not mean "please do not do this today"; it means "this behavior has no place in the covenant community, ever."

In the prophetic writings, lo is used to draw a sharp, irreversible contrast between God's truth and human vanity. When God pronounces a judgment using lo, it signals to the audience that the decreed outcome is factual, immutable, and entirely independent of human wishes. It represents the sovereign veto of Yahweh over human pride and geopolitical schemes.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, lo highlights the immutability of God’s character, law, and decrees.

• The Unchanging Standard of God: Because God’s holiness is absolute, His prohibitions are formulated with lo. They are not conditional suggestions but eternal boundaries reflecting His own nature.

• The Sovereignty of Divine Judgment: When God decrees judgment, human strength cannot overturn it. The absolute "not" of God nullifies all human defense.

• The Assurance of Covenant Preservation: Just as God's negative judgments are absolute, His positive protections are equally unyielding. When God decrees that His covenant people will not be completely destroyed, that lo stands as an ironclad guarantee of their ultimate survival.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:12-14 contains a powerful rhetorical sequence of eight prohibitions directed at the nation of Edom. Historically, when the Babylonians destroyed Jerusalem, the Edomites—who were close relatives of Israel through Esau—stood by, gloated over Judah's misfortune, cut off their escape routes, and participated in the plunder.

In Obadiah 1:13, the NKJV translates the text as: "You should not have entered the gate of My people... Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction... Nor laid hands on their substance."

The Hebrew text of this passage predominantly utilizes the situational negative 'al (H408) to paint a vivid, urgent picture of what Edom should not have done as they watched Judah's fall. However, this rhetorical rebuke is backed by the theological reality of lo (H3808). Edom acted under the delusion that God's covenant with Judah was temporary and that God would not act to defend them. By ignoring the absolute, moral boundaries established by God's law, Edom brought themselves under the ultimate decree of divine negation. Because they crossed the line of brotherly compassion, God's absolute lo would soon fall upon them, rendering their prideful empire completely non-existent.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:17 - "but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die."

• Exodus 20:13 - "You shall not murder."

• Obadiah 1:16 - "For as you drank on My holy mountain, So shall all the nations drink continually; Yes, they shall drink and swallow, And they shall be as though they had not been."

• Hosea 1:9 - "Then God said: 'Call his name Lo-Ammi, For you are not My people, And I will not be your God.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), the Hebrew lo is consistently translated using the Greek absolute negative particle ou (or ouk / ouch), rather than the subjective, conditional negative me. This linguistic consistency across both testaments reinforces that the biblical writers understood lo as a marker of objective reality.

For pastors and teachers, this distinction is highly valuable for application: human "no's" are often negotiable, subject to feelings, or limited by circumstances. God’s absolute "no" (lo), however, is a firm foundation. Whether it is found in His moral boundaries, His promises of protection, or His ultimate judgments, it is an unshakeable, eternal truth that demands our reverence and trust.
