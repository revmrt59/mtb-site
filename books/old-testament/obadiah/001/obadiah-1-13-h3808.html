NOR - ( al , Strong's 408 )

Summary:

In the prophetic indictment of Edom in Obadiah 1:13, the English word "nor" translates the Hebrew negative particle al (often associated with or functioning alongside the general negative lo, Strong's 3808). While lo represents an absolute, objective, and permanent prohibition ("you shall not"), the particle al (Strong's 408) is a subjective, emotional prohibition meaning "do not" or "let it not be." In the context of Obadiah’s rapid-fire prohibitions against Edom's malicious joy over Judah's downfall, "nor" (ve-al) functions as a passionate rhetorical constraint. God warns Edom that they should not have stood by, rejoiced in, or actively participated in the plunder of their brother Jacob. The term serves as an urgent warning barrier, exposing the moral failure of Edom's passive approval and active exploitation of Judah's calamity.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

Historically, Edom and Judah were brother nations, descended from the twins Esau and Jacob. This familial relationship should have bound them in mutual protection and empathy. Instead, Edom harbored a long-standing resentment. When the Babylonians breached Jerusalem's walls, Edom stood by as a malicious spectator, then celebrated, and finally joined in the plundering of the city. The series of prohibitions in Obadiah 1:12–14, utilizing the negative particle al, functions as a dramatic retroversive warning. The background is one of intense emotional betrayal, where a brother actively participates in the ruin of his kin rather than offering aid.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew word al (Strong's 408) is a negative adverb of prohibition, deprecation, or wish. It means "do not," "let not," or "neither/nor" when prefixed with the conjunction ve-. While the absolute negative particle lo (Strong's 3808) expresses an objective, permanent, and unconditional negation ("no" or "shall not"), al is subjective and situational. It is used to halt an action in progress or to plead against an imminent occurrence. In Obadiah 1:13, the negative particle appears with the conjunction ve- (ve-al), translated as "nor." It expresses a severe, emotionally charged dehortation: "do not stretch out your hand" or "you should not have laid hands."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, al serves as an urgent barrier or warning sign. The word picture is that of a passionate appeal to desist from a course of action before it is too late. It is the word used when a father pleads with his children, or when God interposes to prevent a tragedy. In legal contexts, lo (Strong's 3808) establishes the boundary of the law (e.g., "You shall not steal"). In prophetic and personal contexts, al appeals to the heart and the immediate choice of the individual, commanding them to stop their current trajectory.

4. Theological Implications

The repeated use of al in Obadiah 1:12–14 highlights God's active, moral government over the nations and His intense concern for fraternal covenant-keeping.

• Divine Warning and Human Will: God’s prohibitions are not cold, detached decrees; they are immediate, personal appeals to human conscience. The subjective nature of al reveals a God who entreats nations to act with justice and mercy.

• The Progression of Guilt: The transition from passive gloating to active violence is prevented by these successive commands. When Edom ignored the subjective warning (al), they fell under the permanent, objective judgment of God (lo), resulting in their complete eradication as a distinct nation.

• Fraternal Obligation: God fiercely defends covenantal and familial relationships. Edom’s refusal to show mercy to their brother Jacob was a direct rebellion against God's moral order.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the literary structure of Obadiah 1:12–14, the prophet uses al eight times in rapid succession, translated in the NKJV as "should not have" and "nor." In verse 13, "nor" (ve-al) bridges the passive sin of "gazing" on Judah's affliction with the active physical violation of "laying hands" on their wealth. The context demands that Edom should have restrained themselves. By using al, the prophet presents the historical past with the vividness of an ongoing event, as if screaming to the Edomites on the day of Jerusalem's fall to drop the looted goods and step away. The nuance of "nor" here is not a mere grammatical transition; it is a moral stop-sign demanding the immediate cessation of hostile actions against a suffering brother.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew grammar, the distinction between lo (Strong's 3808) and al (Strong's 408) is crucial for correct exegesis. While lo followed by an imperfect verb forms an absolute, permanent prohibition (characteristic of apodictic law), al followed by a jussive or imperfect verb forms an immediate, situational deprecation. In Obadiah, the use of al with the imperfect verbs expresses a strong, retrospective wish of what ought not to have happened, highlighting that Edom's actions were entirely preventable and contrary to God's immediate moral desire.
