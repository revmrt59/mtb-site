PEOPLE - ( am , Strong's H5971 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word am refers to a nation, people group, or family unit bound together by blood, covenant, or common history. Unlike other Hebrew words for nation that focus primarily on political or geographical boundaries, am emphasizes deep relational, familial, and covenantal ties. In Obadiah 1:13, God refers to Israel as "My people" (ammî), highlighting His enduring covenant relationship with them. Even in the midst of their severe judgment and national calamity, God retains His relational ownership of Israel, making Edom’s betrayal, gloating, and exploitation of them a direct assault against God Himself.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun am is derived from a root suggesting association, companionship, or gathering together, sharing a cognate connection with the preposition im, which means "with." The word picture conveys a close-knit community bound by kinship, proximity, and mutual responsibility. In the ancient Near Eastern context, a "people" belonged to a patron deity who was responsible for their protection, while the people owed the deity their allegiance. In the Old Testament, Israel is uniquely designated as the am of Yahweh, established through the Mosaic covenant. This relationship is deeply familial, carrying the connotation that God is the Father and Israel is His family.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Am primarily means "people," "nation," "kinfolk," or "relatives." It defines a collective group sharing a common ancestry, language, or covenant. When used with a pronominal suffix as "My people" (ammî), it functions as a highly personal term of endearment and sovereign covenantal claim. Lexically, it is distinct from goy (plural goyim). While goy emphasizes the political, institutional, and geographical reality of a nation (frequently used of Gentile nations), am emphasizes the personal, social, and relational bonds of a family or covenant community.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term am appears over 1,800 times in the Hebrew Bible. It is most frequently used to depict the unique relationship between God and Israel, formulated in the recurring covenant promise: "I will be their God, and they shall be My people." Scripture employs several rich word pictures to illustrate this relationship:

  • Shepherd and Flock: Israel is described as the "people of His pasture," emphasizing God's guidance, provision, and protection.

  • Inheritance (nachalah): The people are portrayed as God’s treasured possession, redeemed out of bondage to be His own inheritance among the nations.

  • Family/Kinship: The term reinforces that the members of the community are brothers and sisters, bound by a shared covenant family identity.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of am centers on the doctrines of election, covenant faithfulness, and divine ownership. God did not choose Israel because of their power, size, or righteousness, but out of His sovereign love and covenant oath. The relational durability of am is a core biblical theme: even when Israel faces severe divine discipline and exile due to their sins, they do not cease to be "My people." Furthermore, because of this covenant bond, any hostility, injustice, or cruelty committed against God's people by outside nations is treated by God as a direct offense against His own person and holiness.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:13, God addresses Edom, saying, "You should not have entered the gate of My people in the day of their calamity." The historical context is the fall of Jerusalem, likely to the Babylonians. Edom, descended from Esau, was brother to Israel (descended from Jacob). Instead of rendering aid, Edom stood by, gloated, and actively participated in the plundering and capturing of fleeing Judeans.

By utilizing the term ammî ("My people") in this rebuke, God signals that despite Judah's rebellion and subsequent judgment, He had not abandoned His covenant relationship with them. They were still His family. Edom's violation of Israel’s gates was an intrusion into the household of God. The phrase intensifies Edom's guilt, demonstrating that their lack of brotherly compassion was ultimately an act of treason against Yahweh Himself.

  • Hosea 2:23 - "Then I will sow her for Myself in the earth, and I will have mercy on her who had not obtained mercy; then I will say to those who were not My people, 'You are My people!' And they shall say, 'You are my God!'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) translates am as laos in Obadiah 1:13. In Greek, laos similarly denotes a specific, chosen group of people, as opposed to ethnos (which denotes nations or Gentiles at large). This Greek term carries directly into the New Testament, where the Apostle Peter applies these exact covenantal concepts to the Church, calling believers "a chosen generation, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, His own special people (laos)" (1 Peter 2:9). This demonstrates the theological continuity of God's relational ownership over His chosen ones from the Old Covenant to the New.
