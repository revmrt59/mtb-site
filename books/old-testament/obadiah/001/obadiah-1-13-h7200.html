GAZED - ( ra'ah , Strong's H7200 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ra'ah is a foundational Old Testament word meaning "to see," "to look," or "to behold." While it often denotes simple physical sight, its meaning frequently expands to include emotional or intellectual engagement, such as looking with satisfaction, finding pleasure in another's misfortune, or gazing with malicious intent. In Obadiah 1:13, the word carries the severe nuance of gloating—standing by to feast one's eyes on the suffering and ruin of Israel. God condemns Edom not just for physical observation, but for the heart of malicious joy and passive cruelty that watched a brother's downfall without offering aid.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical background of ra'ah is the act of seeing with the physical eyes. However, in ancient Hebrew thought, seeing is rarely a detached, neutral action. To see is to experience, to feel, and often to be moved to action. The eyes are viewed as the window to the soul's attitude and intent. In the ancient Near East, to "gaze" upon a defeated enemy was to participate in their public humiliation. It was a visual victory dance, a gesture of dominance and triumphalism. In the context of Obadiah, Edom (the descendants of Esau) stood on the ridges, watching the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem (the descendants of Jacob, their brother). Their gaze was not one of sorrow, helplessness, or pity, but a lingering, satisfying look of spite, contempt, and malicious joy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb ra'ah means to perceive with the eyes, to look at, or to see.

• In specific contexts, it denotes looking with interest, inspecting, or giving close attention to something.

• When paired with certain prepositions (specifically the preposition be), it frequently carries the sense of looking with satisfaction, pleasure, or triumph upon an enemy's defeat or suffering.

• It can also mean to experience or feel, as in "seeing" life, "seeing" death, or "seeing" affliction.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Sight: The basic act of opening the eyes and perceiving physical reality, such as God seeing the light in the creation account.

• Empathetic Gazing: Looking upon suffering with a heart of deep compassion, as when God "looked" on the affliction of Israel in Egypt and was moved to rescue them.

• Malicious Gloating: The opposite of empathy. It is the act of staring at someone’s misfortune to satisfy one's pride or malice. It is a visual consumption of another’s pain, treating a tragedy as a spectator sport.

4. Theological Implications

• God is the Ultimate Seer: God watches how we watch others. Our gaze is never morally neutral. He notices when we look at the suffering of others with apathy, disgust, or hidden satisfaction.

• Passive Looking as Active Sin: By merely standing and "gazing" at Judah's destruction, Edom participated in the sin of the Babylonians. Neutrality in the face of a brother's ruin is a betrayal of covenant love. God holds us accountable not only for the evil we do, but for the evil we choose to watch without offering help.

• Reciprocal Judgment: God's judgment is often reciprocal. Those who gaze with pleasure at the destruction of God's people will eventually be gazed upon in their own shame and ruin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:13, the prophet uses a series of prohibitions ("You should not have...") to indict Edom for their behavior during the fall of Jerusalem in 586 B.C. The specific phrase is al-tere, "do not look/gaze." Edom did not just happen to notice the destruction; they positioned themselves to watch it, enjoying the spectacle of Judah's calamity. The grammatical construction indicates a lingering, satisfied gaze. By using ra'ah, the prophet exposes the condition of Edom's heart. Their eyes revealed their deep-seated sibling rivalry, jealousy, and hatred. God equates this malicious gazing with direct participation in the violence.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 29:32: "So Leah conceived and bore a son, and she called his name Reuben; for she said, 'The LORD has surely looked on my affliction. Now therefore, my husband will love me.'" (God's gaze of compassion and care).

• Psalm 22:17: "I can count all my bones. They look and stare at me." (Prophetic of Christ's crucifixion, where enemies gaze with satisfaction at His suffering).

• Psalm 118:7: "The LORD is for me among those who help me; Therefore I shall see my desire on those who hate me." (Looking with triumph over defeated enemies).

• Micah 7:10: "Then she who is my enemy will see, And shame will cover her who said to me, 'Where is the LORD your God?' My eyes will see her; Now she will be trampled down Like mud in the streets." (Seeing with vindication).

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The visual nature of Edom's sin is contrasted with their ultimate fate. In Obadiah 1:10, shame will cover them. The ones who stared in pride will be stared at in humiliation.

• This Old Testament concept of sinful looking anticipates Jesus' teachings on the eyes. In the New Testament, looking at someone with lust or hatred is equated with committing the physical act in the heart. Edom committed violence in their hearts long before they joined in the physical looting.
