AFFLICTION - ( ra'ah , Strong's H7451 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ra'ah refers to badness, misery, distress, or calamity. In Obadiah 1:13, it describes the agonizing distress and physical suffering of the people of Judah during the Babylonian invasion. Instead of showing brotherly compassion to their close relatives, the Edomites stood by to gaze upon and exploit Judah's intense suffering. This term highlights both the objective tragedy of Judah's judgment and the deep moral failure of Edom's malicious indifference.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verbal root of ra'ah is ra'a', which means to spoil, break in pieces, or be dysfunctional. The word picture is one of something being shattered, ruined, or broken from its proper, life-giving state into something harmful and destructive. In ancient Near Eastern thought, "evil" or "affliction" was not merely an abstract moral concept but a concrete, physical disruption of order, peace (shalom), and life. When a nation experienced ra'ah, its societal walls, security, and well-being were broken to pieces, leaving the people vulnerable, weeping, and exposed to their enemies.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• ra'ah functions as a feminine noun denoting badness, evil, calamity, distress, or misery. It encompasses both moral evil (wickedness, ethical badness) and physical evil (disaster, misfortune, adversity, suffering). In passages of historical judgment, the word shifts away from moral wrongdoing to emphasize the objective state of ruin and suffering that overtakes a person or nation. It denotes a condition of intense hardship, pain, and sorrow brought about by external calamity, such as war, famine, or divine chastisement.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Hebrew Bible uses ra'ah in a variety of vivid contexts:

• Ethical Badness: Actions that are displeasing, harmful, or injurious to God and neighbor.

• Calamity and Disaster: Natural or political catastrophes sent as a consequence of covenant unfaithfulness.

• Personal Misery: The emotional and physical grief of a parent losing a child, or a sufferer undergoing intense physical pain.

The visual picture of ra'ah in Israel's history is often that of a city under siege—starving children, burning gates, and a scattered remnant. It represents the antithesis of the Promised Land’s ideal of safety and abundance.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, ra'ah demonstrates that sin and rebellion inevitably lead to brokenness and ruin. God is sovereign over both shalom (peace) and ra'ah (calamity), using historical disasters to discipline His covenant people. Furthermore, the divine response to Edom’s reaction to Judah's ra'ah reveals a crucial ethical truth: God holds neighboring nations accountable for how they treat His people during their times of suffering. To gloat over, rejoice in, or profit from another’s affliction is an abomination to Yahweh, who demands justice, mercy, and brotherly solidarity.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:13, ra'ah occurs in the phrase "you should not have gazed on their affliction in the day of their calamity." Here, the word specifically highlights the visible, agonizing suffering of the Judeans as Jerusalem fell to the Babylonians. The nuance is highly relational. Edom, descended from Jacob's brother Esau, should have felt familial grief. Instead, they stood by, "gazing" (staring with malicious pleasure or detached curiosity) at Judah's desperate misery. The text emphasizes that Edom's sin was not just their physical violence, but their cold-hearted joy in witnessing Judah’s absolute ruin.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 44:34 - "For how shall I go up to my father if the lad is not with me, lest perhaps I see the evil that would come upon my father?"

• Psalm 10:6 - "He has said in his heart, 'I shall not be moved; I shall never be in adversity.'"

• Psalm 34:19 - "Many are the afflictions of the righteous, But the LORD delivers him out of them all."

• Jeremiah 18:11 - "Now therefore, speak to the men of Judah and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem, saying, 'Thus says the LORD: "Behold, I am fashioning disaster against you and devising a plan against you."'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The repetition of the phrase "in the day of their calamity" three times in Obadiah 1:13 underscores the tragic and critical nature of Judah's downfall. Edom’s failure was a failure of covenant-like brotherly love. The Hebrew phrasing connects their visual gloating ("gazed on their affliction") directly to their subsequent physical exploitation ("nor laid hands on their substance"). Passive indifference to a brother's affliction almost always transitions into active participation in their destruction.
