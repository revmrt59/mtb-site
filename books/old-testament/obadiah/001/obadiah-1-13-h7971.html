LAID - ( shalach , Strong's H7971 )

Summary:

In Obadiah 1:13, the Hebrew verb shalach (translated as "laid" in the context of "laid hands") fundamentally means to send, stretch out, or extend. In this prophetic context, the word depicts the active, aggressive stretching out of the hand to seize, plunder, and loot the wealth of Judah during their hour of devastating defeat. This term exposes Edom’s opportunistic cruelty; they did not merely watch their brother's downfall from a distance, but actively reached in to snatch away what little remained of their substance.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of shalach is to send, cast, or shoot forth. It is frequently used for sending a messenger on an errand, releasing a captive, or shooting arrows from a bow. When the verb is combined with a physical destination or when an object like "hand" is contextually implied, it evokes the graphic physical motion of reaching out to grab something.

In ancient Near Eastern warfare, plundering was the final, devastating humiliation of a conquered city. The word picture here portrays Edom standing over a broken and bleeding Judah, greedily stretching out their hands to snatch up Judah's remaining possessions. Instead of showing brotherly compassion to the descendants of Jacob, the descendants of Esau chose to extend their hands in greedy exploitation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In its primary Qal stem, shalach means to stretch out, extend, or direct. When followed by the Hebrew preposition be (meaning "in," "into," or "against"), the verb specifically means to lay hands on, reach out to seize, or stretch out the hand against someone or something with hostile intent.

The semantic range of shalach spans from "sending" a message or a person to "stretching out" a staff, or aggressively "laying" hands on plunder. In Obadiah 1:13, the expression is elliptical—the word "hand" (yad) is omitted in the Hebrew text but is fully implied by the grammar and context. The action denotes the physical, illicit seizure of another person's wealth.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, shalach is employed in several distinct ways that enrich its meaning:

  • Sending and Commissioning: Used when God sends a prophet or when a king sends a royal messenger. This represents an authorized extension of the sender's will and presence.

  • Stretching Out for Miraculous Power: Used when Moses stretches out his staff over the Red Sea or toward heaven, representing the extension of divine authority.

  • Aggressive and Predatory Seizure: Used when a predator reaches out to grab its prey, or when a thief reaches into a neighbor's treasury. In Obadiah, Edom behaves like a scavenger. They let the Babylonians do the hard work of breaking Judah's defenses, and then they reach in to steal the leftover spoils.

4. Theological Implications

The use of shalach in Obadiah 1:13 carries heavy covenantal and ethical weight:

  • Violation of Kinship: Edom and Judah were brother nations, tracing their lineages back to Esau and Jacob. Reaching out hands to plunder a brother's ruin violates the natural bond of kinship and the direct ethical commands of God.

  • God’s Sovereignty Over Discipline: God notes not just the physical acts of violence, but the greedy, opportunistic actions of surrounding nations. Even when God disciplines His people using a pagan nation (like Babylon), He holds those who opportunistically exploit that discipline fully accountable.

  • The Progression of Sin: This verb represents the climax of Edom’s sin. Their rebellion against God moved from an internal attitude (pride and gloating) to an external action (entering the gates and laying hands on the plunder).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:13 contains a series of rhythmic, urgent prohibitions ("You should not have..."). The text structures the progression of Edom's sin in three distinct stages:

1. Entry: "You should not have entered the gate of My people..." (The physical intrusion).

2. Observation: "Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction..." (The malicious gloating).

3. Exploitation: "Nor laid hands on their substance..." (The active theft).

By using shalach with the preposition be ("into his wealth/substance"), the writer emphasizes the invasive nature of Edom's sin. They did not merely stand on the border and watch; they reached deep into Judah’s misery to extract wealth for themselves.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 3:22 - "lest he put out his hand and take also of the tree of life..."

  • Genesis 22:12 - "And He said, 'Do not lay your hand on the lad, or do anything to him...'"

  • Exodus 3:20 - "So I will stretch out My hand and strike Egypt with all My wonders..."

  • Esther 9:10 - "the ten sons of Haman the son of Hammedatha, the enemy of the Jews—they killed; but they did not lay their hands on the plunder."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The omission of the word "hand" (yad) in the Hebrew text of Obadiah 1:13 is a powerful poetic device. The verb shalach carries such strong imagery of "reaching out" that the listener immediately visualizes the hand of the plunderer grabbing the goods. This grammatical shorthand highlights the eagerness and haste of the Edomites; they did not hesitate, they simply reached out and took. It serves as a warning that God observes and remembers the exact moment our internal covetousness translates into external, destructive action.
