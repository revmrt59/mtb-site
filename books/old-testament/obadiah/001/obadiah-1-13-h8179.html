GATE - ( sha'ar , Strong's H8179 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sha'ar refers to a gate, primarily of a walled city, representing its security, identity, and place of public, judicial, and commercial life. In Obadiah 1:13, the gate is the entry point of Jerusalem, God’s covenant city, during its hour of deepest vulnerability. Edom's opportunistic entry through this gate during Judah’s defeat was not a peaceful visit but a hostile, treacherous intrusion, demonstrating a severe betrayal of their brother Jacob and a direct violation of God's covenant boundaries.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, a city gate was far more than a simple door in a wall. It was a massive, heavily fortified structure, often built with multiple chambers, towers, and heavy wooden doors reinforced with metal. The gate served as the primary defensive stronghold of a city. Because it was the only point of entry and exit, it naturally became the hub of civic life. It functioned as a marketplace, a court of law where the elders sat to administer justice, and a public square for proclamations. To control the gate of a city was to possess ultimate power and authority over that city. Conversely, a breached gate symbolized total vulnerability, defeat, and the loss of sovereignty.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun sha'ar designates an opening, gateway, or entrance of a city, a palace, a temple, or a camp. It occurs over 370 times in the Old Testament, predominantly in historical and prophetic literature. Lexically, it refers to:

• The physical structure of a city gate, including its posts, bars, and locks.

• The public area immediately inside or surrounding the gate, acting as a forum.

• The city itself by extension of meaning, representing the population and civic authority.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture employs sha'ar in several key ways:

• The Seat of Justice: The gate was where legal transactions occurred. Boaz went to the gate to settle the redemption of Ruth, and the elders of the city sat there to witness covenants.

• The Symbol of Victory: Capturing the "gate" of one's enemies meant complete conquest and dominion over them.

• The Center of Commerce and News: King David stood by the gate to address his army, and prophets often delivered their messages in the gate to ensure the maximum public audience.

• The Boundary of Holiness: The gates of the tabernacle and temple courts demarcated the holy space from the common, serving as checkpoints for ritual purity.

4. Theological Implications

The city of Jerusalem was not merely a political capital; it was the city of the Great King, and its gates were sacred to Yahweh. The gates of Zion were loved by God because they welcomed the righteous and guarded the sanctuary. When an enemy violated these gates, it was not only an act of geopolitical warfare but a direct assault on God’s holiness and His covenant promises. Edom’s intrusion into Jerusalem’s gate reveals the depth of human depravity and the betrayal of fraternal obligations. As descendants of Esau, the Edomites were brothers to the Israelites (descendants of Jacob). By entering the gate of their brother in the day of disaster, Edom allied themselves with pagan plunderers, mocking God's election of Israel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:13, the prophet uses a series of prohibitions to condemn Edom’s conduct during the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC: "You should not have entered the gate of My people in the day of their calamity." Edom did not breach the walls themselves; the Babylonians did. However, once the city was compromised, the Edomites poured through the broken sha'ar to participate in the looting, gloat over the suffering, and capture escaping survivors. The gate, which should have been defended by allies or respected by neighbors, became the pathway for Edom’s opportunistic malice. By calling it "the gate of My people," God highlights that the offense was committed directly against His own family, elevating Edom’s political opportunism to a spiritual rebellion.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 22:17 - "And your descendants shall possess the gate of their enemies."

• Ruth 4:1 - "Now Boaz went up to the gate and sat down there; and behold, the close relative of whom Boaz had spoken came by."

• Psalm 100:4 - "Enter into His gates with thanksgiving, And into His courts with praise."

• Isaiah 62:10 - "Go through, Go through the gates! Prepare the way for the people; Build up, Build up the highway!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The progression of Edom's sin in Obadiah 1:11–14 shows a chilling escalation of hostility. They began by standing "on the other side" as passive spectators (verse 11). They moved to rejoicing and gloating (verse 12). Then, they actively entered the "gate" to plunder (verse 13). Finally, they stood at the crossroads to slaughter the fugitives (verse 14). The gate is the physical and symbolic transition point where passive hatred transforms into active, destructive violence.
