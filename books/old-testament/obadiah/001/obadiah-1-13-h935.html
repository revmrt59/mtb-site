ENTERED - ( bo , Strong's H0935 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bo in Obadiah 1:13 depicts the malicious entry of Edom into Jerusalem ("the gate of My people") during a time of defeat and calamity. Rather than offering aid to their brother nation, the Edomites entered as opportunistic intruders to exploit and plunder. This unauthorized physical entry represents a deep betrayal of kinship and a violation of God's covenant people, which ultimately guaranteed divine judgment upon Edom.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verb bo is one of the most frequently used words in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 2,500 times. At its core, the word presents a physical picture of motion—crossing a threshold, entering a designated boundary, or arriving at a specific destination. In the ancient Near East, the gate of a city was not merely a point of transit but the center of civil life, commerce, administration, and military defense. To "enter the gate" of a city meant to step into its most secure and sacred communal space. In times of war, an enemy entering the gate signaled the total collapse of defense and the beginning of subjection and plunder.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of bo is to come, enter, or go in. It functions in several ways across the Old Testament:

• Spatial Entry: Physically moving into a house, tent, city, or land.

• Temporal Progression: The setting of the sun (going down) or the coming of a specific day or season.

• Relational or Legal Entry: Entering into a covenant, an oath, or a state of peace.

• Causative Action: When used in the causative stem (hiphil), it means to bring, lead, or present.

In Obadiah 1:13, the verb is used in the simple active stem (qal), denoting the physical, historical act of the Edomites marching through the shattered gates of Jerusalem after the city had fallen to foreign invaders.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The physical movement of bo often serves as a metaphor for deeper spiritual and moral realities:

• Crossing Boundaries: It represents the transition from neutrality to active participation. Edom did not merely watch Jerusalem's fall from a distance; they physically "entered" the space, crossing a line from passive observation to active transgression.

• Spiritual Intrusion: The tabernacle and temple were spaces where only authorized priests could enter (bo). Unauthorized entry was punishable by death. By entering Jerusalem—the city of God's presence—to exploit it, Edom committed a form of sacrilege.

### 4. Theological Implications

Edom and Israel shared a common lineage through the twin brothers Esau and Jacob. Consequently, the Lord commanded Israel not to abhor an Edomite because he was a brother. Edom's physical entry into the gates of Jerusalem during Judah's crisis was a profound violation of this family relationship.

Theologically, this passage demonstrates that God fiercely guards His people, even when He is in the process of disciplining them. Jerusalem was experiencing God's righteous judgment at the hands of the Babylonians. However, Edom had no authority to join in the destruction. By entering the gate of "My people," Edom touched the apple of God's eye, bringing swift and irreversible divine judgment upon themselves.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:13 uses a series of urgent prohibitions, translated as "You should not have..." in the NKJV. There is a progressive descent in Edom's sinful actions:

• First, they entered (bo) the gate.

• Second, they gazed (ra'ah) on Judah's affliction with malicious pleasure.

• Third, they laid hands (shalach) on Judah's wealth.

Entering the gate was the physical catalyst for the sins that followed. Once inside the broken walls, Edom was consumed by greed and cruelty. The use of the term "My people" by Yahweh in this verse emphasizes that although Judah was under judgment, God had not disowned them. Edom was entering territory that still belonged to the Lord.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The same Hebrew verb bo (H0935) appears in various contexts to illustrate different aspects of physical and spiritual entry:

• Genesis 7:13 - "On that very same day Noah and Noah’s sons, Shem, Ham, and Japheth, and Noah’s wife and the three wives of his sons with them, entered the ark—" (Physical entry for safety and salvation).

• Psalm 100:4 - "Enter into His gates with thanksgiving, And into His courts with praise; Be thankful to Him, and bless His name." (Entering a holy space with the correct heart attitude).

• Proverbs 11:2 - "When pride comes, then comes shame; But with the humble is wisdom." (The inevitable arrival of spiritual consequences).

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The gate of Jerusalem was meant to be a place of justice and protection. For Edom to enter the gate of God's people in "the day of their calamity" highlights the absolute reversal of natural justice. Edom turned a place of refuge into a trap, even going so far as to stand at the crossroads to cut off Judah's fugitives (Obadiah 1:14). This physical entry was the ultimate expression of pride, which God promises to bring down.
