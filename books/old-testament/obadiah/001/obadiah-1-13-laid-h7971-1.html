LAID - (shâlach, Strong's H7971)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shalach fundamentally means to send forth, stretch out, extend, or release. It is used across various contexts for dispatching messengers, extending a hand to perform an action, or sending forth divine actions. In Obadiah 1:13, the verb describes Edom stretching out their hands to plunder and lay hold of Judah's physical wealth in the day of Jerusalem's distress. In this verse, shalach marks the escalation of Edom's sin from passive, malicious observation to active, predatory theft against their brother nation.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root shalach is a foundational Hebrew action verb denoting movement initiated by an agent toward a target or destination. In ancient Near Eastern cultural and legal settings, the verb applied to sending envoys, releasing captives, stretching forth weapons or tools, or extending a hand to claim or seize property.

In the prophetic vision of Obadiah, the historical setting centers on the fall of Jerusalem under Babylonian invasion. Obadiah delivers a sharp indictment against the neighboring nation of Edom, descendants of Esau. Rather than displaying fraternal loyalty or grief over Judah's downfall, Edom engaged in a series of hostile acts. Obadiah 1:13 captures the physical execution of Edom's treachery: extending their hands to appropriate the remaining goods of the defeated Judeans.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shalach signifies the act of extending, reaching out, sending, or releasing. Its core semantic range includes:

  • To send a person, message, or object from one location to another.

  • To extend or stretch out an extremity (such as a hand, arm, or staff) toward something.

  • To let loose, set free, or release from confinement.

In Obadiah 1:13, the target occurrence is in the Qal stem. The Qal stem represents the simple active verbal form in Hebrew, expressing the straightforward action of reaching out or extending. Here, paired with an object representing material wealth or substance, shalach carries the specific sense of stretching out hands to lay hold of, seize, or plunder goods. The Qal stem simply denotes the active performance of the physical action, leaving the moral gravity of the act to be established by the surrounding context.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament Scriptures, shalach functions as a highly versatile action verb used in several distinct contexts:

  • Diplomatic and Personal Sending: It frequently describes monarchs or individuals dispatching messengers, servants, or military forces to accomplish a mission.

  • Divine Commissioning and Execution: The term frequently expresses God sending forth prophets, plagues, judgment, or deliverers to carry out His redemptive or judicial will.

  • Physical Extension of Hands: When shalach governs "hand" (yad), it can refer to extending help, offering a blessing, or conversely, stretching out a hand in violence, theft, or rebellion.

In passages dealing with property and law, stretching out one's hand against another's possessions denotes illegal appropriation and deliberate robbery. Thus, the nuance of shalach shifts according to the object and intention behind the extension.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of shalach in Obadiah 1:13 carries significant theological and ethical implications:

  • Covenantal Ethics and Kinship: Edom was related to Israel by lineage through Esau and Jacob. By reaching out to plunder Judah, Edom violated both basic humanity and the natural bonds of kinship.

  • Active Participation in Evil: Gloating over a neighbor's ruin is a sin of the heart, but stretching out the hand to seize their goods translates internal malice into open transgression. God holds nations accountable not only for internal hostility but for concrete acts of exploitation.

  • Divine Retribution: The God of Israel guards the helpless and judges those who profit from the ruin of others. Prophetic theology establishes that hands stretched out to exploit God's covenant people will ultimately face the stretched-out hand of divine judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:12–14 forms a tightly structured series of poetic prohibitions ("you should not have..."), detailing Edom's progressive involvement in Jerusalem's disaster.

  • Verse 12 begins with visual and verbal participation: looking down on Judah and gloating over their destruction.

  • Verse 13 advances the offense to physical violation: entering the gate, gazing upon calamity, and finally, laying hands on their substance (halikah / chel).

In Obadiah 1:13, shalach marks the climax of Edom's direct physical intervention in the crisis. Edom did not merely observe Judah's collapse; they reached out into the void of Jerusalem's defenses to expropriate property. The immediate context clarifies that Edom's physical act of reaching out to seize wealth was an act of high treason against brotherly obligation, transforming them from passive spectators into active perpetrators of plunder.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Irony of Retributive Justice: In Obadiah 1:13, Edom extends (shalach) their hands to loot Judah's substance. Later in the prophetic corpus, the same verb describes God sending (shalach) instruments of judgment against Edom itself (as reflected in Amos 1:12). The poetic irony highlights that those who extend their hands in plunder will find divine judgment sent against their own strongholds.

  • The Progression of Sin: Obadiah's sequence reveals how ungodly attitudes escalate into ungodly actions. The heart that rejoices at another's fall will eventually stretch out its hand to exploit their vulnerability.
