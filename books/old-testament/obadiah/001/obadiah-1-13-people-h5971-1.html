PEOPLE - (ʻam, Strong's H5971)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun ʿam primarily denotes a corporate body of human beings linked by common descent, covenant, political allegiance, or kinship ties, translated as "people," "nation," or "kin." In Obadiah 1:13, the Lord refers to Judah specifically as "My people," highlighting the enduring covenant bond between God and His chosen collective that Edom violated when it entered Jerusalem's gate and exploited Judah's day of disaster.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew noun ʿam is a foundational term in Old Testament social and theological thought. It designates a collective human group bound together by shared lineage, social structure, localized identity, or solemn commitment. While other Hebrew terms for nationhood emphasize political structures, geographic territory, or physical power, ʿam highlights organic unity, mutual obligation, and relational identity among members of a community. In ancient Near Eastern contexts, national identity was closely linked to deity worship and ancestral heritage; for Israel, being a ʿam was uniquely defined by Yahweh's sovereign election and covenant promise.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, ʿam carries a core meaning of corporate human collectivity:

  • A national or ethnic entity bound by common ancestry, culture, or tribal origin.

  • A covenant community or religious assembly standing in specific relation to Yahweh or a deity.

  • Kinsmen, relatives, or lineage groups, emphasizing familial roots and blood kinship.

  • A population group, civic assembly, or body of citizens subject to a ruler or leader.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament historical, prophetic, and poetic texts, ʿam functions as the primary designation for corporate identity. When used of non-Israelite nations, the term describes distinct ethnic or geographical groups. However, when applied to Israel, ʿam receives a distinctive theological weight. It is frequently modified by divine possessive pronouns—most notably "My people" (ʿammi). This formula elevates a population from a generic ethnic gathering into a distinct community marked by divine ownership, covenant responsibility, and divine protection.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, ʿam anchors the biblical truth of God's covenant loyalty. Yahweh does not relate to His elect merely as scattered individuals, but as a corporate entity—His treasured ʿam. When God designates a group as "My people," He commits His own character, protection, and redemptive oversight to their preservation. Consequently, an assault upon God's ʿam is not merely an act of human hostility; it is an offense against the covenant honor of Yahweh Himself. Foreign nations that mistreat God's people invite divine judgment for despising the sacred bond existing between Yahweh and His corporate heritage.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:13, God addresses the nation of Edom regarding its predatory behavior during the fall of Jerusalem. The target occurrence of ʿam in Obadiah 1:13 is masculine singular. The divine speaker explicitly identifies Judah as "My people."

The immediate context heightens Edom's guilt through contrasts and repeated temporal markers ("in the day of their calamity"). Edom shared ancestral kinship with Judah, descended from Esau, the brother of Jacob. Yet when Jerusalem fell under God's sovereign judgment, Edom did not show brotherly compassion. Instead, Edom crossed into the gate of "My people," gloated over their ruin, and seized their property. By speaking of Judah as "My people," Yahweh asserts that despite the severe chastisement Jerusalem was enduring, Judah's covenant relationship with God was intact. Edom mistakenly viewed Jerusalem's destruction as proof that God had abandoned Judah, leading Edom to plunder the city freely. Yahweh's use of ʿam in this verse demonstrates that divine discipline does not annul covenant ownership, and those who exploit the suffering of God's people will answer directly to Him.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ruth 3:11 — "And now, my daughter, do not fear. I will do for you all that you request, for all the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman." Here ʿam describes the local civic community of Bethlehem who observe and affirm Ruth's godly conduct.

  • 2 Chronicles 1:11 — "Then God said to Solomon: 'Because this was in your heart... and have asked wisdom and knowledge for yourself, that you may judge My people over whom I have made you king...'" In this royal context, ʿam refers to the covenant nation placed under the shepherd-stewardship of the king.

  • Isaiah 34:5 — "For My sword shall be bathed in heaven; indeed it shall come down on Edom, and on the people of My curse, for judgment." Here ʿam designates Edom as a corporate group designated for divine judgment, standing in stark contrast to God's holy nation.

  • Jeremiah 31:14 — "'I will satiate the soul of the priests with abundance, and My people shall be satisfied with My goodness,' says the Lord." In this promise of restoration, ʿam describes God's renewed covenant community receiving Yahweh's covenant abundance.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A profound thematic lesson in Obadiah is the divine distinction between God's internal discipline of His ʿam and a pagan nation's malice toward them. Yahweh permitted foreign armies to discipline Judah, yet He fiercely held Edom accountable for its pride, gloating, and opportunistic violence. Edom mistook God's temporal judgment upon His people for final repudiation. Yahweh's declaration of "My people" in Obadiah 1:13 reminds the reader that God's covenant commitments endure through trials, and He remains the vigilant defender of those who belong to Him.
