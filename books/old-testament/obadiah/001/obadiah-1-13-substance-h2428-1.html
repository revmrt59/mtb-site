SUBSTANCE - (chayil, Strong's H2428)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun chayil fundamentally denotes capacity, strength, efficiency, or power. Depending on the context, this capacity can express itself as physical vigor, military force, moral capability, or material wealth and possessions. In Obadiah 1:13, chayil refers specifically to material wealth, goods, and property ("substance"). In this prophetic indictment, the word underscores Edom's opportunistic transgression: rather than showing brotherly compassion during Jerusalem's downfall, Edom actively reached out to seize and plunder the remaining material goods and wealth of God's distressed covenant people.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The prophecy of Obadiah centers on divine judgment pronounced against the nation of Edom, descendants of Esau. When foreign invaders breached Jerusalem and devastated the city, Edom failed to act with brotherly solidarity toward Judah. Instead, Edom stood aloof, rejoiced over Judah's ruin, intercepted fleeing refugees, and actively participated in looting the fallen city.

The Hebrew noun chayil carries a basic sense of strength or competence, whether embodied in a mighty warrior, a disciplined military force, a person of noble character, or accumulated material resources. In the agrarian and pastoral economy of the ancient Near East, a household's or nation's "strength" was directly reflected in its tangible assets—livestock, silver, gold, and household goods. Thus, chayil frequently denotes the physical goods and wealth that give a family or community material stability and strength.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, chayil covers a broad semantic range bound together by the underlying concept of power or capacity:

  • Strength and Vigor: Physical energy, endurance, or ability.

  • Military Might / Force: An army, a battalion, or a body of capable soldiers ("men of valor").

  • Ability and Moral Character: Moral strength, efficiency, or capability (as in a "virtuous" or "capable" person).

  • Wealth and Substance: Property, riches, goods, or resources accumulated through labor or inheritance.

In contexts involving plunder, war spoil, or economic inheritance, chayil refers specifically to accumulated possessions, movable goods, and financial resources.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, chayil exhibits distinct contextual nuances:

  • Military Contexts: The word often designates armed forces or individual warriors characterized by bravery and capability ("mighty man of valor").

  • Moral Contexts: When applied to individuals in civic or domestic settings, it highlights integrity, competence, and reliability.

  • Material Contexts: When referring to possessions, chayil highlights the power that wealth provides. To possess chayil is to hold material security; to lose chayil is to be stripped of economic viability.

In prophetic speeches dealing with international conflicts and judgment, chayil frequently highlights the spoil or wealth seized by conquerors or pillagers.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of chayil in prophetic warnings reveals significant theological truths regarding divine justice and human accountability:

  • Divine Sovereign Ownership: All human strength and wealth (chayil) ultimately belong to God. Accumulating wealth through violence or opportunistic plunder brings divine retribution.

  • Judgment on Exploitation: God strictly forbids taking malicious advantage of the vulnerable. When Edom seized Judah's chayil in their day of disaster, Edom defied the covenant obligations of kinship and transgressed God's moral order.

  • Ill-Gotten Wealth Is Fleeting: Prophetic theology consistently establishes that property pillaged from the afflicted will not grant lasting security. God strips the arrogant of their unrighteous gain.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:13, the prophet delivers a series of dramatic prohibitions detailing what Edom ought not to have done during Jerusalem's catastrophe: "You should not have entered the gate of My people in the day of their calamity. Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction in the day of their calamity, nor laid hands on their substance (chayil) in the day of their calamity."

The target word in Obadiah 1:13 appears in the masculine singular absolute form.

The passage constructs a literary progression of Edom's complicity:

1. Spatial intrusion: Entering the city gates.

2. Malicious observation: Gazing with satisfaction upon Judah's suffering.

3. Physical plunder: Reaching out hands to seize Judah's chayil (substance/wealth).

Here, chayil represents the surviving property, goods, and resources left to the traumatized inhabitants of Jerusalem. By laying hands on their chayil, Edom actively enriched itself at the expense of its suffering brother nation. The word highlights the predatory nature of Edom's crime: exploiting another's utter weakness to increase their own material force and property.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 34:29 – "And all their wealth, all their little ones and their wives they took captive; and they looted all that was in the houses."

  • Judges 6:12 – "And the Angel of the LORD appeared to him, and said to him, 'The LORD is with you, you mighty man of valor!'"

  • Job 20:18 – "He will restore that for which he labored, and will not swallow it down; from the wealth that he gained he will have no enjoyment."

  • Joel 2:25 – "So I will restore to you the years that the swarming locust has eaten, the crawling locust, the consuming locust, and the chewing locust, My great army which I sent among you."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural repetition in Obadiah 1:12–14 emphasizes Edom's progressive boundary-crossing during "the day of their calamity." The phrase "laid hands on their substance" exposes that Edom was not merely a passive bystander to foreign invasion, but an active participant in robbing God's judgment-chastened people. The ironic reversal in Obadiah is that the wealth (chayil) Edom violently snatched from Judah would later be pillaged from Edom itself when God summoned nations to execute judgment against them.
