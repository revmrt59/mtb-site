DAY - (yôwm, Strong's H3117)

Summary:

Lexically, yom refers to a "day," designating a 24-hour solar period, daylight hours, or a specific time frame defined by a particular event or prevailing condition. In Obadiah 1:14, yom refers to the specific, appointed time of Judah's acute crisis and suffering—the "day of distress." In this context, the word defines the crucial window of Jerusalem's vulnerability during which Edom maliciously intercepted fleeing refugees and surrendered them to their enemies, highlighting Edom's betrayal during their brother's deepest hour of need.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The prophecy of Obadiah is directed against the nation of Edom, descendants of Esau, brother of Jacob. Despite their shared ancestral bloodline, Edom maintained an enduring animosity toward Israel. Obadiah delivers a severe indictment against Edom for their conduct during a period when Jerusalem was overthrown and pillaged. Rather than coming to the aid of their kin or remaining neutral, Edom gloated over Judah's downfall, plundered their property, cut off escaping survivors at mountain passes, and handed them over to the invading forces. Obadiah 1:14 captures the tragic climax of this fraternal betrayal, setting the scene for God's impending judgment upon Edom.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew noun yom is the foundational term for time and day in the Old Testament. Lexically, it carries three primary senses depending on context:

  • The daylight hours as contrasted with night.

  • A standard 24-hour cycle (a single solar day).

  • A specific period, era, season, or appointed time characterized by a specific circumstance or event (e.g., "day of calamity," "day of battle," "day of trouble").

In constructs where yom is paired with a genitive noun or modifier (such as yom tzarah, "day of distress"), the noun shifts from describing a strict measure of chronological time to defining the specific moral, historical, or experiential character of that time frame.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, yom is frequently joined with descriptive terms to highlight pivotal historical moments or prophetic events. When linked with words for suffering or judgment—such as tzarah (distress, anguish), eyd (calamity), or neker (misfortune)—yom functions as a qualitative marker. It pinpoints a time when crisis compresses normal life into an intense crucible of suffering.

Additionally, yom forms the central structural element of the prophetic phrase "the Day of the LORD" (yom YHWH). In this solemn usage, the term shifts from human history to divine intervention, denoting the moment when God acts decisively to punish covenant unfaithfulness and avenge the cause of His righteous people.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of yom in Obadiah centers on divine providence and moral accountability within history. Human events occur within designated periods of time ("days"), and God observes how nations conduct themselves during these specific seasons. Judah's "day of distress" was an appointed time of divine chastisement upon Jerusalem, yet Edom was not excused for treating their brother's distress as an opportunity for predatory gain.

God's defense of His covenant people establishes that cruelty during a victim's "day" of trouble invites retribution in God's "day" of reckoning. The temporal "day of distress" endured by human beings ultimately gives way to the cosmic "Day of the LORD," wherein perfect divine justice is meted out.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:11–14, the prophet constructs a poetic series of eight prohibition couplets ("You should not have..."), repeatedly employing yom to highlight the various dimensions of Judah's fall: "the day of your brother," "the day of their destruction," "the day of their calamity," and finally, in verse 14, "the day of distress."

Grammatically, yom appears here in the masculine singular noun form within the construct phrase b'yom tzarah ("in the day of distress"). The syntax connects the timing of Edom's actions directly to Judah's crisis. Edom did not merely witness Judah's collapse; they calculated their actions to exploit the exact window when Judah was helpless. Standing at the crossroads to capture and deliver refugees during "the day of distress" demonstrated deliberate malice, converting a period meant for fraternal compassion into an exercise of ruthless opportunism.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 2 Kings 19:3 – "And they said to him, 'Thus says Hezekiah: "This day is a day of trouble and rebuke and blasphemy..."'" Here the noun describes a specific, critical period of national distress and existential threat facing Judah under Assyrian siege.

  • Lamentations 1:7 – "In the days of her affliction and roaming, Jerusalem remembered all her pleasant things..." The word designates the period of grief and captivity following Jerusalem's destruction.

  • Isaiah 13:22 – "...Her time is near to come, and her days will not be prolonged." Used prophetically to mark the limited timeframe before divine judgment falls upon Babylon.

  • Exodus 29:37 – "Seven days you shall make atonement for the altar, and sanctify it." Here the noun denotes standard 24-hour periods designated for ritual consecration.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A profound literary link exists between Judah's "day" in Obadiah 1:14 and the phrase in Obadiah 1:15: "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near." The prophet deliberately transitions from Edom's exploitation of Judah's temporary "day of distress" to God's universal "day of the LORD." The text creates a direct principle of divine retribution: "As you have done, it shall be done to you; your reprisal shall return upon your own head" (Obadiah 1:15). The short-lived human day of opportunistic triumph is swallowed up by the enduring divine day of righteous judgment.
