DELIVERED - (çâgar, Strong's H5462)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb sagar fundamentally means to shut, close, or enclose. In its causative stem, it carries the specialized sense of surrendering, handing over, or delivering someone into the custody and power of an enemy, effectively shutting off all means of escape. In Obadiah 1:14, sagar describes Edom's active, treacherous betrayal of Judah's survivors by capturing them and handing them over to the Babylonian invaders during Jerusalem's fall. The word emphasizes Edom's calculated cruelty and total violation of brotherly obligation, turning what should have been a place of refuge into a prison trap for God's distressed people.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The primary physical concept behind sagar is the act of shutting or blocking an entry, such as closing a door, sealing a city gate, or trapping something within an enclosure. In the cultural context of ancient Near Eastern warfare, to "shut someone up" into the hands of an adversary was an idiom for surrendering captives or traitorously delivering fugitives into the control of their foes.

In the book of Obadiah, the prophet pronounces judgment against the nation of Edom, descendants of Esau, for their actions during the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem. Rather than showing covenantal compassion toward their brother nation Israel, Edom stood aloof, gloated over their disaster, looted their possessions, and actively intercepted fleeing survivors at the crossroads to hand them over to the conquerors. The use of sagar pinpoints this final act of hostility: handing over defenceless refugees to their bitter enemies.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, sagar denotes the physical action of closing or sealing off. Depending on the context, the verb ranges from shutting a literal door or gate to isolating a person or object for inspection or quarantine.

In the Hiphil stem (the causative stem present in Obadiah 1:14), the root expands causatively to mean "to cause to be closed in" or "to surrender/deliver up." When used with a preposition specifying "into the hand of" an enemy, it means depriving a person of liberty by placing them entirely under the power, authority, and control of an opponent. Thus, lexical definitions establish that in this causative context, sagar does not mean rescuing or liberating, but rather trapping and surrendering a victim into hostile hands.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, sagar serves in a variety of literary settings:

  • Physical Closure: It describes closing physical barriers, such as doors, gates, or the entrance of an enclosure.

  • Confinement and Isolation: In legal and priestly texts, it refers to isolating or quarantining individuals suspected of infectious diseases to contain contamination.

  • Military Surrender and Betrayal: In historical narratives and prophetic texts, the causative form frequently denotes handing over military targets, fugitives, or political opponents into the hands of an enemy.

The nuance of the term hinges on who is doing the shutting and for what purpose. When God shuts someone in, it can signify protection or sovereign control. However, when human actors execute this action against their brethren, it signifies calculated betrayal, entrapment, and an act of cruel hostility that strips the victim of any human avenue of escape.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of sagar in prophetic judgment carries significant theological weight regarding divine justice and human accountability:

  • Violation of Covenantal Obligation: Edom was linked to Israel through shared ancestry (Esau and Jacob). Handing over fleeing brothers into enemy hands violated the basic moral expectations of kinship. The word exposes Edom's total abandon of mercy in favor of opportunistic malice.

  • Divine Sovereignty over Human Deliverance: Scripture presents God as the ultimate One who holds the power to deliver up or rescue. When human nations arrogantly seize this prerogative to destroy God's chosen people, they incur divine wrath.

  • Lex Talionis (Just Retribution): Obadiah’s prophecy emphasizes that Edom’s act of surrendering Israel’s survivors would be turned back upon their own heads. Those who close the door of mercy on others will ultimately find themselves closed out of divine protection.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:12–14 forms a rhetorical climax depicting Edom's escalating hostility during Jerusalem’s destruction. The passage moves through a sequence of forbidden actions, transitioning from passive gloating ("do not gaze") to active rejoice in destruction, to entering the city gates, to looting property, to standing at the crossroads to cut off escape, and finally to sagar—delivering up the remaining survivors.

The target occurrence of sagar carries specific grammatical features:

  • Stem: Hiphil (causative)

  • Form: imperfect

  • Person: 2nd person

  • Gender: masculine

  • Number: singular

The Hiphil stem explicitly underlines Edom's active causality: Edom did not merely watch Babylon capture refugees; Edom actively caused those refugees to be trapped and delivered into Babylonian hands. Combined with the negative particle in the prohibition sequence, the imperfect form communicates a strong prohibition against this ongoing, calculated act of surrender ("nor should you have delivered up those among them who remained").

In the immediate context, those "who remained" were the helpless survivors who had escaped the initial military assault on Jerusalem. Edom's act of sagar eliminated their last earthly hope, turning the geography surrounding Judah into a fatal snare.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 7:16 – "So those that entered, male and female of all flesh, went in as God had commanded him; and the LORD shut him in."

  • 1 Samuel 23:11 – "Will the men of Keilah deliver me into his hand? Will Saul come down, as Your servant has heard?"

  • 1 Samuel 26:8 – "Then Abishai said to David, 'God has delivered your enemy into your hand this day. Now therefore, please let me strike him at once...'"

  • Isaiah 22:22 – "The key of the house of David I will lay on his shoulder; so he shall open, and no one shall shut; and he shall shut, and no one shall open."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The semantic contrast in Scripture between opening and shutting highlights the absolute nature of sagar. When a person or city is "shut up" (sagar) by an enemy or by God, human strength cannot reverse the condition.

Furthermore, the structural progression in Obadiah 1:12–14 demonstrates how sin develops in intensity. What began as a inner attitude of pride and gloating eventually materialized as open violence and, ultimately, the active act of turning fugitives over to death. The word sagar marks the final, irrevocable threshold of Edom's treachery in the passage.
