DAY - ( yom , Strong's H3117 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word yom refers to a literal twenty-four-hour period, the daylight hours, or more broadly, an appointed time, epoch, or season of significant events. In Obadiah 1:14, it designates a specific, historic period of calamity and judgment—the "day of distress"—wherein the nation of Edom actively betrayed their brother nation, Judah, during Jerusalem's fall.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near Eastern world, time was understood qualitatively as much as quantitatively. A "day" was not merely a tick of the clock or a unit of calendar time; it was defined by the events that filled it. A day of harvest, a day of battle, or a day of rain each carried a distinct character. In Hebrew thought, when a specific event or condition characterizes a period, that period is called the "day of" that occurrence. The background of yom in prophetic literature often carries a heavy covenantal weight, pointing to specific windows of divine visitation, whether for blessing or, as in the book of Obadiah, for severe national trial and judgment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of yom is the period of light between dawn and dusk, or a full twenty-four-hour cycle. Beyond this literal sense, it frequently functions as a term for an indefinite but distinct period of time, a season, or a historical epoch. When paired with genitives like "distress," "trouble," or "calamity," yom identifies the precise duration of a historical crisis. The semantic range includes:

• The warm, daylight hours of a single day.

• A literal day of twenty-four hours.

• A specific, appointed time or historical event (e.g., "the day of Jerusalem").

• An extended, indefinite period of time or era (e.g., "in the days of the judges").

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, yom is used in rich idiomatic and metaphorical expressions to depict divine activity.

• The "Day of the LORD" (yom YHWH) is the most prominent prophetic word picture, representing a decisive intervention of God in human history to judge wickedness and vindicate His holiness.

• A "day of distress" or "day of trouble" paints the picture of a narrow, high-pressure space of time where human strength fails and divine aid is desperately needed.

• The plural form (yamim) is often used to capture the span of a person's life or the duration of a king's reign, emphasizing the temporary, God-apportioned nature of human existence.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the use of yom demonstrates God's absolute sovereignty over time and history. Human history is not a chaotic spiral of random events; it is a structured tapestry of "days" appointed and measured by God. Every nation has its "day" of opportunity, its "day" of testing, and ultimately, its "day" of accountability. Judah's "day of distress" was a period of divine chastisement under the Babylonians. Edom's failure to show mercy during this appointed time of sisterly grief revealed their deep-seated pride and rebellion, which in turn guaranteed their own coming "day" of irrevocable judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:14, the word yom is used in the phrase "in the day of distress" (bə-yōm ṣā-rāh). This verse sits within a relentless rhetorical catalog of Edom's sins against Judah during the Babylonian siege of Jerusalem in 586 BC. Obadiah uses the word yom ten times in verses 11–14 to drive home the precise timing of Edom's treachery:

• The day that you stood on the other side (v. 11)

• The day of his captivity (v. 12)

• The day of their destruction (v. 12)

• The day of distress (v. 12)

• The day of their calamity (v. 13, repeated three times)

• The day of distress (v. 14)

Edom did not merely watch Judah's suffering from a distance; they exploited this "day" of vulnerability. They stood at the crossroads to intercept and slaughter the fleeing Judean refugees, and delivered the survivors over to the enemy. The repetition of yom emphasizes that Edom's sin was committed during a specific, tragic window of time when brotherly compassion was most demanded but entirely denied.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:5 - God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night. So the evening and the morning were the first day.

• Joel 2:1 - Blow the trumpet in Zion, and sound an alarm in My holy mountain! Let all the inhabitants of the land tremble; for the day of the LORD is coming, for it is at hand:

• Zephaniah 1:15 - That day is a day of wrath, a day of trouble and distress, a day of devastation and desolation, a day of darkness and gloominess, a day of clouds and thick darkness,

• Obadiah 1:15 - For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near; as you have done, it shall be done to you; your reprisal shall return upon your own head.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew grammar, when yom is construct with a noun of distress or calamity, it highlights the intense reality of the experience. It is not an abstract concept of suffering, but a concrete historical reality. Additionally, the contrast between Judah's "day of distress" (v. 14) and the impending "day of the LORD" upon Edom (v. 15) serves as a powerful literary and theological pivot. The temporary "day" of human triumph and persecution is rapidly superseded by the eternal "day" of divine justice.
