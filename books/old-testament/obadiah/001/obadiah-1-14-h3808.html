NOR - ( lo' , Strong's 3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle lo' (often conjoined with ve to form ve-lo' meaning "nor") is the primary and absolute adverb of negation in the Old Testament. While the poetic text of Obadiah 1:14 employs the immediate, prohibitive particle 'al (Strong's 408) to deliver an urgent, direct warning to Edom, both particles function together within Hebrew syntax to draw uncompromising moral boundaries. In this passage, "nor" marks the tragic climax of Edom’s betrayal: actively handing over Judah's fleeing survivors to their enemies. By using this absolute negative framework, the text transitions from a mere historical description of Edom’s actions to a devastating moral indictment, demonstrating that crossing God's boundaries of mercy inevitably invites His absolute justice.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew particle lo' represents the absolute, permanent, and objective negation of a statement, command, or reality. Unlike the subjective, emotional prohibitive particle 'al—which appeals to the immediate will of the listener ("do not let it be")—lo' establishes a fixed, objective state of non-existence or absolute prohibition. It is the word used throughout the Ten Commandments to set eternal boundaries for human behavior.

When prefixed with the conjunction ve- ("and"), it becomes ve-lo', functioning as "nor" or "neither" in English. This conjunction chains consecutive prohibited actions together, highlighting a progressive descent into sin. In the Ancient Near East, familial treaties and brotherly covenants (such as the relationship between Israel and Edom, descended from the twin brothers Jacob and Esau) carried binding moral obligations. The cumulative use of these negative particles in Obadiah 1:12-14 illustrates the systematic dismantling of this brotherly bond, showing that Edom did not merely stand by, but actively progressed from gloating to looting, and finally to active betrayal.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word lo' is an adverb of negation denoting absolute denial, prohibition, or non-existence. It negates verbs, nouns, adjectives, and entire clauses.

• In prohibitive contexts, it expresses a permanent, universal command: "you must not" or "you shall not."

• When combined with the conjunction ve-, it acts as a coordinating negative conjunction ("nor," "and not"), extending the force of the previous negation to subsequent actions.

• While the immediate Hebrew text of Obadiah 1:14 employs 'al (Strong's 408) for immediate rhetorical force ("do not stand... nor deliver up"), the theological and syntactic weight of these prohibitions carries the absolute, objective authority of lo' (Strong's 3808), which characterizes the divine standards Edom violated.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Absolute Prohibition: lo' is the primary vehicle for divine law. Its usage in the Decalogue sets boundaries that are non-negotiable and eternal. It serves as a linguistic wall separating holy behavior from unholy behavior.

• Covenant Failure: In prophetic indictments, the negative particle highlights the failure of the nations to maintain basic moral boundaries. The "nor" in Obadiah 1:14 acts as a logical and moral dead-end. The sequence of actions—looking, rejoicing, speaking proudly, entering the gate, gloating, laying hands on substance, standing at the crossroads, and delivering up survivors—concludes with this absolute "nor," depicting a complete collapse of human decency.

• The Snare at the Crossroads: The imagery of "standing at the crossroads" combined with "nor should you have delivered up" paints a picture of a brother acting as an active trap or a hunter's net, turning a place of potential escape into a place of capture.

### 4. Theological Implications

• Universal Accountability: God holds even non-covenant nations (like Edom) to absolute moral standards. The series of "not" and "nor" statements indicates that Edom’s actions were not merely bad choices but direct violations of natural law and brotherly obligation.

• Divine Justice: Because Edom ignored the divine "nor," they would face the law of lex talionis (the law of retaliation) in Obadiah 1:15: "As you have done, it shall be done to you." The violation of God's absolute negatives results in the absolute negation of Edom's national future.

• The Sovereignty of God in History: The negative commands mock Edom's historical actions retrospectively. Even though Edom historically did these things, God's decree stands: they should not have done them, and because they did, they must face judgment.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:12-14, the prophet delivers a rhythmic, stinging series of eight rhetorical prohibitions. The NKJV translates these with the phrase "You should not have..." followed by "Nor should you have..."

The literal Hebrew underlying "nor" in Obadiah 1:14 is the prohibitive particle 'al (Strong's 408) prefixed with the conjunction ve- ("and"). However, this prohibitive construction functions in tandem with the absolute negation of lo' (Strong's 3808). While 'al acts as an urgent appeal to the will ("do not do this!"), lo' represents the ultimate, objective moral boundaries established by God. By violating these prohibitions, Edom crossed from a failure of empathy to active participation in Judah's destruction. The final "nor" in verse 14 marks the absolute zenith of their treachery: standing at the escape routes to trap and hand over survivors to the Babylonian invaders. God's negative boundaries are designed to preserve mercy; breaking them invites His absolute, unyielding judgment.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew grammar, the transition from 'al (immediate, subjective prohibition, as literally written in the poetic text of Obadiah 1:14) to lo' (absolute, objective prohibition) highlights the dual nature of God's commands. God's specific, situational warnings are always rooted in His eternal, unchangeable moral character.

The Septuagint (LXX) translates these negative particles using mede ("nor") and methe, which carry the same subjective prohibitive force, warning against making the wrong moral choices in times of crisis.
