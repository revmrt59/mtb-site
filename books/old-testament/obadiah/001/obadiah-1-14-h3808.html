NOT - ( lo' , Strong's 3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle lo' expresses absolute, permanent negation, functioning as the ultimate "no" or "not" in the Old Testament. Unlike temporary, conditional, or emotional prohibitions, this word establishes unconditional boundaries, objective facts, and divine vetoes. In the context of prophetic indictments, such as the series of prohibitions surrounding Edom's betrayal of Judah in Obadiah, the negative particle stands as a fierce barrier against covenant cruelty. It demonstrates that God marks, limits, and ultimately forbids the exploitation of His people, turning human acts of defiance into moments of absolute divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primary negative adverb in biblical Hebrew is a simple, unyielding word. It acts like a closed door, an immovable wall, or a hard stop. In the ancient Near Eastern world, covenants and laws relied heavily on this word to draw sharp, non-negotiable lines between permissible and impermissible behavior. In legal texts, it establishes permanent prohibitions that are valid for all people at all times. While Hebrew possesses another negative particle—'al', which expresses a immediate, subjective warning or a temporary "do not"—this word establishes an objective, permanent, and absolute boundary. It represents the sovereign will of God asserting itself against human overreach.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

This particle functions as the absolute adverb of negation.

  • It signifies "no," "not," "neither," or "none" depending on the syntactic structure.

  • When paired with an imperfect verb, it indicates a permanent, categorical prohibition (such as "you shall not").

  • When modifying nouns, adjectives, or prepositions, it denotes complete non-existence, absence, or the absolute opposite of the term modified (e.g., "not My people").

  • It expresses objective reality, declaring what is not, what cannot be, or what must never be, independent of human opinion or passing circumstances.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Unbreakable Boundary: It functions as a spiritual and moral boundary stone. Just as physical boundary lines in Israel were not to be moved, this negative particle draws a line in the sand of human history that cannot be crossed without incurring judgment.

  • The Rhetorical Veto: In prophetic literature, it is frequently used in rhetorical questions to demand an undeniable affirmative answer from the listener, emphasizing that God’s sovereign purposes cannot be thwarted.

  • The Mirror of Reversal: It is used to describe the complete undoing of rebellious nations. When God applies His absolute "not" to a kingdom, it signifies a total negation of their power, presence, and legacy, reducing them to nothingness.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Limits of Human Pride: Human rebellion operates on the assumption of limitless freedom and self-determination. The divine negative particle stands as a monument to God's sovereignty, reminding humanity that there is an ultimate limit to what He will allow.

  • The Protective Nature of Divine Law: God's "shalls" and "shall nots" are expressions of His protective character. The boundaries He sets are designed to protect the weak, preserve covenant order, and maintain justice.

  • The Certainty of Retribution: When humans ignore God's prohibitions, they encounter the absolute negation of their own security. The God who commands "do not" is the same God who decrees "no more" when judgment falls.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:12-14, the prophet delivers a dramatic, multi-layered indictment against the nation of Edom for their malicious joy and active participation in the destruction of Jerusalem. The NKJV translates the repeated negative prohibitions in these verses as "should not have."

In the immediate Hebrew text of Obadiah 1:14, the negative commands utilize the particle 'al' (Strong's 408), which acts as a specific, urgent, and emotional veto against Edom's actions ("should not have stood at the crossroads"). However, this situational warning is inextricably linked to the absolute negative particle lo' (Strong's 3808) used elsewhere in the prophecy. Because Edom refused to heed the urgent moral "should not" ('al') of brotherly compassion, they run headlong into the absolute "not" (lo') of God's historical judgment. Edom's wise men will "not" survive (Obadiah 1:8), and the nation itself will ultimately be "as though they had never [lo'] been" (Obadiah 1:16). The passing warnings they ignored yield a permanent, absolute negation of their entire civilization.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:17: "...but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die."

  • Exodus 20:13: "You shall not murder."

  • Obadiah 1:8: "'Will I not in that day,' says the LORD, 'Even destroy the wise men from Edom, And understanding from the mountains of Esau?'"

  • Obadiah 1:16: "For as you drank on My holy mountain, So shall all the nations drink continually; Yes, they shall drink and swallow, And they shall be as though they had never been."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Contrast of Negatives: Biblical Hebrew carefully distinguishes between 'al' and lo'. The word 'al' is subjective, deprecatory, and often translated "do not" in a specific instance (equivalent to the Greek me). The word lo' is objective, absolute, and translated "shall not" or "not" (equivalent to the Greek ou). Edom's violation of the specific, situational prohibitions ('al') in Obadiah 1:12-14 resulted in the execution of God's absolute, unalterable sentence (lo') in Obadiah 1:16.

  • The Law of Retribution: Edom stood at the crossroads to cut off the survivors of Judah. Because they did not show mercy, God closed the door of survival to them. The negative particle highlights the perfect symmetry of divine justice: those who actively negate mercy will have their own future negated by God.
