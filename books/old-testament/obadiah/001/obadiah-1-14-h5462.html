DELIVERED UP - ( sagar , Strong's H5462 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb sagar fundamentally means to shut, close, or hand over. In the context of Obadiah 1:14, it carries the intense and tragic sense of "delivering up" or surrendering vulnerable survivors into the hands of their enemies. Rather than offering sanctuary to their brother Jacob (Judah) during the Babylonian invasion, the Edomites actively blockaded escape routes and handed over the helpless survivors to their captors. This word exposes the heart of betrayal—turning a place of potential safety into a trap and actively participating in the destruction of God's covenant people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root meaning of sagar is to close, shut, or bar a gate. It depicts an action that restricts movement, seals an opening, or walls someone in.

• In ancient near eastern warfare, to "shut someone up" or "deliver them up" meant to trap them so they had no way of escape, effectively handing them over to their captors. It is the opposite of providing open space, freedom, or refuge.

• When Edom "delivered up" the Judean refugees, they did not merely watch passively. They acted as a human wall, closing off the paths of escape and actively corralling the fugitives into the hands of the Babylonian forces.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb sagar occurs frequently in the Old Testament with several nuances depending on its grammatical stem. In its basic Qal stem, it means to shut or close (such as closing a door or shutting up a city).

• In the Hiphil (causative) stem, which is used in Obadiah 1:14, the meaning shifts to "hand over," "give up," or "deliver up." This causative action means causing someone to be shut up within the power or custody of an enemy.

• It denotes a deliberate act of betrayal where one party surrenders helpless individuals to a hostile power, leaving them with no means of defense or escape.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The verb sagar is used in Scripture to describe:

• Physical closing: Shutting Noah's ark (Genesis 7:16), shutting a door (Genesis 19:10), or closing a city gate (Joshua 2:5).

• Confinement: Shutting up a person suspected of leprosy (Leviticus 13:4) or a house suspected of plague (Leviticus 14:38).

• Surrendering to an enemy: God delivering up His people because of their disobedience (Psalm 78:62) or David asking if the men of Keilah would deliver him up to Saul (1 Samuel 23:11).

• The word picture in Obadiah 1:14 is particularly sharp: the Edomites stood at the crossroads, acting as a lock on the door of escape, shutting the path of survival, and handing the keys over to Babylon.

4. Theological Implications

• God takes betrayal among relatives and neighbors deeply seriously. Edom and Judah shared a common ancestry through Esau and Jacob. By delivering up the survivors of Jerusalem, Edom violated the familial bond of brotherhood.

• Scripture reveals that God is the ultimate judge of those who exploit the vulnerability of His people. The act of "delivering up" those who are already afflicted invites God's righteous anger.

• Theologically, sagar reminds us of our ultimate need for a Deliverer. Left to ourselves under the law, we are "shut up" under sin and handed over to death. However, God does not deliver His faithful into the hands of the ultimate enemy but sets our feet in a spacious place (Psalm 31:8).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:14, the prophet delivers a scathing indictment of Edom's conduct during the fall of Jerusalem (likely in 586 B.C.). The Edomites did not merely rejoice in Judah's ruin; they actively participated in their demise.

• The phrase "nor should you have delivered up those among them who remained" highlights the final stage of Edom's treachery. They progressed from gloating to looting, then to standing at the crossroads to cut off fugitives, and finally to actively capturing and handing over the remaining survivors to the Babylonians.

• This contextual use of sagar stresses the absolute lack of mercy. Edom acted as bounty hunters, hunting down the weak and "delivering them up" to seal their doom.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• 1 Samuel 23:11: "Will the men of Keilah deliver me up into his hand? Will Saul come down, as Your servant has heard? O LORD God of Israel, I pray, tell Your servant." And the LORD said, "He will deliver you up."

• Psalm 31:8: "And have not shut me up into the hand of the enemy; You have set my feet in a wide place."

• Amos 1:6: "Thus says the LORD: 'For three transgressions of Gaza, and for four, I will not turn away its punishment, Because they took captive the whole captivity To deliver them up to Edom;'"

• Amos 1:9: "Thus says the LORD: 'For three transgressions of Tyre, and for four, I will not turn away its punishment, Because they delivered up the whole captivity to Edom, And did not remember the covenant of brotherhood.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between sagar (to shut up/hand over) and merchab (a broad, spacious place) in the Psalms illustrates the Hebrew concept of salvation. Salvation is moving from a state of being "shut up" or trapped (sagar) to being set free in a wide-open space.

• Edom’s betrayal in Obadiah prefigures the betrayal of Jesus Christ, who was also "delivered up" (paradidomi, the Greek equivalent used in the Septuagint for sagar in many contexts) by those who should have loved Him. Edom delivered up their brothers for gain and malice; Judas delivered up the Savior for thirty pieces of silver.
