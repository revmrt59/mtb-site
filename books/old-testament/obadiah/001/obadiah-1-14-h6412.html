ESCAPED - ( palit , Strong's H6412 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun palit refers to a fugitive, refugee, or survivor who has slipped away from a major catastrophe, such as a military invasion, slaughter, or divine judgment. In Obadiah 1:14, it depicts the vulnerable Judeans fleeing the horrific Babylonian siege of Jerusalem. Rather than showing brotherly compassion to these survivors, the Edomites stood at the mountain passes to intercept, capture, and hand them over to their enemies. This term highlights the extreme vulnerability of the refugees and underscores the cruel, treacherous nature of Edom's betrayal, which ultimately invited God's absolute judgment upon them.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun palit is derived from the verbal root palat, which means to escape, deliver, or slip away to safety. In the ancient Near East, warfare was devastating, and those who survived the initial onslaught of an invading army faced the perilous journey of fleeing as refugees. A palit is not merely someone who traveled; they are a survivor who physically slipped through the fingers of death or captivity. The word picture evokes a breathless, traumatized individual who has lost their home, livelihood, and family, running with nothing but their lives.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun palit denotes a fugitive, survivor, or refugee who has escaped a calamity—most frequently a military defeat or a localized slaughter. It refers to the remnant of a population that has undergone severe judgment. The term does not imply that the survivor escaped by their own strength or cunning, but rather notes the objective fact of their survival amidst widespread destruction.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, palit is used in contexts of war and judgment to distinguish those who survived from those who perished.

• It is used of a messenger who escapes a battlefield to bring news of defeat or destruction to those waiting at home.

• It is used in prophetic warnings to describe the near-total annihilation of a nation, where almost no palit (survivor) is left behind.

• It depicts the utter helplessness of the fugitive. Once a person becomes a palit, they are completely at the mercy of the elements, foreign nations, and God. They have no military defense, no legal protection, and no political standing.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the existence of a palit represents God’s mercy amidst wrath. Even in the execution of His righteous judgment upon Judah, God did not allow His people to be completely wiped out. The palit represents the physical seed of the surviving remnant. Therefore, to attack, exploit, or betray these survivors is to oppose God's preservation of His covenant people. Edom's hostility toward these fugitives was not just a humanitarian crime; it was a direct assault on the remnant whom God had spared.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:14, the palit are the Judeans fleeing the destruction of Jerusalem by the Babylonians in 586 B.C. The topography of the region required these refugees to flee eastward through the narrow ravines and mountain passes of the Judean wilderness toward the Jordan Valley and the territory of Edom. Edom, descended from Esau, was Israel's brother nation. Instead of offering sanctuary, Edom acted as a border patrol for the Babylonians, standing at the "crossroads" (the narrow forks in the mountain roads) to cut off the escape of the survivors and deliver them back to the conquerors. This betrayal violated both familial obligations and ancient laws of hospitality, sealing Edom's own doom.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 14:13: "Then one who had escaped came and told Abram the Hebrew, for he dwelt by the terebinth trees of Mamre the Amorite, brother of Eshcol and brother of Aner; and they were allies with Abram."

• Ezekiel 24:26: "that on that day he who escapes will come to you to let you hear it with your ears?"

• Lamentations 2:22: "You have invited as to a feast day My terrors from all around. In the day of the LORD’s anger There was no refugee or survivor. Those whom I have borne and brought up My enemy has destroyed."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The fate of the palit in Obadiah demonstrates the principle of lex talionis (the law of retaliation). Edom watched the destruction of Jerusalem and actively hunted down its survivors. Consequently, God declared that Edom would suffer a similar fate, but with a terrifying difference: when Edom faced judgment, there would be no palit (survivor) left of the house of Esau (Obadiah 1:18). Those who show no mercy to the fugitives of God's people will find no place of escape when their own judgment arrives.
