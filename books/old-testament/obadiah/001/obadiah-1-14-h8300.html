REMAINED - ( sarid , Strong's H8300 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun sarid refers to a survivor, a remnant, or one who has escaped a catastrophic event such as a battle, plague, or divine judgment. In Obadiah 1:14, this term depicts the vulnerable Judeans who survived the Babylonian invasion of Jerusalem, only to be captured at the border and handed over to their enemies by their brother nation, Edom. This word underscores the tragic vulnerability of those who survived the initial onslaught, highlighting Edom's severe betrayal and lack of brotherly compassion.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun sarid is closely linked to the verb root sarad, which means to escape, flee, or be left behind after a crisis. The word picture is that of a weary, battered individual running for their life from a burning city or a bloody battlefield. A sarid is not merely someone who lives in a place, but someone who has survived a sweeping destruction that claimed the lives of the majority. In ancient Near Eastern warfare, to leave no sarid meant the total annihilation of an enemy’s lineage and memory. Therefore, the survival of even a few individuals represented the thin thread of continuation for a family or nation.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun sarid denotes a survivor, remnant, or one who remains left over after a calamity.

• It refers specifically to individuals who have physically escaped death, captivity, or destruction in battle.

• In legal and historical narratives, it designates the remnant of a population that outlives a divine judgment or military campaign.

• The semantic range is narrow and focused, consistently referring to the surviving individuals or small remnant of a larger group that has undergone devastation.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In military contexts, sarid is frequently used to describe the absolute defeat of an army where "no survivor was left" (e.g., Num 21:35). This indicates a complete and devastating victory.

• In prophetic literature, the term shifts to a theological word picture. It represents the preserved remnant of Israel whom God protects through His sovereign mercy during times of national exile and judgment.

• The word evokes extreme vulnerability. A survivor has lost their home, family, and possessions, possessing nothing but their bare life. To target a sarid is to attack the most defenseless members of society.

### 4. Theological Implications

The existence of a sarid points directly to the sovereignty and mercy of God. Even in the execution of severe judgment, God rarely permits the total annihilation of His covenant people; He preserves a remnant. Conversely, when God's judgment is absolute against an unrepentant enemy, the text notes that they will have "no survivor."

Edom’s mistreatment of Judah's survivors was a direct assault on the people God was preserving. By handing over the survivors, Edom attempted to complete the destruction that Babylon started, thereby opposing God’s redemptive purposes and covenant promises.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:14, the prophet rebukes Edom for standing at the crossroads to cut off the fugitives and delivering up "those among them who remained" in the day of distress. This occurred during the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC. As fleeing Judeans sought escape routes through the desert of Edom, the Edomites blocked their path, captured them, and handed them back to the Babylonians.

The nuance of sarid here emphasizes the sheer cruelty of Edom's betrayal. These were not active combatants; they were traumatized survivors of a brutal siege. Edom’s actions violated both the brotherly covenant (as descendants of Jacob and Esau) and basic human decency, turning a potential sanctuary into a trap.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Numbers 21:35 - "So they defeated him, his sons, and all his people, until there was no survivor left him; and they took possession of his land."

• Deuteronomy 3:3 - "So the LORD our God also delivered into our hands Og king of Bashan, with all his people, and we defeated him until no survivor was left to him."

• Jeremiah 42:17 - "So shall it be with all the men who set their faces to go to Egypt to dwell there. They shall die by the sword, by famine, and by pestilence. And none of them shall remain or escape from the evil that I will bring upon them."

• Joel 2:32 - "And it shall come to pass that whoever calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved. For in Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there shall be deliverance, as the LORD has said, among the remnant whom the LORD calls."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound poetic justice in the book of Obadiah. Edom actively sought to eliminate the sarid (survivors) of Jacob (v. 14). Consequently, God pronounces a fitting judgment on Esau’s descendants: "there shall be no survivor (sarid) of the house of Esau" (Obadiah 1:18). The very fate Edom tried to inflict on Judah became their own ultimate destiny under divine judgment. This teaches a sobering pastoral truth: God takes notice of how we treat those who are already down, and cruelty to the vulnerable invites His strict justice.
