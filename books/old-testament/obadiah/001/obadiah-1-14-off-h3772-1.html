OFF - (kârath, Strong's H3772)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb karat basic lexical meaning is to cut, sever, cut down, eliminate, or destroy, as well as to ratify a covenant through the physical cutting of sacrificial animals. In Obadiah 1:14, the word expresses the active, malicious execution of destruction as Edom stationed troops at the crossroads to cut off and destroy fleeing Judean refugees.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb karat is a foundational Hebrew term rooted in the concrete, physical world of ancient Near Eastern life. Its primary physical association involves severing objects with an edge—such as cutting down trees, felling timber, or butchering livestock.

In Israelite society and the broader ancient Near East, karat acquired profound legal and relational significance through the idiom karat berit ("to cut a covenant"). Covenant ratification routinely involved cutting animals into two halves, between which the covenanting parties would walk, invoking self-maledictory curses if either party breached the agreement. Beyond covenant contexts, karat developed a distinct judicial and military sense, describing the destruction, extermination, or cutting off of individuals, royal lines, or entire populations from life, land, or covenant privileges.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, karat denotes "to cut, cut off, chop down, sever, eliminate, destroy, or ratify (a covenant)." Its semantic range spans several distinct nuances depending on the verbal stem and context:

  • Literal Physical Severing: Felling trees, cutting branches, or severing material objects.

  • Covenant Ratification: Cutting animals to formally institute a binding oath or treaty (karat berit).

  • Judicial Excision: Removing transgressors from among the covenant community or land through divine judgment or execution.

  • Military Destruction / Extinction: Eliminating enemies, royal lines, or populations.

In the target occurrence of Obadiah 1:14, the root appears in the Hiphil stem, infinitive construct form, in the absolute state. The Hiphil stem functions with causative and active force, signifying "to cause to be cut off, to eliminate, or to destroy actively."

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Hebrew Scriptures, karat operates across three primary conceptual categories:

First, it describes literal physical cutting. This includes cutting down trees for timber or siege machinery, or severing vines and branches.

Second, it functions as the standard technical verb for initiating covenants. Whether God enters into an everlasting covenant with Abraham, David, or His redeemed people, or human parties establish political treaties, the act is expressed as "cutting" a covenant. The physical ritual underscores the solemn, inviolable nature of the pledge.

Third, karat serves as a primary term for divine judgment and catastrophic elimination. In legal texts, individuals who deliberately violate God's sacred commands are warned that they will be "cut off" from their people. In prophetic contexts, God warns that He will cut off idolaters, oppressive rulers, and hostile nations who oppose His kingdom.

4. Theological Implications

The use of karat in prophetic literature highlights God's righteousness in judgment and His defense of His covenant nation. In Obadiah, the judgment against Edom carries deep covenantal implications. Edom descended from Esau, the twin brother of Jacob (Israel). Consequently, fraternal obligation required Edom to assist or at least show pity to Judah in her day of distress.

Instead, Edom exploited Judah's fall to active, hostile advantage. By standing at the mountain passes to cut off those escaping the Babylonian forces, Edom aligned itself with the destroyers of God's people. Theologically, karat demonstrates that God holds nations morally accountable for how they treat His afflicted people. The destructive intent Edom directed toward Judah ultimately inverted upon Edom itself, as God decreed that Edom would be cut off forever for its violence against Jacob.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:14, the prophet delivers a dramatic series of retrospective prohibitions detailing Edom's treacherous actions during the fall of Jerusalem: "You should not have stood at the crossroads to cut off those of his who escaped; nor should you have delivered up those of his who remained in the day of distress."

The grammatical form of karat in this verse—the Hiphil stem, infinitive construct form, absolute state—carries explicit active purpose. The syntax directly links Edom's spatial positioning ("stood at the crossroads") with the verbal action of cutting off the escapees. Edom did not merely occupy space as a neutral bystander; their presence at the physical pass was executed for the specific purpose of actively intercepting, blocking, and destroying Judean survivors.

Judah's geography forced fleeing refugees into narrow mountain passes toward the Judean wilderness and the Jordan valley. Edom capitalized on this geography, setting an ambush at these critical escape routes. By choosing to "cut off" those fleeing from foreign invaders, Edom shut down the last avenue of preservation for the remnants of Judah, exhibiting cold-blooded betrayal against their brethren.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 20:20 — "Only the trees which you know are not trees for food you may destroy and cut down, to build siegeworks against the city that makes war with you, until it is subdued."

  • Here, in an agrarian military context, the verb is rendered "and cut down," describing the literal severing of non-fruit-bearing trees for warfare.

  • Genesis 26:28 — "But they said, 'We have certainly seen that the LORD is with you. So we said, Let there now be an oath between us, between you and us and let us make a covenant with you.'"

  • This passage uses the term within the phrase "you and us and let us make," reflecting the ancient covenant-making idiom established between Abimelech and Isaac.

  • Amos 1:8 — "'I will cut off the inhabitant from Ashdod, and him who holds the scepter from Ashkelon; I will turn My hand against Ekron, and the remnant of the Philistines shall perish,' says the Lord GOD."

  • In this prophetic announcement of judgment, the word is translated "off," expressing divine execution and the sovereign elimination of Philistine leadership.

  • Ezekiel 21:4 — "'Seeing then that I will cut off the righteous and the wicked from you, therefore My sword shall go out of its sheath against all flesh from south to north.'"

  • Describing sweeping military destruction brought upon Jerusalem, the verb is rendered "off," referring to God's decree that severs both the righteous and the wicked from the land.

  • Isaiah 55:3 — "'Incline your ear, and come to Me. Hear, and your soul shall live; and I will make an everlasting covenant with you—the sure mercies of David.'"

  • In this gospel promise, the term is translated "make," designating the solemn ratifying of God's grace-based, everlasting covenant.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The literary structure of Obadiah 1:10–14 heightens the moral gravity of Edom's action. The passage presents an escalating sequence of eight "should not have" statements detailing Edom's progression in sin:

1. Looking on Judah's captivity with malicious satisfaction.

2. Rejoicing over their downfall.

3. Boasting in their day of distress.

4. Entering the gate of God's people to gloat.

5. Laying hands on their substance.

6. Standing at the crossroads to cut off those escaping.

7. Handing over the survivors to the enemy.

Edom's progression moves from internal attitude (gloat, rejoice, boast) to active plunder (entering, seizing) and finally to lethal betrayal (standing at the crossroads to cut off and deliver up refugees). The use of karat marks the climax of Edom's active violence against their brother Jacob, justifying the divine sentence that Edom would suffer absolute destruction and be cut off forever.
