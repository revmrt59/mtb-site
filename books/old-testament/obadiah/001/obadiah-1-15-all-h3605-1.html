ALL - (kôl, Strong's H3605)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun kol basic lexical meaning signifies "the whole," "all," "every," or "entirety." It serves as a comprehensive term of totality, indicating that no part, individual, or subgroup within the specified category is excluded. In Obadiah 1:15, kol specifies the exhaustive scope of divine accountability, declaring that the imminent "day of the LORD" will encompass "all the nations." The word elevates the specific prophecy against Edom into a universal declaration of God's impartial, cosmic justice, demonstrating that Edom's judgment is part of a comprehensive divine reckoning from which no rebellious nation is exempt.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word kol is one of the most common structural terms in Old Testament Hebrew, functioning as a primary tool for expressed totality, universal scope, and complete summation. In Old Testament literature, it appears across all genres—narrative, law, poetry, and prophecy—to establish boundaries of obligation, historical inclusion, or divine authority.

In the prophetic vision of Obadiah, the term occurs in a crucial transitional moment. The prophet spent the opening portion of his message addressing the pride, betrayal, and violence of Edom, Judah's fraternal neighbor who rejoiced over and participated in Jerusalem's destruction. In verse 15, the scope dramatically expands from a localized word against Edom to a global divine summons. The setting is the approaching "day of the LORD," a central Old Testament prophetic motif designating God's decisive, sovereign intervention in human history to judge evil and vindicate His covenant righteousness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, kol functions as a noun denoting totality or whole, often operating syntactically like a quantifier corresponding to the English "all," "every," or "the whole of." When used in the construct state before a noun, its precise contextual nuance is determined by the form and definiteness of the following noun:

  • Before a collective or singular noun, it frequently denotes "the whole" or "entirety" of that entity.

  • Before a plural noun, it denotes "all" or "every" individual composing that group.

The target occurrence in Obadiah 1:15 is grammatically masculine, singular, and in the construct state. Lexically, it signifies the comprehensive totality of the group that follows. It does not mean a mere majority or an indefinite large portion; it denotes the complete set without exception.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, kol establishes absolute boundaries in legal, historical, and prophetic contexts. In legal texts, it emphasizes absolute obedience or total prohibition (e.g., keeping all commandments). In narrative texts, it records full participation or total loss.

In prophetic discourse, kol carries profound theological weight regarding God's relationship to creation and humanity. It is frequently employed to emphasize:

  • God's absolute ownership of the earth and its contents.

  • The total weakness and mortality of human flesh in contrast to God's eternal word.

  • The universal scope of divine judgment against human pride.

  • The global extent of God's ultimate salvation and kingdom.

The semantic scope of kol is naturally governed by its literary context. Context determines whether "all" refers to cosmic totality, global humanity, or the totality of a defined regional or historical group.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of kol in Obadiah 1:15 reinforces foundational biblical truths regarding God's character and government:

  • Universal Sovereignty: YHWH is not a localized or national deity whose jurisdiction is confined to Israel. He exercises sovereign authority over every nation on earth.

  • Impartial Cosmic Justice: Divine judgment is governed by an unyielding, righteous standard (lex talionis). The phrase "as you have done, it shall be done to you" applies universally to all peoples under God's rule.

  • Typological Representation: By placing Edom's judgment in the context of judgment upon "all the nations," the text establishes Edom as a representative type of human pride and hostility against God's kingdom. Edom's fall is a local guarantee of the eventual global vindication of God's righteousness.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:15, the prophet declares, "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near; as you have done, it shall be done to you; your reprisal shall return upon your own head."

The target word kol appears in the construct state, grammatically governing the noun phrase "the nations." This specific construction accomplishes a vital structural shift in the prophecy:

1. Transition from Particular to Universal: Up to this point, Edom might have viewed its feud with Judah as an isolated geopolitical conflict. Kol shatters this provincial perspective, framing Edom's fate within God's universal judicial appointment for every hostile nation.

2. Removal of Exception: The phrase allows for no exceptions. Edom cannot plead geographical isolation, strategic alliance, or historical privilege to escape the day of YHWH.

3. Grounding for Retribution: The dynamic of exact retribution ("as you have done...") is declared as the universal law governing God's dealing with "all" nations. The comprehensive nature of kol establishes that no act of unrighteous hostility against God's people anywhere on earth will remain unrequited.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The following passages illustrate the lexical use of kol across various Old Testament contexts:

  • Joel 1:2 — "Hear this, you elders, and give ear, all you inhabitants of the land! Has anything like this happened in your days, or even in the days of your fathers?"

  • Isaiah 40:6 — "The voice said, 'Cry out!' And he said, 'What shall I cry?' 'All flesh is grass, and all its loveliness is like the flower of the field.'"

  • Psalms 32:6 — "For this cause everyone who is godly shall pray to You in a time when You may be found; surely in a flood of great waters they shall not come near him."

  • Ezekiel 9:8 — "So it was, while they were killing them, that I was left alone; and I fell on my face and cried out, and said, 'Ah, Lord GOD! Will You destroy all the remnant of Israel in pouring out Your fury on Jerusalem?'"

  • 2 Chronicles 6:3 — "Then the king turned his face around and blessed the whole assembly of Israel, while all the assembly of Israel was standing."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The global horizon introduced by kol in Obadiah 1:15 links this short prophetic book directly to the broader canon of Old Testament eschatology. The prophets consistently maintain that history is moving toward a final, definitive manifestation of YHWH's reign—the "Day of the LORD." By describing this day as arriving upon kol haggôyim ("all the nations"), Obadiah aligns his message with prophets like Joel and Isaiah, who likewise foresaw a cosmic gathering of nations where human pride is brought low and YHWH alone is exalted.
