DAY - (yôwm, Strong's H3117)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun yom basic lexical meaning denotes a day, a period of daylight, or a specific appointed time. In Obadiah 1:15, yom identifies the imminent, God-appointed time of cosmic judgment known as "the day of the LORD." The word contributes directly to Obadiah's argument by marking the dramatic turning point from human arrogance to divine reckoning, declaring that Yahweh's appointed hour of universal retribution upon Edom and all rebellious nations is at hand.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The noun yom is the foundational temporal term in the Hebrew Bible. In ancient Israelite culture and language, it designated the natural light cycle, a 24-hour day, or a distinct point in historical time. Beyond its standard calendar function, yom frequently serves in prophetic literature to denote a fixed season or an appointed moment of God's decisive involvement in human affairs.

In prophetic discourse, the phrase "the day of the LORD" (yom YHWH) builds upon the cultural concept of a king's "day of battle" or victory. Prophetic authors transformed this imagery to describe the supreme moment when Israel's God intervenes visible and decisively in history to defeat His enemies, vindicate His righteousness, and reestablish order.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, standard Hebrew reference works (such as Brown-Driver-Briggs and the Theological Wordbook of the Old Testament) define yom along a spectrum of temporal senses:

  • The daylight hours, as distinguished from night (layil).

  • A full solar cycle of 24 hours.

  • A specific, designated point in time, season, or historical period.

  • An appointed epoch or day of divine action, judgment, or salvation.

The lexical meaning of yom itself remains temporal; its specific theological depth in prophetic contexts arises from syntactic pairings, construct relationships, and surrounding discourse rather than an inherent, hidden etymological payload.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, yom appears in diverse literary genres, adapting its nuance to the functional needs of the context:

  • Narrative Literature: It routinely tracks sequential chronology, calendar dates, and normal human life spans.

  • Law and Ritual: It marks sacred appointed times, Sabbath observances, and ceremonial solemnities.

  • Wisdom and Poetic Literature: It contrasts the brief, ephemeral quality of human life against the enduring sovereignty of God.

  • Prophetic Literature: It functions as a technical temporal marker for divine intervention. When linked with divine titles or descriptive qualifiers, it signifies an appointed climate of divine visitation.

The word yom does not inherently carry a negative or punitive meaning on its own. Rather, whether a designated "day" brings deliverance or destruction depends entirely upon the moral condition of those involved and the sovereign purpose of Yahweh.

4. Theological Implications

In Old Testament theology, the concept of yom YHWH ("the day of the LORD") exposes the illusion of human autonomy and ultimate earthly power. Human nations may enjoy an extended season of pride, violence, or exploitation, but prophetic theology affirms that human history is bound to a divine timetable.

The theology of the "day" emphasizes:

  • Divine Sovereignty over Time: Yahweh fixes the times and seasons; human arrogance operates only within limits set by God.

  • Imperial Accountability: God's rule is not merely localized to Israel but extends over all earth's nations.

  • Strict Retributive Justice: The appointed day brings the manifestation of God's righteous law, wherein human conduct receives its appropriate recompense.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:15, the target word appears in the masculine singular form within the foundational clause, "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near."

This placement constitutes the structural and theological pivot of Obadiah's vision. Earlier in the prophecy, Edom is condemned for gloating over Judah's time of destruction, participating in violence against brothers, and acting as if their prideful security in the rock fortresses would shield them forever. By introducing yom in verse 15, the prophet contrasts human actions with divine timing. Edom thought Judah's downfall was the final word; Obadiah announces that Yahweh has His own appointed yom.

The immediate syntax links yom with two crucial contextual modifiers:

  • It is explicitly named "the day of the LORD" (yom YHWH), establishing Yahweh as the decisive Judge and sovereign Agent of the moment.

  • It is declared "near" (qarob), imparting immediate urgency to the message.

Furthermore, Obadiah broadens the scope of this day: it is "upon all the nations," demonstrating that Edom's impending judgment is part of a universal execution of justice. The verse grounds this judgment in the principle of divine reciprocity ("As you have done, it shall be done to you"), establishing that the appointed yom is the exact moment when human deeds return upon the heads of the doers.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The prophetic announcement of the "day of the LORD" serves as an important bridge between Old Testament judgment themes and broader canonical theology. In Obadiah, the term establishes that temporal judgments in history foreshadow the ultimate, global realignment of all creation under God's righteous rule.

The movement in Obadiah from localized judgment on Edom to global accountability on the "day" reminds readers that no act of injustice escapes divine notice. Human pride may seem unassailable for a time, but in the overarching perspective of biblical theology, every human power structure remains subject to the sovereign calendar of God.
