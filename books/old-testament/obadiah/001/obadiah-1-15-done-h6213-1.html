DONE - (ʻâsâh, Strong's H6213)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ʿasah fundamentally means to make, do, perform, produce, or execute an action. It is one of the most foundational action verbs in the Hebrew scriptures, encompassing acts of physical construction, ethical human behavior, and divine sovereign works. In Obadiah 1:15 ("As you have done, it shall be done to you"), ʿasah establishes the baseline for God's divine retributive justice. The word directly attributes active, guilty conduct to Edom for their betrayal of Judah during Jerusalem's crisis, serving as the pivot upon which God mirrors Edom's past deeds into their coming judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The prophet Obadiah addresses the pride and violent betrayal of Edom, the neighboring nation descended from Esau. When Jerusalem faced invasion and catastrophic loss, Edom did not merely stand aloof; they actively rejoiced over Judah's ruin, looted their wealth, and cut off survivors.

Obadiah 1:15 marks a transition in the prophecy from the localized judgment on Edom to a universal declaration concerning "the day of the LORD... upon all the nations." By employing ʿasah, the text highlights concrete, historically realized deeds rather than vague attitudes or abstract hostility. The historical relationship between Jacob and Esau gives this verb intense moral weight: Edom's actions were acts of covenantal and brotherly betrayal.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, ʿasah denotes the bringing about of an effect through action. Its broad semantic range includes:

  • To perform or execute an act (doing good, evil, justice, or wickedness).

  • To make, fabricate, or fashion a physical object (crafting tabernacle vessels, constructing monuments).

  • To produce, yield, or bring forth (as a tree producing fruit or land yielding a crop).

  • To accomplish, fulfill, or bring to pass (referring to plans, commands, or decrees).

In moral and judicial contexts, ʿasah represents concrete human agency. It shifts focus from internal intent or potentiality to observable conduct that incurs personal or national moral accountability before God.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Hebrew Scriptures, ʿasah functions as a primary verb of action for both God and humanity:

  • Human Responsibility: Human beings are commanded to "do" God's commandments, practicing righteousness and justice. Conversely, doing evil brings guilt upon the actor.

  • Divine Operation: God is portrayed as the One who "does" wondrous works, accomplishes His decrees, and executes righteous judgment in history.

  • Moral Cause and Effect: Because ʿasah represents actualized performance, it frequently appears in contexts of legal retributive judgment where recompense corresponds directly to what an individual or collective group has done.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of ʿasah in prophetic literature centers on divine accountability and retributive justice (lex talionis):

  • Righteous Moral Order: God's government of the world is morally coherent. Human deeds (ʿasah) are not lost to history; God observes, evaluates, and responds to them.

  • Equitable Recompense: God's judgment is tailored to human conduct. The harm a person or nation inflicts on others becomes the pattern for the judgment they receive from God's hand.

  • Sovereignty over the Nations: While nations exercise real human agency through their actions, God remains sovereign over the outcomes, ensuring that unrepentant violence against His people is justly answered.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:15, the target occurrence of ʿasah carries specific grammatical features: the Qal stem, perfect form, 2nd person, masculine singular.

The Qal stem expresses simple active action. The 2nd person masculine singular addresses Edom directly as a singular entity (personified in their ancestor Esau). The perfect form presents Edom's past malicious activity against Judah as a complete, established historical reality. Edom's guilt is not theoretical; their evil acts stand fully realized.

The verse sets up a direct poetic symmetry by using ʿasah twice: "As you have done (ʿasah), it shall be done (ʿasah) to you." Edom's past action becomes the precise template for their future divine sentence. What Edom actively inflicted on their brother Judah will be done back to them by God's sovereign decree. The text explicitly defines this outcome in the next clause: "Your reprisal shall return upon your own head."

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 16:12 – "And you shall remember that you were a slave in Egypt, and you shall observe and do these statutes."

  • 1 Samuel 24:18 – "And you have shown this day how you have dealt well with me; for when the LORD delivered me into your hand, you did not kill me."

  • Psalms 40:8 – "I delight to do Your will, O my God, and Your law is within my heart."

  • Joel 2:26 – "You shall eat in plenty and be satisfied, and praise the name of the LORD your God, who has dealt wondrously with you..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural balance of Obadiah 1:15 reflects a key principle found throughout prophetic literature: God's retributive justice operates with exact moral symmetry. The harm Edom actively worked against Jerusalem forms the legal basis for the sentence declared against Edom on the Day of the LORD.

Furthermore, Edom's status as a brother nation (descended from Esau) heightens the ethical violation of what they did (ʿasah). In Old Testament law and covenant theology, betrayal by a kin group carries severe moral guilt. Obadiah's targeted use of the second-person singular underscores that an entire nation can be held collectively accountable before God for its unified actions against His people.
