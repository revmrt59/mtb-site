NATIONS - ( goy , Strong's H1471 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word goy refers to a distinct nation, people group, or geopolitical body sharing a common territory, language, or political structure. In the prophetic literature, and specifically in Obadiah 1:15, it carries a heavy theological weight, representing the collective corporate entity of the pagan nations who stand in opposition to God and His covenant people. In this context, it emphasizes that God's sovereign justice is not merely individual but global, holding every geopolitical power accountable for how they treat His people and respond to His reign.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The origin of the Hebrew word goy is closely associated with a body or physical mass, which progressed semantically to represent a collective body of people. In the ancient world, a goy was defined not just by geographic borders, but by a shared lineage, government, language, and patron deity. In the Ancient Near East, every nation was understood to have its own national god who fought for them and governed their lands. When the Scriptures speak of the goyim (plural), they are describing the vast, diverse array of human societies that developed after the division of languages at the Tower of Babel. Unlike the term am, which typically denotes a family or a relational covenant people (such as Israel as the "people" of God), goy emphasizes the formal, organized, and geopolitical structure of a population.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word goy denotes a nation, people, or collective political body. In its singular form, it refers to a single national entity, which can occasionally include Israel when viewed in its political or physical structure (as when God promises to make Abraham a "great nation"). However, in the plural form, goyim, the term overwhelmingly designates the non-Hebrew, gentile nations.

The semantic range of the term includes:

• A sovereign political state with geographic borders and centralized leadership.

• The pagan or gentile nations surrounding Israel, characterized by their distinct religious systems and moral codes.

• The global community of humanity standing apart from the unique covenant relationship shared by God and Israel.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, goy is used to draw a contrast between the specific family of Israel (am) and the rest of the world's political powers (goyim). In Hebrew poetry, the nations are frequently depicted through vivid word pictures:

• They are compared to a raging, tumultuous sea, representing the chaotic rebellion of human pride against the sovereign rule of the Creator.

• They are described as wild beasts, symbolizing their predatory nature as they conquer, devour, and oppress weaker peoples.

• They are pictured as tools or instruments in the hand of Yahweh—such as a razor or an ax—which He temporarily uses to discipline His people before holding those very instruments accountable for their own hubris.

4. Theological Implications

The theological treatment of the goyim highlights several foundational biblical truths:

• The Universal Sovereignty of God: Yahweh is not a localized, tribal deity. He is the "King of the nations," exercising supreme authority over the rise, boundaries, and fall of every earthly empire.

• Corporate Moral Responsibility: Scripture treats nations as moral agents. A nation can accumulate corporate guilt through systemic pride, violence, and injustice, culminating in a historical reckoning.

• The Promise of Redemption: While the nations are often depicted as hostile, the ultimate redemptive arc of Scripture promises that the goyim will one day abandon their idols, stream to the mountain of the Lord, and worship the true God of Israel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:15, the prophet declares, "For the day of the Lord upon all the nations is near."

The specific historical target of Obadiah's prophecy is Edom—the brother-nation of Israel that gloated over Judah's downfall, actively participated in her plunder, and cut off her survivors. However, in verse 15, the prophetic lens suddenly widens. The localized judgment of Edom becomes a microcosm of the global judgment of "all the nations" (goyim).

The nuance here is critical: Edom is not an isolated case. Edom represents the quintessential spirit of the goyim—hostile, arrogant, self-reliant, and predatory toward the people of God. By asserting that the Day of the Lord is near for all nations, Obadiah warns that the standard of justice applied to Edom (the law of retaliation: "as you have done, it shall be done to you") is the standard by which every global power will be judged. The geopolitical stage is set for a universal reckoning where God vindicates His covenant promises.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), goy is translated as ethnos (plural ethne), which is the root of the English word "ethnic." This linguistic link directly connects the Old Testament prophets to the New Testament mission. When Jesus commissions His disciples to "make disciples of all the nations (ethne)" in Matthew 28:19, He is sending them to the very goyim who once stood as enemies of Israel. The trajectory of Scripture moves from the goyim being crushed under the judgment of the Day of the Lord (Obadiah 1:15) to the goyim being healed, redeemed, and walking in the light of the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21:24).
