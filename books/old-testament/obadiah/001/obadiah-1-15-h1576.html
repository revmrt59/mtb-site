REPRISAL - ( gemul , Strong's H1576 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun gemul refers to a recompense, reward, or dealing out of desert, whether good or evil. It describes a moral echo in the universe—what a person or nation sows, they will inevitably reap. In the context of Obadiah 1:15, it speaks of God's righteous retribution against Edom. Edom's malicious actions toward Judah during her calamity would recoil upon them, demonstrating that God's justice is reciprocal, measured, and perfectly suited to the offense.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb is gamal, which means to deal out, wean, or ripen. The concrete background of this root relates to a process coming to maturity or completion, such as fruit ripening on a branch or a child being weaned from its mother. Consequently, gemul represents the fully ripened fruit of one's actions. What was quietly sown in secret or pridefully executed in malice eventually ripens into a harvest of consequence. In the ancient Near East, where tribal eye-for-an-eye justice was common, gemul elevates this concept to cosmic, divine justice. It is not arbitrary, uncontrolled vengeance, but the natural, matured outworking of human deeds under the sovereign hand of God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun gemul signifies an action done to someone in return for what they have done. It is a neutral term semantically, capable of meaning either a positive reward or a negative retribution. In the context of judgment, it denotes a penalty, reprisal, or payback that matches the crime. In poetic and prophetic literature, it carries the force of divine retribution—God returning upon the wicked the exact weight of their evil deeds.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Gemul is used to describe both human actions (doing good or evil to one's neighbor) and divine actions (God's judgment or blessing). The word pictures associated with gemul often involve motion or physics:

• A deed "returning" (shub) upon one's own head, suggesting a moral law of gravity where actions do not dissipate into nothingness; they launch a trajectory that ultimately curves back to strike the initiator.

• Being "poured out" like a cup, indicating a measurement that is filled up over time and eventually overflows.

• Being paired with the idea of "rendering" or "paying back" (shalam), portraying God as a cosmic judge who keeps an accurate ledger and ensures every account is settled in full.

4. Theological Implications

• Divine Justice is Reciprocal: God's judgment is not random or capricious. It is structured around the principle of lex talionis (the law of retaliation). The punishment fits the crime perfectly.

• Sovereign Moral Order: The universe is not morally neutral. God has established a moral framework where evil actions contain the seeds of their own destruction.

• The Day of the Lord: Gemul is highly eschatological. It represents the ultimate balancing of the scales when the "Day of the Lord" arrives. Human history is moving toward an ultimate reckoning where every injustice will be answered.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:15, the prophet addresses Edom, the descendants of Esau. When Babylon invaded Jerusalem, Edom did not merely stand aloof; they actively participated in the tragedy. They gloated over Judah's ruin, cut off their fugitives at the crossroads, and delivered survivors to the enemy.

Obadiah declares, "As you have done, it shall be done to you; Your reprisal (gemul) shall return upon your own head."

The nuance here is intensely ironical and fitting. Edom's pride made them feel secure in their high rocky fortresses, thinking they were immune to consequences. God states that their very deeds of betrayal would act as a boomerang. The "reprisal" is the exact counterpart of their crimes: they betrayed their brother Jacob, so they will be betrayed by their own allies; they plundered, so they will be plundered.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Isaiah 3:11 - "Woe to the wicked! It shall be ill with him, For the reward [gemul] of his hands shall be given him."

• Isaiah 35:4 - "Say to those who are fearful-hearted, 'Be strong, do not fear! Behold, your God will come with vengeance, With the recompense [gemul] of God; He will come and save you.'"

• Jeremiah 51:56 - "For the plunderer has come against her, against Babylon, And her mighty men are taken; Every one of their bows is broken; For the LORD is the God of recompense [gemul], He will surely repay."

• Joel 3:4 - "Indeed, what have you to do with Me, O Tyre and Sidon, and all the coasts of Philistia? Will you retaliate against Me? But if you retaliate against Me, Swiftly and speedily I will return your retaliation [gemul] upon your own head;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Contrast with Grace: While gemul is the law of perfect, earned desert, the Gospel presents a striking contrast. In Christ, God does not treat believers according to what they deserve. Instead, Christ received the bitter gemul of our sins on the cross, so that we might receive the gemul (reward) of His righteousness.

• Pastoral Application: Believers can rest in the knowledge that vengeance belongs to the Lord. We do not need to seek personal reprisal or take matters into our own hands, because God is the God of recompense who will ensure perfect justice in His timing.
