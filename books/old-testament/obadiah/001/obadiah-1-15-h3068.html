LORD - ( Yahweh, Strong's H3068 )

Summary:

The name Yahweh (translated as "LORD" in all capital letters in the NKJV) is the personal, covenant name of the one true God of Israel. It denotes the self-existent, eternal Creator who is actively present with His people, faithful to His promises, and supreme over all earthly powers. In Obadiah 1:15, this name stands at the center of the warning concerning the "day of the LORD," emphasizing His absolute authority to execute perfect, retributive justice upon the hostile nations that oppose His covenant people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Yahweh is deeply rooted in the self-revelation of God to Moses at the burning bush in Exodus 3:14. When Moses asked for God's name, God declared, "I AM WHO I AM," linking His personal identity to the Hebrew verb hayah, meaning "to be" or "to exist." Historically, names in the ancient Near East were not mere labels but reflections of character and essence. Yahweh is the name that distinguishes the true God from the dead, localized idols of pagan nations, such as Chemosh of Moab or Qos of Edom. It depicts God as the source of all life and existence, beholden to no one, self-sufficient, and dynamically active in human history to redeem His people and judge evil.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Yahweh is the proper noun representing the personal name of the God of Israel. Lexically, it signifies the self-existent One, the eternal One, or the One who causes all things to exist. This name occurs over 6,800 times in the Hebrew Scriptures, making it the most frequent designation for God in the Old Testament. Unlike Elohim (which functions as a general title for God or deity), Yahweh is a proper name expressing personal relationship, immutable holiness, and covenant commitment. It encompasses His eternal nature—He who was, who is, and who is to come—and His absolute dependability to fulfill His word.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, Yahweh is associated with powerful imagery that conveys His relational and sovereign character:

• The Covenant Keeper: Yahweh is uniquely the God of the covenant, bound by oath to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

• The Divine Warrior: In contexts of judgment, Yahweh is depicted as a mighty warrior who fights for His people and executes vengeance on their oppressors.

• The Sovereign Judge: As the ultimate Lawgiver, He sits enthroned over the nations, weighing their actions in the scales of perfect justice.

• The Shepherd: He is portrayed as the guardian of His flock, ensuring their protection, provision, and ultimate restoration.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of Yahweh in prophetic literature is immense. First, it asserts God's absolute sovereignty over history. No nation, however proud or geographically secure (like Edom in their rocky clefts), is outside His jurisdiction. Second, it guarantees the preservation of His covenant people. Because Yahweh is immutable and faithful, His people will not be utterly consumed, despite their historical failures. Finally, it establishes the foundation for moral accountability. All nations are answerable to the creator-covenant God, who demands justice and will not allow the proud oppression of the vulnerable to go unpunished.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:15, the name Yahweh is used in the vital phrase "the day of the LORD" (yom Yahweh). Edom had proudly assumed that their strategic, high-altitude fortresses in Mount Seir made them invulnerable to human armies. However, Obadiah shifts the focus from human conflict to a divine reckoning. The use of Yahweh alerts Edom that they are not merely dealing with neighboring enemies, but with the eternal, almighty Ruler of the universe. The impending judgment is not a random geopolitical event, but the personal intervention of the covenant God of Israel, who will enforce the law of retribution: "As you have done, it shall be done to you." Edom's malicious celebration over Jerusalem's downfall will be met with Yahweh's righteous wrath.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:4: "This is the history of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens,"

• Exodus 3:15: "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations."'"

• Joel 2:31: "The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, Before the coming of the great and awesome day of the LORD."

• Malachi 3:6: "For I am the LORD, I do not change; Therefore you are not consumed, O sons of Jacob."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In post-exilic Judaism, out of intense reverence for the third commandment ("You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain"), the pronunciation of the Tetragrammaton (YHWH) was avoided. When reading the Scriptures aloud, Jews substituted the word Adonai (Lord) or HaShem (The Name). This historical practice led to the English translation convention of rendering YHWH as "LORD" in all capital letters. In the New Testament, the Greek word Kurios is used to translate both Adonai and Yahweh. By applying the title Kurios to Jesus Christ (e.g., Philippians 2:11), the New Testament writers made a profound theological declaration: Jesus is Yahweh incarnate, the Lord of glory who executes both salvation and final judgment.
