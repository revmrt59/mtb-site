DAY - ( yôm , Strong's H3117 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word yôm primarily denotes a literal twenty-four-hour day, a period of daylight, or a generalized, epochal period of time. In the context of prophetic literature and specifically Obadiah 1:15, it refers to "the day of the LORD" (yôm YHWH)—a specific, appointed time of divine intervention, judgment, and vindication. In this passage, it represents the decisive moment when Yahweh enters human history to judge Edom and all rebellious nations while restoring His covenant people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • yôm is one of the most common nouns in the Hebrew Bible, appearing over two thousand times. Its basic orientation is grounded in the creation account of Genesis, where God divided the light from the darkness, calling the light "day." Culturally, the ancient Near Eastern mindset viewed time not merely as a chronological sequence but as filled with ethical and experiential content. A "day" was defined by what occurred within it. Thus, a "day" of battle or a "day" of trouble represents the character of that time. In the prophetic tradition, the background of yôm shifts from a routine unit of time to a climatic cosmic event when God asserts His sovereign rule over the kingdoms of men.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word yôm carries a broad semantic range:

  • The period of light (daytime) in contrast to night.

  • A literal twenty-four-hour cycle (evening and morning).

  • A generic, indefinite period of time, often translated as "when" or "in the time of."

  • A year (sometimes used in plural, yamim).

  • A specific, appointed point in time or an epoch marked by a significant event.

  • In the construct state with Yahweh (yôm YHWH), it refers to the eschatological or historical day of the Lord, characterized by sovereign judgment and salvation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Old Testament, yôm is frequently used in word pictures that convey finality, suddenness, and inescapable reality.

  • A Day of Darkness: Prophets often use yôm alongside images of thick darkness, clouds, and gloom, depicting the withdrawal of God's favor and the descent of His wrath.

  • The Harvest or Vintage: The "day" is pictured as a time of harvesting or treading the winepress, symbolizing the fullness of wickedness reaching its appointed end.

  • The Day of Battle: It evokes the imagery of a mighty warrior entering the battlefield to vanquish His enemies. God is portrayed as the divine warrior whose "day" brings decisive victory over those who oppose Him.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of yôm in the prophets is immense, centering on the absolute sovereignty of God over human history.

  • The Illusion of Autonomy: Human nations live under the illusion that they control their own destiny. The "day of the LORD" shatters this illusion, demonstrating that human empires exist only by divine permission.

  • Retributive Justice: This day establishes the law of retaliation. God's justice is perfect; the punishment fits the crime. Edom’s pride and cruelty will return upon their own heads.

  • Vindication of the Covenant: The day is not only about destroying the wicked but also about vindicating God's name and restoring His remnant. It marks the transition from the rule of man to the kingdom of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:15, yôm is situated at the turning point of the prophecy. Up to this verse, Obadiah has addressed Edom's specific sins: their pride, their gloating over Judah's downfall, and their active participation in the destruction of Jerusalem. In verse 15, the scope dramatically broadens from local judgment on Edom to global judgment: "upon all the nations." The proximity is emphasized ("is near"). The "day" is imminent and certain. The nuance of yôm here is that of a divine court date. It is the day of sentencing and execution. Edom thought they had their "day" of triumph over Jacob; now God declares that His day is arriving, which will reverse the fortunes of both nations.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Joel 1:15: "Alas for the day! For the day of the LORD is at hand; It shall come as destruction from the Almighty."

  • Amos 5:18: "Woe to you who desire the day of the LORD! For what good is the day of the LORD to you? It will be darkness, and not light."

  • Zephaniah 1:14: "The great day of the LORD is near; It is near and hastens quickly. The noise of the day of the LORD is bitter; There the mighty men shall cry out."

  • Malachi 4:5: "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet Before the coming of the great and dreadful day of the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The concept of the "day of the Lord" has both near-historical and far-eschatological fulfillments. In the immediate historical context, Edom experienced their "day" of judgment when they were conquered by Nabataean Arabs in the subsequent centuries. However, the phrase "upon all the nations" points to a final, ultimate eschatological day when Jesus Christ returns to judge the living and the dead. The grammatical construction of "as you have done, it shall be done to you" echoes the poetic justice inherent in God's appointments of time. He measures out days of grace, but when the day of judgment arrives, the measure of man's sin determines the measure of his ruin.
