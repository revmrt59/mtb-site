DONE - ( asah , Strong's H6213 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb asah is one of the most versatile and foundational words in the Old Testament, representing the act of doing, making, producing, or executing an action. In the context of Obadiah 1:15, it forms the pivot point of divine justice, establishing a direct, poetic correspondence between Edom's malicious actions against Judah and the judgment that will return upon their own head. It emphasizes that human deeds are not lost in the ether of history but are recorded and answered by the sovereign Creator, who acts to establish ultimate righteousness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb asah represents the basic concept of fashioning, performing, or producing something. It focuses on the active execution of a task, whether it is God creating the physical world, a craftsman fashioning a work of art, or a human being committing a moral or immoral act. Unlike the Hebrew verb bara (which is used exclusively of God's unique, effortless creation), asah typically involves the expenditure of effort, the realization of a plan, or the carrying out of a deliberate choice. In the ancient Near Eastern worldview, a person's "doing" was inextricably linked to their identity and ultimate destiny; what a person did defined who they were in relation to the community and the sovereign God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb asah means to do, make, fashion, accomplish, produce, or execute. Its semantic range spans from the creation of the cosmos to the performance of daily labors, the keeping of divine commandments, or the perpetration of wicked deeds. In active contexts, it denotes the transition from intention to physical realization. In judicial and prophetic contexts, it frequently appears in formulas of divine retribution, signifying that God will do to nations exactly what they have done to others, representing the outworking of the law of retaliation on a geopolitical and spiritual scale.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently uses asah to build vivid pictures of human and divine activity:

  • As a creative force: God makes the firmament, the sun, the moon, and humanity, shaping history through His active intervention.

  • As an ethical marker: To do righteousness or to do evil defines a person's standing before Yahweh.

  • As a judicial mirror: The double use of asah in Obadiah 1:15 creates a literary mirror image. The active doing of violence by Edom is mirrored by the passive "it shall be done" of divine consequence, illustrating that human actions carry a boomerang effect under the moral government of God.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, asah in the prophetic corpus underscores the reality of divine justice and the absolute accountability of the nations. God is the ultimate actor who responds to human action. Edom believed their hostile actions against their brother Jacob in his day of calamity would go unnoticed or unpunished. However, the prophetic word reveals that human history is a moral arena supervised by Yahweh. The moral law of harvest—reaping what one sows—is built into the fabric of creation because the Creator Himself executes judgment based on what has been done.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:15, asah is used twice in rapid succession: "As you have done, it shall be done to you." This literary structure emphasizes the exactness of divine retribution. Edom stood by, rejoiced, and actively participated in the plunder and slaughter of Judah during the Babylonian invasion. Their active, malicious doing was characterized by pride and betrayal. The prophet declares that the Day of the LORD is near, a day when the passive recipient of justice becomes the active object of God's judgment. The verb highlights that the punishment is not arbitrary; it is the organic fruit of Edom's own conduct.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The retaliatory use of asah in Obadiah 1:15 points to the biblical principle of lex talionis (eye for an eye), which, on a national scale, ensures that oppressors cannot escape the consequences of their historical atrocities. The absolute certainty of this doing is reinforced by the proximity of the Day of the LORD. While human actors think their deeds are buried in the past, God's memory retains every act of injustice, ensuring that the measure humans mete out is the measure they ultimately receive.
