NEAR - ( qarob , Strong's H7138 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word qarob denotes close proximity in space, time, or relationship. In Obadiah 1:15, it sounds a critical alarm, declaring that the Day of the Lord is imminent and hovering over the nations. This word strips away false security, reminding the reader that divine justice is not a distant theory but an approaching reality that demands immediate attention.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The adjective qarob derives from the root verb qarab, which means to draw near, approach, or present an offering. The word picture is one of decreasing distance—like an approaching storm, a gathering army, or a person walking directly toward you. It conveys a sense of momentum, urgency, and inevitability. When something is qarob, it has entered the immediate sphere of influence, where it can no longer be ignored or avoided.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• qarob expresses three primary dimensions of nearness:

• Spatial Nearness: Physical proximity or being close by in distance.

• Temporal Nearness: Imminence in time, referring to an event that is about to occur.

• Relational Nearness: Closeness in kinship, association, or intimate relationship.

In prophetic literature, the term is repeatedly utilized in a temporal sense to announce the rapid approach of God’s decisive interventions in human history.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Encroaching Threat: In military contexts, qarob describes a battle that is raging just at the gates or an enemy who is closing the gap.

• The Kinsman Redeemer: It describes a close relative who has the legal right and moral obligation to redeem land, preserve the family name, or avenge a wrong.

• The Intimacy of God: It portrays the Lord as physically or spiritually close to His people, rather than distant, detached, or unreachable.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of qarob lies in its disruption of human complacency. Humans naturally relegate future judgment to a distant, comfortable horizon. By declaring that God’s day is qarob, Scripture asserts that:

• God’s timeline overrides human assumptions: Delay must not be confused with dismissal.

• The certainty of justice: Divine retribution is not a stagnant decree but an active force marching toward its target.

• The demand for immediate response: If judgment is near, the opportunity for repentance is shrinking, rendering delayed obedience extremely dangerous.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:15, the prophet addresses Edom, a nation characterized by pride and a false sense of security due to their high rocky fortresses. They believed they were physically and strategically inaccessible to their enemies.

By declaring "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near," Obadiah shattered this illusion. The word qarob serves as an ironic counterpoint to Edom's perceived safety. No matter how high or distant they felt from human threats, God’s cosmic day of accounting was already at their doorstep. Their pride had blinded them to the fact that the gap between their sin and their judgment had virtually closed.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 30:14 - "But the word is very near you, in your mouth and in your heart, that you may do it." (Spatial and spiritual accessibility of God's command)

• Psalm 34:18 - "The LORD is near to those who have a broken heart, and saves such as have a contrite spirit." (Relational comfort and presence in suffering)

• Psalm 145:18 - "The LORD is near to all who call upon Him, to all who call upon Him in truth." (Relational accessibility of God in prayer)

• Isaiah 13:6 - "Wail, for the day of the LORD is near! It will come as destruction from the Almighty." (Temporal imminence of judgment)

• Joel 1:15 - "Alas for the day! For the day of the LORD is near; it will come as destruction from the Almighty." (Temporal imminence of the Day of the Lord)

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), qarob in Obadiah 1:15 is translated using the Greek adverb eggus. This is the same word used in the New Testament to announce the nearness of the Kingdom of God (Mark 1:15) and the return of Jesus Christ (Revelation 1:3; 22:10). This establishes a linguistic and theological bridge between the Old Testament prophetic "Day of the Lord" and the New Testament eschatological hope and warning.
