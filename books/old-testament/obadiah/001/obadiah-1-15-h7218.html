HEAD - ( ro'sh , Strong's H7218 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ro'sh primarily refers to the physical head of a person or animal, but it is frequently used metaphorically to denote chief leadership, the beginning or summit of something, or the locus of personal responsibility. In the context of divine judgment and retribution, particularly in prophetic literature like Obadiah, the "head" serves as the specific place where the consequences of one's deeds return. It emphasizes the direct, inescapable, and highly personal application of God's poetic justice—what a person or nation has measured out to others is poured directly back onto their own head.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun ro'sh is one of the most frequent and foundational words in the Hebrew Bible, appearing over 600 times. Physically, it designates the uppermost part of the human or animal body, which contains the sensory organs and represents the seat of life, choice, and identity.

Culturally and historically, the head is the point of contact for significant life events. To lift up someone's head is to grant them honor or restoration, while to cover or shave the head represents deep mourning, distress, or shame. In legal and covenantal ceremonies, the head is where symbolic actions land; for instance, laying hands on the head of a sacrificial animal transferred guilt to it. Consequently, when biblical writers describe judgment "returning upon your own head," they evoke a vivid spatial image: the violent or sinful deeds sent out into the world curve back through the air like a boomerang, striking the very person who launched them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of ro'sh is broad but structurally coherent:

  • Anatomical Head: The physical head of humans, beasts, or birds.

  • Top or Summit: The highest point of a mountain, hill, tower, or building (such as the "top" of the tower of Babel).

  • Chief or Leader: The person of highest rank, such as the head of a father's house, a tribe, or a nation.

  • Beginning or Source: The start of a period of time (the "beginning" of months), a division of a river (riverheads), or the front of an army.

  • Sum or Total: The reckoning or census of a group of people (literally, "taking the head").

  • Personal Retribution: The localized recipient of blessing, curse, guilt, or punishment.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Boomerang of Retribution: The idiom "upon your own head" (b'ro'sho) portrays judgment as a weight falling from above. In ancient Near Eastern thought, actions had an organic, built-in consequence that God ultimately enforced. The head represents the self; thus, the person who devised the evil becomes the physical target of its inevitable return.

  • The Crown of Public Visibility: Because the head is the highest and most visible part of the body, it is where public status is displayed. Honor is shown through anointing oil or a crown on the head, while disgrace is shown by throwing dust or ashes on the head. Therefore, judgment landing on the head means the sinner's defeat and exposure will be visible to all.

4. Theological Implications

  • Perfect Proportional Justice (Lex Talionis): The biblical concept of justice is not arbitrary or vindictive; it is perfectly proportional. Obadiah 1:15 is the classic theological expression of this: "As you have done, it shall be done to you." The use of ro'sh guarantees that God's justice is never generic. It is tailor-made to match the crime and is delivered directly to the doorstep of the perpetrator.

  • Moral Structure of the Universe: God has structured the cosmos with a moral equilibrium. Evil deeds do not simply vanish into history. By bringing deeds back upon the head of the wicked, God vindicates His righteousness and proves that He governs the affairs of humanity.

  • Substitutionary Atonement: The principle of judgment landing on the head highlights the beauty of the sacrificial system. The transfer of guilt to the head of a substitute (Leviticus 1:4) prefigured Christ, who wore a crown of thorns—the physical symbol of the curse of the earth—upon His own head, absorbing the impact of the judgment due to His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:15, the prophet delivers a heavy sentence of judgment against Edom. When Jerusalem was sacked by foreign invaders, Edom did not merely watch; they gloated, actively participated in the looting, and even stood at the crossroads to cut off escaping Jewish survivors.

Edom felt incredibly secure. They lived in the high, rocky, mountainous regions of Sela—cliff dwellings that were metaphorically referred to as the "heads" or summits of the mountains. They believed their physical elevation made them untouchable.

Obadiah uses ro'sh as a devastating irony. Edom's pride made them lift up their heads in arrogance, but God declares that their cruel deeds will crash directly back "upon your own head." Their high-altitude fortresses cannot protect them; the height from which they looked down on Israel will only increase the velocity of the judgment falling upon them.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 9:57 - "And all the evil of the men of Shechem God returned on their own heads, and on them came the curse of Jotham the son of Jerubbaal."

  • 1 Samuel 25:39 - "So David heard that Nabal was dead, and he said, 'Blessed be the LORD, who has pleaded the cause of my reproach from the hand of Nabal, and has kept His servant from evil! For the LORD has returned the wickedness of Nabal on his own head.'"

  • 1 Kings 2:32 - "The LORD will return his blood on his own head, because he struck down two men more righteous and better than he..."

  • Esther 9:25 - "...but when Esther came before the king, he commanded by letter that this wicked plot which Haman had devised against the Jews should return on his own head..."

  • Psalm 7:16 - "His trouble shall return upon his own head, And his violent dealing shall come down on his own crown."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Contrast of Joy and Sorrow: While the wicked experience their violent deeds returning to their heads, the righteous are promised "everlasting joy on their heads" (Isaiah 35:10).

  • Anointing: The head is the place where the Holy Spirit's empowerment is symbolized. Psalm 23:5 states, "You anoint my head with oil," indicating that the believer's mind, thoughts, and identity are saturated with God's protective grace, shielding them from the blows of the enemy.
