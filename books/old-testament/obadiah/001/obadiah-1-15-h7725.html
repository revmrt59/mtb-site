RETURN - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub fundamentally means to turn back, return, or go back to a starting point. In the context of Obadiah 1:15, it describes the inevitable boomerang effect of divine justice, where Edom's malicious actions against Judah are turned back upon their own heads. It serves as a stark theological declaration that God's moral universe ensures that the violence and betrayal one inflicts on others will ultimately return to punish the perpetrator.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The concrete imagery of shub is movement back to a point of origin. It is a verb of motion, depicting a path walked in one direction that is subsequently retraced in the opposite direction.

• In the ancient Near East, paths and highways were prominent metaphors for one's life choices. To shub is to pivot and head back to where one started.

• In the context of retributive justice (lex talionis), shub functions as a moral boomerang. The evil sent out by a nation travels along a spiritual trajectory, only to reverse course and strike the sender with equal force.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb shub occurs over 1,000 times in the Hebrew Bible, making it one of the most common verbs in the Old Testament. Its primary semantic ranges include:

• To return, turn back, or go back (Qal stem).

• To restore, refresh, or bring back (Hiphil stem).

• To turn away from, or repent (frequently used with the preposition min, meaning "to turn back from" sin).

• To recompense, requite, or repay (rendering back to someone what they deserve).

• In Obadiah 1:15, the verb is in the Qal imperfect form (yashub), indicating an ongoing, guaranteed future action: "your reprisal shall return."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Return: Used of people returning to their land (such as the return from exile) or returning to their homes.

• Spiritual Return (Repentance): Used of a sinner turning back to God, or God turning back to His people in mercy after a period of discipline.

• Reciprocal Action (Justice): Used of evil or blessings returning to the head of the one who generated them. This represents a cyclic closure where the moral ledger is balanced. The "head" represents the seat of responsibility and the target of the descending consequence.

4. Theological Implications

• The use of shub in divine judgments highlights the perfect equity of God’s sovereignty. God does not need to invent arbitrary punishments for the wicked; He simply allows their own sins to return to them.

• Judgment is the logical, completed cycle of human rebellion. This demonstrates that holiness and justice are woven into the fabric of creation. Sin carries its own seed of destruction, which inevitably returns to harvest consequences for the sinner.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:15, the prophet addresses Edom, a brother nation that rejoiced over and actively participated in Judah's destruction. Edom stood at the crossroads to cut off Judah's fugitives and hand them over to their enemies.

• The context is the "Day of the LORD," the final consummation of divine judgment on all nations.

• The specific phrase "Your reprisal shall return upon your own head" mimics the poetic justice of God's law. Because Edom treated their brother with violence, violence will return to them. The word shub underlines that Edom is not a victim of random fate, but of their own returned deeds.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 7:16 - "His trouble shall return upon his own head, And his violent dealing shall come down on his own crown."

• Judges 9:57 - "And all the evil of the men of Shechem God returned on their own heads, and on them came the curse of Jotham the son of Jerubbaal."

• 1 Kings 2:32 - "The LORD will return his blood on his own head, because he struck down two men more righteous and better than he..."

• Joel 3:4 - "...Will you retaliate against Me? But if you retaliate against Me, Swiftly and speedily I will return your retaliation upon your own head;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The concept of shub in retribution aligns with the New Testament principle of sowing and reaping (Galatians 6:7). What a person sows, that they will also reap.

• This principle contrasts with the redemptive use of shub. While the unrepentant experience the return of their sins upon their own heads, those who repent (shub to God) find that God returns to them in mercy and restores what was lost. Edom's refusal to turn to God sealed their fate, leaving them to face the return of their actions.
