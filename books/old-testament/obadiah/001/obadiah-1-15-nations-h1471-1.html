NATIONS - (gôwy, Strong's H1471)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun goy refers to a distinct body of people, a political entity, an ethnic group, or a foreign nation. While it can denote any socio-political group, in prophetic literature it frequently designates non-Israelite Gentile nations. In Obadiah 1:15, the plural form refers to all foreign nations, expanding the prophet's message from a localized indictment against Edom to a universal decree of divine judgment. By placing Edom's specific crimes within the scope of Yahweh's judgment upon "all the nations," the word establishes that no earthly power, Gentile nation, or arrogant neighbor stands outside God's moral governance and retributive justice on the "Day of the LORD."

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The noun goy designates a corporate body of people defined by shared political structure, geographical territory, linguistic identity, or ancestral lineage. Throughout the Old Testament narrative, the term is used to describe the geopolitical landscape of the ancient Near East, encompassing both major empires (such as Egypt, Assyria, and Babylon) and smaller regional kingdoms (such as Moab, Ammon, and Edom).

Although Israel is occasionally referred to as a goy—specifically when highlighting her status as a distinct national body constituted by God's covenant—the word predominantly identifies the foreign, Gentile nations surrounding Israel. In historical and prophetic settings, these nations were often characterized by idolatry, pagan worship practices, and territorial aggression against God's covenant people. In the book of Obadiah, the background centers on the aftermath of Jerusalem's ruin, where Edom actively participated in and celebrated Judah's downfall, prompting a prophetic decree that encompasses both Edom and the surrounding world.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, goy signifies a "nation," "people," or "Gentile body." At its core, the word carries a socio-political definition, describing a structured human population rather than an abstract concept of race.

In standard lexical usage:

  • It denotes a geopolitical nation possessing borders, leaders, and distinct cultural practices.

  • It identifies Gentile or non-Jewish peoples as distinguished from Israel ('am), though both terms can occasionally overlap depending on context.

  • In prophetic and eschatological contexts, it often carries a qualitative association with pagan powers operating in ignorance of, or opposition to, the true God.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Hebrew Scriptures, goy exhibits a range of nuances depending on whether the emphasis is covenantal, historical, or prophetic:

  • Covenantal promise: God promises Abraham that He will make of him a great goy, utilizing the term to describe the physical and national growth of his descendants under divine blessing.

  • Historical geography: The term regularly identifies the pagan tribes of Canaan or the foreign nations that Israel encountered during the Conquest and Monarchy periods.

  • Prophetic judgment: Prophets frequently deliver "oracles against the nations," using goyim to designate foreign world powers brought under divine scrutiny for their pride, violence, and moral corruption.

Regarding the target occurrence in Obadiah 1:15, the authoritative morphology confirms that the noun is masculine plural in the absolute state. The plural form (goyim) refers collectively to the aggregate of Gentile nations existing in the world.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of goy in Old Testament prophetic literature carries substantial theological weight regarding divine sovereignty and international justice:

  • Universal Sovereignty: Yahweh is not merely a regional or tribal deity of Israel; He is the supreme ruler of every goy on earth. International history moves according to His sovereign decrees.

  • Universal Moral Accountability: Gentile nations that do not possess the written Mosaic Law are nonetheless accountable to Yahweh for basic moral principles, including human dignity, justice, and how they treat neighboring peoples.

  • The Eschatological Day of the LORD: The prophetic horizon includes a definitive time when God intervenes in human history to break the oppressive power of hostile nations and establish His righteous reign over all the earth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:1–14 focuses intently on Edom, detailing her pride, her gloating over Judah's destruction, and her active betrayal of Jewish refugees. However, in Obadiah 1:15, the scope dramatically expands: "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near."

The term goyim serves as the pivot of the entire prophecy. By moving from Edom individually to "all the nations" corporate, Obadiah demonstrates that Edom's evil is not an isolated incident, but part of a wider human hostility toward God and His covenant family. Edom serves as a concrete archetype of Gentile pride and opposition to God.

Furthermore, the immediate sentence connects the target word directly to the principle of divine retributive justice (lex talionis): "as you have done, it shall be done to you; your reprisal shall return upon your own head." By using goyim here, the text asserts that the impending judgment is not an act of arbitrary vengeance, but a righteous measure applied impartially to every nation that participates in hostility and injustice.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalms 2:1 — "Why do the nations rage, and the people plot a vain thing?"

  • Psalms 33:10 — "The LORD brings the counsel of the nations to nothing; He makes the plans of the peoples of no effect."

  • Isaiah 52:15 — "So shall He sprinkle many nations. Kings shall shut their mouths at Him..."

  • Jeremiah 1:10 — "See, I have this day set you over the nations and over the kingdoms, to root out and to pull down..."

  • Ezekiel 36:36 — "Then the nations which are left all around you shall know that I, the LORD, have rebuilt the ruined places..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition from local indictment to universal judgment in Obadiah 1:15 illuminates how Old Testament prophecy bridges immediate historical events with end-time theological truths. Edom shared ancestral ties with Judah (through Esau and Jacob), yet reduced herself to the moral posture of the surrounding pagan goyim. Her inclusion among the doomed nations underscores that earthly lineage or geographical proximity offers no shield against divine holiness. For readers of prophetic literature, the treatment of the goyim highlights God's unwavering commitment to vindicate His righteousness across every earthly realm and culture.
