RETURN - (shûwb, Strong's H7725)

Summary:

ecological, spatial, legal, and relational contexts across the Old Testament. At its core, the root denotes physical movement back to a point of origin, turning around, or restoring a previous state or relationship. In covenantal contexts, it frequently signifies turning back to Yahweh in repentance or turning away from evil. In legal and moral contexts, it describes restitution or the return of actions and consequences upon the perpetrator. In Obadiah 1:15, shuv describes divine retribution on the Day of the LORD, where Edom's cruelty and violence against Judah return directly upon Edom's own head. The word contributes to Obadiah's argument by establishing that God's impending judgment operates through strict moral reciprocity: the evil Edom inflicted on others will be turned back upon themselves as an inevitable act of divine justice.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew verb shuv is one of the most fundamental dynamic verbs in Old Testament vocabulary, capturing the basic motion of turning, reversing direction, or returning to a place, person, or status. The prophetic book of Obadiah addresses the kingdom of Edom, descendants of Esau, who participated in and gloated over the destruction of Jerusalem by foreign invaders. Rather than acting as a brotherly neighbor to Judah, Edom looted Judah's wealth, stood at the crossroads to cut off fleeing survivors, and delivered fugitives to the enemy. Against this historical backdrop of betrayal and arrogance, Obadiah delivers a message of impending judgment. Verse 15 marks a transitional pivot in the prophecy, shifting from Edom's specific sins to the cosmic "Day of the LORD upon all the nations." Within this announcement of divine governance, shuv functions as the legal and moral mechanism of divine requital.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shuv spans several distinct categories of movement and restoration:

  • Physical / Spatial Motion: To go back, return to a location, or turn around.

  • Relational / Covenantal Movement: To return to Yahweh after apostasy, to repent, or conversely, to turn away from righteousness.

  • Restoration / Restitution: To restore property, return taken items to an owner, or render back a payment or answer.

  • Moral Reciprocity / Retribution: To cause conduct, bloodguilt, or recompense to return to the person or entity that originated it.

In Obadiah 1:15, the lexical focus rests on moral retribution and reversal. The target word operates in tandem with ghemul ("reprisal" or "reward/requital"), indicating that human conduct is not lost or forgotten in history, but is redirected by God back to its source.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, shuv portrays both human actions toward God and God's sovereign ordering of human destiny:

  • Physical and Spatial Usage: The word describes physical journeys, such as returning home, carrying items back to a owner, or retreating from a battlefield.

  • Covenantal Turning: The prophetic call to "return to the LORD" employs shuv to describe true heart repentance—turning away from idolatry and returning to covenant faithfulness.

  • Legal Reversion: In statutory law, such as the Jubilee regulations, shuv depicts land or property returning to its original family inheritance.

  • Retributive Justice: In prophetic and wisdom literature, shuv frequently appears alongside anatomical metaphors (such as "upon the head") to portray divine judgment. Evil is pictured as an arrow or weight launched by the sinner that God causes to loop back and land upon the perpetrator.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of shuv in contexts of divine judgment underscores several key biblical doctrines:

  • Moral Governance of God: God does not judge arbitrarily. Divine judgment often takes the form of allowing human evil to run its full course until its consequences rebound upon the sinner.

  • The Day of the LORD: Obadiah presents the Day of the LORD not merely as localized destruction, but as the comprehensive alignment of history with divine righteousness. On that day, every nation's treatment of God's covenant people will be met with corresponding divine response.

  • Lex Talionis (Law of Reciprocity): The principle of "as you have done, it shall be done to you" reflects God's perfectly balanced justice. The measure used by the oppressor becomes the measure by which the oppressor is judged.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:15, the target occurrence of shuv carries the following authoritative morphological features: Qal stem, imperfect form, 3rd person masculine.

The Qal stem expresses the simple active action of returning, while the imperfect form presents this returning action as an impending certainty associated with the approaching Day of the LORD. The subject of the verb is Edom's "reprisal" or "requital" (ghemul), which is modified by the directional phrase "upon your own head."

The immediate context (Obadiah 1:10-14) explicitly catalogs Edom's crimes: violent hostility, standing aloof during Judah's disaster, rejoicing over their ruin, looting their substance, and intercepting their fugitives. Verse 15 introduces the grand rule of divine justice: "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near; As you have done, it shall be done to you; Your reprisal shall return upon your own head."

By employing shuv, Obadiah highlights a dramatic ironic reversal. Edom believed their mountain strongholds rendered them immune to consequences. However, God declares that Edom's malice will not cross over to consume Judah permanently; instead, God will turn that malice back around, causing it to descend upon Edom's own head. The perpetrator becomes the ultimate recipient of their own violence.

  • Leviticus 25:28 – "But if he is not able to have it restored to himself, then what was sold shall remain in the hand of him who bought it until the Year of Jubilee; and in the Jubilee it shall be released, and he shall return to his possession."

  • 2 Chronicles 6:37 – "yet if they come to themselves in the land where they were carried captive, and repent [return] and make supplication to You in the land of their captivity, saying, 'We have sinned, we have done wrong, and have committed wickedness'..."

  • Job 36:10 – "He also opens their ear to instruction, and commands that they turn back from iniquity."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Idiom "Return Upon Your Own Head": The combination of shuv with the preposition b' ("in/upon") and rosh ("head") is a recognized Hebrew legal and prophetic idiom for bearing personal guilt and suffering the direct consequences of one's own actions.

  • Prophetic Reversal in Obadiah: The overall structure of Obadiah contrasts Edom's temporary exaltation with Judah's eventual restoration. While Edom's deeds shuv (return) upon them in destruction, God promises that the exiled house of Jacob will return to possess their heritage.
