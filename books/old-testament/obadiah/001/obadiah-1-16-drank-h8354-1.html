DRANK - (shâthâh, Strong's H8354)

Summary:

The basic lexical meaning of the Hebrew verb shatah is to drink, imbibe liquid, or participate in a feast. In Obadiah 1:16, shatah describes Edom's sacrilegious carousal and revelry on Mount Zion during Jerusalem's downfall. It serves as the primary comparative link in the prophet's announcement of judgment: just as Edom arrogance led them to drink in celebration over God's fallen people on His holy mountain, so all surrounding nations will be forced to drink the inescapable cup of Yahweh's retributive justice until they are completely consumed.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Near Eastern culture, drinking was an essential aspect of daily life, hospitality, covenant-making, and festive celebration. In a dry and arid climate, water was vital for survival, while wine and strong drink symbolized agricultural abundance, joy, and communal fellowship. Banquets and feasts regularly centered on eating and drinking together, establishing social bonds or celebrating military victories.

Alongside its everyday literal setting, drinking developed a rich metaphorical significance in ancient Israelite thought and prophetic discourse. Shared drinking could signify divine fellowship or covenantal intimacy. Conversely, taking part in unauthorized or arrogant feasting—especially upon holy ground or in the wake of a neighbor's ruin—was viewed as a grave moral transgression. Furthermore, the act of drinking frequently symbolized receiving one's allotted portion from God, whether that portion was blessing and joy or intoxication and divine wrath.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shatah refers to the act of swallowing liquid for nourishment, refreshment, or pleasure. Its primary definition encompasses taking in water, wine, milk, or other beverages.

Beyond physical liquid consumption, Hebrew literature frequently employs shatah in figurative and literary contexts:

  • Literal Nourishment and Refreshment: Satisfying physical thirst or partaking in everyday meals.

  • Feasting and Celebration: Participating in banquets, covenant meals, or festive gatherings.

  • Metaphorical Receptivity: Imbibing wisdom, accepting instruction, or consuming wickedness.

  • Prophetic Retribution: Drinking the figurative "cup of Yahweh's fury," representing the complete reception of divine judgment.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament Scriptures, shatah appears across various literary genres, including historical narratives, wisdom literature, and prophetic books.

In narrative literature, the verb routinely describes basic human sustenance and hospitality, such as travelers drinking from wells or hosts offering drink to guests. It also appears in contexts of covenant ratification, where leaders eat and drink in the presence of God or high parties to seal an agreement.

In wisdom literature, shatah is paired with eating to convey the simple enjoyment of life's daily provisions under God's providence, or symbolically to describe internalizing wisdom versus partaking in violence and folly.

In the prophetic books, shatah takes on an intense judicial nuance. Prophets frequently depict God holding a cup filled with the wine of His wrath. To "drink" this cup means to experience the full impact of God's righteous retribution for covenant unfaithfulness or prideful oppression. In these contexts, drinking is not a voluntary act of refreshment, but a forced, overwhelming consumption of divine sentence.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of shatah in prophetic literature centers on the principle of lex talionis— divine retributive justice where the punishment matches the offense. When nations or individuals drink in sinful gloating over the ruin of others, God answers by compelling them to drink the bitter cup of His judgment.

The act of drinking judgment underscores Yahweh's absolute sovereignty over human history and international politics. No creature can refuse the cup that God hands down. Sinful carousal and arrogance are temporary, whereas the divine retribution that follows is complete and irreversible. Furthermore, the contrast between drinking on God's holy mountain in desecration versus drinking the cup of wrath highlights the absolute sanctity of Yahweh's presence and holy spaces; profaning what belongs to God guarantees righteous divine intervention.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:16, the prophet delivers a sharp oracle of doom against Edom, Israel's brother nation, who stood aloof, gloated, and actively participated in the plundering of Jerusalem when the Babylonians invaded. The phrase "as you drank on My holy mountain" specifically highlights Edom's callous, arrogant revelry and desecration on Mount Zion following Jerusalem's destruction. They celebrated the destruction of God's sanctuary as if Yahweh had been defeated.

The targeted morphology of shatah in this verse provides critical clarity to Obadiah's argument. The verb appears in the Qal stem, perfect form, second-person masculine plural. The Qal stem expresses the simple verbal action, while the second-person masculine plural directly addresses the perpetrators of this gloating—Edom and its people. The perfect form presents Edom's arrogant drinking on Mount Zion as an established, completed historic reality ("you drank").

This past historic action becomes the direct premise for the future divine response: "so shall all the nations drink continually; yes, they shall drink and swallow, and they shall be as though they had never been." Obadiah uses a striking poetic contrast based on shatah. Edom drank in temporary, unholy celebration; therefore, all the wicked nations will be forced to drink Yahweh's judgment unceasingly. Their forced drinking and swallowing of divine wrath will lead to their ultimate undoing, reversing their boastful joy into complete oblivion.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The repetition of shatah in Obadiah 1:16 creates a vivid literary transition from human arrogance to divine judgment. The transition from the localized, past action of Edom ("you drank") to the universal, ongoing sentence upon the nations ("all the nations drink continually") establishes a broad canonical theme: local injustice against God's people on Earth brings universal accountability before the throne of Heaven.

Additionally, the imagery of drinking and swallowing until one is "as though they had never been" conveys total eradication. The very mouth that opened in proud carousal over Mount Zion is forced open to swallow the cup of Yahweh's wrath, demonstrating the ironic power of divine justice.
