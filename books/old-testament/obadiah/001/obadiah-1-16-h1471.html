NATIONS - ( goyim , Strong's H1471 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word goyim (plural of goy) refers to geopolitical bodies, ethnic groups, or territories, most frequently used in the Old Testament to designate the Gentile nations in distinction from Israel. In the prophetic literature, and specifically in Obadiah 1:16, it represents the collective global community of pagan peoples who stand in opposition to God and His covenant nation. Here, the "nations" serve as both the historical instruments of God's temporal judgment and the ultimate recipients of His divine justice. The passage depicts a dramatic reversal: just as the hostile nations gloated and caroused on God's holy mountain during Jerusalem's fall, they will be forced to drink the cup of His undiluted wrath continually, demonstrating that no worldly power escapes Yahweh's sovereign rule.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun goy originally carried the sense of a defined body or collective group. It shares a conceptual connection with words denoting a physical body, suggesting a unified, visible political entity with a shared territory, government, and language. Unlike am (which emphasizes kinship, family ties, and covenant relationships—typically used to describe Israel as God’s special people), goy emphasizes the political, geographical, and national organization of a group. When pluralized as goyim, it almost always carries an international, geopolitical, or heathen connotation, distinguishing the surrounding pagan empires from the covenant-bound people of God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Nation or People Group: A collective political body defined by geographic boundaries, government, and social organization.

  • Gentiles or Heathen: In the plural (goyim), the word refers to the non-Hebrew nations of the world, often characterized by their worship of false deities and their alienation from the covenants of promise.

  • Israel as a Nation: While am is the preferred term for Israel's covenant relationship, goy is occasionally used to describe Israel when viewed as a geopolitical entity or when God promises to make them a "great nation."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Raging Sea: The Old Testament frequently depicts the goyim as a roaring, chaotic sea or restless waters, symbolizing their spiritual instability and persistent rebellion against God's established order.

  • The Cup of Staggering: A prominent prophetic word picture associated with the nations is the cup of God's wrath. The nations are pictured as being forced to drink from this cup until they stagger, fall, and are completely overcome. In Obadiah 1:16, this imagery is central. The pagan nations who "drank" in wicked celebration on God's holy hill during Jerusalem's defeat will now involuntarily drink the cup of His retributive justice.

  • The Chaff on the Threshing Floor: The collective power of the goyim is pictured as light chaff that is easily blown away by the wind of God's judgment, emphasizing the futility of human empires standing against the Almighty.

4. Theological Implications

  • Sovereignty over Global Powers: The use of goyim highlights the universal rule of Yahweh. He is not merely a localized tribal deity of Israel, but the supreme King over all international empires.

  • Corporate Accountability: Nations rise, fall, and are judged not only as individuals but as collective entities. God holds societies accountable for their systemic sins, pride, and treatment of the vulnerable and His covenant people.

  • Eschatological Reversal: The prophets reveal that the hostile goyim will ultimately be subdued and judged, but they also offer a hope of redemption. Through the seed of Abraham, all the goyim of the earth will be blessed, and some from every nation will flow to the mountain of the Lord to worship Him in truth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:16, the word goyim functions within a strict poetic reversal (the law of retaliation, lex talionis). Edom (a brother nation to Israel) chose to align itself with the pagan goyim to plunder and celebrate over Jerusalem's ruin, participating in profane drinking on Mount Zion.

Obadiah transitions from addressing Edom specifically to addressing "all the nations" (kol-haggoyim). Edom’s localized hostility towards Judah represents the broader, global hostility of the pagan world against God's kingdom. The contrast is stark: the "drinking" of the nations changes from self-indulgent, victorious carousing on God's holy mountain to an forced, ceaseless gulping down of the cup of divine wrath. The phrase "shall drink continually" indicates that the judgment of the hostile world is inexorable, final, and absolute—leading to their complete spiritual and political dissolution.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 12:2 - "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you And make your name great; And you shall be a blessing."

  • Psalm 2:1 - "Why do the nations rage, And the people plot a vain thing?"

  • Isaiah 2:2 - "Now it shall come to pass in the latter days That the mountain of the Lord’s house Shall be established on the top of the mountains, And shall be exalted above the hills; And all nations shall flow to it."

  • Jeremiah 10:7 - "Who would not fear You, O King of the nations? For this is Your rightful due. For among all the wise men of the nations, And in all their kingdoms, There is none like You."

  • Joel 3:2 - "I will also gather all nations, And bring them down to the Valley of Jehoshaphat; And I will enter into judgment with them there On account of My people and My heritage Israel, Whom they have scattered among the nations; They have also divided up My land."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Transition in Divine Address: When Israel broke covenant with God and acted corruptly, God sometimes addressed them using the term goy rather than His intimate covenant term am, signaling a temporary loss of relational closeness (e.g., Isaiah 1:4, "Ah, sinful nation...").

  • New Testament Connection: In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), goy is regularly translated by the Greek word ethnos. This forms the direct linguistic link to the New Testament's "Great Commission" in Matthew 28:19, where Christ commands His disciples to make disciples of all the "nations" (ethne), showing that God’s redemptive plan ultimately encompasses the very goyim that once stood under His judgment.
