BEEN - ( hayah , Strong's 1961 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb hayah is the foundational verb of existence, being, and becoming in the Old Testament. In the context of Obadiah 1:16, it describes the absolute cessation of existence for the enemies of God's people. To "be as though they had not been" is a vivid expression of complete erasure, where nations that once proudly dominated the stage of history are reduced to nothingness under divine judgment, leaving no trace of their former reality.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb hayah does not merely denote passive existence or static being; it carries a dynamic sense of active presence, occurrence, and becoming. Unlike modern philosophical concepts of abstract "being," the ancient Hebrew mindset understood existence through activity, relationship, and historical manifestation. For something to "be" (hayah) meant it was present, active, and holding an established place in the historical order created by God. When a person or nation is reduced to "not being," it signifies more than physical death; it denotes the absolute removal of their presence, memory, and impact from the created order.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb hayah means to exist, happen, become, or come to pass. In its basic Qal stem, it represents:

• To exist or be in a certain state or condition.

• To happen, occur, or take place in history.

• To become, signaling a transition from one state of being to another.

In the specific context of Obadiah 1:16, the verb is used in the perfect aspect (lo hayu - "they had not been") to describe a hypothetical state of non-existence. It emphasizes the total reversal of historical reality, shifting the nations from a state of powerful, proud existence to a state as if they had never existed at all.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word hayah is one of the most frequently used verbs in the Hebrew Bible, serving several vital grammatical and thematic roles:

• The Covenant Formula: It is the root verb behind the divine name Yahweh, representing the self-existent One who acts in history.

• Creative Power: In Genesis 1, God speaks existence into reality, commanding light to "be," and it immediately "was."

• Relational Commitment: It is used in the covenantal promise where God declares that He will "be" their God, and they shall "be" His people.

In judicial contexts, such as Obadiah, the word picture shifts from creation to de-creation. God, who spoke the world into being, commands a judgment that returns rebel nations to a state of non-being.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, hayah highlights God's sovereign authority over existence itself. Creation exists only because God wills it to "be," and its continued existence is entirely dependent on His sustaining grace. When nations rebel against God and abuse His covenant people, they forfeit their right to exist in His world. The ultimate end of wicked nations is not just defeat, but complete historical erasure. This underscores the absolute finality of divine judgment: those who oppose the eternal God will eventually lose their own place in history, whereas those who trust in Yahweh are granted eternal, secure existence.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:16, the prophet addresses the nation of Edom and, by extension, all the pagan nations that rejoiced over the downfall of Jerusalem. Edom had "drunk" in celebratory defiance on God's holy mountain. Therefore, God decrees a measure-for-measure judgment: the nations will drink the cup of His wrath continually until they swallow it down and "shall be as though they had not been."

The nuance of hayah here is devastating. Edom was a proud, secure kingdom carved into the red rocks of Petra, seemingly permanent. Yet, God declares that their historical presence will be completely undone. Their pride, their power, and their very legacy will be vaporized, leaving no trace behind. It is a stark reminder that human security apart from God is an illusion.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 33:9: "For He spoke, and it was done; He commanded, and it stood fast."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint renders this phrase using the Greek verb eimi in the imperfect tense (hos ouk hyparchontes - "as not existing"). This reinforces that the ancient translators understood this passage to mean a complete deprivation of existence and presence. In pastoral application, this word study provides comfort to suffering believers. The oppressive systems and hostile powers that seem so permanent today will, under the judgment of God, become "as though they had not been." Their power is temporary, but God’s kingdom is eternal.
