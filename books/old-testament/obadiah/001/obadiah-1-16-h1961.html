BE - ( hayah , Strong's H1961 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb hayah is the foundational verb of being, existence, and becoming in the Old Testament. In the context of Obadiah 1:16, it is used in a dramatic, absolute negative sense to describe the complete annihilation of God's enemies. To "be as though they had not been" signifies a total reversal of existence, where nations that once proudly dominated the stage of history are reduced to absolute nothingness under divine judgment, leaving no trace, legacy, or memory of their former rebellion.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root hayah carries the dynamic idea of actively existing, happening, becoming, or being present. It does not merely denote a static, passive state of existence, but rather an active, dynamic presence. It is closely related to the divine name Yahweh, the self-existent, covenant-keeping God who actively is. In ancient Near Eastern thought, existence was tied to purpose and presence; to "be" meant to have a defined place and function in the created order. Consequently, to cease to "be," or to "be as though one had not been," represents the ultimate tragedy of losing all created purpose, presence, and remembrance under the weight of divine wrath.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb hayah means to exist, to be, to become, to happen, or to come to pass. Its primary semantic ranges include:

• To exist or have being.

• To become, turn into, or be established as (frequently paired with the preposition le).

• To happen, occur, or take place (often used in historical narrative as "it came to pass").

• To remain or abide in a specified state.

In the negative construction found in Obadiah 1:16 (lo hayah), it denotes non-existence, cessation of being, or complete erasure from history.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb functions in various ways to convey reality and transformation across the Old Testament scriptures. It is the verb used in the creation account of Genesis, where God speaks and things come into active existence. This establishes hayah as a word of divine fiat; what God speaks comes into being.

When paired with prepositions, it describes dynamic relationships, such as becoming a people, a husband, or a curse. In poetic and prophetic literature, its sudden negation serves as a powerful word picture of complete vanishing—like smoke, a dream, or chaff blown by the wind—pointing to the fleeting nature of human pride apart from God.

4. Theological Implications

God is the ultimate source of hayah (existence). He alone is the self-existent One who possesses life within Himself. All other creation derives its "being" from Him.

Consequently, when God withdraws His favor or executes final judgment, the wicked lose their very foundation of existence. The theological weight of "being as though they had not been" is staggering; it implies that rebellion against the Creator leads to the undoing of creation itself. It is a state of de-creation, where the proud mockers of God's people are utterly wiped out from the scroll of history.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:16, the prophet addresses the Southern Kingdom and, more broadly, the hostile nations. Edom gloated over Jerusalem's destruction and participated in its plunder.

The verse sets up a tragic irony: just as the heathen drank in wild revelry on God's holy mountain, celebrating their victory, they will now be forced to drink the cup of God's wrath continually. The climax of this cup of judgment is that "they shall be as though they had not been." The repeated use of hayah in this verse contrasts their temporary, proud "being" with their permanent, future "non-being." Edom's pride of self-preservation in their high rocky fortresses is crushed; they will be so utterly eliminated that it will be as if they never even existed.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The grammar of Obadiah 1:16 employs the perfect tense of hayah twice at the end of the verse. The perfect tense in Hebrew prophecy often represents the "prophetic perfect"—describing a future event with such certainty that it is written in the past or completed tense. Though Edom and the hostile nations seemed strong and secure in Obadiah’s day, their total erasure was already a completed reality in the sovereign counsel of God.
