ALL - ( kol , Strong's 3605 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word kol expresses totality, the entirety of a group, or a whole sum without exception. In Obadiah 1:16, it underscores the universal and exhaustive reach of divine justice. While Edom gloated over Judah's localized ruin, the prophet announces a dramatic reversal: "all the nations" will drink the cup of God's wrath. This word elevates the prophecy from a local dispute between two sibling nations to a cosmic declaration of Yahweh’s sovereign judgment over the entire global stage.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The Hebrew noun kol stems from a root indicating completion, perfection, or making whole.

• In the ancient Near Eastern mind, using this term in a geopolitical sense evoked a gathering of the entire known world under a single decree.

• It functions like a massive net cast over the globe, drawing in every king, kingdom, and coordinate of human society without leaving a single element on the outside.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The term denotes totality, the entirety of a collective group, or the whole sum of a substance.

• Depending on its grammatical construction and the nouns it modifies, it translates as "all," "every," "the whole," "any," or "altogether."

• When paired with a plural noun, such as "nations" (goyim), it acts as an absolute quantifier, indicating the entire group without exception.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Throughout the Old Testament, the word is used in structural, legal, and historical descriptions to demonstrate comprehensive reach.

• In covenantal contexts, it demands the devotion of the "whole" heart, soul, and strength.

• In prophetic literature, it depicts a full cup of judgment that must be drained completely, leaving no dregs.

• The primary word picture is a complete circle or a sealed vessel; there is no crack through which anyone can slip, and there is no boundary left open.

4. Theological Implications

• This word establishes the universal scope of Yahweh's sovereignty. He is not a localized, tribal deity confined to the hills of Judea; His decrees govern the entire global order.

• It reinforces the doctrine of universal accountability. No empire is too vast, and no nation is too isolated, to escape His moral standards and ultimate judgment.

• It highlights the impartiality of divine justice. Just as Israel and Judah were judged for their covenant unfaithfulness, so too will all pagan nations be judged for their pride and violence.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:16, the word contrasts sharply with Edom's narrow, localized perspective. Edom rejoiced over the destruction of Jerusalem, thinking their own high mountain border secured them from disaster.

• By declaring that "all the nations" will drink, the prophet broadens the horizon. Edom's doom is not an isolated event but a preview of the cosmic judgment awaiting all rebellious nations.

• The placement of this word highlights that the cup of wrath will not stop at Edom's borders; it will circulate globally. Edom cannot rely on foreign alliances for rescue, because those very allies are included in the global sweep of divine wrath.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 6:12: "So God looked upon the earth, and indeed it was corrupt; for all flesh had corrupted their way on the earth."

• Psalm 22:27: "All the ends of the world shall remember and turn to the LORD, and all the families of the nations shall worship before You."

• Haggai 2:7: "and I will shake all nations, and they shall come to the Desire of All Nations, and I will fill this temple with glory,' says the LORD of hosts."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• This term is one of the most common words in the Hebrew scriptures, occurring over 5,000 times. Despite its frequency, its placement in prophetic announcements of judgment carries immense theological weight, elevating regional conflicts to cosmic lessons.

• When joined with "continually" (tamid) in Obadiah 1:16, it stresses that the judgment of the nations is an enduring reality of divine history, completely exhausting their pride until they are reduced to nothingness.
