NEVER - ( lo' , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew particle lo' is the primary adverb of absolute negation in the Old Testament, representing an unconditional, permanent "no" or "not." In Obadiah 1:16, when paired with the verb of existence (hayah), it expresses the ultimate consequence of divine judgment upon Edom and the hostile nations: absolute erasure. Rather than a temporary setback or a historical footnote, those who oppose God and His covenant people will face a judgment so thorough and final that they will be reduced to a state of complete non-existence, as if they had never even existed.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word lo' is the standard, objective negative particle in Biblical Hebrew. Unlike 'al, which expresses a subjective, temporary, or advisory deprecation (such as "do not" in a passing command), lo' represents an absolute, objective, and permanent negation. It is the word used throughout the Decalogue for "You shall not (lo') murder" or "You shall not (lo') steal," establishing an unalterable, eternal boundary. In historical or narrative contexts, when combined with the verb of existence (hayah), it shifts from a simple "not" to a declaration of complete absence or non-being. In the ancient Near East, to have one's name, lineage, or memory erased was the ultimate form of judgment, as existence was deeply tied to remembrance and legacy. To be "as though they had never been" is the ultimate reversal of creation—a return to the void.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The term lo' functions as an absolute negative adverb.

• It signifies complete negation, exclusion, or absence.

• When governing a verb in the perfect tense (such as hayu, "they were"), it denotes an objective fact of non-occurrence or non-existence in past time.

• The semantic range spans from simple negation ("not") to absolute prohibition ("never," "by no means") and complete deprivation ("nothing," "without"). In the specific syntax of Obadiah 1:16, the combination of the comparative preposition ke ("as / like"), the negative lo', and the verb hayu ("they were") creates an idiomatic construction meaning "as though they had never been."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The absolute nature of lo' is illustrated in its grammatical behavior:

• Absolute Prohibition: It establishes permanent moral boundaries that cannot be negotiated, defining what is eternally outside of God's will.

• Total Deprivation: It describes a state of utter lack or void, such as a land lo' sown (unsown) or a people lo' pitied (unpitied).

• Erasure of Identity: In prophetic judgment literature, it describes the complete removal of a nation's status, power, and memory, returning them to a state of pre-creation void.

4. Theological Implications

• The use of absolute negation in Obadiah 1:16 highlights the finality of divine judgment on human pride. Edom, secure in her rocky fortresses, believed she was indestructible. God's judgment, however, is not merely punitive but restorative of cosmic order, requiring the complete dismantling of prideful rebellion.

• It contrasts the transience of wicked empires with the permanence of God's kingdom. While the hostile nations "shall be as though they had never been," Mount Zion and the house of Jacob will possess their inheritance and remain forever.

• It underscores the severity of God's wrath. To be reduced to "nothingness" is the ultimate undoing of the blessing of existence. God, who calls things that are not as though they were, can also deem those who rebel as though they never were.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:16, the prophet addresses the remnant of Judah concerning the surrounding nations, especially Edom. The nations drank in revelry and violence upon God's "holy mountain" (defiling Jerusalem during its destruction).

• God reverses this cup of drinking. The nations will drink the cup of His wrath "continually." They will drink, swallow, and be utterly consumed.

• The phrase "as though they had never been" (lo' hayu) marks the transition from active judgment to the final state of the wicked. The drinking of God's wrath results in self-annihilation. Their power, their memory, and their historical footprint will be so thoroughly wiped out by subsequent conquests that their historical dominance is completely neutralized.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The construction ke-lo' hayu (literally "as not they were") is a powerful literary device. It highlights the optical illusion of earthly power. To the suffering Judeans, Edom and Babylon seemed permanent, towering realities. God's word recalibrates their vision: these empires are temporary vapor, destined for absolute negation.

• This Hebrew idiom finds its counterpart in New Testament descriptions of the second death and the finality of Gehenna, where that which opposes God is cast away into outer darkness, removed from the land of the living and the memory of the righteous.
