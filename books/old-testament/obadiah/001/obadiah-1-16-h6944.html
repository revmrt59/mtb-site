HOLY - ( qodesh , Strong's H6944 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word qodesh refers to that which is set apart, sacred, and consecrated for divine use, separated from the common or profane. In Obadiah 1:16, it describes Mount Zion as God’s "holy mountain," the unique earthly dwelling place of His presence. The Edomites committed a grave desecration by treating this sacred site with casual irreverence and drunken celebration during Jerusalem's fall. Because they violated God's holy space, He decrees a reciprocal judgment where the surrounding nations will continually drink the cup of divine wrath.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of qodesh conveys the primary idea of cutting, dividing, or setting a boundary. It represents a clean cut between the sacred and the profane, the divine and the ordinary. In the ancient Near East, things that were qodesh were withdrawn from ordinary, everyday human utility and dedicated exclusively to the service of a deity. When applied to Yahweh, it signifies His absolute uniqueness, His utter separation from the created order, and His moral perfection. Thus, a "holy" place is not holy because of any intrinsic natural quality, but because the holy God has claimed it as His own and manifested His presence there.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Qodesh is a masculine noun meaning holiness, sacredness, or a holy thing. It describes that which belongs to the sphere of the divine. The semantic range includes:

• The essential nature of God Himself, who is the absolute standard of holiness.

• Places consecrated by God's presence, such as the tabernacle, the temple, and Mount Zion.

• People set apart for God's service, such as the priesthood and the nation of Israel.

• Objects dedicated to worship, including sacrifices, priestly garments, and altar utensils.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, qodesh serves as a powerful verbal boundary marker.

• The Holy Ground: In the wilderness, Moses is commanded to remove his sandals because the ground is qodesh due to the burning bush manifestation of God. The physical dirt was ordinary, but God's presence drew an invisible line of sacredness around it.

• The Holy of Holies: Within the tabernacle, the most sacred chamber is the qodesh qodashim (the Holy of Holies), a place of such intense sanctity that only the High Priest could enter, and only once a year with blood.

• The Holy Hill: Jerusalem, specifically Mount Zion, is repeatedly called God's holy hill. It was the geographic center of His earthly dwelling, representing His sovereign rule and sanctuary among His people.

4. Theological Implications

The holiness of God is the organizing attribute of His character. It guarantees His justice, defines His love, and fuels His wrath against sin. Because God is holy, He cannot tolerate the defilement of what He has consecrated. Holiness demands respect, reverence, and distance from the defilement of sin. When humans treat holy things as common, they commit sacrilege, which triggers divine judgment. God's holiness is not passive; it actively defends its own honor and vindicates the sacredness of His name and His dwelling place.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:16, God addresses the Edomites: "For as you drank on my holy mountain, so all the nations shall drink continually..." The "holy mountain" (har qodsi) is Mount Zion. When Jerusalem fell to the Babylonians, the Edomites did not merely stand aloof; they actively rejoiced, gloated, participated in the plunder, and stood at the crossroads to cut off Jewish survivors.

The phrase "drank on my holy mountain" refers to the Edomites joining the pagan invaders in celebratory, drunken revelry on the sacred temple mount. They treated the geographic epicenter of God’s holiness on earth as a common tavern, desecrating His sanctuary with profane joy.

The contextual contrast is stark: Edom’s profane, mocking cup of celebration on God's holy hill will be answered by God forcing them and all pagan nations to drink the cup of His holy wrath. Their violation of the boundary of the qodesh sealed their national doom.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Levitical system, a sharp distinction is maintained between the holy (qodesh) and the common (chol), as well as between the clean (tahor) and the unclean (tame). Priests were commanded to teach the people the difference between the holy and the profane (Leviticus 10:10). Edom's fatal mistake was their complete spiritual blindness to this distinction. By treating Mount Zion as common ground to be plundered and desecrated, they demonstrated a total lack of the fear of Yahweh, bringing about their permanent historical erasure.
