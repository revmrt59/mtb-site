DRINK - ( shathah , Strong's H8354 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shathah primarily refers to the physical act of drinking liquids, but throughout the Old Testament, and specifically in prophetic literature, it takes on a profound, terrifying metaphorical weight. In Obadiah 1:16, the word transitions from describing Edom's arrogant, celebratory revelry on God's holy mountain to the inescapable judgment of God, wherein all rebellious nations are forced to "drink" the cup of divine wrath continually until they are utterly consumed.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Near Eastern culture, drinking was central to both daily sustenance and communal celebrations. To drink together was a sign of covenant, peace, and shared joy. Feasts and banquets involved drinking wine to celebrate victories or seal alliances. However, when a conquering enemy entered a sanctuary, they would desecrate it by drinking and carousing in celebration of their triumph. This background of arrogant, self-indulgent drinking on sacred ground forms the stark contrast to the cup of judgment that God subsequently forces the wicked to drink.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shathah occurs over 200 times in the Hebrew Bible. Its basic, literal meaning is to imbibe or swallow liquid, such as water, milk, or wine. It describes the satisfaction of physical thirst or the enjoyment of a banquet. Lexically, it functions in several distinct ways:

• Literal consumption: To drink liquids for sustenance or enjoyment.

• Part of feasting: To participate in banquets, often associated with eating as a comprehensive term for life's activities or celebrations.

• Figurative assimilation: To drink in or internalize something, such as iniquity or violence.

• Judicial reception: To drink the cup of divine wrath or judgment, indicating the complete, passive reception of God's punishment.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently uses shathah to depict deep spiritual realities:

• The Banquet of Covenant: To eat and drink in the presence of God signifies intimacy and peace, as seen when the elders of Israel beheld God and "ate and drank" on Mount Sinai.

• The Cup of Wrath: The most prominent prophetic word picture is the "cup of staggering" or "cup of fury." The Lord holds a cup of foaming wine mixed with spices, and the wicked of the earth must drain it to the dregs. In this metaphor, shathah represents the unavoidable and exhaustive experience of divine justice.

• Internalizing Sin: The wicked are described as those who "drink iniquity like water," portraying sin not merely as an external act, but as something eagerly consumed and internalized as if it were vital for survival.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shathah in the prophetic context highlights the principle of lex talionis—divine retribution in kind. Those who "drink" in sinful celebration or mock God's holiness will be forced to "drink" His righteous anger. It underscores that God's judgment is not arbitrary but is the direct, logical consequence of human rebellion. The imagery reveals that judgment is a draught that must be swallowed completely; there is no escaping its effects once the cup is handed down by the Sovereign Lord. It also points forward to the ultimate resolution of this theme in the New Testament, where Christ drinks the cup of wrath on behalf of His people so they might drink the water of life.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:16, shathah is used in two contrasting senses within a single verse to demonstrate absolute poetic justice. First, the Lord addresses the house of Jacob (or figuratively exposes Edom's conduct on Jerusalem's holy mountain): "For as you drank on My holy mountain..." Edom had treated God's sacred mountain, Zion, as a place of secular carousing, celebrating the destruction of Jerusalem by drinking and reveling. Second, the Lord declares the reversal: "...so shall all the nations drink continually; yes, they shall drink, and swallow, and they shall be as though they had never been." The celebratory, sacrilegious drinking of the wicked is met with the compulsory, destructive drinking of God's wrath. The repetition of shathah emphasizes that the punishment perfectly fits the crime. The nations will drink "continually"—an unending sequence of judgment—until they are completely blotted out from history.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 24:11 - "But on the nobles of the children of Israel He did not lay His hand. So they saw God, and they ate and drank."

• Psalm 75:8 - "For in the hand of the LORD there is a cup, And the wine is red; It is fully mixed, and He pours it out; Surely its dregs shall all the wicked of the earth Drain and drink down."

• Jeremiah 25:15 - "For thus says the LORD God of Israel to me: 'Take this wine cup of fury from My hand, and cause all the nations, to whom I send you, to drink it.'"

• Amos 6:6 - "Who drink wine from bowls, And anoint themselves with the best ointments, But are not grieved for the affliction of Joseph."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition in Obadiah 1:16 from literal drinking in celebration to figurative drinking in judgment is mirrored in the New Testament imagery of the cup. In the Garden of Gethsemane, Jesus prays concerning the "cup" He must drink (Matthew 26:39). On the cross, Christ drank the bitter cup of divine wrath—the very cup described in Obadiah and the prophets—so that believers might instead receive the "cup of salvation" (Psalm 116:13) and drink of the "water of life freely" (Revelation 22:17). The absolute ruin of the nations in Obadiah highlights the immense weight of the propitiation Christ achieved.
