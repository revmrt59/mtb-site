NATIONS - (gôwy, Strong's H1471)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun goy fundamentally refers to a distinct socio-political body of people, language group, or national entity, commonly translated into English as "nation," "heathen," or "Gentiles." While the word can occasionally describe Israel when emphasizing her national existence, it most frequently denotes the pagan, non-Israelite nations surrounding God's covenant community. In Obadiah 1:16, the word refers to the collective Gentile powers who have arrayed themselves against Yahweh and His people; the term serves to expand the scope of divine retribution from the single neighboring nation of Edom to all pagan nations, establishing that every human empire partaking in hostility against Yahweh's reign will swallow the cup of His ultimate judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Near Eastern culture, corporate identity was foundational to human society. A goy represented a cohesive socio-political entity defined by shared territory, governance, lineage, and language, contrasting with mere clan or family units. While the Hebrew Scriptures frequently use the term 'am to emphasize familial, relational, or covenantal bonds (often reserved for Israel as Yahweh's relational people), goy highlights formal political and national structure. Over the course of Old Testament history, goy increasingly took on a theological orientation to distinguish the surrounding, idolatrous Gentile powers from the unique covenant community of Israel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, goy denotes a nation, foreign group, or political body. Standard Old Testament lexicography (such as Brown-Driver-Briggs and the Theological Wordbook of the Old Testament) defines goy primarily as a nation or people group. The term encompasses both the territorial and political reality of a state and the collective group of people making up that state. Depending on context, it can refer to:

  • A generic nation or foreign people group.

  • Gentile powers distinct from Israel, often characterized by pagan religious practices.

  • Israel herself, when viewed structurally or nationally as a kingdom among other kingdoms.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament narrative and prophetic literature, goy carries nuanced theological weight depending on its setting:

  • The Surrounding Pagan Nations: Most commonly, the plural form refers to the foreign, non-covenant nations. These nations are frequently depicted as engaging in idolatry, opposing Yahweh's reign, and posing a spiritual snare to Israel.

  • Israel as a Distinct Nation: In foundational covenant texts, Yahweh promises to make Abraham a "great nation" and designates Israel as a "holy nation" set apart for His service. In these contexts, the word emphasizes national status under God's ruling law.

  • Objects of Prophetic Judgment and Hope: In the Prophets, the nations (goyim) are consistently subject to Yahweh's universal moral governance. They are held accountable for their cruelty, yet they are also envisioned as future beneficiaries of Abrahamic blessing when they stream to Zion to worship the true God.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of goy underscores the absolute, cosmic sovereignty of Yahweh. He is not merely a localized tribal deity bound to the borders of Canaan; He is the Ruler of all the nations of the earth. The goyim serve as instruments of His providence to discipline His covenant people, yet those same nations are held strictly accountable for their own arrogance, violence, and idolatry. Furthermore, the canonical narrative moves toward the ultimate submission and inclusion of the nations under the Messiah's righteous rule, wherein pagan lands are redeemed from judgment to become worshippers of Yahweh.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the short prophetic oracle of Obadiah, the judgment of Edom serves as a case study in divine justice. Edom, Israel's brother nation, had committed treasonous violence by rejoicing over Jerusalem's fall and plunder. In Obadiah 1:16, the prophet transitions from the specific sentence against Edom to a broader, universal declaration: "For as you drank on My holy mountain, so shall all the nations drink continually; yes, they shall drink and swallow, and they shall be as though they had never been."

Morphologically, goy is used here in its plural form (goyim), categorized grammatically as a masculine plural noun in the absolute state. This plural form expands the horizon from Edom's localized crime to the full sum of surrounding pagan powers. The metaphor of drinking the cup of divine wrath on God's holy mountain contrasts Edom's actual, arrogant revelry on God's conquered hill with the terrifying spiritual reality of divine judgment. The presence of goy in the absolute plural establishes that Edom is not an isolated case; rather, Edom represents the pattern of human hostility toward Yahweh's kingdom. Consequently, all foreign nations that conduct themselves like Edom will consume the full measure of Yahweh's retributive justice until their imperial pride is completely dismantled.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalms 33:10 – "The LORD brings the counsel of the nations to nothing; He makes the plans of the peoples of no effect."

  • Jeremiah 1:10 – "See, I have this day set you over the nations and over the kingdoms, to root out and to pull down, to destroy and to throw down, to build and to plant."

  • Ezekiel 39:28 – "...then they shall know that I am the LORD their God, who sent them into captivity among the nations, but also brought them back to their land, and left none of them captive any longer."

  • Haggai 2:14 – "Then Haggai answered and said, 'So is this people, and so is this nation before Me,' says the LORD, 'and so is every work of their hands; and what they offer there is unclean.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition in Obadiah 1:16 from the singular focus on Edom to the plural goyim demonstrates a vital prophetic principle: local events in biblical history serve as paradigms for the final Day of the Lord. Edom’s gloating over Jerusalem foreshadows the global hostility of the goyim against Yahweh's kingdom. By declaring that all the nations will drink the cup of judgment continuously, the passage establishes the moral consistency of God's throne. No earthly superpower operates outside of Yahweh's moral jurisdiction, and every political entity that sets itself in opposition to His covenant purpose will ultimately face the divine sentence described by the prophet.
