POSSESS - (yârash, Strong's H3423)

Summary:

To take possession of, to inherit, to occupy, or to dispossess. In Obadiah 1:17, yarash describes the house of Jacob reclaiming their rightful, God-given inheritance. It signifies the dramatic reversal of their exile, marking the restoration of God's covenant promises and the repossession of the land that had been lost to invading nations.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The concept of possession in the ancient Near East, particularly within the biblical narrative, is deeply tied to covenant and divine grant. Yarash is a foundational term in the Old Testament for Israel’s relationship to the Promised Land. Unlike modern concepts of inheritance, which primarily involve receiving property after a family member's death, the biblical concept of yarash centers on receiving and occupying a grant from a living Sovereign.

Historically, the word is most prominent in the narratives of the Exodus, the wilderness wanderings, and the conquest of Canaan. It describes the transition of Israel from landless slaves to the rightful occupants of the territory God promised to Abraham. Because the land was often already inhabited, the act of taking possession frequently required the removal of the current occupants, linking the concept of possession closely with dispossession.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yarash means to take possession of, to inherit, to occupy, or to drive out. The word encompasses both the initial action of acquiring territory and the subsequent state of holding it as a rightful inheritance.

In its various biblical contexts, the word can carry a positive sense of receiving a promise (inheriting) or a more forceful sense of military conquest (driving out or dispossessing enemies). In certain contexts, it can also describe being brought to ruin or poverty (as in being dispossessed of one's own goods). In Obadiah 1:17, the verb appears in the Qal stem, functioning to describe the action of taking or reclaiming possession of an inheritance.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The usage of yarash spans several distinct nuances in the Old Testament:

  • Covenantal Inheritance: It frequently describes Israel receiving the land of Canaan as a fulfillment of the Abrahamic covenant.

  • Dispossession/Driving Out: It is often used to describe the removal of pagan nations from the land so that Israel might occupy it. The act of Israel "possessing" the land inherently meant "dispossessing" the Canaanites.

  • Eschatological Restoration: In the prophets, the word is used to describe the future, ultimate restoration of God's people to their land after a period of judgment and exile.

  • Impoverishment: Occasionally, the word is used to describe the loss of possession, resulting in poverty or ruin.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yarash underscores the sovereignty of God over the nations and the earth. The land belongs to God, and He gives it as an inheritance to whomever He chooses. Israel’s possession of the land was never based on their own military supremacy or inherent righteousness, but on God's covenant faithfulness.

Furthermore, the concept of possession is tied to holiness and obedience. When Israel rebelled, they were dispossessed and sent into exile. The prophetic promise that they would once again yarash their possessions highlights God's enduring grace and the unbreakable nature of His covenant. It points forward to the ultimate inheritance of God's people in His restored kingdom.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:17, the prophet declares: "But on Mount Zion there shall be deliverance, And there shall be holiness; The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions."

The immediate context of Obadiah is a pronouncement of severe judgment against Edom for their pride and their violence against their brother Jacob (Israel) during a time of invasion and exile. Edom had gloated over Israel's destruction and looted their wealth. Verse 17 introduces a sharp contrast ("But on Mount Zion..."). While Edom faces total eradication (v. 18), Israel will experience deliverance and restoration.

The use of yarash here is central to this reversal of fortunes. The Qal perfect third-person plural form ("they shall possess") is used within Obadiah's prophetic declaration to describe the future reality of the house of Jacob reclaiming what is rightfully theirs. The nations that had encroached upon Israel's territory and plundered their wealth will be removed, and Israel will take back their God-given inheritance. This physical repossession of the land is directly linked to the spiritual realities of "deliverance" and "holiness" on Mount Zion, showing that the restoration of the land is inseparable from the restoration of their covenant relationship with God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 1:21 — "and possess"

Moses commands the Israelites to go up and take the land God has set before them, using the word to urge them to step into their divine grant.

  • Joshua 24:8 — "that you might possess"

Joshua recounts God's faithfulness in destroying the Amorites so that Israel could take ownership of their territory.

  • Judges 11:23 — "has dispossessed"

Jephthah argues that the Lord God of Israel drove out the Amorites from before His people, demonstrating that possession of the land is determined by divine action.

  • Isaiah 65:9 — "inherit"

In an eschatological promise of restoration, God declares that He will bring forth a descendant from Jacob who will take ownership of His holy mountains.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase "possess their possessions" in Obadiah 1:17 emphasizes the rightful ownership of the land. The territory did not belong to Edom or the other invading nations; it was the covenantal property of the house of Jacob. By using yarash, Obadiah connects the future restoration of the exiles to the original conquest under Joshua. Just as God originally gave them the land to possess, He will act again in history to ensure they reclaim it, demonstrating that His covenant purposes cannot be permanently thwarted by human enemies.
