POSSESSIONS - (môwrâsh, Strong's H4180)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun morash fundamentally denotes a possession, heritage, property, or domain held as a belonging, as well as the cherished mental plans or thoughts held within the heart. In Obadiah 1:17, morash refers to the physical and covenantal heritage belonging to the house of Jacob—the territorial allotment and covenantal blessings bestowed by God. Obadiah uses this word to proclaim that although Edom and other enemies plundered Judah during her crisis, God will execute judgment and restore His covenant people, enabling the house of Jacob to repossess the full extent of their God-given heritage.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The prophecy of Obadiah addresses the pride and violence of Edom, the brother-nation descended from Esau. During Judah's catastrophic vulnerability, Edom failed to show brotherly compassion; instead, they stood aloof, gloated over Judah's downfall, actively participated in plundering Jerusalem, and blocked the escape of survivors. Edom treated Judah's land and wealth as easy spoils to seize for themselves.

In this historical and theological setting, the noun morash enters the narrative at a decisive turning point. While Edom acted as if Judah’s heritage had been permanently erased or annexed, Yahweh speaks through Obadiah to declare that human plunder cannot override divine covenant promises. The noun morash comes from a verbal root denoting the act of taking possession, inheriting, or dispossessing. In Obadiah's oracle, it highlights the territorial and spiritual inheritance that Yahweh granted to Israel, affirming that what was unjustly looted by worldly powers will be restored by God's sovereign hand.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, morash refers to:

  • A concrete spatial possession, domain, or inherited property.

  • Figuratively, the inner "possessions" or cherished plans and thoughts of the mind and heart.

The word designates something that is firmly held, claimed, or designated as a property or legacy. In spatial contexts, it refers to land or territory allotted to an owner. In personal wisdom contexts, it refers to the deep-seated designs or expectations held in a person's inner life.

In Obadiah 1:17, morash carries the spatial and covenantal sense of inherited territory and property. It specifies the full range of physical and national holdings that rightfully belong to God's chosen people by divine decree.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across its biblical usage, morash appears in both concrete territorial contexts and abstract psychological contexts:

  • Mental/Inner Possessions: In wisdom literature, morash describes the deep designs, expectations, or cherished thoughts that a person possesses in their heart, which can be shattered by grief and tragedy.

  • Territorial Possessions: In prophetic literature, the word designates physical territory or domain. In judgment oracles, a proud nation's territory can be turned into a "possession" for wild animals under divine sentence. Conversely, in restoration oracles, God promises that His people will reclaim their rightful inherited territory.

In Obadiah 1:17, morash is paired with a verb from the same underlying root (yarash, meaning "to possess" or "dispossess"). This rhetorical pairing—"the house of Jacob shall possess their possessions"—creates an emphatic statement of divine restitution. The usage here underscores that Jacob's claim to the land is not based on geopolitical power, but on Yahweh's covenantal assignment.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of morash in Obadiah 1:17 anchors several essential Old Testament theological truths:

  • Inviolability of Covenant Inheritance: Yahweh’s land promises to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob are grounded in His eternal faithfulness. Though Israel experienced exile and temporal dispossession due to sin, foreign nations could not permanently erase God's land grant.

  • Divine Retributive Justice: Edom unjustly seized Judah's goods and territory during Jerusalem's calamity. Obadiah emphasizes that human plunderers who take what God has not given them will ultimately be dispossessed, while God's afflicted people will receive back their lost heritage.

  • The Resolution of the Jacob-Esau Struggle: The conflict between Esau (Edom) and Jacob (Israel) spans Old Testament history. Obadiah 1:17 announces the final resolution: Mount Zion becomes a place of deliverance and holiness, while the house of Jacob repossesses its inheritance, illustrating God's sovereign election and ultimate vindication of His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:15–21 describes the universal "Day of the LORD" when God executes judgment upon the nations and establishes His kingdom. Verse 17 serves as the hinge transition from judgment upon Edom to the glorious restoration of Israel.

The text sets up a stark contrast between Mount Esau and Mount Zion:

  • Mount Esau will face complete destruction, with no survivor remaining (Obadiah 1:18).

  • Mount Zion will be a place of deliverance, refuge, and holiness (Obadiah 1:17).

Within this immediate context, the grammatical form of morash is parsed as masculine plural absolute. The plural form highlights the expansive scope of the heritage. It is not merely a single parcel or surviving remnant of land that Jacob will possess, but their plural "possessions"—the full range of borders and inherited territories that Edom and surrounding nations had usurped. Surrounding verses (vv. 19–20) detail this expansion, describing Jacob occupying the Negev, the mountains of Esau, the lowland, the Philistine plain, and Gilead.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 17:11 – "My days are past, my plans are broken off, the thoughts of my heart." Here morash describes the cherished plans or mental "possessions" of Job's heart, which feel ruined amid his extreme suffering.

  • Isaiah 14:23 – "'I will also make it a possession for the porcupine, and marshes of muddy water; I will sweep it with the broom of destruction,' says the LORD of hosts." In this oracle against Babylon, morash designates a land domain that God reduces to a wasteland possessed by wild creatures.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Lexical Nuance versus Cognate Forms: The noun morash is closely related in root to morashah (another Hebrew noun translated "possession" or "heritage," found in passages like Exodus 6:8 and Deuteronomy 33:4). While distinct lexical nouns, both stem from the root yarash and express Israel's conviction that land and law are inherited gifts directly bestowed by Yahweh.

  • Reversal of Human Plunder: In Obadiah 1:13, Edom is condemned because "you should not have entered the gate of My people in the day of their calamity... nor laid hands on their substance in the day of their calamity." The turn in verse 17 shows that human hands may temporarily snatch substance (chayil), but they can never destroy the underlying covenant possession (morash) guaranteed by God.
