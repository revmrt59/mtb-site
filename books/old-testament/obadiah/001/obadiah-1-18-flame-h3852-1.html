FLAME - (lehâbâh, Strong's H3852)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun lehavah refers to a "flame" or the active, burning blaze of a fire, as well as the bright, flashing blade of a weapon such as a spear or sword. In Obadiah 1:18, lehavah describes the house of Joseph as a consuming blaze that, alongside the house of Jacob as fire, completely devours the house of Esau like stubble. The word underscores the divine, complete, and irresistible execution of judgment against Edom for its pride and violence against God's covenant people.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The noun lehavah is derived from the root semantic concept of burning or glowing brightly. Throughout the Old Testament, fire and its visible, radiating light—the flame—are frequently associated with divine manifestations, holy purifications, and righteous judgments.

In the historical and prophetic setting of the Book of Obadiah, the prophet pronounces the divine sentence upon Edom (the descendants of Esau). Edom had gloated over the destruction of Jerusalem, actively assisted its invaders, and cut down its fugitives. In response, Obadiah delivers a vision of poetic justice and divine retribution: those who participated in Israel's destruction will themselves be destroyed by the very remnant they despised.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, lehavah denotes:

  • A "flame" or "blaze"—the active, visible, heat-producing tongue of fire.

  • A "blade" or "glashing tip" of a weapon (such as a sword or spearhead), evoking the image of polished metal catching light like a flash of fire.

In Hebrew poetic structures, lehavah frequently stands in parallel with 'esh ("fire"). While 'esh emphasizes the element or material of fire itself, lehavah stresses its active, reaching, and consuming energy.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Old Testament literature, lehavah appears in a variety of literary contexts:

  • Divine Presence and Manifestation: It portrays the terrifying holiness of God appearing in storm, judgment, or divine glory.

  • Instruments of Divine Wrath: It depicts the destroying forces sent by God to consume the wicked, false nations, or rebellious Israel.

  • Literary Parallelism: As part of poetic pair structures, lehavah often intensifies the image established by fire, moving from the presence of heat to the visible, unstoppable flare that consumes surrounding material.

  • Agricultural Metaphor: It frequently operates alongside metaphors of drought or total destruction, where a flame sweeps through forests, pastures, or dry stubble.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of lehavah carries significant theological weight across the Old Testament canon:

  • The Righteous Judgment of God: Fire and flame demonstrate that God's holiness cannot tolerate unrighteousness, betrayal, and pride. Judgment is not arbitrary; it is the holy energy of God consuming that which opposes His righteous order.

  • The Doctrine of Retribution (Lex Talionis): In Obadiah, Edom sought to extinguish Jacob, but God turns the remnant of Jacob and Joseph into the fire and flame that consumes Edom.

  • Covenant Restoration and Unity: By mentioning both "the house of Jacob" (representing the southern kingdom) and "the house of Joseph" (representing the northern kingdom), the text highlights the ultimate corporate restoration of God's unified covenant people acting as His instrument of judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:18, the target word lehavah occurs in the absolute state as a feminine singular noun: "The house of Jacob shall be a fire, and the house of Joseph a flame (lehavah); the house of Esau shall be stubble; they shall kindle them and devour them, and no survivor shall remain of the house of Esau, for the LORD has spoken."

The syntax sets up a vivid compound metaphor comparing three entities:

1. House of Jacob = Fire ('esh)

2. House of Joseph = Flame (lehavah)

3. House of Esau = Stubble (qash)

The dynamic relationship between these terms is central to the author's message. Stubble (qash) represents dry, lifeless, highly combustible waste left in a field after harvest. When fire ('esh) is introduced, it generates a reaching, hot flame (lehavah) that leaves no part of the stubble unconsumed. By assigning the role of lehavah to the house of Joseph, Obadiah demonstrates that Israel's restoration will be active and intense, resulting in total judgment upon Edom so that "no survivor shall remain." The presence of lehavah emphasizes the complete, consuming effect of the decree guaranteed by the word of Jehovah.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Isaiah 10:17 – "So the Light of Israel will be for a fire, and his Holy One for a flame; it will burn and devour his thorns and his briers in one day."

  • Isaiah 43:2 – "When you pass through the waters, I will be with you; and through the rivers, they shall not overflow you. When you walk through the fire, you shall not be burned, nor shall the flame scorch you."

  • Ezekiel 20:47 – "Say to the forest of the South, 'Hear the word of the LORD! Thus says the Lord GOD: "Behold, I will kindle a fire in you, and it shall devour every green tree and every dry tree in you; the blazing flame shall not be quenched..."'"

  • Joel 2:3 – "A fire devours before them, and behind them a flame burns; the land is like the Garden of Eden before them, and behind them a desolate wilderness..."

  • Lamentations 2:3 – "He has drawn back His right hand from before the enemy. He has blazed against Jacob like a flaming fire consuming all around."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The combination of "fire," "flame," and "stubble" in Obadiah 1:18 belongs to a common prophetic judgment motif found elsewhere in the prophetic canon (e.g., Isaiah 5:24, Isaiah 47:14). In prophetic imagery, while human armies or historical circumstances may act as the immediate physical agents, the ultimate source of the lehavah is God Himself executing His righteous decree against persistent, unrepentant hostility toward His kingdom.
