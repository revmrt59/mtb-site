SPOKEN - ( dabar , Strong's H1696 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb dabar means to speak, declare, converse, or command. In its intensive Piel stem, which is how it almost exclusively appears as a verb, it represents a deliberate, authoritative, and dynamic act of communication. When the subject of this verb is Yahweh, it denotes much more than the mere transmission of information; it signifies an active, creative, and history-shaping decree. In the context of prophetic fulfillment, such as Obadiah 1:18, dabar guarantees the absolute certainty of the word's fulfillment. When "the LORD has spoken," the outcome is as secure as if it had already occurred, establishing God's sovereign authority over nations and history.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The root dbr in Semitic languages carries a primary sense of putting things in order, arranging, or leading. From this root, the noun dabar (word, matter, thing) and the verb dabar (to speak) are derived.

  • To speak, in the ancient Near Eastern mindset, was not merely to utter sounds, but to set a reality in motion. A spoken word was viewed as a concrete entity that possessed power, direction, and substance.

  • When God speaks, His words arrange reality, bring order out of chaos, and drive history forward to its designated end. This stands in stark contrast to the empty speech of idols or the futile boasts of proud nations like Edom.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The verb dabar refers to the physical and intellectual act of speaking, declaring, conversing, or decreeing.

  • In the Qal stem, it is extremely rare, mostly occurring as a participle. In the Piel stem (the intensive verbal stem), it is the standard Hebrew verb meaning "to speak."

  • It denotes a broad range of verbal communication, including:

  • To declare or pronounce a formal decree.

  • To promise or make a covenant.

  • To warn or pronounce judgment.

  • To command with sovereign authority.

  • When God is the subject, dabar carries an irresistible divine efficacy. It is not tentative; it is an active force that accomplishes His precise purpose.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Dabar is often paired with the mouth, lips, or heart, showing that speech is the outward expression of inner character and intent.

  • It is frequently used in the prophetic formula, "The word of the LORD came," or "Thus says the LORD, for He has spoken."

  • In poetic parallelism, dabar (to speak) is often paired with amar (to say) or tsavah (to command). While amar focuses on the substance of what is said, dabar emphasizes the act of speaking and the authority of the speaker.

  • In the context of divine judgment, God's speech is pictured as a consuming fire, a hammer, or a sword. When God speaks judgment, the physical world and human kingdoms crumble before His voice.

4. Theological Implications

  • Divine Sovereignty and Omnipotence: God's speech is never passive. What He speaks is immediately backed by His infinite power. There is no gap between God's word and the reality it describes.

  • The Reliability of Prophecy: The phrase "for the LORD has spoken" serves as a seal of absolute certainty. Human promises fail due to weakness or change of mind, but God's spoken decrees are immutable.

  • The Nature of Divine Revelation: God is not silent. He condescends to speak in human language, making Himself and His purposes known. Revelation is personal, cognitive, and authoritative.

  • Covenant Faithfulness and Judgment: God's spoken word brings both the realization of His covenant promises to His people and the execution of His righteous judgments on their enemies.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Obadiah 1:18, the prophet pronounces the complete destruction of the house of Esau (Edom) and the restoration of the house of Jacob (Israel).

  • Edom was proud, trusting in their mountain fortresses, wisdom, and military alliances. They had spoken boastfully against God's people in the day of their calamity.

  • The verse climaxes with the clause, ki yhwh dibber ("for the LORD has spoken"). This functions as the ultimate guarantee of Edom's doom. Edom's prideful words are contrasted with Yahweh's sovereign decree.

  • Though Edom appeared secure and Israel appeared defeated at the time, the spoken word of Yahweh bypassed human probability. Edom's utter destruction and Israel's final victory were guaranteed because the self-existent, covenant-keeping God had spoken it into history.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 12:4 - "So Abram departed as the LORD had spoken to him, and Lot went with him." (Here, the verb represents the divine promise and directive that initiated the covenant history of Israel.)

  • Exodus 4:30 - "And Aaron spoke all the words which the LORD had spoken to Moses. Then he did the signs in the sight of the people." (Highlighting the human transmission of the divinely spoken word.)

  • Numbers 23:19 - "God is not a man, that He should lie, nor a son of man, that He should repent. Has He said, and will He not do? Or has He spoken, and will He not make it good?" (A powerful theological affirmation of the absolute reliability of God's spoken word.)

  • Isaiah 1:2 - "Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth! For the LORD has spoken: 'I have nourished and brought up children, and they have rebelled against Me.'" (An indictment of covenant rebellion, solemnized by the fact that Yahweh is the speaker.)

  • Joel 3:8 - "I will sell your sons and your daughters into the hand of the children of Judah, and they will sell them to the Sabeans, to a people far off; for the LORD has spoken." (An exact parallel to the prophetic seal found in Obadiah, confirming judgment on Israel's enemies.)

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The noun form of this root, dabar (word), is used in the Hebrew title of the Ten Commandments, known as the Aseret ha-Devarim ("Ten Words").

  • In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), dabar is most frequently translated using the verbs laleo (to speak) and lego (to say), and the noun logos (word) or rhema (spoken word).

  • The dynamic power of God's spoken word (dabar) laid the theological foundation for the New Testament understanding of the Logos in John 1:1, where Jesus Christ is revealed as the living Word of God, the ultimate expression of the Father's speech.
