KINDLE - ( dalaq , Strong's H1814 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb dalaq means to burn, set on fire, or hotly pursue. In its literal sense, it describes the rapid, consuming ignition of dry fuel, while its figurative use depicts an intense, burning chase or hostile persecution. In the prophetic judgment of Obadiah 1:18, the word illustrates the swift and absolute destruction of Edom (the house of Esau). God’s restored people will act as a holy fire, and Edom will be the dry stubble that they kindle and completely consume, leaving no survivors.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The imagery of dalaq is rooted in the agricultural and pastoral realities of the ancient Near East. During the dry summer months, fields of stubble, thorns, and chaff became highly combustible. A single spark could instantly ignite a fire that would sweep across the landscape, leaving nothing but ash. Dalaq captures the suddenness, intensity, and unstoppable momentum of such a fire. When applied to human actions, the word transitions naturally from literal burning to the "hot pursuit" of an enemy, picturing a pursuer who burns with anger and chases their prey with the relentless speed of a spreading wildfire.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb dalaq refers fundamentally to burning or catching fire. In its basic active form, it means to burn, flame, or glow. It also carries the dynamic sense of chasing hotly, persecuting, or closely pursuing. The lexical range spans:

• To set on fire or kindle a flame.

• To burn with heat or fever.

• To hotly pursue or eagerly chase after an enemy.

• To speak with burning, passionate, or deceptive intensity.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Literal Combustion: The word describes the physical act of igniting dry material to consume it. In prophetic literature, this literal burning serves as a vivid picture of sweeping, unavoidable destruction.

• Relentless Pursuit: In historical narratives and poetry, dalaq depicts a hunter or army pressing hard after a fleeing target. The pursuers are "burning" in their chase, driven by an urgent desire to overtake and capture.

• Burning Lips: In wisdom literature, the word describes lips that glow with outward warmth or passion but conceal a cold, malicious heart, resembling a cheap clay vessel covered with a thin, glittering glaze.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, dalaq in the context of judgment reveals the active, consuming holiness of God. Throughout Scripture, God’s presence and His righteous anger are symbolized by fire. When God decrees judgment against pride and wicked rebellion—such as that of Edom—He does not merely watch their demise; He actively kindles a fire of retribution. In Obadiah, the theological shift is profound: God uses His covenant people (the house of Jacob and Joseph) as the instruments of this divine fire. The very people whom Edom plundered and mocked will become the fire that kindles and consumes them, demonstrating that God vindicates the oppressed and executes perfect justice through history.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:18, dalaq is placed within a striking three-part poetic parallel: "The house of Jacob shall be a fire, / And the house of Joseph a flame; / But the house of Esau shall be stubble; / They shall kindle them and devour them..."

Edom had proudly trusted in their secure mountain fortresses of rock, believing themselves to be untouchable. However, God exposes their true spiritual and physical vulnerability by comparing them to dry, dead "stubble" (chaff left after harvest). In contrast, the reunited remnant of Israel is depicted as a raging "fire" and "flame." The nuance of dalaq here is effortless and total destruction. Stubble cannot resist or negotiate with fire. The moment the flame touches the dry stalks, they ignite instantly. This indicates that Edom's fall will be sudden, complete, and devoid of escape, resulting in "no survivor."

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 31:36: "Then Jacob was angry and rebuked Laban, and Jacob answered and said to Laban: 'What is my trespass? What is my sin, that you have hotly pursued me?'" (Here, dalaq is translated as "hotly pursued," capturing Laban’s intense, burning chase after Jacob).

• Psalm 10:2: "The wicked in his pride persecutes the poor; Let them be caught in the plots which they have devised." (In this verse, dalaq is rendered "persecutes," illustrating how the wicked burn with hostile zeal to run down the helpless).

• Proverbs 26:23: "Fervent lips with an evil heart Are like earthenware covered with silver dross." (Here, the participle of dalaq is translated "fervent" or "burning," indicating words that sound warmly affectionate but mask a deceitful heart).

• Lamentations 4:19: "Our pursuers were swifter Than the eagles of the heavens. They pursued us on the mountains, They lay in wait for us in the wilderness." (In the context of Jerusalem's fall, the swift, burning pursuit of the Babylonian conquerors is described using dalaq).

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between the "mountain of Esau" and the "mountain of Zion" runs throughout the book of Obadiah. Edom stood aloof during Jerusalem’s calamity, actively participating in the slaughter of Jewish fugitives. In the economy of God's justice (the law of retaliation, or lex talionis), the punishment fits the crime. Because Edom "burned" with hostile hatred against Israel during their day of trouble, Israel will ultimately be empowered by God to "kindle" (dalaq) Edom like dry stubble.
