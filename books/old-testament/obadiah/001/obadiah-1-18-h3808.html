NO - ( lo' , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew particle lo' is the primary adverb of negation in the Old Testament, expressing absolute, permanent, and objective negation. In Obadiah 1:18, it is paired with the verb hayah ("to be") and the noun sarid ("survivor") to declare the total, irreversible annihilation of the house of Esau. In the prophetic announcement of judgment against Edom, this tiny but powerful word underscores the absolute certainty and completeness of divine justice, leaving absolutely zero remnants of the proud nation that gloated over Judah's downfall.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The word lo' is the most frequent negative particle in Biblical Hebrew, occurring over 5,000 times. Unlike subjective or temporary negatives (such as 'al, which expresses a wish, deprecation, or immediate command "do not"), lo' represents an objective, unconditional, and permanent "no" or "not."

  • In ancient Near Eastern treaty and covenant contexts, lo' is the word used in absolute prohibitions (such as the Ten Commandments) to draw an unyielding moral and legal boundary line.

  • When used in prophetic judgments, lo' acts as an absolute barrier to any future reversal of the decreed doom. It signals a definitive end, shutting the door on any possibility of recovery, restoration, or survival.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The word lo' is an adverb of negation meaning "no," "not," "neither," or "nothing."

  • When paired with a verb, it denies the action, existence, or state completely.

  • When syntactically linked with a noun or adjective expressing quantity or existence, it acts as an absolute exclusive particle (e.g., "not one," "none," "no one").

  • In the specific construction of Obadiah 1:18—wə-hayah sarid lo' yihyeh (literally, "and a survivor there shall not be")—the negation is placed emphatically to stress the total absence of any remaining remnant for the house of Esau. It is an absolute, unconditional negative declaration.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The particle lo' is used to establish permanent realities. For example, it is the particle of absolute prohibition in the Decalogue, setting up unalterable boundaries for covenant life.

  • In poetic and prophetic literature, lo' is often juxtaposed with imagery of devouring fire or barren deserts to paint a picture of utter cessation.

  • In Obadiah 1:18, the word picture is a raging fire (Jacob/Joseph) completely consuming dry stubble (Esau). The resulting ash heap contains nothing left to salvage. The particle lo' stands as the final, absolute legal verdict over the ashes: there is nothing remaining.

4. Theological Implications

  • The use of lo' in divine decrees emphasizes the unchangeable nature of God's sovereign judgment. When God says lo', human pride, wealth, and natural strongholds cannot alter the outcome.

  • This absolute negation highlights the contrast between God's treatment of Israel and Edom. While Israel faced severe judgment and exile, God promised they would not be completely destroyed; a remnant would always remain. For Edom, however, the judgment is absolute—no survivor shall remain.

  • This demonstrates that God's covenant loyalty (hesed) preserves His people, while persistent, unrepentant hostility toward God's people leads to absolute eradication.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Obadiah 1:18, the context is the eschatological triumph of the house of Jacob and Joseph over their historic oppressor, Edom (the house of Esau).

  • Edom had gloated over the destruction of Jerusalem, stood on the crossroads to cut off Jewish fugitives, and delivered survivors to their enemies.

  • In poetic justice, God declares that Edom will face the exact fate they sought to inflict on others. Because they hunted down Israel's survivors, they themselves will have no (lo') survivor remaining. The negation directly answers their historical cruelty, demonstrating that the measure they meted out is measured back to them in full.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 20:13: "You shall not murder." (Translates lo' as an absolute moral prohibition).

  • Joshua 1:5: "No man shall be able to stand before you all the days of your life; as I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will not leave you nor forsake you." (Translates lo' to express God's absolute, unwavering faithfulness).

  • Psalm 23:1: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want." (Translates lo' to show the complete absence of lack under the Good Shepherd's care).

  • Isaiah 42:3: "A bruised reed He will not break, and smoking flax He will not quench; He will bring forth justice for truth." (Translates lo' twice to signify the absolute gentleness and certainty of the Messiah's ministry).

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The structural contrast in Hebrew prophecy between partial judgments (often qualified by "yet I will not utterly destroy") and the absolute judgment of lo' on Edom serves as a solemn warning against pride.

  • Edom trusted in its mountainous terrain, wise men, and mighty warriors, believing they were secure from destruction. The absolute negation of lo' strips away every false security, reminding the reader that human strength is nothing against the divine word.
