DEVOUR - ( akal , Strong's H0398 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb akal primarily means to eat, consume, or devour. While most frequently used in the Old Testament to describe the literal act of consuming food to sustain life, it also serves as a potent metaphor for total, irreversible destruction. In prophetic contexts, it frequently describes the work of war, sword, or fire. In Obadiah 1:18, akal depicts the absolute and dramatic consumption of the house of Esau by the house of Jacob, acting as God's instrument of judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root meaning of akal lies in the basic human necessity of eating. It is a highly active, sensory verb in Hebrew culture, representing the taking in of nourishment, chewing, and digesting.

• In an agricultural and nomadic society, the action of eating was observed not only in humans but also in grazing animals clearing a pasture, or wild beasts tearing and consuming their prey.

• When transferred to non-living elements, the word creates vivid personification. Fire is described as having a mouth that "eats" wood and stubble, and the sword is personified as a ravenous devourer that drinks the blood and "eats" the flesh of the slain.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Akal refers to the literal consumption of food by humans or animals.

• In its figurative sense, the word denotes the act of wasting, ruining, destroying, or burning up.

• In the passive stems (Niphal and Pual), it means to be consumed, eaten, or devoured by fire or sword.

• In the causative stem (Hiphil), it means to feed, cause to eat, or hand over to be consumed.

• In the context of divine judgment, the verb signifies complete annihilation, where the subject is reduced to nothing, much like food is completely broken down and ceases to exist in its original form.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Consuming Fire: Fire is the most frequent agent of figurative devouring in the Old Testament. When fire "eats" an object, the destruction is visible, rapid, and complete.

• The Devouring Sword: Warfare is regularly described as the sword "eating" people, highlighting the violent, insatiable nature of military conflict.

• Devouring Locusts: In judgment contexts, locusts or pestilence "eat" the harvest, stripping the land bare and leaving the population in famine.

• Devouring the Poor: Oppressors are described as "eating" or devouring the needy, symbolizing social injustice and exploitation where the powerful exhaust the resources of the weak.

4. Theological Implications

• Total Judgment: The use of akal in judgments underscores the finality of God's wrath against sin and pride. When God decrees that a nation will be devoured, it signals that their power, status, and heritage will be utterly dissolved.

• Lex Talionis (Law of Retribution): Often, those who devoured others are themselves devoured. Edom actively participated in the stripping and devouring of Jerusalem during her calamity. Consequently, God's righteous judgment reverses the roles.

• Divine Instrumental Agency: God frequently uses earthly agents (nations, natural disasters, or fire) to accomplish His akal judgment, showing His sovereign orchestration of history.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:18, akal is set within a striking three-part metaphorical sequence: Jacob is a fire, Joseph is a flame, and Esau is stubble.

• Stubble (the dry, worthless stalks left in the field after harvest) has absolutely no resistance to fire.

• The pairing of fire and stubble with the verb akal emphasizes the speed, ease, and totality of Edom's destruction. Just as a dry field of stubble is instantly consumed by a raging flame without leaving a trace, the house of Esau will be completely destroyed by the remnant of Israel, leaving "no survivor."

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:6 - "So when the woman saw that the tree was good for food, that it was pleasant to the eyes, and a tree desirable to make one wise, she took of its fruit and ate. She also gave to her husband with her, and he ate."

• Exodus 24:17 - "The sight of the glory of the Lord was like a consuming fire on the top of the mountain in the eyes of the children of Israel."

• Deuteronomy 4:24 - "For the Lord your God is a consuming fire, a jealous God."

• Joel 1:19 - "O Lord, to You I cry out; For fire has devoured the open pastures, And a flame has burned all the trees of the field."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The lack of "survivors" (Obadiah 1:18) confirms that akal here is absolute. Unlike other prophetic judgments where a remnant is spared, Edom’s consumption is total because of their deep-seated pride and violence against their brother Jacob.

• Ancient Near Eastern treaty curses frequently invoked the "devouring sword" or "devouring pests" as consequences for breaking covenants. God’s use of this language aligns with the ancient understanding of severe, covenantal judgment.
