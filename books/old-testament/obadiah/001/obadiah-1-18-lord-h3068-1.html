LORD - (YHWH, Strong's H3068)

Summary:

Lexically, yhwh is the proper personal name of the God of Israel. Distinct from general titles such as elohim ("God") or adonai ("Lord" or "Master"), this proper noun identifies the personal God who revealed Himself to Israel and entered into covenant relationship with them. In Obadiah 1:18, yhwh appears as the subject of the divine speech clause ("for the LORD has spoken"). The name contributes the ultimate personal and divine authority behind the prophetic declaration, guaranteeing that the impending judgment upon Edom and the restoration of the house of Jacob will certainly come to pass because the God of Israel Himself has decreed it.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The proper noun yhwh is the specific personal name of the God of Israel. In the Old Testament text, it is distinct from generic terms for deity, such as elohim, or titles of sovereign authority, such as adonai. While generic titles emphasize divine power or rule, yhwh functions as the distinct personal name by which God established His covenant identity with the patriarchs and the nation of Israel.

In Jewish scribal tradition, out of reverence for the sacred name, the written four-letter name (the Tetragrammaton) was read aloud as adonai ("Lord"), which led to the traditional English translation "LORD" printed in capital letters. In the historical and prophetic setting of the Old Testament, the name designates the living God who acts in history, speaks through His prophets, and fulfills His sovereign purposes among the nations.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Standard Old Testament lexicons (such as Brown-Driver-Briggs and the Theological Wordbook of the Old Testament) classify yhwh as a proper noun designating the God of Israel. Grammatically, the target occurrence in Obadiah 1:18 is a masculine singular proper noun in the absolute state.

Lexically, yhwh is a personal name rather than an abstract concept or general title. While the name is historically linked in the biblical narrative with the verbal root hayah or hawah ("to be"), the name itself functions fundamentally as a personal proper noun identifying the divine subject. It designates the specific God who speaks, covenants, judges, and redeems, rather than serving as a compressed dictionary definition of divine attributes.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, yhwh is used when the text highlights God's personal identity, His covenant relationship with Israel, or His direct interaction with human history.

In prophetic literature, the name yhwh appears constantly in formal divine speech signatures, such as kōh 'āmar yhwh ("thus says the LORD") or kî yhwh dibbēr ("for the LORD has spoken"). When prophets use these formulas, the name yhwh serves to distinguish the true God of Israel from false deities and to identify the speaker of the prophecy as the sovereign Ruler of creation.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of yhwh is drawn from the broader canonical narrative in which God reveals His character through His actions and words. As the proper personal name of God, yhwh identifies the One who is faithful to His covenant promises and righteous in His judicial decisions.

In canonical context, when a prophet declares that yhwh has spoken, the authority of the message rests entirely on God's personal truthfulness and power. Because God is holy and supreme over all nations, His spoken word carries absolute weight. The name yhwh reminds God's people that their security lies not in political alliances or human strength, but in the character and word of their covenant God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:18, the prophet concludes a dramatic oracle of judgment and restoration with the clause, "for the LORD has spoken" (kî yhwh dibbēr). The immediate context describes a complete reversal of fortunes between the house of Jacob and the house of Esau (Edom). Edom had acted arrogantly, gloating over Jerusalem's downfall and participating in the violence against Judah. Obadiah announces that the house of Jacob will be a fire and Esau stubble, consumed without survivor.

Grammatically, yhwh functions as the masculine singular subject performing the action of speaking. The immediate syntax places yhwh as the direct authority behind the sentence. By appending "for the LORD has spoken" to verse 18, the text establishes that Edom's coming destruction and Israel's vindication are not speculative human calculations or military predictions. The decree carries the weight of yhwh's personal word, making its execution absolute and unalterable.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 19:22 — "The priest shall make atonement for him with the ram of the trespass offering before the Lord for his sin which he has committed. And the sin which he has committed shall be forgiven him."

In this sacrificial context, the name designates the holy God in whose presence atonement is made and who bestows forgiveness.

  • Psalms 33:8 — "Let all the earth fear the Lord; Let all the inhabitants of the world stand in awe of Him."

Here, the name identifies the God who is worthy of universal awe and reverence from every nation and creature.

  • Isaiah 37:14 — "And Hezekiah received the letter from the hand of the messengers, and read it; and Hezekiah went up to the house of the Lord, and spread it before the Lord."

In this historical narrative, the name identifies the living God to whom King Hezekiah turns in prayer when threatened by Assyria.

  • Ezekiel 25:5 — "And I will make Rabbah a stable for camels and Ammon a resting place for flocks. Then you shall know that I am the Lord."

In this judgment oracle against neighboring nations, the name appears within the recognition formula, showing that God's acts of judgment reveal His identity.

  • Haggai 2:14 — "Then Haggai answered and said, '“So is this people, and so is this nation before Me,” says the Lord, “and so is every work of their hands; and what they offer there is unclean.”'"

Here, the divine name introduces God's authoritative assessment of the post-exilic community's moral and spiritual condition.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In prophetic discourse, the clause kî yhwh dibbēr ("for the LORD has spoken") functions as an authoritative seal on an oracle. In ancient Near Eastern international disputes, nations frequently relied on regional deities to guarantee their borders and political longevity. Edom trusted in its mountainous strongholds and worldly wisdom. By sealing the prophecy with "for the LORD has spoken," Obadiah emphasizes that no human fortification or earthly wisdom can withstand the declared purpose of the God of Israel.
