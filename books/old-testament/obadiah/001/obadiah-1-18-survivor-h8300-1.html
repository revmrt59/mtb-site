SURVIVOR - (sârîyd, Strong's H8300)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun sarid denotes a survivor, a fugitive, or an escaped remnant who remains after a catastrophic event, military invasion, or severe divine judgment. Lexically, it signifies the individual or group that outlives a slaughter or disaster. In Obadiah 1:18, sarid is used in a absolute negative statement regarding Edom: "there shall be no survivor of the house of Esau." Within the argument of Obadiah, the term establishes the finality and totality of God's judgment on Edom. While God graciously preserves a sarid for His covenant people despite their chastisement, Edom’s pride and treacherous violence against Judah result in total obliteration, leaving no remaining legacy or descendants.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The noun sarid originates in the historical and military realities of the ancient Near East, where warfare often resulted in the systematic annihilation of conquered populations. In such contexts, a sarid was an individual who managed to flee the battlefield or escape a besieged city, thereby preserving the seed, family name, or line of a people group.

In the prophetic literature, particularly during the exilic and post-exilic periods, sarid took on significant theological weight. The historical backdrop of Obadiah centers on the destruction of Jerusalem by Babylon, during which the nation of Edom—descendants of Esau—actively participated in or celebrated the calamity that befell their brother nation, Jacob. The word appears against this backdrop of violent conflict, carrying both military and covenantal weight.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, sarid refers to a "survivor," an "escaped one," or a "remnant." It is a substantive noun describing an entity that outlasts a widespread destruction, plague, or military execution.

Unlike general words for a remaining portion, sarid almost exclusively implies survival from a life-threatening crisis or deliberate effort at extermination. Lexicons such as Brown-Driver-Briggs define it as "one left alive, survivor, fugitive." The primary emphasis of the word is not merely being present, but having escaped an event that claimed the lives of others.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, sarid is employed across three primary genres:

  • Conquest and Military Narratives: In the historical books (such as Deuteronomy and Joshua), the word frequently appears in reports of Israel's conquest of Canaan. Capturing a city utterly often meant leaving no sarid—no survivor remaining to perpetuate the population or its idolatrous practices.

  • Wisdom Literature: In books like Job, sarid refers to what remains of a wicked man's wealth, household, or offspring after divine retribution strikes his house.

  • Prophetic Decrees: In the prophets, the term functions in a dual manner. For pagan nations or enemies of God, the total absence of a sarid signifies irreversible destruction. For Israel, even under severe judgment, God promises to preserve a sarid by His grace, demonstrating His covenantal loyalty.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of sarid centers on the divine prerogative over judgment and mercy. Human military power cannot secure a sarid if God decrees complete devastation; conversely, human wrath cannot eliminate a sarid if God promises deliverance.

The term highlights a profound canonical contrast between Israel and the hostile nations. Although Israel faced devastating judgments due to her covenant unfaithfulness, God in His mercy always maintained a surviving remnant (sarid). By contrast, nations characterized by unrepentant pride and hostility toward God's kingdom—exemplified by Edom—face an unmitigated judgment where no sarid is spared. God's holiness demands the utter removal of evil, while His grace preserves a people for Himself.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:18, the prophet depicts a dramatic reversal through vivid imagery: "The house of Jacob shall be a fire, and the house of Joseph a flame; the house of Esau shall be stubble; they shall kindle them and devour them, and no survivor shall remain of the house of Esau."

The noun sarid appears here in the masculine, singular, absolute state, modified by a negative particle to express absolute exclusion. Grammatically, it signifies that not even a single individual will remain from the lineage of Esau.

This statement directly answers the sin of Edom exposed earlier in the prophecy. In Obadiah 1:14, the same Hebrew word sarid is used to describe Edom's crime: when Jerusalem fell, Edom stood at the crossroads to capture and deliver up Judah's "survivors" (sarid) to the enemy. Edom sought to eradicate Judah's survivors; therefore, in God's righteous retribution (the principle of lex talionis), Edom itself will be left with no survivor (sarid). The precise lexical pairing between verse 14 and verse 18 underscores that divine judgment matches the exact nature of the offense.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Obadiah 1:14 — "You should not have stood at the crossroads to cut off those of his who escaped [survived]; nor should you have delivered up those of his who remained in the day of distress."

  • Joel 2:32 — "For in Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there shall be deliverance, as the LORD has said, among the remnant [survivors] whom the LORD calls."

  • Deuteronomy 3:3 — "So the LORD our God also delivered into our hands Og king of Bashan and all his people, and we defeated him until he had no survivor left."

  • Isaiah 1:9 — "Unless the LORD of hosts had left us a very small remnant [survivor], we would have become like Sodom, we would have been made like Gomorrah."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The poetic structure of Obadiah 1:18 emphasizes complete destruction through the metaphor of fire and stubble. Jacob and Joseph (representing the unified people of God) act as the instrument of fire, while Esau is reduced to passive, highly combustible material. The total absence of a sarid confirms that Edom's destruction is not merely a temporary political defeat, but a permanent historical termination.

Historically, this prophecy was fulfilled as Edom was progressively subjugated, forced into territorial displacement, eventually incorporated into Judea, and finally erased from history following the Roman destruction of Jerusalem. While the line of Jacob endured to bring forth the Messiah, the house of Esau left no sarid, illustrating the absolute reliability of divine prophecy.
