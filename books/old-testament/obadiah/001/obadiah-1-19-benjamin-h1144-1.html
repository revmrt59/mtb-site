BENJAMIN - (Binyâmîyn, Strong's H1144)

Summary:

The proper noun binyamin originates from Hebrew terms denoting "son of the right hand" or "son of the south," serving in Scripture as the name of Jacob's youngest son, the tribe descended from him, and their designated territorial inheritance. In Obadiah 1:19, binyamin identifies the tribal remnant of Benjamin in the context of Israel's end-time territorial restoration. The prophet highlights Benjamin as taking possession of Gilead across the Jordan River. This specific designation underscores God's complete reversal of Israel's exile and dispossession, portraying a restored, unified covenant people expanding into fertile territory under the sovereign reign of the LORD.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name binyamin is a compound proper noun derived from the Hebrew noun for "son" (ben) and the term for "right hand" or "south" (yamin). In ancient Semitic idiom, geographic directions were oriented facing east; thus, the right hand signified the south. The name carries connotations of honor, favor, and strength, reflecting Jacob's choice to name his youngest son (originally called Ben-Oni, "son of my sorrow," by his dying mother Rachel) with a name of blessing.

Historically, the tribe of Benjamin occupied a narrow yet highly strategic strip of land between the northern territory of Ephraim and the southern territory of Judah. Jerusalem itself lay on the border of Benjamin's allotment. Despite being one of the smaller tribes in Israel's history—at times reduced near to extinction as recorded in the book of Judges—Benjamin maintained a pivotal place in biblical history, producing Israel's first king, Saul, and remaining allied to the Davidic house alongside Judah following the division of the kingdom. In prophetic visions of restoration, such as Obadiah's, the tribal name represents an essential component of God's renewed covenant community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, binyamin functions as a proper noun in the Hebrew Bible, designating:

  • The patriarchal individual, Benjamin, twelfth son of Jacob.

  • The Israelite tribe descended from him.

  • The geographic region assigned to and inhabited by the tribe of Benjamin.

In the specific grammatical context of Obadiah 1:19, the term appears as a singular proper noun in the absolute state, identifying the restored tribal entity of Benjamin as an active recipient and owner of covenant land.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Hebrew Scriptures, binyamin appears across narrative, genealogical, historical, and prophetic genres:

  • Patriarchal Narrative: In Genesis, Benjamin is depicted as Jacob's cherished youngest son, central to the emotional resolution of Joseph's testing of his brothers in Egypt.

  • Tribal Allotment and History: In Joshua and Judges, the term designates both the boundaries of the tribe's land and the military forces of Benjamin.

  • Monarchic and Post-Exilic Registers: In Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, Ezra, and Nehemiah, Benjamin is frequently paired with Judah as the enduring southern remnant that preserved the sanctuary and city of Jerusalem.

  • Prophetic Restoration: In prophetic texts, Benjamin represents the faithful remnant re-inheriting promised territory after judgment and exile.

4. Theological Implications

The inclusion of Benjamin in Obadiah's vision of final restoration carries significant theological weight. First, it demonstrates the thoroughness of divine covenantal faithfulness. God does not merely restore a generic nation; He preserves and reestablishes specific tribes according to His ancient promises. Second, Benjamin's expansion into Gilead illustrates the theme of divine reversal. A tribe once small, geographically constrained, and historically vulnerable is granted rich, sprawling territory east of the Jordan. Finally, the re-establishment of Benjamin alongside Judah and the northern territories (Ephraim and Samaria) reflects the ultimate reunification of all God's people under the divine kingdom.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:19 forms part of the prophet's closing vision of victory, where the judgment of Edom gives way to the full restoration and enlargement of God's remnant. The verse presents a precise geography of inheritance: those in the South (Negev) possess Esau's mountains; those in the Lowland (Shephelah) possess Philistia; Israel possesses the fields of Ephraim and Samaria; and "Benjamin shall possess Gilead."

The specific pairing of Benjamin with Gilead is contextually striking. Gilead was historically a fertile pastureland situated east of the Jordan River, originally allotted to Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh. By stating that Benjamin—whose historical territory lay west of the Jordan—will possess Gilead, Obadiah communicates an expansive, supernatural growth for the remnant. The immediate sentence structure highlights Benjamin as a direct subject acting under divine authorization to reclaim and occupy land previously lost or beyond its traditional borders.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between the destiny of Edom and that of Benjamin in Obadiah highlights the grand theme of divine justice. Edom, who violently seized opportunistically upon Jerusalem's downfall and dispossessed God's people, is slated for total dispossession and destruction. Conversely, Benjamin, though small and afflicted during Jerusalem's fall, is exalted and awarded the rich, fruitful heights of Gilead. This structural contrast underlines that worldly aggression yields total loss, whereas patient endurance under God's covenant secures an enduring and expanded inheritance.
