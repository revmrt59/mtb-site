FIELDS - (sâdeh, Strong's H7704)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun sadeh refers to open country, fields, arable agricultural land, or uncultivated rural territory, as distinct from walled cities or urban centers. In Obadiah 1:19, sadeh identifies the fertile rural lands and agricultural territories of Ephraim and Samaria. Within Obadiah's prophecy of restoration, the term emphasizes that God's redeemed people will repossess not only prominent strongholds, but also the entire fruitful, crop-bearing countryside of the northern region, demonstrating the comprehensive nature of Israel's promised territorial inheritance.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Israelite culture and geography, human life was categorized largely around two primary physical environments: the city (‘ir), which offered fortification and civic administration, and the open field (sadeh), which provided pasture for livestock and arable soil for agricultural production. The sadeh was essential to daily survival, serving as the locus of planting, harvesting, grazing, and hunting.

Because ancient Near Eastern life depended upon land productivity, the sadeh carried immense social, economic, and spiritual significance. Land was distributed by tribe and clan as a divine allotment, making every sadeh a visible sign of covenant stewardship and familial heritage. In prophetic literature, the condition of the open fields often reflected the spiritual state of the nation: fruitful fields represented divine covenant blessing, while devastated or uncultivated fields signaled covenant curses and national judgment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, sadeh denotes an open space, rural expanse, or defined plot of outdoor ground. Standard Hebrew lexicons outline its semantic range across three related nuances:

  • Open, uncultivated countryside or wilderness where wild animals live and roam freely.

  • Cultivated agricultural ground, such as grain fields, vineyards, or pastures designated for farming and herding.

  • Regional territory or countryside associated with a specific group, city, or geographic region.

Unlike terms that specifically denote a vineyard or a garden, sadeh is the broad, foundational Hebrew word for the open, non-urban landscape. It encompasses both wild terrain and managed farmland.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament historical, legal, and poetic texts, sadeh functions in several distinct contexts:

  • Agrarian and Legal Contexts: Israelite law carefully protected the boundaries of the sadeh. Laws governing gleaning required landowners to leave the edges of their sadeh for the poor and the stranger (Leviticus 19:9). Real estate redemption laws governed how ancestral fields could be bought, sold, or returned in the Year of Jubilee (Leviticus 27:21; Ruth 4:3).

  • Creation and Nature: Poetic literature uses sadeh when describing the broad natural world, such as the "beasts of the field" or the "trees of the field" that depend on divine providence (Psalms 8:7; Ezekiel 31:6).

  • Prophetic and Judgment Nuances: In judgment passages, God warns that covenant rebellion will cause the sadeh to lie fallow or be consumed by enemies (Jeremiah 7:20; Jeremiah 13:27). Conversely, in restoration passages, the reclaiming and cultivation of the sadeh symbolizes the renewal of divine favor and national peace.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, sadeh connects directly to the doctrine of creation and the theology of the land:

  • Divine Ownership: The sadeh ultimately belongs to Yahweh. Israel held the land as stewards under God's sovereign tenure. Divine blessing upon the fields—manifested through seasonal rains and crop abundance—was contingent upon covenant loyalty (Deuteronomy 11:15).

  • Reversal of the Curse: Earthly barrenness entered creation as a result of human sin. The restoration of productive fields throughout prophetic vision serves as an earthly picture of cosmic redemption, where God lifts desolation and restores productivity to the land.

  • Comprehensive Covenant Restoration: God's promises of salvation do not merely concern internal spiritual renewal; they encompass the physical renewal of space, community, and economic vitality. Repossessing the sadeh signifies the total recovery of what was lost through judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:19 forms part of the prophet's concluding vision concerning the Day of the Lord, the divine judgment upon Edom, and the ultimate restoration of the house of Jacob. The verse outlines a systematic reoccupation of promised territory by the returning remnant of Israel.

Grammatically, the target occurrence of sadeh in Obadiah 1:19 is masculine singular in the construct state. It functions in direct construct relationship with the place names Ephraim and Samaria ("the fields of Ephraim" and "the fields of Samaria").

This construction carries significant contextual weight:

  • Beyond Urban Control: By specifying the fields (sadeh) of Ephraim and Samaria rather than merely their capital cities, Obadiah emphasizes complete territorial control. Possessing the fields means controlling the agricultural engine, rural settlements, and full regional expanse of the former northern kingdom.

  • Reunification of God's People: Ephraim and Samaria represent the northern kingdom of Israel, which had been broken, conquered, and scattered by Assyria centuries earlier. The prophecy announces that the restored remnant will possess these very fields, pointing toward the ultimate reunification and healing of the divided tribes under Yahweh's reign.

  • Reversal of Dispossession: Whereas foreign invaders had previously overrun Israel's countryside, God promises a full reversal wherein His people re-inherit the productive lands that were lost.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Ruth 2:2 — "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.' And she said to her, 'Go, my daughter.'"

  • Here sadeh describes the open agricultural field where grain was harvested and where divine providence provided for the needy through the practice of gleaning.

  • Ruth 4:3 — "Then he said to the close relative, 'Naomi, who has come back from the country of Moab, sells the piece of land [field] which belonged to our brother Elimelech.'"

  • In this legal context, sadeh refers to an inherited family landholding requiring redemption to preserve the family name and heritage in Israel.

  • Deuteronomy 11:15 — "And I will send grass in your fields for your livestock, that you may eat and be filled."

  • This passage uses sadeh to denote rural pastureland where God sovereignly grants vegetation as a covenant blessing for His people.

  • 1 Samuel 8:14 — "And he will take the best of your fields, your vineyards, and your olive groves, and give them to his servants."

  • Here Samuel warns Israel that a human king would exercise monarchical power to confiscate their private agricultural lands (sadeh).

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Contrast with Urban Fortresses: In Hebrew thought, human pride often concentrated in fortified cities, whereas the sadeh represented human dependence upon God's environmental provision. Possessing the sadeh highlights security grounded in divine blessing rather than human walls.

  • Literary Balance in Obadiah: Obadiah 1:19 pairs mountainous regions ("mountains of Esau"), lowland plains ("Philistia"), and open agricultural country ("fields of Ephraim"). This structural balance underscores the absolute completeness of the territorial restoration—no topographic zone is left unrestored.
