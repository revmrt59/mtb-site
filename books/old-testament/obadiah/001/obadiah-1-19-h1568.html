GILEAD - ( gil'ad , Strong's H1568 )

Summary:

Gilead is a rugged, fertile mountainous region east of the Jordan River, famous in the Old Testament for its rich pastures, therapeutic balm, and historical significance as a place of refuge and covenant. In the prophetic vision of Obadiah 1:19, the promise that "Benjamin shall possess Gilead" represents the sweeping restoration of God's covenant people. The small tribe of Benjamin, originally situated west of the Jordan, will expand across the river to claim this vast, bountiful territory, symbolizing the complete reversal of exile, the fulfillment of divine inheritance, and the abundant provision of God for His redeemed flock.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Gilead lies east of the Jordan River, bounded by the Yarmuk River to the north and the plains of Moab to the south. It is characterized by high ridges, deep ravines, and abundant rainfall, making it one of the most fertile areas in the ancient Near East.

• The name is closely linked to the Hebrew term for a "witness heap" (galeed). This references the covenant monument of stones piled by Jacob and Laban in Genesis 31:47–48, establishing Gilead as a geographical boundary of peace, safety, and divine witness.

• Historically, Gilead was coveted for its rich soil, prime livestock grazing lands, and dense forests of oak and pine. It was also the source of the famous "balm of Gilead," a prized medicinal ointment exported throughout the ancient world for its healing properties.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The proper noun gil'ad designates a "rugged country" or "rocky region." Lexically, it functions in three ways across the Old Testament:

• The geographical region east of the Jordan River allotted to the tribes of Reuben, Gad, and the half-tribe of Manasseh.

• The mountain range itself (Mount Gilead) that divides the high plateau of Transjordan.

• A personal name representing several biblical figures, most notably a grandson of Manasseh who became the patriarch of the clan of Gileadites, and the father of Jephthah the judge.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• A Place of Refuge: Due to its rugged terrain, Gilead frequently served as a sanctuary for those fleeing danger. Jacob sought refuge here when running from Laban; David fled to Mahanaim in Gilead during the rebellion of his son Absalom.

• A Symbol of Abundance: The lush pastures of Gilead became a poetic picture of beauty and prosperity. In the Song of Solomon, the bride's hair is compared to "a flock of goats going down from Mount Gilead," evoking images of rich, undulating abundance.

• A Picture of Healing: The term "balm of Gilead" became a metaphor for spiritual and emotional recovery. Jeremiah used this imagery to lament the spiritual sickness of Judah, asking why no healing had been applied when the spiritual physician and balm were readily available.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Faithfulness and Restoration: During the Assyrian conquest, Tiglath-Pileser III overran Gilead and carried its inhabitants into exile (2 Kings 15:29). The prophetic reclamation of Gilead represents the theological reality that God does not abandon His boundaries or His people. What the enemy plunders, God intends to restore.

• The Expansion of Grace: The allocation of Gilead to Benjamin in the restoration era demonstrates the expansive nature of God’s grace. Benjamin was the smallest of the tribes, yet under God’s sovereign restoration, they are given a vast, fertile territory far beyond their original borders.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:19, the prophet declares, "Benjamin shall possess Gilead." This occurs within a larger passage detailing the geopolitical reversal of fortunes. Edom, who gloated over Judah's downfall, will be completely destroyed, while the displaced exiles of Israel will return to occupy and expand their territories.

• Historically, the tribe of Benjamin was located west of the Jordan River in a small, landlocked territory. The prophecy that Benjamin will cross the Jordan to "possess Gilead" emphasizes that the post-exile restoration will not merely be a return to the status quo. It will be an era of supernatural expansion, where boundaries are enlarged, and even the smallest among God's people will inherit vast, rich territories.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Numbers 32:1: "Now the children of Reuben and the children of Gad had a very great multitude of livestock; and they saw the land of Jazer and the land of Gilead, that indeed the place was a place for livestock,"

• Deuteronomy 34:1: "Then Moses went up from the plains of Moab to Mount Nebo, to the top of Pisgah, which is opposite Jericho. And the LORD showed him all the land of Gilead as far as Dan,"

• Jeremiah 8:22: "Is there no balm in Gilead, Is there no physician there? Why then is there no recovery for the health of the daughter of my people?"

• Hosea 6:8: "Gilead is a city of evildoers And defiled with blood."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Gilead was the home of Elijah the Tishbite (1 Kings 17:1). The rugged, uncompromising frontier of Gilead shaped Elijah's wilderness lifestyle and his bold, direct prophetic ministry to the corrupt northern kingdom of Israel.

• The geographical barrier of the Jordan River often created a spiritual and political rift between the eastern tribes in Gilead and the western tribes in Canaan. The prophecy of Obadiah hints at a final, complete unification where the geographical barriers of the past are spiritually dissolved.
