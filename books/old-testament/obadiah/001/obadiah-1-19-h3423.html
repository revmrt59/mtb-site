POSSESS - ( yarash , Strong's H3423 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yarash carries a dynamic dual meaning of dispossessing an enemy and taking legal, physical possession of their land as an inheritance. In Obadiah 1:19, this word signals a dramatic divine reversal where God’s formerly exiled and oppressed people return to drive out their historic adversaries—specifically Edom and the Philistines—and inherit the territories God originally promised to them. It is not merely about legal ownership, but about the active fulfillment of God's covenant promises through the reclamation and occupation of the land.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The cultural and historical background of yarash is rooted in the ancient Near Eastern concept of land ownership, heritage, and warfare. In the ancient world, land was not merely real estate; it was a divine allotment tied to a nation's identity and their god's favor. For Israel, the land of Canaan was their nachalah (inheritance), but to enjoy it, they had to actively yarash (dispossess) the corrupt nations occupying it. The word picture is one of active displacement: one party must leave so that another can move in and take over the estate. It portrays a transition of power, authority, and residency, where the rightful heir steps in to claim what has been legally and divinely designated as theirs.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yarash primarily means to take possession of, inherit, or dispossess. Depending on the conjugation and context, it oscillates between two sides of the same coin:

• To inherit or occupy: It refers to receiving a heritage or property, stepping into the rights of an heir, and establishing residency.

• To dispossess or drive out: It refers to the aggressive displacement, expulsion, or destruction of the current inhabitants of a territory.

The term combines the legal right of inheritance with the physical, historical action of taking control of a territory. It is the active verb used when a nation conquers land to make it their permanent home.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Across the Old Testament, yarash is used over 200 times. It is a key theological term in the books of Deuteronomy and Joshua, where Israel is repeatedly commanded to go in and "possess" (yarash) the land that the Lord swore to their fathers.

• The Heir Apparent: In Genesis 15:3–4, Abraham laments that a servant in his house will "inherit" (yarash) his estate, demonstrating the legal aspect of the word where a son succeeds his father to take over the household.

• The Military Conquest: In the historical books, yarash frequently describes the military campaign of driving out the Canaanites. It shows that taking possession is rarely passive; it requires faith-filled action to step into what God has promised.

• The Vineyard of Naboth: In 1 Kings 21:18, Elijah tells Ahab to go down and "take possession" (yarash) of Naboth's vineyard. This illustrates a forced, physical seizure of property, though in Ahab's case, it was an unjust and wicked usurpation.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yarash is deeply connected to the Abrahamic Covenant and the sovereignty of God.

• Sovereignty Over Nations: God is the ultimate owner of the earth. He has the sovereign right to dispossess nations of their lands because of their wickedness and grant those lands to whom He pleases.

• Covenant Faithfulness: Israel's possession of Canaan was not due to their own righteousness or strength, but because of God's covenant faithfulness.

• The Condition of Obedience: If Israel failed to remain faithful, they themselves would be dispossessed (yarash) by foreign invaders. The land belongs to God, and possession of it requires alignment with His moral and spiritual demands.

• Prophetic Restoration: In prophetic literature, yarash points to a future day when God will gather His scattered people, purge their enemies, and permanently establish them in their inheritance. This final possession is an act of ultimate divine justice and restoration.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:19, the use of yarash is a powerful declaration of divine justice and poetic reversal. Throughout the prophecy of Obadiah, the Edomites (the descendants of Esau) are condemned for their pride and their violent exploitation of Judah's downfall. When Babylon destroyed Jerusalem, Edom stood by, rejoiced, and even helped capture Jewish fugitives, hoping to annex Judah’s territory for themselves.

In verse 19, God reverses this scenario completely using the word yarash three times:

1. "But those in the South shall possess the mountains of Esau"

2. "And those in the lowland [shall possess] the Philistines."

3. "They shall possess the fields of Ephraim..."

The nuance here is a complete territorial swap. Edom tried to greedily consume Judah's land, but instead, Judah’s remnant (the South/Negev) will return from exile and dispossess Edom of their own mountainous homeland. The weak and exiled survivors of Israel will become the conquerors, expanding their borders in every direction to reclaim the full boundaries of the Promised Land. God’s covenant promise overrides the geopolitical realities of exile, ensuring that His people will ultimately inherit what was promised to them.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 1:8: "See, I have set the land before you; go in and possess the land which the LORD swore to your fathers—to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—to give to them and their descendants after them."

• Numbers 33:53: "you shall dispossess the inhabitants of the land and dwell in it, for I have given you the land to possess it."

• Psalm 44:3: "For they did not gain possession of the land by their own sword, Nor did their own arm save them; But it was Your right hand, Your arm, and the light of Your countenance, Because You favored them."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A helpful point of contrast is the difference between yarash and nachal. While both are translated as "inherit" or "possess," nachal focuses heavily on the legal distribution, allocation, and inheritance of an estate as a permanent portion or destiny. Yarash, on the other hand, is far more active, kinetic, and experiential. It emphasizes the actual taking, driving out, and settling in. It reminds the reader that while God's promises are secure (nachal), experiencing those promises often requires active, obedient steps of faith to conquer obstacles and claim the territory (yarash).
