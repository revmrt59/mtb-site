FIELDS - ( sadeh , Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to an open field, cultivated land, or pasture territory. In the agricultural context of ancient Israel, the sadeh was the primary source of life, sustenance, and livelihood, representing both the blessing of divine provision and the vulnerability of undefended land. In Obadiah 1:19, the restoration of the "fields of Ephraim" and "fields of Samaria" to the returning exiles of Israel signifies a complete reversal of judgment and the full restoration of covenant inheritance, where God's people repossess and securely enjoy the productive, fertile territories once stripped away by their enemies.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, life was intimately bound to the land. The sadeh—the open field—was the canvas of daily survival, labor, and divine interaction. Unlike the walled cities that offered human-engineered defense and security, the open fields were expansive, flat, or rolling lands where crops were cultivated, vineyards were planted, and flocks were grazed. The sadeh was the place where a family's livelihood was forged through the sweat of the brow, completely subject to the seasonal rains and the sovereign hand of God. To have one's fields flourishing was a sign of divine favor and blessing; to have them ravaged by drought, pests, or invading armies was the ultimate picture of judgment and destitution. It was also a place of exposure, lacking walls to protect against wild beasts or marauding enemies, making it a powerful symbol of vulnerability and absolute dependence on God's protection.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew noun sadeh designates an open field, a tract of land, cultivated ground, pastureland, or a specific region. The term describes several related concepts:

• Cultivated agricultural land used for growing grain, sowing seeds, and harvesting food.

• Open, uncultivated country where wild animals live and herds graze.

• The specific territory, domain, or regional borders belonging to a nation or tribe (such as the "field of Moab" or the "field of Edom").

• Legally owned property or hereditary land holdings passed down through generations.

The word derives from a root that signifies to spread out, depicting flat, open, or rolling plains that are suitable for farming and grazing.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, sadeh is used to construct vivid theological and practical word pictures:

• The Blessed Field: A field that the Lord has blessed is compared to the fragrant and abundant life of a righteous family (Genesis 27:27).

• The Neglected Field: The field of the lazy person, overgrown with thorns and nettles, serves as a moral and spiritual warning about the consequences of spiritual apathy and physical sloth (Proverbs 24:30-31).

• The Exposed Field: Because it lacks defensive walls, the field represents a place of danger where violence can happen out of sight, as seen when Cain killed Abel in the field (Genesis 4:8).

• The Beasts of the Field: Wild animals are consistently called "beasts of the field," highlighting their untamed nature and their direct dependence on God rather than human cultivation for survival (Psalm 104:11).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, sadeh is deeply intertwined with the concepts of covenant, inheritance, and redemption:

• Divine Ownership: The ultimate ownership of all fields belongs to Yahweh. The land is His, and Israel holds it as a stewardship and covenantal inheritance.

• Covenantal Obedience: The fruitfulness of the fields is directly linked to Israel's covenant faithfulness. Obedience brings rain in its season and abundance in the field, while disobedience turns the fertile field into a barren wasteland.

• Redemption and Reclamation: In Israelite law, if a man fell into poverty and had to sell his family field, the kinsman-redeemer had the right to buy it back, keeping the inheritance within the family. This legal practice serves as a picture of God's ultimate redemption of His people and their lost inheritance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:19, the plural form "fields" (sadeh) is used to describe the territories of Ephraim and Samaria. Obadiah's prophecy is a message of comfort to a defeated, exiled, and humiliated Israel. The Edomites had rejoiced over Jerusalem's downfall and actively participated in its plunder. However, God promises a dramatic reversal of fortunes.

While Edom's mountainous stronghold will be possessed and laid bare, the returning remnant of Israel will expand and possess the fertile "fields of Ephraim" and the "fields of Samaria." These regions, which had been overrun and occupied by Assyrian and Babylonian conquests, will be restored to their rightful covenant heirs. The use of "fields" emphasizes not just the recovery of political boundaries, but the restoration of agricultural prosperity, economic stability, and territorial freedom. To possess these fields means that the days of lack, exile, and fear are over, replaced by the secure enjoyment of the covenant blessing.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A striking spiritual contrast exists between the walled city (ir) and the open field (sadeh). Walled cities in the ancient world symbolized human strength, self-defense, and centralized power. In contrast, the open fields were places where humans stood exposed, completely vulnerable to the elements, drought, pestilence, and invading forces. To "possess the fields" in peace, without the protection of fortified city walls, requires absolute trust in Yahweh as the ultimate Shield and Protector. It speaks of a time of such profound peace and divine security that God's people can live and work in the open, unfortified countryside, fully enjoying the fruit of their labor without fear of plunder.
