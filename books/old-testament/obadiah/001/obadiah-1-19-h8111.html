SAMARIA - ( Shomeron , Strong's H8111 )

Summary:

Samaria, historically the capital of the Northern Kingdom of Israel, represents both a physical territory and a tragic history of idolatry, rebellion, and subsequent exile. In Obadiah 1:19, Samaria is mentioned as part of the future restoration and expansion of God's covenant people, who will repossess the land formerly occupied by the northern tribes. It stands as a powerful symbol of divine restoration, demonstrating that what was lost through sin and judgment will ultimately be reclaimed and healed under God's sovereign rule.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The name Shomeron is derived from the Hebrew root verb shamar, which means "to watch," "to guard," "to keep," or "to preserve."

  • Historically, King Omri of Israel purchased a strategic hill from a man named Shemer for two talents of silver. Omri built a fortified city on this hill and named it Samaria (Shomeron) after its original owner, Shemer (1 Kings 16:24).

  • The name carries the word picture of a "watchtower," "guard post," or "mountain of watching."

  • Because of its high elevation, Samaria commanded excellent military views and controlled key trade routes, symbolizing human strength, security, and prideful self-reliance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Geographically, Samaria refers to the fortified hill and capital city of the Northern Kingdom of Israel.

  • Administratively, it refers to the entire mountainous district and region of central Palestine surrounding the capital.

  • Politically and spiritually, the name functions as a synecdoche representing the entire Northern Kingdom (often referred to as Ephraim or the ten tribes) in its state of apostasy and separation from Judah.

  • Lexically, the noun designates a place of watching or a fortified stronghold that ultimately became synonymous with Israel's rebellion against the Davidic covenant and the temple in Jerusalem.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Watchtower of Self-Sufficiency: Samaria's lofty, fortified position led to a false sense of security. The prophets frequently exposed this arrogance, warning that its physical watchtowers could not save it from divine judgment.

  • The Center of Syncretism and Apostasy: Rather than watching over the law of God, Samaria became the center for Baal worship under Ahab and Jezebel, and later for the corrupt worship of Jeroboam’s golden calves.

  • The Deserted Stronghold: Following its fall to the Assyrians in 722 B.C., Samaria became a haunting picture of desolation, exile, and the scattering of God's people among the nations.

4. Theological Implications

  • The prophetic inclusion of Samaria in restoration passages carries profound covenantal implications. Although Samaria was the birthplace of deep systemic apostasy and was the first to go into exile, God’s redemptive plan does not abandon it.

  • The recovery of Samaria demonstrates that God’s covenantal faithfulness outlasts human unfaithfulness. The division of the kingdom into Judah and Israel was a consequence of sin, but the ultimate goal of Yahweh is the reunification and healing of the entire house of Jacob.

  • It reveals God as the Sovereign Redeemer who reclaims territories polluted by pagan influence and transforms them once again into holy inheritances for His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Obadiah 1:19, the context is the Day of the Lord, where God executes judgment on the proud nations (especially Edom) and restores His covenant people.

  • Edom had maliciously gloated over the destruction of Jerusalem and sought to seize Judah’s territory. In a dramatic reversal of fortunes, Obadiah prophesies that the restored remnant of God's people will expand far beyond their previous borders.

  • The reference to possessing the "fields of Samaria" indicates that the southern survivors (Judah) will push northward to reclaim the land once occupied by the northern ten tribes.

  • Spiritually and geographically, this represents a total restoration. The ancient division between north and south is erased, and the land defiled by pagan captivity is fully recovered and sanctified under God's sovereign rule.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Kings 16:24: "And he bought the hill Samaria from Shemer for two talents of silver; then he built on the hill, and called the name of the city which he built, Samaria, after the name of Shemer, owner of the hill."

  • 2 Kings 17:6: "In the ninth year of Hoshea, the king of Assyria took Samaria and carried Israel away to Assyria, and placed them in Halah and by the Habor, the River of Gozan, and in the cities of the Medes."

  • Hosea 8:5: "Your calf is rejected, O Samaria! My anger is aroused against them— How long will it be before they attain to innocence?"

  • Amos 3:12: "Thus says the LORD: 'As a shepherd takes from the mouth of the lion Two legs or a piece of an ear, So shall the children of Israel be taken out Who dwell in Samaria— In the corner of a bed and on the edge of a couch!'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • New Testament Connection: The animosity between Jews and Samaritans in the Gospels stems directly from the fall of Samaria in 722 B.C. The Assyrians settled pagan foreigners in the region, who intermarried with the remaining Israelites, producing a syncretistic religion and a mixed population.

  • Gospel Fulfillment: In Acts 1:8, Jesus commands His disciples to take the Gospel to "Samaria." This acts as the spiritual fulfillment of Obadiah’s prophecy. The reclaiming of Samaria is not merely political; it is a spiritual harvest where those once deemed outcasts and apostates are brought back into the fold of God through Jesus Christ.
