LOWLAND - ( shephelah , Strong's H8219 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shephelah designates the fertile, rolling foothills situated between the high central mountain range of Judah and the coastal plain of Philistia. Historically a strategic and agricultural buffer zone, it represents a territory of transition, vulnerability, and abundance. In the prophetic context of Obadiah 1:19, the repossession of the lowland by God's people signifies complete restoration, the expansion of their borders, and the reclaiming of areas once lost to hostile neighbors.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word shephelah is derived from the verbal root shaphel, which means to be low, humbled, or abased. Rather than indicating a flat, low-lying plain, shephelah refers geographically to the wide tract of rolling limestone hills, ranging from 300 to 1,000 feet above sea level, that lie between the maritime plain of Philistia and the steep mountain range of Judah.

In the ancient Near East, this region served as a vital buffer zone. It was cut by several wide, fertile valleys running east to west, which became famous biblical battlegrounds (such as the Valley of Elah). Because of its physical layout, the lowland was a transition zone where highland Israelite culture constantly met and clashed with lowland Canaanite and Philistine cultures. It was also an agricultural treasure, highly prized for its extensive olive groves, vineyards, and sycamore-fig trees.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun shephelah refers specifically to the western foothills of Judea. It is a technical geographical term used to designate this specific region, often translated in English versions as "the lowland," "the lowlands," "the valley," or "the foothills."

Lexically, the term stands in contrast to the high mountain country (har) and the dry southern desert (negev). While it shares a root with words denoting humility or lowliness, its usage in historical and prophetic texts is almost exclusively geographical, representing fertile, highly desirable, yet easily invaded territory.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term occurs 20 times in the Hebrew Bible. It is frequently listed alongside other distinct geographical zones of the Promised Land to outline the full scope of Israel’s territory.

Because of its root connection to shaphel (to bring low), the shephelah carries an underlying word picture of leveling and accessibility. Unlike the isolated, rugged safety of the Judean heights, the lowland is open, accessible, and invites cultivation. It represents a place of daily labor, agricultural fruitfulness, and constant interaction with the wider world. To possess the lowland is to possess the keys to agricultural wealth and territorial security.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of the shephelah centers on God's covenant faithfulness and His role as the sovereign land-giver.

• Sovereignty over Vulnerable Places: The lowland was historically difficult to defend and was often the first territory lost to foreign invaders like the Philistines. By promising the restoration of the shephelah, God demonstrates His power to secure and reclaim the highly vulnerable, easily targeted areas of His people's lives and inheritance.

• The Exaltation of the Lowly: There is a beautiful thematic play on the root shaphel. While prideful nations like Edom built their dwellings high in the rocky clefts of the mountains, God promises to bless, expand, and elevate those associated with the humble, low-lying areas.

• Comprehensive Restoration: God's redemptive plan is not limited to the secure, spiritual heights of the mountains (Jerusalem). It extends downward into the valleys and foothills where everyday life, farming, and commerce occur.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:19, the prophet declares the future restoration of Israel following the devastating judgment of Edom. Edom had rejoiced over Judah’s exile and assisted in her destruction. Obadiah prophesies a dramatic reversal of fortunes.

The phrase "And those of the lowland [shall possess] the Philistines" holds great contextual significance. Historically, the Philistines repeatedly encroached upon the Judean shephelah, pushing the Israelites back into the hills. In this prophetic restoration, the boundary lines are redrawn. The inhabitants of the vulnerable foothills will not merely defend their land; they will expand westward, crossing the shephelah to fully conquer and occupy the coastal plains of Philistia. It is a picture of absolute victory, expanding boundaries, and the total removal of long-standing threats.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 1:7: "Turn and take your journey, and go to the mountains of the Amorites, to all the neighboring places in the plain, in the mountains and in the lowland, in the South and on the seacoast..."

• Joshua 9:1: "And it came to pass when all the kings who were on this side of the Jordan, in the hills and in the lowland and in all the coasts of the Great Sea toward Lebanon..."

• 1 Kings 10:27: "The king made silver as common in Jerusalem as stones, and he made cedar trees as abundant as the sycamores which are in the lowland."

• Jeremiah 17:26: "And they shall come from the cities of Judah and from the places around Jerusalem, from the land of Benjamin and from the lowland, from the mountains and from the South..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Under King David and King Solomon, the economic value of this region was highly recognized. David went so far as to appoint a specialized royal administrator, Baal-Hanan the Gederite, specifically to oversee the olive and sycamore trees in the shephelah (1 Chronicles 27:28).

• In physical geography, the Shephelah is separated from the higher Judean mountains by a series of north-south valleys. This unique geological trough made the Judean hills a natural fortress, while leaving the Shephelah as the grand stage for Israel's historical struggles for freedom and identity.
