LOWLAND - (shᵉphêlâh, Strong's H8219)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun shefelah refers lexically to a low-lying region, foothill zone, or lowland plain, specifically designating the geographic area situated between the central Judean mountains and the Mediterranean coastal plain. In Obadiah 1:19, shefelah identifies the covenant inhabitants of Judah's western foothill district who, in the promised future restoration, will expand westward to possess the coastal territory of Philistia, highlighting God's complete sovereign restoration of Israel's territorial inheritance.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew noun shefelah functions as a prominent geographical term in the Old Testament. Physically, the Shephelah comprises the region of rolling limestone hills and valleys located between the high mountain ridge of Judea to the east and the coastal plain of Philistia to the west. Elevational gradients transition from the high Judean hills down through these foothills toward the Mediterranean Sea.

Because of its position, the Shephelah historically served as a critical buffer zone and key strategic corridor. Major trade routes and military access roads passed through its valleys, making it the locus of frequent territorial conflict between Israel and neighboring powers, particularly the Philistines. In Obadiah 1:19, the word appears in the feminine singular in the absolute state, referring to this specific physical region and its inhabitants.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shefelah denotes "lowland," "low country," or "foothills." It derives from the primary verbal root shaphel, which means "to be low," "to be abased," or "to be humbled." While the verbal root often carries ethical or metaphorical senses regarding human pride and divine humbling, the noun shefelah functions almost exclusively as a concrete geographical proper noun or regional designation for the low-lying Judean foothills.

The term designates lower elevation relative to the adjacent central mountain range. It does not signify a deep valley (‘emeq) or a flat plain (mishor), but rather the distinct zone of low, arable hills.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, shefelah is employed in three primary descriptive contexts:

  • Territorial Boundaries and Conquest: In the books of Joshua and Judges, shefelah appears alongside other distinct geographic regions (such as the mountain country, the Negev/South, and the coastal plain) to comprehensively outline the full extent of the Promised Land apportioned to the tribes of Israel.

  • Agricultural Abundance and Prosperity: Historical accounts in Kings and Chronicles emphasize the fertility of the Shephelah. Known for its abundant sycamore-fig trees, olive groves, and pastureland, the region symbolized economic prosperity under strong kings like Solomon, Uzziah, and Hezekiah.

  • Prophetic Restoration: In prophetic books such as Jeremiah, Zechariah, and Obadiah, the repopulation and security of the shefelah signifies the renewal of covenant blessings, national security, and the comprehensive reversal of exile and foreign oppression.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of shefelah carries significant theological weight regarding God's faithfulness to His covenant promises:

  • God's Sovereign Rule Over Geopolitical Boundaries: The allocation and restoration of specific physical territories, including the contested shefelah, demonstrates that the Lord remains sovereign over earthly geography, national borders, and historical destinies.

  • Faithful Fulfillment of Covenant Inheritance: Land inheritance in Israel was not merely real estate; it was a tangible sign of God's covenant with Abraham and his descendants. The reclaim of the lowlands underscores God's commitment to restore the fullness of His people's heritage.

  • Reversal of Desolation and Divine Judgment: Where foreign invaders had breached Judah's defenses and annexed borderlands, prophetic promise declares that God will undo the devastation, restoring peace, agricultural productivity, and physical security to His people.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:19, the prophet outlines the dramatic eschatological reversal and victory of the house of Jacob. The verse utilizes parallel geographic pairings to describe the expansion of God's people: those from the Negev (South) will possess the mountains of Esau, while those from the Lowland (shefelah) will possess the land of the Philistines.

During periods of Judean weakness, neighboring nations like Edom and Philistia exploited Jerusalem's collapse, encroaching upon Judah's borders. Obadiah pronounces judgment upon Edom for its pride and violence against Israel. In contrast to Edom's temporary triumph, Obadiah promises that God's remnant will reoccupy their rightful territories and expand beyond them.

By specifically naming the shefelah, Obadiah highlights the western frontier of Judah. The inhabitants of these vulnerable buffer hills will not merely hold their ground; they will advance to claim the Philistine plain itself. The presence of shefelah here emphasizes that God's ultimate kingdom established on Mount Zion brings comprehensive restoration, reclaiming every contested borderland and fulfilling the ancient boundaries promised to Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Joshua 10:40 — "So Joshua conquered all the land: the mountain country and the South and the lowland and the wilderness slopes, and all their kings; he left none remaining, but utterly destroyed all that breathed, as the LORD God of Israel had commanded."

  • 1 Kings 10:27 — "The king made silver as common in Jerusalem as stones, and he made cedar trees as abundant as the sycamores which are in the lowland."

  • Jeremiah 32:44 — "Men shall buy fields for money, sign deeds and seal them, and take witnesses, in the land of Benjamin, in the places around Jerusalem, in the cities of Judah, in the cities of the mountains, in the cities of the lowland, and in the cities of the South; for I will cause their captives to return,’ says the LORD."

  • Zechariah 7:7 — "Should you not hear the words which the LORD proclaimed through the former prophets when Jerusalem and the cities around it were inhabited and prosperous, and the South and the lowland were inhabited?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

Understanding the geography of the shefelah clarifies many narrative passages in Scripture. Key biblical accounts—such as Samson's exploits against the Philistines in the Valley of Sorek, David's victory over Goliath in the Valley of Elah, and the fortified cities of Lachish and Azekah defending against Assyrian and Babylonian invasions—all occurred within the Shephelah.

When Obadiah prophecies that the inhabitants of the shefelah will possess Philistia, ancient listeners would immediately grasp the profound significance: the very region that served for centuries as a vulnerable battleground against Philistine aggression would become the springboard for God's restored people to possess the entire western coast.
