MOUNTAINS - (har, Strong's H2022)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun har denotes a mountain, hill, ridge, or elevated hill country. Throughout the Old Testament, it describes physical landforms that serve as boundary lines, natural military fortresses, places of divine encounter, or regional domains. In Obadiah 1:19, where the word appears in the masculine singular construct state linked directly with Esau ("mountain of Esau"), it designates the formidable highland region of Edom. Its specific function in Obadiah's prophecy is to declare that God's redeemed remnant will dispossess Edom, overturning Edom's arrogant self-reliance in its natural mountain fortresses and demonstrating YHWH's sovereign redistribution of the land to His covenant people.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Near East, terrain dictated political security, regional identity, and agricultural life. A har (mountain or hill country) provided a community with crucial military advantages, offering natural barriers against invading armies and high vantage points for defense. The region associated with Esau—Mount Seir—was a rugged, high-altitude sandstone range south of the Dead Sea. Its natural canyons and high peaks made Edom feel virtually invincible. In Israelite thought, mountains were also associated with divine revelation and covenant encounters, such as Mount Sinai and Mount Zion. Thus, a mountain was rarely just a physical feature; it carried strong associations of strength, refuge, boundary, or spiritual assembly.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, har refers to:

  • A prominent physical mountain or mountain peak.

  • A hill or elevated ridge.

  • An entire mountainous range or hill region (often translated "hill country").

The basic meaning is spatial and topographical, denoting land that rises significantly above surrounding terrain. Dependent on context, it can refer to a single specific peak or collectively to an entire highland region occupied by a specific nation or tribe.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The noun har is used across wide narrative, poetic, and prophetic contexts in the Old Testament:

  • Geographic Boundaries and Tribal Possessions: It frequently delineates regional territories assigned to the tribes of Israel or neighboring nations, such as the hill country of Judah or Ephraim.

  • Places of Divine Manifestation and Worship: God routinely met His people on mountains (such as Mount Sinai during the Exodus or Mount Zion in Jerusalem). The elevation of the mountain underscored God's transcendence and holy distinction from the surrounding earth.

  • Strongholds and Military Refuge: In times of warfare or persecution, individuals and armies fled to the har for safety, taking advantage of steep cliffs and caves.

  • Symbols of Human Pride and Earthly Stability: Prophetically and poetically, mountains symbolize stability, earthly kingdoms, or lofty human pride that God promises to bring low.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of har develops along several biblical lines:

  • God's Sovereign Elevation and Abatement: Though human nations view their mountain strongholds as indestructible, God claims sovereign authority over every height. He created the mountains, and He can level them or hand them over to whom He pleases.

  • Covenant Fulfillment and Reinheritance: The redistribution of mountain regions in prophetic literature underscores God's faithfulness to His covenant oaths. Terrific spatial transformations demonstrate that worldly power cannot prevent the ultimate expansion of God's kingdom.

  • True Security vs. False Security: Human kingdoms frequently trust in physical topography (har) for protection. Biblical theology continually contrasts confidence in earthly heights with ultimate trust in YHWH, who alone is the true Rock and whose holy hill (Zion) remains forever established.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:19, the prophet communicates God's final judgment upon Edom and the restoration of Israel. Edom had mistreated Judah during Jerusalem's distress, gloating over their brother's downfall and relying on their own defensive geography ("you who dwell in the clefts of the rock," Obadiah 1:3).

The target word in Obadiah 1:19 is in the masculine singular construct state, binding har directly to Esau ("mountain of Esau"). Grammatically, this construct relationship highlights the specific mountainous territory belonging to the descendants of Esau. Although translated as plural "mountains" in some English versions, the singular construct form functions as a collective singular for the entire elevated homeland of Edom.

By declaring that "the South [Negeb] shall possess the mountain of Esau," Obadiah shows a dramatic reversal of fortunes. The very natural fortress that fueled Edom's pride and rendered them complacent will be taken over by the surviving remnant of Israel. The elevated stronghold of the oppressor becomes the quiet inheritance of God's redeemed people, proving that no earthly elevation can defend against YHWH's righteous decree.

  • Deuteronomy 9:15 – "So I turned and came down from the mountain, and the mountain burned with fire; and the two tablets of the covenant were in my two hands."

  • Psalms 99:9 – "Exalt the LORD our God, And worship at His holy hill; For the LORD our God is holy."

  • Amos 6:1 – "Woe to you who are at ease in Zion, And trust in Mount Samaria, Notable persons in the chief nation, To whom the house of Israel comes!"

  • Ezekiel 7:16 – "Those who survive will escape and be on the mountains like doves of the valleys, all of them mourning, each for his iniquity."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Structural Contrast in Obadiah: Obadiah constructs a intentional contrast between two specific heights: the "mountain of Esau" (Obadiah 1:8, 19, 21) and "Mount Zion" (Obadiah 1:17, 21). While the mountain of Esau represents proud human security destined for dispossession and fire, Mount Zion represents holy deliverance, sanctuary, and the ultimate site from which YHWH's kingdom is proclaimed.

  • Irony of Edomite Geography: Edom's high altitude gave its inhabitants a psychological sense of moral and military superiority over the lowlands. The judgment in Obadiah 1:19 specifically assigns the low-lying Negeb ("South") to inherit and possess Edom's high mountain, emphasizing God's delight in exalting the humble and bringing down the lofty.
