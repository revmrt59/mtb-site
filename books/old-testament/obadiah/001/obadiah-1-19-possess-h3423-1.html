POSSESS - ( ya.rash , Strong's H3423 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yarash primarily means "to take possession of," "to inherit," or "to dispossess." Across the Old Testament, it describes the act of occupying territory, receiving a legal or covenant inheritance, or expelling prior occupants under divine mandate. In Obadiah 1:19, the word describes the triumphant restoration of God's covenant people as they reoccupy their assigned inheritance and expand into lands previously held by hostile neighbors, demonstrating the sovereign reversal of Judah's exile and the certainty of divine covenant promises.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root yarash is a central covenantal term in ancient Hebrew thought, deeply tied to the concepts of land, lineage, and divine grant. In the Old Testament, land was not viewed merely as real estate or political territory; it was the primary physical tangible expression of God's covenant with Abraham and his descendants. To yarash the land meant to enter into active enjoyment and stewardship of what God had promised.

The term is particularly prominent in the historical context of the Exodus, the Wilderness Wanderings, and the Conquest under Joshua, as well as in the prophetic books addressing Israel's exile and future restoration. In the ancient Near Eastern context, nations claimed lands by right of conquest or ancestral lineage, but Israel's right to possess land rested entirely upon the sovereign gift of Yahweh. Consequently, dispossession was the consequence of covenant unfaithfulness or divine judgment, while repossessing the land signaled divine mercy, judgment on oppressor nations, and covenant renewal.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yarash encompasses a dynamic semantic range centered on control and ownership of property or heritage:

  • To take possession of or occupy land, cities, or property.

  • To inherit or succeed to a possession, becoming the rightful heir.

  • To dispossess or drive out previous inhabitants, depriving them of their property or territory.

  • In specific context, to come to poverty or suffer loss of property as a result of dispossession.

The core idea is the transition of authority and occupation from one party to another. When used with God as the initiator, it highlights His sovereign right to grant territory to whom He wills and to dispossess those who incur His judgment.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament narrative, yarash functions in both positive (taking possession, inheriting) and negative (dispossessing, expelling) senses.

In the Pentateuch, particularly Deuteronomy, the verb frequently appears as a command and a promise. God commands Israel to go in and "possess" the land that He swore to give to their ancestors. Here, yarash involves human action responding to divine grant; Israel must actively step into and claim what God has designated as their heritage.

In military and historical contexts, such as Joshua and Judges, yarash often carries the nuance of driving out or dispossessing hostile nations. The driving out of the Canaanites was presented not as arbitrary aggression, but as divine execution of justice against entrenched wickedness.

Conversely, yarash can describe the tragic loss of inheritance when God's people faced exile, or the economic reality of falling into poverty through the loss of ancestral holdings. In prophetic literature, the term shifts toward eschatological restoration: though God dispossessed Israel due to covenant infidelity, He promises a future day when a remnant will once again repossess their rightful inheritance.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yarash underscores several foundational biblical doctrines:

  • Divine Sovereignty over the Earth: God owns the earth and assigns territories according to His holy purpose. Human nations hold land only by His allowance.

  • Covenant Faithfulness: The gift of land is bound to God's sworn promises. Human failure cannot permanently annul God's ultimate intention to grant His people their inheritance.

  • Divine Retribution and Justice: The dispossession of nations (including Israel during the exile) demonstrates that persistent unrighteousness leads to loss of privilege and territory under divine judgment.

  • Restoration and Reversal: The prophetic use of yarash points to God's ultimate reversal of human injustice. Oppressors who unlawfully seize what belongs to God's people will themselves be dispossessed, while the humble remnant will inherit the kingdom.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah's prophecy concentrates on God's judgment against Edom for their violence and pride during the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem. Edom had gloated over Judah's downfall, actively participated in their plunder, and seized border lands belonging to Jacob's descendants.

In Obadiah 1:19, the prophet declares the overarching divine reversal: "The South shall possess the mountains of Esau, and the Lowland shall possess Philistia. They shall possess the fields of Ephraim and the fields of Samaria. Benjamin shall possess Gilead."

The target word yarash appears in the Qal stem, perfect form, 3rd person plural. In prophetic Hebrew discourse, the perfect form often presents a promised future event as an accomplished certainty within the decree of God. Here, yarash communicates that Israel's future expansion and repossession of land is guaranteed by Yahweh Himself.

Rather than remaining confined to a diminished state, God's restored people will reclaim Mount Esau (the traditional home of Edom) alongside Philistia, Ephraim, Samaria, and Gilead. The word choice highlights complete territorial and covenantal recovery. The very lands taken or threatened by hostile neighbors will be occupied by the remnant of Jacob. The word yarash thus forms the linchpin of Obadiah’s message: Edom’s arrogance and unlawful plunder will end in total dispossession, whereas God's judgment results in the full restoration of His people's heritage.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Jeremiah 49:2 — "Therefore behold, the days are coming,” says the LORD, “That I will cause an alarm of war to be heard in Rabbah of the Ammonites; It shall be a desolate mound, and her villages shall be burned with fire. Then Israel shall take possession of his inheritance,” says the LORD."

  • Isaiah 65:9 — "I will bring forth descendants from Jacob, and from Judah an heir of My mountains; My elect shall inherit it, and My servants shall dwell there."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A notable thematic pattern in prophetic literature is the moral link between divine gift and legitimate occupation. In Obadiah, Edom sought to gain land through opportunism, violence, and pride. However, human effort to seize land apart from divine grant is ultimately futile. In contrast, Judah’s future repossession of territory is entirely grounded in Yahweh's covenant initiative.

Furthermore, the geographical scope outlined in Obadiah 1:19 covers regions to the south, west, north, and east, symbolizing a complete, multi-directional expansion. The use of yarash across these regions demonstrates that when God restores His people, the scope of restoration surpasses the immediate loss, bringing full spiritual and physical realization to His ancient promises.
