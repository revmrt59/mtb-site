SAMARIA - (Shômᵉrôwn, Strong's H8111)

Summary:

The proper noun shomron refers to Samaria, the strategic hill capital and central territorial district of the Northern Kingdom of Israel. Historically established as a royal stronghold, the name came to represent both the fortified capital city and the surrounding regional countryside. In Obadiah 1:19, shomron identifies the northern territory whose fertile fields will be repossessed by God's redeemed remnant, contributing to the prophet's declaration of total territorial and spiritual restoration for a reunited covenant people under the rule of Yahweh.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The designation shomron originates in Old Testament history as the name given to the mountain purchased by King Omri of Northern Israel from a man named Shemer (1 Kings 16:24). Omri fortified the hilltop and established it as the new capital city of the Northern Kingdom. From that point forward, Samaria served as the political center, royal seat, and symbolic hub of Northern Israel. Over time, the name expanded beyond the city walls to encompass the entire surrounding hill country and central region of Israel.

In the historical setting of Obadiah, Judah had suffered devastating defeat, pillage, and exile. The neighboring nation of Edom had maliciously rejoiced over Jerusalem's collapse, seizing territory and betraying Judean fugitives. Obadiah's prophecy pronounces divine judgment on Edom while promising ultimate vindication for God's covenant people. In the closing vision of the book, the prophet outlines the full geographic scope of Israel's restoration, explicitly naming Samaria as part of the repossessed inheritance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shomron is a proper geographic noun used in Old Testament Hebrew to denote:

  • The fortified capital city of the Northern Kingdom of Israel.

  • The broader geographical region and central highlands comprising Northern Israel.

In terms of formal grammar, the word functions as a proper noun indicating a specific location. The term refers to the actual historical site and surrounding countryside rather than an abstract theological concept. In Obadiah 1:19, shomron appears in the singular, absolute state, forming the genitive construction "fields of Samaria" (sde shomron), designating the regional agricultural land surrounding the former capital.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament narrative and prophetic literature, shomron carries distinct historical and thematic nuances depending on the context:

  • Political Capital: In the books of Kings, Samaria is primarily the seat of royal administration for northern monarchs like Ahab, Jehu, and Jeroboam II. It is portrayed as a city of wealth, ivory palaces, and military fortification, but also as the epicentral location of Baal worship and official state apostasy.

  • Center of Divine Judgment: In the pre-exilic prophets (such as Micah and Isaiah), Samaria stands for Northern Israel's covenant unfaithfulness. Its imminent fall to Assyria serves as a sobering warning to Judah.

  • Territory of Shattered Identity: Following the Assyrian conquest, Samaria's elite population was deported and replaced by foreign captives, rendering the region a place of tragic exile and fragmented identity.

  • Territory of Eschatological Hope: In restoration passages, such as Obadiah 1:19, Samaria ceases to represent apostasy or foreign occupation; instead, it is viewed as rightful covenant soil that God will clean, restore, and return to His unified people.

4. Theological Implications

The mention of Samaria in Obadiah 1:19 holds substantial theological significance within the overarching story of redemption:

  • Reversal of Northern Division: The division of Solomon's kingdom into North (Israel/Ephraim/Samaria) and South (Judah) was one of the most tragic fractures in Old Testament history. By specifying that God's remnant will possess the fields of Ephraim and Samaria, Obadiah envisions the total healing of the ancient schism and the reunion of all twelve tribes under Yahweh's reign.

  • Sanctification of the Land: Samaria had long been stained by idolatry and foreign rule. Its inclusion among the repossessed territories demonstrates that no land or region ruined by sin is beyond the scope of divine redemption and holy reclamation.

  • Fidelity to Covenant Promises: God's promise to Abraham included specific geographic boundaries. The reclamation of Samaria affirms that God's covenant pledges remain unbroken despite human unfaithfulness and foreign devastation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:19, the target word shomron occurs in the singular, absolute state within a poetic list detailing the expansive territorial reclamation promised to God's remnant.

The immediate syntax places "the fields of Samaria" in direct structural parallelism with "the fields of Ephraim":

"They shall possess the fields of Ephraim and the fields of Samaria."

This literary arrangement is precise and purposeful:

  • Phrase Coupling: Ephraim and Samaria together represent the heartland of Northern Israel. Ephraim denotes the prominent tribal territory, while Samaria denotes the regional capital and its agricultural lands.

  • Broadening Context: Obadiah constructs a compass-like survey of restoration. The South (Negeb) reclaims Edom's territory; the Lowland (Shephelah) reclaims Philistia; the remnant occupies the central northern territories of Ephraim and Samaria; and Benjamin expands across the Jordan into Gilead.

  • Contrast with Edom: While Edom sought to expand its borders by exploiting Jerusalem's destruction, Obadiah announces that Edom will be completely dispossessed, whereas God's people will expand to possess the entire length and breadth of the Promised Land. The fields of Samaria, once depopulated and overrun, will once again nourish the resurrected house of Jacob.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The following passages illustrate how shomron is employed across historical narrative and prophetic judgment:

  • 1 Kings 21:1 – "And it came to pass after these things that Naboth the Jezreelite had a vineyard which was in Jezreel, next to the palace of Ahab king of Samaria."

Here, Samaria is identified as the royal residence and political capital of the northern kingdom during the reign of Ahab.

  • 2 Kings 6:24 – "And it happened after this that Ben-Hadad king of Syria gathered all his army, and went up and besieged Samaria."

In this narrative, Samaria represents the heavily fortified center of northern military defense under severe siege.

  • 2 Kings 17:6 – "In the ninth year of Hoshea, the king of Assyria took Samaria and carried Israel away to Assyria..."

This passage marks the historical climax of divine judgment against the northern kingdom, recording the fall of the capital city and the subsequent exile.

  • Micah 1:6 – "'Therefore I will make Samaria a heap of ruins in the field, places for planting a vineyard; I will pour down her stones into the valley, and I will uncover her foundations.'"

Micah uses Samaria as the central object of prophetic judgment, declaring that the prominent city will be reduced to bare agricultural soil because of her idolatry.

  • Ezekiel 16:46 – "'Your elder sister is Samaria, who dwells with her daughters to the north of you; and your younger sister, who dwells to the south of you, is Sodom and her daughters.'"

Ezekiel utilizes Samaria in a prophetic allegory to personify Northern Israel's spiritual unfaithfulness in comparison with Judah.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A meaningful canonical contrast emerges when comparing Micah 1:6 and Obadiah 1:19. In Micah, God threatens to turn the fortified city of Samaria into "a heap of ruins in the field" because of its persistent idolatry. In Obadiah, however, the target word appears in the phrase "the fields of Samaria," representing fruitful countryside to be joyfully repossessed by God's restored people.

What judgment reduced to mere field and ruin, God's redeeming grace ultimately restores as a fruitful inheritance. The shift highlights the grand biblical arc: divine judgment on sin is never God's final word for His covenant purpose, which culminates in full restoration and the establishment of His everlasting kingdom (Obadiah 1:21).
