MAKE - (nâthan, Strong's H5414)

Summary:

The primary Hebrew verb natan broadly means "to give," "to put," "to set," or "to establish/make." In Obadiah 1:2, Yahweh uses natan in the first person singular to issue a sovereign decree against the nation of Edom: "Behold, I will make [or have rendered] you small among the nations." In this specific passage, the verb functions as an absolute determination of status, demonstrating that God Himself actively lowers the proud kingdom of Edom, stripping away its self-exalted position and assigning it a reduced, despised standing among neighboring peoples.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root natan is one of the most common and versatile verbs in the Hebrew Bible. It serves as the standard Northwest Semitic term for physical transfer, spatial positioning, or functional assignment. Across the Old Testament narrative, poetic, and prophetic literature, natan spans a broad spectrum of human and divine interactions, ranging from literal gifts and transactional handovers to legal appointments and sovereign divine decrees.

In the historical and prophetic setting of Obadiah, the nation of Edom—descendants of Esau—had long traded on its formidable geography. Dwelling in the rugged, high-altitude cliff dwellings of the region of Mount Seir (Sela/Petra), Edom viewed itself as invincible and superior to its neighbors. Following the catastrophe that befell Judah, Edom gloated and participated in the exploitation of God's covenant people. Obadiah's prophecy opens with Yahweh breaking Edom's boastful silence, using natan to announce an inescapable readjustment of Edom's global standing.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, natan denotes the act of placing, granting, or causing something to enter a specific state or context. Standard Hebrew lexicons classify its primary semantic dimensions into four main categories:

  • To give / grant: Transferring ownership, control, or possession of an item, land, or authority to another party.

  • To put / place: Physically depositing an object or person into a specific spatial location.

  • To appoint / assign: Setting a person into a official role, office, or functional commission.

  • To render / make: Effecting a total change of condition or establishing a status for an individual or nation (e.g., rendering someone small, making a covenant, or causing a city to become a ruin).

Lexically, natan is neutral regarding the favorability of the action; its theological force—whether signaling gracious grant or punitive judgment—is entirely dictated by the subject performing the action, the object receiving it, and the surrounding syntactic framework.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

When natan appears with a personal object and a predicate adjective (such as "small"), it carries the specific nuance of "rendering" or "making" someone into a designated condition.

Throughout the Old Testament, human beings may attempt to establish their own fame, height, or political security, but human positioning is inherently fragile. By contrast, when God is the subject of natan, the verb emphasizes absolute authority. God "gives" kingdoms to whomever He pleases, "places" rulers on thrones, and "renders" proud empires low. The usage of natan in prophetic judgment highlights that international rank and national longevity are not dictated by military power or natural geography, but by divine determination.

4. Theological Implications

The employment of natan in Obadiah 1:2 bears significant theological weight regarding divine sovereignty and the judgment of human pride:

  • Sovereignty Over Gentile Nations: Yahweh is not merely the local God of Israel; He exercises supreme jurisdiction over all earth's borders and international rankings. Edom's standing among the nations (baggoyim) is subject to Yahweh's assignment.

  • The Demotion of Human Arrogance: Pride leads human powers to imagine that they are self-made and self-secured. God’s act of "making" Edom small exposes the ultimate reality that human exaltedness is an illusion unless established by God.

  • Unmediated Divine Execution: Judgment in the prophetic worldview is not an accidental turn of fortune or mere geopolitical luck. The verb highlights direct, personal divine agency; Edom’s reduced state is directly authored by the Lord.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:2, the target word appears in the Qal stem, perfect form, 1st person, singular (nətattîḵā).

The Qal stem presents the basic, direct action of the verb, while the 1st person singular explicitly identifies Yahweh as the sole agent: "I have made/set you." The perfect form presents the action as completed and settled in the divine mind. In Hebrew prophetic speech, this construction often functions as a prophetic perfect—stating a future judgment with such fixed divine certainty that it is communicated as an already accomplished reality.

The immediate sentence context sets up a striking antithesis between God's decree and Edom's self-perception:

  • In verse 2, Yahweh declares: "Behold, I have made (natan) you small (qaton) among the nations; you shall be greatly despised."

  • In verse 3, Edom's internal heart-boast is exposed: "The pride of your heart has deceived you, you who dwell in the clefts of the rock... Who will bring me down to the ground?"

Edom claims high security based on its mountainous terrain, but Yahweh answers Edom's arrogant question before it is even fully uttered. By using natan, Yahweh asserts that no cliff is high enough to reverse what He has already appointed. Edom's geopolitical reduction is a direct result of Yahweh's irreversible decree.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The following passages demonstrate how natan functions across different literary and historical contexts within the Old Testament:

  • Genesis 39:20 — "Then Joseph's master took him and put him into the prison, a place where the king's prisoners were confined." (Here the verb describes physical placement into a restricted location.)

  • Deuteronomy 30:15 — "See, I have set before you today life and good, death and evil." (Exhibits the usage of placing a solemn, decisive moral choice before a people.)

  • 1 Kings 20:13 — "Suddenly a prophet approached Ahab king of Israel, saying, 'Thus says the LORD: "Have you seen all this great multitude? Behold, I will deliver it into your hand today, and you shall know that I am the LORD."'" (Illustrates the sovereign grant of military victory into human hands.)

  • Isaiah 49:6 — "Indeed He says, 'It is too small a thing that You should be My Servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob... I will also give You as a light to the Gentiles, that You should be My salvation to the ends of the earth.'" (Demonstrates divine assignment and appointment to a global, redemptive role.)

7. Other Relevant Insights

The introductory particle "Behold" (hinneh) directly precedes natan in Obadiah 1:2. In Hebrew prophetic discourse, pairing hinneh with a 1st-person verbal form focuses intense attention on the immediate, undeniable intervention of Yahweh.

While Edom looked at its soaring red sandstone cliffs and felt expansive and exalted, Yahweh called Edom to look at reality from the heavenly perspective: Edom had already been rendered "small" (qaton) by divine decree. The contrast between Edom's perceived grandeur and its true status established by natan underscores the theme that human arrogance is inherently blind to divine truth.
