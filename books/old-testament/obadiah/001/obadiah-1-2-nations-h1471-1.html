NATIONS - (gôwy, Strong's H1471)

Summary:

The Hebrew word goy primarily refers to a people, nation, or distinct ethnic and political group. While it can occasionally refer to Israel, in its plural form (goyim) it most frequently designates the Gentile nations of the world, often highlighting their distinction from God's covenant people. In Obadiah 1:2, goy is used to describe the surrounding international community of the Ancient Near East. Its use here emphasizes the public, global scale of Edom’s impending humiliation, as God declares He will reduce this proud kingdom to insignificance in the eyes of the very nations they sought to impress and ally with.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew term goy is one of the primary words used in the Old Testament to describe a nation or a people group. In the ancient Near Eastern context, a goy was typically defined by shared territorial boundaries, a centralized government or king, and a common culture or religion.

In biblical literature, a distinction is often made between 'am (which frequently emphasizes kinship, blood relations, or a gathered people) and goy (which leans more toward the political, territorial, and national entity). As the Old Testament narrative progresses, the plural form goyim becomes the standard designation for the Gentile nations—the peoples outside the Abrahamic covenant, who often worshipped other gods and operated according to human pride and power.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, goy means a nation, a people, or a Gentile. It denotes a massing of people into a recognized political or ethnic body.

The authoritative morphology for this occurrence in Obadiah 1:2 is masculine plural absolute. The plural form (goyim) indicates that the prophet is not speaking of one specific enemy nation, but of the nations collectively. The absolute state means the word stands independently in the grammar ("the nations") rather than being bound in a construct phrase (such as "the nations of...").

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The biblical usage of goy spans a wide theological spectrum. Early in the Pentateuch, God promises to make Abraham a great nation (goy), and Israel is called to be a "holy nation." However, the overwhelming majority of its usage refers to the non-Israelite nations.

In the Prophets and the Psalms, the goyim are frequently depicted in two primary ways:

First, they are seen as rebellious entities that rage against God's sovereignty and oppress His people.

Second, they are presented as instruments in God's hand—tools He raises up to discipline Israel, or objects of His direct judgment when their own wickedness reaches its full measure. Ultimately, the prophets also envision a day when the goyim will stream to Jerusalem to worship the Lord, demonstrating that God's redemptive plan encompasses the whole world.

4. Theological Implications

The use of goy in the context of divine judgment underscores the absolute sovereignty of Yahweh over the entire international stage. In the ancient world, deities were often viewed as localized, territorial gods whose power was limited to their own nation's borders. The biblical use of goy shatters this paradigm. Yahweh is not merely the God of Israel; He is the supreme Monarch over all goyim. He dictates the rise and fall, the expansion and the reduction, of every empire and kingdom on earth. A nation's standing, security, and significance are entirely dependent on His sovereign decree.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:2, God speaks directly to Edom: "Behold, I will make you small among the nations [goyim]; You shall be greatly despised."

Edom was a nation characterized by immense pride, largely due to its seemingly impregnable geographic location in the rock fortresses of Mount Seir (Petra) and its reputation for political wisdom and strong alliances. Edom viewed itself as great, secure, and elevated among the surrounding powers.

The use of goyim here is central to the irony of God's judgment. God does not merely say He will destroy Edom; He says He will make them "small among the nations." The very international community that Edom trusted in, allied with, and sought to be esteemed by would become the audience of their humiliation. The masculine plural absolute form points to this broad, collective audience. Edom's reduction would be a public spectacle, proving that no nation can maintain its greatness when the God of Israel decrees its insignificance.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalms 2:1 – "Why do the nations rage, And the people plot a vain thing?"

Here, the plural word highlights the collective hostility and rebellion of the Gentile world against God and His anointed, a rebellion that God ultimately laughs at from His sovereign throne.

• Jeremiah 1:10 – "See, I have this day set you over the nations and over the kingdoms, To root out and to pull down, To destroy and to throw down, To build and to plant."

This verse demonstrates God's absolute authority over the international political order, granting His prophet the authority to declare the fate of the surrounding peoples.

• Deuteronomy 4:27 – "And the LORD will scatter you among the peoples, and you will be left few in number among the nations where the LORD will drive you."

In this covenantal warning, the nations are presented as the destination of exile—foreign territories where Israel would be scattered as a consequence of their disobedience.

• Isaiah 49:6 – "I will also give You as a light to the Gentiles [nations], That You should be My salvation to the ends of the earth."

This prophetic use reveals the redemptive scope of God's plan; the Messiah is not only for Israel but is appointed to bring salvation to the non-Israelite peoples of the world.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast in Obadiah is striking: Edom thought they were high and mighty, but God declares they will be small and despised. In the ancient Near East, a nation's glory was measured by how other nations perceived them. By stating that Edom will be despised among the goyim, God is stripping away the core of their national identity—their pride and their international standing. The judgment fits the crime: because Edom proudly looked down on their brother Jacob (Judah) in his day of distress, the surrounding nations will now look down on Edom.
