CAPTIVES - (gâlûwth, Strong's H1546)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun galut refers to exile, captivity, or a displaced group of exiles removed from their homeland. Lexically, it functions abstractly to denote the state or condition of exile, and concretely as a collective noun designating the exiled community itself. In Obadiah 1:20, galut identifies the displaced host of Israel and the exiled citizens of Jerusalem residing in distant lands, emphasizing that those currently uprooted and stripped of their earthly heritage are precisely the ones whom God promises to restore to their covenant inheritance.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The term galut arises within the historical and prophetic context of displacement, forced deportation, and foreign subjugation experienced by Israel and Judah. Ancient Near Eastern conquest routinely involved the mass deportation of conquered populations to break national resistance and assimilate subject peoples. In Old Testament history, major exilic events—such as the fall of the northern kingdom to Assyria and the later Babylonian deportations of Judah—profoundly shaped Israel's historical self-understanding and prophetic narrative.

In prophetic literature, galut describes both the historical reality of judgment through exile and the specific group of people residing in foreign dispersion. The term carries historical weight, marking distinct epochs in Israel's history (such as the years following the deportation of King Jehoiachin) as well as describing the social condition of a covenant people removed from the Promised Land.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, galut denotes:

  • The state or condition of exile and captivity.

  • A collective body of exiles or captives living in deportation.

In classical Hebrew usage, abstract nouns ending in the suffix -ut frequently express a state of being or a collective group sharing a common status. Thus, galut can refer either to the concept/duration of captivity or concretely to "the exiles" as a unified group. In Obadiah 1:20, the word appears in the feminine singular construct state, binding the exiled group directly to its place of origin and identity ("the captives/exiles of this host of the children of Israel" and "the captives/exiles of Jerusalem").

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, galut exhibits two dominant semantic nuances depending on context:

Temporal or Historical Marker: In historical and prophetic chronology, galut marks the timeline of exile, designated as the period following deportation. In these settings, the term functions as a historical anchor, identifying specific years relative to the arrival of the exiles in a foreign land.

Collective Identity of the Remnant: More frequently, galut functions as a collective noun for the people themselves. It does not merely describe individual prisoners in chains, but an entire displaced population surviving in a foreign environment. Prophetically, this collective usage often distinguishes the exiles from those remaining in the land, depicting the galut as the primary community through whom God's long-term covenant purposes would continue.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, galut stands at the intersection of covenant judgment and covenant restoration. Exile in the Old Testament is never presented as mere geopolitical misfortune; it is the ultimate covenant curse threatened in the Law for persistent infidelity. When God subjects His people to galut, He demonstrates His holiness and the truth of His word.

However, prophetic revelation transforms galut from an end point into a transitional stage. While exile represents the undoing of land ownership and temple worship, prophetic promises assure the galut that physical displacement does not sever God's covenant loyalty. Instead, God promises to preserve the galut in foreign lands, visit them with mercy, and ultimately gather them back. Thus, galut becomes the precise locus of divine hope: those who are scattered and stripped of worldly power become the object of God's redemptive re-gathering.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:20, galut appears twice in close succession near the conclusion of Obadiah's oracle against Edom. The immediate context contrasts the arrogance and violence of Edom—who gloated over Judah's destruction and actively participated in their plunder—with the ultimate destiny of God's displaced people.

The sentence structure explicitly pairs the galut ("captives/exiles") with divine territorial restoration:

  • "The captives [galut] of this host of the children of Israel" will possess the territory of the Canaanites up to Zarephath.

  • "The captives [galut] of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad" will possess the cities of the South.

Through this term, Obadiah emphasizes the complete reversal of human circumstances wrought by divine intervention. Humanly speaking, an exiled group in distant locations (such as Sepharad) possesses no land, no military force, and no geopolitical leverage. Yet Obadiah designates this exact group—the galut—as the rightful heirs who will dispossess former oppressors and reoccupy the promised borders. The word highlights that God's deliverance is not realized by those who maintained worldly strength, but is granted to the helpless displaced community whom He faithfully remembers.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Amos 1:6: "Thus says the LORD: 'For three transgressions of Gaza, and for four, I will not turn away its punishment, because they took captive the whole captivity [galut], to deliver them up to Edom.'"

Here the term describes an entire population captured and handed over to Edom, underscoring the cruelty of neighboring nations against God's displaced people.

  • Jeremiah 24:5: "Thus says the LORD, the God of Israel: 'Like these good figs, so will I acknowledge those who are carried away captive [galut] of Judah, whom I have sent out of this place for their own good into the land of the Chaldeans.'"

Jeremiah uses the noun to designate the exiled Judeans in Babylon as the preserved object of God's favorable providence and future restoration.

  • Ezekiel 1:2: "On the fifth day of the month, which was the fifth year of King Jehoiachin’s captivity [galut]..."

Ezekiel employs the word as a temporal marker to date his prophetic visions relative to the onset of the exile.

  • Isaiah 45:13: "I have raised him up in righteousness, and I will direct all his ways; he shall build My city and let My exiles [galut] go free, not for price nor reward,' says the LORD of hosts."

In this messianic and historical promise concerning Cyrus, the term specifies the displaced covenant community whom God will sovereignly liberate without purchase.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the structure of prophetic literature, the restoration of the galut serves as a thematic demonstration of God's ultimate kingdom rule. Obadiah concludes his vision with the declaration that "the kingdom shall be the LORD's" (Obadiah 1:21). The re-establishment of the galut in their God-given inheritance demonstrates that human empires and hostile neighbors do not possess final sovereignty over the earth or over God's people.

Furthermore, the construct phrases in Obadiah 1:20 preserve the covenant connection between the exiles and their spiritual identity. Though situated far away in Zarephath or Sepharad, they remain "the exiles of Israel" and "the exiles of Jerusalem." Their temporary location in exile does not redefine their true identity or extinguish their divine inheritance.
