CHILDREN - (bên, Strong's H1121)

Summary:

Lexically, ben refers primarily to a son, male offspring, child, or descendant, while also serving in corporate designations to identify members of a specific lineage, family, or national group. In Obadiah 1:20, the plural form of ben designates the exiled remnant as the corporate "children" of Israel. Its presence in this prophetic context asserts that despite exile, displacement, and foreign subjugation, the scattered captives retain their covenant lineage and remain the legal heirs appointed by God to inherit and repossess the promised land.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew noun ben is one of the foundational kinship terms in the Old Testament. At its most basic level, it denotes immediate filial relationship—a son born to parents. Beyond immediate offspring, ancient Hebrew usage extended the word to indicate multi-generational lineage, corporate solidarity, and covenant identity. When combined with the name of a patriarch or nation, as in benei Yisrael ("children of Israel"), the term transcends simple biological paternity to describe an entire covenant community bound together by shared ancestry, heritage, and divine promise.

In prophetic literature, particularly in texts dealing with judgment and restoration, terms of lineage carry legal and theological weight. To be designated among the "children" of a nation is to hold a recognized claim to the heritage, land, and covenant obligations belonging to that ancestral line.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

According to standard Hebrew lexicography (BDB; TWOT), ben encompasses several related senses:

  • An immediate male child or son.

  • A descendant or offspring across generations (e.g., grandsons or subsequent generations within a family tree).

  • A member of a specific nation, tribe, or collective group defined by a common ancestor.

  • An individual or group characterized by a specific quality, age, or condition.

In the target verse, the word functions in its collective sense to identify the corporate descendants of Israel who endured captivity following the destruction of Jerusalem.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Hebrew narrative and prophecy, ben serves as the primary linguistic vehicle for tracing family lines, establishing legal inheritance rights, and defining the collective body of God's covenant people.

When used in genealogical and historical records, the term secures lineage and documents the preservation of family lines through times of crisis. In corporate settings, "children of Israel" distinguishes the recipient community of God's revealed law and land grants from surrounding gentiles. The term carries strong nuances of covenantal continuity: physical displacement or geopolitical disaster does not erase the status of those who are the "children" of the promise.

4. Theological Implications

Theological significance attaches to ben because God's redemptive dealings in the Old Testament operate through covenant promises made to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and transmitted to their physical descendants. The term highlights the following biblical themes:

  • Covenant Faithfulness: God remembers His promises to past generations by preserving their "children."

  • Inheritance and Rights: The land was granted to Israel and their descendants as an enduring possession; being counted among the "children" establishes one's legal standing before God regarding that land.

  • Restoration Beyond Exile: Judgment may disperse the children, but covenant grace guarantees their eventual gathering and re-establishment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the short prophecy of Obadiah, the prophet pronounces divine judgment upon Edom for taking pleasure in Judah's disaster and looting Jerusalem during its overthrow. Edom presumed that Judah's fall was permanent and that the exiles were stripped of their heritage.

Obadiah 1:20 directly refutes Edom's presumption. The prophet uses ben in describing the exiled host as "the children of Israel." The target morphology supplies a masculine plural form, identifying the exiles as a collective masculine body of covenant heirs.

By applying this corporate title to captives residing in foreign locations such as Zarephath and Sepharad, the text communicates that national catastrophe has not severed their covenant identity. Though currently displaced captives, they remain the legitimate "children of Israel." Consequently, they are the rightful recipients of the eschatological reversal promised in Obadiah, in which the exiles return to repossess the territory previously lost or claimed by enemy nations.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The syntax of Obadiah 1:20 connects the identity of the exiles directly with their prospective action: as the "children of Israel," their appointed destiny is to possess the surrounding territory. The grammatical specification of the target word as masculine plural reflects the standard Hebrew usage for corporate national entities, representing the entirety of the exilic population under a single covenantal designation.
