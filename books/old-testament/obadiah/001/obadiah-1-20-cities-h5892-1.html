CITIES - (ʻîyr, Strong's H5892)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun 'ir denotes an inhabited population center, town, or walled city, ranging from small local settlements to fortified regional strongholds. In Obadiah 1:20, 'ir refers to the urban centers of the South (the Negev) that will be inherited and repossessed by the returned exiles of Jerusalem. Its presence in this prophecy emphasizes the concrete, territorial nature of God's covenant restoration, assuring His displaced people that their former captivity will yield to a full re-establishment of community, defense, and societal life in their promised land.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew noun 'ir is the standard Old Testament term for a permanent human settlement, typically translated as "city" or "town." In the ancient Near East, an 'ir was distinguished from open countryside, nomadic encampments, or unfortified rural villages (chatzerim). Towns and cities were generally characterized by permanent dwellings, centralized governance, defensive walls, towers, and gates that provided security against invaders and plunderers.

Throughout Israel's history, cities served as administrative, judicial, economic, and defensive hubs. While some ancient cities began as defensive strongholds, the term broadly encompasses any established center of population where human society gathered for mutual protection, commerce, and communal governance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, 'ir designates an inhabited urban site or town. The semantic range of 'ir includes:

  • A small town or local walled settlement.

  • A major administrative center or fortified regional hub.

  • A capital city or royal stronghold housing leadership and military assets.

At its core, the lexical definition is civic and spatial, identifying a location of settled human habitation. Lexically neutral in itself, the word acquires moral and theological significance based on the spiritual character of the city's inhabitants and God's sovereign actions toward them.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, 'ir appears in historical narratives, legal codes, prophetic declarations, and wisdom literature.

  • Narrative Foundations: In early narrative accounts, cities represent both human attempts at self-preservation and regional strongholds, such as the fortified cities of Canaan encountered during the conquest.

  • Prophetic Judgment and Promise: The prophets frequently speak of 'ir in dual contexts. Cities characterized by idol worship, social violence, and arrogance face destruction under divine judgment. Conversely, in prophetic promise, the rebuilding and repopulating of ruined cities signify divine favor, spiritual restoration, and the renewal of covenant blessings.

  • Poetic and Civic Imagery: In poetic texts, cities symbolize protection, administrative order, or human vulnerability depending on whether the settlement relies on human power or God's sovereign protection.

4. Theological Implications

In Old Testament theology, the city represents human corporate life under divine accountability. While human beings often build cities to establish autonomous security apart from God, God remains the ultimate ruler over every 'ir.

  • Human Fortifications vs. Divine Sovereignty: High walls and strong defenses cannot preserve a city when God brings judgment against injustice and idolatry.

  • Covenant Inheritance and Restoration: Physical towns and territories are integral to the Abrahamic and Davidic covenant promises. Israel's exile involved the loss and desolation of her cities; therefore, redemptive restoration involves God restoring His people to possess, rebuild, and inhabit these tangible locations.

  • Redemptive Reversal: God turns desolate, conquered urban centers into places of peace, order, and praise, demonstrating that redemption touches concrete physical and social reality.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:20 forms part of the final triumphant vision of Obadiah's prophecy, contrasting the complete destruction of arrogant Edom with the exaltation and restoration of Israel. During the Babylonian crisis, Edom had celebrated Judah's downfall and encroached upon the southern territory of Judah, the Negev.

In verse 20, Obadiah promises that "the captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad shall possess the cities ('ir) of the South." The term 'ir here refers to the strategic towns and urban settlements of the Negev region. Authoritative grammatical analysis identifies the noun 'ir in this target occurrence as feminine in gender.

The contextual nuance of 'ir in Obadiah 1:20 highlights the thoroughness of God's restoration:

  • Territorial Reclaiming: Exiled Judeans will not merely survive in foreign lands, nor will they return to empty wilderness; they will reoccupy and govern the established towns of their ancestral heritage.

  • Reversal of Dispossession: The very cities that were abandoned during the exile and occupied by hostile neighbors will be dispossessed and restored to God's covenant people.

  • Security and Stability: Reclaiming the 'ir of the South represents the re-establishment of defense, governance, and organized community life along Judah's southern border.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 13:28 — "Nevertheless the people who dwell in the land are strong; the cities ('ir) are fortified and very large; moreover we saw the descendants of Anak there."

  • 1 Samuel 9:6 — "And he said to him, 'Look now, there is in this city ('ir) a man of God, and he is an honorable man; all that he says surely comes to pass.'"

  • Jeremiah 25:18 — "Jerusalem and the cities ('ir) of Judah, its kings and its princes, to make them a desolation, an astonishment, a hissing, and a curse, as it is this day..."

  • Nahum 3:1 — "Woe to the bloody city ('ir)! It is all full of lies and robbery. Its victim never departs."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The promise that exiles will possess the "cities of the South" correlates closely with Jeremiah's assurances of restoration for the Negev towns (e.g., Jeremiah 32:44). Obadiah's specific mention of these urban centers assures God's people that divine redemption is comprehensive, restoring not only spiritual peace but also tangible community structures, geographic borders, and civic security.
