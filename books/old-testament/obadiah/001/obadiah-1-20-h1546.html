CAPTIVES - ( galut , Strong's H1546 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun galut refers to a group of exiles, a body of captives, or the state of captivity itself. Derived from a root meaning to strip, uncover, or make bare, it paints a vivid picture of a people stripped of their homeland, security, and dignity. In Obadiah 1:20, this term designates the displaced remnants of Israel and Jerusalem. Though currently powerless and exiled in foreign lands, these captives are the subjects of a remarkable promise of restoration, destined to return and possess the territories of their former oppressors.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root verb galah fundamentally means to uncover, lay bare, or reveal. In the ancient world, when a city was conquered, the inhabitants were stripped of their garments, their possessions, and their homeland. They were marched away in forced deportations, entirely exposed to their enemies, the elements, and public humiliation.

• To go into exile was to be laid bare. The noun galut represents the concrete reality of this action. It designates either the state of captivity or the actual group of people who have been uprooted, exposed, and carried away.

• For the ancient Israelite, land was not merely real estate; it was the physical token of God's covenant with Abraham. To be removed from the Promised Land and designated as galut meant a shattering of national security, the loss of the temple, and a profound crisis of identity. It meant living in spiritual and physical dislocation, cut off from the place of covenant blessings.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Galut is a feminine noun that refers to the condition of exile, captivity, or the exile-community as a collective whole.

• The term carries a collective sense, focusing on the community of captives rather than isolated individuals. It represents:

• The state of captivity or being in exile.

• The actual group of exiled people (the captivity).

• The historical deportations of the Northern and Southern Kingdoms.

• In Obadiah 1:20, the word is used twice in its collective sense, identifying "the captives of this host of the children of Israel" and "the captives of Jerusalem." It highlights the historical reality of the dispersed people of God who were scattered throughout the regions of Phoenicia and Asia Minor.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• In Hebrew thought, galut is the antithesis of rest (menuchah) and dwelling securely (yashab). While resting suggests deep roots and fruitful branches, galut depicts a plant torn up by its roots, dried out, and cast aside.

• The term is often used as a collective singular noun. This grammatical feature underscores the shared destiny of the community. In exile, individual distinctions faded; the prince, the priest, and the peasant all shared the singular identity of the galut.

• The word picture also conveys divine exposure. When God sent His people into galut, He removed the hedge of protection that surrounded them, exposing their spiritual nakedness and idolatry to the scrutiny of the surrounding pagan nations.

4. Theological Implications

• The threat of galut stands as the ultimate covenant curse in Deuteronomy 28. It represents a tragic reversal of the Exodus. God had brought His people out of bondage into a land of milk and honey; disobedience, however, resulted in being cast out of the land and sent back into bondage.

• While galut represents the execution of God's holy justice, it also contains the seeds of divine mercy. The preservation of the galut proves that God did not completely destroy His people. He preserved a remnant in the midst of judgment. The exile was a crucible for refining, not an oven for total destruction.

• Furthermore, the prophets consistently declared that the galut did not lie beyond the reach of Yahweh. He went with them into exile, and because He is sovereign over the nations, He alone possessed the authority and power to bring them back.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• Obadiah’s prophecy is directed against Edom, Israel's neighbor and brother nation. When Babylon invaded Jerusalem, Edom did not help Judah; instead, they gloated over their misfortune, stood at the crossroads to cut off fugitives, and actively participated in looting Jerusalem.

• In Obadiah 1:20, the term galut is used to contrast the present humiliation of God's people with their glorious future. To the human eye, the galut of Israel and Jerusalem were weak, forgotten, and utterly defeated.

• However, the prophetic word turns human expectations upside down. The very captives who are currently displaced in distant lands (such as Zarephath and Sepharad) will return to possess the land of Canaan, the cities of the South, and the mount of Esau (Edom). The dispossessed exiles will become the conquerors. God uses the weak things of the world—the helpless galut—to display His sovereignty and establish His kingdom.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The geographical locations mentioned alongside galut in Obadiah 1:20 emphasize the vast and seemingly hopeless distance of the exile. Zarephath was in Phoenician territory to the north, and Sepharad is traditionally identified with Sardis in Asia Minor (modern-day Turkey). This wide scattering made any physical return by human strength an absolute impossibility, highlighting the miraculous nature of God’s eventual restoration.

• In later rabbinic and Jewish history, the word galut became the standard term for the Jewish Diaspora—the ongoing state of living in exile, scattered among the nations, away from the homeland, and awaiting the ultimate messianic redemption.
