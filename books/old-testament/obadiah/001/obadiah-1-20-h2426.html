HOST - ( cheyl , Strong's H2426 )

Summary:

In Obadiah 1:20, the word translated "host" refers to a body of people, specifically a company or army of exiles, though its root meaning carries the strong physical imagery of a fortification, rampart, or defensive wall. In this prophetic context, it depicts the shattered and exiled community of Israel as a defined group that God will miraculously preserve, protect, and restore to their land, transforming a displaced, vulnerable "host" into a secure, thriving people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun cheyl is deeply connected to the concept of defense, strength, and security. In ancient Near Eastern warfare and architecture, a cheyl refers to the low outer wall, bulwark, or rampart built just outside a city’s primary wall to act as the first line of defense. It represents physical protection, stability, and containment. When applied to a group of people, as in Obadiah, it evokes the image of a community that was once fortified but is now displaced. Alternatively, it points to a "host" or force of people who, though presently weak and in exile, are viewed by God as a cohesive, structured unit destined for restoration.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term cheyl possesses a dual range of meaning in biblical Hebrew:

• First, it denotes a physical fortification, specifically a rampart, bulwark, or outer wall protecting a city.

• Second, it refers to an army, host, force, or company of people.

In Obadiah 1:20, the word is used in this second sense to mean a "host" or "company" of captives (the exiled Israelites). It highlights the collective identity of the exiles, identifying them not as scattered, insignificant individuals but as a recognized, cohesive group of God's people.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the majority of its Old Testament occurrences, cheyl is a military or architectural term. It visualizes the physical barrier that keeps enemies at bay. For example, when a city is besieged, the cheyl (rampart) is what the enemy must breach before reaching the main walls.

By translating this defensive noun into a collective description of the captive Israelites, the writer draws a striking word picture. The "host" of captives is a group that has lost its defensive ramparts, yet they themselves remain a distinct, defined "bulwark" of the covenant community in the eyes of Yahweh. They are a displaced army waiting for their divine Commander to lead them home.

4. Theological Implications

The use of cheyl in a context of exile carries profound theological weight. It emphasizes God's sovereign preservation of His people. Though Israel was defeated, stripped of her physical walls, and scattered among the nations, God still identifies them as a "host" (a structured camp or force). They have not dissolved into the surrounding pagan cultures; they retain their covenant identity.

This term also highlights the theme of divine reversal. The vulnerable, captive "host" will possess the land of their former captors. God takes what is broken, defenseless, and exiled, and reconstitutes them as a victorious, possession-inheriting assembly.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:20, the prophet addresses the future of the northern and southern kingdoms. The "captivity of this host of the children of Israel" refers to the exiled northern tribes (or the combined exiles of Israel).

The nuance here is one of identity amidst humiliation. Though they are "captives," they are still called a "host." They are not a forgotten remnant but a registered army of the Lord. The passage promises that this specific group will possess the land of the Canaanites as far as Zarephath, turning their captivity into a triumphant march of reclamation.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Septuagint (LXX) translates cheyl in Obadiah 1:20 using the Greek word arche (meaning "beginning," "ruler," or "domain"), which reflects a desire to emphasize the leadership or first-fruits nature of this specific group of exiles.

• There is a close etymological relationship between cheyl (Strong's H2426 - rampart/host) and chayil (Strong's H2428 - strength, wealth, army). This connection reinforces the idea that even in captivity, this "host" possesses an inherent, God-given strength (chayil) that will be realized in their restoration.
