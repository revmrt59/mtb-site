JERUSALEM - ( yerushalayim , Strong's H3389 )

Summary:

Jerusalem, the historical capital of the southern kingdom of Judah and the designated dwelling place of God’s name, represents the heart of the covenant community. In Obadiah 1:20, "Jerusalem" designates the exiled community from the capital city who, despite their displacement to far-off Sepharad, are promised a complete restoration. The word underscores God’s unyielding commitment to His covenant; though His people were judged and scattered, He identifies them still by their holy city and guarantees their return to inherit their promised land.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The name yerushalayim is ancient, appearing in West Semitic records and Egyptian Execration texts long before David captured the city.

• Linguistically, the name is composed of two primary elements: yaru (meaning "foundation" or "city") and shalem (associated with peace, wholeness, or the Semitic deity of dusk). For the Hebrews, this ancient name was transformed to mean "the foundation of peace" or "city of peace," reflecting the theological reality of shalom—covenant wholeness, safety, and divine order.

• Geographically, Jerusalem sits on a rocky ridge in the Judaean mountains. It was historically difficult to conquer, defended by steep valleys on three sides. It became the capital under King David and the site of the Temple under King Solomon, cementing its status as the earthly footstool of God's presence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The proper noun yerushalayim refers specifically to the capital city of Israel and Judah, situated on the boundary between the tribes of Judah and Benjamin.

• Lexically, the term denotes the center of theocratic governance, the place where God chose to put His name, and the destination of the annual pilgrim feasts.

• In prophetic literature, the noun often functions as a metonymy for the entire nation of Judah or the faithful remnant. To speak of "Jerusalem" is to speak of the covenant people at their spiritual and political core.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Jerusalem is used over 600 times in the Old Testament, making it one of the most prominent geographical and theological terms in Scripture.

• It is pictured as a secure fortress: "As the mountains surround Jerusalem, so the Lord surrounds His people" (Psalm 125:2).

• It is personified as a mother grieving for her exiled children, and later, as a bride being adorned for her husband in the restoration.

• In times of judgment, Jerusalem is described as a "cup of trembling" and a "heavy stone" to the nations that attempt to subjugate her, showing that God's protection of the city remains active even during times of chastisement.

4. Theological Implications

• The theological weight of Jerusalem is rooted in the Davidic Covenant. God chose Zion as His resting place forever, establishing a permanent link between the Davidic dynasty, the temple worship, and the physical city.

• The city serves as a physical witness to God's holiness and His justice. Because Jerusalem was the center of revelation, its citizens were held to a higher standard. Its destruction by the Babylonians demonstrated that God does not tolerate covenant unfaithfulness.

• Conversely, the promised restoration of Jerusalem stands as the ultimate proof of God’s mercy. The survival and return of its captives demonstrate that God's sovereign decrees cannot be thwarted by pagan empires or geographical displacement.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:20, the prophet addresses the tragic aftermath of the Babylonian conquest, during which the neighboring Edomites gloated over and participated in the plunder of Jerusalem.

• The mention of "the captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad" provides a sharp contrast to the tragic dispersion of the exile. Sepharad (historically identified with Sardis in western Asia Minor) represents the extreme boundary of the exile—a place far removed from the covenant land.

• By calling them "the captives of Jerusalem," the text asserts that despite their physical distance and captivity, their identity remains securely anchored to the holy city. They are not forgotten refugees; they are still citizens of the city of God.

• Their promised inheritance—the "cities of the South" (the Negev)—indicates a complete reversal of fortunes. Edom had sought to encroach on Judah’s southern territory, but the returning citizens of Jerusalem will reclaim and possess it.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 122:3: "Jerusalem is built / As a city that is compact together,"

• Psalm 125:2: "As the mountains surround Jerusalem, / So the LORD surrounds His people / From this time forth and forevermore."

• Isaiah 40:2: "Speak comfort to Jerusalem, and cry out to her, / That her warfare is ended, / That her iniquity is pardoned; / For she has received from the LORD’s hand / Double for all her sins."

• Zechariah 12:3: "And it shall happen in that day that I will make Jerusalem a very heavy stone for all peoples; all who would heave it away will surely be cut in pieces, though all nations of the earth are gathered against it."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The identification of Sepharad in Obadiah 1:20 is significant. While later Jewish tradition associated Sepharad with Spain (the origin of Sephardic Jewry), historical and cuneiform evidence links it to Saparda in Asia Minor. This highlights the vast diaspora of the Judaean exiles, making the promise of their return to the Negev a remarkable display of God's gathering power.

• The geographic movement in the verse—from Sepharad in the far north/west back to the Negev in the far south—underscores the total restoration of the land's boundaries, proving that no portion of the promised inheritance will be lost to the enemy.
