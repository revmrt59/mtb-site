POSSESS - ( yarash , Strong's H3423 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yarash carries the powerful, double-edged meaning of dispossessing an enemy in order to take legal and physical possession of their land as a permanent inheritance. It is a covenant-heavy word, used throughout the Old Testament to describe God's sovereign authorization for Israel to claim the Promised Land. In the prophetic context of Obadiah 1:20, yarash signals a dramatic reversal of fortunes: the captive, exiled, and seemingly defeated remnant of Israel will return to actively dispossess their historical oppressors and fully possess the territory God originally swore to their fathers.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, land was not merely a commodity or real estate to be bought and sold; it was the physical manifestation of a family's identity, security, and covenant standing. To be landless was to be vulnerable, cursed, and cut off from the community. The root meaning of yarash presents a vivid, active picture of displacement and relocation. It is not a passive receiving of a gift, but an active stepping into a territory, driving out the current illegal or hostile occupants, and establishing undisputed, permanent residency. The word picture involves a rightful heir dispossessing squatters to claim their rightful heritage.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of yarash is to take possession of, inherit, dispossess, or drive out. It occupies a unique semantic space that bridges military conquest and legal inheritance. The word can mean:

• To take possession of property or land by driving out the previous inhabitants.

• To inherit property or position as a legal heir.

• To become poor or dispossessed (in its causative or passive stems, though the active stems emphasize gaining possession).

The context determines whether the emphasis is on the military act of driving out (dispossessing) or the legal act of occupying (inheriting). Frequently, both aspects are present simultaneously: Israel cannot possess the land without dispossessing the nations under divine judgment.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs yarash in several rich, illustrative ways:

• The Heir's Inheritance: It describes the legal transfer of family property from father to son, as seen in the household of Abraham.

• The Divine Dispossession: It is used of God driving out great and mighty nations before Israel. God is the active agent who dispossesses the wicked, and Israel is the recipient who steps into the vacated space.

• The Permanent Tenant: It refers to settling down permanently in the land. To "possess" the land means to build houses, plant vineyards, and establish a multi-generational legacy.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yarash is deeply intertwined with the faithfulness and sovereignty of God:

• Sovereignty over Nations: The earth is the Lord's, and He has the absolute right to dispossess one nation and give its land to another. Land ownership is always subject to God's moral standards.

• Covenant Faithfulness: The possession of Canaan is the direct fulfillment of the oath God made to Abraham. The land belongs to Israel by divine decree, even when they are temporarily exiled from it due to sin.

• Divine Justice: The expulsion of the Canaanites was not arbitrary; it was an execution of divine judgment due to their extreme wickedness.

• Restoration and Grace: In prophetic literature, yarash becomes a word of hope. Though Israel forfeited their immediate possession of the land through disobedience, God promises a future day of restoration when the exiles will return and possess their inheritance once again.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:20, yarash occurs twice in the context of the restoration of Israel and the judgment of Edom. Historically, Edom had rejoiced over the destruction of Jerusalem and actively participated in looting and seizing Israelite territory. The people of Israel were taken into exile, seemingly dispossessed of everything.

However, Obadiah delivers a message of dramatic reversal. The "captives"—those who are currently powerless, displaced, and landless—will become the conquerors. They will not merely return to their old borders; they will expand. They will possess the territory of the Canaanites as far as Zarephath, and the exiles in Sepharad will possess the cities of the South (the Negev). In this context, yarash guarantees that the remnant of Israel will actively reclaim their covenant inheritance, completely driving out those who occupied it during their absence. What was lost in shame will be repossessed in triumph.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 1:8: "See, I have set the land before you; go in and possess the land which the LORD swore to your fathers—to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob—to give to them and their descendants after them."

• Joshua 1:11: "Pass through the camp and command the people, saying, ‘Prepare provisions for yourselves, for within three days you will cross over this Jordan, to go in to possess the land which the LORD your God is giving you to possess.’"

• Deuteronomy 30:5: "Then the LORD your God will bring you to the land which your fathers possessed, and you shall possess it. He will prosper you and multiply you more than your fathers."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Active Faith: The relationship between God's gift and human responsibility is vividly illustrated by yarash. God had already "given" the land to Israel, yet they still had to go in and "possess" it. Divine sovereignty does not negate the need for active, courageous steps of faith.

• New Testament Connection: The concept of yarash is translated in the Septuagint (LXX) using kleronomeo (to inherit). This bridges to the New Testament truth that believers are heirs of God and co-heirs with Christ, called to possess the spiritual inheritance of the Kingdom of God, which is imperishable, undefiled, and unfading.
