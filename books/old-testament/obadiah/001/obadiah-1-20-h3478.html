ISRAEL - ( Yisra'el , Strong's H3478 )

Summary:

The name Yisra'el (Israel) means "he strives with God" or "God rules." Originating from Jacob’s life-altering encounter with the Angel of the Lord at Peniel, the name shifts from designating a single patriarch to identifying the chosen covenant nation of Yahweh. In Obadiah 1:20, "Israel" represents the enduring covenant community, emphasizing that despite defeat, exile, and the mockery of Edom, God's people remain His possession and are destined for complete restoration, territorial expansion, and ultimate victory.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Yisra'el first appears in Genesis 32:28 during Jacob’s midnight wrestling match at the Jabbok River. Jacob, whose name meant "heel-catcher" or "supplanter," had lived a life characterized by striving with men through deception and self-reliance. In this physical and spiritual crisis, he wrestled with a divine opponent—identified as the Angel of the Lord. Upon refusing to let go until he received a blessing, Jacob was given a new name: Israel.

This name change marked a fundamental shift from a life of manipulative striving with men to a life of spiritual dependence on God. The word became a permanent title for Jacob's descendants, serving as a perpetual reminder of their national identity: they are a people born out of a divine encounter, marked by struggle, and sustained by grace.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper noun Yisra'el is a compound name formed from two primary roots:

  • The verb sarah, which means "to persist," "to exert oneself," "to contend," "to fight," or "to rule."

  • The noun El, the generic Semitic term for "God" or "the strong One."

Combined, the name carries a dual significance. Active translation renders it as "he strives/contends with God," reflecting Jacob's struggle at Peniel. Passive or subjective translation renders it as "God strives" or "let God rule," highlighting God's sovereign initiative on behalf of His people.

Lexically, the term functions in four distinct ways across the Old Testament:

  • The personal name given to the patriarch Jacob.

  • The collective designation for the twelve tribes descended from Jacob's sons (the "children of Israel").

  • The geopolitical designation for the Northern Kingdom during the era of the divided monarchy, distinct from the Southern Kingdom of Judah.

  • The theological designation for the entire covenant people of God, transcending historical divisions, especially in post-exilic and prophetic literature.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs Yisra'el over 2,500 times, making it one of the most significant theological terms in Scripture. The usage of the term goes far beyond simple national identity; it is a repository for God’s covenantal self-disclosure.

Scripture uses several vivid word pictures to describe Israel:

  • The Firstborn Son: God demands Israel's release from Pharaoh by declaring, "Israel is My son, My firstborn" (Exodus 4:22), indicating a relationship of unique affection, privilege, and inheritance.

  • The Vineyard: Israel is portrayed as a vineyard carefully planted, fenced, and tended by God, from whom He expects the fruit of righteousness (Isaiah 5:1-7).

  • The Wife of Yahweh: Hosea and Jeremiah picture Israel as a betrothed wife. Her idolatry is spiritual adultery, yet God promises to woo her back into the wilderness and restore her marriage covenant.

  • The Flock: God is the Shepherd of Israel, leading, feeding, and protecting His sheep from predators (Psalm 80:1).

4. Theological Implications

The theology of Yisra'el is built upon the foundation of sovereign election. God did not choose Israel because of their size, strength, or righteousness, but because of His love and His oath to their forefathers (Deuteronomy 7:7-8).

  • Covenantal Faithfulness: The survival of Israel is the ultimate proof of God's chesed (loyal covenant love). Despite chronic rebellion, idolatry, and subsequent exile, God’s covenant guarantees that Israel cannot be completely cast off.

  • Divine Representation: Israel was designated as a "kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:6). They were meant to be the canvas upon which God painted His character, His laws, and His salvation for the Gentile nations to see.

  • Eschatological Hope: Prophetic theology links the destiny of Israel to the vindication of God’s own name. The restoration of Israel is not merely a political event but a demonstration of God's holiness and power before the eyes of the nations.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:20, the term "Israel" carries a powerful, comforting nuance for a shattered community. Obadiah’s prophecy is directed against Edom, the descendants of Esau, who gloated over, participated in, and profited from the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem.

In this dark context, verse 20 states: "And the captives of this host of the children of Israel / Shall possess the land of the Canaanites / As far as Zarephath..."

Here, the use of "Israel" serves several vital purposes:

  • A Refusal to Recognize Defeat: Geopolitically, the nation was destroyed, the temple burned, and the people carried off as captives. By calling them the "children of Israel," the prophet asserts that their covenant identity remains intact. They are still the people of the Jabbok—destined to survive the struggle.

  • The Rehealing of the Division: By mentioning both "the children of Israel" (traditionally associated with the Northern tribes) and "Jerusalem" (the capital of the Southern Kingdom of Judah) in the same verse, Obadiah looks forward to a reunited nation. The historical wounds of the civil split of 931 BC are healed in God’s restorative future.

  • Reversal of Fortunes: While Edom (Esau) will be utterly cut off and possess nothing, Israel (Jacob) will return from exile to possess the land of their enemies. Their borders will expand far beyond previous limits, demonstrating that the God who strives for them has conquered their captors.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 4:22 - "Then you shall say to Pharaoh, 'Thus says the LORD: "Israel is My son, My firstborn."'"

  • Psalm 121:4 - "Behold, He who keeps Israel / Shall neither slumber nor sleep."

  • Isaiah 41:8 - "But you, Israel, are My servant, / Jacob whom I have chosen, / The offspring of Abraham My friend."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between Jacob (Israel) and Esau (Edom) is the thematic backbone of Obadiah. This sibling rivalry spans biblical history. Esau despised his birthright for immediate, earthly gratification, representing the flesh and worldly pride. Jacob, despite his flaws, valued the spiritual inheritance and was broken by God to receive it.

Obadiah 1:20 demonstrates that those who rely on earthly fortresses (like Edom in the red rocks of Petra) will fall to nothing, while those who are allied with the God of Israel will inherit the kingdom. The ultimate fulfillment of Israel's restoration in Obadiah points forward to the establishment of the kingdom of Yahweh (Obadiah 1:21), which finds its climax in the messianic reign of Jesus Christ.
