CANAANITES - ( Kena'aniy , Strong's H3669 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word Kena'aniy refers to the ethnic and geographical group descending from Canaan, the grandson of Noah, who inhabited the fertile lowlands, coastal plains, and valleys of the ancient Levant prior to the Israelite conquest. Because of their highly developed maritime trade networks, the name also evolved a secondary semantic meaning referring to "merchants" or "traders." In the context of Obadiah 1:20, the word describes the pagan coastal territories stretching north toward Phoenicia. The prophecy declares that the returning exiles of Israel will fully possess this historic territory, demonstrating the absolute faithfulness of God to restore His people to the full boundaries of His original covenant promise.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The Canaanites were descendants of Canaan, the son of Ham, who was cursed after Noah's flood. They settled in the highly desirable coastal strip and Jordan valley of the ancient Near East.

  • Geographically, the term is tied to the lowlands. The root of the word likely relates to being low or humbled, referencing either the low-lying coastal geography they inhabited or a state of subjugation.

  • Culturally and religiously, the Canaanites represent the epitome of pagan idolatry in the Old Testament. Their worship of Baal, Asherah, and Molech involved moral degradation, child sacrifice, and ritual prostitution. This constant proximity to paganism served as Israel’s primary spiritual snare throughout the periods of the Conquest, the Judges, and the Monarchy.

  • Historically, the Phoenicians of the northern coast (including Tyre and Sidon) were direct descendants of these Canaanite groups. Because they dominated Mediterranean trade, the word "Canaanite" became synonymous with wealthy, shrewd merchant classes.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The term Kena'aniy functions primarily as an ethnic gentilic noun, meaning "Canaanite" or "Canaanites."

  • In terms of geographic boundaries, it denotes the inhabitants of the land west of the Jordan River before the conquest of Joshua.

  • In secondary semantic applications, the term functions as a professional noun meaning "merchant," "trader," or "trafficker."

  • In Obadiah 1:20, the word is used in its primary geographic and ethnic sense, designating the coastal territory historically dominated by Canaanite-Phoenician populations, extending north into modern-day Lebanon.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • A Symbol of Spiritual Snare: Throughout scripture, the Canaanites are depicted as a persistent threat to Israel's holiness. Failing to completely drive them out led to the pollution of Israel's worship. Thus, possessing their territory represents the final subduing of spiritual compromise.

  • A Symbol of Worldly Commerce: In the prophetic books, the Canaanite represents the spirit of materialism, greed, and pagan trade practices. Driving the "Canaanite" out represents the purification of God's house from commercial exploitation and worldly values.

  • The Complete Inheritance: In geographic descriptions, "the land of the Canaanites" is used as a benchmark for the complete boundary of the Promised Land. Reclaiming this land is a physical picture of reclaiming everything God has promised, leaving no area in the hands of the enemy.

4. Theological Implications

  • Covenant Restoration: Obadiah’s prophecy emphasizes that God does not abandon His geographic promises. Despite Israel's exile, failure, and apparent destruction, God promises a reversal of fortunes where the dispossessed exiles will dispossess their former conquerors and neighbors.

  • The Geography of Grace: God’s reclamation projects are thorough. By naming the "Canaanites as far as Zarephath," the text shows that God is not just restoring a small, safe remnant to a tiny pocket of land. He is expanding their borders to the absolute limits of the idealized Promised Land, showcasing the abundance of His restoring grace.

  • Sovereignty over History: While Israel seemed utterly defeated by Babylon and mocked by Edom, God remains the ultimate landlord of the earth. He determines the boundaries of the nations and redistributes land according to His holy, sovereign will.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Obadiah 1:20, the prophet addresses the northern exiles ("this host of the children of Israel") and promises they will possess "the land of the Canaanites as far as Zarephath."

  • Zarephath was a Phoenician city situated between Tyre and Sidon, well outside the historical borders of the northern kingdom of Israel's actual habitation, though within the grand boundaries promised to Abraham.

  • This specific detail is highly encouraging: even the pagan strongholds of Baal worship (Zarephath was the very region from which Jezebel came) would be absorbed into the holy inheritance of God's people.

  • This creates a powerful literary contrast: while Edom (the proud mountain dwellers) will be completely cut off and lose their land, Israel (the humbled, exiled captives) will expand and inherit the rich, prosperous coastal plains of the Canaanites.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 12:6: "Abram passed through the land to the place of Shechem, as far as the terebinth tree of Moreh. And the Canaanites were then in the land."

  • Exodus 3:8: "So I have come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians, and to bring them up from that land to a good and large land, to a land flowing with milk and honey, to the place of the Canaanites and the Hittites and the Amorites and the Perizzites and the Hivites and the Jebusites."

  • Proverbs 31:24: "She makes linen garments and sells them, / And supplies sashes for the merchants." (Here, Kena'aniy is translated as "merchants," demonstrating the word's secondary commercial meaning).

  • Zechariah 14:21: "Yes, every pot in Jerusalem and Judah shall be holiness to the LORD of hosts. Everyone who sacrifices shall come and take them and cook in them. In that day there shall no longer be a Canaanite in the house of the LORD of hosts."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The mention of Zarephath in connection with the Canaanites carries deep historical resonance. Zarephath was the location where the prophet Elijah was sent during the great drought, where he was sustained by a poor Gentile widow. By pointing to this region, Obadiah reminds the readers of God’s miraculous provision and His absolute power to sustain His servants even in the heart of pagan, hostile territory.

  • The structure of Obadiah 1:20 pairs the northern exiles with the northern territory (Canaanites/Zarephath) and the southern Jerusalem exiles with the southern territory (the Negev/South). This symmetry guarantees a complete, orderly, and absolute restoration of the entire covenant land from north to south.
