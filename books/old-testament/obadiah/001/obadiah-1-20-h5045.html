SOUTH - ( negeb , Strong's H5045 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word negeb primarily refers to the dry, parched steppe region situated to the south of the Judean hill country, and by extension, it serves as the standard biblical term for the cardinal direction "south." In the prophetic context of Obadiah 1:20, it designates the specific geographic territory of the Negev that had been encroached upon and occupied by Edom during Jerusalem's downfall. The promise that God’s exiled people will return to "possess the cities of the South" is a powerful declaration of total territorial restoration. It assures the faithful that God will reclaim every inch of their lost inheritance, transforming what was once a dry, neglected, and enemy-occupied wilderness back into a flourishing home under His sovereign rule.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun negeb is derived from an unused root meaning "to be dry" or "parched." Geographically, the Negev is the massive, arid wilderness triangle extending south of Beersheba down to the Red Sea. Because this prominent, dry landscape lay directly to the south of Israel's heartland, the Hebrew mind naturally associated the physical region of the dry country with the geographical direction of the south. To look toward the negeb was to look toward the dry, sun-scorched horizon. It is a region characterized by limestone hills, deep ravines, and sparse rainfall, requiring deep wells and divine seasonal rains for survival.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In biblical Hebrew, negeb carries a dual significance depending on the context:

• The Geographical Negev: A specific, defined territory in the southern portion of Judah's inheritance.

• The Cardinal Direction: "South" or "southward." Whenever writers needed to indicate the southern compass point, they directed their readers toward the negeb.

• Arid Land: A designation for dry, parched soil that requires irrigation or seasonal water sources to sustain life.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term negeb is used frequently to describe the journeys of the patriarchs, the boundaries of the Promised Land, and the eventual restoration of Israel.

• The Wilderness of Testing: The Negev was the transition zone between Egypt and the fertile hills of Canaan. Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob frequently journeyed through this arid landscape. It represents a place of physical vulnerability where God's people had to rely completely on His provision of water and security.

• The Dryness of the Soul: Because the Negev is naturally parched, it serves as a powerful metaphor for spiritual dryness and captivity. The "streams in the South" (Psalm 126:4) represents a sudden, miraculous flash flood of blessing and restoration that rushes through dry, dusty desert ravines, revitalizing everything in its path.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of negeb rests on God’s covenant faithfulness. When Israel fell into sin, their enemies plundered their territories, and the southern region became vulnerable to foreign occupation—most notably by the Edomites. The loss of the negeb was a physical manifestation of the curse of exile.

Conversely, when God promises the reclamation of the negeb, He is promising a complete restoration. God does not merely restore the easy, fertile lands; He reclaims the dry, difficult, and contested borders. It demonstrates that under God's kingdom, no part of our inheritance is permanently lost to the enemy, and no dry wilderness is beyond His power to reclaim and populate.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:20, the prophet addresses the survivors of Jerusalem who were carried away into exile (Sepharad, likely Sardis in Asia Minor). During the Babylonian invasion, the Edomites had gloated over Judah’s disaster, assisted the invaders, and physically moved into the vacated southern territories of Judah (the Negev).

By declaring that "the captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad shall possess the cities of the South," Obadiah delivers a message of dramatic reversal. Edom’s prideful expansion will be dismantled, and the dispossessed exiles will return to inherit the very lands their enemies tried to steal. The phrase "cities of the South" underscores that even the most remote, dry, and heavily disputed frontier cities of God’s covenant land will be fully reoccupied and governed by His people.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Near Eastern geography, orientation was determined by facing the rising sun (East). Therefore, the left was North, the right was South, and behind was West. The term negeb provided a concrete, local geographic anchor for the right-hand direction (South), reminding the Israelites daily of their specific geographic location on the globe and their proximity to the wilderness where God had preserved them for forty years. In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), negeb is frequently translated using the Greek word notos, which represents the south wind or the southern sky.
