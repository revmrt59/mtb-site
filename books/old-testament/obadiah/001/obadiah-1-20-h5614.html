SEPHARAD - ( Sepharad , Strong's H5614 )

Summary:

Sepharad is a proper noun occurring only once in the Hebrew Bible, designating a specific location of exilic captivity for the displaced inhabitants of Jerusalem. Historical-grammatical evidence identifies it with Sparda (Sardis) in Asia Minor, while later Jewish tradition associated it with the Iberian Peninsula (Spain). In the context of Obadiah's prophecy, Sepharad represents the extreme, distant limits of the Jewish dispersion, highlighting the miraculous scope of God's restoration, where even the most remote captives will return to inherit their promised land.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • Sepharad is a geographic place name of significant historical interest.

  • Old Persian cuneiform inscriptions from the reign of Darius I list Sparda (corresponding to the satrapy of Lydia in Asia Minor, with its capital at Sardis) as a conquered territory. This location was a major administrative and cultural hub under the Persian Empire.

  • During the exilic period, captives from Jerusalem were deported to this distant northern and western region, separating them from their homeland by hundreds of miles of rugged terrain and sea.

  • Over time, the historical memory of this location shifted. By the early medieval period, Jewish commentators and translators associated Sepharad with the Iberian Peninsula (modern-day Spain and Portugal). This association gave rise to the term "Sephardic" to designate Jews of Spanish or North African descent, contrasting with "Ashkenazic" (Germanic/Eastern European) Jews.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • Sepharad is a proper locative noun.

  • The primary lexical meaning refers to Sardis, a region and prominent city in western Asia Minor.

  • It designates a far-off land of captivity and diaspora for the Judean exiles.

  • The word is transliterated directly into English as "Sepharad."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • As a geographical marker of exile, Sepharad serves as a word picture of extreme displacement, isolation, and systemic vulnerability.

  • To be an exile in Sepharad was to be at the outer limits of the known civilized world, completely cut off from the covenant land, the temple, and the visual reminders of Yahweh's presence.

  • It illustrates the deep pain of the diaspora—scattered, marginalized, and seemingly forgotten in a foreign empire.

4. Theological Implications

  • Sovereignty over the Diaspora: Even though God's people are scattered to the furthest corners of the earth, they are never lost to Him. Yahweh maintains a precise ledger of His exiles, knowing exactly who is "in Sepharad."

  • The Certainty of Restoration: The geographic distance of the exile cannot hinder divine restoration. The covenant faithfulness of Yahweh guarantees that even the most distant captives will return to possess their inheritance.

  • The Reversal of Fortune: The passage depicts a dramatic reversal where those who were powerless captives in distant lands will return to govern and possess the very lands of their former oppressors.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Obadiah 1:20, the prophet addresses the humiliation of Judah at the hands of Edom during the fall of Jerusalem. Edom had gloated over Judah's disaster and assisted the Babylonians in capturing and plundering the survivors.

  • Obadiah contrasts the total destruction of Edom with the triumphant restoration of Israel.

  • While Edom will be cut off forever, the scattered exiles of Israel—ranging from those near Zarephath (Phoenicia) in the north to those far away in Sepharad (Asia Minor) in the west—will return.

  • The returning captives from Sepharad will specifically "possess the cities of the South" (the Negev), representing a complete reoccupation and dominion over the southern territories that Edom had sought to usurp.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because Sepharad is a hapax legomenon (a word that occurs only once in the entire Hebrew Bible), there are no other verses that use this exact Hebrew word. Its sole occurrence is:

  • Obadiah 1:20 — "And the captives of this host of the children of Israel shall possess the Canaanites as far as Zarephath; and the captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad shall possess the cities of the South."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The linguistic shift from Sparda (Sardis) to Spain in Jewish history was likely influenced by phonetic similarities and the desire of Jewish communities in the Iberian Peninsula to trace their ancestry to the ancient, noble exiles of Jerusalem mentioned by Obadiah.

  • Targum Jonathan (an ancient Aramaic translation and commentary) translates Sepharad as Aspamia, which is the Aramaic word for Spain, cementing this tradition early in rabbinic literature.

  • This single geographic term bridges ancient Near Eastern history with the long-term identity and migration patterns of the Jewish people throughout the global diaspora.
