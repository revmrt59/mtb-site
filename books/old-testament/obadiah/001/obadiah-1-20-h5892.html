CITIES - ( 'ir , Strong's H5892 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ir refers to a fortified, permanent settlement, contrasting with open, vulnerable villages or temporary nomadic tents. In the context of Obadiah 1:20, the promise that returning exiles will possess the "cities of the South" represents a complete, secure, and permanent restoration of God's people to their covenant inheritance. Even the arid, vulnerable borderlands of the Negev will be transformed into fortified strongholds of peace and divine order under Yahweh's ultimate reign.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, an 'ir was far more than a mere cluster of dwellings; it was a symbol of security, social order, and permanence. Unlike open villages (chatser) or nomadic tents, an 'ir was characterized by defensive fortifications—most notably surrounding walls, towers, and heavily guarded gates. To live within a city meant protection from wild beasts, marauding bandits, and invading armies. The city gate served as the civic center where elders gathered, legal transactions were finalized, and covenant promises were preserved. Thus, the concept of a city is deeply tied to the ideas of safety, community, and established authority.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'ir refers to a permanent, walled, or fortified settlement. It denotes a populated center, ranging from small fortified watchtower outposts to massive royal capitals. The term emphasizes structural security and communal organization. In its plural form ('arim), it encompasses the network of fortified population centers within a specific territory or province. Lexically, the word stands in sharp contrast to the temporary, nomadic lifestyle of the wilderness, representing instead a settled, anchored existence.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, 'ir is used in several vivid ways:

• The City of God: Jerusalem is frequently designated as the 'ir of Yahweh (the city of God), representing the earthly dwelling place of His divine presence, glory, and justice.

• A Picture of Self-Control: A person without self-discipline is compared to a "city broken down, without walls," illustrating vulnerability to ruin and spiritual defeat.

• Cities of Refuge: These specialized cities served as places of asylum and legal protection, highlighting the city as an instrument of divine justice and mercy.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theological frameworks regarding cities in Scripture are dualistic. While the first city was built by Cain in rebellion, God redeems the concept of the city by choosing Jerusalem as His dwelling and preparing a heavenly city (the New Jerusalem) for His people.

• Restoration from Exile: When God promises to restore His people to their cities, He is promising a transition from wandering to inheritance, from vulnerability to safety, and from shame to glory.

• Covenant Faithfulness: Rebuilding and inhabiting cities is a concrete sign of covenant fulfillment, showing that God's people are no longer scattered outcasts but are established under His sovereign protection.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:20, the prophet speaks to the exiles of Israel who have suffered displacement and loss. The "South" (Negev) was historically a dry, barren, and highly vulnerable frontier region, constantly threatened by Edomite incursions.

• To "possess the cities of the South" means that the most remote, desolate, and contested borders of Israel’s inheritance will be completely reclaimed and fortified.

• God is not merely returning His people to live in temporary shelters or ruined fields; He is restoring them to fully functioning, secure, and inhabited cities.

• This demonstrates the thoroughness of God’s restorative justice: what was once laid waste and occupied by enemies will become an secure stronghold of Yahweh's kingdom.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Numbers 35:6 - "Now among the cities which you give to the Levites there shall be six cities of refuge, which you shall appoint for the slayer, that he may flee there; and to these you shall add forty-two cities."

• Joshua 6:3 - "You shall march around the city, all you men of war; you shall go all around the city once. This you shall do six days."

• Psalm 46:4 - "There is a river whose streams shall make glad the city of God, The holy place of the tabernacle of the Most High."

• Proverbs 25:28 - "Whoever has no rule over his own spirit Is like a city broken down, without walls."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between the "captives" (galut) and the "cities" ('arim) in Obadiah 1:20 highlights a profound spiritual reversal. Captivity implies displacement, chains, and transience. Cities imply settlement, ownership, and strength. This juxtaposition assures the broken-hearted believer that God’s ultimate plan is always to move His people from a state of vulnerable exile into a position of secure, fortified inheritance.
