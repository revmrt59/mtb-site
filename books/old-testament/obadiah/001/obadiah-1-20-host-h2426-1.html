HOST - (chêyl, Strong's H2426)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun chēl denotes an outer wall, rampart, or protective fortification, as well as a collective body, force, host, or army. In Obadiah 1:20, the word is translated "host" and designates a company or group of Israelite exiles. Within Obadiah's prophetic announcement of judgment upon Edom and restoration for Israel, chēl identifies this specific group of displaced exiles who, despite their present captivity, are appointed by Yahweh to return and inherit their assigned territory.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Book of Obadiah is a focused prophetic response to Edom's pride and opportunistic hostility during a time of Jerusalem's distress. When foreign invaders breached Judah's defenses, the Edomites rejoiced over Israel's downfall, cut off fugitives, and participated in plunder. Obadiah delivers a divine sentence of complete judgment against Edom while announcing the eventual vindication and restoration of Yahweh's covenant people.

The closing vision of the book contrasts the utter destruction of Esau (Edom) with the expansive restoration of Jacob. In Obadiah 1:19–21, the prophet outlines the territorial repossession that will accompany Israel's restoration. Verse 20 specifically identifies displaced exile groups who will participate in this return, using chēl to designate the captive Israelite force that will reclaim land in Canaan.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, the noun chēl exhibits two main senses in Biblical Hebrew:

  • An architectural sense referring to an outer fortification, rampart, or low defense wall positioned outside a city's main inner wall.

  • A collective sense referring to a military force, body of troops, host, or detachment.

In Obadiah 1:20, chēl appears in the masculine singular absolute form and functions in this second, collective sense. It refers to "this host of the children of Israel." The lexical meaning here is a body, host, or company of people, rather than a physical stone fortification.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, chēl is used to describe both urban defensive structures and human forces:

  • In architectural contexts, chēl describes the defensive rampart or outer wall of a city, often mentioned alongside the main wall (chōmāh).

  • In military contexts, chēl refers to a host, army, or armed force sent on an expedition or stationed for defense.

In Obadiah 1:20, the word is deployed in this collective human sense. It designates the exiled company of Israelites currently living among the Canaanites. The word identifies them as a distinct host or group within the prophetic narrative of return and repossession.

4. Theological Implications

  • Sovereign Recognition of the Dispersed: Though scattered and living in exile, Israel is not forgotten by Yahweh. By designating them as a "host" (chēl), the prophetic text emphasizes that God still recognizes and accounts for His displaced people.

  • Fulfillment of Covenant Promises: Obadiah’s prophecy connects the return of this host to the broader theme of God's sovereign rule. The text concludes with the declaration that "the kingdom shall be the Lord's" (Obadiah 1:21). The exiled host serves as an instrument through which Yahweh fulfills His historical and territorial promises.

  • Divine Reversal of Fortune: While proud nations rely on physical fortifications that ultimately fail, Yahweh reverses the condition of His exiled host, taking them from captivity to territorial possession.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:20, the syntax sets up a parallel description of two exiled groups: "the captivity of this host [chēl] of the children of Israel" and "the captivity of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad."

The noun chēl appears in the masculine singular absolute state. Grammatically, it functions as a collective noun identifying the exiled community as a single body or group. The text itself clarifies what this host will do: they "shall possess the land of the Canaanites as far as Zarephath."

The immediate context establishes that despite foreign conquest and exile, this group's displacement is temporary. The prophet does not describe them as a literal military force conquering by human power, but rather as a host designated by divine promise to inherit the land under Yahweh's sovereign restoration.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Kings 21:23 — "The dogs shall eat Jezebel by the wall [chēl] of Jezreel."

  • 2 Kings 18:17 — "Then the king of Assyria sent... the Rabshakeh from Lachish to King Hezekiah, with a great army [chēl] against Jerusalem."

  • Isaiah 26:1 — "We have a strong city; God will appoint salvation for walls and bulwarks [chēl]."

  • Lamentations 2:8 — "Therefore He caused rampart [chēl] and wall to lament; they languished together."

  • Psalms 122:7 — "Peace be within your walls [chēl], prosperity within your palaces."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Relationship to Chayil: Etymologically and lexically, chēl is closely connected to chayil, a broad Hebrew term meaning strength, military force, or wealth. Both words share an underlying association with strength and defense, manifested either as physical ramparts or as organized forces.

  • Clear Distinctions in Exegesis: Maintaining a clear distinction between the architectural sense ("rampart/wall") and the collective sense ("host/army") prevents transferring structural metaphors into passages where the word simply refers to a group of people. In Obadiah 1:20, the word identifies the exiled group itself, not an architectural feature.
