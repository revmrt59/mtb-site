JERUSALEM - (Yᵉrûwshâlaim, Strong's H3389)

Summary:

Lexically, Yerushalaim is a proper noun identifying the ancient city of Jerusalem, the capital of the kingdom of Judah and the spiritual and administrative center of Israel. In Obadiah 1:20, the term designates the displaced exile community originative of the holy city ("the captivity of Jerusalem"). In this context, it functions to identify the specific covenant community that, despite current displacement in distant lands like Sepharad, is guaranteed full territorial restoration and inheritance in the southern lands of Israel under God's sovereign rule.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The proper noun Yerushalaim denotes the prominent ridge city situated in the Judean mountains, historically captured by David from the Jebusites and established as the throne city of the Davidic dynasty and the central site of Yahweh's sanctuary. Throughout Old Testament history, Jerusalem was not merely an urban capital but the primary geographical center of covenantal worship and royal governance. In the prophetic book of Obadiah, the city forms the historical and theological backdrop of the oracle. Obadiah responds to Edom's opportunistic gloating and violence during Jerusalem's devastation and exile. The prophet contrasts Edom's pride and imminent destruction with the ultimate vindication, return, and expansion of the exiled remnant associated with Jerusalem.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Yerushalaim is a proper geographical noun designating the historic city of Jerusalem. Lexically, the term serves as a specific place name rather than a conceptual abstract. It identifies the historic city, its environs, or metonymically its inhabitants and exile population. In standard lexical resources (such as BDB and TWOT), it denotes the capital city of Judah where the temple was situated and where the Davidic kings reigned.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Hebrew narrative, poetry, and prophecy, Yerushalaim carries distinct contextual nuances depending on the discourse setting:

  • Administrative and Royal Center: In historical narratives, it functions as the seat of Davidic kingship and administrative authority.

  • Sanctuary and Cultic Locus: In the Psalms and prophetic literature, the city is identified with Mount Zion, the earthly dwelling place of Yahweh's glory and the destination of pilgrim assemblies.

  • Object of Covenant Warning and Judgment: Prophets frequently address Jerusalem as a personified entity when reproving idolatry, social injustice, and covenant unfaithfulness, signaling incoming judgment via siege and exile.

  • Focus of Eschatological Restoration: In exilic and post-exilic prophetic visions, Jerusalem represents the core site of divine restoration, security, and global spiritual light.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Yerushalaim embodies the intersection of God's covenant promises with human history. It is the place Yahweh chose to place His name and the central stage for the unfoldment of the Davidic covenant. When the city fell to foreign conquerors, it demonstrated that God's moral righteousness supersedes mere national or spatial security; ritual proximity to the temple could not protect a rebellious people. Conversely, the prophetic insistence on Jerusalem's future restoration underscores God's unbreakable covenant faithfulness. The ultimate restoration of the community linked to Jerusalem highlights the triumph of divine grace over historical judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:20, Yerushalaim occurs in the singular number and absolute state as part of a construct phrase translated "the captivity of Jerusalem." Here, the name functions metonymically to refer not to the physical stone walls or ruined architecture of the city, but to its exiled population—specifically those exiles residing in Sepharad.

The immediate context contrasts the arrogance and eventual dispossession of Edom (Esau) with the complete reversal of fortune for the covenant people (Jacob). Obadiah specifies two main exilic groups who will participate in this restoration: the host of Israel from the northern territories and the captivity of Jerusalem located in far-off regions. By naming Jerusalem explicitly, the prophet reassures the readers that the displaced population of the royal city has not been forgotten or severed from God's promise. Instead, these very exiles are designated as the heirs who will possess the cities of the Negev ("the South"). Thus, the term reinforces the certainty of covenant inheritance and territorial reversal.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The literary structure of Obadiah moves from severe doom against Edom for its violence against Judah to a dramatic vision of restoration centered on Mount Zion and the exiles of Jerusalem. While Edom's mountains are handed over to inheritance by Israel's remnant, Jerusalem's displaced sons are restored to their surrounding regional territories. This placement demonstrates the thematic unity of the book: human proud kingdoms fall under divine judgment, whereas the kingdom associated with Yahweh's named city endures and expands.
