ZAREPHATH - (Tsârᵉphath, Strong's H6886)

Summary:

Lexically, tzorfat (Zarephath) is a proper noun designating a Phoenician coastal city located between Tyre and Sidon. In Obadiah 1:20, the word serves as a geographic boundary marker defining the northern extent of the land that the restored Israelite captives will possess. By establishing Zarephath as the northern limit, the prophet communicates that God's future restoration of His exiled people will not merely return them to their former borders, but will expand their inheritance deep into lands historically dominated by Gentile nations.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

Tzorfat (Zarephath) was an ancient Phoenician coastal town situated on the Mediterranean coast between Sidon and Tyre (in modern-day Lebanon). Positioned outside the historic covenant borders of the twelve tribes of Israel, Zarephath belonged culturally and politically to the Phoenician domain of Sidon.

In Israelite history, Zarephath was best known as the refuge to which God sent the prophet Elijah during the severe famine under King Ahab (1 Kings 17). There, outside the borders of Israel, God sustained Elijah through a Gentile widow whose meager supplies of flour and oil were miraculously multiplied.

In the prophetic vision of Obadiah, the historic context shifts from drought and exile to eschatological vindication and territorial recovery. Writing in the aftermath of Jerusalem's humiliation and Edom's treacherous complicity in Judah's plunder, Obadiah announces the righteous judgment of Yahweh upon Edom and promises the comprehensive restoration of God's covenant people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, tzorfat functions as a proper geographic noun identifying the specific settlement of Zarephath (rendered Sarepta in Greek translations and the New Testament).

Etymologically, the name is related to the Hebrew verbal root tsaraf, which carries the primary meaning of smelting, refining, or testing metals through fire. While the historical origin of the town's name likely reflected local industrial activity such as metalworking or glassmaking, as a proper place name in biblical Hebrew it functions strictly as a geographic identifier.

Grammatically, in the target occurrence of Obadiah 1:20, the proper noun tzorfat is parsed as singular in the absolute state. It appears following the preposition 'ad ("as far as"), designating the terminal point of a geographic region.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The proper noun tzorfat appears in two distinct genres within the Hebrew Scriptures: narrative history and prophetic promise.

In narrative history (1 Kings 17), Zarephath represents a refuge in Gentile territory. During a time of widespread apostasy and drought within Israel, Yahweh demonstrated His sovereign lordship over nature and foreign lands by maintaining His prophet in Zarephath. The city thus carries a narrative nuance of unexpected divine provision and sovereignty beyond the borders of Israel.

In prophetic restoration literature (Obadiah 1:20), tzorfat carries a geographic and scope-defining nuance. It does not refer to a place of flight or refuge, but rather to a boundary marker for kingdom expansion. Positioned at the far northern reach of the Phoenician coast, it signifies that the dispossession of Israel's captors will culminate in the full realization of the promised land's northern limits.

4. Theological Implications

The inclusion of Zarephath in the restoration vision of Obadiah establishes several key theological truths:

  • Divine Sovereignty over Land and Nations: Yahweh is not a localized deity whose jurisdiction ends at Israel's political borders. By assigning Zarephath as part of the restored remnant's possession, the text affirms God's absolute right to reallocate territories according to His sovereign redemptive purpose.

  • Reversal of Oppression and Exile: Exile involved the stripping away of Israel's land and heritage. The promise that captives will possess territory stretching as far as Zarephath showcases the radical nature of divine restoration—replacing dispossession with extraordinary territorial inheritance.

  • Extension Beyond Historic Realities: Throughout much of Israel's monarchy, northern Phoenician cities like Zarephath remained outside practical Israelite control. Its inclusion in the prophetic horizon highlights that God's ultimate kingdom promises exceed the historical achievements of Israel's earthly monarchies.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:20 occurs within the final oracle of the book (Obadiah 1:17-21), which contrasts the total destruction of the house of Esau (Edom) with the holiness, deliverance, and expansion of the house of Jacob and Joseph on Mount Zion.

In verse 20, the prophet outlines a dual movement of territorial recovery for two groups of exiles:

1. "The captivity of this host of the children of Israel shall possess the land of the Canaanites as far as Zarephath."

2. "The captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad shall possess the cities of the South [Negev]."

The immediate syntax creates a vast geographic perimeter. By pairing tzorfat in the far north with the Negev in the far south, the passage constructs a comprehensive north-to-south sweep of total recovery. The immediate context clarifies that tzorfat marks the northern limit of "the land of the Canaanites" being reclaimed by the restored host. The spatial designation emphasizes that no portion of the promised heritage will remain lost or dominated by hostile powers when Yahweh establishes His kingdom (Obadiah 1:21).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Kings 17:9 — "Arise, go to Zarephath, which belongs to Sidon, and dwell there. See, I have commanded a widow there to provide for you."

  • 1 Kings 17:10 — "So he arose and went to Zarephath. And when he came to the gate of the city, indeed, a widow was there gathering sticks."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Luke 4:25-26, Jesus draws upon the historical memory of Zarephath (using the Greek form Sarepta) during His inaugural sermon at Nazareth. Jesus notes that during the three-and-a-half-year drought in Israel, Elijah was sent to none of the widows in Israel, but exclusively to a Gentile woman in Zarephath of Sidon.

Understanding the prophetic role of Zarephath in Obadiah 1:20 enriches this New Testament connection. Zarephath represents both the historical locus where God extended His life-giving word to a Gentile outsider during Israel's apostasy, and the prophetic boundary mark where God promised His redeemed people would exercise kingdom inheritance. In both contexts, Zarephath stands as a monumental signpost that divine grace and kingdom authority consistently break through humanly erected national and geographic boundaries.
