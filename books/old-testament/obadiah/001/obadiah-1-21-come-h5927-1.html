COME - (ʻâlâh, Strong's H5927)

Summary:

Lexically, ʿalah means to go up, ascend, climb, or mount. In the context of ancient Near Eastern geography and Old Testament theology, spatial movement toward topographically elevated sites—most notably Jerusalem and Mount Zion—was regularly expressed by this verb. In Obadiah 1:21, ʿalah describes deliverers who ascend Mount Zion to govern and judge. The verb marks the triumphant climax of Obadiah's prophecy, contrasting Edom's arrogant self-elevation with the God-ordained elevation of His righteous champions upon His holy mountain, culminating in the complete establishment of Yahweh's kingdom rule.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root ʿalah is an essential motion verb in ancient Hebrew, denoting movement from a lower location or state to a higher one. Geographically, the terrain of Israel heavily influenced the usage of the term. Traveling to Jerusalem or Mount Zion invariably required ascending from surrounding lower regions such as the Jordan Valley, the coastal plain, or the desert. Consequently, going to the central sanctuary to worship, fight, or govern was idiomatic for "going up."

In cultural and religious contexts, physical elevation carried spiritual significance. Altars were elevated platforms, high places dominated the hills, and God's dwelling place on Mount Zion sat atop the holy mountain. Movement toward God's presence, temple, or royal court consistently employed ʿalah.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, ʿalah signifies physical ascension: to go up, mount, climb, or rise. The lexical scope encompasses a wide array of contextual applications:

  • Physical movement from a lower geographic elevation to a higher one (e.g., traveling up to the hill country).

  • Upward motion of inanimate elements, such as smoke ascending from an altar or waters overflowing banks.

  • Military maneuvers where troops ascend to invade, capture, or fortify a city or mountain stronghold.

  • Sacrificial activity where offerings go up as smoke to God.

  • Institutional or judicial ascension to positions of authority or prominent legal standing.

In the Qal stem, the verb describes the subject directly engaging in the simple action of ascending or going up.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament narratives, poetry, and prophecy, ʿalah functions as both a concrete spatial verb and a covenantal indicator.

  • Spatial and Historical Journey: The exodus from Egypt into the Promised Land is repeatedly described as "going up" out of Egypt into Canaan, framing redemption as an upward trajectory into God's provision.

  • Worship and Sacred Space: Sacrifices burning on the altar ascend toward heaven. Pilgrims participating in seasonal feasts "go up" to Jerusalem, reflecting both physical climbing and spiritual approach to Yahweh's residence.

  • Royal and Judicial Authority: Approaching a monarch, mounting a judicial seat, or taking control of a territory is framed as an ascension.

4. Theological Implications

Theological significance attaches to ʿalah based on the destination of the ascent. Mount Zion is presented throughout Scripture as the earthly footstool of Yahweh's heavenly throne. Ascending Zion is not merely a topographical movement; it signifies entering the domain of divine authority, righteousness, and order.

Scripture maintains a sharp theological distinction between human self-elevation and divine ascension. When humans attempt to exalt themselves in pride, God brings them low. Conversely, those whom God raises up ascend under divine sanction to execute His justice. The ultimate trajectory of redemptive history envisions the righteous ascending God's holy hill to participate in the manifest establishment of His universal kingdom.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:21 serves as the final, triumphant resolution of the prophecy of Obadiah. The vision begins with Edom’s arrogant presumption: Edom dwelt in the high clefts of the rock and boasted, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" (Obadiah 1:3). Yahweh promised to bring Edom down in judgment.

In Obadiah 1:21, the verb ʿalah appears in the Qal stem, perfect form, third-person plural. The perfect form presents this future prophetic act as a whole, completed reality within the vision. The subjects carrying out the action are "saviors" or "deliverers"—champions raised up by God to liberate His people and execute righteousness.

These deliverers "shall go up" (ʿalah) on Mount Zion. Unlike Edom, whose elevation was self-manufactured pride, these deliverers ascend Mount Zion under divine authority. Their ascent to Zion serves two distinct contextual functions:

1. To judge and govern the mountains of Esau (Edom), proving that false earthly strongholds cannot withstand divine justice.

2. To declare that ultimate sovereignty belongs not to earthly nations, but to Yahweh: "and the kingdom shall be the LORD’s."

The verb ʿalah here establishes Mount Zion as the supreme seat of divine jurisdiction from which righteousness goes forth to subdue surrounding nations.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 13:18 – "So God led the people around by way of the wilderness of the Red Sea. And the children of Israel went up in orderly ranks out of the land of Egypt."

  • Exodus 19:18 – "Now Mount Sinai was completely in smoke, because the LORD descended upon it in fire. Its smoke ascended like the smoke of a furnace, and the whole mountain quaked greatly."

  • Psalms 104:8 – "They went up by the mountains; they went down to the valleys, to the place which You founded for them."

  • Amos 2:10 – "Also it was I who brought you up from the land of Egypt, and led you forty years through the wilderness, to possess the land of the Amorite."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural symmetry of Obadiah is grounded in the contrast of height and ascent. Edom's prideful dwelling on the heights of Seir led to complete humiliation, whereas God's deliverers ascending the heights of Mount Zion brings about everlasting kingdom rule. The verse highlights that true judicial power and elevation originate exclusively from Yahweh's presence on Zion.
