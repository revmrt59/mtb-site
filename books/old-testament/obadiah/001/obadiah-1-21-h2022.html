MOUNTAINS - ( har , Strong's H2022 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun har refers to a mountain, hill, or mountainous region, serving as a primary geographical and theological landmark throughout the Old Testament. In the prophetic text of Obadiah 1:21, it establishes a powerful contrast between "Mount Zion"—the low, humble hill chosen as the dwelling place of God's presence and the source of salvation—and "the mountains of Esau," the lofty, seemingly impregnable rock fortresses of Edom that symbolized human pride, self-sufficiency, and rebellion against God. Ultimately, this word underscores the supreme sovereignty of God, who humbles the proud heights of human kingdoms and exalts His own holy mountain to establish His eternal, visible kingdom on earth.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, mountains were far more than mere geological formations; they were loaded with spiritual and military significance. Physically, the land of Israel and its neighbors was defined by rugged ridges, deep ravines, and high peaks. Mountains served as natural fortresses, offering safety from invading armies. The Edomites, descendants of Esau, inhabited the rugged, mountainous territory of Mount Seir, south of the Dead Sea. This region, characterized by steep red sandstone cliffs and high altitude peaks, made Edom virtually impregnable to ancient warfare. Because of this natural, mountainous terrain, the Edomites developed a deeply ingrained attitude of pride and self-reliance, believing no enemy could ever bring them down. In contrast, Mount Zion, though topographically modest compared to the towering peaks of Hermon or the rugged heights of Seir, was the mountain of divine choice—the place where Yahweh established His temple and chose to dwell among His people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew word har refers to a mountain, a hill, or a mountainous range. It is derived from an unused root meaning to loom up or project highly. The word can denote a single, specific peak, such as Mount Sinai, Mount Hor, or Mount Zion. It is also frequently used in the collective plural or construct state to describe an entire mountainous district or hill country, such as the "hill country of Ephraim" or the "mountains of Judah." Lexically, the term emphasizes elevation, visibility, and permanence. It stands in contrast to the valleys (emeq) and plains (mishor). In prophetic literature, the physical loftiness of the har is frequently employed as a metaphor for majesty, governmental power, and spiritual high places.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, har is used to construct several powerful theological word pictures:

  • Places of Divine Encounter: Mountains are the primary locations where God reveals Himself, covenants with His people, and demands worship. Mount Sinai is the mountain of law, while Mount Moriah (and later Zion) is the mountain of sacrifice and temple worship.

  • Symbols of Human Pride: Lofty mountains represent the arrogance of nations that lift themselves up against the Creator. The "high mountains" are frequently targeted in judgments where God promises to bring down everything that is proud and lofty.

  • Symbols of Stability: Because of their massive size and deep foundations, mountains represent stability and permanence. Yet, even the mountains tremble, melt, or are thrown into the sea at the presence of Yahweh, demonstrating His superior power.

  • Governmental Kingdoms: In prophetic dreams and visions, a mountain represents a kingdom, empire, or ruling authority. The mountain of God's house represents the global, visible rule of the Messiah.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of har centers on the absolute sovereignty of God over both physical creation and human history. God is the one who "forms the mountains" (Amos 4:13), and they belong entirely to Him. When God chooses "Mount Zion" as His holy hill, He demonstrates that divine election is not based on worldly power, height, or grandeur. Zion was smaller and less imposing than other regional mountains, yet God chose it to reveal that true security and salvation come from Him alone, not from military heights. Furthermore, the ultimate destiny of the cosmos involves a final ordering of these "mountains." The kingdoms of the world (symbolized by proud mountains) will be brought low, and the mountain of the Lord's house will be established as supreme over all other heights, showing that God's kingdom is the only immovable and eternal reality.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:21, the term har is used twice in two contrasting ways: "Mount Zion" (har tsiyyon) and "the mountains of Esau" (har esav). This contrast is the climax of Obadiah's prophecy. Throughout the book, Edom (Esau) is condemned for boasting in the loftiness of its dwelling, hiding "in the clefts of the rock" and asking, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" God answers that even if they make their nest as high as the eagle, He will bring them down. In verse 21, the reversal is complete. "Saviors" or deliverers will ascend Mount Zion—the place of mercy and God’s presence—to execute judgment upon the mountains of Esau. The very heights in which Edom trusted for safety will become the place of their ultimate defeat and judgment. The final phrase, "And the kingdom shall be the LORD’s," seals this truth: human empires and proud fortresses will fail, and only the mountain of God's presence and covenant faithfulness will stand.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 19:11 - "And be ready for the third day. For on the third day the LORD will come down upon Mount Sinai in the sight of all the people."

  • Psalm 2:6 - "Yet I have set My King On My holy hill of Zion."

  • Psalm 121:1 - "I will lift up my eyes to the hills—From whence comes my help?"

  • Isaiah 2:2 - "Now it shall come to pass in the latter days That the mountain of the LORD’s house Shall be established on the top of the mountains, And shall be exalted above the hills; And all nations shall flow to it."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In prophetic and apocalyptic literature, there is a consistent transition from the physical mountains of geography to the spiritual and political mountains of destiny. In Daniel 2, the stone cut out without hands strikes the statue of human empires and grows into a "great mountain" that fills the whole earth. This directly corresponds to the climax of Obadiah 1:21. The judgment of the "mountains of Esau" by those dwelling on "Mount Zion" represents the final, historic dismantling of every system of human pride, persecution, and self-exaltation that has opposed the people of God. The ultimate end of all biblical history is not the survival of earthly strongholds, but the total triumph of the kingdom of God.
