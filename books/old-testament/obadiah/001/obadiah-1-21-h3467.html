SAVIORS - (yāšaʿ, Strong's H3467)

Summary:

The Hebrew root yāšaʿ means to rescue, deliver, or save. In Obadiah 1:21, the plural participle form refers to human instruments whom God raises up to rescue His people and execute His righteous judgment. Rather than acting as independent heroes, these "saviors" or "deliverers" serve as direct agents of God’s sovereignty. They embody the historical pattern of the judges, showing that temporal rescue serves the ultimate goal of establishing God’s supreme reign.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • The root yāšaʿ communicates the idea of moving from a confined, narrow, and dangerous space into a broad, open, and safe environment. In the ancient Near Eastern context, to be in distress was to be physically hemmed in by enemies or circumstances. To be saved was to be brought out into a wide-open space where one could breathe, move, and live freely.

  • The active participle form môšîʿîm translates to "ones who bring about this spacious freedom"—hence, deliverers or saviors. This imagery directly connects to the wilderness and conquest eras where Israel’s survival depended on God carving out a safe, open land for them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The verb yāšaʿ in the Hiphil stem (causative) means to deliver, rescue, save, or preserve from harm.

  • The masculine plural participle môšîʿîm functions as a noun meaning "deliverers" or "saviors." It describes individuals who successfully intervene to rescue others from physical danger, oppression, or impending destruction.

  • The term denotes active, victorious agency. It is not limited to spiritual salvation; it frequently denotes political, military, and physical deliverance in the Old Testament.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Open Field: The term stands in stark contrast to ṣārar (to bind, distress, or compress). To experience salvation is to have one's boundaries expanded and safety restored.

  • The Instrumental Redeemer: While God is the ultimate Savior, the Old Testament frequently applies the term môšîaʿ (singular) or môšîʿîm (plural) to human leaders. These individuals are not the source of salvation but the physical instruments through whom God's saving power is manifested.

  • Judicial Deliverance: True deliverance often requires the destruction of the oppressor. The savior is not merely a defensive protector but an active champion who executes justice and subdues the enemy.

4. Theological Implications

  • The Divine-Human Synergy: God is the ultimate Author of salvation, yet He consistently chooses to execute His deliverance through human instruments. God's sovereignty does not bypass human agency; it empowers it.

  • The Pattern of Grace: Human deliverers are raised up in response to the cries of God's people. This pattern highlights God's attentiveness to human suffering and His faithfulness to covenant promises.

  • Eschatological Antitype: The temporary physical deliverances wrought by Old Testament "saviors" point forward to a permanent, cosmic deliverance. The ultimate expression of yāšaʿ is found in Jesus (Yeshua), whose very name means "Yahweh is salvation," who rescues His people not merely from physical oppressors but from sin, death, and judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

  • In Obadiah 1:21, "saviors" refers to the leaders and restored exiles of Israel who will ascend Mount Zion to judge and rule over the territory of Edom ("the mountains of Esau").

  • Historically, Edom actively participated in the destruction of Jerusalem and gloated over Israel's downfall. In the prophetic future, God reverses this dynamic. Instead of being victims, the restored remnant of Israel becomes the instrument of divine judgment.

  • The phrase "the kingdom shall be the Lord's" at the end of the verse establishes the crucial contextual nuance: these human saviors do not establish their own dynasties. Their victory and rule are purely representative, culminating in the undisputed, universal sovereignty of Yahweh.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 3:9: "When the children of Israel cried out to the Lord, the Lord raised up a deliverer [H3467] for the children of Israel, who delivered [H3467] them: Othniel the son of Kenaz, Caleb’s younger brother."

  • Judges 3:15: "But when the children of Israel cried out to the Lord, the Lord raised up a deliverer [H3467] for them: Ehud the son of Gera, the Benjamite, a left-handed man."

  • 2 Kings 13:5: "Then the Lord gave Israel a deliverer [H3467], so that they escaped from under the hand of the Syrians; and the children of Israel dwelt in their tents as before."

  • Nehemiah 9:27: "Therefore You delivered them into the hand of their enemies, Who oppressed them; And in the time of their trouble, When they cried to You, You heard from heaven; And according to Your manifold mercies You gave them deliverers [H3467] who saved [H3467] them from the hand of their enemies."

  • Isaiah 19:20: "And it will be for a sign and for a witness to the Lord of hosts in the land of Egypt; for they will cry to the Lord because of the oppressors, and He will send them a Savior [H3467] and a Mighty One, and He will deliver [H3467] them."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) occasionally translates môšîʿîm in Obadiah 1:21 as sōzomenoi ("those who are saved"), viewing the returning remnant as the objects of salvation rather than the agents. However, the Hebrew Masoretic Text uses the active participle, emphasizing their role as active instruments of victory and governance under God's supreme command.

  • The linguistic connection between yāšaʿ and the name Joshua (Yəhôšûaʿ) highlights the messianic undertones of the passage. Just as Joshua led Israel to conquer the promised land, and just as the "saviors" of Obadiah rule from Mount Zion, the ultimate Joshua (Jesus) returns to establish the eternal kingdom of God.
