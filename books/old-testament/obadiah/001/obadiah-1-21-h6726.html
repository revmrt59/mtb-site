ZION - ( tsiyyon , Strong's H6726 )

Summary:

Zion originally designated the ancient Jebusite fortress in Jerusalem captured by King David, but it expanded semantically to represent the Temple Mount, the entire city of Jerusalem, the covenant people of Israel, and ultimately the seat of God's sovereign, messianic rule. In Obadiah 1:21, Zion stands in stark, triumphant contrast to the proud "mountains of Esau" (Edom). While Edom represents human pride, hostility to God's people, and impending judgment, Mount Zion is established as the locus of deliverance, holiness, and the ultimate realization of Yahweh's everlasting kingdom.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

  • tsiyyon is a proper noun of ancient Semitic origin. It is likely related to a root meaning "dry land" or "parched place," or alternatively connected to a root meaning "fortress," "citadel," or "monument." Historically, Zion was the specific south-eastern hill of Jerusalem, a fortified stronghold held by the Jebusites. When King David captured this stronghold, he renamed it the "City of David" (2 Samuel 5:7). With the transfer of the Ark of the Covenant to this location, and the subsequent construction of the Temple by Solomon on the adjacent Mount Moriah, the name Zion expanded to encompass the temple site and, eventually, the entire city of Jerusalem as the place where God chose to dwell among His people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

  • The geographic location of the eastern hill of Jerusalem.

  • The city of Jerusalem as a whole, viewed specifically as the religious and political capital of Yahweh's covenant nation.

  • The covenant community itself—the citizens of Jerusalem or the exiled remnant of Israel.

  • The eschatological and messianic seat of God’s global reign, where salvation, justice, and divine law go forth to the nations.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • The Dwelling Place of God: Zion is frequently depicted as Yahweh's earthly home. It is the place of His presence and the focal point of worship.

  • The Place of Deliverance: Scripture repeatedly paints Zion as a sanctuary of refuge. When the world is shaken, Zion remains immovable because God is in her midst.

  • Mother Zion: In exilic and post-exilic literature, Zion is personified as a mother grieving for her exiled children, who will one day rejoice when they are gathered back to her.

  • The Mountain of Holiness: In contrast to pagan high places, Zion is the "holy hill" representing God's transcendent holiness brought down to earth.

4. Theological Implications

  • Divine Election: Zion is the sovereignly chosen site of God's presence. Its significance does not stem from its geographical size or military defensibility, but purely from God’s electing love.

  • The Messianic Throne: Zion is the stage for the ultimate Davidic King. The Messiah is established upon Zion to rule the nations with justice.

  • Eschatological Victory: The ultimate destiny of history is tied to Zion. It is the site where God defeats the forces of chaos and rebellion, establishing His perfect peace and order.

  • The Spiritual Heritage of Believers: In the New Testament, Zion is transfigured into a heavenly reality (Hebrews 12:22), representing the eternal home of all believers, both Jewish and Gentile, who are united in Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:21, Mount Zion serves as the climactic focal point of the entire prophecy. Obadiah's oracle is structured around a sharp contrast between two mountains: Mount Esau (representing Edom, characterized by pride, self-sufficiency, and violence against Israel) and Mount Zion (representing Israel, the locus of God's covenant presence). While Edom's rocky fortresses will be brought down to the dust in judgment, Mount Zion will experience restoration and deliverance. The "saviors" (or deliverers) who ascend Mount Zion are instruments of God’s deliverance, reminiscent of the judges in Israel's history. They will govern and "judge the mountains of Esau." The ultimate climax of the verse—and the entire book—is that "the kingdom shall be the LORD's." Thus, Zion in Obadiah 1:21 is not just a symbol of national survival, but the very throne room from which Yahweh's universal, unchallenged reign over all nations is displayed and executed.

  • Psalm 2:6 - "Yet I have set My King / On My holy hill of Zion."

  • Psalm 48:2 - "Beautiful in elevation, / The joy of the whole earth, / Is Mount Zion on the sides of the north, / The city of the great King."

  • Isaiah 2:3 - "Many people shall come and say, / 'Come, and let us go up to the mountain of the LORD, / To the house of the God of Jacob; / He will teach us His ways, / And we shall walk in His paths.' / For out of Zion shall go forth the law, / And the word of the LORD from Jerusalem."

  • Joel 2:32 - "And it shall come to pass / That whoever calls on the name of the LORD / Shall be saved. / For in Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there shall be deliverance, / As the LORD has said, / Among the remnant whom the LORD calls."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • The Contrast of the Two Mountains: Scripture often uses the physical characteristics of mountains to convey theological truths. Edom's Mount Seir is characterized by high, jagged, defensive cliffs where Edom thought they were secure. Mount Zion, though physically less imposing than many surrounding mountains, is spiritually exalted because of the presence of Yahweh.

  • Saviors and Deliverers: The Hebrew word for "saviors" (moshia'im) in Obadiah 1:21 shares a root with the name Joshua and Jesus (Yeshua), pointing typologically to the ultimate Deliverer who establishes the kingdom on Zion.

  • New Testament Fulfillment: The writer of Hebrews points out that believers have not come to Mount Sinai (fear, judgment, law) but to Mount Zion (grace, the city of the living God, heavenly Jerusalem) in Hebrews 12:22.
