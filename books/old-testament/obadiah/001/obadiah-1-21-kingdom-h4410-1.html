KINGDOM - (mᵉlûwkâh, Strong's H4410)

Summary:

The Hebrew word melukhah refers fundamentally to kingship, royalty, royal authority, or sovereign reign. In Obadiah 1:21, melukhah signifies the ultimate, undisputed royal reign that belongs exclusively to the LORD (Yahweh). Following the downfall of Edom's arrogant human power and the deliverance established on Mount Zion, the passage declares that sovereign, earthly and cosmic kingly rule rightfully resides in God alone.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The term melukhah is derived from the primary Hebrew verbal root m-l-k, which means to reign, rule, or act as king. Throughout the Old Testament narrative and prophetic literature, melukhah denotes the office, dignity, authority, power, or territorial domain of a royal sovereign. It is frequently employed in historical accounts regarding the establishment, transfer, and administrative order of human monarchies—most notably the rise of Saul, David, and the Davidic dynasty in Israel.

In the prophetic vision of Obadiah, the shortest book in the Hebrew Scriptures, the prophet confronts the pride, security, and violence of Edom, a nation that gloated over Judah's downfall. The book builds toward a decisive reversal: though human kingdoms boast in their fortress-like heights, they will be brought low. In the final resolution of history, human claims to ultimate authority give way to the realization that true royal power (melukhah) belongs to Yahweh.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, melukhah means "kingship," "royalty," "royal authority," or "royal realm." It spans the following nuances:

  • Royal authority or sovereign reign: The actual exercise or right of kingly rule.

  • Royal status or kingship: The position or dignity inherent to being king.

  • Royal kingdom or domain: The kingdom realm over which a king holds authority.

While related Hebrew terms like malkhut or mamlakhah often emphasize the territorial realm or organizational structure of a kingdom, melukhah frequently highlights the royal power, dignity, and divine/human right of kingship itself. In Obadiah 1:21, the term functions as a feminine singular noun in the absolute state, referring directly to the final, absolute rule and sovereign dominion that belongs to the LORD.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament usage, melukhah appears in distinct contextual settings:

  • Human Monarchies: It frequently describes the institution and transfer of Israelite kingship. It is used when Samuel explains the rights and duties of the "kingdom" to Israel, when Saul assumes "kingship," or when Solomon settles onto the royal throne.

  • Royal Lineage and Royal Dignity: It describes members of the "royal family" or "royal seed" (e.g., royal officers or pretenders to the throne) and imagery of honor, such as a "royal diadem."

  • Theocratic and Divine Rule: In strategic prophetic and liturgical passages, melukhah transcends human political offices to express Yahweh's cosmic, universal rule over all nations.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of melukhah in Obadiah 1:21 carries deep theological weight:

  • Yahweh's Exclusive Sovereignty: The verse forms an eschatological climax. Edom trusted in its natural strongholds, human wisdom, and military alliances, claiming self-sufficient sovereignty. The proclamation that "the kingdom shall be the LORD's" reveals that all human political power is derivative, temporary, and subject to God's ultimate reign.

  • Judgment and Deliverance: Divine kingship in the Old Testament is not abstract; it is active. Yahweh's melukhah is manifested when He delivers His oppressed covenant people from Mount Zion and executes righteous judgment upon tyrannical oppressors.

  • The Hope of the Kingdom: By placing the melukhah in Yahweh's hands at the conclusion of the vision, the text establishes the theological principle that history moves toward the public, visible manifestation of God's undisputed reign.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:21, the prophet completes the dramatic thematic movement of the entire book. The prophecy began with Edom's self-exaltation ("Who will bring me down to the ground?") and their active participation in the looting and oppression of Jerusalem. Obadiah responds by tracing Edom's imminent downfall and the vindication of God's people.

In verse 21, deliverers (or saviors) ascend Mount Zion to judge the mountains of Esau. The immediate syntax links the act of judgment and deliverance directly to the crowning climax: "and the kingdom [the melukhah] shall be the LORD's."

The target word melukhah, parsed as a feminine singular noun in the absolute state, acts as the subject of this closing statement. The grammar presents this royal dominion as singular and undivided. The spatial contrast between Mount Esau (representing prideful human authority destined for judgment) and Mount Zion (representing the locus of divine deliverance) serves to clarify what melukhah means here: human rulers do not possess ultimate authority. When Yahweh acts, false earthly sovereignties collapse, and the sovereign right to rule the world returns visibly to its true Owner.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 10:25 – "Then Samuel explained to the people the behavior of the kingdom [ melukhah ], and wrote it in a book and laid it up before the LORD."

  • 1 Kings 2:15 – "Then he said, 'You know that the kingdom [ melukhah ] was mine... however the kingdom [ melukhah ] has turned around and become my brother's; for it was his from the LORD.'"

  • Psalms 22:28 – "For the kingdom [ melukhah ] is the LORD's, and He rules over the nations."

  • Isaiah 62:3 – "You shall also be a crown of glory in the hand of the LORD, and a royal [ melukhah ] diadem in the hand of your God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A notable canonical parallel exists between Obadiah 1:21 and Psalm 22:28. Both passages explicitly state, using identical phrasing (la-YHVH ha-melukhah), that "the kingdom is the LORD's." In Psalm 22, this declaration comes after the righteous sufferer is delivered, causing all the families of the nations to turn to Yahweh in worship. In Obadiah 1:21, the declaration follows the destruction of Edom's pride and the vindication of Zion. Together, these passages demonstrate that Yahweh's royal authority (melukhah) is displayed both through the salvation and exaltation of the afflicted and through the righteous overthrow of arrogant oppressors.
