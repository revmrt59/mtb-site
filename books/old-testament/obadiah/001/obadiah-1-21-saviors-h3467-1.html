SAVIORS - ( ya.sah , Strong's H3467 )

Summary:

The Hebrew root yashaʿ fundamentally means to save, rescue, deliver, or bring into a broad space free from oppression and danger. In Obadiah 1:21, where the word functions as a Hiphil masculine plural absolute participle translated "saviors" or "deliverers," it identifies the designated human agents who ascend Mount Zion to execute divine judgment upon the mountains of Esau. By carrying out this God-ordained mission, these deliverers serve as the historical instruments through whom Yahweh vindicates His covenant nation and establishes His ultimate universal kingship.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verbal root yashaʿ operates in the ancient Hebrew world within contexts of distress, warfare, judicial oppression, and physical bondage. The core spatial idea associated with the root is that of moving from a narrow, constrained, or threatening position into a open, safe space. In the political and covenantal life of Old Israel, deliverance was rarely an abstract spiritual idea; it described tangible liberation from hostile military powers, physical rescue from tyranny, and the restoration of covenant peace.

In the book of Obadiah, the shortest book in the Old Testament, the prophet directs his oracle against the nation of Edom (descendants of Esau). Edom had gloated over Judah's downfall, actively participated in violence against God's people, and looted Jerusalem during her vulnerability. Obadiah 1:21 stands at the climax of the vision, announcing the final reversal of fortunes: while Edom faces total destruction, a restored remnant on Mount Zion will possess victorious deliverers who establish divine justice.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yashaʿ means "to be saved," "to be delivered," "to assist," or "to give victory."

  • In the Qal stem (rare), it carries the passive/intransitive sense of receiving help or safety.

  • In the Hiphil stem, the verb takes an active, causative orientation: "to save," "to rescue," "to deliver," or "to grant victory."

In Obadiah 1:21, the term is parsed specifically as a Hiphil participle, masculine plural, in the absolute state (moshiaʿim). As a substantive participle, it designates those who perform the action of rescuing or delivering—hence "deliverers" or "saviors." These individuals act as military, judicial, or administrative champions through whom Yahweh works to bring about political liberation and divine justice.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, yashaʿ is employed across diverse literary genres—narrative, wisdom, psalmody, and prophecy—to describe both divine and human initiative in granting freedom from peril.

  • Human Instruments: Throughout the period of the Judges and early monarchy, God appointed human leaders (moshiaʿim) to free Israel from foreign domination. While these human deliverers exercised military leadership and governance, the biblical text routinely emphasizes that their ability to rescue stemmed entirely from divine authorization.

  • Yahweh as Sole Ultimate Savior: Though human agents are repeatedly called "saviors" (moshiaʿim), Israel's theology consistently insists that true, ultimate salvation belongs to Yahweh alone. Human deliverers are merely instruments created and empowered by God to carry out His sovereign will.

  • Judicial and Salvation Nuances: Deliverance in the Old Testament frequently carries a judicial dimension. To save the oppressed involves punishing the wicked oppressor; thus, saving the righteous automatically entails executing righteous judgment upon those who harm them.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of moshiaʿim in Obadiah 1:21 encapsulates three key theological themes:

  • The Delegated Sovereignty of God: God frequently chooses to mediate His reign through human agents. The presence of deliverers on Mount Zion demonstrates that Yahweh fulfills His promises to His people through tangible historical leaders whom He raises up.

  • Divine Retributive Justice: The task assigned to these deliverers is "to judge the mountains of Esau." Edom's pride, arrogance, and violence against Jacob are answered by God's righteous sentence, executed from Jerusalem. Deliverance for God's people requires the decisive overthrow of anti-covenant powers.

  • The Uncontested Reign of Yahweh: The final clause of Obadiah 1:21 ("And the kingdom shall be the LORD's") provides the theological framework for the entire verse. The ultimate end of all human deliverance is not human glory or political supremacy, but the clear manifestation of Yahweh's reign over all creation. The deliverers do not establish their own kingdom; they demonstrate that the kingdom belongs to God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:21 serves as the final, triumphant resolution of the entire prophetic oracle. Throughout the prophecy, Edom's prideful height ("you who dwell in the clefts of the rock," verse 3) is contrasted with its coming humiliation. In verse 21, Mount Zion—the location of God's sanctuary and Jerusalem's seat of royal power—becomes the elevated command center from which righteous governance extends.

The Hiphil participle masculine plural absolute (moshiaʿim) highlights a company of conquerors or leaders who ascend Mount Zion. Rather than being passive survivors of exile, these individuals step forward as victorious champions. Contextually, their action of "judging" Esau's mountainous territory signifies subduing the historical enemy and establishing order, justice, and security for Israel's restored borders.

The sentence structure creates an intentional movement: human agents ("saviors") initiate the administrative action on Mount Zion, but the narrative immediately resolves into divine monarchical rule ("And the kingdom shall be the LORD's"). The text prevents any reader from concluding that Israel's triumph is a result of self-sufficient human power. The work of the deliverers serves exclusively as the historical mechanism that reveals Yahweh's ultimate kingship.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the broader canonical context, the plural "saviors" on Mount Zion foreshadows the structural pattern of divine redemptive history. Throughout Israel's historical narratives, God periodically raised up individual leaders—such as the judges, David, and Zerubbabel—to secure deliverance for His covenant community.

In prophetic imagery, the convergence of Mount Zion, victorious champions, and the universal reign of Yahweh points forward to the complete vindication of God's covenant promises. The administrative victory of the deliverers over the mountains of Esau demonstrates that no hostile power can permanently withstand God's decreed purpose for His people.
