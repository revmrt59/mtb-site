DECEIVED - (nâshâʼ, Strong's H5377)

Summary:

Lexically, nashaʾ means to deceive, beguile, mislead, or delude, causing a recipient to accept a false impression or false ground of confidence as true. In Obadiah 1:3, the word describes how Edom's arrogant self-confidence blinded them to their true vulnerability before God. The prophet identifies "the pride of your heart" as the active agent that misled Edom into believing their high, rock-cut mountain fortresses rendered them immune to divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew verb nashaʾ functions in contexts where an individual, nation, or spiritual entity is misled into accepting a false reality. The historical setting of Obadiah addresses the nation of Edom, descendants of Esau, who inhabited Mount Seir—a region renowned for its steep, jagged sandstone cliffs and inaccessible high-altitude strongholds (such as Sela and Petra).

Geographically protected by natural defenses, Edom developed a reputation for military invulnerability and political arrogance. In prophetic literature, nashaʾ frequently surfaces when describing tactical deceit, false prophecy, or moral delusion where human beings trust in compromised security systems rather than the living God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, nashaʾ signifies misleading someone through craftiness, false promises, or distorted perception.

In standard lexical resources (such as Brown-Driver-Briggs and the Theological Wordbook of the Old Testament), the verb denotes:

  • To beguile or deceive

  • To mislead or trick into a wrong conclusion

  • To entice someone into placing confidence in an unstable or false foundation

The verb describes a cognitive and spiritual fracture: a person or group operates under a conviction of safety or advantage that directly contradicts actual reality.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament narrative and prophetic text, nashaʾ appears in several distinct operational contexts:

  • Primal Deception: The initial misdirection of humanity in Genesis, where the adversary enticed Eve to question divine truth.

  • Prophetic and Political Miscalculation: False expectations regarding military allies or political security, where leaders misjudge the intentions of foreign powers or project false outcomes.

  • False Religious Security: The delusion produced by listening to false prophets who promise peace when divine judgment is imminent.

  • Internal Self-Deception: The blinding effect of pride, where a heart deceives itself without requiring an external human deceiver.

In prophetic discourse, the word frequently highlights the tragic gap between human assumptions and divine decree.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nashaʾ reveals the deceptive nature of sin, particularly the sin of pride. Pride is not merely an emotional attitude; it is an epistemological distortion. It causes human beings to evaluate reality inaccurately, mistaking temporal advantages for eternal security.

Furthermore, the word underscores the limits of human self-reliance. When individuals or nations ground their security in physical resources, strategic terrain, or military power, those very objects become instruments of self-delusion. Scripture presents God as the absolute judge who exposes every lie and dismantling every elevated refuge built upon deception.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:3, the grammatical structure employs nashaʾ in the Hiphil stem and perfect form, 3rd person masculine singular. The subject performing the action of deceiving is "the pride of your heart" (zedon libbeka).

Obadiah presents Edom's pride not simply as an abstract trait, but as the personified force that actively misleads the nation. Edom dwelt in the "clefts of the rock" (chagavē-sela), commanding high elevations that were practically impregnable by ancient military standards. From this position, Edom declared in their heart, "Who will bring me down to the ground?"

The use of nashaʾ highlights the precise nature of Edom's error:

  • Their geographic height created an illusion of total safety.

  • Their internal pride took that natural terrain and converted it into a false theology of invulnerability.

  • The verb demonstrates that Edom was victimized by their own arrogance; their internal posture tricked them into ignoring the sovereignty of God.

While Edom asked who could bring them down, the context immediately supplies the answer in verse 4: God Himself would pull them down from the height. Nashaʾ exposes that Edom’s sense of security was a tragic miscalculation generated by their own inflated heart.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 3:13 – "And the LORD God said to the woman, 'What is this you have done?' The woman said, 'The serpent deceived me, and I ate.'"

  • Obadiah 1:7 – "All the men of your confederacy shall force you to the border; the men at peace with you shall deceive you and prevail against you."

  • Jeremiah 49:16 – "Your fierceness has deceived you, the pride of your heart, O you who dwell in the clefts of the rock, who hold the height of the hill!"

  • Jeremiah 37:9 – "Thus says the LORD: 'Do not deceive yourselves, saying, "The Chaldeans will surely depart from us," for they will not depart.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Parallel Prophetic Traditions: A direct textual parallel to Obadiah 1:3 occurs in Jeremiah 49:16, where Jeremiah uses nashaʾ alongside the identical imagery of dwelling in the rock clefts. This demonstrates a shared prophetic vocabulary exposing how regional topography fueled Edom's self-deception.

  • Structural Irony in Obadiah: A striking literary irony appears when comparing Obadiah 1:3 and Obadiah 1:7. In verse 3, Edom's internal pride deceives them (nashaʾ). In verse 7, the same verb describes how Edom's trusted military allies will deceive them (nashaʾ). The delusion that begins internally in heart-pride culminates externally in betrayal by trusted partners.

  • The Monologue of Pride: The internal statement "Who will bring me down?" stands in direct opposition to God's ultimate decree. Nashaʾ identifies that internal monologue as the voice of delusion, proving that human arrogance is inherently self-destructive.
