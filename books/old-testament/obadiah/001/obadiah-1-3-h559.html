AMAR - ( amar , Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb amar is a primary root meaning "to say," "speak," or "command." When paired with the phrase "in the heart" (belibbo), it shifts from external vocalization to internal monologue, meaning "to think," "reason," or "assert within oneself." In Obadiah 1:3, amar exposes the unspoken, arrogant self-sufficiency of Edom. Nestled securely in the high cliffs of Mount Seir, Edom did not need to shout their pride to the world; their internal narrative convinced them they were invincible. This word reveals that God hears the silent conversations of the heart and holds nations accountable for their secret pride.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, speech was understood as the outer expression of a person's inner reality, will, and character. The word amar is highly versatile, occurring over 5,000 times in the Old Testament. In its basic sense, it denotes the verbalization of thoughts. However, because ancient Israelites viewed the heart (leb or lebab) as the seat of the intellect, volition, and emotion, "saying in the heart" is the equivalent of a deeply held conviction or a firm internal resolution. In Obadiah 1:3, Edom's "saying" is not an open announcement to neighboring nations, but a quiet, deeply rooted self-delusion of absolute safety. It is the internal foundation upon which their external arrogance is built.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb amar has a wide semantic range depending on its context:

• To speak, utter, or declare aloud.

• To command, direct, or decree (often used of God's creative or authoritative words).

• To think, intend, or resolve (specifically when used with the preposition be [in] and leb [heart]).

• To call, name, or designate.

In the Qal stem, as used in Obadiah 1:3 in the active participle form (omer), it refers to an ongoing, habitual act of speaking or thinking. Edom is in a continuous state of telling themselves they are untouchable.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently uses amar to depict the correlation between inner thoughts and outer actions.

• When God speaks (amar), reality is created (Genesis 1) or history is directed. His word is active and powerful.

• In contrast, when proud humanity "says in their heart," it is almost always a picture of foolishness, rebellion, or self-deception.

The word picture in Obadiah 1:3 is one of an internal fortress built on pride. Edom's physical dwelling in the high, rugged rock clefts of Sela (Petra) matches their internal state. They have spoken to themselves a lie of absolute invincibility, confusing physical height with spiritual security.

4. Theological Implications

• The Nature of Pride as Self-Talk: Pride is fundamentally an internal dialogue of self-exaltation. Before pride manifests in outward behavior, it exists as a quiet, confident assertion within the heart.

• The Sovereignty of God Over Thoughts: Humans may say whatever they wish in the privacy of their hearts, believing no one hears. However, God hears and exposes the unspoken monologues of the proud. He responds directly to Edom's silent question ("Who will bring me down?") with an absolute decree of destruction.

• The Deceptiveness of False Security: External advantages (wealth, military position, geographic defenses) feed the internal narrative of self-sufficiency, leading to ultimate ruin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:3, the active participle form omer ("you who say") describes a continuous, lifestyle-defining state of mind. Edom's physical geography is crucial to this nuance. Nestled in the steep sandstone cliffs of Mount Seir, Edom's capital was virtually impregnable. They literally looked down on everyone else. Their physical elevation fueled their spiritual arrogance. The phrase "Who will bring me down to the ground?" is a rhetorical question showing supreme confidence. By using amar, the text reveals that Edom has converted their physical fortress into a spiritual idol. God cuts through their silent self-assurance, showing that no height is too high for His judgment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3: "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light." (Demonstrates the creative power of God's spoken word).

• Exodus 2:14: "Then he said, 'Who made you a prince and a judge over us? Do you intend to kill me as you killed the Egyptian?'" (Shows the word used to express accusation and inner intent).

• Psalm 14:1: "The fool has said in his heart, 'There is no God.' They are corrupt, they have done abominable works, there is none who does good." (A parallel use of amar with "in his heart" to denote moral and intellectual folly).

• Isaiah 47:8: "Therefore hear this now, you who are given to pleasures, who dwell securely, who say in your heart, 'I am, and there is no one else besides me; I shall not sit as a widow, nor shall I know the loss of children;'" (A direct parallel to Edom's pride, where Babylon speaks a self-exalting lie in her heart).

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew narrative, "saying in the heart" is often contrasted with "saying" aloud to others. It emphasizes that God judges the hidden motives and private thoughts of individuals and nations, not just their public declarations. This word highlights the direct link between theology and geography in the ancient world. Edom believed their rocks guaranteed their future, ignoring the Creator who formed the mountains.
