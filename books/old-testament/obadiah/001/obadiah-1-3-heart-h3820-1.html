HEART - (lêb, Strong's H3820)

Summary:

In ancient Hebrew, lev refers to the inner self—the seat of intellect, will, emotions, motives, and moral choices. Rather than merely describing personal feelings, it designates the central command center of human personality where thoughts are pondered, decisions are weighed, and spiritual commitments are formed. In Obadiah 1:3, lev describes the hidden internal center of Edom, where arrogant self-assurance bred a false sense of security that led directly to their self-deception and impending divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In Old Testament thought, the physical heart was understood as the functional core of a person's inner life. Unlike modern Western conventions that often assign emotions to the heart and rational thought to the brain, Hebrew anthropology recognized lev as an integrated whole. Mind, will, emotion, and conscience operate together within the lev.

In the prophetic proclamation of Obadiah, God addresses the nation of Edom—the descendants of Esau. Edom inhabited the rugged, mountainous territory south of the Dead Sea, characterized by steep rock formations and virtually impregnable natural fortresses such as Sela. This formidable geography fostered a sense of invulnerability among the Edomites. However, the prophet reveals that their primary problem was not physical or geopolitical, but spiritual and internal—originating in the condition of their lev.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, lev signifies the inner part, midmost core, mind, will, or seat of human reflection and intention. Its semantic range encompasses:

  • Cognitive processes: thinking, understanding, evaluating, and remembering.

  • Volitional activity: planning, intending, determining, and choosing.

  • Affective states: fearing, rejoicing, loving, or desiring.

  • Moral character: integrity, stubborness, pride, or deceitfulness.

In the grammatical structure of Obadiah 1:3, the noun is masculine singular in the absolute state. It designates the singular, internal source from which Edom's national pride and false sense of security proceeded.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, lev serves as the primary term for the invisible life of humanity. It is where human character is evaluated by God, who searches the heart rather than judging by outward appearances.

  • In wisdom literature, lev is the vessel that receives instruction or harbors folly (Proverbs 18:15; Proverbs 20:5).

  • In historical narratives, lev can become hardened or stubborn against divine commands, as seen in the accounts of Pharaoh (Exodus 7:22; Exodus 9:12, 34).

  • In prophetic literature, lev describes the rebellious inclination of the human heart (Jeremiah 9:14; Ecclesiastes 8:11) as well as the site of ultimate covenantal transformation, where God writes His law directly upon the inner person (Jeremiah 31:33).

4. Theological Implications

The biblical depiction of lev highlights the doctrine of human depravity and the internal nature of sin. Sin is never merely a matter of external behavior; its root lies within the intentions and disposition of the lev.

Furthermore, lev highlights the danger of spiritual self-deception. When human self-reliance replaces quiet trust in God, the inner counsel of the heart becomes untrustworthy. Edom believed their mountain strongholds ensured their safety, failing to recognize that a proud lev brings divine opposition. God's judgment penetrates beyond outward physical defenses to address the underlying state of the heart.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:3, lev functions as the primary agent of Edom's undoing: "The pride of your heart [lev] has deceived you." The prophet establishes a contrast between Edom's elevated physical position ("You who dwell in the clefts of the rock, whose habitation is high") and their interior corruption ("pride of your heart").

Their physical elevation fostered an internal posture of boastfulness, leading Edom to ask in their lev, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" Obadiah demonstrates that the heart can act as its own deceiver. Edom believed that their natural surroundings made them invincible, but that confidence was an illusion generated by a proud lev. The passage reveals that Edom's ultimate undoing came not primarily from external military invasion, but from the spiritual blindness cultivated within their own heart.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 9:34 – "And when Pharaoh saw that the rain, the hail, and the thunder had ceased, he sinned still more; and he hardened his heart, he and his servants."

  • Proverbs 18:15 – "The heart of the prudent acquires knowledge, and the ear of the wise seeks knowledge."

  • Proverbs 20:5 – "Counsel in the heart of man is like deep water, but a man of understanding will draw it out."

  • Ecclesiastes 8:11 – "Because the sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily, therefore the heart of the sons of men is fully set in them to do evil."

  • Jeremiah 31:33 – "But this is the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel after those days, says the LORD: I will put My law in their minds, and write it on their hearts; and I will be their God, and they shall be My people."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Reflexive Deception: In Obadiah 1:3, the pride of the lev actively deceives the person or group holding it. Arrogance warps human perception, leading people to mistake temporary earthly advantages for absolute safety.

  • Physical vs. Internal Elevation: Edom looked down on their neighbors from high mountain cliffs, yet God viewed their internal pride as an abomination. True security is never defined by physical height or worldly defense, but by a righteous and humble standing before God.
