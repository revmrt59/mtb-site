SAY - (ʼâmar, Strong's H559)

Summary:

The primary lexical meaning of ʾamar is to say, speak, declare, or express internal thought ("to say in one's heart"). In Obadiah 1:3, where it appears in reference to Edom as the one saying in his heart, "Who will bring me down to the ground?", the word identifies the deep-seated, self-reliant boast of a nation deceived by its own geographical security. It conveys not merely a casual remark, but an ongoing internal posture of defiant arrogance that directly provokes divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root ʾamar is the standard primary Hebrew verb for verbal communication across Old Testament literature, appearing in narrative, legal, poetic, and prophetic contexts. In biblical Hebrew, speaking encompasses both outward vocalization and inward reflection. To "say in one's heart" (ʾamar bllibbo) is a standard idiom denoting personal conviction, unuttered intent, or silent reflection. In prophetic literature, human speech—especially the prideful inner discourse of rebellious nations—is repeatedly placed in sharp contrast with the sovereign, irresistible word of Yahweh (debar YHWH).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, ʾamar signifies the act of uttering words, making a speech, issuing a command, or declaring a stance. Standard Hebrew lexicons (such as Brown-Driver-Briggs and the Theological Wordbook of the Old Testament) categorize its primary semantic range as:

  • To say or speak (vocal communication)

  • To command or direct (authoritative discourse)

  • To think or intend (internal speech, often indicated by pairing with "in the heart")

Unlike verbs that focus on the emotional tone or musical character of communication, ʾamar emphasizes the semantic content of what is expressed, whether communicated aloud to an audience or held as a private conviction within the mind.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament genres, ʾamar functions as the fundamental vehicle for transmitting discourse.

  • In narrative texts, it introduces dialogue, divine commands, and human responses.

  • In prophetic texts, it frequently introduces divine oracles ("Thus says the LORD"), grounding prophetic authority in God's explicit speech.

  • In wisdom and prophetic literature, when combined with "heart" (leb), ʾamar describes the human internal thought life. Scriptural usage reveals that what a person or nation "says in the heart" exposes their true worldview, trust structure, and spiritual orientation before God.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, human speech—including internal speech—is never neutral in Scripture. God evaluates not only public actions but also the hidden thoughts and declarations of the human heart.

  • Self-Deception and Pride: In prophetic indictments of arrogant nations, the internal declaration recorded by ʾamar reveals the insidious nature of pride. Pride convinces the sinner that human strongholds offer absolute security against divine accountability.

  • Sovereign Reversal: When human speech defies God, divine revelation routinely answers and shatters human boasts. What human beings say in self-assurance is subjected to what the Sovereign Lord declares in judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:3, the prophet addresses Edom, the nation descended from Esau, occupying the rugged mountain strongholds of Mount Seir (Sela/Petra). The text states: "The pride of your heart has deceived you, You who dwell in the clefts of the rock, Whose habitation is high; Who says in his heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?'"

The TARGET OCCURRENCE of ʾamar in Obadiah 1:3 carries specific grammatical features: it is set in the Qal stem, functioning as a masculine singular participle.

  • Grammatical Function: The participle form acts descriptively, characterizing Edom by its habitual internal posture. Edom is identified as "the one saying in his heart."

  • Contextual Interaction: Edom’s physical dwelling in inaccessible cliffs fueled a false sense of invulnerability. This physical security translated into a moral and spiritual tragedy: heart pride. The content of Edom's internal monologue ("Who will bring me down to the ground?") is a rhetorical question implying that no power on earth or in heaven can overthrow them.

  • Prophetic Reversal: Obadiah immediately follows this internal boast with Yahweh's direct counter-declaration in verse 4: "Though you exalt yourself as high as the eagle, and though you set your nest among the stars, from there I will bring you down, says the LORD." The arrogant human ʾamar in verse 3 is answered and completely dismantled by the sovereign divine decree in verse 4.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The following passages illustrate the range of ʾamar across Old Testament contexts using verses where the same Hebrew word occurs:

  • Genesis 22:16 — "...and said: 'By Myself I have sworn, says the LORD, because you have done this thing, and have not withheld your son, your only son...'"

  • Illustrates the authoritative divine declaration establishing an unconditional covenantal promise.

  • Deuteronomy 1:42 — "And the LORD said to me, 'Tell them, "Do not go up nor fight, for I am not among you; lest you be defeated before your enemies."' "

  • Demonstrates ʾamar used for divine instruction and solemn warning communicated through a prophet.

  • Psalms 58:11 — "So that a man will say, 'Surely there is a reward for the righteous; Surely He is God who judges in the earth.'"

  • Shows human public confession recognizing God's righteous moral order and judgment in history.

  • Job 32:10 — "Therefore I say, 'Listen to me, I also will declare my opinion.'"

  • Represents human speech used to introduce reflection, opinion, and earnest appeal in wisdom discourse.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Internal Speech as Character: Obadiah 1:3 demonstrates that God hears and judges unuttered thoughts just as clearly as vocalized claims. Edom's downfall was precipitated not merely by outward military aggression, but by the quiet hubris entertained within its high fortresses.

  • The Irony of Human Boasting: The question posed in Edom's heart ("Who will bring me down?") assumes an answer of "No one." However, the surrounding prophetic context establishes that the very God Edom ignored is the One who guarantees their complete bring-down.
