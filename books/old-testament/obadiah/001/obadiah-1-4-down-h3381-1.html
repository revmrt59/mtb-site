DOWN - (yârad, Strong's H3381)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yarad fundamentally denotes movement from a higher location to a lower one, translated by basic glosses such as "go down," "descend," or "bring down." In the causative Hiphil stem, it signifies causing someone or something to descend, lowering them, or casting them down. In Obadiah 1:4, the word serves as Yahweh's decisive, sovereign answer to Edom's arrogant boast. Where Edom believed its mountainous strongholds rendered it unassailable—asking who could ever bring it down—Yahweh uses yarad to declare that He Himself will forcibly bring the nation down from its exulted heights, demonstrating that no human elevation or earthly defense can withstand divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The prophecy of Obadiah is directed against Edom, the nation descended from Esau. Edom inhabited the mountainous territory of Mount Seir, south of the Dead Sea. This region was renowned for its high, narrow canyons, sheer sandstone cliffs, and natural rock fortresses (such as Sela). Because of this formidable topography, Edom developed a deep sense of military security and national pride, believing its lofty dwelling places were virtually impregnable to foreign invaders.

In Obadiah 1:3–4, the prophet exposes the self-deception born of this secure setting. Edom boasted in its heart, asking, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" In response, Yahweh addresses Edom's false confidence directly, declaring through Obadiah that even if Edom were to build its nest among the stars, Yahweh would bring the nation down from those high places.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yarad expresses spatial movement from a higher altitude or position to a lower one. In its simple active contexts, it describes physical actions such as walking down a mountain, going down to Egypt, descending stairs, or flowing downward like water.

When translated in the Hiphil stem, yarad functions causatively, meaning "to cause to descend," "to bring down," "to take down," or "to cast down." In contexts involving power, status, or national conflict, the Hiphil sense carries the meaning of dethroning, humbling, or bringing low an exalted figure or nation. The core lexical idea remains physical descent, but the context determines whether that descent is a spatial journey, a physical lowering, or a forced humiliation under divine judgment.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Hebrew Bible, the verb yarad is employed in three primary theological and narrative contexts:

  • Geographical and Spatial Descent: It frequently describes physical travel from higher elevations to lower ones. For instance, traveling from the hill country of Canaan down into the Nile delta of Egypt is routinely described using yarad.

  • Theophanic Condescension: The verb describes Yahweh's condescension when He manifests His presence on earth to inspect human affairs, execute judgment, or reveal His covenant law (such as at Babel or Mount Sinai).

  • Sovereign Judgment and Humiliation: In prophetic literature, yarad in the causative Hiphil stem often describes God bringing low those who are exalted in pride. Proud rulers, fortified cities, and rebellious nations are "brought down" to the dust, the Pit, or Sheol as a consequence of divine sentence.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of yarad in prophetic literature centers on Yahweh's absolute supremacy over human pride and creaturely confidence. Pride in Scripture often manifests as self-exaltation—attempting to raise oneself above others or beyond the reach of divine accountability.

When Yahweh declares that He will bring down the proud, yarad highlights the total futility of human self-reliance. No geographic height, military defense, or worldly wisdom can insulate a person or nation from the sovereign will of God. Human beings may attempt to ascend through pride, but Yahweh remains the ultimate arbiter of who is raised up and who is brought low.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:3–4, yarad functions as the linguistic and thematic pivot of God's indictment against Edom.

In verse 3, Edom asks in arrogant self-assurance: "Who will bring me down to the ground?" Edom uses the verb yarad to mock the possibility of any conqueror breaching its high mountain refuges.

In verse 4, Yahweh answers Edom using the exact same verb: "Though you ascend as high as the eagle, and though you set your nest among the stars, from there I will bring you down, says the LORD."

The grammatical form of yarad in Obadiah 1:4 is the Hiphil stem, imperfect form, 1st person singular.

  • Hiphil Stem: The Hiphil stem expresses causative action, signifying that Yahweh Himself will cause Edom's downward fall.

  • 1st Person Singular: The 1st person singular explicitly identifies Yahweh as the direct actor ("I will bring [you] down"). Edom asked who could bring it down; Yahweh answers that He will do it personally.

  • Imperfect Form: The imperfect form presents this divine act as Yahweh's declared decree regarding Edom's impending future.

The passage contrasts Edom's attempt at extreme elevation ("nest among the stars") with Yahweh's effortless ability to cause its downfall. By using yarad, the text stresses that Edom's high geography offers no protection when Yahweh decrees a nation's humiliation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 42:2 – "And he said, 'Indeed I have heard that there is grain in Egypt; go down there and buy for us from there, that we may live and not die.'"

  • Nehemiah 9:13 – "You came down also on Mount Sinai, And spoke with them from heaven, And gave them right judgments and true laws, Good statutes and commandments."

  • Isaiah 14:15 – "Yet you shall be brought down to Sheol, To the lowest depths of the Pit."

  • Ezekiel 28:8 – "They shall throw you down to the Pit, And you shall die the death of the slain In the midst of the seas."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The imagery surrounding yarad in Obadiah 1:4 employs deliberate hyperbolic escalation. Edom lived in the high cliffs of Seir, but God speaks of Edom soaring like an eagle and placing its nest among the stars. By extending Edom's boast to cosmic heights, the prophetic text emphasizes that even if human pride could transcend earth itself, Yahweh's authority extends higher still. Wherever pride attempts to build its nest, Yahweh's word can reach it and bring it down.
