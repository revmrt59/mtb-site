EXALT - ( gabah , Strong's H1361 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb gabah primarily means to be high, lofty, or physically elevated, but it is frequently used in a figurative sense to denote pride, haughtiness, or self-exaltation. In Obadiah 1:4, the word appears in the causative stem, expressing Edom's futile attempt to raise itself to an inaccessible height of security and pride, only to be cast down by the sovereign judgment of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The physical background of gabah lies in heights, mountains, and lofty structures. Edom's geographical territory was the rugged, mountainous region of Mount Seir, characterized by high sandstone cliffs, deep ravines, and naturally fortified strongholds like Petra. This physical elevation created a false sense of absolute security. The word picture links physical height with spiritual attitude: because the Edomites lived in the high clefts of the rocks, they internally "exalted" themselves, translating their geographical safety into spiritual arrogance. The eagle, nesting in the highest, most inaccessible mountain peaks, serves as the ultimate ancient symbol of this lofty, unreachable status.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb gabah means to be high, lofty, or tall in its basic Qal stem. When applied to people, it can describe physical stature. Financially or politically, it can refer to being exalted in status or honor. However, its most common theological usage is figurative, describing a heart that is lifted up in pride or haughtiness. In the Hiphil (causative) stem used in Obadiah 1:4, the verb means to make high, to mount up, or to exalt oneself. It denotes an active, self-directed effort to elevate one's position, power, or security above others and even above God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb gabah is used to contrast the lofty holiness of God with the prideful heights of humanity:

• Physical Height: It describes trees, mountains, or clouds that reach high into the heavens, representing the outer limits of creation.

• Spiritual Pride: It frequently describes the "lifting up" of the heart, which is the standard Old Testament idiom for pride and self-sufficiency.

• The Eagle's Flight: The eagle's flight is a common biblical metaphor for soaring far beyond human reach. To gabah like an eagle is to attempt to place oneself beyond the reach of earthly calamity and human accountability.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, gabah highlights the fundamental nature of pride as an attempt to usurp God's position. God alone is truly exalted. When human beings attempt to exalt themselves, they commit idolatry by claiming self-sufficiency and invulnerability. The scriptures consistently establish a spiritual law of reciprocity: those who exalt themselves will be humbled, and those who humble themselves will be exalted. Edom's self-exaltation is a case study in this divine law. Their pride was not merely an attitude but a direct challenge to the sovereignty of Yahweh.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:4, Edom is addressed directly. Their mountainous territory made them feel completely secure from enemy invasions. The prophet uses gabah in the Hiphil stem to mock their presumption. Even if they could soar as high as the eagle and place their nests among the stars—transcending physical limits to reach cosmic heights—their self-exaltation is useless against the Almighty. The verb functions to expose the delusion of human security apart from God. The contrast is sharp and immediate: "From there I will bring you down," says the LORD. No matter how high a nation or individual climbs, they remain within the reach of God's sovereign hand.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Job 39:27: "Does the eagle mount up at your command, And make its nest on high?"

• 2 Chronicles 26:16: "But when he was strong his heart was lifted up, to his destruction, for he transgressed against the LORD his God by entering the temple of the LORD to burn incense on the altar of incense."

• Proverbs 17:19: "He who loves transgression loves strife, And he who exalts his gate seeks destruction."

• Isaiah 55:9: "For as the heavens are higher than the earth, So are My ways higher than your ways, And My thoughts than your thoughts."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A profound paradox exists within the semantic range of gabah. While it is almost always associated with the sinful arrogance of man, it is also used to describe God’s transcendent holiness and ways. It can even describe a holy confidence, as seen in King Jehoshaphat, whose heart took delight in the ways of the LORD. This teaches us that the desire for elevation is not inherently evil, but its object determines its righteousness. When we lift ourselves up in pride, we court destruction; when God lifts our hearts in devotion to Him, we find true life.
