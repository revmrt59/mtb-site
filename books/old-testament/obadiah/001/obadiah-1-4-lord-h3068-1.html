LORD - (YHWH, Strong's H3068)

Summary:

The Hebrew word yhwh is the proper personal name of the God of Israel. Translated standardly as "the LORD" in English Bibles, it identifies the singular divine Person who revealed Himself to Israel and distinguished Himself from all false gods. In Obadiah 1:4, yhwh functions as the authoritative signature attached to the decree of judgment against Edom. While Edom relied on its inaccessible mountain heights and human pride, the declaration ends with the explicit affirmation that it is yhwh Himself who speaks and who will personally bring Edom down from its exalted heights.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The term yhwh is the primary proper name for the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob throughout the Old Testament Scriptures. Geographically and historically, Obadiah addresses the nation of Edom—descendants of Esau—who occupied the rugged, elevated terrain south of the Dead Sea. The Edomites lived in mountain strongholds such as Sela (Petra), surrounded by steep cliffs that provided natural defense against invading armies. This formidable terrain fostered deep national hubris, leading Edom to believe it was invincible.

In contrast to the pagan deities of Edom and surrounding ancient Near Eastern nations, yhwh is presented in Hebrew Scripture not as a regional nature deity, but as the personal, unique God who speaks, covenants, and rules over all nations. His personal name was revealed in Israel's foundational history (most notably in Exodus 3 in connection with the burning bush). In prophetic discourse, the introduction or conclusion of an oracle with "says the LORD (yhwh)" serves to establish that the message originates directly from the supreme sovereign Creator rather than human speculation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yhwh is a proper noun designating the distinct personal identity of Israel's God. Unlike generic Hebrew words for deity or master (such as elohim or adonai), yhwh functions exclusively as a proper personal name.

While biblical narrative connects the revelation of the divine name in Exodus 3 to the Hebrew verb for being or existence (hayah), the lexical item itself functions strictly as a personal name. Care must be taken not to confuse canonical and systematic theological conclusions regarding God's eternal nature with the simple lexical definition of the name itself. Lexically, yhwh designates the specific God of Israel, distinguishing Him by name from all other entities. In grammatical form here, the noun is masculine singular in the absolute state, identifying a single personal subject.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament narrative, wisdom, legal, and prophetic literature, yhwh is used when emphasizing God's personal identity, His covenant relationship with His people, and His direct intervention in human history.

In prophetic texts, the phrase "says the LORD" (ne'um yhwh or amar yhwh) functions as a formal divine solemn declaration. When prophets use this formula, they communicate that the word spoken carries the full authority of God Himself. It marks the statement as an inescapable reality guaranteed by the God who issued it. In contexts involving judgment against arrogant foreign nations, the inclusion of yhwh emphasizes that human arrogance is ultimately an offense against the true Sovereign of the universe.

4. Theological Implications

While the word yhwh is lexically a proper name, broader canonical theology illuminates the profound significance of the God whom this name identifies. Throughout Scripture, yhwh reveals Himself as the faithful covenant God who rescues His people, demands righteousness, and executes justice against oppression and pride.

Theologically, the contrast between yhwh and human nations is central to Old Testament theology. Nations like Edom may boast in military position, geographical advantages, or political allies, but canonical revelation demonstrates that yhwh alone holds ultimate authority over history. When yhwh decrees the downfall of a proud nation, no earthly power can prevent the execution of His word.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:4, the name yhwh provides the ultimate decisive weight to the sentence of judgment. Verses 3 and 4 present a stark contrast between Edom's self-exaltation and divine humiliation:

  • Edom's prideful delusion: "Though you ascend as high as the eagle, and though you set your nest among the stars..."

  • yhwh's decisive action: "...from there I will bring you down."

The climax of the verse rests on the final clause: "says the LORD (yhwh)." Edom lived in high rock fortresses that appeared unassailable by human armies. They asked in their heart, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" (v. 3). The text answers their arrogant challenge directly: the one who will bring them down is yhwh.

The presence of yhwh in verse 4 establishes that Edom's security was entirely illusory. Edom measured its safety against human foes, but failed to reckon with the God whose personal word rules all nations. The masculine singular absolute form specifies yhwh as the distinct, individual speaker whose direct declaration seals Edom's fate.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalms 33:8 — "Let all the earth fear the LORD; let all the inhabitants of the world stand in awe of Him."

  • Deuteronomy 6:12 — "then beware, lest you forget the LORD who brought you out of the land of Egypt, from the house of bondage."

  • Jeremiah 49:18 — "‘As in the overthrow of Sodom and Gomorrah and their neighbors,’ says the LORD, ‘no one shall remain there, nor shall a son of man dwell in it.’"

  • Jonah 2:1 — "Then Jonah prayed to the LORD his God from the fish’s belly."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the prophetic structure of Obadiah, the declaration in verse 4 highlights the absolute reliability of divine speech over human self-confidence. Edom relied on physical heights—the natural fortifications of the mountains. The prophet uses hyperbole ("nest among the stars") to reflect Edom's extreme pride, only to show that no height is beyond the reach of yhwh.

In the canonical progression of Scripture, the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) translated yhwh using the Greek word kyrios ("Lord"). This textual pattern laid the groundwork in the New Testament for applying the title kyrios to Jesus Christ, identifying Him with the sovereign authority and personal divine presence associated with yhwh in the Old Testament.
