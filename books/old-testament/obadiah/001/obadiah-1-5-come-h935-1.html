COME - (bôwʼ, Strong's H935)

Summary:

The primary verb bo' basic lexical definition means to enter, come, or go in. It denotes movement across a boundary, arriving at a destination, or entering a specific spatial, conditional, or temporal state. In Obadiah 1:5, the word describes the hypothetical entry of thieves or grape-gatherers into Edom's domain. Obadiah uses this common verb of arrival to contrast human plunderers—who come only until they have enough—with the absolute, unsparing destruction coming upon Edom in divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew verb bo' is a foundational motion verb in Old Testament Hebrew. Geographically and culturally, ancient West Semitic societies relied on physical metaphors of entry and departure to describe physical movement, social interactions, political invasions, and spiritual encounters. To bo' is to cross a spatial threshold—entering a house, city, field, sanctuary, or foreign territory.

In prophetic literature, verbs of movement often carry military or judicial weight. When human forces or divine agents bo' into a land, their arrival signals an event of consequence. In the book of Obadiah, the shortest book in the Old Testament, the prophet pronounces judgment against Edom for its pride and violence against Judah during Jerusalem's crisis. Sectioning off Edom's security, Obadiah introduces a rhetorical comparison involving those who "come" to plunder or harvest.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, bo' denotes entering, coming, or arriving. Depending on the context and stem, its standard semantic range includes:

  • Moving from an outside position to an inside position (entering a dwelling, territory, or structure).

  • Reaching a destination or arriving at a specified location.

  • Entering into a state, time, or condition (e.g., coming of age, coming of a day of trouble).

  • Bringing or introducing something (when used in causative stems, though in the Qal stem it expresses simple subject motion).

In Obadiah 1:5, the verb appears in the Qal stem, perfect form, 3rd person plural. The Qal stem denotes simple, active movement ("they came" / "they entered"). The verbal form presents the hypothetical movement as a completed, whole action within the prophet's rhetorical illustration.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout Old Testament literature, bo' spans diverse contexts ranging from everyday physical travel to high theological encounters:

  • Physical Entry and Spatial Arrival: The verb frequently portrays individuals or groups crossing thresholds, entering tents, reaching cities, or entering holy places.

  • Military and Hostile Entry: In invasion or plunder contexts, bo' describes armies or raiders entering a enemy territory to strip its resources.

  • Temporal and Eschatological Arrival: The word marks the onset of designated days, seasons, or prophetic fulfillments, such as the coming of the Day of the LORD.

The simple motion of bo' derives its mood and qualitative weight entirely from who is entering and for what purpose. When raiders enter, it brings destruction; when God enters into judgment or covenant blessing, it determines destiny.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, bo' highlights the reality of incoming divine judgment and human accountability. Human pride frequently assumes immune security behind geographic fortifications or political alliances, as Edom did in her rocky strongholds. However, when judgment comes (bo'), no human boundary can prevent the arrival of God's appointed agents or decrees.

Furthermore, Obadiah 1:5 sets up a striking theological contrast regarding divine thoroughness. Human invaders, even when hostile, operate under human limitations—they take what they want until satisfied and leave remnants behind. But when the sovereign Lord decrees judgment, the incoming devastation is exhaustive. The contrast underscores that human plunderers operate by limited self-interest, whereas God's righteous judgment is complete and inescapable.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, the prophet asks two parallel rhetorical questions using bo':

"If thieves came (bo') to you, if robbers by night—oh, how you will be cut off!—would they not have stolen till they had enough? If grape-gatherers came (bo') to you, would they not leave some gleanings?"

The immediate sentence uses two hypothetical entry scenarios—thieves entering by night and harvesters entering a vineyard. Obadiah highlights the normal protocol of such arrivals:

1. Midnight thieves enter (bo') and take only what satisfies them or what they can carry, leaving the rest.

2. Grape-gatherers enter (bo') a field and leave behind gleanings according to custom and practical limitations.

By using bo' in both clauses, Obadiah sets a baseline for standard entry and plundering. He then interrupts his own sentence with the exclamatory grief, "Oh, how you will be cut off!" Edom's judgment will not resemble a standard arrival of common thieves or regular harvesters who leave remnants behind. Instead, Edom's upcoming judgment will leave nothing behind—a total pillaging that exposes the futility of Edom's arrogant self-reliance.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Proverbs 1:27 — "When your terror comes (bo') like a storm, and your destruction comes like a whirlwind, when distress and anguish come upon you."

  • Leviticus 26:36 — "And as for those of you who are left, I will send faintness into their hearts in the lands of their enemies; the sound of a shaken leaf shall cause them to flee; they shall flee as fleeing from a sword, and they shall fall when no one pursues." (Contrast of terror when judgment comes upon covenant breakers).

  • Deuteronomy 6:10 — "So it shall be, when the LORD your God brings (bo') you into the land of which He swore to your fathers..."

  • Malachi 4:5 — "Behold, I will send you Elijah the prophet before the coming (bo') of the great and dreadful day of the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The rhetorical power of Obadiah 1:5 depends on the ordinary, predictable behavior of human intruders. By selecting bo', a standard verb of arrival, the prophet highlights normal human expectations only to shatter them. Human thieves coming by night possess limited time and capacity; grape-gatherers coming to a vineyard intentionally or unintentionally leave gleanings behind. The theological weight of the passage rests on the contrast: human arrivals are limited and partial, but the impending judgment from God on Edom will be absolute and complete.
