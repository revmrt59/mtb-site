ENOUGH - (day, Strong's H1767)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun day signifies a measure of sufficiency, adequacy, or what is needed to satisfy a particular requirement. In Obadiah 1:5, day describes the natural limit of plunder taken by ordinary human thieves, who steal only until they have taken enough to satisfy their immediate capacity or desire. By contrasting this standard human limit with the destruction coming upon Edom, the word highlights the absolute, unsparing, and divine nature of the judgment decreed against Edom's pride.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew noun day is a common Old Testament term used to express sufficiency, adequate quantity, or a measure determined by a specific need, capacity, or requirement. It appears across various genres of Biblical Hebrew literature, including legal codes, historical narratives, wisdom sayings, and prophetic oracle poetry. Syntactically, it often appears in prepositional phrases (e.g., kedi, jede) to indicate proportion—such as "according to the need of" or "sufficient for." In ancient Israelite society, where resources were carefully managed, the concept of day served as an important social, legal, and economic baseline for defining adequacy, moderation, and proportionate responsibility.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, day denotes an adequate supply, a sufficient amount, or the point at which an appetite, requirement, or obligation is fully met. It identifies the boundary line between deficiency and excess. The core senses of the word include:

  • Sufficiency or adequacy (what is required to fulfill a specific purpose or need).

  • Satisfaction or capacity (an amount that quenches a desire or fills a vessel).

  • Proportion (a measure matching an individual's capability or obligation).

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, day operates in several distinct contexts to establish boundaries of sufficiency:

  • Legal and Social Provision: In Mosaic law, day determines appropriate amounts for charitable assistance (giving to the poor according to their need) or religious offerings (sacrificing according to one's financial capability).

  • Wisdom and Temperance: In poetic and wisdom literature, the term warns against overindulgence, commending the practice of consuming only what is sufficient for physical health.

  • Prophetic Rhetoric: In prophetic literature, day is frequently employed in hypothetical comparisons. Prophets use the standard human understanding of "enough" to illustrate by contrast either God's limitless judgment or His overflowing, unmeasurable covenant blessings.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, day highlights the distinction between human limitations and divine thoroughness. Human actions, whether driven by legal obligation, physical appetite, or even criminal plunder, operate within natural boundaries of sufficiency (day). A thief eventually stops stealing when he has taken enough to satisfy his immediate capacity. In contrast, divine judgment against unrepentant pride is not bound by human measures of moderation. When God decrees retribution against a nation like Edom, the judgment is exhaustive and absolute, demonstrating that God's sovereign justice completes its work without leaving remnants of self-reliance behind.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, the prophet delivers an oracle of doom against Edom, a nation boasting in its mountain fortresses and perceived invulnerability. To convey the severity of Edom's coming ruin, Obadiah presents two rhetorical illustrations from everyday life: night thieves and grape-gatherers.

Under ordinary circumstances, thieves operating in the dark steal only until they reach a point of personal sufficiency (dayam, "their enough"), leaving behind whatever they cannot carry or do not value. Similarly, grape-gatherers leave behind gleanings for the poor. The presence of day in the rhetorical question ("Would they not have stolen till they had enough?") establishes the baseline of normal human restraint and capacity.

However, Obadiah breaks this expectation by declaring that Edom will be completely stripped. The plunderers brought against Edom by divine decree will not stop when they have "enough"; they will thoroughly pillage everything. Grammatically, the target word appears as a masculine singular noun in the absolute state, functioning as the limit of the thieves' plunder. By using day, Obadiah emphasizes that Edom's judgment will violate the expected human limit of plunder, resulting in total devastation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Deuteronomy 15:8 — "...and shall surely lend him sufficient for his need, whatever he needs."

  • Proverbs 25:16 — "Have you found honey? Eat only as much as you need, lest you be filled with it and vomit."

  • Jeremiah 49:9 — "If grape-gatherers came to you, would they not leave some gleanings? If thieves by night, would they not destroy until they have enough?"

  • Malachi 3:10 — "...and pour out for you such blessing that there will not be room enough to receive it."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The parallel usage of day in Jeremiah 49:9 provides a strong canonical connection. Jeremiah utilizes the exact same imagery and vocabulary to pronounce judgment against Edom, indicating a shared prophetic tradition regarding Edom's fall. In both passages, day serves as the precise point of contrast between human plunder—which operates within natural limits—and divine judgment, which completely eradicates the arrogant security of Edom.
