GLEANINGS - (ʻôlêlâh, Strong's H5955)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun olelah refers lexically to gleanings—the small, scattered grapes or minor residual fruit left behind on vines after the main harvest has been gathered. In Obadiah 1:5, the word is deployed within a striking rhetorical contrast: whereas ordinary grape-gatherers systematically leave behind minor gleanings, the destruction and plundering coming upon Edom will be so exhaustive that not even the smallest leftover remnant will remain.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the agricultural setting of ancient Israel and the ancient Near East, the harvesting of grape vineyards was a major seasonal event. Harvesters would pass through the rows of vines picking the heavy clusters. Because leaves obscured some fruit or because small clusters matured late, a portion of the crop was invariably left behind. Under Mosaic law and covenantal agrarian practice, landholders were explicitly instructed not to strip their vineyards bare, leaving these leftovers—olelah—for the poor, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow.

Beyond its legal and social context, the word naturally came to denote anything small, secondary, or residual remaining after a thorough clearing. In prophetic literature, agricultural imagery frequently serves as a vivid metaphor for divine judgment, military invasion, or the reduction of a population to a small remnant.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, olelah refers to residual fruit, specifically the gleanings or small leftover clusters of grapes left on a vine after the primary pickers have finished their work. The term denotes the sparse remnants that require careful searching to find.

In figurative and metaphorical contexts, olelah expands beyond literal viticulture to represent:

  • A tiny residual portion left after a major event or judgment.

  • A minor or secondary achievement in comparison to a greater accomplishment.

  • A small surviving remnant of a people or nation following a devastating crisis.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the prophetic books, olelah carries a dual nuanced application depending on whether the emphasis is on salvation or judgment:

  • As a Picture of Preservation: When God judges His own covenant people, the presence of olelah signifies His enduring mercy. Even when the "harvest" of judgment is severe, a few gleanings remain—a faithful remnant preserved by divine grace.

  • As a Picture of Total Deprivation: Conversely, when prophets depict the total overthrow of hostile, unrepentant nations, the complete absence of olelah underscores a judgment that leaves nothing behind.

Grammatically, in Obadiah 1:5, the noun appears in the feminine plural absolute form, denoting the multiple individual residual clusters or scattered grapes that a gatherer would typically overlook or leave behind.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of olelah in prophetic discourse highlights the absolute nature of divine justice. Human judgment and human activity operate under inherent constraints: thieves steal only until they are satisfied, and agricultural workers leave behind gleanings due to human limitation or moral duty. However, when God decrees judgment against entrenched pride—such as the hubris of Edom—the execution of that decree is total.

The contrast reveals that human security systems, natural strongholds, and worldly wealth offer no protection when God decides to execute justice. The absence of even a gleaning serves as a profound warning regarding the vulnerability of human self-reliance before a sovereign God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah 1:5 forms a key component of the prophecy against Edom. Edom had exalted itself, trusting in its secure, mountainous geography and treacherous alliances. To expose the utter futility of Edom's pride, the prophet presents two hypothetical scenarios from everyday life, framed as rhetorical questions:

1. If thieves or robbers come by night, do they not steal only until they have enough?

2. If grape-gatherers come, do they not leave behind at least some gleanings (olelah)?

In both ordinary human theft and standard agricultural harvesting, a boundary exists. Thieves take what they want and leave; harvesters harvest the main yield and leave the residual olelah. But in Edom's coming retribution, those sent against Edom will clear out everything. The interjection in the middle of the verse—"Oh, how you will be cut off!"—breaks the parallel structure to emphasize the catastrophic completeness of Edom's ruin. The word olelah functions as the benchmark of normal human limitation; by denying that even olelah will remain, Obadiah stresses that Edom will be stripped entirely bare.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Jeremiah 49:9 – "If grape-gatherers came to you, would they not leave some gleanings? If thieves by night, would they not destroy until they have enough?"

  • Isaiah 17:6 – "Yet gleanings will be left in it, like the shaking of an olive tree, two or three olives at the top of the uppermost bough, four or five on its fruitful branches, says the LORD God of Israel."

  • Micah 7:1 – "Woe is me! For I am like those who gather summer fruits, like those who glean vintage grapes; there is no cluster to eat of the first-ripe fruit which my soul desires."

  • Judges 8:2 – "So he said to them, 'What have I done now in comparison with you? Is not the gleaning of the grapes of Ephraim better than the vintage of Abiezer?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The comparison between Jeremiah 49:9 and Obadiah 1:5 demonstrates a shared prophetic tradition regarding the fate of Edom. Both prophets utilize the imagery of grape-gatherers and night thieves to emphasize Edom's unexpected and thorough plundering.

While Jeremiah places the grape-gatherers first and the thieves second, Obadiah places the thieves first and incorporates the dramatic exclamation of ruin directly into the verse. This literary technique intensifies the shock of the judgment. In both texts, the concept of olelah represents the absolute minimum that should remain under normal circumstances, making its total absence in Edom's fate a powerful testament to the severity of divine judgment upon persistent arrogance.
