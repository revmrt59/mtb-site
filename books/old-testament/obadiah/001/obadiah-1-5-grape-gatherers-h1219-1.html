GRAPE-GATHERERS - (bâtsar, Strong's H1219)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb batzar fundamentally denotes the act of cutting off, clipping, or harvesting grapes, as well as the related spatial sense of making inaccessible or fortifying. In Obadiah 1:5, the term appears as a plural substantival participle translated "grape-gatherers" or vintagers. Obadiah introduces these agricultural harvesters into a rhetorical comparison to highlight the severity of Edom's impending judgment. While ordinary grape-gatherers customarily leave behind gleanings according to societal norms and Mosaic law, God's judgment on Edom will be absolute and uncompromising, leaving no remnant or unsearched reserve behind.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root batzar belongs to the agricultural and structural vocabulary of ancient Israel. In an agrarian society, the grape harvest (batzir) was a critical season in the economic and cultural calendar, taking place in late summer. Harvest workers entered the vineyards with cutting tools to sever grape clusters from the vine.

Alongside its agricultural sense of clipping or harvesting fruit, the root carries a related semantic branch associated with cutting off access, enclosing, or making unassailable. This dual dimension reflects an underlying concept of separation or cutting off—whether severing fruit from a vine or isolating a city by building high, fortified walls. In Obadiah 1:5, the agricultural setting is directly in view, portraying harvesters executing their routine seasonal labor in the vineyard.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, batzar refers to the action of gathering or clipping grapes (gather grapes, vintagize). As a verbal root, standard lexicons (such as Brown-Driver-Briggs and the Theological Wordbook of the Old Testament) define it as:

  • To cut off, vintage, or gather grapes.

  • Substantival participle: grape-gatherer, vintager.

  • Extended sense: to fence in, make inaccessible, fortify, or withhold.

In Obadiah 1:5, the term is parsed as a Qal masculine plural absolute participle. Grammatically, this participle functions as a noun designating the agent of the action—the "grape-gatherers" or vintagers who systematically pluck the vineyard's fruit.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, batzar operates across two distinct but semantically coherent domains:

  • Agricultural Law and Harvest Practices: The root is frequently employed in Torah legislation governing the harvest. Israelite landholders were commanded not to strip their vineyards bare during the harvest, but to leave gleanings for the poor and the stranger.

  • Prophetic Imagery of Thorough Judgment: Prophets utilize grape-gathering as a vivid metaphor for divine judgment or military plunder. The image of harvesters systematically stripping a vine conveys thoroughness, while the remnant of gleanings often signifies a surviving remnant.

  • Fortification and Inaccessibility: When applied to cities or physical structures, the root describes walls that are fortified or walled up, rendered unassailable to human power. In wisdom literature, it can describe plans or purposes that cannot be restrained or cut off.

4. Theological Implications

The theological force of batzar in prophetic literature often rests on the contrast between human limits and divine thoroughness. Human activity—whether economic harvesting or covert theft—is inherently limited. Human thieves steal only what they can carry or until they are satisfied; human grape-gatherers are bounded both by natural oversight and by God's moral law requiring gleanings to be left behind.

When God executes judicial decree against a rebellious nation, however, the judgment is exhaustive. In Obadiah's prophecy, Edom had relied on her inaccessible, fortified dwellings (batzar in its structural sense) and had proudly gloated over Judah's downfall. In response, God asserts that His judgment will sweep away every defense and leave no hidden reserve, contrasting divine thoroughness with the customary restraint of human grape-gatherers.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, the prophet delivers an oracle of judgment against the nation of Edom. The verse presents a pair of hypothetical conditions constructed as rhetorical questions:

"If thieves came to you, if robbers by night—oh, how you will be cut off!—would they not have stolen till they had enough? If grape-gatherers came to you, would they not leave some gleaning grapes?"

The target word batzar appears in the second hypothetical condition as a Qal masculine plural absolute participle. The Qal stem represents the simple action of harvesting, while the plural masculine absolute participle identifies the class of workers performing this task.

Obadiah draws a deliberate contrast between standard human practice and Edom's coming destruction:

  • Normal Human Action: When grape-gatherers (batzar) work through a vineyard, they inevitably leave behind a portion of the crop—either inadvertently or in compliance with agricultural customs that permit gleanings ('olelot).

  • Divine Judgment: Unlike the grape-gatherers who leave gleanings, the forces executing God's sentence against Edom will leave nothing. Verse 6 immediately reinforces this contrast by declaring how Esau will be searched out and his hidden treasures pillaged.

By invoking the familiar figure of the batzar, Obadiah emphasizes that Edom's devastation will exceed even the thoroughness of a vineyard harvest, stripping the nation entirely bare.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Leviticus 25:11 — "That fiftieth year shall be a Jubilee to you; in it you shall neither sow nor reap what grows of its own accord, nor gather the grapes of your untended vine."

  • In this Sabbath-year legislation, the verb describes the prohibited activity of systematically harvesting vineyard produce.

  • Deuteronomy 24:21 — "When you gather the grapes of your vineyard, you shall not glean it afterward; it shall be for the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow."

  • Here the Torah establishes the legal requirement that grape-gatherers must deliberately leave behind gleanings for vulnerable members of society.

  • Jeremiah 6:9 — "Thus says the Lord of hosts: 'They shall thoroughly glean as a vine the remnant of Israel; pass your hand again over the branches like a grape-gatherer.'"

  • Jeremiah utilizes the singular participle to depict incoming judgment as a harvester repeatedly reaching into the branches to ensure thorough gathering.

  • Jeremiah 49:9 — "If grape-gatherers came to you, would they not leave some gleaning grapes? If thieves by night, would they not destroy until they had enough?"

  • In a passage closely parallel to Obadiah, Jeremiah uses the plural participle to contrast the partial nature of ordinary harvesting with the total plunder decreed against Edom.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The rhetorical power of Obadiah 1:5 relies heavily on the audience's familiarity with Mosaic harvest laws (Leviticus 19:10; Deuteronomy 24:21). Under covenant law, a vineyard owner or hired harvester was explicitly forbidden to go back over the branches to pick every last grape. The skipped or missed clusters were legally reserved for the poor, the fatherless, and the widow.

Consequently, to an Israelite listener, the phrase "grape-gatherers" naturally evoked the expectation of leftover fruit. A harvest without gleanings was a practical impossibility and a violation of covenant norms. By pointing out that even grape-gatherers leave something behind, Obadiah underscores the terrifyingly complete nature of Edom's overthrow: divine judgment will tolerate no unsearched corners and leave no surviving remnant.
