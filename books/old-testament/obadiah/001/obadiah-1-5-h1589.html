STOLEN - ( ganab , Strong's H1589 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ganab refers to the act of taking something stealthily, secretly, or without permission. In the context of Obadiah 1:5, it describes the actions of night-thieves who take only what they need or desire, leaving the rest behind. This stands in stark contrast to the total, merciless plundering that Edom faces under God's judgment, where absolutely nothing is spared.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word ganab carries the primary idea of secrecy, stealth, and deception. It is not merely the open, violent seizure of goods (which is often represented by other Hebrew words for plundering or robbing), but rather a quiet, deceptive removal of property under the cover of darkness or behind someone's back. The word picture is that of a thief slipping into a home or pasture undetected, selecting specific items of value, and escaping quietly.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• ganab is a primitive root meaning to steal, carry away by stealth, or secrete. The semantic range includes:

• To steal property or livestock (the classic violation of the Eighth Commandment).

• To kidnap or steal a person (selling them into slavery).

• To deceive or "steal the heart" (an idiom meaning to trick or outwit someone, bypassing their cognitive awareness).

• To act secretly or slip away unnoticed.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, ganab is used to illustrate both physical theft and ethical deception.

• Literal Theft: The law of Moses heavily regulates cases where an ox or sheep is ganab (stolen), requiring multiple-fold restitution. This shows that theft violates the covenant community's peace and economic survival.

• Deception (Stealing the Heart): In Genesis 31:20, Jacob "stole the heart" of Laban the Syrian (translated as "stole away secretly" or "deceived"), meaning he departed without Laban's knowledge, catching him completely off guard.

• Stealing Words: In a powerful spiritual metaphor, false prophets are accused of "stealing" God's words from one another—mimicking true prophecy to deceive the people.

4. Theological Implications

The prohibition against stealing (ganab) in the Decalogue underscores God's respect for personal stewardship and order within the community. Theft is an act of unbelief; it assumes God will not provide and that one must bypass His moral boundaries to secure life's needs. Furthermore, when God compares His divine judgment to the work of thieves who have "stolen" only what they wanted, it highlights a profound theological truth: human wickedness and greed have natural boundaries, but divine justice, when fully unleashed against unrepentant pride, is absolute and leaves nothing untouched.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, the prophet uses a vivid rhetorical question to expose the utter ruin awaiting Edom. He writes, "If thieves came to you, if robbers by night... would they not have stolen only what they wanted?"

Even the most ruthless night-thief is limited by time, capacity, and fear of detection. They steal what they can carry and flee, leaving the remaining household intact. Similarly, grape-gatherers always leave gleanings on the vines. However, Edom's judgment will not be like a standard burglary. Their former allies will ransack them completely, searching out every hidden treasure. By contrasting the thoroughness of God's judgment with the limited reach of those who have "stolen," Obadiah emphasizes that Edom's prideful security in their mountain fortresses will be utterly shattered.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 31:19: "Now Laban had gone to shear his sheep, and Rachel had stolen the household idols that were her father’s."

• Exodus 20:15: "You shall not steal."

• Proverbs 9:17: "Stolen water is sweet, and bread eaten in secret is pleasant."

• Jeremiah 23:30: "Therefore behold, I am against the prophets,” says the Lord, “who steal My words everyone from his neighbor."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Near Eastern culture, thieves operated with a degree of pragmatism. They calculated risk versus reward. The rhetorical force of Obadiah's message lies in this pragmatism: even criminals operate under boundaries of self-interest and physical limits. God’s judgment upon Edom, however, is a holy, consuming fire that leaves no "gleanings" because Edom’s sin of pride and betrayal against Israel was absolute.
