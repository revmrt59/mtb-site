THIEVES - ( gannab, Strong's H1590 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun gannab refers to a thief or stealer who operates by stealth, secrecy, and deception, usually under the cover of night. In Obadiah 1:5, the word serves as a powerful rhetorical foil to describe the total, unsparing nature of God's judgment on Edom; unlike common thieves who steal only what they can carry and leave the rest behind, the instruments of divine wrath will pillage Edom completely, leaving absolutely nothing.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun gannab is derived from the root verb ganab, which means to steal, carry away, or deceive. The core concept of this root is stealth, secrecy, and operating undetected. In the ancient Near East, a thief was distinct from an open robber or bandit; the thief relied on cunning, darkness, and evasion to take what belonged to another without their knowledge. This word evokes the sudden, unexpected violation of one's private space, occurring when the victim is asleep or unaware. It represents an intrusion that disrupts the peace and security of the home.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term gannab denotes a thief, stealer, or one who commits larceny. Lexically, it refers to anyone who secretly takes another person's property, such as livestock, household possessions, or money. The word does not imply physical violence, which is represented by other Hebrew terms for bandits or robbers, but rather emphasizes the unauthorized and covert acquisition of goods. In the legal framework of the Torah, a gannab was subject to strict civil penalties, requiring multiple restitution rather than physical mutilation or capital punishment, unless the theft involved kidnapping a human being.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Literal Theft: The word frequently designates common criminals who break into homes or steal livestock from pastures under the cover of darkness.

• The Shame of Discovery: Scripture uses the imagery of a thief being caught to illustrate profound, sudden shame and exposure. Just as a thief is disgraced when his stealthy deeds are brought into the light, so too are the rebellious people of God when their secret idolatry is exposed.

• Deceptive Actions: The verbal root is sometimes used to describe "stealing the heart," which is a Hebrew idiom for deception, manipulation, or winning someone over by false pretenses (as Absalom did to the people of Israel).

4. Theological Implications

The prohibition against the gannab is deeply rooted in the covenant community, codified in the Eighth Commandment ("You shall not steal"). Property rights in Israel were tied to the divine allocation of land and inheritance; thus, to steal was not merely a crime against a neighbor, but an act of rebellion against God's providential distribution of resources. Furthermore, the prophetic use of the thief imagery illustrates the suddenness and inevitability of divine judgment. Human thieves are limited by physical capacity, fear of discovery, and simple satisfaction—they steal only until they have "enough." However, when God enacts covenant justice, His judgment is absolute, thorough, and completely bypasses the self-preservation limits that govern human actions.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, the prophet addresses the prideful nation of Edom, which lived securely in the high, rocky clefts of the mountains, believing itself to be completely invulnerable. Obadiah uses a vivid rhetorical contrast to shatter this false sense of security. He poses a hypothetical scenario: "If thieves came to you, if robbers by night... would they not have stolen till they had enough?" Even a nocturnal thief has physical limitations and will only take what is valuable and portable, leaving the rest of the household intact. Likewise, grape-gatherers leave gleanings behind. But the judgment coming upon Edom will have no such limits. The invaders sent by God will not act like ordinary thieves; they will thoroughly strip Edom bare, searching out every hidden treasure and leaving absolutely nothing behind. The contrast highlights the terrifying, exhaustive nature of divine wrath against pride.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 22:7 - "If a man delivers to his neighbor money or articles to keep, and it is stolen out of the man’s house, if the thief is found, he shall pay double."

• Proverbs 6:30 - "People do not despise a thief If he steals to satisfy himself when he is starving."

• Hosea 7:1 - "When I would have healed Israel, Then the iniquity of Ephraim was uncovered, And the wickedness of Samaria. For they commit falsehood; The thief comes in, And a band of robbers plunders outside."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Parallelism with Robbers: Obadiah pairs "thieves" (gannabim) with "robbers by night" (sodedey laylah). While the thief operates by stealth, the robber (soded) often acts with destructive violence. Pairing them together covers the entire spectrum of predatory behavior, emphasizing that no matter the method of human plundering, the devastation of Edom will far exceed it.

• New Testament Connection: This Old Testament picture of the unexpected thief in the night serves as the conceptual foundation for the New Testament descriptions of the Day of the Lord, which will come suddenly and unexpectedly upon those who are spiritually asleep (1 Thessalonians 5:2; 2 Peter 3:10).
