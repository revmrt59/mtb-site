NOT - ( lo', Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle lo' serves as the primary word for absolute negation in the Old Testament, equivalent to "no" or "not." In Obadiah 1:5, it is combined with an interrogative prefix to form a double rhetorical question ("would they not?"). This grammatical device establishes an undeniable truth: even the most destructive human forces—like night thieves or grape-harvesters—operate within natural limits and leave something behind. By contrast, the impending divine judgment on Edom will be absolute, leaving no remnants, no survivors, and no hope of recovery.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The adverb lo' is the most frequent negative particle in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 5,000 times. Unlike subjective or temporary prohibitions, lo' represents an objective, permanent, and absolute negation. It draws a clear, unyielding line between what is and what is not.

In Obadiah 1:5, this absolute negative is prefixed with the interrogative particle to create a rhetorical question (halo'). In ancient Near Eastern culture, harvesting and property laws recognized natural boundaries. Even the most ruthless thieves would not steal everything in a house; they only took what they wanted or could carry. Likewise, grape-gatherers were legally and practically bound to leave gleanings for the poor. By using the rhetorical "not," the prophet appeals to these universally understood boundaries of human behavior to set up a shocking contrast with the boundless nature of God’s upcoming judgment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• lo' functions as an adverb of negation indicating the absolute denial of a statement, command, or reality. It translates as "not," "no," "neither," or "by no means."

• When used rhetorically with an interrogative prefix (halo'), it serves as a strong, emphatic affirmation phrased as a question. It expects an immediate and unquestioned "yes" from the listener, meaning "Is it not true that...?" or "Would they not surely...?"

• In the context of Obadiah 1:5, it establishes the logical baseline of human limitations. The question "Would they not have stolen till they had enough?" forces the reader to agree that even thieves have a saturation point where they stop.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, lo' is the word used to establish boundaries, covenants, and permanent realities. It is the word of the Ten Commandments ("Thou shalt not"). It designates what is impossible, forbidden, or non-existent.

In agricultural metaphors, lo' defines the limits of gathering. The law required that landowners must not (lo') strip their vineyards bare, leaving some grape clusters for the marginalized. Thus, the grape-gatherers in Obadiah's word picture naturally operate under this negation—they do not take everything.

By contrasting this with Edom's fate, the word picture reveals that God’s judgment will completely override these natural limitations. The enemies sent by God will not leave gleanings; they will strip the nation entirely bare.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, this passage uses the rhetorical negation to highlight the terrifying completeness of divine judgment against pride. Edom lived in the high clefts of the rock and boasted that no one could bring them down. They believed their security was absolute.

God uses the absolute negation lo' to shatter this illusion. Human disasters are limited, but divine wrath against persistent, arrogant rebellion is thorough. The theological truth is clear: when God judges a proud nation that has set itself against His covenant people, He does not merely discipline or diminish them; He removes their very foundation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, the double rhetorical "not" acts as a dramatic foil. The verse begins with a cry of lament: "Oh, how you will be cut off!"

To illustrate the depth of this "cutting off," the prophet asks:

• "Would they not (lo') have stolen till they had enough?"

• "Would they not (lo') have left some gleanings?"

The implied answer to both questions is a resounding "Yes, of course they would." Thieves leave the heavy furniture; harvesters leave the hidden grapes. But for Edom, there will be no leftover goods, no hidden survivors, and no remnants. The nuance of the negative here is to show that Edom's catastrophe will defy the normal laws of human tragedy. It will be an unparalleled, total devastation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:4: "Then the serpent said to the woman, 'You will not surely die.'" (An absolute negation of God's warning).

• Exodus 20:3: "You shall have no other gods before Me." (The absolute prohibition forming the foundation of the covenant).

• Deuteronomy 8:3: "So He humbled you, allowed you to hunger, and fed you with manna which you did not know..." (Expressing human limitation and ignorance of God's supernatural provision).

• Jeremiah 49:9: "If grape-gatherers came to you, would they not leave some gleanings? If thieves by night, would they not destroy no more than they wanted?" (The exact parallel prophetic warning directed against Edom).

7. Other Relevant Insights

The rhetorical use of lo' in Hebrew poetry is an intentional tool designed to disarm the listener. By asking questions that have obvious, common-sense answers, the speaker builds a rapport of agreement with the audience. Once the audience agrees that even thieves and harvesters leave something behind, the prophet drops the hammer of the divine decree: Edom will be completely cleaned out. This structure shifts the passage from a simple prediction of doom into a powerful, logically undeniable warning.
