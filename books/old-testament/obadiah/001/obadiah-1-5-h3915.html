NIGHT - ( layelah , Strong's H3915 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word layelah refers to the period of darkness between sunset and sunrise. In Obadiah 1:5, it is used to describe the cover of darkness favored by thieves and plunderers. While typical night-robbers take only what they want or can carry, leaving the rest behind, the coming judgment on Edom will be absolute and total, leaving nothing. The word emphasizes the sudden, stealthy, and devastating nature of the divine ruin that will overtake Edom, contrasting human thieves who operate under the literal cover of night with the inescapable, all-consuming judgment of God.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Semitic root behind layelah is associated with the turning away of light, the onset of darkness, or the cycle of the twenty-four-hour day. In the ancient Near East, night was a time of vulnerability, uncertainty, and potential danger. Without modern illumination, the night belonged to wild beasts, adversaries, and thieves who used the darkness to conceal their deeds. It represents a boundary of human limitation—a time when work ceases and vigilance must be heightened.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun layelah denotes the night, the nighttime, or darkness. It stands in contrast to yom (day). Lexically, it refers to:

• The literal dark phase of the diurnal cycle.

• An adverbial usage meaning "by night" or "during the night," indicating the temporal setting of an event.

• A figurative representation of distress, adversity, spiritual blindness, or sudden calamity.

In Obadiah 1:5, it functions adverbially ("robbers by night") to describe the timing of a stealthy, illicit raid.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Layelah appears over 220 times in the Hebrew Bible. It frequently carries rich metaphorical associations:

• The Veil of Adversity: Night is often a picture of sorrow, weeping, and divine silence (Psalm 22:2; 42:8).

• The Cover of Deceit and Crime: Because the dark conceals wrongdoing, it is the preferred hour for conspirators, adversaries, and thieves (Job 24:14; Jeremiah 49:9).

• Divine Intervention: Paradoxically, night is also the stage for God's dramatic acts of deliverance (the Passover in Exodus 12:12; the escape through the Red Sea) and divine revelation (night visions in Genesis 46:2; Zechariah 1:8).

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the night highlights the contrast between human vulnerability and God's sovereign protection. Humans must sleep and are easily ambushed in the dark, but the Keeper of Israel neither slumbers nor sleeps (Psalm 121:4).

In the prophetic literature, the "night" of judgment underscores that when God decides to execute His decrees, no earthly defense or natural stronghold can offer protection. Edom trusted in her high, rocky fortresses (Obadiah 1:3-4), believing they were inaccessible. Yet, just as robbers strike unexpectedly under the cover of night, God's judgment would penetrate their secure defenses, proving that human fortresses are useless against divine decree.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, the prophet uses a powerful a fortiori (how much more) rhetorical argument. He compares the coming destruction of Edom to two common scenarios of loss: a home invasion by "robbers by night" and a harvest by "grape-gatherers."

Ordinary thieves who operate under the cover of layelah are limited by time, fear of detection, and physical capacity; they steal only "till they have enough" and leave the rest. Grape-gatherers are required by law or practical limits to leave "gleanings" behind.

However, the judgment coming upon Edom will violate these natural limits. God's instruments of judgment will not operate like typical night-robbers who leave scraps. The plunder of Edom (specifically Esau's hidden treasures in verse 6) will be absolute. The mention of "night" intensifies the sense of sudden terror and total vulnerability, illustrating that Edom's ruin will be complete and merciless.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:5 - "God called the light Day, and the darkness He called Night. So the evening and the morning were the first day."

• Exodus 12:12 - "For I will pass through the land of Egypt on this night, and will strike all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, both man and beast; and against all the gods of Egypt I will execute judgment: I am the LORD."

• Psalm 1:2 - "But his delight is in the law of the LORD, And in His law he meditates day and night."

• Jeremiah 49:9 - "If grape-gatherers came to you, would they not leave some gleanings? If thieves by night, they would destroy only what they want."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The parallel between Obadiah 1:5 and Jeremiah 49:9 is highly significant. Both prophets use the imagery of "thieves by night" (ganabim balaylah) to describe the judgment of Edom. This suggests either a shared prophetic tradition or that one prophet was inspired by the other's revelation. The slight variation in Obadiah—adding the exclamation "Oh, how you will be cut off!"—heightens the emotional and dramatic impact of the message. It portrays the prophet reacting in shock to the sheer completeness of the destruction.
