GLEANINGS - ( olelah , Strong's H5955 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word olelah refers to the small clusters of grapes left on the vine after the main harvest is complete, which by biblical law were to be left for the poor, the stranger, and the vulnerable. In prophetic literature, particularly in Obadiah 1:5, this term serves as a powerful metaphor for thorough and absolute judgment. While ordinary grape-gatherers would always leave behind some gleanings—whether due to oversight, exhaustion, or compliance with Mosaic charity laws—the divine judgment coming upon Edom would be so complete that not even these minimal remnants would remain.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israelite agriculture, harvesting was a vital community activity governed by both economic necessity and divine law. When grape-gatherers harvested a vineyard, they stripped the majority of the ripe clusters from the vines. However, some smaller, less developed, or hidden grapes were always left behind. According to the Torah (Leviticus 19:10; Deuteronomy 24:21), landowners were explicitly forbidden to strip their vineyards bare. These remaining grapes, the olelot (gleanings), were legally designated for the poor, the resident alien, the fatherless, and the widow. Consequently, the "gleanings" became a cultural symbol of mercy, providence, and the intentional limitation of human greed to provide for the vulnerable.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The feminine noun olelah (frequently appearing in the plural form olelot) refers specifically to the gleaning of grapes or the sparse, late-ripening clusters left behind after the main vintage (batsir) is complete. It originates from a root associated with thoroughness, gleaning, or dealing with something repeatedly. Lexically, the term describes the minimal, leftover portion of a crop, contrasting sharply with the abundance of the primary harvest.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term is utilized in the Old Testament in several key figurative ways:

  • As an emblem of survival and hope: In the midst of judgment, the existence of "gleaning grapes" (as in Isaiah 17:6) represents a remaining remnant. Though the nation is severely judged, God mercifully preserves a small, surviving portion.

  • As an idiom of comparison: In Judges 8:2, Gideon defuses tribal jealousy by asking, "Is not the gleaning of the grapes of Ephraim better than the vintage of Abiezer?" Here, a minor, late action by one group is praised as far superior to the massive initial effort of another.

  • As an expression of absolute devastation: When prophets declare that no gleanings will remain, it indicates a judgment so severe that absolutely nothing of the original entity will survive.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of olelah in prophetic literature centers on the nature of divine judgment and the concept of the remnant. While human wrath, war, and greed are limited by physical constraints, divine judgment can be total and exhaustive when directed at unrepentant, proud nations. Furthermore, the presence or absence of gleanings highlights the difference in how God treats His covenant people versus hostile nations. For Israel, God always promises a "gleaning"—a remnant preserved by grace. For proud nations like Edom that actively oppose God and mock His people, the judgment contains no gleanings, illustrating the complete removal of mercy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, the prophet addresses the pride and false security of Edom. Nestled in the high, rocky cliffs of Mount Seir, the Edomites believed they were utterly invincible. Obadiah dismantles this illusion by comparing God's coming judgment with common human experiences of loss. If thieves came by night, they would only steal what they wanted or could carry. If grape-gatherers came to a vineyard, they would naturally leave behind some gleanings, whether due to human oversight or the legal requirement of charity. Yet, the judgment of Yahweh against Edom will defy these norms. The invaders will search out every hidden treasure and strip the nation bare. The complete absence of olelot underscores that Edom’s destruction will be absolute, leaving no survivors, no hidden wealth, and no remnant.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Judges 8:2 - "So he said to them, 'What have I done now in comparison with you? Is not the gleaning of the grapes of Ephraim better than the vintage of Abiezer?'"

  • Isaiah 17:6 - "Yet gleaning grapes will be left in it, like the shaking of an olive tree, two or three olives at the top of the uppermost bough, four or five in its most fruitful branches,' says the LORD God of Israel."

  • Isaiah 24:13 - "When it shall be thus in the midst of the land among the people, It shall be like the shaking of an olive tree, Like the gleaning of grapes when the vintage is done."

  • Jeremiah 49:9 - "If grape-gatherers came to you, would they not leave some gleaning grapes? If thieves by night, they would destroy only what they wanted."

  • Micah 7:1 - "Woe is me! For I am like those who gather summer fruits, Like those who glean vintage grapes; There is no cluster to eat of the first-ripe fruit which my soul desires."

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Parallel Prophecy: The language of Obadiah 1:5 is almost identical to Jeremiah 49:9. This close parallel emphasizes the shared prophetic warning concerning Edom's pride and its inevitable, thorough ruin.

  • Reversal of the Harvest: The vineyard is a frequent biblical metaphor for a nation. While Israel is often portrayed as a vineyard that failed to produce good fruit but is still preserved with a remnant, Edom is depicted as a vineyard that is so completely harvested by the enemies of God that not a single grape is left on the vine.

  • Warning Against Pride: Edom’s secure fortress in the rocks did not prevent a complete stripping of their wealth. This stands as a permanent pastoral warning that earthly security systems cannot withstand the thoroughness of divine discipline.
