COME - ( bo' , Strong's H0935 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bo' is a foundational word of motion denoting entering, coming, or arriving at a specific destination. In Obadiah 1:5, it describes the hypothetical arrival of thieves or grape-gatherers to illustrate the shocking severity of Edom’s impending judgment. While ordinary plunderers operate within natural human limits—taking only what they want and leaving remnants behind—the divine destruction coming upon Edom will be absolute, leaving nothing untouched.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of bo' denotes movement toward a specific goal, destination, or state. It is the functional opposite of yatsa' (to go out). In the ancient Near East, the boundaries of a home, a city, or a nation were sacred. Crossing a threshold to "come in" or "enter" implied a transition of ownership or influence. When applied to invaders or thieves, bo' evokes the image of crossing a defensive boundary to breach security, disrupt peace, and seize possessions.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb bo' means to go in, enter, come, or arrive. It represents physical entry into a space, such as a tent, house, city, or territory. It also describes the onset of temporal events, such as the setting of the sun or the arrival of a specific time or prophetic day. In its causative form, it means to bring in, lead in, or introduce. In the context of Obadiah 1:5, the term expresses the physical entry of hostile forces or opportunists into a domestic or agricultural space.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, bo' paints several vivid pictures:

• Entering a Covenant or Tabernacle: It describes priests entering the sanctuary to minister, or the nation entering into a binding covenant with God.

• The Onset of Judgment: It characterizes the unstoppable approach of calamity, invasion, or the "Day of the Lord."

• The Triumph of God: It depicts the King of glory entering the gates of Jerusalem.

• Inevitable Fulfillment: It marks the arrival of prophetic words coming to pass in history.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, bo' highlights the reality of sovereign arrival. Nothing enters history or crosses the borders of a nation apart from the permissive or active will of God. When judgment "comes," it is not an accidental tragedy but a directed, purposeful visitation. Edom believed their high rocky fortresses made them inaccessible to any intruder, but the arrival of God's decreed judgment demonstrates that human defenses cannot prevent the entry of divine retribution.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, the word is used twice in rhetorical "what-if" scenarios: "If thieves came to you... If grape-gatherers came to you..." Edom prided themselves on their security in the mountain clefts of Sela, asking arrogantly, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" (v. 3). The prophet uses bo' to show that even if common thieves entered their domain, they would only steal until they had enough, and grape-gatherers would leave gleanings. However, the judgment coming upon Edom will violate this natural law of limitation; their invaders will enter and strip them bare, leaving absolutely nothing behind.

• Isaiah 35:10: "And the ransomed of the Lord shall return, And come to Zion with singing, With everlasting joy on their heads. They shall obtain joy and gladness, And sorrow and sighing shall flee away."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The repetition of bo' in Obadiah 1:5 heightens the dramatic irony of Edom's security. Edom's entire foreign policy and national identity were built on stopping enemies from "entering" their mountain passes. By using this common verb of entry, the prophet dismantles their geopolitical confidence. No amount of natural fortification can prevent the entry of those whom God has appointed to execute His judgment.
