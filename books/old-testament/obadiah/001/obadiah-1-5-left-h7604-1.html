LEFT - (shâʼar, Strong's H7604)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb sha'ar fundamentally means to remain, be left over, or cause a remainder to be left behind after a process of harvest, departure, or destruction. In Obadiah 1:5, the word functions within a striking pair of rhetorical questions contrasting ordinary human harvesters with the absolute nature of divine judgment upon Edom. By pointing out that even night thieves take only what satisfies them and grape-gatherers leave behind some gleanings, the prophet uses sha'ar to demonstrate that God's coming judgment against Edom will be completely thorough, leaving no remainder or hidden store untouched.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the agricultural and social context of ancient Israel, the concept of leaving a remainder (sha'ar) was deeply embedded in daily life and covenant law. During grain and fruit harvests, gatherers routinely left behind a portion of the crop—either by accident or intentionally. Israelite law codified this practice, commanding harvesters not to strip a vineyard clean so that gleanings would be left for the poor, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow.

In military and historical contexts, sha'ar described survivors who remained after a battle, plague, or siege. Whether referring to leftover food, preserved seed, or surviving human beings, the root carries the core notion of that which abides after an event or judgment has taken its toll.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, sha'ar indicates being left over, remaining behind, or causing something or someone to remain as a remnant. Standard Old Testament lexicons establish its primary senses:

  • To remain or stay behind after others have departed or perished.

  • To survive a crisis, military campaign, or destruction.

  • To leave over, preserve, or allow a remainder to remain (when functioning in causative stems like the Hiphil).

The word does not inherently carry a positive or negative moral sense; rather, its meaning is determined by the action that produces the remainder. It focuses attention on the relationship between what was taken or destroyed and what remains intact afterward.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament historical, prophetic, and poetic literature, sha'ar appears in three main settings:

  • Agrarian and Material Contexts: Describing leftover food, intact physical structures, or ungathered crops left in a field or vineyard after the primary harvest.

  • Military and National Contexts: Describing survivors of war or calamity. Frequently, historical narratives note whether a defeat was partial (where a remnant remained) or total (where no survivors were left).

  • Prophetic and Covenantal Contexts: Describing the faithful "remnant" whom God preserves through divine judgment. In these contexts, the surviving remainder becomes the seed of future hope and restoration.

Throughout these usages, the semantic core remains stable: sha'ar designates the portion or population that stays behind after a wider sweeping action has taken place.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, sha'ar is intimately tied to the Old Testament doctrine of the Remnant (she'arit, a noun derived from this verbal root). In God's dealings with His covenant people, divine judgment is rarely an absolute end. Even in severe exile or destruction, God sovereignly preserves a remainder (sha'ar) to carry forward His promises.

Conversely, when judgment is pronounced against arrogant nations that set themselves against God and His people, the absence of a remainder highlights the finality of divine retribution. Where covenant grace preserves a remnant for Israel, unrepentant pride brings total destruction upon hostile nations. Thus, whether a remainder is left (sha'ar) or completely cut off serves as a key marker of divine favor or final judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, the prophet delivers an oracle of judgment against Edom, a nation condemned for its pride and violence against its brother Jacob. To illustrate the terrifying totality of Edom's coming ruin, Obadiah presents two earthly analogies:

1. Thieves and robbers by night: Even criminal pillagers stop once they have stolen enough to satisfy themselves.

2. Grape-gatherers: Even commercial or hungry harvesters leave (sha'ar) gleanings behind in the vineyard.

The target word in Obadiah 1:5 appears in the Hiphil stem, imperfect form, 3rd person masculine plural. Grammatically, this form presents the subject (the grape-gatherers) as carrying out the action of causing a remainder to be left behind. The rhetorical structure expects an emphatic "Yes!"—of course grape-gatherers leave gleanings!

However, the immediate context of verses 5 and 6 creates a stark contrast: God's judgment on Edom will not resemble human thieves or human gatherers. Edom's pillagers will not leave any remainder behind. Esau's hidden treasures will be thoroughly searched out and pillaged. By using sha'ar to state what ordinary gatherers naturally do, Obadiah emphasizes the complete and unsparing nature of Edom's destruction, where no remnant will be left.

  • 2 Kings 10:14 – "And he said, 'Take them alive!' So they took them alive, and killed them at the well of a shearing house, forty-two men; nor did he leave any of them."

  • Nehemiah 1:3 – "And they said to me, 'The survivors who are left from the captivity in the province are there in great distress and reproach.'"

  • Jeremiah 39:10 – "But Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard left in the land of Judah the poor people, who had nothing, and gave them vineyards and fields at the same time."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A compelling canonical connection exists between Obadiah 1:5 and earlier patriarchal narratives involving Edom's ancestor, Esau. In Genesis 32:8, when Jacob feared Esau's approaching force, he divided his family into two camps so that if Esau attacked one, the remaining (sha'ar) camp might survive. Centuries later, in Obadiah 1:5–6, the descendants of Esau face a divine judgment in which no camp or hidden treasure will be left (sha'ar) at all.

Furthermore, the contrast highlights the unique nature of divine judgment versus human activity. Human effort, whether benevolent (harvest gleanings) or malicious (theft), is inherently limited and incomplete. Divine judgment, by contrast, is exhaustive and exact, accomplishing its precise sovereign end without leaving unaddressed remnants.
