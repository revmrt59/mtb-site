NIGHT - (layil, Strong's H3915)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun layil refers basic-lexically to the period of darkness between sunset and sunrise—"night" or "by night." It designates the nocturnal hours of the natural day created and governed by God.

In Obadiah 1:5, layil sets the hypothetical scene of marauders striking covertly under cover of darkness ("robbers by night"). By establishing the image of nocturnal thieves who plunder only until they have taken enough and still leave something behind, the word serves as a vivid foil for Obadiah's argument: the impending judgment against Edom will not be like a ordinary night raid, but will result in absolute, exhaustive destruction leaving no remnant of their proud security.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word layil (also occurring in the alternate form laylah) is the primary Hebrew noun for "night." In ancient Israelite thought, night was understood as an integral component of the created order, created by God to divide the time of light from darkness.

Culturally and pragmatically, night was the hour when regular labor ceased, movement was restricted, and vulnerability increased. Because physical darkness concealed human activity, night was also the standard time for secret raids, stealthy movements, and criminal acts such as burglary. In prophetic and wisdom literature, the cover of night frequently serves as a realistic setting for human stealth and unexpected calamity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, layil denotes the physical time of darkness between dusk and dawn. Its primary senses include:

  • The literal period of night in contrast to day (yom).

  • An adverbial temporal designation meaning "at night" or "by night."

The root itself is simple and concrete, carrying no inherent mystical or allegorical meaning in its basic lexical definition. Any symbolic or theological nuance in a given passage arises not from an expanded lexical definition of the noun itself, but from how biblical writers contextualize the temporal setting of night within narrative, poetic, or prophetic arguments.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, layil functions in several distinct contexts:

  • Creation and Cosmic Order: It marks the nocturnal segment of the solar cycle established in Genesis, demonstrating God's sovereign control over time and natural boundaries.

  • Temporal Setting for Divine Revelation: God frequently communicated with patriarchs, leaders, and prophets during the night hours through visions or dreams.

  • Setting for Stealth and Danger: In narrative and law, layil identifies the hour when enemies launch surprise attacks or thieves breach security under cover of darkness.

In prophetic discourse, the mention of night raids highlights human calculation—thieves operate at night to minimize detection and maximize speed, taking only what is immediately accessible or satisfying to them.

4. Theological Implications

Theological reflection on layil begins with God's absolute sovereignty over darkness. Night is not an independent or chaotic force, but a creation subject to divine decree. Though human sinners often attempt to use the darkness of night to hide their actions from God or their fellow men, Scripture affirms that night offers no concealment from the Almighty.

Furthermore, in the context of divine judgment, human actions taking place in the night—such as theft or military plundering—are restrained by human limitations and self-interest. Human thieves take what they want until they are satisfied and then flee before dawn. God's righteous judgment, by contrast, is unbounded by human limits; when God decrees the downfall of a proud nation, no hidden place or mountain stronghold remains untouched.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, the prophet uses a rhetorical argument from the lesser to the greater (a fortiori) to announce the total ruin of Edom. The verse opens with two parallel rhetorical questions: "If thieves came to you, if robbers by night—Oh, how you will be cut off!—Would they not have stolen till they had enough? If grape-gatherers came to you, would they not leave some gleaning grapes?"

The masculine singular noun layil functions here to characterize the opportunistic nature of human plunderers. "Robbers by night" enter covertly, operating under time constraints and personal limits; they take what contents them and leave the rest behind. Similarly, agricultural workers harvesting grapes intentionally or unintentionally leave gleaning grapes behind.

Through this double comparison, Obadiah underscores the catastrophic uniqueness of Edom's upcoming fall. Edom boasted in its high, impregnable rock fortress, assuming itself safe from nocturnal surprise attacks or open invasion. Obadiah uses layil to demonstrate that even if common night-thieves attacked Edom, their destruction would still be partial. But God's judgment against Edom's pride will be so thorough that nothing will be spared—Edom will be completely pillaged and stripped bare, far exceeding the partial destruction caused by thieves raiding in the night.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 1:14 — "Then God said, 'Let there be lights in the firmament of the heavens to divide the day from the night; and let them be for signs and seasons, and for days and years...'" The Hebrew noun layil designates the nocturnal half of the daily cycle established by God at creation.

  • Amos 5:8 — "He made the Pleiades and Orion; He turns the shadow of death into morning and makes the day dark with night..." Here the Hebrew word highlights God's sovereign control over the transition between light and physical darkness.

  • Job 34:25 — "Therefore He knows their works; He overthrows them in the night, so that they are crushed." In this Elihu speech, layil forms the temporal setting for sudden, unavoidable divine judgment against the wicked.

  • Psalms 139:11 — "If I say, 'Surely the darkness shall fall on me,' even the night shall be light about me." In this psalm of divine omnipresence, layil demonstrates that physical darkness presents no barrier to God's vision or presence.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The location of Edom adds historical context to Obadiah's night-robber imagery. Edom occupied the rugged, mountainous territory of Mount Seir, characterized by steep cliffs and natural rock fortifications. The Edomites believed their high elevation rendered them secure against unexpected night raids or military sieges.

Obadiah's argument exposes the falsity of their confidence. By contrasting the limited plunder of thieves acting by night (layil) with the absolute annihilation God would bring upon Edom (Obadiah 1:6: "How Esau shall be searched out! How his hidden treasures shall be sought after!"), the text demonstrates that human fortresses cannot protect against divine retribution. Where human night-raiders leave behind gleanings, God's judgment uncovers every hidden defense.
