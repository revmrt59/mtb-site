THIEVES - (gannâb, Strong's H1590)

Summary:

gannav refers to a thief, someone who secretly or unlawfully takes the property of another. In Obadiah 1:5, the word is used in a rhetorical comparison to emphasize the total devastation coming upon Edom. While ordinary thieves only steal what they need or can carry, leaving something behind, the judgment upon Edom will strip the nation completely bare.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In ancient Near Eastern and Israelite society, theft was a serious violation of community trust and covenant law, explicitly forbidden in the Ten Commandments. The gannav typically operated in secret, often under the cover of darkness, breaking into homes or stealing livestock from the fields. Because wealth in the ancient world was largely tied to tangible goods, livestock, and agricultural produce, the thief represented a direct threat to a family's survival and livelihood. The noun is derived from the verbal root ganav (to steal), focusing specifically on the person who commits the unlawful act.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic lexical meaning of gannav is "thief" or "stealer." It describes a person who takes another's property without permission. While the term often implies stealth and secrecy—distinguishing it slightly from open, violent robbery—the distinction can sometimes blur in poetic parallelism, as seen when it is paired with "robbers" in prophetic literature. The authoritative morphology for this occurrence in Obadiah 1:5 is masculine plural absolute, indicating multiple thieves acting independently of a grammatical construct relationship.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In the Old Testament, gannav is frequently used in legal contexts, particularly in the Torah, to outline the penalties, restitution, and rules of liability concerning theft. In wisdom literature, the word illustrates the destructive behavior, desperation, or ultimate shame of the thief. Prophetically, the term is sometimes used metaphorically to describe corrupt leaders who exploit the vulnerable, or it is used in similes to describe the sudden, invasive nature of an attacking army. The word consistently carries a negative moral weight, denoting someone who violates the boundaries of justice and ownership.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical condemnation of the gannav underscores God's establishment of personal property and His demand for justice and neighborly love within the covenant community. Theft is not merely a horizontal crime against another person; it is a vertical offense against God, who provides for His people and commands them to trust His provision rather than taking what belongs to another. In the prophetic books, a society characterized by thieves is often symptomatic of a deeper spiritual rebellion, indicating that the people have abandoned God's law and are ripe for divine discipline.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:5, gannav is paired with "robbers by night" to create a vivid hypothetical scenario. The prophet asks a rhetorical question: if thieves came to Edom, wouldn't they only steal until they had enough? The implied answer is yes. A normal thief operates with limitations; he takes what is valuable and portable, then flees to avoid getting caught, inevitably leaving the rest of the house intact.

Obadiah uses this common understanding of a thief's behavior to highlight the terrifying uniqueness of Edom's impending doom. Edom's enemies will not act like normal thieves. They will not stop when they have enough, nor will they leave anything behind. The contrast between the limited damage of a common gannav and the absolute plundering of Edom amplifies the severity and completeness of God's judgment upon the proud nation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 22:2 - "If the thief is found breaking in, and he is struck so that he dies, there shall be no guilt for his bloodshed."

  • Proverbs 6:30 - "People do not despise a thief If he steals to satisfy himself when he is starving."

  • Isaiah 1:23 - "Your princes are rebellious, And companions of thieves; Everyone loves bribes, And follows after rewards."

  • Joel 2:9 - "They run to and fro in the city, They run on the wall; They climb into the houses, They enter at the windows like a thief."

  • Jeremiah 2:26 - "As the thief is ashamed when he is found out, So is the house of Israel ashamed; They and their kings and their princes, and their priests and their prophets."

7. Other Relevant Insights

Jeremiah 49:9 contains a nearly identical parallel to Obadiah 1:5, using the same Hebrew word: "If grape-gatherers came to you, Would they not leave some gleaning grapes? If thieves by night, Would they not destroy until they have enough?" This shared prophetic imagery underscores an established rhetorical device: comparing the partial destruction caused by human greed (thieves) to the total, unsparing devastation of divine judgment. The imagery of the thief in the night, established in the Old Testament prophets, also carries forward into the New Testament to describe the sudden, unexpected nature of the Day of the Lord, though Obadiah's specific focus is on the exhaustive extent of the plundering.
