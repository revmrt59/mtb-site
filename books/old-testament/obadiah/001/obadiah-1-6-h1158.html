SOUGHT AFTER - ( ba'ah , Strong's H1158 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ba'ah carries the primary meaning of seeking, inquiring, or searching out, often with an intense, earnest, or urgent desire. In Obadiah 1:6, it is used in the passive (Niphal) to describe the thorough, systematic plundering of Edom (Esau). Every hidden, prized possession of the Edomites—who felt secure in their high clefts of the rock—would be relentlessly sought out and laid bare by their enemies under God's sovereign judgment. This word underscores the impossibility of hiding anything from divine scrutiny and retribution.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root ba'ah carries a concrete, physical picture of swelling, boiling up, or erupting, as seen in its usage for boiling water or a bulging wall. When applied metaphorically to human action, this root translates into a swelling desire, an eager seeking, or a boiling, active inquiry. It suggests an intense, agitated, or highly active pursuit rather than a passive, casual look. In the ancient Near East, treasures were often hidden in deep, inaccessible caves, rocky clefts, or buried underground to protect them from invading armies. To "seek after" (ba'ah) these hidden things denotes a relentless, aggressive search that pries open every secret crevice until nothing remains hidden.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb ba'ah means to inquire, seek, request, or search out. In its basic active stem (Qal), it denotes the act of inquiring or asking for information, as well as causing water to boil or swell. In the passive stem (Niphal), which is employed in Obadiah 1:6, it means to be sought out, searched through, or exposed. The semantic range spans from physical boiling and swelling to intellectual inquiry, and finally to a thorough, physical search for hidden objects.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Boiling and Swelling: The physical sense of ba'ah refers to the agitation of water heated by fire, which swells and bubbles over. This imagery conveys an energetic, unstoppable force.

  • Eager Inquiry: In contexts of communication, the word denotes an urgent or repeated asking, representing a mind actively seeking answers or a soul urgently petitioning.

  • Thorough Exposure: In passive contexts, the word picture shifts to a vessel or location being completely ransacked or emptied, where every concealed item is brought to the surface, much like boiling brings sediment to the top of a pot.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, ba'ah highlights the ultimate futility of human self-reliance, pride, and secrecy when confronted by divine judgment. Edom believed their geographical stronghold in the rugged cliffs of Petra made them invulnerable. However, God's judgment is all-searching. Nothing can be hidden from God, and the agents of His judgment will search out what humans believe is safely concealed. The passage illustrates that wealth, secret alliances, and geographical security cannot shield a proud nation from the exhaustive, sovereign scrutiny of Yahweh.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:6, the prophet uses a parallel structure to emphasize the complete devastation of Edom: "How Esau shall be searched out! How his hidden treasures shall be sought after!" The use of ba'ah in the passive voice indicates that the plundering of Edom's secret treasures is thorough and absolute. While a typical thief might steal only what is easily visible, the enemies sent by God will leave no stone unturned. Every single hidden cache of wealth—stored in the deep, narrow gorges and mountain caves of the territory—will be eagerly and systematically uncovered. The passive form emphasizes that Edom is the helpless recipient of this aggressive divine search.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Isaiah 21:12 - "The watchman said, 'The morning comes, and also the night. If you will inquire, inquire; return, come back.'" (The verb is used twice in the sense of seeking information or spiritual inquiry, urging the seeker to pursue truth eagerly).

  • Isaiah 64:2 - "As fire burns brushwood, As fire causes water to boil— To make Your name known to Your adversaries, That the nations may tremble at Your presence!" (The physical sense of the root is used, illustrating the dramatic, bubbling agitation caused by fire, paralleling the terrifying effect of God's presence on the nations).

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint, the Greek translation of Obadiah 1:6 uses the verb katalambano (specifically katelemphthesan, "were overtaken" or "seized"), which highlights the inescapable nature of this search. The treasures were not merely looked for, but actively seized and overtaken by the enemy. This reinforces the historical reality of Edom's fall to the Nabataeans, who thoroughly dismantled the Edomite kingdom and drove them from their mountain fortresses, fulfilling the precise parameters of this prophecy.
