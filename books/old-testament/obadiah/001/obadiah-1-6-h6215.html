ESAU - ( 'esaw , Strong's H6215 )

Summary:

Esau represents both a historical individual—the twin brother of Jacob and eldest son of Isaac—and the nation of Edom that descended from him. In prophetic literature, particularly in the Book of Obadiah, "Esau" serves as a representative name for a proud, self-sufficient, and hostile people who turned their backs on their brother Jacob (Israel) in times of distress. To "search out Esau" in Obadiah 1:6 signifies the complete, unavoidable exposure and judgment of this proud nation by God, stripping away their illusion of security, wealth, and impenetrable mountain fortresses.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The name Esau is historically rooted in the Genesis narrative, where it is closely associated with the physical description of Isaac's firstborn twin at birth. He was born red and "like a hairy garment all over" (Genesis 25:25).

• This physical hairiness (se'ar) sounds phonetically similar to Seir (se'ir), the rugged, mountainous region that Esau and his descendants eventually conquered and settled, driving out the Horites.

• Esau is also called Edom, meaning "red," because he sold his precious birthright to his brother Jacob for a bowl of red lentil stew. This defining moment of spiritual apathy and immediate physical gratification cast a long shadow over his descendants.

• The geography of the land of Esau (Edom) featured high, inaccessible sandstone cliffs, deep gorges, and natural fortresses like Sela (Petra). This terrain fostered a national spirit of pride, self-reliance, and perceived invincibility.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The proper noun 'esaw translates directly as "hairy" or "rough."

• In the semantic landscape of the Old Testament, the name functions on two primary levels: as a designation for the individual patriarch of the Edomites, and as a collective corporate name for the nation of Edom, its territory, and its mountains ("the mountain of Esau").

• Lexically, when the prophets address "Esau," they are addressing the collective identity of Edom, emphasizing their ancestral traits of carnality, pride, and hostility toward the covenant people of God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The name Esau is often paired or contrasted with Jacob to illustrate the division between the fleshly, worldly line of development and the spiritual, covenantal line of promise.

• In prophetic poetry, "the house of Esau" is depicted as stubble destined for consumption by fire (Obadiah 1:18), a vivid picture of sudden, complete destruction.

• In Obadiah 1:6, the word picture is one of a thorough, systematic plunder. The phrase "searched out" translates the Hebrew verb chaphas, which refers to a meticulous, invasive search, like a home being ransacked by thieves. Even the most carefully concealed treasures in the deep, dark caves of the red cliffs are dragged into the light and confiscated.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereignty and Divine Justice: The judgment of Esau demonstrates that no nation is beyond the reach of God's sovereign hand. Edom believed their high-altitude geography made them untouchable, but God brings down the proud.

• The Danger of Pride: Esau is the classic biblical archetype of pride. Their self-sufficiency led them to gloat over Judah's downfall and actively participate in looting their brother. God's judgment on Esau establishes the truth that pride goes before destruction.

• The Contrast of Election: The theological tension between Jacob and Esau highlights God's sovereign grace. Esau chose immediate, earthly gratification over eternal, spiritual promises. Consequently, "Esau" represents those who rely on human strength, intellect, and physical security, only to find them completely useless under divine scrutiny.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:6, "How Esau shall be searched out!" uses the collective name of the patriarch to emphasize that the entire nation is experiencing the consequences of its ancestral attitude of pride and betrayal.

• The exclamation "How..." ('ek) sets a tone of shocked astonishment. Edom's security was so legendary in the ancient Near East that their total vulnerability and collapse would stun onlookers.

• The "hidden treasures" (matmôn) refer to the accumulated wealth of Edom, acquired through their control of the lucrative King's Highway trade routes and stored in seemingly inaccessible mountain vaults. God's judgment is so thorough that even these secret hoards are sought out and plundered, leaving the nation utterly bankrupt.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 25:25: "And the first came out red. He was like a hairy garment all over; so they called his name Esau."

• Genesis 36:1: "Now this is the genealogy of Esau, who is Edom."

• Jeremiah 49:10: "But I have made Esau bare; I have uncovered his secret places, and he shall not be able to hide himself. His descendants are plundered, his brethren and his neighbors, and he is no more."

• Malachi 1:3: "But Esau I have hated, and laid waste his mountains and his heritage for the jackals of the wilderness."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The wise men of Teman (a district of Edom/Esau) were famous throughout the ancient world for their intellectual depth. Job's friend Eliphaz was a Temanite. In Obadiah 1:8, God promises to "destroy the wise men from Edom, and understanding from the mount of Esau." This emphasizes that human wisdom apart from God cannot deliver a nation from judgment.

• The New Testament picks up the trajectory of Esau's character in Hebrews 12:16-17, warning believers not to be "profane" like Esau, who traded his birthright for a single meal and later found no place for repentance, though he sought it diligently with tears.
