OUT - (châphas, Strong's H2664)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb chafas primary denotes searching, tracking down, examining thoroughly, or searching out concealed items or places. Depending on its stem, it can also describe disguising oneself (as if masking or searching out another identity). In Obadiah 1:6, chafas describes the complete, exhaustive ransacking and exposing of Esau's hidden treasures during divine judgment. The word contributes to Obadiah’s message by emphasizing that no hidden stronghold, covert stockpile, or secret refuge of Edom can escape the scrutinizing judgment of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root chafas belongs to the semantic domain of discovery, investigation, and thorough examination. In ancient Israelite society and warfare, hidden items—whether plundered treasures, fugitives in hiding, sacred objects, or concealed motives—could only be uncovered through a deliberate, energetic, and painstaking search. The term implies more than a casual glance; it describes a detailed inspection that leaves no stone unturned.

In historical and prophetic contexts, chafas appears in situations involving physical searches of belongings (such as searching tents or grain sacks), strategic military operations, personal disguises, and figurative or prophetic divine inspections where God exposes what human beings attempt to conceal.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, chafas conveys the core concept of seeking out or investigating meticulously. Its primary senses across its various verb stems include:

  • Qal Stem: To search, search through, track down, or examine thoroughly (e.g., inspecting a residence, city, or container to find concealed goods or hidden persons).

  • Niphal Stem: To be searched out, thoroughly exposed, or systematically ransacked through an exhaustive search.

  • Piel Stem: To search diligently or devise a thorough search.

  • Hitpael Stem: To disguise oneself or alter one's appearance (literally changing or hiding one's identity so as to require a search to be known).

In every nuance, the root retains the idea of dealing with that which is covered, secret, or disguised, bringing it into view through thorough investigation.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, chafas functions in several distinct contexts:

  • Physical and Domestic Searches: The word describes physical searches for hidden property, such as Laban searching Rachel's tent for stolen idols or Joseph's steward searching grain sacks for a missing cup.

  • Wisdom and Internal Scrutiny: In wisdom literature, chafas is applied to human self-examination and moral evaluation. Wisdom is sought like hidden treasure, and the human spirit is described as a lamp by which hidden motives are scrutinized.

  • Prophetic and Divine Judgment: Prophetic texts employ chafas to portray God's unyielding judgment. Human beings often attempt to store up wealth in secret places or hide from accountability, but divine inspection uses figurative lamps and unstoppable agents to expose every hidden thing.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of chafas centers on divine omniscience, the futility of human self-reliance, and the absolute thoroughness of God's judgment.

Human pride frequently creates a false sense of security, leading nations and individuals to believe that secret reserves, natural fortifications, or covert plans offer protection against divine reckoning. The use of chafas demonstrates that God's judgment penetrates every human defense. Before Him, no hidden stash of wealth remains undiscovered, and no secret motive remains concealed. Where human eyes fail to see behind closed doors, divine judgment uncovers every hidden place.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:6, the prophet proclaims: "Oh, how Esau shall be searched out! How his hidden treasures shall be sought after!"

The target verb chafas is parsed in the Niphal stem, perfect form, 3rd person. The Niphal stem functions passively here, presenting Esau (representing the nation of Edom) as the recipient of an exhaustive search. The perfect form portrays this future prophetic judgment as an established, accomplished reality in God's sovereign decree.

Contextually, Obadiah addresses Edom's overweening pride. The Edomites lived in the rugged, mountainous territory of Mount Seir, boasting in their impregnable rock fortresses and hidden mountain caches. In Obadiah 1:5, the prophet notes that ordinary thieves steal only until they have enough, and grape-gatherers leave gleanings behind. However, verse 6 uses chafas to show that God's judgment on Edom will be qualitatively different: enemies serving as instruments of divine justice will leave nothing behind. They will search out every mountain cave, secret storehouse, and hidden treasure. The word underscores that human security built on secret wealth and natural fortresses collapses completely when confronted by divine holy judgment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 44:12 — "So he searched. He began with the oldest and left off with the youngest; and the cup was found in Benjamin’s sack."

  • Exposition: Here chafas describes an systematic, item-by-item physical investigation through personal baggage to uncover a hidden item, illustrating the literal sense of thorough searching.

  • Proverbs 20:27 — "The spirit of a man is the lamp of the LORD, Searching all the inner depths of his heart."

  • Exposition: In this wisdom proverb, chafas refers to God's probing of human consciousness and conscience, penetrating the deepest, most hidden motives of the human heart.

  • Lamentations 3:40 — "Let us search and try our ways, And turn back to the LORD."

  • Exposition: Following the destruction of Jerusalem, the prophet calls the community to deliberate moral self-examination, using chafas for searching out personal and communal sins in order to return to God.

  • Zephaniah 1:12 — "And it shall come to pass at that time That I will search Jerusalem with lamps, And punish the men Who are settled in complacency..."

  • Exposition: Zephaniah uses chafas to picture divine judgment as an active, illuminating search through the dark corners of the city, exposing those who hide in complacent sin.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural parallelism of Obadiah 1:6 pairs chafas ("searched out") with the Hebrew verb bawa ("sought after" or "swallowed up/ransacked"). This poetic coupling intensifies the thematic message: the judgment is not merely external or superficial, but intrusive and total.

Furthermore, the contrast between Edom's self-perception ("Who will bring me down to the ground?") and the reality of being searched out highlights a recurring canonical theme: pride obscures reality, but divine judgment reveals the truth of human weakness and accountability.
