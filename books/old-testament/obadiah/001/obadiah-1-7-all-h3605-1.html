ALL - (kôl, Strong's H3605)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun kol designates totality, the entire quantity of a singular entity, or every individual member within a specified plural group. In Obadiah 1:7, kol stresses the comprehensive and unexcepted betrayal of Edom by every single one of its political allies. By modifying the "men in your confederacy," kol communicates that Edom's collapse will not come from isolated defeats, but from universal treachery across the entirety of its trusted covenant network.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Book of Obadiah contains a divine judgment pronounced against the nation of Edom. Historically descendants of Esau, the Edomites inhabited the rugged, mountainous terrain south of the Dead Sea. Edom's sense of national security rested upon three pillars: their high, defensible rock fortresses, their reputation for human wisdom, and their strategic international treaties and military alliances.

In Obadiah 1:7, the prophet confronts Edom's misplaced trust in its international agreements. Edom had stood by indifferently—and even participated gleefully—when Jerusalem was raided and taken captive. Now, prophetic judgment decrees that Edom will experience similar betrayal. The word kol sets the boundary of this judgment, establishing that Edom's reliance on human covenants will prove entirely futile because every single one of their allies will turn against them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, kol functions as a noun denoting "totality," "the whole," "all," or "every." It serves as the standard Hebrew term to indicate that no exception or sub-category is excluded from the statement being made.

When kol appears in a construct relationship with a following noun, its translation depends on whether that noun is singular or plural:

  • With a singular noun, it generally means "the whole of" or "all of."

  • With a plural or collective noun, it generally means "every" or "all."

In Obadiah 1:7, the authoritative target morphology identifies kol as a masculine, singular noun in the construct state. It is bound directly to the collective phrase "men in your confederacy" (anshe beritekha). In this construction, kol establishes total qualification: every single individual and group bound to Edom by political covenant is included in the impending act of expulsion and treachery.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Hebrew Scripture, kol is used in narrative, legal, wisdom, and prophetic texts to define scope. It is an essential term for defining covenant obligations, universal human realities, and the exhaustive nature of divine acts.

The primary nuance of kol is governed by its immediate context. It does not inherently mean "every single item in the entire universe" in an abstract sense, but rather "the whole entirety of the specific group or object in view." In prophetic contexts, kol is frequently deployed to demonstrate the impossibility of escaping divine judgment or to emphasize the absolute integrity of God's word. When God acts, whether in judgment or redemption, the scope represented by kol highlights that the outcome is complete and exhaustive.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of kol in Obadiah 1:7 reinforces several major biblical-theological themes:

  • The Vanity of Human Trust: Edom trusted in human treaties (berit) rather than divine righteousness. By declaring that all allies will turn, Scripture highlights the ultimate unreliability of human strength when pitted against divine decree.

  • Divine Retributive Justice (Lex Talionis): Edom betrayed their brother Jacob (Judah) during their time of distress. In turn, God turns all of Edom's own allies against them, reflecting the precise measure of Edom's own sin.

  • Sovereignty Over Nations: God exercises supreme authority over international relations, turning human political loyalties to fulfill His sovereign righteous judgments.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, kol opens a carefully constructed poetic description of Edom's betrayal:

  • "All the men in your confederacy shall force you to the border;"

  • "The men at peace with you shall deceive you and prevail against you;"

  • "Those who eat your bread shall lay a trap for you."

The term kol stands at the head of this entire stanza, setting the structural scope for the parallel descriptions that follow. The "men at peace" and "those who eat your bread" expand upon the initial phrase "all the men in your confederacy." Thus, kol ensures that the reader understands the betrayal as exhaustive.

Furthermore, the immediate verse concludes with the declaration: "No one has understanding in him." Edom was famous for political wisdom, yet their wisdom failed to discern that their own covenant partners were actively plotting their downfall. By introducing this verse with kol, Obadiah emphasizes that Edom's defeat is not partial—every single partner pushes them to the border, rendering Edom's self-reliant confidence completely broken.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Joel 1:2 — "Hear this, you elders, / And give ear, all you inhabitants of the land!"

  • Here kol specifies a universal summons, demanding the attention of every single occupant of the land without exception.

  • Psalms 32:6 — "For this cause everyone who is godly shall pray to You / In a time when You may be found;"

  • Here kol describes the universal characteristic and duty belonging to the entirety of the godly community.

  • Isaiah 40:6 — "The voice said, 'Cry out!' / And he said, 'What shall I cry?' / 'All flesh is grass, / And all its loveliness is like the flower of the field.'"

  • Here kol emphasizes the complete, universal frailty of all mankind in contrast to the eternal permanence of God's word.

  • Jeremiah 31:25 — "For I have satisfied the weary soul, and I have replenished every sorrowful soul."

  • Here kol highlights the exhaustive scope of divine restoration, ensuring that no suffering individual within His promise is overlooked.

  • Ezekiel 43:12 — "This is the law of the temple: The whole limit of its surrounding area on top of the mountain shall be most holy."

  • Here kol defines the absolute spatial scope of temple holiness, designating the entire area as set apart for God.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The placement of kol in Obadiah 1:7 produces a striking literary irony. Edom believed that having a broad, comprehensive network of allies provided absolute security. However, Obadiah uses kol to reveal that this same comprehensive network will become the precise vehicle of their absolute ruin. The very allies who sat at Edom's table and shared their bread are the ones who lay the trap. The totality represented by kol shifts from total security to total vulnerability in a single prophetic sentence.
