CONFEDERACY - (bᵉrîyth, Strong's H1285)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun berit fundamentally denotes a covenant, treaty, alliance, or binding agreement between two or more parties. Depending on the context, it describes solemn divine promises made by God, interpersonal pacts between individuals, or international political and military treaties. In Obadiah 1:7, berit is rendered as "confederacy," referring specifically to Edom's political allies and treaty partners. Obadiah uses this term to highlight the complete collapse of Edom's human security: the very nations bound to Edom by covenant obligations would betray, deceive, and expel them, demonstrating that human alliances cannot withstand divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Near East, a berit was a formal mechanism establishing legal, relational, or political obligations between entities. Covenants were used to seal peace treaties between kingdoms, settle border disputes, establish mutual defense pacts, or define the terms of loyalty between a sovereign king and a vassal state. These agreements were typically solemnized through symbolic rites, oaths, and shared meals.

Within the Hebrew Scriptures, berit serves as a foundational concept for understanding both human relationships and divine redemption. Politically, nations formed alliances (berit) to secure trade routes and ensure collective defense. Religiously, Yahweh established covenants with Noah, Abraham, Israel at Sinai, and David. Whether human or divine, a berit established clear obligations of faithfulness and loyalty between the contracting parties.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, berit signifies a "covenant," "pact," "pact of friendship," "treaty," or "agreement." When applied to international relations, it carries the sense of a league or "confederacy."

The term itself designates a binding obligation or pledge. It does not inherently imply either equal status between parties or an unchangeable bond; rather, its specific dynamics are determined by the context and the nature of the participants. Human berit arrangements could be honored, ignored, or treacherous, whereas divine berit arrangements rest upon the absolute faithfulness of Yahweh. In Obadiah 1:7, the phrase translated "men of your confederacy" (anshe beritekha) refers directly to Edom's trusted foreign allies bound by international treaties.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, berit displays a broad range of applications:

  • Divine Covenants: It describes Yahweh’s sovereign initiative to bind Himself to humanity or His chosen people, such as the covenant with Noah (Genesis 9:13), the covenant with Abraham (Genesis 15:18), or His enduring covenant pledges remembered across generations (Psalms 105:8).

  • Interpersonal Pacts: It denotes intimate personal vows of friendship and mutual loyalty, as seen in the covenant struck between David and Jonathan (1 Samuel 18:3).

  • National Obligations: It refers to the covenant Law given to Israel, which required obedience and faithful adherence (Jeremiah 11:6).

  • Political Treaties and Alliances: It designates political leagues between rulers or nations. In this geopolitical sense, a berit created a network of pledged military and economic support. It is precisely this sense that Obadiah employs when confronting Edom.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of berit in Obadiah 1:7 stands in stark theological contrast to the divine covenants of Scripture. Edom placed its trust in human political treaties rather than the sovereign God. In scripture, human political alliances made apart from Yahweh are portrayed as fragile and ultimately unreliable.

Edom believed that its strategic mountain fortress, coupled with its formal international confederacies (berit), rendered it invulnerable. However, Obadiah demonstrates that human treaties offer no protection against divine retribution. While God’s berit is characterized by steadfast love and enduring loyalty, human covenants formed on the basis of political expediency are easily broken when self-interest intervenes. Edom's judgment reveals the foolishness of placing ultimate faith in earthly alliances rather than the creator God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, the prophet pronounces judgment against Edom for its pride and its violence against Judah. In this verse, berit appears in the feminine singular form in the absolute state, functioning within a construct relationship ("men of your covenant" / "men of your confederacy").

The verse builds a striking poetic parallelism to depict total betrayal, grouping three related terms:

1. "The men of your confederacy [berit]"

2. "The men at peace with you"

3. "Those who eat your bread"

The prophet exposes a devastating reversal of expectations. The "men of your confederacy"—Edom's sworn treaty partners—will not come to Edom's defense. Instead, they will "force you to the border" and "lay a trap for you." Those bound by treaty obligations become the very instrument of Edom's ruin. Through this choice of language, Obadiah emphasizes that Edom's downfall will come from within its own inner circle of allies, turning their misplaced trust into the mechanism of their exile.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 18:3 — "Then Jonathan and David made a covenant, because he loved him as his own soul."

  • Psalms 105:8 — "He remembers His covenant forever, The word which He commanded, for a thousand generations..."

  • Jeremiah 11:6 — "Then the LORD said to me, 'Proclaim all these words in the cities of Judah and in the streets of Jerusalem, saying: "Hear the words of this covenant and do them."'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structure of Obadiah 1:7 presents a progression of intimacy that heightens the severity of the betrayal. The verse moves from formal political alliance ("men of your confederacy"), to diplomatic harmony ("men at peace with you"), to table fellowship ("those who eat your bread"). In ancient Near Eastern culture, sharing bread was a pledge of protection and hospitality. By showing that even table companions break the covenant (berit), Obadiah emphasizes the complete disintegration of Edom's social and political security networks.

Furthermore, this betrayal reflects a principle of divine justice: because Edom betrayed its brother nation Israel in its time of distress, Edom itself would experience betrayal at the hands of its own covenant allies.
