DECEIVE - (nâshâʼ, Strong's H5377)

Summary:

The primary lexical meaning of nashaʾ is to beguile, mislead, deceive, or lead into false confidence. In Obadiah 1:7, the word describes the sudden, devastating betrayal Edom experiences at the hands of its own trusted allies, who active misguide Edom into a false sense of security before turning against them in divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew verb nashaʾ belongs to a semantic domain concerned with intellectual and spiritual leadings astray, where a person or nation is induced to believe what is false or trust in what is unreliable. In ancient Near Eastern diplomatic and social contexts, covenant bonds, military treaties, and shared meals established formal expectations of loyalty and protection. To deceive a covenant partner was not merely a private lie, but a breach of diplomatic faith. In the prophetic message of Obadiah, Edom—a nation long hostile to Israel despite their shared ancestry through Esau—reposed its sense of safety in rugged mountain strongholds and political coalitions. The prophetic word exposes how the very alliances Edom relied upon for protection become the instrument of its ultimate exposure and downfall.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, nashaʾ denotes causing someone to err, deceiving, misleading, or beguiling a target into accepting a falsehood or false hope. In the causative Hiphil stem, the verb emphasizes the deliberate act of inducing deception—actively leading another party into error or misguided trust. Rather than describing simple misunderstanding, nashaʾ involves an active influence that obscures reality, leaving the victim vulnerable to ruin while believing themselves secure.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament narrative and prophetic texts, nashaʾ appears in contexts where an entity is drawn into catastrophic error through deceptive speech or misplaced trust. Its range includes:

  • Beguilement by a subtle actor, as when a victim is tricked into directly violating a divine boundary.

  • Political and military deception, where nations or leaders are deceived by false reports, unstable treaties, or political self-delusion.

  • Spiritual delusion, where God permits or sends judgment through the deceptive claims of false prophets upon those who reject truth.

In each instance, nashaʾ conveys an element of misplaced trust: the person or group deceived actively relies on the treacherous source, rendering the resulting collapse all the more sudden and severe.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of nashaʾ in biblical judgment highlights significant canonical themes:

  • The Folly of Ungodly Security: Human structures, political treaties, and natural defenses cannot secure a people who stand under divine sentence. Relying on human promises apart from God leaves individuals and nations vulnerable to ruin.

  • Retributive Justice: In divine providence, pride and deceit often boomerang upon those who practice them. Edom relied on deceitful aggression against Judah; consequently, God ordained that Edom would fall through the deceit of its own inner circle.

  • The Sovereignty of God Over Nations: Human treaties and international politics do not operate outside God's sovereign governance. God can turn human alliances into mechanisms of judgment to accomplish His holy purpose.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, the authoritative grammar specifies the Hiphil stem, perfect form, 3rd person, plural.

  • The Hiphil stem highlights that Edom's allies are the active agents who actively misguide and lead Edom into false confidence.

  • The 3rd person plural subject explicitly links this deceptive action to Edom's trusted partners—identified in the surrounding poetic lines as "the men in confederacy with you," "the men at peace with you," and "those who eat your bread."

  • The perfect form presents this future prophetic judgment with settled certainty, viewing the allies' breach of faith as a completed reality in God's divine decree.

The immediate literary context sharpens this nuance through parallelism. Edom is expelled to the border and overpowered by those with whom it shared solemn covenant meals. The word nashaʾ underscores that Edom's collapse is not merely an external military invasion, but an inside betrayal: those who pledged peace actively misled Edom, setting a trap beneath them while Edom remained entirely unaware ("no one has understanding in him").

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 3:13 — "And the LORD God said to the woman, 'What is this you have done?' The woman said, 'The serpent deceived me, and I ate.'"

  • 2 Kings 18:29 — "Thus says the king: 'Do not let Hezekiah deceive you, for he shall not be able to deliver you from his hand.'"

  • Obadiah 1:3 — "The pride of your heart has deceived you, you who dwell in the clefts of the rock, whose habitation is high; who is in his heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?'"

  • Jeremiah 37:9 — "Thus says the LORD: 'Do not deceive yourselves, saying, "The Chaldeans will surely depart from us," for they will not depart.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A notable textual echo exists within the book of Obadiah itself through the repeated use of nashaʾ. In Obadiah 1:3, the prophet declares that Edom's internal "pride of your heart has deceived (nashaʾ) you." Then in Obadiah 1:7, the prophet states that Edom's external allies "shall deceive (nashaʾ) you."

This literary pairing demonstrates the two-fold operation of deception in Edom's downfall: internal arrogance blinded their spiritual discernment, which in turn made them ripe for external betrayal by their political confederates. Deception began within the heart before it was executed by covenant allies.
