FORCE - (shâlach, Strong's H7971)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shalach fundamentally signifies "to send," "to dispatch," "to let go," or "to expel." In its intensive Piel stem, the verb often carries the force of sending off decisively, casting out, or driving away. In Obadiah 1:7, shalach depicts Edom's trusted allies forcibly expelling or driving Edom away to its own borders. The word emphasizes the shocking betrayal and total expulsion Edom experiences at the hands of its former confederates, establishing that human alliances offer no security against divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root shalach is a foundational Ancient Near Eastern verb used across biblical literature to denote movement directed or ordered by an authority. It spans contexts ranging from royal diplomatic missions and prophetic commissioning to the release of captives or the forced expulsion of enemies.

In the ancient prophetic context of Obadiah, the nation of Edom—descendants of Esau—prided itself on its strategic mountain fortresses, geopolitical alliances, and reputation for wisdom. Obadiah's prophecy addresses Edom's arrogance and proud indifference during the calamity of Judah. Edom had relied on political pacts and military treaties for protection. The prophet uses shalach within a covenantal and military framework to demonstrate how Edom's trusted political partners would become the very instruments of its undoing.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shalach in the Qal stem means "to extend," "to stretch out" (as a hand), or "to send" a messenger, report, or item. When used in the Piel stem—as in Obadiah 1:7—the verb gains an intensive or causative force, frequently translated as "to send away," "to drive out," "to dismiss," or "to let loose."

Rather than a peaceful dismissal, the Piel usage often involves decisive compulsion or forceful removal. Depending on the context, shalach can designate:

  • The official sending of an ambassador, servant, or prophet.

  • The forceful driving out or exile of a population.

  • The release or letting loose of something previously restrained.

  • The stretching forth of a hand in harm, blessing, or task.

In Obadiah 1:7, the Piel stem underscores the physical force and urgency with which Edom's former allies push them out to the border.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament, shalach functions as a primary verb of divine and human commission. God "sends" His prophets to deliver His word, "sends" plagues or deliverance upon nations, and "sends" His Word to perform His sovereign will.

In military and political contexts, shalach describes the dispatching of armies, diplomatic envoys, or the expulsion of defeated populations. When rendered as "force" or "driven out" in English translations, the nuance focuses on the lack of choice on the part of the recipient: those who are sent off by shalach in such settings are ejected beyond a boundary or domain by superior power or treacherous deception.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of shalach in judgment contexts reflects divine retribution (lex talionis). Edom stood by and participated in Israel's displacement and plunder; consequently, Edom itself is subjected to displacement.

Furthermore, the passage highlights the ultimate vanity of reliance on human strength and pagan treaties over God's covenant loyalty. Those whom Edom trusted as "men of your confederacy" become the agents who "send away" or "force" Edom to its borders. God's ultimate sovereignty is manifested through historical events, where human betrayal serves as the instrument through which divine righteousness and holy judgment are executed.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, the author employs a rapid succession of parallel clauses to detail Edom's complete undoing by its trusted inner circle: "All the men of your confederacy shall force (shalach) you to the border; the men at peace with you shall deceive you and prevail against you."

The verb shalach is grammatically set in the Piel stem, perfect form, third person plural. The Piel stem expresses an intensive action—here, an aggressive pushing or driving out. The third person plural identifies the subjects as Edom's allies ("men of your confederacy").

The passage highlights a striking reversal of expectations:

  • Edom believed its allies were partners in security; instead, those allies turn upon Edom and force them out to the frontier.

  • The phrase "to the border" indicates total ejection from their homeland and secure strongholds. Edom is not merely counseled or escorted; they are pushed to the absolute periphery of their territory.

  • The syntax connects shalach with terms of peace and fellowship ("eat your bread"), highlighting the irony that close covenantal table-fellows become the active drivers of Edom's expulsion.

  • Psalms 107:20 – "He sent (shalach) His word and healed them, and delivered them from their destructions."

  • Ezekiel 3:5 – "For you are not sent (shalach) to a people of unfamiliar speech and of hard language, but to the house of Israel."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural symmetry of Obadiah 1:7 reveals a progressive downfall:

1. Allies force (shalach) Edom to the border.

2. Friends deceive and prevail against Edom.

3. Table-companions set a trap underneath Edom.

The initial action described by shalach sets the physical drama in motion: Edom finds itself pushed out to the margins of its own geography by the very neighbors in whom it placed its confidence. The prophetic author demonstrates that when divine protection is forfeited through pride and violence, human treaties offer no sanctuary.
