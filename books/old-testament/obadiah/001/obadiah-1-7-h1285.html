CONFEDERACY - ( beriyth , Strong's H1285 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word beriyth primarily means "covenant," "treaty," or "binding alliance." In the context of Obadiah 1:7, it is translated as "confederacy," representing the political and military treaties Edom relied upon for national security. Rather than finding safety in these human agreements, Edom experienced devastating betrayal as their own treaty partners turned against them, illustrating the spiritual truth that relying on human strength instead of God's sovereignty leads to ruin.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun beriyth is derived from a root meaning "to bind" or "to fetter," suggesting a bond that constrains both parties. Historically, the establishing of a beriyth was not merely a verbal agreement but a solemn, life-and-death commitment. This is illustrated by the idiom "to cut a covenant" (karat beriyth), which refers to the practice of cutting sacrificial animals in two. The treaty partners would walk between the pieces, symbolically declaring, "May I be torn apart like these animals if I break this pact." In the ancient Near East, a "confederacy" or international treaty was sealed with religious oaths and a shared covenant meal, making betrayal of such an alliance an act of supreme treachery and spiritual rebellion.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Beriyth denotes a formal, legally binding agreement, league, or alliance between two or more parties. It encompasses several distinct categories of relationship:

• A divine covenant initiated by God with humanity (such as with Noah, Abraham, or Israel at Sinai).

• A political treaty or military alliance between nations (a "confederacy" or league).

• A personal pact or social agreement between individuals (such as the friendship covenant between David and Jonathan).

• A domestic covenant, such as marriage.

The word carries the weight of a solemn obligation, establishing a state of peace (shalom) and mutual responsibility between the bound parties.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Beriyth occurs over 280 times in the Old Testament. The word pictures associated with it emphasize binding durability:

• The "Covenant of Salt": Salt represents preservation and incorruptibility. A covenant of salt is an unbreakable, enduring alliance (Numbers 18:19).

• The "Passing Under the Rod": This refers to the shepherd's counting and securing of the sheep, representing being brought into the protective bond of a covenant relationship (Ezekiel 20:37).

• The "Cutting" of the Covenant: Highlighting the bloody seriousness of treaty-making and the curse of death that accompanies treaty violation.

4. Theological Implications

The contrast between human beriyth and divine beriyth is a major theological theme. God's covenants are grounded in His unchanging character, mercy (chesed), and truth. They are steadfast and eternal. Conversely, human alliances—such as Edom’s "confederacy"—are inherently fragile and vulnerable to human depravity, greed, and political shift. Edom’s pride led them to trust in their geopolitical alliances rather than the living God. Scripture consistently warns God's people against entering into ungodly alliances (beriyth) with pagan nations, as such treaties inevitably lead to spiritual compromise and eventual ruin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, the prophet pronounces judgment on Edom: "All the men in your confederacy (beriyth) have forced you to the border." Edom had retreated into their secure mountain fortresses of Petra, boasting of their wisdom and impregnability. They trusted in their confederates—likely neighboring Arab tribes, Moabites, or Ammonites—with whom they had formal treaties of mutual defense.

The passage contains a sharp, ironic twist. The very partners of their beriyth did not come to their defense; instead, they drove Edom out to their own borders. Those who ate Edom's bread laid a trap beneath them. The context demonstrates that when God decrees the downfall of a proud nation, no human treaty, military alliance, or political confederacy can thwart His sovereign hand. Edom's trusted "peace partners" became the instruments of their destruction.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient treaty-making, sharing a meal was the ultimate sign of goodwill and covenant loyalty. Obadiah 1:7 underscores this by saying, "Those who eat your bread have laid a trap for you." To eat bread with someone and then betray them was regarded as the most despicable form of treachery in the ancient Near Eastern world. This exact imagery of covenant betrayal is echoed in Psalm 41:9 ("My close friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted his heel against me"), a verse Jesus quoted in John 13:18 to describe the betrayal of Judas Iscariot.
