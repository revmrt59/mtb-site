PREVAIL - ( yakol , Strong's H3201 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yakol refers to the capacity, ability, or power to achieve an end, often translated as "to be able," "can," "overcome," or "prevail." In the context of Obadiah 1:7, it describes the tragic betrayal of Edom by her trusted allies who "prevail" against her. Rather than a standard military conquest by an open enemy, this term highlights how Edom’s close treaty partners successfully used their position, intimacy, and deceptive schemes to completely overpower, deceive, and defeat them.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of yakol fundamentally relates to the possession of power, capability, or endurance. In physical, legal, or military contexts, it denotes the tipping point in a struggle where one party gains decisive mastery or successfully endures a heavy trial. In the ancient Near East, covenant alliances and treaties were sacred, solemn pacts often sealed with shared meals and oaths. For one's treaty partners to "prevail" against them represents a total collapse of relational and national security. It paints a picture of a victim who is utterly helpless because those they trusted to protect them have turned their own strength and insider knowledge against them to secure a total victory.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yakol occurs nearly 200 times in the Old Testament and carries several specific nuances depending on its context:

• To be able, to have power, or to possess the capability to perform an action.

• To overcome, conquer, or gain the victory in a physical conflict, lawsuit, or spiritual struggle.

• To endure, withstand, or tolerate a difficult situation or pressure.

• In Obadiah 1:7, the verb is used transitively with a hostile preposition, meaning to successfully exert superior force or cunning over another, resulting in their complete subjugation and ruin.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Power to Overcome: In contexts of warfare, yakol is the word used to measure whether one army has the physical or strategic capability to defeat another. When a nation "prevails," it has successfully neutralised the defenses of its opponent.

• The Struggle of Faith: The term is famously used in Jacob’s wrestling match with the Angel of the Lord. Jacob refused to let go and "prevailed," illustrating a relentless, divinely enabled endurance that wins a blessing.

• The Limits of Human Power: Frequently, the Old Testament uses the negative form of yakol ("not able") to describe human limitations. No matter how wise or strong men believe they are, they are ultimately unable to thwart the sovereign plans of God.

4. Theological Implications

• The Illusion of Human Alliances: Edom trusted in political treaties, military confederacies, and mutual defense pacts. The use of yakol here shows that human security apart from God is a dangerous delusion. When God decrees judgment, He can easily turn a nation's closest allies into the very instruments that overpower them.

• Sovereignty and Human Ability: Human capability is entirely subject to divine permission. Edom’s allies could only "prevail" because God withdrew His protection from prideful Edom. God rules over the outcomes of human schemes, humbling those who rely on their own strength or the strength of others.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, Edom’s pride is thoroughly exposed. They believed their high, rocky dwellings made them impregnable. This false security was bolstered by their "confederacy" and "the men at peace." The prophet uses Hebrew parallelism to describe a sudden, shocking betrayal: first, their allies drive them to the border; second, they deceive them; third, they "prevail" against them; fourth, they set a trap under them. The placement of yakol shows that this was not an honorable battle where Edom fought bravely and was simply outmatched; rather, it was an insidious betrayal where the very people who ate Edom’s bread used their access to successfully conquer and ruin them from the inside out.

• Jeremiah 20:10: "For I heard many mocking: 'Fear on every side!' 'Report,' they say, 'and we will report it!' All my acquaintances watched for my stumbling, saying, 'Perhaps he can be induced; Then we will prevail against him, And we will take our revenge on him.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A profound irony lies in the timing of Edom's defeat. Edom was internationally renowned for its wisdom and counsel. Yet, their wise men were completely blind to the treachery of their allies. The phrase "those who eat your bread" refers to a covenant of hospitality. In the ancient Near East, sharing bread was a sacred pledge of mutual protection. To break this bond and "prevail" against a covenant partner was considered the ultimate, most despicable act of betrayal. This closely mirrors the heartbreak of Psalm 41:9—where a close friend lifts up his heel against the psalmist—a passage later applied by Jesus to the betrayal of Judas Iscariot.
