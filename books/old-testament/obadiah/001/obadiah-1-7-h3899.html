BREAD - ( lechem , Strong's H3899 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word lechem represents the foundational element of physical sustenance, hospitality, and covenantal relationship in the ancient Near East. While translated literally as "bread" or "food," it carries profound theological weight, symbolizing life itself and the mutual obligations of peace and loyalty shared between those who break bread together. In the context of Obadiah 1:7, lechem serves as a poignant symbol of ultimate betrayal. Edom's trusted political allies—those who sat at their table and consumed their bread—grossly violated the sacred covenant of hospitality by turning against them. This study explores how a word synonymous with life and fellowship became, through human treachery, a backdrop for deception and judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, bread (lechem) was not merely a side dish or a culinary option; it was the primary staple of daily survival. The process of making bread—from sowing grain, harvesting, grinding, kneading, to baking—demanded daily labor and depended entirely on God’s provision of rain and fertile soil. Consequently, sharing bread with someone was a deeply significant act. It was the ultimate gesture of peace, hospitality, and covenant. To invite someone to eat lechem at your table was to pledge your protection, loyalty, and friendship to them, and to expect the same in return. Breaking bread established a sacred bond that was not to be broken under any circumstances.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun lechem refers to bread, food, grain, or sustenance. It is derived from the verbal root lacham, which means to feed on or consume (and in some contexts, to fight or battle, representing the struggle for survival). The semantic range of lechem includes:

  • Specific baked loaves of wheat or barley.

  • General food or provisions necessary for daily sustenance.

  • The fruit of agricultural labor (grain or harvest).

  • Figuratively, the source of life, comfort, or fellowship.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, lechem is used in rich literal and metaphorical expressions:

  • The Bread of Heaven: God miraculously sustained Israel in the wilderness with manna, described as bread from heaven, reinforcing their absolute dependence on His daily grace.

  • The Bread of Adversity: In times of judgment or grief, scripture speaks of eating the "bread of adversity" or "bread of tears," where physical nourishment is mingled with sorrow.

  • Covenantal Table Fellowship: When Melchizedek met Abraham, he brought out bread (lechem) and wine, signaling a covenant of peace and mutual blessing.

  • The Snare of Betrayal: In poetic and prophetic literature, eating someone's bread and then turning against them is portrayed as the deepest, most unnatural form of treachery.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, lechem points to God as the ultimate Sustainer of life. Every loaf of bread is a testament to His faithfulness to His creation. Furthermore, because sharing bread represents a covenant relationship, it mirrors God’s desire for fellowship with His people. The Showbread (literally "Bread of the Presence") kept in the Tabernacle symbolized God's perpetual communion and covenant with the twelve tribes of Israel. When humans violate the trust of shared lechem, they are not merely breaking a social contract; they are violating a sacred, God-ordained boundary of relational integrity. This biblical motif reaches its climax in the New Testament when Judas Iscariot betrays Jesus immediately after dipping his bread into the dish with Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, the prophet pronounces judgment on the proud nation of Edom. The NKJV reads: "All the men in your alliance have dismissed you to the border; the men at peace with you have deceived you and prevailed against you. Those who eat your bread have laid a trap for you." Here, "your bread" (lachmeka, a pronominal form of lechem) is used metonymically for Edom's close allies and confederates. Edom had relied on secure political alliances, trusting in those with whom they shared table fellowship. By eating Edom’s bread, these nations had bound themselves to loyalty. Yet, they exploited this intimate access to set a trap. The passage underscores the total collapse of Edom’s security: the very people who received sustenance and hospitality from Edom’s hand became the instruments of their destruction. It vividly illustrates the folly of placing ultimate trust in human alliances rather than in the sovereign Lord.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 14:18: "Then Melchizedek king of Salem brought out bread and wine; he was the priest of God Most High."

  • Exodus 16:4: "Then the LORD said to Moses, 'Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you. And the people shall go out and gather a certain quota every day, that I may test them, whether they will walk in My law or not.'"

  • Psalm 41:9: "Even my own familiar friend in whom I trusted, Who ate my bread, Has lifted up his heel against me."

  • Proverbs 22:9: "He who has a generous eye will be blessed, For he gives of his bread to the poor."

7. Other Relevant Insights

For the pastor and teacher, lechem in Obadiah 1:7 offers a powerful warning against misplaced trust and spiritual pride. Edom felt secure because of their strategic geographic location in the high rocks and their strong diplomatic ties with neighboring nations. However, human treaties are fragile and easily broken when self-interest prevails. The ultimate betrayal of Edom by those who ate their lechem serves as an Old Testament shadow of the betrayal of Jesus Christ by Judas, who fulfilled the words of Psalm 41:9. While human tables of fellowship can be marred by deception, the table of the Lord stands secure. True security is found not in worldly networks or material provisions, but in the Bread of Life who offers a covenant that can never be broken.
