MEN - ( enosh , Strong's 582 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word enosh refers to mortal, frail humanity, highlighting the inherent weakness, vulnerability, and transient nature of mankind in contrast to the eternal, all-powerful Creator. In its plural form (anashim or the construct anshe), it serves as the general term for "men," often designating specific groups defined by a relationship, alliance, or shared activity. In Obadiah 1:7, the term identifies Edom's trusted political allies—the "men" of their confederacy and peace—exposing the fatal folly of relying on mortal, untrustworthy human relationships instead of the sovereign God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun enosh is distinct from other Hebrew words for man, such as adam (humanity from the dust) or ish (man as an individual of status or strength). The root of enosh points to being weak, frail, or terminally ill. It paints a picture of humanity defined by post-Fall vulnerability, subject to mortality, physical decay, and moral instability. When used in the plural construct (anshe), it denotes "men of" a specific association, gathering individuals into a shared identity. In the ancient Near East, political alliances were cemented by personal treaties between leaders, binding these "men of covenant" together in mutual defense.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Enosh refers to a mortal man, mankind, or a human being, with an underlying emphasis on physical frailty and mortality.

• In the plural (anashim or construct anshe), it refers to men, people, or specific associates, companions, and allies.

• In the context of Obadiah 1:7, the term is used in construct forms (anshe beriteka and anshe shelomeka) to specifically mean "the men of your confederacy" (covenant allies) and "the men at peace with you."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Mortality Contrast: The word is frequently used in poetic Old Testament texts to contrast the greatness of God with the insignificance of mortal man. Man (enosh) is like grass, passing away quickly, while God remains forever.

• Collective Alliances: The plural form often depicts groups bound by a common purpose, whether for good or evil. In military and political narratives, the "men of" a city or king represent those who share a common destiny.

• Treacherous Companionship: In treaty language, to be the "men of one's peace" is a word picture of close, shared table-fellowship and mutual protection. To have these very "men" turn against you is the ultimate picture of betrayal.

4. Theological Implications

• The Folly of Human Trust: The Scriptures consistently warn against putting ultimate trust in mortal man (enosh), whose breath is in his nostrils and who cannot save. Edom's reliance on their political allies was a form of idolatry that substituted human treaties for divine security.

• Divine Sovereignty over Human Alliances: God rules over the political arrangements of nations. When He decrees the downfall of a proud nation, He can easily turn their closest human allies into the instruments of their destruction.

• Depravity and Treachery: The betrayal of Edom by the "men of their peace" reflects the deceitful nature of the fallen human heart. Apart from God's grace, human covenants are fragile and easily broken for selfish gain.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, the prophet addresses the pride of Edom. Nestled in the secure, rocky clefts of Petra, Edom believed they were invincible. They relied heavily on their wisdom, military strength, and secure political alliances. The prophet uses anshe repeatedly to dismantle this false security. The very "men" they trusted—their treaty partners and friends who ate their bread—are the ones who drive them to the border, deceive them, and lay a trap beneath their feet. The repetition of "men" emphasizes that Edom's ruin did not come from a distant, expected enemy, but from the intimate circle of mortal men they foolishly trusted.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 8:4 - "What is man that You are mindful of him, And the son of man that You visit him?"

• Psalm 9:20 - "Put them in fear, O LORD, That the nations may know themselves to be but men. Selah"

• Isaiah 51:7 - "Listen to Me, you who know righteousness, You people in whose heart is My law: Do not fear the reproach of men, Nor be afraid of their insults."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Covenant of Bread: In ancient Near Eastern culture, sharing bread was a sacred act of covenant making. The phrase "Those who eat your bread" in Obadiah 1:7 (implied as "the men of your bread") indicates a deep level of betrayal. To eat someone's bread and then lay a trap for them was considered the most heinous form of treason, echoing the betrayal of David by Ahithophel (Psalm 41:9) and Jesus by Judas Iscariot (John 13:18).

• Spiritual Blindness: Edom pridefully believed they possessed superior wisdom. Yet, they were completely blind to the treachery of the "men" closest to them. The verse concludes with, "No one has understanding in him," showing how trusting in mortal man strips away true spiritual discernment.
