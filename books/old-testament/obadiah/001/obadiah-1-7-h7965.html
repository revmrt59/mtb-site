PEACE - ( shalom , Strong's H7965 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shalom goes far beyond the mere absence of conflict; it represents wholeness, completeness, safety, and sound relationship. In Obadiah 1:7, however, this beautiful word is used with tragic irony. Edom placed its ultimate trust in "men of peace"—allies and friends who bound themselves to Edom in covenant loyalty. Instead of experiencing the security shalom promises, Edom was utterly betrayed, proving that true, unshakable peace cannot be found in human alliances, but only in covenant relationship with the living God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb of shalom is shalam, which means to be safe, completed, finished, or to make amends and make something whole. Historically, this root was used to describe a wall with no breaches, a stone that is uncut and whole, or a transaction that has been paid in full, restoring balance and harmony between two parties. In the ancient Near East, shalom was the standard greeting and the basis of covenant relationships. To be in shalom with someone meant sharing a state of mutual well-being, trust, and integrated life where nothing was broken or missing.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun shalom refers to completeness, soundness, welfare, peace, and safety. It signifies a state of health, prosperity, and security, both materially and spiritually. In relational terms, it denotes friendship, harmony, and the faithful fulfillment of covenant obligations. In a political or military context, it represents the absence of war, the cessation of hostilities, or the establishment of a treaty.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • Completeness and Wholeness: A state where nothing is missing, fractured, or damaged. It refers to the preservation of life and property in its intended, undamaged state.

  • Relational Harmony: Covenant partnerships, friendships, and familial relationships characterized by mutual care, absolute loyalty, and trust.

  • Security and Well-being: Living without fear of sudden destruction, betrayal, or lack.

  • The Greeting of Peace: Wishing another person the fullness of physical health and spiritual vitality.

4. Theological Implications

True shalom is fundamentally a gift from God, who is Himself the source of peace. Because God is the Author of peace, genuine shalom is always tied to righteousness. Where there is no righteousness, there can be no true peace. Human attempts to manufacture shalom through political treaties, military strength, or compromised alliances apart from God always end in failure and judgment. The ultimate fulfillment of shalom is messianic, realized in the Prince of Peace, who establishes an everlasting kingdom of reconciliation and cosmic restoration.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, Edom (the descendants of Esau) lived in the secure, rocky heights of Petra, boasting in their self-sufficiency, wisdom, and military alliances. The phrase "men at peace with you" (literally, "men of your peace" or anshe shelomeka) refers to Edom’s close political allies. Edom trusted these covenant partners to guarantee their safety and mutual prosperity.

The tragic irony in Obadiah's prophecy is that the very people bound to Edom by covenants of shalom became the instruments of their destruction. The "men of peace" deceived them, lured them to the border, and laid a trap under them. This highlights the fragility of human-centered peace. When God decrees judgment, worldly alliances dissolve into betrayal. Edom sought shalom through foreign treaties while mocking the plight of their brother Jacob, showing that peace built on the ruin of others is an illusion.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 6:26: "The LORD lift up His countenance upon you, And give you peace."

  • Psalm 119:165: "Great peace have those who love Your law, And nothing causes them to stumble."

  • Isaiah 26:3: "You will keep him in perfect peace, Whose mind is stayed on You, Because he trusts in You."

  • Jeremiah 6:14: "They have also healed the hurt of My people slightly, Saying, 'Peace, peace!' When there is no peace."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), shalom is almost always translated by the Greek word eirene. The New Testament concept of peace is deeply saturated with this Hebrew understanding of wholeness and reconciliation with God through Christ. Furthermore, the betrayal of Edom by the "men of their peace" who "eat your bread" closely mirrors the betrayal of Jesus by Judas Iscariot. This connection is underscored in Psalm 41:9, which uses similar covenantal language to describe a close friend turning against the righteous.
