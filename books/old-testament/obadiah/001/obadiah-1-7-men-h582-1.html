MEN - (ʼĕnôwsh, Strong's H582)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun ʾenosh fundamentally refers to "man," "mankind," or human beings, often designating specific groups of men or allies when used in plural construct forms. In Obadiah 1:7, the word is used in descriptions of Edom's international allies ("men of your covenant," "men of your peace") to highlight how Edom's trusted human confederates and diplomatic partners would ultimately turn against her, executing God's judgment by deceiving her and driving her into exile.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew term ʾenosh functions across the Old Testament to designate human beings individually, corporately, or generically. In poetic and wisdom texts, the singular form often contrasts weak, mortal mankind with the transcendent power and majesty of God. In narrative and prophetic texts, plural and construct forms frequently designate specific groups of men defined by a shared role, community, or relationship—such as soldiers, citizens, or covenant partners.

In the prophetic book of Obadiah, the author pronounces divine judgment against the nation of Edom for her pride and her violence against her brother nation, Judah. Edom considered herself invincible due to her rocky mountain fortresses and her strategic international alliances. Obadiah 1:7 addresses Edom directly, exposing the total collapse of those human trust networks.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, ʾenosh denotes "man," "mankind," or in plural forms, "men" or "persons." The term itself simple identifies human beings as distinct from God, animals, or spiritual entities.

When ʾenosh enters into construct phrases with other nouns (such as covenant or peace), the word identifies the human subjects involved in that relationship. Lexically, ʾenosh does not inherently mean "ally" or "traitor"; rather, it specifies the human persons who are bound by the covenant or relationship named in the governing phrase.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Hebrew Scriptures, ʾenosh displays a range of usage depending on genre and context:

  • Mortal Humanity: In poetic literature, the singular noun often underlines human frailty, mortality, and dependence when contrasted with the eternal Creator.

  • Grouped Associates or Soldiers: In narrative and historical texts, plural forms refer to bands of men, followers, townspeople, or military companions gathered under a leader or shared purpose.

  • Covenant and Diplomatic Partners: In prophetic discourse, the word appears in construct phrases to designate men bound by political pacts, treaties, or social obligations.

It is important to maintain lexical precision: the noun ʾenosh identifies human beings, while the moral character, durability, or deceitfulness of their actions comes entirely from the surrounding syntax and narrative context.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of ʾenosh within prophetic judgment passages underscores a core biblical theme: the peril of trusting in human alliances rather than the living God. Edom placed her confidence in her political savvy, defensive terrain, and foreign treaties. By identifying Edom's betrayers as the very "men" of her covenant and peace, Scripture illustrates the ultimate fragility of human promises apart from divine righteousness.

Theologically, human beings (ʾenosh) remain fallible and finite. When nations or individuals exalt human strength and contractual pacts above God's sovereign authority, those very human connections can become the instruments of divine chastisement and disillusionment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, the target word appears in construct expressions rendered in English as "men of your confederacy" (or covenant) and "men at peace with you" (literally, "men of your peace"). Obadiah uses these designations to construct a bitter irony: the very diplomatic partners in whom Edom placed her absolute trust would be the ones to force her to the border, deceive her, and prevail against her.

Textually, ʾenosh functions to identify the agents of Edom's downfall as her closest human associates. The verse continues by describing those who eat her bread setting a trap for her, completing the picture of intimate betrayal. The passage demonstrates that human treaties offer no protection against the decree of God; the allies Edom depended upon for peace became the active agents of her destruction.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Job 7:17 — "What is man, that You should magnify him, / And that You should set Your heart on him..." Here the singular target word "man" refers to fragile human existence under God's intense scrutiny.

  • Isaiah 2:17 — "The loftiness of man shall be bowed down, / And the haughtiness of men shall be brought low..." In this prophetic context, "men" describes human beings in their pride, destined to be humbled in the day of the Lord.

  • Jeremiah 39:17 — "'But I will deliver you in that day,' says the LORD, 'and you shall not be given into the hand of the men of whom you are afraid.'" Here "men" designates threatening human adversaries whose power is restricted by divine deliverance.

  • 1 Samuel 23:13 — "So David and his men, about six hundred, arose and departed from Keilah and went wherever they could go." In this historical narrative, "and his men" refers to the specific band of loyal followers accompanying David during his exile.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The downfall of Edom in Obadiah 1:7 serves as a classic biblical illustration of misplaced confidence. Edom believed her international standing was secure because of her treaties with neighboring "men of peace." However, human covenant partners without divine truth are prone to self-interest and betrayal. The verse demonstrates that when God brings a proud nation into judgment, the very human networks constructed for security become the channels of ruin.
