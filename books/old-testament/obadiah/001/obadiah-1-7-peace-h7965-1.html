PEACE - (shâlôwm, Strong's H7965)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun shalom denotes wholeness, completeness, sound condition, safety, and harmonious relationships. Beyond the mere absence of conflict, it signifies a state of well-being, pact-based security, and relational integrity. In Obadiah 1:7, shalom appears in the idiom "men at peace with you" ("men of your peace"), referring to Edom's trusted diplomatic allies and covenant partners. In this specific verse, the word highlights the depth of Edom's impending betrayal: the very allies bound to them in formal peace and reciprocal trust turn against them, exposing Edom's fatal lack of discernment and proving that human alliances built on pride offer no shelter against divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The prophet Obadiah brings a divine indictment against Edom, the neighboring nation descended from Esau. Throughout its history, Edom maintained a tense relationship with Israel (descended from Jacob). When Jerusalem was attacked and plundered by foreign invaders, Edom did not merely stand aloof; they gloated over Judah’s destruction, joined in plundering the city, and intercepted Jewish fugitives. In response, God announced that Edom’s worldly confidence—their mountain strongholds, their wisdom, and their international military alliances—would fail completely.

In Obadiah 1:7, the prophet specifically addresses Edom’s foreign alliances. Edom believed itself secure behind a network of treaties and international agreements. The noun shalom is employed within a parallel poetic structure alongside terms for political confederacy ("men of your covenant") and shared table fellowship ("those who eat your bread"). In the Ancient Near East, entering a state of shalom with a neighboring power involved formal oaths, mutual commitments, and shared economic interests. Edom relied upon these human pledges of peace for its national security.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shalom expresses the core concept of wholeness, soundness, health, safety, and unbroken integrity. Standard Hebrew lexicography outlines several key dimensions within its semantic range:

  • Soundness of body or condition (health, safety, welfare).

  • Harmony in interpersonal or political relations (tranquility, absence of strife).

  • Covenantal or contractual accord (treaties, pacts, state of friendship).

  • Wholeness and prosperity under God’s order.

In Obadiah 1:7, the target word appears in the masculine singular absolute state. It functions within a construct phrase ("men of your peace" / "men at peace with you"). Here, the word designates a relational state of non-hostility and formal alliance, emphasizing that those bound to Edom in a recognized status of peace and trust become the very instruments of Edom's downfall.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Old Testament, shalom functions across several distinct contexts:

  • Personal Well-Being and Health: It is frequently used in greetings and inquiries regarding an individual's or family's health and safety.

  • Relational and Social Harmony: It describes quietness and freedom from strife among neighbors, families, and tribes within a community.

  • Political and Military Alliances: In diplomatic contexts, shalom signifies a state of non-aggression or mutual defense established between nations or rulers.

  • Covenantal Peace with God: In Israel's theological framework, ultimate shalom is grounded in right relationship with God, who promises peace, protection, and fruitful life to those who walk in His ways.

In diplomatic contexts, "men of peace" (anshe shalom) describes trusted allies who have sworn friendship. However, human treaties of shalom remain inherently vulnerable to human treachery, political expediency, and divine sovereignty. When nations trust in human shalom while standing opposed to God's righteousness, that peace yields false security.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical usage of shalom carries significant theological depth across Scripture:

  • God as the True Source of Shalom: Scripture establishes that genuine peace and security are gifts from God, not products of human political maneuvering or military power. Human efforts to secure shalom apart from God are fragile and temporary.

  • The Illusory Peace of Human Reliance: Edom's judgment demonstrates the folly of placing ultimate trust in human alliances. A nation or individual may claim to have secured shalom through diplomacy, wealth, or human wisdom, but if they stand under divine condemnation, those earthly peace structures collapse.

  • Treachery as Divine Retribution: In the prophetic perspective, Edom's betrayal by its own "men of peace" reflects divine irony. Because Edom acted treacherously toward their brother Jacob, God allowed Edom’s trusted allies to act treacherously toward them. Human instruments of peace become instruments of judgment when God executes His righteous purpose.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, the word shalom functions within a three-fold poetic parallelism that describes the complete collapse of Edom's international security network:

1. "The men in confederacy with you" (anshe beritekha - men of your covenant) have brought you to the border.

2. "The men at peace with you" (anshe shelomekha - men of your peace) have deceived you and prevailed against you.

3. "Those who eat your bread" (lakhmekha - your bread) lay a trap under you.

This structural alignment intensifies the sense of complete, intimate betrayal. Shalom here is not an internal feeling of calm; it refers to concrete, obligation-bound partners in peace. To be "men of peace" implied shared security and mutual protection.

The immediate context emphasizes Edom's complete lack of discernment regarding these allies: "No one has understanding of it." Edom prided itself on its legendary wise men, yet they failed to recognize that their peace partners were actively undermining them. The word shalom highlights the dramatic contrast between Edom's perceived state of peace and safety and the actual reality of betrayal being executed by those very peace partners.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 26:29 – "that you will do us no harm, since we have not touched you, and since we have done nothing to you but good and have sent you away in peace (shalom). You are now the blessed of the LORD."

  • Here shalom refers to peaceful, non-hostile relations and safe departure in a covenantal context between Abimelech and Isaac.

  • Psalms 28:3 – "Do not take me away with the wicked and with the workers of iniquity, who speak peace (shalom) to their neighbors, but evil is in their hearts."

  • This verse highlights hypocritical peace, where the word shalom is spoken outwardly as a posture of friendship while deceit and malice reside within—a parallel to the deceptive peace described in Obadiah 1:7.

  • Psalms 29:11 – "The LORD will give strength to His people; The LORD will bless His people with peace (shalom)."

  • David contrasts the power of God's voice in creation with the divine gift of shalom bestowed by God upon His covenant people.

  • Jeremiah 33:9 – "Then it shall be to Me a name of joy, a praise, and an honor before all nations of the earth, who shall hear all the good that I do to them; they shall fear and tremble for all the goodness and all the prosperity (shalom) that I provide for it."

  • In this prophetic promise of restoration, shalom encompasses complete welfare, security, and restoration granted by God to Jerusalem, inducing awe among the surrounding nations.

  • Malachi 2:6 – "The law of truth was in his mouth, and injustice was not found on his lips. He walked with Me in peace (shalom) and equity, and turned many away from iniquity."

  • Here shalom characterizes a faithful covenant relationship marked by moral uprightness, harmony, and walk with God.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A key thematic connection emerges when comparing Obadiah 1:7 with the broader biblical theology of betrayal by intimate peace partners. The phrase "men at peace with you" (anshe shelomekha) reflects the biblical pattern where table fellowship and peace treaties represent the highest degree of human loyalty.

In Obadiah, Edom's reliance on human shalom is directly contrasted with their proud violence against Jacob (Obadiah 1:10). Because Edom breached brotherly ties, God ordained that Edom's own diplomatic shalom partners would turn against them. This illustrates a recurring canonical principle: peace established apart from righteousness and divine truth is illusory, and those who trust in untrustworthy human pacts while opposing God's covenant purposes will find their human peace turning into ruin.
