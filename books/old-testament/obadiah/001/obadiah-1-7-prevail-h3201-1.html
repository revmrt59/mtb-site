PREVAIL - (yâkôl, Strong's H3201)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yakhol fundamentally means "to be able," "to have power or capacity," "to endure," or "to prevail/overcome." In legal, military, and relational contexts, it often describes one party gaining the upper hand or possessing the necessary power to accomplish a purpose against an opponent. In Obadiah 1:7, yakhol describes how Edom's trusted confederates and covenant allies turn against them, overpower them, and prevail over them in devastating betrayal. Rather than providing security, Edom's political alliances become the very instrument through which their defense is broken and their destruction is secured.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root yakhol is a standard Semitic verb widespread in ancient Hebrew literature to express physical capability, mental or emotional endurance, legal standing, or military superiority. It encompasses both internal capacity (being physically or emotionally able to perform an act) and external dynamic power (prevailing over a obstacle, circumstance, or enemy).

In the ancient Near East, international relations were frequently governed by formal treaties, trade pacts, and military alliances. Edom, situated in the rugged, easily defensible mountain region of Mount Seir, relied heavily upon its natural topography, strategic trade routes, and diplomatic treaties with surrounding nations. To the human eye, Edom appeared secure and invincible. The prophetic announcement of Obadiah addresses this false sense of security, revealing that human capability and diplomatic pacts are entirely subject to God's sovereign hand.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, yakhol refers to capacity, capability, or power. Its primary lexical senses include:

  • To be able / capable: To possess the physical strength, resources, or authority required to complete an action.

  • To endure / bear: To be able to withstand pressure, suffering, or a heavy presence without collapsing.

  • To prevail / overpower: When followed by the preposition l- (meaning "against" or "over"), it signifies overcoming, mastering, or defeating an adversary.

It is important to distinguish the pure lexical meaning of yakhol—having capacity or prevailing—from the broader theological conclusions drawn from its biblical contexts. The word itself simply denotes ability or victory; the moral quality or divine purpose behind that prevailing action is determined by the surrounding text.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Hebrew Scriptures, yakhol operates in a variety of situational contexts:

  • Physical Capability and Limitation: It describes human physical ability, such as carrying a heavy load, building a structure, or standing before a daunting task. Conversely, when negated, it highlights human weakness, exhaustion, or incapacity.

  • Emotional and Spiritual Capacity: The verb is used to describe whether a person can endure intense grief, hold back deep emotion, or stand in the presence of overwhelming holy majesty.

  • Military and Legal Superiority: In warfare, yakhol denotes defeating an opponent or successfully besieging a city. When a nation or leader "cannot prevail," it signifies their defeat or the frustration of their military strategy.

Because yakhol spans these different domains, its exact nuance depends heavily on whether it appears with a negative particle, what preposition accompanies it, and who the subject and object are.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of yakhol throughout Scripture underscores a fundamental biblical theme: human power is finite and derived, whereas divine power is absolute.

Human beings frequently place their trust in political alliances, physical defenses, or personal wisdom, assuming these will enable them to prevail against trouble. However, Scripture demonstrates that without divine sanction, human capacity fails. When God determines to bring judgment, no military power or diplomatic treaty can enable a nation to stand. Conversely, when God protects a person or nation, no earthly force can prevail against them. The word thus serves as a theological marker distinguishing genuine, God-given strength from illusory human self-sufficiency.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:7, the prophet confronts Edom's severe pride and misplaced confidence. Edom boasted in her impregnable rock-dwellings and her clever political alliances. Obadiah dismantles this pride through a series of poetic parallel phrases describing total covenantal betrayal:

  • "All the men of your confederacy shall force you to the border;"

  • "The men at peace with you shall deceive you and prevail (yakhol) against you;"

  • "Those who eat your bread lay a trap for you."

The target occurrence in Obadiah 1:7 uses the Qal stem, perfect form, 3rd person, plural morphology. The Qal stem expresses the simple, active action of prevailing, while the perfect form presents this future prophetic judgment as a completed, settled reality in the decree of God. The 3rd person plural identifies Edom's trusted allies as the ones carrying out this action.

The contextual irony is sharp: the very allies in whom Edom placed their trust for peace become the agents who possess the capability and upper hand (yakhol) to overpower them. Edom believed their diplomatic partners would make them strong; instead, those partners turned, deceived them, and prevailed over them. The immediate context concludes by noting, "No one has understanding in him," highlighting that human wisdom fails precisely when people rely on human strength rather than God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 45:1 – "Then Joseph could not (yakhol) restrain himself before all those who stood by him..."

  • Judges 16:5 – "...and see where his great strength lies, and by what means we may overpower (yakhol) him..."

  • Isaiah 7:1 – "...went up toward Jerusalem to wage war against it, but could not prevail (yakhol) against it."

  • Nehemiah 4:10 – "Then Judah said, 'The strength of the laborers is failing, and there is so much rubbish that we are not able (yakhol) to build the wall.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Near Eastern treaty culture, eating bread together signified a solemn bond of covenant peace and mutual defense. For table-companions to turn against a covenant partner was considered the ultimate form of treachery. By using yakhol in connection with Edom's peace partners and table-companions, Obadiah emphasizes that human treaties offer no guarantee of protection against the sovereign judgments of God. What Edom viewed as their greatest geopolitical asset became the exact locus of their vulnerability and defeat.
