DAY - (yôwm, Strong's H3117)

Summary:

Lexically, yom is the standard Hebrew word for "day," referring to a 24-hour period, the daylight hours, a general season, or a specific point in time. In Obadiah 1:8, yom refers to a specific, appointed time of divine judgment. Its use here anchors God's promise to dismantle Edom's pride to a certain, impending moment in history, anticipating the broader eschatological "day of the LORD" mentioned later in the prophecy.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew word yom is the most common term for "day" in the Old Testament. In ancient Near Eastern and biblical contexts, time was often measured by the cycles of the sun and the moon, making the "day" a fundamental unit of timekeeping, labor, and religious observance. Beyond its literal calendrical use, the term frequently developed into a marker for specific historical eras or appointed events. In the Prophets, the concept of a specific "day" became a central theological motif, used to designate a time when God would decisively intervene in human history to execute justice, deliver His people, or judge the nations.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yom means "day." Its semantic range is broad, encompassing the daylight hours (as opposed to night), a literal 24-hour cycle, or a general period of time (often translated as "time" or "season"). When pluralized or used in construct phrases, it can refer to a person's lifespan, a king's reign, or even perpetuity (e.g., "all the days").

In Obadiah 1:8, the noun is masculine singular. When combined with the demonstrative pronoun (forming the phrase "in that day"), it points to a specific, distinct occasion rather than a general era.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The biblical usage of yom falls into several distinct categories:

  • Literal/Calendrical: Referring to the days of creation, the Sabbath, or specific dates for festivals.

  • Historical/Biographical: Referring to the era in which someone lived or ruled (e.g., "in the days of...").

  • Idiomatic: Used in phrases to mean "today," "now," or "forever."

  • Prophetic/Eschatological: Used to designate a specific future moment of divine action. The phrase "in that day" (bayyom hahu) is a hallmark of prophetic literature, signaling a shift from present circumstances to God's guaranteed future intervention.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of yom in prophetic literature rests on the sovereignty of God over time and history. God is not a passive observer of human events; He appoints specific "days" for His purposes to be fulfilled. The concept of "that day" assures the reader that human pride, injustice, and rebellion will not continue indefinitely. There is a fixed time on God's calendar when He will address sin and vindicate His righteousness. This brings both a severe warning to the proud and profound comfort to the oppressed, as it guarantees that divine justice is certain.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:8, the LORD asks rhetorically, "Will I not in that day... Even destroy the wise men from Edom?" Here, yom functions as the temporal anchor for God's judgment. Edom was historically renowned for its geographical security and its political wisdom, leading to deep national pride.

By specifying "in that day," the author communicates that Edom's self-sufficiency will suddenly and decisively fail at an appointed time. The wisdom they relied upon to navigate political alliances and secure their borders will be entirely removed by God. Furthermore, this specific "day" of judgment against Edom's wise men serves as a precursor to the universal "day of the LORD" (yom YHWH) explicitly announced in Obadiah 1:15. The use of yom in verse 8 thus connects the immediate, historical judgment of Edom to God's ultimate, overarching justice over all nations.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Zephaniah 1:1 — "The word of the LORD which came to Zephaniah... in the days of Josiah..." Here, the word is used to establish the historical timeframe and era of the prophet's ministry.

  • Joshua 5:9 — "Then the LORD said to Joshua, 'This day I have rolled away the reproach of Egypt from you.'" The word marks a specific, monumental occasion of divine action and covenant renewal.

  • Deuteronomy 4:40 — "You shall therefore keep His statutes and His commandments which I command you today..." The word is used idiomatically to emphasize the immediate, present urgency of covenant obedience.

  • Isaiah 58:3 — "'Why have we fasted,' they say, 'and You have not seen? Why have we afflicted our souls, and You take no notice?' In fact, in the day of your fast you find pleasure..." Here, it refers to a specific calendar day set aside for religious observance.

  • 1 Samuel 2:35 — "Then I will raise up for Myself a faithful priest... and he shall walk before My anointed forever." The Hebrew phrase literally translates to "all the days," demonstrating how yom can be used idiomatically to express perpetuity or continuous time.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The prophetic formula "in that day" often serves a dual purpose in the Minor Prophets. It addresses an impending historical judgment (such as the fall of Edom to invading nations) while simultaneously foreshadowing the final, eschatological judgment. In Obadiah, the "day" of Edom's localized destruction becomes a typological preview of how God will ultimately deal with all human pride and opposition to His kingdom.
