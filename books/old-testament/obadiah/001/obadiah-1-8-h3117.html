DAY - ( yôm , Strong's H3117 )

Summary:

In Obadiah 1:8, the word "day" (yôm) does not refer to a literal twenty-four-hour cycle, but rather to a specific, divinely appointed epoch of judgment. Known in prophetic literature as "the day of the LORD," this phrase points to a decisive moment in history when God sovereignly intervenes to humble human pride, execute perfect justice, and vindicate His covenant people. In this specific context, the "day" marks the sudden and complete dismantling of Edom’s highly esteemed wisdom, military security, and national pride.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primary root of yôm is associated with the warmth of the sun or the period of heat during the daylight hours, contrasting with the cool of the night. In the ancient Near Eastern world, a "day" was often identified by the monumental event that occurred within it. For example, a king's "day" referred to the day of his coronation or a day of major military victory. In the Hebrew Scriptures, this concept elevates to a theological plane. The "day of the LORD" is the day of God's sovereign victory, a time when human agency recedes into the background and God alone is exalted in His power and justice.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun yôm has a broad semantic range determined entirely by its context:

  • The daylight hours (from sunrise to sunset) as opposed to night.

  • A literal twenty-four-hour period (evening and morning).

  • A generic point in time or a lifetime (often translated "days" or "years").

  • A specific, designated era or epoch characterized by a particular event or condition (e.g., "day of trouble," "day of harvest").

  • When used in the prophetic formula "in that day" (bayyôm hahû’), as in Obadiah 1:8, it refers to a future, appointed time of divine visitation, judgment, or salvation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

  • yôm appears over 2,200 times in the Old Testament, making it one of the most common nouns in the Hebrew Bible.

  • The Courtroom Picture: The prophets use "day" to depict a scheduled court date where Yahweh summons the nations to stand trial before His heavenly tribunal.

  • The Day of Battle: It is frequently used as a military metaphor. The "day of Midian" (Isaiah 9:4) or the "day of Jezreel" (Hosea 1:11) refers to decisive historical battles. Similarly, the "day of the LORD" is the day Yahweh goes forth as a divine warrior to defeat His adversaries.

  • The Reversal of Gloating: In Obadiah, there is a deliberate play on the word "day." Edom gloated over Judah's "day of calamity" (Obadiah 1:12). God responds by bringing about "that day" of reckoning for Edom, turning their proud laughter into silent destruction.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of yôm in prophetic contexts centers on the doctrine of divine sovereignty.

  • History is Linear and Purposeful: History is not an endless, cyclical loop of random occurrences. It is moving toward a definite climax—a "day" when God will resolve the problem of evil.

  • The Collapse of Human Sufficiency: When God’s "day" arrives, all human systems of security fail. In Obadiah 1:8, Edom’s intellectual elite (their "wise men") are suddenly blinded, proving that human wisdom is foolishness when confronted by divine judgment.

  • The Imminence of Justice: Though God is long-suffering, His patience has a boundary. The "day" serves as a solemn warning that there is a fixed limit to the rebellion of the nations.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:8, the phrase "Will I not in that day..." acts as a rhetorical guarantee of Edom’s impending doom. Edom was famous throughout the ancient world for its wisdom, particularly associated with the region of Teman. The Edomites lived in high, rocky fortresses (Sela/Petra) and deemed themselves secure from any military threat. By using the term "in that day," God declares that their intellectual and physical defenses will instantly evaporate when His appointed hour arrives. The wisdom they relied on to navigate political crises will be utterly destroyed by the Lord, exposing the futility of trusting in human intellect over the living God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Joel 1:15 - "Alas for the day! For the day of the LORD is at hand; It shall come as destruction from the Almighty."

  • Amos 5:18 - "Woe to you who desire the day of the LORD! For what good is the day of the LORD to you? It will be darkness, and not light."

  • Obadiah 1:12 - "But you should not have gazed on the day of your brother In the day of his captivity; Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah In the day of their destruction; Nor should you have spoken proudly In the day of distress."

  • Zephaniah 1:14 - "The great day of the LORD is near; It is near and hastens quickly. The noise of the day of the LORD is bitter; There the mighty men shall cry out."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew prophecy, the "day" often exhibits a dual horizon. It has a near-historical fulfillment and a far-eschatological fulfillment. The historical fulfillment of Obadiah 1:8 occurred when the Babylonians and Nabataeans eventually conquered and displaced the Edomites, rendering their wise men powerless to save the nation. The ultimate eschatological fulfillment points forward to the final Day of the Lord (Obadiah 1:15), when all nations that oppose God and persecute His people will face final justice, and the kingdom will be the Lord’s (Obadiah 1:21).
