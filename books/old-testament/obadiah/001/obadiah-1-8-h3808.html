NOT - ( lo , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew particle lo is the primary adverb of negation in the Old Testament, expressing absolute, objective, and permanent negation. In Obadiah 1:8, it is paired with a rhetorical question ("Will I not...?") to deliver a powerful, undeniable assertion of divine judgment. Rather than indicating a mere possibility, this negative particle functions as an emphatic guarantee of God's sovereign intervention to dismantle the pride and self-reliance of Edom.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word lo is the most frequent negative particle in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 5,000 times.

• Unlike al, which is subjective and often expresses a temporary deprecation, warning, or wish ("do not"), lo represents objective, unconditional negation. It establishes an absolute boundary.

• In ancient covenantal and legal contexts, lo drawing an unbendable line between what is and what is not. When spoken by Yahweh, it carries the weight of an unalterable decree.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The particle functions as an adverb of negation meaning "no" or "not."

• It signifies the absolute non-existence, non-occurrence, or prohibition of an action, state, or event.

• When combined with the interrogative prefix (halo'), the negation is inverted rhetorically. Instead of expressing doubt, it demands an emphatic, positive agreement, equivalent to "Is it not true that...?" or "Most certainly!"

• In Obadiah 1:8, this rhetorical construction (halo') demands an unconditional affirmative response, turning a question into an absolute declaration of divine action.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Permanent Prohibition: Used extensively in the Decalogue (e.g., "You shall not murder") to denote permanent, absolute moral boundaries.

• Divine Inability to Lie: Used to describe the unalterable character of God, whose words and covenants cannot be negated or broken.

• Rhetorical Certainty: Used by the prophets to arrest the attention of complacent audiences, forcing them to acknowledge undeniable spiritual realities.

4. Theological Implications

• The Inevitability of Judgment: Edom relied heavily on its intellectual wisdom and strategic mountain fortresses. By asking "Will I not... destroy?" God demonstrates that human wisdom is completely powerless when He negates it.

• Sovereign Disruption: God's "not" overrides human "will." Edom's wise men planned for survival, but God's decree cancels their counsel.

• The Failure of Human Wisdom: Apart from God, human understanding eventually comes to nothing. The absolute negation of Edom's wisdom shows that intellectual capability cannot save a nation under divine wrath.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:8, the prophet addresses the proud nation of Edom, which boasted of its wisdom and security in the high clefts of the rock. The city of Teman was internationally famous for its sages.

• The rhetorical question "Will I not in that day...?" functions as a divine irony. Edom believed they were secure and could not be overthrown. God uses the negative particle to show that their security will not remain.

• "In that day" points to the historical day of the Lord's visitation. By using lo, God guarantees that the destruction of Edom's wisdom is already an accomplished reality in His sovereign decree.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 20:13 - "You shall not murder." (Absolute moral negation).

• Genesis 3:4 - "Then the serpent said to the woman, 'You will not surely die.'" (The serpent's deceptive attempt to negate God's absolute warning).

• Jeremiah 23:24 - "'Can anyone hide himself in secret places, So I shall not see him?' says the LORD; 'Do I not fill heaven and earth?' says the LORD." (Double use of the particle to establish the impossibility of hiding from God).

• Amos 3:6 - "If a trumpet is blown in a city, will the people not be afraid? If there is calamity in a city, will the LORD not have done it?" (Rhetorical use to show the absolute certainty of divine cause and effect).

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The structural placement of the negative particle at the very beginning of the sentence in the Hebrew text (halo') gives it supreme emphasis. It is the first sound of the divine oracle in this verse, immediately arresting the hearer's attention.

• This grammatical construction strips away any room for negotiation or doubt. For pastors and teachers, this highlights that God's word offers no middle ground when it comes to the execution of His justice and the fulfillment of His prophecies.
