DESTROY - ( abad , Strong's H6 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb abad in the Hiphil (causative) stem means to destroy, wipe out, or cause to perish. In the context of Obadiah 1:8, it depicts God's sovereign, active judgment against Edom's legendary intellectual pride. By destroying their renowned wise men and removing understanding from the mount of Esau, Yahweh demonstrates that human wisdom is powerless against divine wrath. The word highlights the total collapse of Edom's strategic, political, and philosophical defenses, leaving them completely vulnerable to their impending ruin.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root of the Hebrew verb abad conveys a picture of wandering away, losing oneself, or perishing. It evokes the image of a sheep going astray in a vast desert, vanishing from the safety of the flock, or a path disappearing from view.

• When shifted to its active, causative form, the word picture moves from a passive "disappearing" to an active "eradicating." It presents the vivid image of a sweeping force that clears the landscape, leaving a complete void where there once was structure, strength, or security.

• In the ancient Near East, a nation's stability relied heavily on its wise counsel, strategic thinkers, and geographical defenses. To have these "destroyed" or "lost" meant the immediate collapse of the society's internal architecture.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• In the simple active form (Qal stem), abad means to perish, die, vanish, or be lost. It describes things or people that cease to exist or lose their usefulness.

• In the intensive form (Piel stem), the verb means to cause to perish, ruin, or actively destroy.

• In the causative form (Hiphil stem), which is the exact form used in Obadiah 1:8, the verb means to exterminate, wipe out, or systematically destroy. It indicates an external, sovereign agent who purposefully and definitively brings about the end of a group, resource, or status.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The verb is used extensively throughout the Old Testament to contrast the enduring nature of God's truth with the temporal, perishing nature of human systems.

• It describes the physical destruction of pagan altars, high places, and idols, representing a clean sweep of abominations from the land.

• It is utilized in legal and covenantal warnings where Moses tells Israel that turning to other gods will cause them to "surely perish" from the Promised Land.

• The term paints a picture of a sudden transition from apparent strength and self-sufficiency to absolute nothingness.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereign Execution of Judgment: The use of abad shows that God is the primary agent of history. Edom's intellectual demise is not a natural decline; it is a deliberate act of divine execution.

• The Vulnerability of Human Wisdom: Edom was famous in antiquity for its wisdom, particularly through the region of Teman. God's target in this judgment is their intellectual self-reliance. He demonstrates that when He decides to destroy, no level of human ingenuity, counsel, or philosophy can avert the disaster.

• Covenantal Retribution: Edom stood by and gloated while Jerusalem was plundered. Because they participated in Israel's ruin, God applies the covenant principle of lex talionis (the law of retaliation): Edom will be utterly destroyed in return.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Obadiah 1:8, the word appears as ve-ha'abadti ("and I will destroy"). The first-person singular construction highlights Yahweh's personal involvement.

• The destruction is directed specifically at "the wise men" and "understanding." Edom's mountainous stronghold (the clefts of the rock) made them feel physically secure. But God does not begin by attacking their physical walls; He attacks their mental clarity.

• By destroying their wise counsel, God leaves Edom leaderless, confused, and unable to make rational decisions during the invasion. The removal of intellectual capacity is the precursor to their ultimate physical defeat.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 8:19: "Then it shall be, if you by any means forget the LORD your God, and follow other gods, and serve them and worship them, I testify against you this day that you shall surely perish."

• Esther 4:16: "Go, gather all the Jews who are present in Shushan, and fast for me; neither eat nor drink for three days, night or day. My maids and I will fast likewise. And so I will go to the king, which is against the law; and if I perish, I perish!"

• Psalm 1:6: "For the LORD knows the way of the righteous, But the way of the ungodly shall perish."

• Jeremiah 18:18: "Then they said, 'Come and let us devise plans against Jeremiah; for the law shall not perish from the priest, nor counsel from the wise, nor the word from the prophet.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Jeremiah 49:7 mirrors the warning in Obadiah, asking rhetorically if wisdom has departed from Teman and if their counsel has perished. The parallel shows that the intellectual pride of Edom was widely recognized and that its systematic destruction was a specific focus of divine judgment.

• When God destroys physical possessions, a nation can rebuild; when He destroys their "understanding," they are left without the capacity to recover. The destruction of Edom's wise men serves as a warning that intellectual capability is a gift from God that can be rescinded when used to fuel arrogance and opposition to His purposes.
