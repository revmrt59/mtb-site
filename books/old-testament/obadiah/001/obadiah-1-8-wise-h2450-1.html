WISE - (châkâm, Strong's H2450)

Summary:

The Hebrew adjective and substantive chakham denotes one who is wise, prudent, skilled, or shrewd, encompassing practical technical expertise, political sagacity, and administrative insight. In Obadiah 1:8, the word refers to Edom's renowned political strategists and sage counselors. By announcing the destruction of these "wise men," Obadiah communicates that God will strip Edom of its famed intellectual resources, exposing the futility of human political acumen and self-reliant strategic counsel when brought under divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

In the ancient Near East, wisdom was prized not merely as abstract philosophy, but as practical skill, political diplomacy, and strategic insight. Edom—specifically its region of Teman—maintained a widespread reputation for producing famed counselors and sages. The term chakham was used across Old Testament history to designate individuals who possessed specialized expertise, whether in physical crafts, judicial ruling, royal administration, or international diplomacy. In the prophetic setting of Obadiah, Edom had long relied upon its natural mountain defenses, strategic trade routes, and renowned intellectual leadership. The prophet addresses a nation confident in its human council, announcing that no amount of political shrewdness can preserve a prideful nation against Yahweh's sovereign decree.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, chakham functions as an adjective or substantive meaning "wise," "skilful," "prudent," or "shrewd." Its core semantic range includes:

  • Technical or practical skill (e.g., artisans, craftsmen, or skilled mourners).

  • Administrative, political, and judicial discernment (e.g., rulers, judges, and royal advisors).

  • Ethical and moral discernment grounded in the reverence of Yahweh (as emphasized in Wisdom literature).

  • Human intellectual cleverness or political self-reliance that operates apart from divine truth.

The word itself describes the presence of skill, intellect, or insight; the moral quality or ultimate validity of that wisdom depends upon its source, orientation, and context.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Hebrew Scripture, chakham adapts to various literary genres:

  • Torah and Historical Writings: It often highlights practical, God-given craftsmanship for sacred duty, or administrative capacity required for righteous governance.

  • Wisdom Literature: In Proverbs and Job, chakham frequently characterizes the righteous person who lives in humble submission to Yahweh, contrasting with the fool (kesil) who rejects divine instruction. However, it can also describe God's unsearchable, absolute wisdom.

  • Prophetic Literature: Prophets often employ chakham critically to describe political counselors, foreign advisors, or self-sufficient leaders whose human shrewdness leads to pride and rebellion against God.

In all uses, the term emphasizes practical competency and intellectual capacity, though the prophets repeatedly demonstrate that human chakham is severely limited when detached from divine revelation.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical usage of chakham establishes that Yahweh is the source and judge of all wisdom. While human skill and political intelligence are real capacities, they are finite and subject to divine authority. When human leaders trust in their own chakham to secure peace, build power, or evade justice, their wisdom becomes an object of judgment rather than a source of safety. Theologically, Obadiah 1:8 underscores that human intellect cannot out-maneuver Yahweh. True wisdom begins with the fear of the Lord; apart from Him, the most strategic human minds are rendered helpless under divine scrutiny.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:8, God speaks directly regarding the day of Edom's reckoning: "Will I not in that day, says the LORD, even destroy the wise men out of Edom, and understanding from the mount of Esau?"

The target word chakham appears here in the masculine plural absolute form, identifying Edom's collective class of political strategists, royal advisors, and traditional sages. The immediate context reinforces this judgment by pairing the removal of the "wise men" (chakham) in verse 8 with the downfall of the "mighty men" (gibborim) in verse 9. By dismantling both Edom's intellectual council (chakham) and military strength (gibborim), Yahweh strips away every pillar of human confidence. The rhetorical question ("Will I not...?") emphasizes divine initiative: the loss of Edom's strategic leadership will not be a mere accident of history, but the deliberate execution of divine judgment against Edom's pride and malice toward Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 35:10 – "All who are gifted [chakham] by heart among you shall come and make all that the LORD has commanded."

  • 1 Kings 3:12 – "...behold, I have given you a wise [chakham] and understanding heart, so that there has not been anyone like you before you, nor shall any like you arise after you."

  • Job 9:4 – "God is wise [chakham] in heart and mighty in strength. Who has hardened himself against Him and prospered?"

  • Isaiah 31:2 – "Yet He also is wise [chakham] and will bring disaster, and will not call back His words, but will arise against the house of evildoers, and against the help of those who work iniquity."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The historical association of Edom—and Teman in particular—with wisdom is reflected elsewhere in the Old Testament, such as in the Book of Job, where Eliphaz the Temanite is presented as an authoritative voice of traditional ancient Near Eastern wisdom. Jeremiah 49:7 similarly asks concerning Edom, "Is wisdom no more in Teman? Has counsel perished from the prudent?" Obadiah 1:8 directly addresses this cultural reputation. By targeting the chakham of Edom, the prophecy directly addresses Edom's primary source of national pride, illustrating how divine judgment systematically disproves human self-sufficiency.
