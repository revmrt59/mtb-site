DISMAYED - (châthath, Strong's H2865)

Summary:

The Hebrew word chatat primarily means to be shattered, broken, dismayed, or terrified. It describes a profound collapse of courage, strength, or stability, often in the face of overwhelming opposition or divine judgment. In Obadiah 1:9, chatat describes the complete demoralization and breaking of Edom’s elite warriors (the mighty men of Teman), whose sudden loss of courage and power leaves the nation defenseless and ensures their total destruction.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The word chatat frequently appears in the Old Testament in military, judicial, and prophetic contexts. In ancient Near Eastern warfare, the psychological state of an army was as critical as its physical weaponry. The collapse of an army's champions often preceded the physical destruction of the nation. Chatat bridges these two realities, encompassing both the internal, psychological terror of being dismayed and the external, physical reality of being shattered or broken. It is the word used when human strength, no matter how formidable, suddenly fails in the presence of a greater, often divine, threat.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, chatat means to be shattered, broken, dismayed, confounded, or terrified. The word carries a dual nuance depending on its context: it can refer to physical breaking (such as a bow being shattered) or psychological breaking (such as a person's courage failing). In contexts involving people, particularly warriors or leaders, it denotes a state of profound demoralization where panic sets in and the capacity to resist is completely lost. It is the opposite of being strong, courageous, and steadfast.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In biblical usage, chatat is frequently found in negative commands, where God or a leader instructs the people, "do not fear, nor be dismayed," assuring them of divine presence and protection. Conversely, in prophetic literature, it is often used to describe the reaction of God's enemies when they face His judgment. When the Lord acts against a rebellious nation, their strongest defenses and bravest soldiers experience chatat—they are shattered by terror. The Qal stem, as seen in Obadiah 1:9, typically expresses this state or action of being shattered or dismayed.

4. Theological Implications

The use of chatat highlights the absolute sovereignty of God over human strength and pride. Edom was a nation that trusted in its geographical fortresses (the clefts of the rock) and its military alliances. However, this word demonstrates that true security cannot be found in human might. When God decrees judgment, He easily breaks the courage of the most arrogant and capable warriors. It reveals that divine judgment often involves a psychological dimension; God shatters the pride and confidence of the wicked, rendering their physical strength useless before their ultimate downfall.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:9, the prophet declares, "Then your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed..." Teman was a region of Edom renowned for its wisdom and military prowess. The target of this dismay is specifically the "mighty men"—the elite warriors who were the pride and defense of the nation. The authoritative morphology for this occurrence is the Qal stem, perfect form, 3rd person plural. The perfect form in this prophetic context presents the shattering of these warriors as a settled, certain reality.

The immediate context explicitly states the result of this dismay: "to the end that everyone from the mountains of Esau may be cut off by slaughter." The chatat of the warriors is not merely a temporary fright; it is a fatal breaking of their capacity to fight. Because the mighty men are shattered and paralyzed by dismay, Edom's last line of defense falls, ensuring that God's judgment of total destruction is carried out without hindrance.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • 1 Samuel 17:11 – "When Saul and all Israel heard these words of the Philistine, they were dismayed and greatly afraid."

  • This illustrates the psychological paralysis of chatat. The entire Israelite army, including the king, is broken in courage by the taunts of Goliath, rendering them unable to fight.

  • Joshua 8:1 – "Now the Lord said to Joshua: 'Do not be afraid, nor be dismayed; take all the people of war with you, and arise, go up to Ai...'"

  • Following the defeat at Ai, God commands Joshua not to remain in a state of chatat. Divine assurance is given to reverse their demoralization before returning to battle.

  • Jeremiah 48:1 – "Against Moab. Thus says the Lord of hosts, the God of Israel: 'Woe to Nebo! For it is plundered, Kirjathaim is shamed and taken; The high stronghold is shamed and dismayed.'"

  • Similar to Obadiah's prophecy against Edom, this verse uses the word to describe the shattering of a rival nation's strongholds under God's judgment.

  • 1 Chronicles 28:20 – "And David said to his son Solomon, 'Be strong and of good courage, and do it; do not fear nor be dismayed, for the Lord God—my God—will be with you...'"

  • David charges Solomon with the monumental task of building the temple, contrasting the state of being dismayed with the courage that comes from knowing God is present.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The pairing of Obadiah 1:8 and 1:9 shows a comprehensive dismantling of Edom's societal pillars. In verse 8, God promises to destroy the wise men from Edom; in verse 9, the mighty men are dismayed (chatat). Edom's intellectual and military superiority were their greatest sources of pride. By shattering both their wisdom and their warriors, the text underscores the totality of God's judgment against human arrogance. The physical slaughter that follows is the inevitable result of this internal, divinely ordained collapse.
