ESAU - (ʻÊsâv, Strong's H6215)

Summary:

The proper noun ʿesaw refers to Esau, the eldest son of Isaac and twin brother of Jacob, whose descendants settled in the mountainous region of Seir and became the nation of Edom. Lexically, the term serves as a personal name and, by extension, a corporate national designation for Edom and its mountainous domain. In Obadiah 1:9, the word appears in the phrase "the mount of Esau," where it identifies the sovereign, mountainous territory of Edom under impending divine judgment. By calling Edom "Esau," the prophet brings into focus Edom's ancestral brotherhood with Israel, underscoring the solemn moral responsibility Edom betrayed and establishing the righteousness of God's complete judgment against them.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The name ʿesaw originates in the narrative of Genesis, given to the firstborn twin son of Isaac and Rebekah. From his birth, Esau was associated with the rugged, outdoor life of a hunter, eventually settling in the hill country of Mount Seir south of the Dead Sea. Over generations, Esau's line expanded into the kingdom of Edom. In the Old Testament narrative and prophetic traditions, the name ʿesaw functions both historically for the patriarch himself and collectively for the nation that descended from him.

By the time of the prophet Obadiah, the historic relationship between the descendants of Jacob (Israel/Judah) and the descendants of Esau (Edom) was marked by deep strain and hostility. Obadiah's prophecy focuses entirely on God's announcement of judgment against Edom. Using the ancestral name ʿesaw, the prophet deliberately reminds the hearers of the ancient family tie, exposing Edom's gloating and violence against Judah during Jerusalem's calamity as an egregious violation of fraternal duty.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, ʿesaw is a proper noun designating the historical figure Esau or the political and geographical entity derived from him. Standard Old Testament Hebrew lexicons define ʿesaw as:

  • The proper personal name of Isaac's firstborn son, twin brother of Jacob.

  • A collective proper noun designating the nation of Edom.

  • A territorial designation, particularly when joined with har ("mount"), referring to the mountainous region of Seir inhabited by Edom.

The authoritative target morphology for this occurrence in Obadiah 1:9 identifies ʿesaw grammatically as a proper noun in the masculine singular absolute state. In this grammatical construction, the singular proper name identifies the single ancestral head whose name designates the entire community and its highland region.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across the Old Testament Scriptures, the usage of ʿesaw unfolds across distinct historical and literary settings:

  • Narrative Foundations in Genesis: In the book of Genesis, ʿesaw refers primarily to the individual person, recording his birth, his relinquishment of his birthright, his interaction with his brother Jacob, and his eventual migration to Mount Seir.

  • Historical and Genealogical Summaries: In genealogical records (such as Genesis 36 and 1 Chronicles 1), ʿesaw serves as the covenantal marker for the line of Edom, tracing the chiefs and kings who descended from him.

  • Prophetic and Retributive Contexts: In prophetic literature, such as Jeremiah and Obadiah, ʿesaw is frequently used metonymically for Edom. It often carries a tragic nuance: the nation that shared common blood with Israel chose hatred and violence instead of covenantal brotherhood, placing itself directly under divine retribution.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical usage of ʿesaw carries profound theological significance:

  • Moral and Fraternal Accountability: God holds nations accountable not only for general righteousness but also for specific relational obligations. Edom's descent from Esau meant that Israel and Edom were brothers. Edom's failure to assist Judah—and worse, their active participation in Judah's plunder—was a violation of brotherly duty.

  • The Illusion of Human Security: Edom trusted in its natural fortification, "the mount of Esau." Theologically, the passage demonstrates that no height, wisdom, or natural mountain defense can protect a nation when God brings judgment upon unrighteousness.

  • Divine Sovereignty and Covenant History: The parallel histories of Jacob and Esau highlight God's sovereign administration of redemptive history. While God granted mountain lands to Esau as a temporal inheritance, His redemptive covenant promise proceeded through Jacob.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:9, the text reads: "Then your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed, To the end that everyone from the mount of Esau May be cut off by slaughter."

The immediate context emphasizes the total collapse of Edom's renowned strength and wisdom. Teman, a region famed for its wise men and warriors, will offer no defense. The phrase "the mount of Esau" (har ʿesaw) combines the geographic elevation of Edom's territory with the ancestral identity of the nation.

Through this specific phrasing, Obadiah accomplishes two primary objectives:

1. It directly addresses Edom's misplaced pride in their inaccessible mountain fortresses, declaring that every single person within "the mount of Esau" will be reached by divine judgment.

2. It frames the judgment in light of covenant family history. By calling the nation "Esau," the text highlights the tragic failure of a brother who turned against his kin, justifying the complete removal of Edom's power.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The following passages illustrate the range of usage for ʿesaw:

  • Genesis 25:26 — "Afterward his brother came out, and his hand took hold of Esau's heel; so his name was called Jacob. Isaac was sixty years old when she bore them."

  • Genesis 27:34 — "When Esau heard the words of his father, he cried with an exceedingly great and bitter cry, and said to his father, 'Bless me—me also, O my father!'"

  • Obadiah 1:19 — "The South shall possess the mountains of Esau, And the lowland shall possess Philistia. They shall possess the fields of Ephraim And the fields of Samaria. Benjamin shall possess Gilead."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew geographical terminology, associating a nation's named ancestor with a mountain range ("mount of Esau") reflects both physical geography and national identity. The rocky, high-altitude terrain of Mount Seir provided Edom with a sense of invulnerability. In Obadiah, the high elevation of "the mount of Esau" becomes a literary and theological symbol of Edom's lofty pride, which God promises to bring down.
