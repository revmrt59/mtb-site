MOUNTAINS - ( har , Strong's H2022 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word har refers to a mountain, hill, or mountainous ridge, and is often used collectively to describe an entire hill country or mountainous region. In the Old Testament, mountains are symbols of permanence, security, strength, and divine presence, but they also represent the pride of nations that set themselves up against God. In Obadiah 1:9, "the mountains of Esau" (the rugged, seemingly impregnable territory of Edom, specifically Mount Seir) represents Edom's false security, self-exaltation, and national pride. God's judgment will penetrate this fortressed height, demonstrating that no human stronghold can withstand His sovereign decree.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun har refers to a prominent geological elevation. In the ancient Near East, mountains were highly significant. They served as natural fortresses, national borders, and sites for religious worship. The topography of Edom—the descendants of Esau—was defined by the rugged, red sandstone cliffs of Mount Seir. This mountainous region rose up to 5,000 feet above sea level, providing excellent natural defenses. The Edomites carved dwellings directly into the rock cliffs and felt entirely secure from enemy invasions. This physical elevation fostered a spiritual attitude of pride and invincibility.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word har means a mountain, hill, or hill country. It derives from an unused root meaning to loom up or project. It can refer to a single specific peak (such as Mount Sinai, Mount Hor, or Mount Zion) or to an entire mountainous range or region (such as the hill country of Judah). In Obadiah 1:9, har is used in the singular construct form (har `ēśāw, "mount of Esau"), which the NKJV renders plurally as "mountains of Esau" to accurately capture the collective sense of the entire mountainous territory of Edom.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses har over 500 times. It carries several rich word pictures:

  • Symbol of Stability and Permanence: Mountains are described as ancient, lasting, and unshakable. Yet, they melt like wax before the presence of the Lord, showing that even the most stable earthly structures are subject to God.

  • Symbol of Pride and Self-Reliance: High places and mountains represent human pride, self-exaltation, and false security. The day of the Lord is described as being against all the high mountains and lofty hills.

  • Places of Divine Encounter: God meets His people on mountains. Mount Sinai is the site of the giving of the Law, and Mount Zion is established as the place of God's holy dwelling.

4. Theological Implications

The confrontation between the "mountains of Esau" (Obadiah 1:9) and "Mount Zion" (Obadiah 1:17, 21) is a central theological theme of the book of Obadiah. Edom's mountains represent the heights of human pride, self-deification, and rebellion against God. Edom believed their mountainous strongholds made them completely invincible. God's judgment on these mountains proves that human strongholds are completely vulnerable before the Sovereign Lord. True security is not found in physical elevation or military fortresses, but in Yahweh on His holy mountain, Zion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:9, the context is the total destruction and slaughter of Edom's leadership and population. Teman, a key region in southern Edom known for its wise men and mighty warriors, will be dismayed. The phrase "everyone from the mountains of Esau may be cut off by slaughter" emphasizes that the judgment will be comprehensive. Even those hidden in the deepest, most inaccessible canyons and highest peaks of the mountainous terrain will not escape. The use of har here highlights a tragic irony: the very mountains that once guaranteed Edom's protection and isolation will become the site of their total annihilation.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Hebrew word har stands in stark contrast to valleys and plains. In ancient Near Eastern warfare, battles were typically fought on the plains, while mountains served as places of retreat and defense. For Edom, the mountains were their identity. Obadiah uses har in a deliberate contrast with Mount Zion. While the "mountains of Esau" are brought low and cut off by slaughter, "Mount Zion" becomes a place of deliverance, holiness, and the location from which the kingdom of the Lord is administered.
