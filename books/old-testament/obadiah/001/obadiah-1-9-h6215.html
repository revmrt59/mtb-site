ESAU - ( 'Esaw , Strong's H6215 )

Summary:

The name Esau refers to the twin brother of Jacob and the father of the Edomites. Meaning "hairy" or "rough," the name carries both historical weight and prophetic significance. In Obadiah 1:9, "Esau" is used metonymically to represent the entire nation of Edom—his descendants—who inhabited the rugged, mountainous territory south of the Dead Sea. The term highlights the tragic trajectory of a brother nation that turned in pride and hostility against Israel, ultimately facing complete divine judgment and excision from history.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Esau first appears in Genesis 25:25 to describe the firstborn twin of Isaac and Rebekah, who emerged from the womb red and covered with hair like a garment. The physical description of Esau matches the landscape his descendants would eventually settle: the rugged, red sandstone mountains of Seir. The narrative of Genesis portrays Esau as a man of the field, a skilled hunter, and a man governed by immediate physical desires. He famously sold his birthright to Jacob for a single bowl of red lentil stew, earning him the additional name Edom, meaning "red." This historical background forms the foundation for the prophetic books, where Esau represents a fleshly, proud, and profane mindset that stands in stark opposition to the spiritual inheritance of God's covenant people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper noun 'esaw translates directly as "Esau." The etymology of the name points to an ancient root meaning "hairy" or "rough." In the Hebrew Bible, the term functions in three distinct ways depending on the literary context:

  • As a personal name for the historical son of Isaac (Genesis 25:25).

  • As a collective singular noun representing the descendants of Esau, the Edomites (Deuteronomy 2:5).

  • As a geographical designation for the territory settled by his descendants, often phrased as the "mount of Esau" or the "mountains of Esau" (Obadiah 1:9, Malachi 1:3).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The biblical narrative uses the figure of Esau as a powerful word picture of the natural, earthly man. Unlike Jacob, who, despite his flaws, valued the covenant promise, Esau lived for the immediate present. His hairiness and wild lifestyle symbolize a rough, untamed nature.

In prophetic literature, "the mount of Esau" becomes a symbol of human security and arrogant self-reliance. The Edomites carved their dwellings into the high, red rock cliffs of Sela (later known as Petra). This high-altitude fortress created an illusion of absolute invincibility. Thus, "Esau" represents the pride of man that nests among the stars but is ultimately brought low by the hand of God.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of Esau spans both testaments:

  • Sovereign Election: The division between Jacob and Esau serves as a primary biblical illustration of divine election. God chose Jacob over Esau before their birth, establishing that covenant blessings flow from divine grace rather than human merit or natural birthright.

  • Profaneness and Worldliness: New Testament commentary identifies Esau as the archetype of a "profane person" who bartered eternal treasures for temporary, physical gratification. He represents those who desire God's blessings (tears of regret) but despise the repentance and submission required to receive them.

  • The Betrayal of Brotherhood: The relationship between Israel (Jacob) and Edom (Esau) was meant to be fraternal. Edom’s sin was not merely geopolitical; it was a betrayal of family. When Babylon destroyed Jerusalem, Edom stood by, rejoiced, and actively participated in the slaughter of Jewish fugitives. This violation of brotherly covenant brought down the severity of divine judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Obadiah 1:9, the prophet declares, "Then your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed, To the end that everyone from the mount of Esau May be cut off by slaughter."

Here, "the mount of Esau" refers to the mountainous stronghold of Edom, specifically around Mount Seir. Teman was a region in Edom famed for its wisdom and military strength. By addressing "the mount of Esau," the text highlights the complete failure of Edom’s natural defenses. The very mountains that provided military isolation and a sense of absolute safety would become a slaughterhouse. The use of the ancestor's name, Esau, reminds the readers of the ancient family feud and underscores that Edom's impending doom is a direct consequence of their ancestral pride and persistent hatred toward the house of Jacob.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The historical fulfillment of Obadiah’s prophecy against the house of Esau is striking. Edom was successively conquered by the Babylonians, the Nabataeans, and the Maccabeans. During the Roman period, the Edomites (then called Idumeans) were forcibly integrated into Judea. Herod the Great, an Idumean, represented the final, desperate attempt of the house of Esau to rule over and destroy the seed of Jacob (Jesus the Messiah). Following the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in A.D. 70, the Idumeans disappeared entirely from the pages of history, fulfilling Obadiah's prophecy that "no survivor shall remain of the house of Esau."
