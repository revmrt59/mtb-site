OFF - (kârath, Strong's H3772)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb karat fundamentally signifies "to cut," "to sever," or "to fell." In Old Testament usage, it ranges from the physical felling of trees or sacrificial animals to the judicial cutting off of individuals from the community, as well as the solemn ratification of covenants ("cutting a covenant"). In Obadiah 1:9, karat appears in the passive sense (Niphal stem, imperfect form, 3rd person, masculine) to express the total, prospective destruction decreed against Edom. God declares that Edom's mighty men will be dismayed so that every inhabitant from the mountains of Esau will be "cut off" by slaughter—a sentence of retributive justice that terminates Edom's national existence and security for their betrayal of Judah.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root karat is a primary Hebrew verb whose core physical action is severing an object by cutting. In ancient Near Eastern life, cutting was an essential action in timber harvesting, agrarian labor, animal sacrifice, and execution.

Beyond physical labor, the verb acquired two prominent theological and social idioms in ancient Israel. The first is covenantal: to establish a covenant was literally to "cut a covenant" (karat berit), referring to the custom of severing sacrificial animals to solemnize a binding agreement. The second is judicial and penal: individuals who high-handedly violated covenant laws were warned that they would be "cut off" (karat) from among their people, suffering excommunication or divine execution. In prophetic discourse, this penal sense expanded to describe Yahweh's decisive judgment upon hostile, arrogant nations.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, karat means to cut down, cut off, chop off, eliminate, or destroy. When used in active stems (Qal or Hiphil), it refers to an agent performing the act of cutting down trees, executing enemies, or making a covenant. When used in passive stems, it describes the condition of being severed, destroyed, or excised from a population or land.

In Obadiah 1:9, the verb is configured in the Niphal stem, imperfect form, 3rd person, masculine.

  • Stem: The Niphal stem functions passively here, presenting the subject ("everyone from the mountains of Esau") as receiving the action of being cut off or eliminated.

  • Form: The imperfect form presents this divine judgment as an impending, prospective outcome that will unfold according to God's decree.

  • Person and Gender: The 3rd person masculine construction grammatically aligns with the collective subject ("everyone" / "each man") targeted by the decree.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, karat carries distinct nuances based on context:

  • Physical Cutting: It denotes the literal felling of trees, timber, or idols.

  • Covenant Making: Combined with the noun berit ("covenant"), karat expresses the formal enactment or ratification of a covenantal relationship between God and humanity or between earthly leaders.

  • Judicial Penalty: In the Mosaic Law, being "cut off" designates divine excommunication or death, severing a transgressor from the spiritual and social life of Israel.

  • Prophetic Judgment: In prophetic announcements against foreign nations, the verb denotes comprehensive military destruction and the total termination of a kingdom or lineage.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of karat in prophetic literature emphasizes God's sovereign role as the ultimate Judge of nations. While human armies may serve as the earthly instrument, the destruction represented by karat is fundamentally a divine act.

Furthermore, the term highlights the moral governance of God. Nations that exalt themselves in pride and exercise cruelty against God's people forfeit their security. In the broader canonical context, while Israel experiences chastisement followed by covenantal restoration, persistent pagan arrogance against Yahweh's rule leads to absolute severance—a complete cutting off without remedy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Obadiah's prophecy centers on the divine indictment and impending doom of Edom (the descendants of Esau). Edom relied on its strategic mountain strongholds in Teman and prized its reputation for wisdom. However, during Jerusalem's fall, Edom stood aloof, gloated over Israel's distress, and actively participated in plundering and intercepting Jewish fugitives.

In Obadiah 1:8-9, Yahweh announces a two-fold dismantling of Edom's earthly confidence:

1. Dismantling Wisdom (v. 8): God will destroy the wise men from Edom and understanding from Esau's mountains.

2. Dismantling Might (v. 9): Teman's mighty men will be dismayed.

The direct goal and result of this divine intervention is expressed by karat: "To the end that everyone from the mountains of Esau may be cut off by slaughter." Edom's human wisdom cannot devise an escape, and Edom's military strength cannot defend against Yahweh. The verb karat establishes that Edom's upcoming defeat will not be a temporary setback, but a complete, permanent severance of their national lineage and power.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 26:28 — "But they said, 'We have certainly seen that the LORD is with you. So we said, "Let there now be an oath between us, between you and us, and let us make a covenant with you."'" Here the same Hebrew root karat is used in the customary covenant idiom ("make [cut] a covenant"), denoting a solemn, binding agreement.

  • Leviticus 20:17 — "If a man takes his sister... it is a wicked thing. And they shall be cut off in the sight of their people." In this legal context, the passive form of the verb specifies judicial excommunication or execution from the covenant community.

  • 1 Samuel 24:21 — "Therefore swear now to me by the LORD that you will not cut off my descendants after me, and that you will not destroy my name from my father’s house." Saul uses the verb to express his fear that his dynastic line will be totally eradicated upon David's ascension.

  • Psalms 89:3 — "I have made a covenant with My chosen, I have sworn to My servant David." The verb describes Yahweh solemnly cutting an enduring royal covenant with David.

  • Obadiah 1:14 — "You should not have stood at the crossroads to cut off those of his who escaped..." In another occurrence within this short prophecy, the verb depicts Edom's criminal act of ambushing and severing the escape route of Judah's survivors.

7. Other Relevant Insights

A notable literary feature in Obadiah is the measure-for-measure correspondence between Edom's sin and Edom's judgment, highlighted by the repetition of karat:

  • In Obadiah 1:14, Edom actively stood at the crossroads to "cut off" (karat) the fleeing survivors of Judah.

  • In Obadiah 1:9, God decrees that Edom itself will be "cut off" (karat) from the mountains of Esau by slaughter.

Edom used force to sever the escape of their brother Jacob's descendants; consequently, Yahweh severs Edom's future entirely.
