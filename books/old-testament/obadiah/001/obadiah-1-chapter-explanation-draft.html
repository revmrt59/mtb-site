Obadiah 1

Verse-by-Verse
Obadiah 1:1
NKJV

Obadiah 1:1 The vision of Obadiah. Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom (We have heard a report from the LORD, And a messenger has been sent among the nations, saying, "Arise, and let us rise up against her for battle"): (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:1 This is the vision that the Sovereign LORD revealed to Obadiah concerning the land of Edom. Edom’s Judgment Announced We have heard a message from the LORDthat an ambassador was sent to the nations to say,“Get ready, everyone!Let’s assemble our armies and attack Edom!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Obadiah's vision is God's message about Edom, the nation descended from Esau and a longtime rival of Israel. God has already stirred up the surrounding nations to rise against Edom in battle, showing that He controls the events of history to carry out His purposes.

Verse Explanation

Obadiah's vision is God's message about Edom, the nation descended from Esau and a longtime rival of Israel. God has already stirred up the surrounding nations to rise against Edom in battle, showing that He controls the events of history to carry out His purposes.

"The vision of Obadiah."

In the prophets, a "vision" is a message God revealed to His servant. It is not necessarily something seen with the eyes. Isaiah's book opens the same way: "The vision of Isaiah the son of Amoz" (Isaiah 1:1). The name Obadiah means "servant of the LORD." Several men in the Old Testament had this name, but nothing in Scripture connects this prophet with any of them, and he tells us nothing about his family, his hometown, or when he lived. His book is the shortest in the Old Testament. All attention falls on the message, not the messenger.

"Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom"

These are God's words, not Obadiah's opinions. "Lord" means master, and "GOD" in capital letters stands for God's personal name, the LORD. Together they present Him as the sovereign Master who is also Israel's own God.

Edom was the nation descended from Esau, the son of Isaac. Genesis says plainly, "Esau is Edom" (Genesis 36:8). The name means "red." It recalls Esau's red appearance at birth and the red stew he traded his birthright for (Genesis 25:25, 30). The Edomites lived in the mountainous region south and southeast of the Dead Sea, and for centuries they were Israel's rivals. When Israel came out of Egypt, Edom refused to let them pass through its land and came out against them with an army (Numbers 20:14–21). David later conquered Edom (2 Samuel 8:14), and Edom rebelled again in the days of King Jehoram (2 Kings 8:20–22). Other prophets also spoke against Edom (Jeremiah 49:7–22; Ezekiel 25:12–14; Amos 1:11–12), but Obadiah devotes his entire prophecy to it.

"(We have heard a report from the LORD,"

A "report" is news or tidings. By saying "we," Obadiah includes himself with his own people, Judah, who are hearing what God has announced. The message is spoken to Edom, yet it comes to God's people. The ones Edom had wronged learn that the LORD has seen what happened and is already acting. Jeremiah's prophecy against Edom contains nearly the same words (Jeremiah 49:14), which shows that God gave His prophets a consistent message about this nation.

"And a messenger has been sent among the nations,"

The word "messenger" refers to an envoy or ambassador, someone sent between nations to stir them to action or arrange alliances. Obadiah does not name the nations or say who sent the envoy. What matters is that this report comes "from the LORD." From the outside, it looks like ordinary politics and war planning. But the nations moving against Edom are moving because God has set events in motion.

"saying, 'Arise, and let us rise up against her for battle'):"

The nations urge one another to go to war against "her," meaning Edom as a nation. The call is already going out before God's message to Edom even begins. Edom felt secure, but the nations around it were already preparing to march. Some of those turning against Edom would be the very nations it had trusted (verse 7). God's own words to Edom follow in the next verse.

Study Notes

Much of Obadiah's message also appears in Jeremiah's prophecy against Edom. The report from the LORD, the messenger among the nations, Edom being made small and despised, the pride of those who live in the clefts of the rock, and the eagle's nest from which God will bring Edom down closely match Jeremiah 49:14–16. The pictures of thieves by night and grape-gatherers, and of Esau's hidden places being uncovered, match Jeremiah 49:9–10. The order is different, though. Obadiah gives these lines as one compact, tightly connected passage. In Jeremiah they are spread through a longer prophecy and arranged differently.

No one knows for certain how the two are related. Some think Jeremiah drew on Obadiah's earlier words. Others think Obadiah drew on Jeremiah. Many think both prophets used an older message against Edom that God's people already knew. The evidence does not settle the question, and it affects how Obadiah is dated (see the remark on verse 11). What is clear is that two prophets delivered the same warning to Edom. It was not one man's isolated opinion.

Spirit & Life

[Seed] A Servant Who Leaves Only a Word. “The vision of Obadiah.” His name means “servant of Yahweh” or “worshiper of Yahweh,” and beyond that name and these twenty-one verses nothing is known of him. Every attempt to identify him with the other Obadiahs of Scripture is guesswork, and so is the old rabbinic tradition that made him an Edomite convert. The shortest book in the Old Testament is carried by a man with no biography, no genealogy, and no status. The messenger fades into the background so that the sovereign voice of the Lord can thunder without distraction. God does not need a famous messenger, only a servant who will carry what he was given and then step out of the way. The word outlived the man, and that is the only kind of ministry that lasts.

[Foundational Truth] The Prophet Hears Before He Speaks. “We have heard a report from the LORD.” Jeremiah’s parallel oracle says “I have heard” (Jeremiah 49:14), but Obadiah says “we,” counting himself among the people who must receive the word. A report (shemu’ah) is only as certain as its source, and this one comes from the LORD. God does not act in a vacuum; He speaks to His prophets before He brings judgment or deliverance (Amos 3:7). Hearing the report in the spirit comes before the battle cry is ever raised: “Arise, and let us go against her.” The preacher is first a listener. Before we announce what God will do about our enemies, His word must land on us.

[Insight] Nations Answering a Summons They Never Heard. “An envoy has been sent among the nations, saying, ‘Arise, and let us go against her for battle.’” The envoy moves through the capitals of the world, and the call to arms is urgent and doubled. Notice the “us”: the messenger speaks for the One who sent him, so the LORD Himself marches at the head of the coalition. While earthly kings believe they decide war and peace, Obadiah’s vision pulls back the curtain on an unseen reality. God stirs the nations and mobilizes pagan armies against Edom to carry out His discipline. Treaties and troop movements are never autonomous; earthly superpowers march unwittingly to heaven’s drumbeat (Proverbs 21:1). Nothing in the headlines is outside the reach of the God who sends envoys among the nations.

[Insight] Spoken About Edom, Delivered to Judah. The word translated “vision” (chazon) stresses the message received rather than pictures in the prophet’s mind, and the speaker is “the Lord GOD,” Adonai Yahweh, the Master who owns all things and the covenant God bound to Israel. The oracle is “concerning Edom,” but it was delivered to Judah. A wounded people needed to know that God had not forgotten what had been done to them. When God speaks about our enemies, He is often speaking to us, to assure us that He keeps the record. (Verses 1–9 run parallel to Jeremiah 49:7–22, and the book’s date is disputed, but its message does not depend on the date.)

Obadiah 1:2
NKJV

Obadiah 1:2 "Behold, I will make you small among the nations; You shall be greatly despised. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:2 The LORD says to Edom,“I will cut you down to size among the nations;you will be greatly despised. (NLT)

Main Idea

God announces that He will make Edom small and despised among the nations. Edom saw itself as strong and secure, but the nation's standing depended on God, not on its own power.

Verse Explanation

God announces that He will make Edom small and despised among the nations. Edom saw itself as strong and secure, but the nation's standing depended on God, not on its own power.

"Behold, I will make you small among the nations;"

After Obadiah introduces the message, the LORD now speaks directly to Edom, the nation descended from Esau. "Behold" calls for attention: what follows is God's own announcement. In Hebrew the verb can be read as "I have made you small," spoken as though it had already happened. When God declares what He will do, it is as certain as if it were finished.

"Small" here does not refer to Edom's size on a map. It means low in rank, unimportant, a nation that no longer counts for much. Edom thought highly of itself and believed no one could touch it (1:3), but its place among the nations was never in its own hands. The same God who stirs up the nations against Edom (1:1) decides where Edom stands among them. Hannah said of the LORD, "He brings low and lifts up" (1 Samuel 2:7), and that is true of nations as well as people. Jeremiah's message against Edom uses almost the same words (Jeremiah 49:15), so two prophets carried the same warning to this proud nation.

"You shall be greatly despised."

To be despised is to be looked down on, treated with contempt, considered worthless. Edom would be not just mildly disrespected but "greatly" despised, scorned by the very nations whose respect it valued.

The Hebrew word is a form of the same verb used of Esau himself, who sold his birthright for a bowl of stew: "Thus Esau despised his birthright" (Genesis 25:34). The man who treated God's promised blessing as worthless became the father of a nation that would itself be treated as worthless. Edom would also look down on its brother Judah in the day of Judah's ruin (1:12), and now God declares that Edom will be the one looked down on.

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Discovery] The Elder Brother Cut Down to Size. “Behold, I will make you small among the nations.” The Hebrew is a prophetic perfect, “I have made you small,” a decision of God so settled that He speaks of it as done. “Small” (qaton) reaches back to the oracle over Rebekah’s womb: “the older shall serve the younger” (Genesis 25:23). Esau the firstborn, the great one, is made the little one. God’s word over a family does not expire; what He speaks at the beginning He brings to its end, however many centuries lie between.

[Discovery] The Despiser Becomes the Despised. “You are greatly despised.” The verb is bazah, the very word used when “Esau despised his birthright” (Genesis 25:34). The man who treated a holy inheritance as cheap fathered a nation that would itself be treated as cheap. Whatever God gives is of infinite worth, and to treat His gift lightly is finally to be treated lightly. Esau’s bowl of stew is still being eaten wherever God’s Word, His presence, or His table is handled as a small thing. Is there a gift of God that we have begun to treat as small?

[Insight] The Divine Reversal of Self-Perception. Edom thought of itself as great, impregnable, and elite. God says, “I will make you small … you are greatly despised.” What a nation, or a person, thinks of itself means nothing; God is the One who establishes true stature. The fall from self-exaltation to divine demotion is sudden and absolute.

[Seed] Least Reason, Most Pride. Edom was in fact a small nation, a narrow strip of mountains roughly a hundred miles long and at most twenty wide, and its pride grew in proportion to its smallness. A low estate can be the mother of either lowliness or pride. Sometimes those who have the least reason for pride have the most of it.

Obadiah 1:3
NKJV

Obadiah 1:3 The pride of your heart has deceived you, You who dwell in the clefts of the rock, Whose habitation is high; You who say in your heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?' (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:3 You have been deceived by your own pridebecause you live in a rock fortressand make your home high in the mountains.‘Who can ever reach us way up here?’you ask boastfully. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom's pride fooled it into thinking it was untouchable, since its people lived in rocky cliffs and high places that seemed impossible to attack. Pride blinds people to their real weakness and leads them to believe no one, not even God, can bring them down.

Verse Explanation

Edom's pride fooled it into thinking it was untouchable, since its people lived in rocky cliffs and high places that seemed impossible to attack. Pride blinds people to their real weakness and leads them to believe no one, not even God, can bring them down.

"The pride of your heart has deceived you,"

The Hebrew word for "pride" describes arrogance, an inflated sense of one's own importance that refuses to answer to anyone. Edom, the nation descended from Esau, had not been fooled by an enemy. Its own heart fooled it. Pride told Edom it was strong, safe, and better than others, and Edom believed it. That is what makes pride so dangerous: the person caught in it is the last to see it. Jeremiah warned that "the heart is deceitful above all things" (Jeremiah 17:9), and Proverbs states plainly, "Pride goes before destruction, And a haughty spirit before a fall" (Proverbs 16:18).

Jeremiah delivered a very similar message against Edom: "Your fierceness has deceived you, The pride of your heart, O you who dwell in the clefts of the rock, Who hold the height of the hill!" (Jeremiah 49:16). Two prophets saw the same flaw at the center of Edom's life.

"You who dwell in the clefts of the rock, Whose habitation is high;"

Edom's pride was rooted in its homeland. The Edomites lived in the rugged mountains south and southeast of the Dead Sea, a region of steep sandstone cliffs, deep gorges, and narrow passes. Scripture calls it Mount Seir, the hill country where Esau settled (Genesis 36:8). Its towns and strongholds sat high on ridges and tucked into rock walls, where a few defenders could hold off a much larger army coming up through the narrow approaches. The Hebrew word for "rock" here is sela, which was also the name of an Edomite stronghold (2 Kings 14:7), so the phrase may also call to mind one of the fortresses Edom trusted.

To the Edomites, this setting looked like perfect protection. They lived above their neighbors, physically and in their own minds. The heights that kept invaders out also fed the attitude that Edom was out of reach.

"You who say in your heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?'"

This was Edom's private confidence, the thing it told itself when no one else was listening. The question expects the answer "No one." Edom pictured itself on its high perch and could not imagine anyone forcing it down to the ground below, the place of defeat and humiliation.

The boast reveals what Edom had left out of its thinking. It measured its safety against human armies and forgot the God who rules over all nations. Mountains could stop soldiers, but they could not stop Him. The LORD answers this question directly in the next verse. Scripture repeatedly shows God opposing exactly this kind of self-assured pride: "God resists the proud, But gives grace to the humble" (1 Peter 5:5). Edom's confidence in its rocky heights was the very thing that would bring it low.

Study Notes

Many Bible readers link this verse with Petra, the famous city in southern Jordan whose buildings are carved into red sandstone cliffs. Petra does lie in Edom's old territory, but its well-known carved monuments were made by the Nabataeans, an Arab people who took over the region centuries after Obadiah, mostly around the time of Christ. When Obadiah spoke, Petra's carved city did not yet exist.

The Edomite stronghold called Sela (2 Kings 14:7) has not been located with certainty. One candidate is Umm el-Biyara, a steep mountain overlooking the later site of Petra. Excavations there uncovered a seventh-century BC Edomite settlement and a seal bearing the name of an Edomite king. Another candidate is es-Sela, a rocky height near Bozrah, Edom's capital. Either site illustrates what the verse describes: settlements perched on cliffs that could be reached only by narrow paths.

Spirit & Life

[Discovery] The Pride That Boiled Over. “The arrogance of your heart has deceived you.” “Arrogance” is zadon, from a root meaning “to boil, to seethe”: pride that boils over and sets itself above God. The same root describes the day the family feud began, when “Jacob boiled stew” and Esau sold his birthright for it (Genesis 25:29–34). The pride that destroyed Edom and the pot that cost Esau everything share a root. What boils over in the heart eventually spills on everyone near it, and the sins of a family tend to keep the same shape for generations until grace breaks the line.

[Insight] Deceived by the Liar Who Lives Inside. “Has deceived you” is nasha, the word Eve used in the garden: “The serpent deceived me” (Genesis 3:13). But in Obadiah no serpent is needed; Edom’s own heart does the serpent’s work. Pride is an optical illusion, a kind of self-hypnosis. As an old preacher put it, in every way pride lays a man open to being deceived. The most dangerous deception is the one we have no idea we are under, and pride guarantees we will not look for it. Only a heart that asks God to search it (Psalm 139:23–24) has any defense against its own lies. Where might pride be deceiving us right now, and whom have we actually given permission to tell us the truth?

[Discovery] Which Rock Are You Hiding In?. “You who live in the clefts of the rock.” The rock is sela, also the name of Edom’s capital, Sela, later called Petra: a city hidden at the end of a mile-long gorge, honeycombed with chambers cut into the cliffs, a fortress ancient armies failed to take. David used the same word for another refuge: “The LORD is my rock (sela) and my fortress” (Psalm 18:2). The same word names two hiding places, a mountain carved by human hands and the LORD Himself. Every one of us lives in the clefts of some rock; the only question is which one.

[Christ & Life] The Counterfeit Fortress. Edom, a picture of the flesh, instinctively borrows the language of security that belongs to the Spirit. The believer’s true safety is to be hidden in the cleft of the Rock, Christ, seated in the heavenly places. But the flesh builds a counterfeit fortress. It finds a natural “rock,” self-reliance, human wisdom, material security, and raises itself to a “lofty dwelling.” The tragedy is that pride does not merely inflate; it deceives. The flesh genuinely believes it is untouchable. It mimics the eternal security of the believer, but roots it in self-exaltation instead of grace.

[Seed] The Deceitfulness of Natural Security. Petra was entered through the Siq, a narrow gorge between towering sandstone walls, and Edom’s physical geography became psychological arrogance. The same cliffs had sheltered the Horites before them (Deuteronomy 2:12), and the Horites had been driven out too. Geography, military muscle, and financial fortresses lull the heart into a practical atheism that says, “Who will bring me down?” What natural advantages, wealth, intellect, upbringing, a stable career, credentials, independence, deceive our hearts into thinking we are beyond the reach of spiritual downfall or beyond the need of total dependence on God? What would be left of our peace if that rock were taken away?

[Insight] The Silent Conversation of the Heart. The word “heart” appears twice: the arrogance of your heart, who say in your heart. God listens to what we say in our hearts. Edom’s outward words may or may not have been boastful, but God indicts the inner monologue. Pride is first an undetected conversation with self.

[Insight] Not “Who Will,” but “Who Can”. “Who will bring me down to the ground?” is best heard as “Who can bring me down?” Edom was not predicting the future; it was denying that a fall was even possible. Pride does not only forget God’s power. It rules it out.

[Discovery] Two Who Hid in the Clefts. The word for “clefts” appears only three times in Scripture: here, in Jeremiah’s parallel (Jeremiah 49:16), and in the Song of Songs, “O my dove, in the clefts of the rock” (Song of Songs 2:14). The same hiding place holds two very different occupants: a proud nation hiding from God, and a beloved dove hidden by the One who calls her. It is not the cleft that saves; it is who put us there.

Obadiah 1:4
NKJV

Obadiah 1:4 Though you ascend as high as the eagle, And though you set your nest among the stars, From there I will bring you down," says the LORD. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:4 But even if you soar as high as eaglesand build your nest among the stars,I will bring you crashing down,”says the LORD. (NLT)

Main Idea

Even if Edom could soar like an eagle or build its home among the stars, God would still bring it down. No height or human security can place anyone beyond the reach of God's judgment.

Verse Explanation

Even if Edom could soar like an eagle or build its home among the stars, God would still bring it down. No height or human security can place anyone beyond the reach of God's judgment.

"Though you ascend as high as the eagle,"

The eagle was known for building its nest on the highest cliffs, far out of reach of any enemy. Job speaks of the eagle that makes "its nest on high" and dwells "on the crag of the rock" (Job 39:27–28). Edom lived among rocky heights, and its people trusted those heights to keep them safe (1:3). God takes up that picture and pushes it further: even if Edom could fly as high as an eagle, it would not be high enough.

"And though you set your nest among the stars,"

No nation could actually build its home among the stars. God is describing the highest place imaginable to show that no height could put Edom out of His reach. The language echoes the proud boast of the king of Babylon in Isaiah: "I will ascend into heaven, I will exalt my throne above the stars of God." That king's end came quickly: "Yet you shall be brought down to Sheol, to the lowest depths of the Pit" (Isaiah 14:13, 15). Reaching for the stars is the picture of pride trying to place itself above everyone, even God, and in both passages it ends in a fall.

"From there I will bring you down,"

These words answer Edom's own boast. In its heart Edom had asked, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" (1:3). The verb God uses here is the same Hebrew word Edom used, and His reply is direct: "I will." Edom thought its question had no answer. God gives the answer Himself.

"From there" means God would act from the very place where Edom felt most secure. He did not need to wait for Edom to come down from its heights. Through Amos, God gave the same warning to those in Israel who thought they could escape His judgment: "Though they climb up to heaven, from there I will bring them down" (Amos 9:2). No place is too high or too far for God to reach. Jeremiah delivered nearly the same message against Edom, using the image of an eagle's nest and the promise that the LORD would bring it down from there (Jeremiah 49:16).

"says the LORD."

This phrase is a solemn formula that marks the words as God's own declaration. Edom's confidence rested on cliffs and fortresses, but the outcome would be decided by the word of the LORD, the God who rules over every nation. Once He has spoken, Edom's heights cannot protect it.

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] God Answers Pride in Pride’s Own Verb. Edom asked, “Who will bring me down?” (v. 3), and God answers with the same verb: “From there I will bring you down.” Pride’s boast becomes the exact wording of its sentence, sealed with “declares the LORD,” the formula of a divine oath. God does not always answer pride at once, but He always answers it, and He tends to answer it in its own language. The question we ask most defiantly is often the one God eventually answers most precisely.

[Discovery] A Nest Among the Stars. “Though you build high like the eagle, though you set your nest among the stars.” The bird is most likely the griffon vulture, which nests in the gorges of Petra, so the picture starts with a bird Edom saw every day and then climbs past anything a bird could reach, one of the boldest images in Hebrew poetry. It anticipates the king of Babylon who said, “I will raise my throne above the stars of God” (Isaiah 14:13), and answers him as Amos does: “Though they ascend to heaven, from there I will bring them down” (Amos 9:2). When human ambition builds its nest above the stars, it only builds a higher cliff from which God will cast it down. God reserves His swiftest arrows for those who crown themselves. There is no altitude above His reach (Psalm 139:8). The only safe height is the one to which He lifts us.

[Christ & Life] The True High Place. The way of the flesh, the way of Esau, is upward mobility through self-exaltation, like the first sin of Lucifer: “I will ascend” (Isaiah 14:13–14). The way of Christ is downward mobility through the incarnation and the cross (Philippians 2:5–11). Only God decides who remains in the heavenly places.

[Christ & Life] An Eagle That Was Never Carried. Edom built its nest high “like the eagle,” straining upward by its own strength. Israel’s story began the other way around: “I bore you on eagles’ wings and brought you to Myself” (Exodus 19:4). Edom wanted the eagle’s height without the eagle’s Carrier. People still spend their lives building a nest among the stars when God is offering to lift them on His wings and bring them to Himself (Isaiah 40:31). The height He gives is always a height toward Him. Are we building our own nest, or letting Him carry us?

[Seed] A Nest of Hay and Stubble. One old commentator applied Edom’s “nest” to the religious works in which a person rests, a nest made of hay and stubble. The word “stubble” returns in verse 18 as the name for Esau himself. What Edom built its security out of turned out to be what it was made of.

NKJV

Obadiah 1:5 "If thieves had come to you, If robbers by night"” Oh, how you will be cut off!"” Would they not have stolen till they had enough? If grape-gatherers had come to you, Would they not have left some gleanings? (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:5 “If thieves came at night and robbed you(what a disaster awaits you!),they would not take everything.Those who harvest grapesalways leave a few for the poor.But your enemies will wipe you out completely! (NLT)

Main Idea

Ordinary thieves take only what they want, and grape pickers leave a few grapes behind, but Edom's coming destruction will be far more complete than either. This picture shows how total God's judgment on Edom will be.

Verse Explanation

Ordinary thieves take only what they want, and grape pickers leave a few grapes behind, but Edom's coming destruction will be far more complete than either. This picture shows how total God's judgment on Edom will be.

"If thieves had come to you, If robbers by night—"

The LORD is speaking to Edom, the nation descended from Esau, and He compares its coming ruin to a break-in. Robbers who come at night work quickly and quietly. They grab what is valuable and easy to carry, and then they slip away before anyone wakes. A family robbed in the night suffers a real loss, but in the morning the house is still standing and much of what they own is still there.

"Oh, how you will be cut off!—"

The LORD interrupts His own comparison before finishing it. The Hebrew word carries the sense of being destroyed or brought to silence. The thought of what is coming upon Edom is so terrible that it bursts into the middle of the picture. Edom will not suffer an ordinary loss.

"Would they not have stolen till they had enough?"

Thieves take only "enough," as much as they want or can manage. Once they have that, they stop. Even the worst robbery has a limit. Edom's judgment will have no such limit, because it is not coming from greedy men who eventually get their fill. It is coming from God, and He will finish what He intends to do.

"If grape-gatherers had come to you, Would they not have left some gleanings?"

"Gleanings" were the grapes left on the vines after the harvest, the small clusters missed or passed over. In Israel, God's law required harvesters to leave these behind on purpose so the poor and the foreigner could gather them (Leviticus 19:10; Deuteronomy 24:21). Even when pickers had no such concern, a vineyard was never stripped completely bare. Something was always left.

Both pictures make the same point. Thieves stop when they have enough, and harvesters leave some grapes behind, but Edom will be stripped of everything. Nothing will be overlooked and nothing will remain. Jeremiah used nearly the same pictures of grape-gatherers and thieves by night in his own prophecy against Edom (Jeremiah 49:9). Edom trusted in its high mountain homeland, but its ruin will be more thorough than any robbery or harvest, because it will come from the LORD Himself.

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] Even Thieves Know When to Stop. “If thieves came to you, if robbers by night … would they not steal only until they had enough?” Ordinary thieves and grape gatherers work on the principle of satisfaction: they take what they can carry and leave the rest. Their greed has a limit called “enough.” Human ruin is usually partial. But the judgment coming on Edom is not a burglary; it is a total accounting. Earthly robbers spare what they cannot carry, but when God finally deals with entrenched, unrepentant pride, He strips it completely bare. God takes no pleasure in it, but when He settles accounts, nothing is overlooked. The thief’s “enough” is a mercy the proud will not receive from the LORD.

[Insight] The Sorrow Inside the Sentence. In the middle of the verse the prophet breaks in: “O how you will be ruined!” The cry sits awkwardly between the two pictures, as if Obadiah could not wait for the sentence to finish, and it carries the prophet’s own grief over an enemy’s fall. The verb means “to be cut off,” and also “to be silenced”: the mouth that asked, “Who will bring me down?” will be stilled. God’s messengers are allowed to grieve the judgments they announce. A prophet who can announce ruin without a catch in his voice has stopped sounding like the God who sent him.

[Discovery] No Gleanings Left. “If grape gatherers came to you, would they not leave some gleanings?” Israel’s harvesters were commanded to leave gleanings for the stranger, the orphan, and the widow (Deuteronomy 24:21); that is how Ruth was fed. Edom will be stripped so bare that not even the poor’s portion remains. Edom had left its brother nothing on the day of his calamity (vv. 13–14), and now its own vineyard will be picked clean. Mercy shown is the seed of mercy received, and “judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy” (James 2:13).

Obadiah 1:6
NKJV

Obadiah 1:6 "Oh, how Esau shall be searched out! How his hidden treasures shall be sought after! (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:6 Every nook and cranny of Edomwill be searched and looted.Every treasure will be found and taken. (NLT)

Main Idea

Esau, another name for Edom, will be thoroughly searched, and even its hidden treasures will be found and taken. Nothing Edom tried to protect would remain safe from the coming invaders.

Verse Explanation

Esau, another name for Edom, will be thoroughly searched, and even its hidden treasures will be found and taken. Nothing Edom tried to protect would remain safe from the coming invaders.

"Oh, how Esau shall be searched out!"

The cry "Oh, how" picks up the same exclamation from verse 5, where God compared Edom's coming ruin to a visit from thieves and grape-gatherers. Both leave something behind, but Edom's invaders will not. The verse calls the nation "Esau," the name of the ancestor it came from, as if the whole people were one man whose house is being ransacked.

The Hebrew verb translated "searched out" means to search carefully and thoroughly, the way a person goes through every corner looking for something. It is the word used when Laban went through Jacob's tents hunting for his stolen household idols (Genesis 31:35), and when Joseph's steward went through his brothers' sacks one by one until the silver cup turned up in Benjamin's (Genesis 44:12). God used the same verb when He warned Jerusalem, "I will search Jerusalem with lamps" (Zephaniah 1:12). The invaders God stirs up against Edom would go through the land the same way, missing nothing.

"How his hidden treasures shall be sought after!"

Edom had real wealth to protect. Its territory lay along major trade routes, including the road later known as the King's Highway, which carried goods between Arabia, Egypt, and the lands to the north, and the region south of the Dead Sea was known for its copper. Edom's rugged mountains, full of caves and cracks in the rock, offered natural hiding places for valuables. The phrase "hidden treasures" describes exactly that kind of wealth: things deliberately put away where no one could find them.

The searchers would find even those. Jeremiah gave a closely related message about Edom: "But I have made Esau bare; I have uncovered his secret places, and he shall not be able to hide himself" (Jeremiah 49:10). Whatever Edom thought it had secured, whether its wealth or itself, would be uncovered. There is also a fitting justice in this. Edom would later be charged with laying hands on Judah's goods in the day of its disaster (verse 13), and now Edom's own carefully hidden riches would be taken from it.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] God Calls the Nation by Its Father’s Name. “O how Esau will be ransacked.” From here on the prophet drops “Edom” and uses the personal name: Esau, the mountain of Esau, the house of Esau. The nation is never allowed to forget the man it came from, the brother who sold a birthright for one meal and swore to kill Jacob (Genesis 27:41; Hebrews 12:16–17). Nations and families carry the character of their roots until grace interrupts the line. The good news hidden in the name is that the God who remembers Esau also remembers that Esau was once Jacob’s brother (v. 10).

[Foundational Truth] Nothing Remains Hidden Before God. “His hidden treasures searched out!” Edom felt secure because its treasures were literally buried deep in the caves of the rock. Picture the conquerors going through Petra like a man with a lamp, into every dark corner. Spiritually, everything the flesh tries to conceal, hidden sins, secret motives, stockpiled resentments, will finally be searched out and exposed by the light of God.

[Discovery] The Treasure No Searchlight Can Find. The word for “hidden treasures” occurs only here, from a root meaning “to hide,” and fits a trading people storing wealth in rock-cut chambers along the King’s Highway. Treasures on earth can never be laid up so safely that thieves cannot break through. Yet the same root appears in “Your word I have hidden in my heart” (Psalm 119:11). What we hide in God, and what God hides in us, is the one treasure no invader can search out (Matthew 6:20; Colossians 3:3).

Obadiah 1:7 “All your allies will turn against you.They will help to chase you from your land.They will promise you peacewhile plotting to deceive and destroy you.Your trusted friends will set traps for you,and you won’t even know about it. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom's own allies, nations that shared its table and claimed friendship, will turn against it, drive it from its land, and set traps for it without Edom even realizing it. The betrayal Edom practiced against Judah would come back on Edom through its own trusted partners.

Verse Explanation

Edom's own allies, nations that shared its table and claimed friendship, will turn against it, drive it from its land, and set traps for it without Edom even realizing it. The betrayal Edom practiced against Judah would come back on Edom through its own trusted partners.

"All the men in your confederacy"

Edom relied on more than its rugged mountain homeland. It also relied on alliances with surrounding peoples who had joined with it by agreement. In the ancient world, treaties like these gave a small nation protection, trade, and help in war. Edom trusted these partners to stand with it. God says they would be the ones to turn against it.

"Shall force you to the border;"

The Hebrew says the allies will "send" Edom to the border. This most likely means they will drive Edom out of its own land and push it beyond its borders. Some understand it to mean that when Edom sends to its allies for help, they turn its messengers back at the border with nothing. Either way, the friends Edom counted on would offer no refuge when trouble came. History points in this direction: Arab peoples, especially the Nabataeans, gradually took over Edom's territory, and many Edomites moved west into southern Judah.

"The men at peace with you Shall deceive you and prevail against you."

"The men at peace with you" is literally "the men of your peace," people bound to Edom by agreements of peace and friendship. These are the people who would deceive Edom and overpower it. The danger would not come only from open enemies. It would come from partners Edom never thought to suspect.

This is exactly what Edom had done to Judah. Edom was Judah's brother nation, and yet when Jerusalem fell, Edom stood with the invaders and acted "as one of them" (1:11). Now Edom would learn what it is like to be betrayed by those closest to it. This fits the principle God states a few verses later: "As you have done, it shall be done to you" (1:15).

"Those who eat your bread shall lay a trap for you."

Sharing a meal was a serious sign of friendship and loyalty. Covenants were often sealed with a meal. Jacob and Laban ate bread together after making their agreement (Genesis 31:54), and Isaac held a feast with Abimelech before they swore peace with each other (Genesis 26:30). To eat someone's bread and then plot against him was a deep betrayal. David felt that pain: "Even my own familiar friend in whom I trusted, Who ate my bread, Has lifted up his heel against me" (Psalm 41:9). Edom's table companions would quietly set a snare for it while still enjoying its hospitality.

"No one is aware of it."

The Hebrew literally says, "There is no understanding in him." Edom would not see the betrayal coming. A nation proud of its secure position and confident in its friendships would be caught completely off guard. Edom was known for its wisdom, yet it would be unable to recognize the danger growing at its own table.

Study Notes

Edom's alliances were real, and its record shows how quickly loyalties could shift. Jeremiah 27:3 records that envoys from Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon came to King Zedekiah in Jerusalem. This was most likely a meeting to plan resistance against Babylon. A few years later, when Babylon besieged Jerusalem, Edom sided with the conqueror against its brother (vv. 11–14).

The partners Edom later trusted proved no more faithful. In the 550s BC the Babylonian king Nabonidus campaigned through this region on his way into Arabia. A damaged line in a Babylonian record of his reign appears to mention Edom. A rock carving at es-Sela, near Bozrah, is widely attributed to him. The Edomite kingdom seems to have come to an end around this time. In the following centuries, Arab tribes and then the Nabataeans moved into Edom's mountains. The Edomites who remained were pushed west into southern Judah. The prophecy does not name the "men at peace" with Edom, so these events show how the warning came true rather than identifying those men.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] Three Circles of Trust, All Broken. “All the men allied with you … the men at peace with you … they who eat your bread.” Literally: men of your covenant, men of your peace, men of your bread. Treaty partners drive Edom to its own border, peaceful neighbors deceive it, and table companions set the trap. As an old commentator put it, those who make flesh their trust arm it against themselves. Human alliances are gifts from God, but they make fragile foundations (Psalm 118:8–9; Jeremiah 17:5–8), and the circle we trust most is the one that can hurt us most.

[Christ & Life] The Wound Under the Table. “They who eat your bread will set an ambush for you.” The rare word can mean a snare, or a festering wound underneath, hard to reach and slow to heal. Shared bread was a pledge of loyalty in the ancient world, which is why David grieved over the friend “who ate my bread” and “lifted up his heel against me” (Psalm 41:9). Jesus applied those words to Judas at the table (John 13:18), and handed him the morsel. Betrayal by someone we fed is among life’s deepest wounds. Jesus knows it from the inside, and He washed the feet of the one who did it.

[Seed] The Inevitable Betrayal of the World. Edom sought security in alliances with worldly powers rather than resting in truth, and the world turned on it. The “men at peace with you” were the very ones who laid the snare. Any alliance built on worldly pragmatism rather than truth is unstable at its core. Compromise with the world buys neither loyalty nor lasting safety. The world will finally turn on those who sell their souls to belong to it, and the friends we court to escape God will be the ones who push us into the ditch. Worldly alliances offer the illusion of safety and deliver ultimate vulnerability.

[Application] The Loss of Spiritual Discernment. The verse ends abruptly: “There is no understanding in him.” It will all take Edom by surprise. Pride blinds. Edom was famous for its wise men, yet its arrogance made it gullible to the most obvious betrayal. When we walk in pride, we lose the ability to discern who our true friends are.

Obadiah 1:8 At that time not a single wise personwill be left in the whole land of Edom,”says the LORD.“For on the mountains of EdomI will destroy everyone who has understanding. (NLT)

Main Idea

On the day of judgment, God will remove Edom's famous wise men and their understanding. Edom was known for its wisdom, but human cleverness offers no protection when God acts against a nation.

Verse Explanation

On the day of judgment, God will remove Edom's famous wise men and their understanding. Edom was known for its wisdom, but human cleverness offers no protection when God acts against a nation.

"“Will I not in that day,” says the LORD,"

"That day" is the day God acts against Edom, when the judgment announced in the previous verses arrives. The LORD asks a question, but He is not uncertain. "Will I not" expects a firm yes: of course He will. Adding "says the LORD" marks this as His own declaration, not a guess about how events might turn out. Edom may have believed its leaders could outthink any threat, but the One who decides the outcome has already spoken.

"“Even destroy the wise men from Edom,"****

Edom was known for its wisdom. Teman, one of its leading clans and regions, was named for a grandson of Esau (Genesis 36:11), and Job's friend Eliphaz, one of the men who came to counsel him, was "the Temanite" (Job 2:11). Jeremiah, prophesying against Edom, asked whether wisdom had disappeared from Teman and counsel had perished from the prudent (Jeremiah 49:7). These wise men were the advisers and counselors a nation relied on for sound judgment in politics, trade, and war.

God says He will destroy them. The Hebrew verb means to make something perish or vanish. Edom's pride rested partly on its mountain fortresses and partly on its clever leaders. Neither could save it. "There is no wisdom or understanding or counsel against the LORD" (Proverbs 21:30).

"And understanding from the mountains of Esau?"

"The mountains of Esau" means Edom's rugged mountain homeland, the land of Esau's descendants. God will remove not only the wise men but understanding itself, the ability to see a situation clearly and respond wisely.

That loss has already begun in the verse just before. Edom's allies would trap it, and "No one is aware of it" (1:7). The Hebrew word behind "aware" there is the same word translated "understanding" here. Edom's famous counselors would not see their own friends' betrayal coming. A nation proud of its insight would be caught completely off guard, because when God brings judgment, He can take away even the good sense people depend on to protect themselves.

None.

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] When God Takes Away the Wise Men. “Will I not on that day … destroy the wise men from Edom, and understanding from the mountain of Esau?” Edom was famous for wisdom (Jeremiah 49:7), and Job’s friend Eliphaz came from Teman (Job 2:11), yet Eliphaz’s polished counsel ended under God’s verdict: “you have not spoken of Me what is right” (Job 42:7). The wise are not necessarily killed; their wisdom is simply withdrawn. The old proverb says, “Whom God wishes to destroy, He first makes mad.” When God’s judgment falls, human brilliance, diplomatic intellect, and political pragmatism evaporate into folly. Understanding is a gift held on loan. Brilliance cut off from the fear of the LORD goes blind at the exact moment it is needed (Isaiah 29:14; 1 Corinthians 1:19).

[Foundational Truth] The Removal of Wisdom as Judgment. God does not only destroy Edom’s walls; He destroys its intellect. One of the most terrifying forms of divine judgment is when God hands a person or a culture over to a depraved mind (Romans 1:28), removing the restraint of common grace and basic wisdom.

[Insight] Counselors Who Could Not See the Day. “On that day” begins the “day” language that runs through verses 11–15: Edom’s day of reckoning answers Judah’s day of calamity. Edom’s “wise men” were likely royal counselors, perhaps tied to divination (compare Isaiah 47:10, 13). The experts who claimed to read the future could not see their own day coming.

Obadiah 1:9
NKJV

Obadiah 1:9 Then your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed, To the end that everyone from the mountains of Esau May be cut off by slaughter. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:9 The mightiest warriors of Temanwill be terrified,and everyone on the mountains of Edomwill be cut down in the slaughter. (NLT)

Main Idea

Teman, a leading city in Edom, will see its warriors lose their courage, so that the people of Edom's mountains face slaughter. With both its wisdom and its military strength gone, Edom will have no defense left.

Verse Explanation

Teman, a leading city in Edom, will see its warriors lose their courage, so that the people of Edom's mountains face slaughter. With both its wisdom and its military strength gone, Edom will have no defense left.

"Then your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed,"

Teman was one of Edom's leading towns or districts, named after a grandson of Esau (Genesis 36:10–11). It was important enough that prophets used its name to stand for the whole nation (Jeremiah 49:20; Amos 1:12). Teman was also known for wisdom. Eliphaz, the oldest of Job's friends, was a Temanite (Job 2:11), and Jeremiah asked of Edom, "Is wisdom no more in Teman?" (Jeremiah 49:7). The verse before this one announced that God would destroy the wise men of Edom (1:8). Now He turns to its soldiers.

The "mighty men" were Edom's warriors, its trained and proven fighting men. The Hebrew word translated "dismayed" means to be shattered or broken, especially by terror. These soldiers would not simply lose a battle. They would lose their nerve. Edom trusted its mountain strongholds, its clever counselors, and its fighting men. God would empty all three. Courage is not something a nation can simply count on, and when God brings judgment, even its strongest men can be overcome by fear.

"To the end that everyone from the mountains of Esau may be cut off by slaughter."

"To the end that" shows the result. Once Edom's warriors were broken, nothing would stand between its people and the enemy. "The mountains of Esau" refers to Edom's rugged mountain homeland, the high, rocky country that made the Edomites feel no one could reach them (1:3). The name recalls that the nation came from Esau. The heights they trusted for safety would become the place of their slaughter.

"Everyone" shows how complete the judgment would be. It would not stop with a defeated army or a few captured towns, but would reach throughout the land. "Cut off" is a strong word for destruction, and Obadiah uses it again for Edom's future (1:10) and for what Edom did to Judah's survivors (1:14). Edom would suffer the same fate it had brought on others.

Study Notes

The Hebrew text that the NKJV follows ends verse 9 with the words "by slaughter." The ancient Greek translation, called the Septuagint, and some other early translations attach those words to the beginning of verse 10 instead: "Because of the slaughter and violence against your brother Jacob." Some modern translations follow that arrangement.

The Hebrew consonants are the same either way. The question is only where the sentence breaks. The NKJV's arrangement is well supported and reads naturally. It says that Edom would be "cut off by slaughter." The other reading would make verse 10 name slaughter alongside violence as Edom's crime. Both readings fit the prophecy's larger point, because Edom's punishment would match what Edom had done to Judah (v. 15).

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] Courage Is Not a Personality Trait. “Then your mighty men will be dismayed, O Teman.” “Dismayed” means shattered, terrified, the very word God uses when He tells Joshua, “Do not tremble or be dismayed, for the LORD your God is with you” (Joshua 1:9). Teman was known for fierce warriors, yet the seasoned, hardened soldiers would tremble like frightened children, like the panic from God that fell on the Philistines (1 Samuel 14:15). What God forbids His people because of His presence, He sends on those who trust in themselves. Physical strength and military training dissolve into dismay; courage evaporates when the Lord withdraws His hand. Teman’s warriors lost heart before they lost the battle. Courage is an unearned gift and the fruit of God’s presence, not of a strong constitution.

[Insight] Room Left for a Remnant. Teman, named for Esau’s grandson (Genesis 36:11), stands for the whole nation, and “everyone” in Hebrew idiom often means the nation as a whole. That leaves room for the remnant of Edom that Amos foresaw coming under the name of the LORD (Amos 9:12). Even a sweeping sentence on a nation does not close the door on its people.

Obadiah 1:10
NKJV

Obadiah 1:10 "For violence against your brother Jacob, (H3290) Shame shall cover you, And you shall be cut off forever. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:10 “Because of the violence you didto your close relatives in Israel,you will be filled with shameand destroyed forever. (NLT)

Main Idea

The reason for Edom's judgment is its violence against its brother Jacob, the nation of Israel, since Edom and Israel descended from the twin brothers Esau and Jacob. Edom's cruelty toward its own relatives will bring it lasting shame and permanent destruction as a nation.

Verse Explanation

The reason for Edom's judgment is its violence against its brother Jacob, the nation of Israel, since Edom and Israel descended from the twin brothers Esau and Jacob. Edom's cruelty toward its own relatives will bring it lasting shame and permanent destruction as a nation.

"For violence against your brother Jacob,"

"For" gives the reason behind the judgment announced in the previous verses. The Hebrew word for "violence" describes cruel, ruthless wrongdoing that tramples on others. It is the word used when "the earth was filled with violence" before the flood (Genesis 6:11). Joel names the same sin when he says Edom would become "a desolate wilderness, because of violence against the people of Judah" (Joel 3:19). The verses that follow spell out what this violence looked like: Edom gloated over Judah's ruin, joined in the looting, and handed over people trying to escape (11–14).

What made Edom's violence especially wicked is that it was done to a brother. Edom descended from Esau, and Israel descended from Esau's twin brother, Jacob. The two boys struggled even in their mother's womb, and the LORD told Rebekah, "Two nations are in your womb" (Genesis 25:23). Esau became the father of the Edomites (Genesis 36:1), and Jacob, whom God renamed Israel (Genesis 32:28), became the father of the twelve tribes. "Jacob" here means the people of Israel, and especially Judah, whose capital, Jerusalem, suffered the attack Obadiah describes.

The two nations never forgot they were related. When Israel traveled toward the Promised Land, Moses sent word to the king of Edom, "Thus says your brother Israel" (Numbers 20:14). God told His people, "You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deuteronomy 23:7). Israel was commanded to treat Edom as family, but Edom treated Israel as an enemy. Amos condemned Edom because "he pursued his brother with the sword, and cast off all pity" (Amos 1:11). The bond between them should have moved Edom to help. Instead, it made Edom's cruelty a betrayal.

"Shame shall cover you,"

Shame would wrap around Edom like a garment until nothing else could be seen. Edom had been a proud nation, confident in its mountain strongholds and admired for its wisdom. It had looked on Judah's humiliation with contempt, but the disgrace Edom enjoyed watching would now cover Edom itself. A nation that prized its honor would be remembered for how it treated its brother.

"And you shall be cut off forever."

To be "cut off" means to be destroyed and removed, like a branch cut from a tree. The word "forever" shows that Edom's fall would be permanent. It would not recover and rise again as a nation. This stands in sharp contrast to the end of the prophecy, where God promises that Jacob will be delivered and restored (17–21). Both peoples would suffer, but only Israel had a future promised by God. History bore this out, as Edom eventually disappeared as a distinct nation. The judgment fell on Edom as a people and a kingdom; it does not mean that every individual descended from Esau died.

Study Notes

None.

Spirit & Life

[Foundational Truth] God Shows His Reasons. “Because of violence to your brother Jacob.” The word “because” turns the book into a courtroom: God does not strike arbitrarily; He lays out the evidence. The charge is chamas, violent wrong, the word that filled the earth before the flood (Genesis 6:11, 13) and the word Habakkuk cried out against (Habakkuk 1:2). Judgment flows from God’s goodness, not against it. A God who never named violence and never answered it would not be good.

[Foundational Truth] The Law of Brotherhood. The indictment pivots here. Edom’s great sin was not merely pride; it was the violation of blood ties. Esau and Jacob were twins, and God remembers family bonds. God measures our spiritual integrity by how we treat our brothers and sisters, especially in their moments of weakness.

[Insight] Your Brother Jacob. The victim is not called “Judah” but “your brother Jacob.” Israel had been commanded, “You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother” (Deuteronomy 23:7), and Esau had once run to Jacob, embraced him, and wept on his neck (Genesis 33:4). Edom’s descendants reopened the wound their forefather had closed; Amos says Edom “cast off all pity” and “kept his wrath forever” (Amos 1:11). Sins against family carry extra weight because they betray a bond God Himself gave. The nearer the relationship, the heavier the violence weighs on God’s scale.

[Application] Covered With the Opposite of What You Wore. “Shame shall cover you, and you shall be cut off forever.” In verse 3 Edom was wrapped in pride; now it is wrapped in shame, the garment of the humbled (Micah 7:10). Ezekiel calls Edom’s hatred an “ancient” and “perpetual” enmity (Ezekiel 35:5), and the sentence matches it: an enmity kept forever ends in being cut off forever. God’s sentences are not always completed at once; Edom’s unwound over centuries until the Idumeans vanished from history after A.D. 70. A grudge nursed for a lifetime does not stay small. It becomes who we are, and then it becomes our verdict. Is there an old family or church wound we have kept alive for years, that we need to let God close instead of reopening it?

[Application] When Family Bitterness Festers. The blood feud reached back over a thousand years to the struggle in Rebekah’s womb (Genesis 25:22–23). Unattended family bitterness and generational jealousy fester into corporate, destructive hatred that finally brings down the judgment of God. Is there a family grudge, a sibling feud, or an old root of bitterness that we are keeping alive? What step of reconciliation or radical forgiveness must we take now, before it poisons the next generation?

Main Idea

When foreign armies, most likely the Babylonians in 586 BC, captured Jerusalem, carried off its people, and divided up the city by casting lots, Edom stood by and acted just like the invaders. By refusing to help its brother and joining the enemy's side, Edom shared in the enemy's guilt.

Verse Explanation

When foreign armies, most likely the Babylonians in 586 BC, captured Jerusalem, carried off its people, and divided up the city by casting lots, Edom stood by and acted just like the invaders. By refusing to help its brother and joining the enemy's side, Edom shared in the enemy's guilt.

"In the day that you stood on the other side—"

The LORD is still speaking to Edom, the nation descended from Esau, and here He points to a specific day. In Hebrew this is literally "in the day of your standing opposite," a picture of someone keeping his distance and watching. The same kind of language appears when David grieves that "my loved ones and my friends stand aloof from my plague, and my relatives stand afar off" (Psalm 38:11). Edom watched from a distance while its brother was being destroyed. That is serious enough on its own, because Israel had been told, "You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother" (Deuteronomy 23:7). Edom owed Judah the same loyalty and gave none.

"In the day that strangers carried captive his forces,"

"His" refers to Jacob, that is, the people of Israel, the brother Edom wronged (v. 10). Obadiah does not name the invaders. Most likely they were the Babylonians, who captured Jerusalem in 586 BC. King Nebuchadnezzar's army broke through the city wall, burned the temple and the great houses, and carried the people into exile in Babylon (2 Kings 25:1–11). The Hebrew word translated "forces" can mean an army or strength, but it can also mean wealth. The same word is translated "substance" in verse 13. Both happened that day. Judah's people were led away as captives, and its treasures, including the bronze and gold from the temple, were hauled off to Babylon (2 Kings 25:13–17).

"When foreigners entered his gates"

A city's gates were its main defense. When enemies walked through them, the city had fallen. Jerusalem's gates had once stood for the safety of God's people, and now foreign soldiers walked through them freely.

"And cast lots for Jerusalem—"

Conquering armies often cast lots to divide what they had captured, including land, goods, and even people. Joel describes the same cruelty against God's people: "They have cast lots for My people, have given a boy as payment for a harlot, and sold a girl for wine, that they may drink" (Joel 3:3). Jerusalem, the city where God had placed His name, was being divided up like spoils of war, and its people were being handed out as slaves.

"Even you were as one of them."

This is the heart of the charge. Edom did not carry Jerusalem's people away or cast the lots, but God says Edom was no different from those who did. A brother who stands by while his brother is attacked has already chosen a side. Edom's guilt went beyond watching, too. In the verses that follow, the LORD lists what Edom actually did: it gloated over Judah's ruin, entered the city to plunder, and caught fleeing survivors to hand them over (vv. 12–14). Other passages confirm that Edom sided with Jerusalem's enemies when the city fell. Psalm 137:7 remembers the Edomites shouting, "Raze it, raze it, to its very foundation!" Ezekiel also condemns Edom for shedding Israel's blood "at the time of their calamity" (Ezekiel 35:5). Edom should have been the first to help its brother. Instead, it stood with the foreigners and joined in the destruction.

Study Notes

Obadiah never names the foreigners who entered Jerusalem's gates, so his date has to be worked out from the evidence. The strongest case points to the Babylonian destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC. Several other passages accuse Edom of hostility at exactly that time (Psalm 137:7; Lamentations 4:21–22; Ezekiel 25:12–14; 35:5). The language of captivity, of lots cast for Jerusalem, and of exiles in distant places (v. 20) also fits that disaster well.

An earlier setting has also been proposed: the reign of King Jehoram, around 850 BC, when Edom revolted (2 Kings 8:20–22). In those days the Philistines and Arabians invaded Judah and carried off the king's possessions and his family (2 Chronicles 21:16–17). Supporters point to Obadiah's place between Amos and Jonah in the Bible's order and to the possibility that Jeremiah drew on Obadiah. However, the account of Jehoram's reign does not mention Edom taking part in that invasion, and it does not describe Jerusalem falling or its people being exiled. For these reasons the Babylonian setting remains the more likely one, though it is not certain.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] Standing Aloof Is Standing Somewhere. “On the day that you stood aloof … you too were as one of them.” Literally Edom “stood opposite,” on the other side. Strangers carried off Jerusalem’s wealth, foreigners entered its gates, and Edom watched from across the road. Idle spectators of a neighbor’s troubles, when able to help, have a great deal to answer for. We hear the priest and the Levite who “passed by on the other side” (Luke 10:31–32). God does not accept a chosen distance from a brother’s suffering as neutrality. When the bond is close and the need is clear, the other side of the road is a side.

[Foundational Truth] The Guilt of the Spectator. Edom did not start the attack on Jerusalem; Babylon did. Edom did not at first swing the sword; it simply stood aloof. Yet the divine verdict is, “you were like one of them.” In God’s kingdom there is no neutral ground regarding the suffering of a brother. Passive complicity in the face of injustice is counted as active participation. To stand by with folded arms while God’s people are crushed is to be charged as a co-conspirator; to do nothing when a brother is crushed is to join the crushers. Withholding love when it is in our power to act is counted as alignment with the enemy. Silence in the face of injustice is complicity, and cold indifference carries the same moral guilt as open hostility. Is there someone in our family, church, or workplace being slandered or mistreated while we watch from across the road and call it neutrality?

[Christ & Life] They Rolled Dice for Jerusalem. “Foreigners entered his gates and cast lots for Jerusalem.” Casting lots was how conquerors divided captives and spoil (Joel 3:3; Nahum 3:10); the city of God was gambled over like loot. The verse climbs: plunder in the countryside, then the gates breached, then lots thrown inside the city. Centuries later soldiers cast lots for the clothing of Jerusalem’s King at the foot of His cross (Psalm 22:18; John 19:24). What the world treats as spoil, God counts as His own, and He keeps the record of who stood by while the dice were thrown.

[Seed] “And You, Brutus?”. The emphatic “you too” has the sting of “And you, Brutus?” The betrayal of a stranger wounds; the betrayal of a brother wounds deeper. One old devotional writer turned the phrase on believers: when we sin, who are the chosen favorites of heaven, we sin with an emphasis.

"But you should not have gazed on the day of your brother In the day of his captivity;"

"Gazed" here means more than seeing what happened. It describes looking on with satisfaction, watching an enemy's suffering and enjoying the sight. Edom, the nation descended from Esau, did this while the Babylonians destroyed Jerusalem. The one it watched was its "brother," and that word makes Edom's satisfaction even uglier. A brother should have grieved.

"The day of your brother" means the day of his trouble, the day disaster fell on him. The word translated "captivity" points to that disaster and to the calamity of being overrun. It is related to the word for "foreigners" in the previous verse. The day foreign armies entered Jerusalem's gates was the day Judah's life was overturned and its people were carried away.

"Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah In the day of their destruction;"

Now the satisfaction in Edom's eyes becomes joy in its heart. The LORD names the victims plainly as "the children of Judah," the people of Jerusalem and the southern kingdom. Their day was a day of destruction, when their city was ruined and many perished. Edom celebrated it.

Scripture treats this kind of joy as a serious sin, even against an enemy: "Do not rejoice when your enemy falls, and do not let your heart be glad when he stumbles; lest the LORD see it, and it displease Him" (Proverbs 24:17–18). It also warns, "He who is glad at calamity will not go unpunished" (Proverbs 17:5). Edom's gladness was not a private feeling God overlooked. Ezekiel recorded the LORD's response: "As you rejoiced because the inheritance of the house of Israel was desolate, so I will do to you" (Ezekiel 35:15).

"Nor should you have spoken proudly In the day of distress."

From the eyes and the heart, Edom's sin came out through its mouth. "Spoken proudly" is literally "made your mouth large." It describes big, boastful talk and mocking words spoken over someone who has fallen. This is the same pride that had already deceived Edom's heart (verse 3), now aimed at a brother who was helpless. Psalm 137 remembers what the Edomites said that day: "Remember, O LORD, against the sons of Edom the day of Jerusalem, who said, 'Raze it, raze it, to its very foundation!'" (Psalm 137:7). Ezekiel adds that God heard every word: "Thus with your mouth you have boasted against Me and multiplied your words against Me; I have heard them" (Ezekiel 35:13). Mocking God's people in their distress was boasting against God Himself.

All through these lines, the word "day" keeps returning: the day of his captivity, the day of their destruction, the day of distress. Edom treated Judah's worst day as an occasion to enjoy, but God was keeping account of that day, and a day of reckoning was coming for Edom as well (verse 15).

[Insight] Gloating Starts in the Eyes. “Do not gloat over your brother’s day.” The verb is simply “look,” but with its preposition it means to look on with satisfaction, to feast the eyes. Edom’s slide began not with its hands but with its eyes. Savoring the discipline or ruin of a brother is an immediate insult to God’s mercy. “Do not rejoice when your enemy falls … lest the LORD see it, and it displease Him” (Proverbs 24:17–18): while we are watching a brother fall, God is watching our face. Jesus looked at Jerusalem in its coming day of calamity and wept (Luke 19:41). What happens in our eyes when someone falls shows whose heart we have.

[Application] The Danger of Schadenfreude. There is a deep, fleshly sickness that secretly delights in the failure or suffering of rivals. God forbids us to rejoice when others fall, even when we feel they deserve it; Proverbs warns that if we do, the Lord may turn His anger away from them (Proverbs 24:18). Whose fall have we secretly enjoyed, a rival, a competitor, someone who hurt us, and what did that pleasure reveal about our hearts? How can we learn Christlike grief over their stumbling instead of gloating?

[Discovery] The Day He Became a Stranger. “The day of his misfortune” uses a word found only here, built on the root “to be strange, foreign”; the KJV renders it “the day that he became a stranger.” On that day Judah was made a foreigner in its own city, handled by strangers as if it belonged to no one. The one neighbor who was not a stranger, the brother, chose that day to treat him like one. There are days when a person needs someone who still knows his name. God calls His people to be that someone, because He Himself has promised never to treat His own as strangers (Isaiah 49:15–16).

[Insight] A Mouth Made Large. “Do not boast in the day of their distress” is literally “do not make your mouth great.” The pride of the heart in verse 3 has reached the mouth in verse 12, as it always does: “out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks” (Matthew 12:34). God tells Edom elsewhere, “With your mouth you have boasted against Me and multiplied your words against Me; I have heard them” (Ezekiel 35:13). The tongue that enlarges itself over another’s ruin reveals a heart that has forgotten its own need of mercy. And God is listening.

[Insight] Eight Lines Edom Crossed. Verse 11 describes a finished event; verses 12–14 switch to eight “do not” commands. Some read them as warnings against a repeat, others as the prophet reliving the day: “You should not have …” Either way, each “do not” names a line Edom crossed, and God remembers every one.

"You should not have entered the gate of My people"

In Hebrew this line, like the others in verses 12–14, is phrased as a direct command: "Do not enter." It is as if the LORD were standing at Jerusalem's gate on that terrible day, forbidding Edom to come through. A city's gate was its main entrance and the center of its public life, where people met, traded, and settled disputes. Once an enemy passed through it, the city lay open.

Two verses earlier, the LORD described the day when "foreigners entered his gates" and then charged Edom, "Even you were as one of them" (1:11). This verse shows what that meant. When the invaders, most likely the Babylonians, broke into Jerusalem, Edom did not stay on the other side of the wall. It went in through the gate right behind them.

The LORD calls the city "the gate of My people." Edom was not simply taking advantage of a weakened neighbor. The people it moved against belonged to God, and He took their mistreatment personally.

"In the day of their calamity."

The word "calamity" means disaster or ruin, the kind that crushes a people. It appears three times in this one verse, and each time the LORD presses the same point: Edom chose the very day Judah was falling. It did not wait for a fair fight or a time of strength. It came when its brother was helpless. Many interpreters also hear a play on words, since the Hebrew for "their calamity" sounds much like the name "Edom." Edom's character is tied to the disaster it exploited. Ezekiel likewise condemned Edom for striking Israel at the time of its calamity (Ezekiel 35:5), and Proverbs warns, "He who is glad at calamity will not go unpunished" (Proverbs 17:5).

"Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction"

Verse 12 already charged Edom with gazing on its brother's day of disaster. Now the gazing happens inside the city. The Hebrew carries the sense of "you too," placing Edom alongside the invaders as one more onlooker staring at Judah's suffering. This was not the look of someone heartbroken by what he saw. It was the look of someone enjoying the sight of a hated rival brought low.

"Nor laid hands on their substance"

Edom finally moved from watching to taking. "Substance" refers to wealth and possessions. In verse 11 the same Hebrew word describes what the strangers "carried captive" (there translated "forces"). The invaders hauled away Judah's wealth, and Edom reached for the same goods. It took part in the looting as a full partner in the plunder, while its brother's city was being destroyed around it.

[Christ & Life] “My People,” Even in the Day of Discipline. “Do not enter the gate of My people in the day of their calamity.” Jerusalem fell because God was disciplining real sin, and Obadiah never pretends otherwise; yet in that very hour God calls the sufferers “My people.” Zechariah would later say God was “a little angry” while the nations “helped forward the disaster” (Zechariah 1:15). Verse Explanation

Edom even waited at the crossroads to catch Judah's fleeing survivors and handed over those who remained to the enemy. Cutting off people's last hope of escape was the cruelest form of Edom's betrayal.

"You should not have stood at the crossroads"

This is the last of eight warnings in verses 12–14, each beginning with "you should not have." Together they trace how far Edom went. It began by standing aloof while foreigners attacked Jerusalem (1:11), moved on to gloating and boasting (1:12), and then entered the city to loot it (1:13). Here Edom takes the final step: it actively hunts down the people who were trying to get away.

The Hebrew word translated "crossroads" describes a place where a road divides, such as a fork or a narrow pass. When Babylon destroyed Jerusalem, people fled the city any way they could. Even King Zedekiah and his soldiers slipped out by night and ran toward the plain of the Jordan, only to be overtaken near Jericho (2 Kings 25:4–5). Edom knew the roads and passes of the region. Its men stationed themselves where fleeing people would have to pass, like hunters waiting along a game trail.

"To cut off those among them who escaped;"

"Those who escaped" are refugees who had survived the fall of the city and were running for their lives. They had already lost their homes and their capital. Now, at the very moment they thought they might live, they ran into their own relatives blocking the way.

The verb "cut off" is the same word God used for Edom's punishment: "you shall be cut off forever" (1:10), and "everyone from the mountains of Esau may be cut off by slaughter" (1:9). Edom cut off people who had nowhere left to go, and God declares that Edom itself will be cut off. The punishment matches the crime. The nation that closed the last way of escape for its brother would find no escape of its own.

"Nor should you have delivered up those among them who remained"

"Delivered up" means to hand someone over into the power of another, here to the enemy. "Those who remained" are the survivors who had not been killed in the attack. Edom captured them and turned them over to the Babylonians for death or slavery. Ezekiel describes the same guilt, saying Edom "shed the blood of the children of Israel by the power of the sword at the time of their calamity" (Ezekiel 35:5).

The same Hebrew verb appears in the Law of Moses: "You shall not give back to his master the slave who has escaped from his master to you" (Deuteronomy 23:15). God commanded His people not to hand over even a runaway slave who came to them for refuge. Edom did the opposite to its own brother nation, Israel. It handed over people who had every reason to expect shelter from family. Nearly two centuries earlier, Amos had already condemned Edom because "he pursued his brother with the sword, and cast off all pity" (Amos 1:11). At Jerusalem's fall, that old hostility showed itself at its worst.

"In the day of distress."

This is the final mention of the "day" that runs through verses 11–14: the day of Judah's calamity, destruction, and distress. "Distress" describes being hemmed in with no way out, which is exactly the situation of the refugees. Edom chose that moment, when its brother was most helpless, to strike.

[Application] The Cruelty of the Crossroads. Edom set up checkpoints along the escape routes to catch fleeing Judeans and hand them over to Babylon, or sell them into slavery. It positioned itself exactly where Jacob’s survivors were most vulnerable and exploited their lowest moment. Exploiting the vulnerability of an exhausted, fleeing soul is the lowest moral abyss a culture can reach: it blocks the path to mercy for someone running from disaster. We are warned never to use another believer’s season of discipline, failure, or distress to raise ourselves up. When a brother or sister stands at a crossroads of crisis, the carnal response is to capitalize on their weakness or pile on more judgment; the Christlike response is to offer refuge. Do we ever trap people in their past failures, cutting off their avenues of escape and repentance? Who in our lives is standing at a crossroads right now, and are we a barrier or an open road?

[Insight] The Receiver Became the Hander-Over. “Do not hand over their survivors in the day of distress.” Amos had already condemned Gaza and Tyre because they “delivered up the whole captivity to Edom” (Amos 1:6, 9); Edom had been the buyer of handed-over captives. Verse Explanation

The day of the LORD, when God steps in to judge, is coming near for all nations, not only Edom. Edom will receive exactly what it gave to others, showing that God's justice fits the crime rather than ignoring it.

"For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near;"

"The day of the LORD" was a familiar phrase among Israel's prophets. It describes a time when God Himself steps in to judge, bringing down the proud and setting right what has been wrong. Joel cried out, "Alas for the day! For the day of the LORD is at hand; it shall come as destruction from the Almighty" (Joel 1:15). Amos warned Israelites who looked forward to that day, assuming it would only mean trouble for their enemies, that it would be "darkness, and not light" for them as well (Amos 5:18). The prophets spoke of the day of the LORD falling on particular nations at particular times, and they also looked ahead to a final day when God would judge the whole world.

In the verses just before this one, the LORD repeatedly speaks of Judah's "day": the day of their captivity, their destruction, their distress, and three times "the day of their calamity" (verse 13). That was the day Edom took advantage of. It watched, gloated, plundered, and caught those trying to escape, as though Judah's worst day was Edom's opportunity. Now the LORD announces that He has a day of His own. Psalm 137:7 shows how that day of Jerusalem's fall stayed in Israel's memory: "Remember, O LORD, against the sons of Edom the day of Jerusalem, who said, 'Raze it, raze it, to its very foundation!'" Edom thought the day belonged to it, but the final day belongs to the LORD.

This day comes "upon all the nations," not on Edom alone. Edom's judgment is one example of a reckoning that reaches every people who set themselves against God. The word "near" means that this judgment was certain and already approaching. Edom could not count on having more time.

"As you have done, it shall be done to you;"

This is the principle that runs through the whole prophecy. God's judgment on Edom would match what Edom had done. Edom had stood by while strangers carried off Judah's wealth, and its own hidden treasures would be searched out (verse 6). It had turned against its brother, and its own allies would turn against it (verse 7). It had cut off Judah's survivors, and it would be cut off forever (verse 10). Ezekiel gives the same message about Edom: because it rejoiced when Israel's land became desolate, God would make Edom desolate too (Ezekiel 35:15).

Scripture shows this kind of justice elsewhere. When the Canaanite king Adoni-Bezek suffered what he had done to seventy other kings, he admitted, "As I have done, so God has repaid me" (Judges 1:7). Paul states the principle plainly: "Do not be deceived, God is not mocked; for whatever a man sows, that he will also reap" (Galatians 6:7). God's judgment is not random or excessive. It fits the wrong done. This does not mean that every hardship a person faces is payback for a specific sin. It means that God sees exactly what the proud and violent have done to others, and He does not let it go unanswered.

"Your reprisal shall return upon your own head."

The Hebrew word translated "reprisal" refers to the way someone has treated others, the harm a person has dealt out. Here that harm comes back on Edom like something thrown that circles back and strikes the one who threw it. The "head" stands for the person himself. David uses the same picture for the wicked man: "His trouble shall return upon his own head, and his violent dealing shall come down on his own crown" (Psalm 7:16).

The one who brings this repayment is God, not Judah. Edom's victims did not have to take revenge, because the LORD had promised to repay. The same truth stands behind Paul's instruction to believers: "Beloved, do not avenge yourselves, but rather give place to wrath; for it is written, 'Vengeance is Mine, I will repay,' says the Lord" (Romans 12:19).

[Foundational Truth] Their Day, Then His Day. “For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near.” Ten times in verses 11–14 the prophet spoke of Judah’s “day,” and every one was a day Edom treated as its own. Now comes a different day, with a different owner. “The day of the LORD” names God’s decisive intervention, whether in history or at its end. God has marked an appointment on heaven’s calendar that no nation can move. The injustices against Judah were not overlooked; they were part of a comprehensive reckoning that will reach every proud power. People may have their day over others, and the gloating may last a season, but days do not finally belong to the strongest. The last day belongs to the LORD.

[Foundational Truth] The Stone Thrown Upward. “As you have done, it shall be done to you; your reprisal shall return upon your own head.” Edom’s “dealing” is its own deed, coming back like a stone thrown straight up, like the mischief of Psalm 7:16 that “returns upon his own head.” This is the law of the harvest: “whatever a man sows, that he will also reap” (Galatians 6:7). God’s justice is perfectly symmetrical; the very methods Edom used to torment Israel become the means of its own undoing. Whatever we sow into the lives of others, cruelty, betrayal, theft, or mercy, returns to us, and divine justice makes sinners drink the exact poison they mixed for their brothers. That is a warning, and it is also a comfort: we do not have to settle every score ourselves, because God’s justice is exact (Romans 12:19).

"For as you drank on My holy mountain,"

"For" ties this verse to the warning just given: "the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near," the day when God steps in to judge, and "as you have done, it shall be done to you" (1:15). "My holy mountain" is Mount Zion, Jerusalem's temple hill. God calls it His own. Whatever happened there happened on ground that belonged to Him.

Throughout verses 2–15 the LORD speaks to Edom with a singular "you." Here the "you" is plural, so the verse can be read in two ways. It may still be speaking to Edom, along with those who joined in. If so, the drinking is a victory celebration. After gloating over Judah's ruin and plundering Jerusalem (1:12–13), they drank and celebrated on the very hill where God's temple stood. Their toast would be answered by a very different cup.

The plural may instead turn to God's own people in Judah. Then the drinking is the cup of God's judgment, which Jerusalem drank when the city fell. Isaiah speaks to Jerusalem this way: "You who have drunk at the hand of the LORD the cup of His fury" (Isaiah 51:17). On this reading, the point is that if God did not spare His own people, the nations will not escape. Jeremiah reasons the same way in his prophecy against Edom. If others who did not deserve the cup have had to drink it, Edom will certainly drink it too (Jeremiah 49:12). Either way, the verse moves from what happened on God's mountain to what will happen to every nation.

"So shall all the nations drink continually;"

The "as... so" pattern of verse 15 now reaches beyond Edom to all the nations. Edom's punishment is one part of a judgment that God will bring on the whole world. Throughout the Old Testament, judgment is pictured as a cup of wine that God Himself pours and hands to the guilty. The LORD told Jeremiah, "Take this wine cup of fury from My hand, and cause all the nations, to whom I send you, to drink it" (Jeremiah 25:15). The cup is in God's hand, and no nation chooses whether to drink it. "Continually" means the drinking does not stop. No one takes a sip and sets the cup aside.

"Yes, they shall drink, and swallow,"

"Swallow" means to gulp down. The nations will not merely taste God's judgment. They will drain the cup to the bottom. The repetition stresses that this will surely happen. Jeremiah describes the nations who drink this cup staggering like drunken men, overcome by the sword God sends among them (Jeremiah 25:16). Whatever the nations once drank in triumph, they will now drink in defeat.

"And they shall be as though they had never been."

The judgment ends with complete disappearance. Nations that once seemed powerful and permanent will lose their strength and standing so thoroughly that it will be as if they had never existed. This is a sobering word for Edom, which trusted in its mountain strongholds and believed no one could bring it down (1:3). The same holy mountain where the drinking took place will not end in ruin, though. God promises deliverance and holiness there (1:17).

[Foundational Truth] From the Party to the Cup. “For as you drank on My holy mountain, so shall all the nations drink continually.” The first drinking is most likely Edom and its allies carousing in the ruins of Jerusalem, holding drunken feasts on Zion. God turns the image: the second drinking is the cup of His wrath, the prophets’ familiar picture of judgment passed from nation to nation (Jeremiah 25:15–29; 49:12). Have we grown casual in places God calls holy, in worship, at the Lord’s Table, in how we speak of His things?

[Insight] As If They Had Never Been. “They shall drink, and swallow, and they shall be as though they had never been.” “Swallow” is the gulp of the throat, drinking in full draughts, and the result is not merely defeat but erasure, like the chaff in Daniel’s vision that the wind carried away “so that no trace of them was found” (Daniel 2:35). Nations that seemed permanent can be made to look as if they never were. You simply will not meet an Edomite today. Only one kingdom in this book is promised to last, and no nation builds it.

[Christ & Life] He Drank the Cup So We Could Drink the Other One. “All the nations shall drink continually” the cup of God’s wrath. In Gethsemane Jesus prayed over that very image, “O My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as You will” (Matthew 26:39), and then He drank it to the bottom. Because He swallowed the cup the nations deserved, His people are handed a different cup: “This cup is the new covenant in My blood” (1 Corinthians 11:25). There are only two cups in Obadiah’s world, and Jesus took the one that was ours. Every communion table is a place where the cup of wrath has already been emptied.

Verse Explanation

In contrast to the judgment on the nations, Mount Zion, the hill where Jerusalem and God's temple stood, will be a place of rescue and holiness. God's people will regain what belonged to them, showing that His purpose goes beyond punishing Edom to restoring His people.

"But on Mount Zion there shall be deliverance,"

Mount Zion was the hill in Jerusalem where God's temple stood. David captured "the stronghold of Zion" from the Jebusites and made it his capital (2 Samuel 5:6–7). Later the ark was brought there and the temple was built, and Zion became known as the place where God dwelt among His people. In time the name stood for Jerusalem as a whole and for God's presence with Israel.

"But" sets Zion against what comes just before. All the nations will drink judgment "and they shall be as though they had never been" (1:16), but on this mountain some will be rescued. The same holy mountain that had seen Jerusalem's ruin and humiliation will become the place where God saves.

The Hebrew word for "deliverance" means escape, and it can also refer to the people who escape. It comes from the same root as "those among them who escaped" in verse 14, the fugitives Edom waited for at the crossroads and cut off. Edom tried to make sure no one from Judah got away, but God promises that on Zion there will be survivors whom no enemy can cut off. Joel gives the same promise: "For in Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there shall be deliverance, as the LORD has said, among the remnant whom the LORD calls" (Joel 2:32).

"And there shall be holiness;"

To be holy is to be set apart for God and belong to Him. The phrase can also be translated "it shall be holy," meaning Zion itself. Jerusalem had been defiled. Foreigners had entered its gates and cast lots for it (1:11), and Edom had marched in to plunder it (1:13). God promises that His mountain will again be wholly His, no longer trampled by the nations. Joel says the same: "Then Jerusalem shall be holy, and no aliens shall ever pass through her again" (Joel 3:17).

This holiness reaches the people as well as the place. Isaiah speaks of the survivors in Jerusalem: "he who is left in Zion and remains in Jerusalem will be called holy" (Isaiah 4:3). God does not simply rescue His people and leave them as they were. Those He delivers become a people set apart for Him.

"The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions."

"The house of Jacob" means the people of Israel, the brother nation Edom had wronged (1:10). The Hebrew verb "possess" is the word often used for Israel taking possession of the Promised Land, the inheritance God had promised to Abraham and his descendants (Genesis 17:8). That land had been lost. Invaders had carried off Judah's wealth and its people, and Edom moved into the territory of southern Judah. Ezekiel records Edom's boast about Israel and Judah: "These two nations and these two countries shall be mine, and we will possess them" (Ezekiel 35:10).

God reverses that. What others seized will return to its rightful owners, and Jacob's descendants will again hold the inheritance God gave them. The following verses name the regions in every direction that God's people will occupy, including the mountains of Esau (1:19–20). God's purpose is not only to bring Edom down but to bring His own people home to the land He promised them.

Study Notes

The NKJV follows the standard Hebrew text, which reads "shall possess their possessions." In other words, Israel will recover its own inheritance. Ancient Hebrew was written without vowels. With different vowels, the same consonants can be read as "shall possess those who dispossessed them." That is how the Septuagint understood the line, and some modern translations follow it. On that reading, Israel takes over the nations that had taken its land.

The two readings lead to much the same place. Verses 19–20 describe God's people taking possession of land that others had seized, including Edom's own mountains. Either way, God reverses Edom's boast that it would take its brother's land (Ezekiel 35:10).

Spirit & Life

[Insight] The Hinge of the Book. “But on Mount Zion there shall be deliverance.” That “But” turns the whole prophecy, following the denunciations the way Israel’s path through the sea followed Pharaoh’s overthrow. Those who escape share a root with the fugitives Edom cut off at the crossroads (v. 14). Edom shut the road of escape; God makes His own mountain the place of escape. Joel uses the same phrase, “on Mount Zion … there shall be deliverance” (Joel 2:32), the very verse Peter preached at Pentecost: “whoever calls on the name of the Lord shall be saved” (Acts 2:21). The way out that Edom blocked, God opened in Jerusalem.

[Christ & Life] Mount Zion, the Place of Deliverance. Against Edom’s rocky heights stands Zion, God’s chosen mountain. While Mount Esau is wiped clean into desolation, Mount Zion becomes the center of refuge, holiness, and the restoration of covenant inheritance. In the New Covenant, “you have come to Mount Zion … the heavenly Jerusalem” (Hebrews 12:22). It is our final place of refuge, holiness, and restored inheritance in Christ.

[Christ & Life] Deliverance Means Holiness and Possession. “And it shall be holy, and the house of Jacob shall possess their possessions.” True deliverance in the kingdom is never mere survival or evacuation from trouble; it is bound up with holiness and restoration. The escape on Mount Zion turns refugees into holy vessels who reclaim their inheritance. Christ does not only rescue us from the consequences of sin; He brings us to His holy hill so we can possess our possessions, stepping fully into the authority, peace, and identity the enemy tried to steal.

[Application] Possess Your Possessions. “Possess” is the conquest verb of Joshua, used three times in verses 17–20. Picture an Israelite who owns terraces and vineyards and never tastes a grape, or a mill owner who refuses to use the power already supplied to his mill. All things are ours, and yet we live as if nothing were ours. Christ is like a treasure chest that is wholly ours but seldom opened. God has already blessed His people “with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ” (Ephesians 1:3); the inheritance is not in question, only the possessing. Faith is the hand that takes hold of what is already in our name. What has God already given us in Christ that we have not yet taken hold of?

[Insight] Holy Means Inviolable. “It shall be holy”: a sanctuary the nations will no longer dare to trample (compare Joel 3:17). Some translate the last clause, “will dispossess those who dispossessed them,” which fits verses 19–20. The place Edom’s allies partied on becomes a place no one can defile.

"The house of Jacob shall be a fire, And the house of Joseph a flame;"

"The house of Jacob" means the people of Israel, the descendants of Esau's twin brother. "The house of Joseph" points especially to the northern tribes. Joseph's sons Ephraim and Manasseh became the leading tribes of the northern kingdom, which Assyria had carried into exile in 722 BC, long before Judah fell to Babylon. Naming both houses together pictures the whole family of Israel, north and south, restored and standing together again. The prophets looked forward to this reunion, as when Ezekiel joined two sticks, one for Judah and one for Joseph, into one in his hand (Ezekiel 37:15–22).

Israel is pictured as fire, but the power behind that fire belongs to God. Isaiah uses the same kind of picture for God Himself: "So the Light of Israel will be for a fire, And his Holy One for a flame; It will burn and devour His thorns and his briers in one day" (Isaiah 10:17). The people Edom thought it could trample become the means through which God carries out His judgment.

There is a reversal here. Edom had done violence "against your brother Jacob" (1:10) while Jerusalem burned. Now Jacob, the brother Edom wronged, becomes the fire that consumes the house of Esau. Edom is repaid in the same terms as its own conduct.

"But the house of Esau shall be stubble;"

Stubble is the dry stalks left in a field after the harvest. It has no strength and catches fire instantly, so a field of stubble can burn in moments. Edom had trusted in its rugged mountain homeland and its strength, but before God it is nothing more than dry straw. Malachi uses the same image for the day of judgment: "all the proud, yes, all who do wickedly will be stubble. And the day which is coming shall burn them up" (Malachi 4:1).

"They shall kindle them and devour them,"

Fire set to stubble does not stop until everything is gone. Judgment on Edom will not be partial or temporary, and nothing will be left to grow back.

**"And no survivor shall remain of the house of Esau,”"**

The word translated "survivor" is the same word used in verse 14 for those of Judah who "remained" after Jerusalem's fall. Edom had stood at the crossroads to catch those survivors and hand them over to the enemy. Now Edom itself will have no survivors. The contrast with the verse just before is just as sharp: "on Mount Zion there shall be deliverance" (1:17). God's people will have escapees, but Esau's house will have none.

This describes Edom's end as a nation, not the death of every person descended from Esau. History shows how that happened. Edom's land was already laid waste by Malachi's day (Malachi 1:3–4), and Arab peoples later took over its mountain homeland. Many Edomites moved into southern Judah, an area later called Idumea. In the second century BC the Jewish ruler John Hyrcanus conquered them and forced them to adopt Jewish customs, so they were absorbed into the Jewish people. Herod the Great came from this Idumean background. After the Romans destroyed Jerusalem in AD 70, the Idumeans disappear from history. Today no nation of Edom exists. Because Obadiah ties Edom's fall to the day of the LORD upon all nations (1:15), many interpreters also see a fuller fulfillment still to come.

"For the LORD has spoken."

Obadiah began with "Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom" (1:1), and now he closes the judgment the same way. Edom trusted its high cliffs, its wisdom, and its warriors. None of that could stand against what God had said. The outcome was certain because the LORD Himself had declared it, and Edom's disappearance from history shows that His word did not fail.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] Fire, Flame, and Stubble. “The house of Jacob shall be a fire, and the house of Joseph a flame; but the house of Esau shall be stubble.” The house of Joseph, the northern tribes exiled in 722 B.C., stands beside Judah here: a reunited Israel (Ezekiel 37:16–22) becomes God’s instrument. Stubble does not resist fire; it simply disappears (Malachi 4:1). Isaiah says it is God Himself who “will be for a fire, and His Holy One for a flame” (Isaiah 10:17), so the fire in Israel is God’s own fire carried in His people. The historically oppressed become an unquenchable blaze, while the proud persecutors are exposed as dry, hollow stubble. The old missionary David Brainerd prayed to be a flame of fire in God’s cause. The question is not whether we will be near fire, but whether we will be the flame or the stubble.

[Christ & Life] The Consuming Unity of the Spirit. Jacob represents the chosen people; Joseph represents the one who suffered and was exalted to save his brothers. Together, as fire and flame, they picture the unified, consuming life of the Spirit. Esau, the flesh, is reduced to stubble. When the Spirit encounters the flesh, the flesh has no substance to withstand it. It has volume, visible presence, and historical weight, but before the pure, blazing reality of Christ’s life it is utterly combustible. It cannot fight the fire; it can only be consumed.

[Foundational Truth] No Survivor for the House That Handed Over Survivors. “There shall be no survivor of the house of Esau.” “Survivor” is the same word as the “survivors” Edom handed over at the crossroads (v. 14). Edom took away Judah’s survivors; Edom will have no survivor. The vocabulary of the crime has become the vocabulary of the sentence, sealed with “for the LORD has spoken.” History bore it out; after A.D. 70 the Idumeans disappear as a people. (The Greek Old Testament says not even a “fire-bearer” would be left, the torch-bearer who led an army and was customarily spared even in defeat.) God does not need to invent consequences; He lets our own deeds come home.

[Christ & Life] A Remnant of Edom at the King’s Feet. Obadiah leaves “no survivor of the house of Esau,” and as a nation Edom did vanish. Yet Amos had promised that David’s restored house would “possess the remnant of Edom, and all the Gentiles who are called by My name” (Amos 9:11–12), and James quoted that passage at the Jerusalem council to show that God was taking from the Gentiles “a people for His name” (Acts 15:14–17). Already in Jesus’ ministry crowds came to Him from Idumea (Mark 3:8). The judgment on Edom’s pride never closed the door of grace to Edom’s people; the King of Zion opened it. No bloodline is too far gone for the Deliverer who comes out of Zion (Romans 11:26).

"The South shall possess the mountains of Esau,"

"The South" is the Negev, the dry southern region of Judah that bordered Edom. Its people lived closest to Edom and suffered from its hostility. Now they will take over Edom's rugged mountain homeland, the heights Edom thought no one could reach. The word "possess" is repeated throughout this verse, and it also appears in verse 17: "The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions." It is the word used when Israel entered Canaan under Joshua to take possession of the land God was giving them. The promise describes God giving His people their land once more.

The promise also reverses Edom's ambitions. Ezekiel records that Edom had said of Israel and Judah, "These two nations and these two countries shall be mine, and we will possess them" (Ezekiel 35:10). Edom wanted to take its brother's land. Instead, its brother would hold Edom's mountains. Amos gave the same promise when he spoke of the day God would restore David's fallen house, "that they may possess the remnant of Edom" (Amos 9:12).

"And the Lowland shall possess Philistia."

"The Lowland" is the Shephelah, the low hills between Judah's central highlands and the Mediterranean coast. To the west lay Philistia, the coastal plain of the Philistines. They had been Israel's enemies since the days of the judges, Saul, and David. The people of the foothills would spread down to the coast and claim it.

"They shall possess the fields of Ephraim And the fields of Samaria."

Ephraim was the leading tribe of the northern kingdom of Israel, and Samaria was its capital. Assyria conquered that kingdom and carried its people into exile, then settled foreigners in their towns (2 Kings 17:6, 24). The verse does not say exactly who "they" are, but it is most naturally the people of Judah. God's restoration would not stop at Judah's own borders. The northern heartland, long lost to foreign rule, would belong to God's people again. The two kingdoms that had divided after Solomon would no longer be separated.

"Benjamin shall possess Gilead."

Gilead was the hill country east of the Jordan River. It was originally given to the tribes of Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh (Joshua 13:8–13), and it was among the first regions lost to Assyria. Benjamin was a small tribe just north of Jerusalem that stayed with Judah when the kingdom split (1 Kings 12:21). Benjamin had a small inheritance, but now it would reach across the Jordan into Gilead.

The verse moves from south to west, then north, then east. The land God first gave to Israel would be recovered on every side, including regions long held by enemies and foreigners. These are real places with real borders, and the promise concerns God restoring His people to them. Obadiah does not say when this will be completely fulfilled. His point is that God's people will not remain scattered and robbed, because He will return to them what He gave them.

Spirit & Life

[Discovery] The Lowland Takes the Heights. “The South shall possess the mountains of Esau, and the lowland shall possess Philistia.” The people of the Negev, the dry south, the lowest and poorest of Judah’s land, inherit the mountain fortress that boasted no one could climb it; the foothills take the plain of the old enemy. The map then turns north to Ephraim and Samaria and east to Gilead: south, west, north, and east, the four directions promised to Jacob at Bethel, “you shall spread abroad to the west and the east, to the north and the south” (Genesis 28:14). God’s covenant boundaries cannot be cancelled by foreign occupation; the lost inheritance is returned down to the exact boundary stone. The borders here exceed anything the returning exiles held, which is why many look for their fulfillment in Messiah’s kingdom. God’s promises come with place names. The God who keeps His word to Jacob down to the Negev and Gilead will not forget the smallest promise He has made to us.

Verse Explanation

Israelites taken captive will return and possess land as far north as Zarephath, and exiles from Jerusalem living in Sepharad, a distant location, will possess the cities in the south. God promises to gather His scattered people from far away and bring them home.

"And the captives of this host of the children of Israel"

"Host" means a company or band of people. Here it refers to the Israelites who had been carried away from their land. Later in the verse, Obadiah speaks separately of "the captives of Jerusalem," so many interpreters see two groups of exiles here. The first would be the people of the northern kingdom of Israel, whom Assyria deported long before (2 Kings 17:6). The second would be the people of Judah, whose capital was Jerusalem. However long they had been gone, God had not forgotten them. People who had lost everything and lived under foreign rule are the ones He promises to bring back.

"Shall possess the land of the Canaanites As far as Zarephath."

"Possess" is the same word used for Jacob taking possession of what belonged to him (verse 17). It is also the word used when Israel first entered the promised land under Joshua. These returning captives will not live as guests in someone else's territory. They will settle and own the land.

"The land of the Canaanites" here means the coast north of Israel, where the Phoenician cities of Tyre and Sidon stood. Genesis lists Sidon as the firstborn son of Canaan (Genesis 10:15). Zarephath was a small town on that coast between Tyre and Sidon. It is best known as the place where Elijah stayed with a widow during a famine and raised her son from the dead (1 Kings 17:9–24). By naming Zarephath, God shows that His people's land will reach far to the north, beyond the territories listed in verse 19.

"The captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad"

These are people of Judah who had been taken from Jerusalem, most likely after the Babylonian destruction of the city. No one today knows for certain where Sepharad was. What matters is that it was far from home. Even exiles living in a distant land will be brought back. Long before, Moses had promised that if Israel were scattered among the nations and later turned back to the LORD, He would gather them again, even from the farthest places (Deuteronomy 30:1–5). Obadiah shows God keeping that promise. Distance does not put any of His people out of His reach.

"Shall possess the cities of the South."

"The South" is the Negev, the dry region in the southern part of Judah that bordered Edom. After Jerusalem fell, Edomites moved into this area and took over land that had belonged to Judah. The promise is that Jerusalem's exiles will return and possess these very cities. Edom had helped hand over Judah's fleeing survivors (verse 14), but those taken captive will come home and take back what was theirs. A return began after Babylon fell, when exiles went back to Judah under the decree of Cyrus (Ezra 1:1–4). Still, the promise here reaches farther than that early return. It points toward God fully restoring His people in their own land, as verse 21 goes on to describe.

Study Notes

The location of Sepharad is uncertain, but there are three main proposals. Inscriptions show that Sardis, a city in western Asia Minor (modern Turkey), was called by a closely matching name. Persian records use a similar name for the surrounding region. Most scholars favor this identification. Others suggest a region in Media, in the east, where Israelites had been resettled. A long Jewish tradition, going back to an ancient Aramaic translation of the prophets, identified Sepharad with Spain. That is why Jews of Spanish descent are called Sephardic Jews. Whichever place is meant, it was far from Jerusalem.

The first half of the verse is also hard to translate. The Hebrew has no verb there, so the NKJV supplies "shall possess" from the second half. Another possible reading is "the exiles of Israel who are among the Canaanites as far as Zarephath," meaning captives living in Phoenicia. That reading fits Amos's charge that Tyre and Gaza had sold whole populations of captives into slavery, handing them over to Edom (Amos 1:6, 9). Some scholars have suggested that "this host" was originally the name Halah, one of the places in Assyria where northern Israelites were deported (2 Kings 17:6). No Hebrew manuscript reads that way, though.

[Discovery] As Far as the Refining Place. “The captives … shall possess the land of the Canaanites as far as Zarephath.” Zarephath, on the Phoenician coast between Tyre and Sidon, takes its name from a root meaning “to smelt, to refine.” It is the town where a starving Gentile widow fed Elijah from her last handful of flour (1 Kings 17:9–16), the story Jesus preached at Nazareth (Luke 4:25–26). God’s map of restoration runs through places of famine and refining, and His grace runs past every border we would draw. (Sepharad, likely Sardis in Asia Minor, later gave the Sephardic Jews their name.) Whatever the places, no exile is too far for God to bring home.

"Then saviors shall come to Mount Zion"

The Hebrew word translated "saviors" means deliverers, people God uses to rescue His people from their enemies. It recalls the time of the judges, when Israel cried out under oppression and "the LORD raised up a deliverer for the children of Israel" (Judges 3:9). Men like Othniel, Ehud, and Gideon were not saviors in their own strength. God raised them up, gave them victory, and through them set His people free. Obadiah promises that God will do this again. After the scattering and humiliation described earlier in the prophecy, He will raise up deliverers for His people.

They come to Mount Zion, Jerusalem's temple hill, which God has already promised will be a place of deliverance and holiness (1:17). The Hebrew verb behind "come" literally means to go up. Edom had climbed high in its pride and would be brought down, but these deliverers go up to God's mountain.

"To judge the mountains of Esau,"

The Hebrew word for "judge" means more than issuing a verdict. It also means to govern and to set things right, which is why Israel's deliverers in the book of Judges were called judges. They rescued the people and then led them. Here the deliverers rule over Edom's territory and carry out God's justice there.

The verse sets two mountains side by side. Edom lived in a rugged, lofty homeland and boasted, "Who will bring me down to the ground?" (1:3). Mount Zion, by comparison, was a modest hill. Yet in the end the mountains of Esau are governed from Mount Zion. Edom's heights could not protect it, and the city Edom mocked and plundered becomes the seat of authority over it. This also echoes what God told Rebekah before her twins were born: "the older shall serve the younger" (Genesis 25:23). Esau's descendants had refused to treat Jacob's people as brothers, and now they come under the rule God established on Zion. Obadiah does not say exactly when or how this would be fulfilled, only that it would surely happen.

"And the kingdom shall be the LORD’s."

This is the last line of the book, and it explains everything before it. Edom thought its strength and wisdom made it secure. Babylon seemed unstoppable when it destroyed Jerusalem. But neither empire has the final word. The same God who stirred the nations against Edom (1:1) and whose day of judgment reaches all nations (1:15) is the true King over them all. Even the deliverers on Mount Zion do not rule for themselves. They serve the LORD's kingdom. The psalmist says it the same way: "For the kingdom is the LORD's, and He rules over the nations" (Psalm 22:28).

The deliverers in this verse are human leaders like those God raised up in Israel's past, but the promise reaches beyond them. Each deliverer God gave His people was limited and temporary. Jesus is the Savior and King in whom God's rule over the whole earth reaches its fulfillment. When the seventh trumpet sounds in Revelation, heaven declares, "The kingdoms of this world have become the kingdoms of our Lord and of His Christ, and He shall reign forever and ever!" (Revelation 11:15). A short prophecy about the judgment of one small nation ends with the truth that the whole world belongs to the LORD.

Study Notes

The NKJV's "saviors" follows the standard Hebrew text, which uses an active form meaning "those who deliver." With different vowels, the same consonants can mean "those who have been delivered." The Septuagint read it that way, and some modern translations follow it: "those who have been saved shall go up to Mount Zion."

The difference matters. On the reading the NKJV follows, God raises up leaders who rescue and govern, like the deliverers in the book of Judges. On the other reading, the rescued people themselves go up to Zion, which would echo the survivors of verse 17. The standard Hebrew reading has good support and fits the following phrase, "to judge the mountains of Esau," because judging and governing is what deliverers do. Either way, the deliverance comes from God, and the final line gives the kingdom to the LORD alone.

How has God’s deliverance made us an instrument of deliverance, and to whom is He sending us now?

[Christ & Life] The True and Greater Deliverer on Mount Zion. The “saviors” on Mount Zion pointed historically to leaders like Zerubbabel and Nehemiah and the Maccabean deliverers. But the verse finds its true fulfillment in Jesus Christ, the supreme Savior, who went up to Jerusalem, conquered sin and death on Calvary’s hill, and now reigns from the heavenly Jerusalem.

[Insight] Who Are the Deliverers?. Interpreters have heard three answers: the leaders of the return and the Maccabean era; the apostles and ministers of the gospel; and redeemed Israel with the saints reigning with Messiah, which fits the land promises of verses 19–20. And the deliverers “judge” the mountain of Esau in three ways: Edom has no deliverers of its own, the deliverers rule its territory, and they sit in judgment on its sins.

[Foundational Truth] And the Kingdom Shall Be the LORD’s. The last words of the book are the last word of history: “and the kingdom shall be the LORD’s.” In Hebrew it is a short, majestic declaration. Not Edom’s kingdom, and not finally even Israel’s, but His. The line matches Psalm 22:28, “the kingdom is the LORD’s,” and Zechariah 14:9, “the LORD shall be King over all the earth,” and Jewish worship still recites it at the New Year among the texts proclaiming God’s kingship. Human kings fail and fleshly nations fall, but every dynasty, empire, and rogue nation will finally give way to the uncontested, eternal reign of Jesus Christ. People are in His kingdom now, but not all willingly; the day is coming when His reign will be welcomed everywhere. Every “Your kingdom come” ever prayed is traveling toward this sentence. Where are we still building our own small kingdom, and what would it look like to declare over our plans, resources, and future, “the kingdom shall be the LORD’s”?
