Chapter Study

Obadiah 1

Verse-by-Verse
Obadiah 1:1

NKJV

Obadiah 1:1 The vision of Obadiah. Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom (We have heard a report from the LORD, And a messenger has been sent among the nations, saying, "Arise, and let us rise up against her for battle"): (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:1 This is the vision that the Sovereign LORD revealed to Obadiah concerning the land of Edom. Edom’s Judgment Announced We have heard a message from the LORDthat an ambassador was sent to the nations to say,“Get ready, everyone!Let’s assemble our armies and attack Edom!” (NLT)

Main Idea

God announces judgment against Edom and summons the nations to act against it. Though the nations will carry out the judgment, the LORD is directing events and holding Edom accountable.

Verse Explanation

“The vision of Obadiah.”

“Vision” identifies the message as revelation from God rather than Obadiah's own political analysis or opinion. The Hebrew word can refer broadly to prophetic revelation and does not necessarily mean everything was received through a visual experience. Little is known about Obadiah himself. His name means “servant of the LORD” or “worshiper of the LORD,” but Scripture provides no further information that allows us to identify him with certainty.

“Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom”

The expression “Thus says the Lord GOD” establishes the authority of the prophecy. Obadiah is the messenger, but the message comes from God.

Edom was the nation descended from Esau, Jacob's brother (Genesis 36:1, 8–9). Edom and Israel were therefore not merely neighboring nations but related peoples. This relationship becomes significant as the prophecy exposes Edom's pride and mistreatment of Judah during Jerusalem's calamity.

“We have heard a report from the LORD”

The plural “we” is not explicitly identified. It may include Obadiah and others who received the announcement. The important point is that the report comes “from the LORD.” Edom's coming judgment is not based on political speculation. God has spoken.

“And a messenger has been sent among the nations”

A messenger has been sent to summon the surrounding nations against Edom. Although the messenger is not identified, the summons is presented as part of God's judgment. The nations may act according to their own ambitions, but God remains sovereign over the events through which His purposes are accomplished.

“Arise, and let us rise up against her for battle”

The verse concludes with a call for military action against Edom. This introduces the conflict developed in the following verses. Edom considers itself secure because of its mountainous territory and natural defenses, but the LORD will show that no position is beyond His reach.

Study Notes

Which Obadiah? Obadiah was a common name in the Old Testament. Later Jewish tradition identified the prophet with Ahab's steward who hid the LORD's prophets (1 Kings 18:3–16), but nothing in the book supports this, and the date that best fits verses 10–14 makes it unlikely.

“Lord GOD.” The Hebrew is Adonai Yahweh. When the divine name Yahweh follows Adonai (“Lord”), the NKJV prints it “GOD” in small capitals rather than repeating “Lord.” The title joins God's authority as Master with His covenant name.

Obadiah and Jeremiah 49. Verses 1–9 closely parallel Jeremiah 49:7–22, especially Jeremiah 49:9–10 and 14–16. The relationship between the two passages is uncertain. Both prophets may draw on an earlier oracle, or one may draw on the other. The parallel places Obadiah within a substantial prophetic witness concerning Edom (see also Isaiah 34:5–17; Ezekiel 35; Amos 1:11–12).

Spirit & Life

Obadiah is known only by his name and his message. His name means “servant of the LORD,” and he serves by passing on what he has heard. As he introduces the message concerning Edom, he says, “We have heard a report from the LORD,” counting himself among those who must receive it. Anyone who speaks for God begins in the same place, as a listener. The word has to reach us before it can be carried through us.

The nations rise against Edom for their own reasons, yet the summons comes from the LORD. For Judah, still reeling from Jerusalem's fall, this was assurance that the nations were not beyond God's control. God's people can read the news of their own day with the same confidence: rulers make their plans, but “the king's heart is in the hand of the LORD” (Proverbs 21:1).

Obadiah 1:2

NKJV

Obadiah 1:2 "Behold, I will make you small among the nations; You shall be greatly despised. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:2 The LORD says to Edom,“I will cut you down to size among the nations;you will be greatly despised. (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD declares that He will humble Edom, reducing its standing among the nations and bringing contempt upon a people who had become proud of their strength and security.

Verse Explanation

Having summoned the nations against Edom in verse 1, the LORD now announces what He will accomplish through their actions. Edom will be humbled, and the following verse will expose the pride behind its false confidence.

“Behold”

The LORD calls attention to a decisive reversal in Edom's future. The nation considers itself secure, but God is about to bring it low.

“I will make you small among the nations”

God Himself will bring about Edom's humiliation, even though other nations will carry out the military action. Since Edom was already a relatively small nation, “small” refers to its diminished power, influence, and standing rather than merely its geographical size.

“You shall be greatly despised”

Edom will lose not only its standing but also the respect of other nations. The two declarations describe different aspects of the same judgment: Edom will be reduced in importance and regarded with contempt.

This judgment also corresponds to Edom's treatment of Judah. Obadiah later charges that Edom looked on its brother's suffering with contempt (v. 12), and Edom will itself become despised. Verse 15 makes this principle explicit: “As you have done, it shall be done to you.”

Study Notes

“I will make you small.” The Hebrew verb is in the perfect tense, literally “I have made you small.” Prophets often use this form for a future event God has determined, speaking of it as though it were already done.

“Despised.” The same Hebrew verb (bazah) describes Esau when he “despised his birthright” (Genesis 25:34). The verbal link is real. Obadiah does not draw attention to it, however, so it should not be pressed as the point of the verse.

Spirit & Life

Edom was a small nation that saw itself as untouchable. God's answer is simple: “I will make you small.” For Edom, being made small was judgment. For those who belong to God, His humbling is a work of mercy. When He brings our pride down to its true size, it can feel like loss, but it is often the way He brings us to see Him clearly. “Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and He will lift you up” (James 4:10).

The word for “despised” also describes Esau when he “despised his birthright” (Genesis 25:34). Edom is judged for its own pride, not for its ancestor's choice, yet the echo is sobering. Esau treated as cheap what God had given, and the same word now describes the nation that came from him. What we treat lightly in God's gifts-His Word, His presence, the inheritance He offers-we hold lightly at our own cost.

Obadiah 1:3

NKJV

Obadiah 1:3 The pride of your heart has deceived you, You who dwell in the clefts of the rock, Whose habitation is high; You who say in your heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?' (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:3 You have been deceived by your own pridebecause you live in a rock fortressand make your home high in the mountains.‘Who can ever reach us way up here?’you ask boastfully. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom's pride has deceived it into believing that its natural defenses make it secure. Its greatest weakness is not its military position but the false confidence that has blinded it to God's authority.

Verse Explanation

Having announced in verse 2 that He will make Edom small among the nations, the LORD now exposes the reason for its coming humiliation: pride. Edom trusted in its mountainous homeland and seemingly inaccessible strongholds. These were genuine advantages, but pride had convinced the nation that it was beyond the reach of its enemies.

“The pride of your heart has deceived you”

Edom's fundamental problem was an inward attitude of arrogance and self-sufficiency. In Scripture, the “heart” includes a person's thoughts, desires, and decisions. Pride had distorted Edom's understanding of itself and its circumstances.

The word “deceived” is especially important. Edom had not simply deceived others; its own pride had deceived it. The nation believed its defenses guaranteed security when they could not protect it from God's judgment. The same Hebrew verb appears in verse 7, where Edom's trusted allies will deceive it.

“You who dwell in the clefts of the rock, Whose habitation is high”

The LORD identifies the source of Edom's false confidence. The Edomites occupied the mountainous region southeast of the Dead Sea, where steep cliffs, narrow passages, and elevated settlements provided considerable protection against invading armies.

These geographical advantages were real, but Edom had placed too much confidence in them. Its elevated position also prepares for the LORD's response in verse 4, where He declares that even if Edom rises as high as an eagle and sets its nest among the stars, He will bring it down. Edom's defenses might place it beyond the reach of some armies, but not beyond the reach of God.

“You who say in your heart, ‘Who will bring me down to the ground?’”

The LORD now reveals the thinking behind Edom's pride. The question is rhetorical, expressing the nation's conviction that no enemy could defeat it. Edom had evaluated its security according to its military advantages without recognizing the God who ruled over the nations.

The contrast across verses 2–4 is significant. In verse 2, God declares, “I will make you small.” In verse 3, Edom asks, “Who will bring me down to the ground?” In verse 4, the LORD answers, “From there I will bring you down.” Edom believed no one could bring it down, but God had already announced its humiliation.

Study Notes

The Clefts of the Rock and Sela. The Hebrew word sela means “rock” or “crag” and is also used as a place-name elsewhere in Scripture (2 Kings 14:7). Some interpreters understand Obadiah's reference as an allusion to the Edomite stronghold of Sela, while others take it as a description of the rocky terrain in which the Edomites lived. The precise location of ancient Sela remains uncertain, and it should not automatically be identified with Petra. Petra's rugged landscape illustrates Edom's natural defenses, but most of its famous architecture belongs to the later Nabataean period.

“Pride.” The Hebrew word (zadon) describes arrogance that oversteps its proper place. Proverbs uses the same word when it warns that pride brings shame and strife (Proverbs 11:2; 13:10).

Spirit & Life

Pride does not announce itself. It works from the inside, and its first victim is the one who holds it. Edom was not lying to others about its strength. It was lying to itself about what its strength could do. That is why pride is so hard to see in ourselves: a deceived person does not feel deceived; he feels secure. The only protection is to ask God to search the heart (Psalm 139:23–24) and to welcome the people He sends to tell us the truth.

Edom's ancestor gives a picture of the same heart. Esau traded his birthright for a meal because the hunger in front of him felt more real than the inheritance God had promised (Genesis 25:29–34). Scripture holds him up as a warning (Hebrews 12:16–17). In this sense Esau pictures the flesh: the part of us that wants security and satisfaction now, on its own terms, without depending on God. The flesh always asks Edom's question, “Who will bring me down?” It trusts what it can see and build. Believers still feel this pull, but they are not ruled by it. The Spirit leads us to find our security in God rather than in the rock we have climbed (Galatians 5:16–17).

Obadiah 1:4

NKJV

Obadiah 1:4 Though you ascend as high as the eagle, And though you set your nest among the stars, From there I will bring you down," says the LORD. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:4 But even if you soar as high as eaglesand build your nest among the stars,I will bring you crashing down,”says the LORD. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom trusts in the height of its home, but the LORD directly answers its proud confidence: “From there I will bring you down.”

Verse Explanation

“Though you ascend as high as the eagle”

The bird named here was known for nesting on high, inaccessible cliffs (Job 39:27–28), the kind of terrain Edom lived among. The LORD allows, for the sake of argument, that Edom might rise as high as the bird that nested where no one could follow.

“And though you set your nest among the stars”

The picture now goes higher than any bird could fly. “Among the stars” is deliberate exaggeration for the highest place imaginable. A nest is a home built for safety, and Edom's confidence rested on the height of its home. Scripture uses similar language for those who exalt themselves against God (Isaiah 14:13–15).

“From there I will bring you down,” says the LORD.

“There” is wherever Edom might climb, even to the heavens (Amos 9:2; Psalm 139:8). The LORD answers Edom's question from verse 3 with the same verb. “Says the LORD” marks the statement as His own solemn declaration and closes the first part of the announcement (vv. 2–4). The prophecy then turns to how completely Edom will be stripped (vv. 5–9).

Study Notes

Eagle or vulture. The Hebrew nesher is usually translated “eagle,” but it can also refer to the griffon vulture, a large bird that nests on high cliffs in the rocky regions of the Near East. Either way, the point is height and inaccessibility.

Spirit & Life

In its pride, Edom pictured itself rising as high as the eagle by its own strength. Israel's story began differently. At Sinai the LORD reminded them, “I bore you on eagles' wings and brought you to Myself” (Exodus 19:4). One nation lifted itself up in its own eyes and set its nest among the stars. The other was carried, and carried toward God.

The difference is not height but who does the lifting and where it leads. God does not oppose His people rising. He promises that “those who wait on the LORD … shall mount up with wings like eagles” (Isaiah 40:31). What He brings down is the height we seize for ourselves. The height He gives is always a height closer to Him.

Obadiah 1:5

NKJV

Obadiah 1:5 "If thieves had come to you, If robbers by night"” Oh, how you will be cut off!"” Would they not have stolen till they had enough? If grape-gatherers had come to you, Would they not have left some gleanings? (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:5 “If thieves came at night and robbed you(what a disaster awaits you!),they would not take everything.Those who harvest grapesalways leave a few for the poor.But your enemies will wipe you out completely! (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom faces devastation more thorough than ordinary theft or harvesting. Thieves take what they want, and grape-gatherers leave gleanings, but Edom's coming judgment will be far more complete.

Verse Explanation

The LORD now describes the extent of Edom's ruin by comparing it with two ordinary kinds of loss.

“If thieves had come to you, If robbers by night”

Thieves who break in at night usually take what they can find and carry, and then they leave.

“Oh, how you will be cut off!”

The prophet interrupts his own comparison with an exclamation. Before he finishes the picture, he announces the outcome: Edom will be destroyed. The interruption gives the verse its urgency.

“Would they not have stolen till they had enough?”

Even thieves ordinarily stop once they have what they want. Their victims suffer real loss, but they are not stripped of everything.

“If grape-gatherers had come to you, Would they not have left some gleanings?”

Harvesters ordinarily missed some grapes, and Israel's law told them to leave what remained for the poor, the stranger, the fatherless, and the widow (Leviticus 19:10; Deuteronomy 24:21). In both pictures the loss has limits. Edom's judgment will be complete, as verse 6 goes on to show.

Study Notes

“Cut off.” The Hebrew verb (damah) means to be destroyed or ruined. Some interpreters connect it with a similar word meaning “to be silent” and render the clause “how you are silenced.” The NKJV's “cut off” reflects the main sense.

Spirit & Life

The gleanings left in a vineyard were God's provision for the poor, the stranger, and the widow (Deuteronomy 24:21). Even in an ordinary harvest, God built mercy into the field. Edom's judgment will leave no gleanings. Read alongside verses 13–14, the picture is sobering: Edom showed no mercy to its brother in the day of his calamity, and now the LORD declares that Edom itself will be stripped bare.

Scripture states the principle plainly: “judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy. Mercy triumphs over judgment” (James 2:13). “Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy” (Matthew 5:7).

Obadiah 1:6

NKJV

Obadiah 1:6 "Oh, how Esau shall be searched out! How his hidden treasures shall be sought after! (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:6 Every nook and cranny of Edomwill be searched and looted.Every treasure will be found and taken. (NLT)

Main Idea

The treasures Edom hid in its rocky strongholds will be searched out and taken. The prophet now calls the nation “Esau,” which keeps its descent from Jacob's brother in view as the judgment falls.

Verse Explanation

“Oh, how Esau shall be searched out!”

For the first time the prophet calls the nation by the name of its ancestor, Esau. He will do so repeatedly (vv. 8, 9, 18, 19, 21). The name keeps in view that Edom descended from Jacob's brother, and that relationship becomes the basis of the charge in verse 10. “Searched out” pictures enemies going through Edom carefully, leaving no place unexamined.

“How his hidden treasures shall be sought after!”

Edom's position along trade routes brought it wealth, and its rocky terrain offered places to store that wealth out of sight. What Edom hid where no one could find it will be hunted down. Thieves would take only what they wanted (v. 5), but Edom's conquerors will search until nothing remains.

Study Notes

“Hidden treasures.” This Hebrew word (matspunim) appears only here in the Old Testament. It comes from a verb meaning “to hide” or “to store up.”

The King's Highway. The main north–south trade route east of the Jordan passed through Edom. When Israel asked permission to travel it, Edom refused (Numbers 20:14–21). Control of such routes was one source of Edom's wealth.

Spirit & Life

Edom stored its wealth where no one could find it, and still it was found. No hiding place on earth is beyond God's search. Jesus told His followers to lay up treasure in heaven, “where neither moth nor rust destroys and where thieves do not break in and steal” (Matthew 6:20).

The verb behind Edom's “hidden treasures” also appears in Psalm 119:11: “Your word I have hidden in my heart.” What Edom hid in the rock was searched out and taken. What the believer hides of God's Word in his heart cannot be taken. Paul says the believer's life itself “is hidden with Christ in God” (Colossians 3:3). The safest treasure is the one kept in God.

Obadiah 1:7

NKJV

Obadiah 1:7 All the men in your confederacy Shall force you to the border; The men at peace with you Shall deceive you and prevail against you. Those who eat your bread shall lay a trap for you. No one is aware of it. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:7 “All your allies will turn against you.They will help to chase you from your land.They will promise you peacewhile plotting to deceive and destroy you.Your trusted friends will set traps for you,and you won’t even know about it. (NLT)

Main Idea

The allies Edom trusted will turn against it. Those bound to it by treaty, peace, and shared bread will push it to its border, deceive it, and set a trap, and Edom will not recognize the danger.

Verse Explanation

After stripping away Edom's wealth, the LORD turns to the relationships Edom relied on.

“All the men in your confederacy Shall force you to the border”

“Confederacy” refers to nations bound to Edom by covenant or treaty. Instead of helping, they will push Edom to its border. This may mean driving Edom's people from their land, or turning away the envoys Edom sends to ask for help. Either way, those Edom counted on will reject it.

“The men at peace with you Shall deceive you and prevail against you.”

Those who seemed to be at peace with Edom will deceive it and overpower it. “Deceive” is the same Hebrew verb used in verse 3. Edom's pride deceived it from within, and now its friends will deceive it from without.

“Those who eat your bread shall lay a trap for you.”

To eat someone's bread meant sharing his table and receiving his hospitality, a bond that carried an expectation of loyalty (Psalm 41:9). Even Edom's closest companions will set a trap for it.

“No one is aware of it.”

The Hebrew reads more literally, “There is no understanding in him.” Edom, so confident in its own wisdom, will not see the betrayal coming. The phrase leads directly into verse 8, where the LORD removes understanding from Edom.

Study Notes

“Trap.” The Hebrew word translated “trap” (mazor) is uncommon, and its exact meaning is uncertain. Most translations render it “trap” or “ambush.” Some interpreters connect it with a similar word meaning “wound” (Hosea 5:13), picturing a hidden injury. The context makes the betrayal clear in either case.

Edom's neighbors. Jeremiah 27:3 names Edom with Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon as nations whose envoys came to Zedekiah in Jerusalem, likely to plan resistance against Babylon. Obadiah does not identify Edom's allies.

Spirit & Life

Edom's allies were real friends in the ordinary sense: partners by treaty, neighbors at peace, companions at the table. Those are good gifts, but they are poor foundations. “It is better to trust in the LORD than to put confidence in man” (Psalm 118:8). Security that rests on people is only as steady as the people it rests on.

Betrayal by someone who has shared our bread is one of the deepest wounds a person can know. David felt it: “Even my own familiar friend in whom I trusted, who ate my bread, has lifted up his heel against me” (Psalm 41:9). Jesus applied those words to Judas at the Last Supper (John 13:18), and that same evening He had washed Judas's feet. Edom's betrayal was a judgment it had earned. Christ's betrayal was a wound He chose to bear for others. Whoever has been betrayed by someone close can bring that wound to a Savior who knows it from the inside.

Obadiah 1:8

NKJV

Obadiah 1:8 "Will I not in that day," says the LORD, "Even destroy the wise men from Edom, And understanding from the mountains of Esau? (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:8 At that time not a single wise personwill be left in the whole land of Edom,”says the LORD.“For on the mountains of EdomI will destroy everyone who has understanding. (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD will destroy Edom's wise men and remove the understanding for which the nation was known. Its wisdom will provide no protection against His judgment.

Verse Explanation

“Will I not in that day,” says the LORD,

The question expects the answer yes. “That day” is the day of Edom's judgment. It is the first mention of “day” in the prophecy, a word that becomes central in verses 11–15.

“Even destroy the wise men from Edom,”

Edom was known for its wisdom. Jeremiah asks, “Is wisdom no more in Teman?” (Jeremiah 49:7). The LORD will remove the counselors Edom depended on.

“And understanding from the mountains of Esau?”

The LORD will take away more than Edom's wise men. Understanding itself will be gone from Edom's mountain homeland. A nation without understanding cannot see its danger or find a way out, which is why Edom will not recognize its allies' betrayal (v. 7).

Study Notes

Wisdom in Teman. Job's friend Eliphaz the Temanite (Job 2:11) is the best-known wise man associated with Teman and reflects the region's reputation for wisdom.

Spirit & Life

Edom's wisdom was famous, yet in the moment of danger it was gone. Its wise men could not protect a nation whose heart had been lifted up in pride. Understanding is not something we keep by our own ability. It is a gift the LORD gives, and He can withdraw it (Isaiah 29:14; 1 Corinthians 1:19). Even Eliphaz the Temanite, for all his eloquence, heard the LORD say, “you have not spoken of Me what is right” (Job 42:7).

“The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom” (Proverbs 9:10). Wisdom cut off from that root may impress for a season, but it cannot see what matters most. Lasting wisdom is a dependent wisdom, the kind that keeps asking God (James 1:5).

Obadiah 1:9

NKJV

Obadiah 1:9 Then your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed, To the end that everyone from the mountains of Esau May be cut off by slaughter. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:9 The mightiest warriors of Temanwill be terrified,and everyone on the mountains of Edomwill be cut down in the slaughter. (NLT)

Main Idea

Even Edom's mighty men will be dismayed. Its military strength will fail, and the people of the mountains of Esau will face slaughter.

Verse Explanation

“Then your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed,”

Teman, named for a grandson of Esau (Genesis 36:11), was a leading part of Edom and here stands for the whole nation. Its wise men are gone, and now its warriors fail as well. “Dismayed” means shattered with fear: Edom's strongest soldiers will be overcome with terror.

“To the end that everyone from the mountains of Esau May be cut off by slaughter.”

The collapse of Edom's warriors leads to its defeat. With this verse, the LORD has announced the failure of every source of Edom's confidence named in verses 3–9.

Study Notes

Teman. Teman may refer to a district of Edom or to a town. Its exact location is uncertain.

“By slaughter.” Some translations, including the NRSV, attach this phrase to the beginning of verse 10: “For the slaughter and violence done to your brother Jacob.” The NKJV keeps it with verse 9. The overall sense of the passage is the same either way.

Spirit & Life

Edom's mighty men had every natural reason to be brave: training, experience, and mountain strongholds. Yet they will be dismayed. The same Hebrew word appears in God's charge to Joshua: “Do not be afraid, nor be dismayed, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go” (Joshua 1:9). Joshua's courage did not rest on his own strength but on God's presence.

Edom's warriors, trusting in their own strength, are overcome by dismay. Strength without God gives way under pressure. Lasting courage is not a personality trait. It grows from knowing that God is with us.

Obadiah 1:10–14 - The Reason: Edom Mistreated His Brother

Obadiah 1:10

NKJV

Obadiah 1:10 "For violence against your brother Jacob, (H3290) Shame shall cover you, And you shall be cut off forever. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:10 “Because of the violence you didto your close relatives in Israel,you will be filled with shameand destroyed forever. (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD names the reason for Edom's judgment: violence against its brother Jacob. Because Edom turned against its own kin, it will be covered with shame and cut off forever.

Verse Explanation

Verses 1–9 announced that every source of Edom's security will fail. Verse 10 now gives the reason, and verses 11–14 set out the charges against Edom.

“For violence against your brother Jacob,”

“Violence” is a strong word for violent wrongdoing and injustice. It is the same word used for the corruption that filled the earth before the flood (Genesis 6:11).

The victim is called “your brother Jacob.” Obadiah could have said Judah, but he uses the name of Edom's ancestor's twin brother. Edom and Judah were family, and the Law had told Israel, “You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother” (Deuteronomy 23:7). The family bond made Edom's violence more serious, not less.

“Shame shall cover you,”

Edom had been proud of its height and security (v. 3). Now shame will cover it like a garment.

“And you shall be cut off forever.”

“Cut off” continues the language of verse 9. “Forever” marks the judgment as final and complete. The LORD is announcing more than a temporary defeat.

Study Notes

Edom's long hostility. Other prophets also condemn Edom's hostility toward Israel. Amos says Edom “pursued his brother with the sword” and “kept his wrath forever” (Amos 1:11). Ezekiel speaks of Edom's “ancient hatred” (Ezekiel 35:5).

Spirit & Life

The charge is not simply violence but violence “against your brother.” Generations earlier, Esau had run to meet Jacob, embraced him, and wept (Genesis 33:4). The brothers had been reconciled. Yet the hostility returned among their descendants and was kept alive for generations (Amos 1:11; Ezekiel 35:5).

A grudge that is nursed does not stay the same size. Over time it affects a person, a family, or a people until it becomes part of who they are. The closer the relationship, the more seriously God takes how it is treated, because He is the One who gave the bond. Where grace has healed an old wound in a family or a church, the call is to keep it healed rather than reopen it.

Obadiah 1:11

NKJV

Obadiah 1:11 In the day that you stood on the other side"” In the day that strangers carried captive his forces, When foreigners entered his gates And cast lots for Jerusalem"” Even you were as one of them. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:11 When they were invaded,you stood aloof, refusing to help them.Foreign invaders carried off their wealthand cast lots to divide up Jerusalem,but you acted like one of Israel’s enemies. (NLT)

Main Idea

When foreigners plundered Jerusalem and cast lots for it, Edom stood on the other side and did nothing to help. By standing aloof, Edom became “as one of them.”

Verse Explanation

“In the day that you stood on the other side-”

The Hebrew pictures Edom standing opposite, at a distance. While its brother was being attacked, Edom positioned itself apart and watched. The first thing the LORD charges is Edom's refusal to help.

“In the day that strangers carried captive his forces,”

“Strangers” and, in the next line, “foreigners” are the invading army. The Hebrew word behind “forces” can mean either an army or wealth. Either way, invaders were carrying off what belonged to Judah.

“When foreigners entered his gates And cast lots for Jerusalem-”

The enemy broke through Jerusalem's gates. Conquerors cast lots to divide captives and plunder among themselves (Joel 3:3; Nahum 3:10). The city of God was being divided up like spoil.

“Even you were as one of them.”

This is the LORD's verdict on Edom's standing aside. Even apart from any blow Edom struck, its refusal to help its brother placed it on the side of the invaders. Verses 12–14 show that its involvement went beyond watching.

Study Notes

The historical setting. Verses 10–14 best fit Babylon's destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC (2 Kings 25:8–12). Psalm 137:7 recalls Edom's words on “the day of Jerusalem”: “Raze it, raze it, To its very foundation!” Lamentations 4:21–22 and Ezekiel 35:5 also condemn Edom's conduct when Jerusalem fell. Some interpreters place Obadiah earlier, during the raid on Jerusalem in Jehoram's reign (2 Chronicles 21:16–17).

Spirit & Life

Edom did not have to strike a blow to be counted with the enemy. Standing on the other side was itself a choice. When a brother is in trouble and we are able to help, distance is not neutral. In Jesus' parable, the priest and the Levite “passed by on the other side” (Luke 10:31–32), and their distance revealed their hearts as clearly as the Samaritan's mercy revealed his.

The gospel shows the opposite of Edom. Edom kept its distance from its brother, but Christ came all the way to our side. He “is not ashamed to call them brethren” (Hebrews 2:11). He did not watch our ruin from a distance; He entered it and carried it. Those who belong to Him are called to the same nearness with one another.

Obadiah 1:12

NKJV

Obadiah 1:12 "But you should not have gazed on the day of your brother In the day of his captivity; Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah In the day of their destruction; Nor should you have spoken proudly In the day of distress. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:12 “You should not have gloatedwhen they exiled your relatives to distant lands.You should not have rejoicedwhen the people of Judah suffered such misfortune.You should not have spoken arrogantlyin that terrible time of trouble. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom should not have gazed with satisfaction on its brother's disaster, rejoiced over Judah's destruction, or spoken proudly in their distress. The LORD condemns Edom's inward pleasure in Judah's suffering and the proud words that came from it.

Verse Explanation

Verses 12–14 list eight things Edom should not have done. They range from watching its brother's ruin with pleasure to taking an active part in it.

“But you should not have gazed on the day of your brother In the day of his captivity;”

“Gazed” here means more than seeing. It means looking on with satisfaction, enjoying the sight. Edom watched its brother's disaster and was pleased by it. “The day of his captivity” is the day disaster fell on Judah and its people were carried away.

“Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah In the day of their destruction;”

The inward pleasure becomes open rejoicing. Edom rejoiced over the fall of a brother.

“Nor should you have spoken proudly In the day of distress.”

The Hebrew reads literally, “Nor should you have made your mouth great.” Edom boasted and mocked while Judah suffered. The pride exposed in verse 3 now shows itself in Edom's speech, and the LORD heard it (Ezekiel 35:12–13).

Study Notes

“Should not have.” The Hebrew uses the ordinary form of a prohibition, “Do not ….” Because verse 11 describes Jerusalem's fall as past, these lines are best read as the NKJV renders them: a rebuke of what Edom had already done. Some interpreters read them instead as warnings against doing it again.

“His captivity.” The Hebrew word is related to the word for “foreigner” and means misfortune or disaster. It appears elsewhere only in Job 31:3. The KJV renders it “in the day that he became a stranger.”

Spirit & Life

The first wrong named in verse 12 happens in the eyes. Edom watched its brother suffer and enjoyed the sight, and the pleasure in its eyes found its way into its mouth. Satisfaction in someone else's trouble rarely stays private. “Do not rejoice when your enemy falls … lest the LORD see it, and it displease Him” (Proverbs 24:17–18). God watches not only the one who falls but the face of the one watching.

Jesus looked at Jerusalem as its day of calamity approached, and He wept over it (Luke 19:41). The way we look at another person's fall reveals whose heart we share. A heart filled with Christ’s love grieves where Edom gloated.

Obadiah 1:13

NKJV

Obadiah 1:13 You should not have entered the gate of My people In the day of their calamity. Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction In the day of their calamity, Nor laid hands on their substance In the day of their calamity. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:13 You should not have plundered the land of Israelwhen they were suffering such calamity.You should not have gloated over their destructionwhen they were suffering such calamity.You should not have seized their wealthwhen they were suffering such calamity. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom should not have entered the gate of the LORD's people, gazed on their affliction, or seized their goods in the day of their calamity. The LORD still calls Judah “My people,” so Edom's actions were an offense against people who belonged to Him.

Verse Explanation

“You should not have entered the gate of My people In the day of their calamity.”

Edom moves from watching outside the city to entering it. The LORD calls Judah “My people.” Judah was suffering judgment for its own sins, yet the LORD had not disowned them. His discipline of Judah gave Edom no right to take part in their ruin.

“Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction In the day of their calamity,”

The word “gazed” repeats verse 12, but now Edom looks on Judah's suffering from inside the gates.

“Nor laid hands on their substance In the day of their calamity.”

Edom joins the looting and takes Judah's goods for itself. “In the day of their calamity” closes each line of the verse, three times in a row. The repetition keeps the timing in front of the reader: Edom acted when its brother was most helpless.

Study Notes

“Calamity.” The Hebrew word for “calamity” (ʾed, with the suffix ʾedam, “their calamity”) sounds like the name Edom, a wordplay many interpreters note. Ezekiel 35:5 uses the same word when it charges Edom with violence against Israel “at the time of their calamity.”

Spirit & Life

In the very day of His discipline, the LORD calls Judah “My people.” His correction never meant He had let them go. Zechariah records the same concern: God was “a little angry” with His people, but the nations “helped-but with evil intent” (Zechariah 1:15).

This is a comfort for believers under God's discipline. Hardship that comes from our own failure does not mean we have been disowned. “Whom the LORD loves He chastens” (Hebrews 12:6), and the One who disciplines us still calls us His own. It is also a warning: when someone else is under correction, that is never permission to take advantage of their weakness.

Obadiah 1:14

NKJV

Obadiah 1:14 You should not have stood at the crossroads To cut off those among them who escaped; Nor should you have delivered up those among them who remained In the day of distress. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:14 You should not have stood at the crossroads,killing those who tried to escape.You should not have captured the survivorsand handed them over in their terrible time of trouble. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom should not have stood at the crossroads to cut off those fleeing Jerusalem or delivered up those who remained. Edom's conduct ends in direct participation in Judah's destruction.

Verse Explanation

“You should not have stood at the crossroads To cut off those among them who escaped;”

The crossroads were places where roads divided, the routes people used to flee the city. Obadiah charges that Edom stood there to cut off those who escaped Jerusalem. “Cut off” is the same Hebrew verb used of Edom's own judgment in verses 9 and 10.

“Nor should you have delivered up those among them who remained In the day of distress.”

“Delivered up” means handing survivors over to the enemy. The charges that began with standing aloof (v. 11) end here with Edom taking an active part in Judah's destruction.

Study Notes

“Crossroads.” The Hebrew word (pereq) is usually understood as a fork in the road or a mountain pass. The same word appears in Nahum 3:1, where it means “robbery” or plunder.

“Delivered up.” Amos uses the same verb when he condemns Gaza and Tyre for delivering captives up “to Edom” (Amos 1:6, 9). Edom had received captives handed over by others. Here Edom hands over the captives itself.

Spirit & Life

The crossroads were the places where escape was still possible. Those fleeing the burning city needed only an open road. Edom stood at the very place where it could have helped most and used it to do the most harm.

God places His people at the crossroads of other people's lives: a moment of failure, crisis, or flight, when a word, an open door, or a meal can make the way to safety. The same position can close a road or open it. Scripture calls believers to restore the one who has been overtaken and to “bear one another's burdens” (Galatians 6:1–2).

Obadiah 1:15–21 - The Day of the LORD and the Deliverance of Israel

Obadiah 1:15

NKJV

Obadiah 1:15 "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near; (H7138) As you have done, it shall be done to you; Your reprisal shall return upon your own head. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:15 “The day is near when I, the LORD,will judge all godless nations!As you have done to Israel,so it will be done to you.All your evil deedswill fall back on your own heads. (NLT)

Main Idea

The day of the LORD is near upon all nations, and Edom will receive what it has done. The judgment now widens beyond Edom, and the principle by which God judges Edom applies to every nation.

Verse Explanation

Verse 15 is the hinge of the book. The view widens from Edom to all the nations, and Judah's day of calamity gives way to the day of the LORD.

“For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near;”

In verses 11–14 the word “day” appears ten times for Judah's day of disaster. Edom treated that day as its opportunity. Now the prophet announces a different day, one that belongs to the LORD.

The day of the LORD is the time when God acts decisively to judge His enemies and deliver His people (Joel 2:31–32; Zephaniah 1:14–18). It is “near,” and it comes “upon all the nations.” Edom's judgment is one example of a judgment that will reach every nation.

“As you have done, it shall be done to you;”

This is the principle of God's judgment: Edom will receive what it gave. Edom looted Judah's goods (v. 13), and its own hidden treasures will be plundered (v. 6). Edom cut off those who escaped (v. 14), and it will be cut off forever (v. 10).

“Your reprisal shall return upon your own head.”

“Reprisal” is what someone deals out to others. What Edom did will come back on Edom (Psalm 7:16). God's judgment is not arbitrary. It answers what was done, and because God is good, He does not ignore violence done to the helpless.

Study Notes

The day of the LORD in the New Testament. The New Testament connects the final day of the Lord with Christ's return, when He will bring judgment on those who oppose God and rest to His people (1 Thessalonians 5:2; 2 Thessalonians 1:6–10).

Spirit & Life

For a time it looked as if the day belonged to Edom. Judah's day of disaster became Edom's day of opportunity. But days do not finally belong to the strong. The day of the LORD is coming, and on that day every deed returns to its owner.

“As you have done, it shall be done to you” is a sobering word, because God's justice is exact. It is also a comfort to those who have been wronged. They do not have to repay every injury themselves: “‘Vengeance is Mine, I will repay,’ says the Lord” (Romans 12:19). God's justice flows from His goodness.

Obadiah 1:16

NKJV

Obadiah 1:16 For as you drank on My holy mountain, (H2022) So shall all the nations drink continually; Yes, they shall drink, and swallow, And they shall be as though they had never been.(NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:16 Just as you swallowed up my peopleon my holy mountain,so you and the surrounding nationswill swallow the punishment I pour out on you.Yes, all you nations will drink and staggerand disappear from history. (NLT)

Main Idea

The day of the LORD will bring judgment upon the nations, pictured as drinking continually until they are as though they had never existed. The reference to drinking on God's holy mountain connects their judgment with what happened at Jerusalem.

Verse Explanation

“For as you drank on My holy mountain,”

“My holy mountain” is Mount Zion, where the LORD's temple stood in Jerusalem. Interpreters differ on who drank there. Many understand it as Edom and its allies celebrating on the ruins of Jerusalem, drinking in triumph where the LORD's house had stood. Others understand it as Judah, which drank the cup of God's discipline when Jerusalem fell. In Jeremiah's vision of the cup, Jerusalem is the first to drink it (Jeremiah 25:15–18).

“So shall all the nations drink continually;”

In either reading, the nations now drink the cup of God's judgment, a familiar picture in the prophets (Jeremiah 25:15–29; 49:12). “Continually” means the judgment will not end after a single sip.

“Yes, they shall drink, and swallow, And they shall be as though they had never been.”

“Swallow” pictures gulping the cup down to the bottom. The result is more than defeat. The nations that opposed God will be as though they had never existed.

Study Notes

“You drank.” In verse 15, “you” is singular and addresses Edom. In verse 16 the verb is plural. Some take the change as a turn to address Judah. Others take the plural as Edom together with its allies.

Spirit & Life

The LORD calls Zion “My holy mountain.” Whatever drinking took place there, God was not indifferent to it, and the nations will be handed a cup they cannot put down.

The prophets' cup of judgment appears again in the Gospels. In Gethsemane Jesus prayed, “O My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as You will” (Matthew 26:39). Read in the light of the whole Bible, the cup of judgment we deserved is the cup Christ willingly drank in our place. Because He drank it, those who trust Him receive a different cup: “This cup is the new covenant in My blood” (1 Corinthians 11:25). The Lord's Table reminds us that the cup of judgment has already been drunk on our behalf.

Obadiah 1:17

NKJV

Obadiah 1:17 "But on Mount Zionthere shall be deliverance, And there shall be holiness; The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:17 “But Jerusalem will become a refuge for those who escape;it will be a holy place.And the people of Israel will come backto reclaim their inheritance. (NLT)

Main Idea

On Mount Zion there will be deliverance and holiness. The LORD provides escape for His people, makes Zion holy, and the house of Jacob will possess their possessions.

Verse Explanation

“But on Mount Zion there shall be deliverance,”

“But” marks the turn from judgment to deliverance. The nations will drink the cup of judgment, but on Mount Zion there will be people who escape. The Hebrew word for “deliverance” shares a root with the word for those who “escaped” in verse 14. Edom blocked the escape of Judah's survivors, and the LORD provides escape on His own mountain. Joel uses the same promise: “in Mount Zion and in Jerusalem there shall be deliverance” (Joel 2:32).

“And there shall be holiness;”

Mount Zion will be holy, set apart to the LORD. Mount Zion, violated when Jerusalem fell, will no longer be trampled by the nations (Joel 3:17). The LORD does not only rescue His people from their enemies. He makes them and their dwelling place His own.

“The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions.”

“Possess” is the word used for Israel taking and holding the land as its inheritance. What Judah lost will be restored. The same verb returns in verses 19–20, which name the territories the LORD's people will possess.

Study Notes

Mount Zion. Zion was first the name of the fortified hill in Jerusalem that David captured from the Jebusites, “the stronghold of Zion (that is, the City of David)” (2 Samuel 5:7). This was the southeastern ridge of the city. The name soon extended to the temple mount just to its north and then to Jerusalem as a whole. The hill called Mount Zion today, on Jerusalem's southwest side, received the name much later. Zion matters because it is the place the LORD chose for His dwelling: “For the LORD has chosen Zion; He has desired it for His dwelling place” (Psalm 132:13). In the prophets, Zion stands for God's presence among His people, the center of His rule, and the place of their future restoration (Isaiah 2:2–3; Joel 3:17). In Obadiah, Mount Zion (vv. 17, 21), the LORD's “holy mountain” (v. 16), stands opposite the mountains of Esau. Edom's heights were the seat of its pride, while Zion is where the LORD dwells, delivers, and reigns. The New Testament tells believers, “you have come to Mount Zion and to the city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem” (Hebrews 12:22).

Deliverance and holiness. The pairing of rescue and holiness anticipates the New Testament, where Christ gave Himself “that He might redeem us from every lawless deed and purify for Himself His own special people” (Titus 2:14).

An alternative reading. The NKJV follows the traditional Hebrew reading, “the house of Jacob shall possess their possessions.” Some translations read the final phrase as referring to those who had dispossessed Israel. The CSB renders it “the house of Jacob will dispossess those who dispossessed them,” while the NET expresses the idea as Jacob's descendants conquering those who had conquered them. Both readings fit the promise that God's people will regain possession and overcome their enemies.

Spirit & Life

“But” turns the whole prophecy. What Edom shut, God opens. Edom stood at the crossroads to cut off those who escaped, and the LORD makes His own mountain a place of escape. Joel gives the same promise, and Peter preached from it at Pentecost: “whoever calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved” (Joel 2:32; Acts 2:21). No one can close a road God has chosen to open.

Deliverance on Zion does not stop at rescue. “There shall be holiness.” God rescues His people to make them wholly His, set apart for Himself and for His presence. Then comes inheritance: “The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions.” What God gives must still be taken hold of. Believers have been blessed “with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ” (Ephesians 1:3), yet many live as if they owned very little. Faith is the hand that takes hold of what God has already given.

Obadiah 1:18

NKJV

Obadiah 1:18 The house of Jacob shall be a fire, And the house of Joseph a flame; But the house of Esau shall be stubble; They shall kindle them and devour them, And no survivor shall remain of the house of Esau," For the LORD has spoken. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:18 The people of Israel will be a raging fire,and Edom a field of dry stubble.The descendants of Joseph will be a flameroaring across the field, devouring everything.There will be no survivors in Edom.I, the LORD, have spoken! (NLT)

Main Idea

The house of Jacob and the house of Joseph will be a fire, and the house of Esau stubble, until no survivor of Esau remains. The LORD has spoken, and His restored people become the means of His judgment on Esau.

Verse Explanation

Verse 17 promised deliverance on Mount Zion. Verse 18 joins that deliverance with judgment on Esau.

“The house of Jacob shall be a fire, And the house of Joseph a flame;”

“The house of Joseph” refers to the northern tribes, led by Ephraim and Manasseh, the sons of Joseph. Set beside “the house of Jacob,” the phrase appears to include Judah and the northern tribes together. The divided people of God will be restored and act as one (Ezekiel 37:15–22), and they will be like fire.

“But the house of Esau shall be stubble; They shall kindle them and devour them,”

Stubble is the dry stalks left after harvest. It catches fire quickly and burns completely (Isaiah 5:24; Malachi 4:1). The image shows how fully Esau's house will be consumed.

“And no survivor shall remain of the house of Esau,”

“Survivor” is the same Hebrew word used for those who “remained” in verse 14. Edom handed over Judah's survivors, and Esau's house will be left with none. As verse 15 said, Edom receives what it did.

“For the LORD has spoken.”

The closing formula seals the announcement with God's authority. The outcome rests on the word of the LORD, not on Judah's strength or hope.

Study Notes

Judah and Joseph together. Zechariah 10:6 joins the two names in a promise of restoration: “I will strengthen the house of Judah, And I will save the house of Joseph.” Zechariah 12:6 also pictures Judah's leaders as “a firepan in the woodpile” and “a fiery torch in the sheaves” among the surrounding peoples.

Spirit & Life

Stubble does not fight fire. It simply cannot stand before it. Esau's house looked strong in its mountains, but when the fire comes it proves to be dry stalks.

The fire is not Israel's own. Isaiah says that “the Light of Israel will be for a fire, and his Holy One for a flame” (Isaiah 10:17). When God's people become a fire, it is His fire burning in them. Notice too that the fire is Jacob and Joseph together; the divided people of God burn as one. At Pentecost the Spirit came on the gathered believers with “divided tongues, as of fire” (Acts 2:3). The strength of God's people has never been their own heat. It is the presence of the Holy One among them.

Paul uses the same picture of combustible material before fire. Christ is the only foundation (1 Corinthians 3:11), and each believer's work “will become clear; for the Day will declare it, because it will be revealed by fire; and the fire will test each one's work, of what sort it is” (1 Corinthians 3:13). What is built on Christ with gold, silver, and precious stones endures and receives a reward. What is built with wood, hay, and straw (“stubble” in the KJV) is burned up, and the builder “will suffer loss; but he himself will be saved, yet so as through fire” (1 Corinthians 3:12–15). Paul is speaking of the testing of believers' works, not of a judgment like Edom's. Yet both passages picture material that cannot withstand fire, and both bring out the same sobering reality: what appears substantial or secure may prove unable to endure God's examination.

We can build our lives, ministries, and works on pride, self-reliance, human wisdom, and temporary things that look impressive but cannot endure God's testing. They burn like stubble. Christ is the only foundation on which anything lasting can be built. Peter adds that the trials of believers test the genuineness of their faith, “being much more precious than gold that perishes, though it is tested by fire” (1 Peter 1:6–7). God's fire refines what is real and exposes what is not. A life built on Christ, and a faith that has been tested and found genuine, will stand.

Obadiah 1:19

NKJV

Obadiah 1:19 The South shall possess the mountains of Esau, And the Lowland shall possess Philistia. They shall possess the fields of Ephraim And the fields of Samaria. Benjamin shall possess Gilead. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:19 “Then my people living in the Negevwill occupy the mountains of Edom.Those living in the foothills of Judahwill possess the Philistine plainsand take over the fields of Ephraim and Samaria.And the people of Benjaminwill occupy the land of Gilead. (NLT)

Main Idea

God's restored people will possess the territories named in the prophecy, including the mountains of Esau, Philistia, Ephraim, Samaria, and Gilead.

Verse Explanation

Verses 19–20 show on the map what it means for the house of Jacob to “possess their possessions” (v. 17).

“The South shall possess the mountains of Esau,”

“The South” is the Negev, the dry region of southern Judah that bordered Edom. Its people will take possession of Edom's mountains, the heights Edom believed no one could reach (v. 3).

“And the Lowland shall possess Philistia.”

The Lowland is the region of low hills west of Judah's mountains. Its people will possess Philistia, the coastal plain beyond it, held by one of Israel's long-standing enemies.

“They shall possess the fields of Ephraim And the fields of Samaria.”

Ephraim and Samaria were the heart of the northern kingdom, lost when Assyria conquered it. They will be possessed again.

“Benjamin shall possess Gilead.”

Gilead lay east of the Jordan River. Benjamin, a small tribe just north of Jerusalem, will extend its possession across the river. Taken together, the restored territory reaches out from Judah to the south, west, north, and east.

Study Notes

The scope of the promise. The borders described here go beyond anything the returning exiles held. During the Hasmonean period, in the second century BC, Judah extended its rule over Idumea (the former Edomite territory) and parts of the coast. Many interpreters see the full scope of the promise in the messianic kingdom.

Spirit & Life

The people of the dry South will possess the mountains Edom thought no one could climb. The lowly inherit the heights, and the proud lose them. This is how God works throughout Scripture: “He has put down the mighty from their thrones, and exalted the lowly” (Luke 1:52).

The territories spread out in every direction. God once promised Jacob at Bethel, “you shall spread abroad to the west and the east, to the north and the south” (Genesis 28:14). God's promises come with names and places, and He does not forget them. The God who keeps His word down to the Negev and Gilead can be trusted with the specific promises He has made to His people.

Obadiah 1:20

NKJV

Obadiah 1:20 And the captives of this host of the children of Israel Shall possess the land of the Canaanites As far as Zarephath. The captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad Shall possess the cities of the South. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:20 The exiles of Israel will return to their landand occupy the Phoenician coast as far north as Zarephath.The captives from Jerusalem exiled in the northwill return home and resettle the towns of the Negev. (NLT)

Main Idea

The exiles of Israel and Jerusalem will possess the territories assigned to them, including land extending toward Zarephath and the cities of the South.

Verse Explanation

“And the captives of this host of the children of Israel Shall possess the land of the Canaanites As far as Zarephath.”

“This host” refers to a company of Israelite exiles, most likely those of the northern tribes taken captive by Assyria. They will return and possess “the land of the Canaanites,” here the Phoenician coast north of Israel, as far as Zarephath. Zarephath was a town between Tyre and Sidon, where Elijah stayed with the widow (1 Kings 17:9).

“The captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad Shall possess the cities of the South.”

Exiles from Jerusalem, even those carried as far as Sepharad, will return and possess the towns of the Negev. With verse 19, the promise includes both the northern and southern exiles. No exile is too far away to share in the LORD's restoration.

Study Notes

Sepharad. The location of Sepharad is uncertain. Many scholars identify it with Sardis in Asia Minor, whose name appears in a similar form in ancient inscriptions. Later Jewish tradition identified it with Spain, which is why Jews of Spanish descent came to be called Sephardic.

“This host.” The Hebrew phrase is difficult. Some translations follow a slight change in the text and read it as Halah, a place in Assyria to which northern Israelites were deported (2 Kings 17:6; compare CSB).

Spirit & Life

Obadiah identifies people scattered in exile and names the territories they will possess when God restores them. God knew exactly where His scattered people were. Those who feel far from God, carried away by circumstances or by their own choices, are not lost to His sight. The Good Shepherd goes after the one that is lost “until he finds it” (Luke 15:4).

Zarephath has its own story. There God sent Elijah to a Gentile widow, who fed the prophet from her last handful of flour, and her flour and oil did not run out (1 Kings 17:9–16). Jesus recalled her at Nazareth to show that God's grace reaches beyond the borders people expect (Luke 4:25–26). The edge of Israel's restored territory was already a place where God had shown mercy to an outsider.

Obadiah 1:21

NKJV

Obadiah 1:21 Then saviors shall come to Mount ZionTo judge the mountains of Esau, And the kingdom shall be the LORD's. [LORD’S] (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:21 Those who have been rescued will go up to Mount Zion in Jerusalemto rule over the mountains of Edom.And the LORD himself will be king!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Saviors will come to Mount Zion to judge the mountains of Esau, and the kingdom will be the LORD's. The chapter ends with the LORD's reign, not with Edom's fall.

Verse Explanation

“Then saviors shall come to Mount Zion”

“Saviors” (“deliverers” in the NLT) is plural. The same word describes the deliverers God raised up to rescue Israel in the time of the judges (Judges 3:9, 15). Nehemiah recalls that period: “You gave them deliverers who saved them” (Nehemiah 9:27). The text does not identify these saviors. They come to Mount Zion, the place of deliverance and holiness (v. 17).

“To judge the mountains of Esau,”

To “judge” includes both passing judgment and ruling, as Israel's judges did. From Zion the saviors will exercise authority over Edom's territory. The mountains of Esau, once the seat of Edom's pride (v. 3), come under the rule of Zion.

“And the kingdom shall be the LORD's.”

These are the book's final words. The prophecy does not end with Edom's fall or even with Israel's restoration, but with the LORD's reign. Edom's kingdom, the nations' kingdoms, and finally every kingdom come under His rule (Psalm 22:28; Zechariah 14:9).

Study Notes

“Shall come to Mount Zion.” The Hebrew reads literally, “shall go up on Mount Zion.” The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation) reads “men who have been saved shall go up from Mount Zion,” describing the deliverers as people who have themselves been rescued.

The LORD's kingdom. Revelation 11:15 carries this closing declaration to its completion in Christ's reign: “The kingdoms of this world have become the kingdoms of our Lord and of His Christ, and He shall reign forever and ever!”

Spirit & Life

Obadiah does not name the saviors, but the wider witness of Scripture helps us see what the Holy Spirit is revealing through them. Read prophetically, they point to an end-time generation of highly empowered, victorious believers whom God raises up to share in the advance and manifestation of His kingdom. Like the judges of old, they are rescued people who become instruments of rescue; the Septuagint calls them “men who have been saved.” Jesus promised that those who believe in Him would do the works He did, and greater (John 14:12). He promised the one who overcomes a share in His authority and His throne (Revelation 2:26–27; 3:21). Daniel saw the day when “the time came for the saints to possess the kingdom” (Daniel 7:22).

Their authority is never their own. They ascend Mount Zion, the place of deliverance and holiness, and their victory comes from Christ, whose kingdom it is. The book's final words keep the order clear: “the kingdom shall be the LORD's.” God's purpose is not only to rescue His people from their enemies but to make them partners in His reign. Every believer who has been delivered is meant to become part of God's deliverance for others, and every prayer of “Your kingdom come” (Matthew 6:10) looks toward this day.
