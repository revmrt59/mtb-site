Chapter Study

Obadiah 1

Verse-by-Verse
Obadiah 1:1

NKJV

Obadiah 1:1 The vision of Obadiah. Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom (We have heard a report from the LORD, And a messenger has been sent among the nations, saying, "Arise, and let us rise up against her for battle"): (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:1 This is the vision that the Sovereign LORD revealed to Obadiah concerning the land of Edom. Edom’s Judgment Announced We have heard a message from the LORDthat an ambassador was sent to the nations to say,“Get ready, everyone!Let’s assemble our armies and attack Edom!” (NLT)

Main Idea

God reveals His coming judgment against Edom and announces that He is already stirring the nations to carry out His purpose.

Verse Explanation

"The vision of Obadiah."
The book begins by identifying its message as a vision—a revelation given directly by God. This is not Obadiah's opinion or political analysis. God is revealing what He intends to do. We know very little about Obadiah himself. His name means "Servant (or Worshiper) of Yahweh." The focus of the book is not the prophet, but the God who speaks through him.

"Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom."
This familiar prophetic expression announces that the message carries God's full authority. The prophecy is directed against Edom, the nation descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother (Genesis 25). Although Edom and Israel shared a common ancestry, Edom repeatedly opposed and mistreated God's people throughout their history. The judgment that follows is not based on a single event but on a long pattern of pride, hostility, and violence that reached its climax when Jerusalem fell. Rather than helping their brother nation in its day of distress, Edom rejoiced over Judah's downfall, entered the city to plunder it, captured those trying to escape, and handed survivors over to the enemy.

"We have heard a report from the LORD..."
The "we" most naturally refers to Obadiah and the other prophets who had received God's revelation. The "report" is God's official announcement that the time for judgment has arrived. This prophecy does not begin with human observation but with God's declaration. Before the nations act, God announces what He is about to do.

"A messenger has been sent among the nations..."
God summons the surrounding nations to rise against Edom. Although the nations believed they were pursuing their own political and military interests, God was directing history to accomplish His purposes. Throughout Scripture, God often uses one nation to discipline another while remaining completely sovereign over both.

"Arise, and let us rise up against her for battle."
This is the rallying cry calling the nations to war against Edom. The battle has not begun, but God's decree makes its outcome certain. Once God has spoken, His purposes cannot be overturned.

Remark(s)

Before describing Edom's sins, God first declares His authority over the events of history. Obadiah reminds us that kingdoms rise and fall under God's sovereign hand. What appears to be the movement of armies and the rise and fall of nations, is ultimately the fulfillment of God's word. For God's people, this is a source of confidence: no circumstance is outside His control, and history is moving toward the day when "the kingdom shall be the LORD'S" (Obadiah 1:21).

Obadiah 1:2

NKJV

Obadiah 1:2 "Behold, I will make you small among the nations; You shall be greatly despised. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:2 The LORD says to Edom,“I will cut you down to size among the nations;you will be greatly despised. (NLT)

Main Idea

God declares that He will humble proud Edom, bringing down its greatness and making it an object of contempt among the nations.

Verse Explanation

"Behold..."
This word calls attention to what follows. God is announcing something certain and significant. The judgment He is about to describe is not a possibility but a settled decree.

"I will make you small among the nations..."
Edom considered itself powerful and secure. Its cities were built high in the rugged mountains of Seir, giving its people a sense of invincibility. But God declares that He Himself will reduce Edom's standing among the nations. The nation that believed it was great would become insignificant. This is not merely about population or territory; it speaks of losing influence, prestige, and power.

Notice that God says, "I will make you small." The emphasis is on His action. Edom's downfall would not happen by chance or simply because stronger nations arose. God was actively bringing down a proud nation.

"You shall be greatly despised."
Instead of being respected or feared, Edom would become an object of contempt. The nation that sought honor for itself would experience disgrace. History confirms this prophecy. History confirms this prophecy. Edom gradually disappeared as a nation and eventually vanished from history, while God preserved Israel and continued to fulfill His promises to His people.

Remark(s)

Edom believed no one could bring it down, but God brought it low. Edom's strength, location, and reputation could not protect it when God opposed it. True security is never found in our position, achievements, or resources, but in walking humbly with the Lord.

Obadiah 1:3

NKJV

Obadiah 1:3 The pride of your heart has deceived you, You who dwell in the clefts of the rock, Whose habitation is high; You who say in your heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?' (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:3 You have been deceived by your own pridebecause you live in a rock fortressand make your home high in the mountains.‘Who can ever reach us way up here?’you ask boastfully. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom's pride gave it a false sense of security, blinding it to its true condition before God.

Verse Explanation

"The pride of your heart has deceived you..."
God identifies the root of Edom's problem- pride. Pride is deceptive because it hinders our ability to see ourselves and others clearly. It convinces us that we are stronger, wiser, or more secure than we truly are. Before God addresses Edom's actions, He exposes the condition that produced them.

Notice that the deception came from within. Their pride did not merely influence them; it blinded them. They believed their own false assessment of themselves.

"You who dwell in the clefts of the rock, whose habitation is high..."
Edom occupied the rugged mountains southeast of the Dead Sea. Many of its cities, including the famous rock city of Petra, were built among steep cliffs and narrow mountain passes. These natural fortifications made invasion extremely difficult. From a human perspective, Edom's confidence seemed reasonable.

Yet God points out their location not to admire it, but to show what they had come to trust.

"You who say in your heart..."
Their pride was more than outward boasting. It had become the settled belief of their hearts. God sees not only what people say publicly but also the assumptions they quietly live by.

"'Who will bring me down to the ground?'"
This was the attitude behind Edom's confidence. They believed no enemy could reach them. Their question was not one of curiosity but of arrogance. They assumed they were beyond the reach of destruction and judgment.

The irony is striking. They asked, "Who will bring me down?" In the very next verse, God answers: "I will bring you down." (Obadiah 1:4).

Remark(s)

Pride often begins by trusting external things and what God has given us more than God Himself. Edom's mountains were real advantages, but they became dangerous when they replaced dependence on the Lord. Any strength—our abilities, success, possessions, knowledge, or position—can become a source of false security when we begin to believe, "Nothing can bring me down." The safest place is not the highest mountain but humble dependence on God.

This verse also reveals an important biblical principle: the greatest danger is often not the weakness we recognize but the strength we trust. Edom did not fall because its cliffs failed; it fell because its heart did..

Obadiah 1:4

NKJV

Obadiah 1:4 Though you ascend as high as the eagle, And though you set your nest among the stars, From there I will bring you down," says the LORD. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:4 But even if you soar as high as eaglesand build your nest among the stars,I will bring you crashing down,”says the LORD. (NLT)

Main Idea

No position is too high and no fortress too secure to place anyone beyond God's reach.

Verse Explanation

"Though you ascend as high as the eagle..."
The eagle was known for building its nest in inaccessible places high on rocky cliffs. God continues the imagery from the previous verse, describing Edom as thinking it had risen above every possible threat. Their lofty position symbolized both their physical security and their pride.

"And though you set your nest among the stars..."
This is deliberate hyperbole. God is not saying Edom literally lived among the stars but emphasizing the highest position imaginable. Even if they could establish themselves beyond the reach of every earthly enemy, they would still not be beyond God's reach.

The image also exposes the ambition of pride. Pride is never satisfied with simply being secure; it continually seeks to exalt itself higher.

"From there I will bring you down..."
This is God's answer to Edom's question in verse 3: "Who will bring me down to the ground?" The answer is simple: God Himself. No height can protect those whom God has determined to humble.

Notice again that God does not say, "Someone will bring you down," but "I will bring you down." The issue is no longer Edom's military strength versus another nation's strength. The issue is Edom versus the Lord.

"...says the LORD."
The verse closes with the authority of the Lord Himself. This is not an empty threat or a human prediction. The God who rules over heaven and earth has spoken, and His word will surely come to pass..

Remark(s)

Pride always believes it has found a place where accountability no longer applies. Whether through wealth, success, influence, intelligence, or power, people can begin to think they are untouchable. Obadiah reminds us that there is no position so high that God cannot reach it. The higher pride lifts a person, the farther there is to fall.

At the same time, this verse is a comfort to God's people. When evil seems entrenched, powerful, and beyond anyone's ability to confront, God assures us that no fortress and no one is beyond His reach. No one is too high for His justice, and no situation is beyond His control.

Obadiah 1:5

NKJV

Obadiah 1:5 "If thieves had come to you, If robbers by night"” Oh, how you will be cut off!"” Would they not have stolen till they had enough? If grape-gatherers had come to you, Would they not have left some gleanings? (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:5 “If thieves came at night and robbed you(what a disaster awaits you!),they would not take everything.Those who harvest grapesalways leave a few for the poor.But your enemies will wipe you out completely! (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom's destruction would be so complete that it would far exceed the losses normally caused by thieves or harvesters.

Verse Explanation

"If thieves had come to you, if robbers by night..."
God begins with a comparison everyone would understand. Thieves steal only what they want or can carry. Even the most determined robbers leave something behind.

"Oh, how you will be cut off!"
This sudden exclamation interrupts the comparison to emphasize the shocking nature of Edom's coming destruction. The phrase "cut off" speaks of being ruined or destroyed. God pauses, as it were, to highlight how devastating the judgment will be.

"Would they not have stolen till they had enough?"
Ordinary thieves stop when they have taken what satisfies them. They are motivated by greed, not total destruction. They do not usually strip a place completely bare.

"If grape-gatherers had come to you..."
The second illustration comes from the harvest. Under God's law, Israel's farmers were instructed not to harvest every grape but to leave some behind for the poor and the foreigner (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 24:21). Even apart from that law, grape harvesters normally missed some fruit.

"Would they not have left some gleanings?"
A vineyard is never harvested to absolute completion. Some grapes always remain. God uses this familiar picture to say that Edom's judgment would be unlike a normal harvest. Nothing of significance would be left behind.

Verse 6 explains the point directly: Edom's hidden treasures would be searched out and taken away.

Remark(s)

Edom believed it could never lose everything. God tells them to imagine the worst theft they can think of, and then says His judgment will be more complete than that.

Obadiah 1:6

NKJV

Obadiah 1:6 "Oh, how Esau shall be searched out! How his hidden treasures shall be sought after! (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:6 Every nook and cranny of Edomwill be searched and looted.Every treasure will be found and taken. (NLT)

Main Idea

God declares that Edom's wealth and carefully hidden resources will be completely discovered and taken away.

Verse Explanation

"Oh, how Esau shall be searched out!"
God continues the thought from verse 5. The name Esau is used here to represent the nation of Edom, his descendants. The picture is no longer of ordinary thieves, who take only what they can find easily, but of an enemy conducting a thorough search. Every hiding place would be examined. Every place Edom thought was secure would be exposed.

The use of Esau's name also reminds the reader that this judgment falls upon the descendants of Jacob's own brother, preparing for the repeated emphasis later in the chapter that Edom sinned against "your brother Jacob."

"How his hidden treasures shall be sought after!"
Edom was strategically located along major trade routes and had accumulated considerable wealth. It also had the advantage of mountain caves and rock fortresses where valuables could be hidden from invading armies.

But what Edom considered safely concealed would not remain hidden. The invading forces would search diligently until they uncovered every valuable possession. The emphasis is not simply on losing wealth but on the complete removal of everything Edom trusted for security and prosperity.

This fulfills the comparison of verses 5–6. Unlike thieves who leave something behind, God's judgment would leave nothing untouched.

Remark(s)

People often hide what they value most because they believe it provides security. Edom hid its treasures in the rocks, but no hiding place can protect what God has determined to expose.

This verse also reminds us that God sees what others cannot. Things that are hidden from people are never hidden from Him. Whether hidden wealth, secret pride, or concealed sin, everything lies open before the Lord. The safest place is not where we can hide our treasures, but where our treasure is entrusted to God.

Jesus echoed this principle when He taught, "For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21). Edom's hidden treasures revealed where its confidence rested—and those treasures could not save it.

Obadiah 1:7

NKJV

Obadiah 1:7 All the men in your confederacy Shall force you to the border; The men at peace with you Shall deceive you and prevail against you. Those who eat your bread shall lay a trap for you. No one is aware of it. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:7 “All your allies will turn against you.They will help to chase you from your land.They will promise you peacewhile plotting to deceive and destroy you.Your trusted friends will set traps for you,and you won’t even know about it. (NLT)

Main Idea

The very allies Edom trusted would betray them, showing that human alliances cannot provide security when God has determined to judge.

Verse Explanation

"All the men in your confederacy shall force you to the border..."
Edom had formed alliances with neighboring nations for protection and mutual benefit. God declares that these allies would not rescue Edom but would instead drive them to the border, either expelling them from their land or abandoning them to their enemies. Those whom Edom expected to stand beside them would instead help bring about their downfall.

"The men at peace with you shall deceive you and prevail against you."
These were nations with whom Edom had peaceful treaties and friendly relations. Yet their peace was only temporary and self-serving. When circumstances changed, they turned against Edom. The nation that trusted in political agreements discovered that human promises are only as dependable as the people making them.

"Those who eat your bread shall lay a trap for you."
In the ancient Near East, sharing a meal symbolized friendship, loyalty, and peace. To eat someone's bread and then betray them was considered a particularly grievous act of treachery. God says that even Edom's closest companions would secretly plot against them.

The phrase "lay a trap" literally refers to placing a snare or hidden obstacle. Edom would not recognize the danger until it was too late.

"No one is aware of it."
The likely refers to Edom's lack of understanding. They were blind to the betrayal unfolding around them. Pride had given them such confidence in their position and alliances that they failed to recognize the danger until judgment had already begun.

Remark(s)

One of pride's greatest deceptions is believing we have more dependable allies and relationships than we actually do. Edom trusted its strategic relationships, only to discover that human loyalties can quickly change.

This verse also prepares us for a greater biblical contrast. Human friendships sometimes fail, promises are broken, and allies disappoint us. But the Lord never abandons those who trust in Him. When every earthly support proves unreliable, God remains completely faithful. As David wrote, "It is better to trust in the LORD than to put confidence in princes" (Psalm 118:9).

Obadiah 1:8

NKJV

Obadiah 1:8 "Will I not in that day," says the LORD, "Even destroy the wise men from Edom, And understanding from the mountains of Esau? (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:8 At that time not a single wise personwill be left in the whole land of Edom,”says the LORD.“For on the mountains of EdomI will destroy everyone who has understanding. (NLT)

Main Idea

God declares that He will remove Edom's celebrated wisdom, proving that human intelligence cannot stand against His judgment.

Verse Explanation

"Will I not in that day," says the LORD..."
The phrase "in that day" refers to the time God has appointed for Edom's judgment. The rhetorical question expects only one answer: Yes. What follows is certain because the Lord Himself has declared it.

"Even destroy the wise men from Edom..."
Edom was renowned for its wise men. The nearby region of Teman, mentioned in the next verse, was especially known for wisdom. Other prophets also speak of Edom's reputation for wise counselors (Jeremiah 49:7).

God is not merely saying these men will die in battle. He is declaring that the wisdom in which Edom trusted will completely fail. Their best advisors will have no answer when God's judgment comes.

"And understanding from the mountains of Esau?"
The "mountains of Esau" refers to the mountainous homeland of Edom. Throughout the chapter, the mountains symbolize both Edom's physical strength and its sense of security.

God now strikes at another foundation of Edom's confidence. Earlier He exposed their trust in their location (vv. 3–4). Then He showed the failure of their wealth (vv. 5–6) and their allies (v. 7). Now He removes their wisdom. Every source of human confidence is systematically stripped away.

Remark(s)

God is not opposed to wisdom—true wisdom comes from Him. He is opposed to self-sufficient and carnal wisdom that refuses to acknowledge Him.

Obadiah shows that Edom trusted four things: its position, its wealth, its alliances, and its wisdom. One by one, God demonstrates that none of them can save a nation that stands in pride against Him.

Knowledge, experience, and careful planning are valuable gifts from God, but they become dangerous when they lead us to believe we no longer need Him. The wisest person is not the one who knows the most, but the one who fears the Lord, for "the fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (Proverbs 9:10).

Obadiah 1:9

NKJV

Obadiah 1:9 Then your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed, To the end that everyone from the mountains of Esau May be cut off by slaughter. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:9 The mightiest warriors of Temanwill be terrified,and everyone on the mountains of Edomwill be cut down in the slaughter. (NLT)

Main Idea

Even Edom's strongest warriors will lose courage, and the nation will suffer complete military defeat under God's judgment.

Verse Explanation

"Then your mighty men..."
God now turns from Edom's wisdom to its military strength. The "mighty men" were Edom's trained warriors, the soldiers in whom the nation placed its confidence for protection. In times of war, these were the men expected to defend the nation and inspire courage in others.

"O Teman..."
Teman was one of Edom's principal cities or regions and was especially known for its wise men (Jeremiah 49:7). Here it likely represents the whole nation. By addressing Teman, God speaks to the heart of Edom's strength and reputation.

"...shall be dismayed..."
The word means to be terrified, shattered, or lose heart. Before the battle is even won, Edom's courage will collapse. Throughout Scripture, fear is often one of the first signs of God's judgment on an army. Victory is not determined merely by weapons or numbers but by the Lord who gives or removes courage.

"To the end that everyone from the mountains of Esau may be cut off by slaughter."
The purpose of the collapse of Edom's warriors is the complete defeat of the nation. The "mountains of Esau" again refer to Edom's homeland. The people who believed their mountain fortresses guaranteed safety would instead perish there.

The phrase "cut off" points to decisive destruction. This concludes the opening section of the prophecy (vv. 1–9), showing that every source of Edom's confidence has failed.

Remark(s)

The progression of verses 3–9 is striking. God systematically removes every foundation of Edom's security:

  • Their position could not protect them (vv. 3–4).

  • Their wealth could not save them (vv. 5–6).

  • Their alliances betrayed them (v. 7).

  • Their wisdom failed them (v. 8).

  • Their military strength collapsed (v. 9).

What remains when everything we trust is stripped away? That question lies at the heart of Obadiah. The chapter reminds us that any confidence built apart from God will eventually fail. Lasting security is found not in what we possess or accomplish, but in the Lord alone.

This progression is one of the literary strengths of Obadiah. Rather than simply announcing judgment, God exposes layer after layer of false confidence until only one conclusion remains: there is no refuge from God except in God Himself.

Obadiah 1:10

NKJV

Obadiah 1:10 "For violence against your brother Jacob, Shame shall cover you, And you shall be cut off forever. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:10 “Because of the violence you didto your close relatives in Israel,you will be filled with shameand destroyed forever. (NLT)

Main Idea

God reveals the fundamental reason for Edom's judgment: they committed violence against their own relatives and would therefore experience lasting disgrace and destruction.

Verse Explanation

"For violence against your brother Jacob..."
This verse marks a turning point in the prophecy. In verses 1–9, God announced what He would do to Edom. Beginning here, He explains why.

The word "for" introduces the reason for the judgment.

God describes Judah as "your brother Jacob." This is a reminder of the relationship between the two nations. Israel descended from Jacob, while Edom descended from Esau, Jacob's twin brother (Genesis 25). Although generations had passed, God still viewed them as brother nations. Edom's sin was therefore more than hostility toward another country—it was betrayal of family.

The word "violence" is broad. It includes physical violence, oppression, injustice, and cruel treatment. The verses that follow (11–14) explain exactly what this violence looked like: standing by while Jerusalem was attacked, rejoicing over Judah's calamity, looting the city, and capturing those trying to escape.

"Shame shall cover you..."
Edom sought honor and security, but God declares that disgrace will become its covering instead. Instead of being admired, the nation would become known for its treachery and cruelty.

The image of being "covered" with shame suggests complete public humiliation. The nation that exalted itself would be humbled before all.

"And you shall be cut off forever."
This speaks of Edom's end as a nation. While individual Edomites continued to exist for a time, the nation itself would disappear from history. God's judgment would be permanent.

This also forms a striking contrast with Israel. Though Judah would suffer exile and discipline, God promised to restore His people. Edom, however, would not receive that national restoration because of its persistent pride and violence.

Remark(s)

The first reason God gives for judging Edom is not their pride but their treatment of others. Pride had produced violence, betrayal, and indifference toward people God loved.

Notice that God does not say merely, "You sinned against Judah," but "your brother Jacob." He intentionally reminds Edom of the relationship they had forgotten. Sin often begins when we stop seeing people as God sees them. When others become obstacles, rivals, or opportunities instead of people made in God's image, cruelty becomes much easier.

This verse also reveals something about God's heart. He does not overlook how people treat one another. The Lord identifies with the oppressed, remembers acts of injustice long after the world has forgotten them, and will one day bring every wrong into perfect judgment.

Obadiah 1:11

NKJV

Obadiah 1:11 In the day that you stood on the other side"” In the day that strangers carried captive his forces, When foreigners entered his gates And cast lots for Jerusalem"” Even you were as one of them. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:11 When they were invaded,you stood aloof, refusing to help them.Foreign invaders carried off their wealthand cast lots to divide up Jerusalem,but you acted like one of Israel’s enemies. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom became guilty not only by what it did, but by what it refused to do. By standing by while Jerusalem was attacked, it identified itself with Judah's enemies.

Verse Explanation

"In the day that you stood on the other side..."
God now begins describing Edom's specific sins. The first is striking because it is a sin of indifference. Edom did not initially attack Jerusalem. Instead, it stood by, watching the disaster unfold without offering help.

The expression "stood on the other side" means more than remaining physically distant. It describes choosing not to stand with their brothers in their time of greatest need. They deliberately remained spectators while God's people suffered.

"In the day that strangers carried captive his forces..."
Foreign invaders defeated Judah's army and carried many people away into captivity. This most likely refers to Jerusalem's fall to Babylon in 586 B.C. As Judah's strength was broken and its people were taken away, Edom watched.

"When foreigners entered his gates..."
Jerusalem's gates represented the city's strength, government, and security. Once foreign armies entered the gates, the city had fallen. The place that once provided protection was now occupied by enemies.

"And cast lots for Jerusalem..."
To cast lots was to divide property or determine possession. The conquerors treated Jerusalem as conquered spoil, dividing its wealth and possessions among themselves as though the city belonged to them.

"Even you were as one of them."
This is God's verdict.

Edom may have thought, "We didn't invade the city. We didn't start the war." But God says their neutrality made them partners with the invaders.

By refusing to help, they sided with those who harmed Judah. In God's eyes, standing by while evil is done—when we have the opportunity and responsibility to act—is not neutrality. It is participation.

Remark(s)

This verse teaches one of the most sobering principles in Obadiah: there are times when failing to do good is itself a serious sin.

Edom was condemned before it ever looted Jerusalem or captured fugitives. Their first offense was simply standing by. They watched the suffering of their own brothers and chose self-preservation over compassion.

Scripture consistently teaches this principle. James writes, "Therefore, to him who knows to do good and does not do it, to him it is sin" (James 4:17).

Obadiah reminds us that God calls His people not merely to avoid doing evil, but to stand with those who suffer. Indifference in the face of injustice is never the posture of the people of God. This is why verse 11 becomes the foundation for the escalating sins described in verses 12–14: once Edom chose not to stand with Judah, it became increasingly easy to stand against Judah.

Obadiah 1:12

NKJV

Obadiah 1:12 "But you should not have gazed on the day of your brother In the day of his captivity; Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah In the day of their destruction; Nor should you have spoken proudly In the day of distress. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:12 “You should not have gloatedwhen they exiled your relatives to distant lands.You should not have rejoicedwhen the people of Judah suffered such misfortune.You should not have spoken arrogantlyin that terrible time of trouble. (NLT)

Main Idea

God condemns Edom for taking pleasure in Judah's suffering instead of responding with compassion.

Verse Explanation

"But you should not have gazed on the day of your brother..."
The phrase "you should not have" is repeated throughout verses 12–14, emphasizing the actions Edom deliberately chose to take. The word "gazed" means more than simply seeing what happened. It carries the idea of looking with satisfaction, lingering over another person's misfortune, or watching without compassion.

Again God calls Judah "your brother." This reminds Edom that the people suffering were not strangers but their own relatives. Their lack of compassion made their sin even more serious.

"In the day of his captivity..."
This refers to the time when Judah was conquered and many of its people were taken into exile. Instead of grieving over the tragedy, Edom watched it unfold with satisfaction.

"Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah in the day of their destruction..."
Edom's sin became even worse. They did not merely watch—they celebrated. The destruction of Jerusalem became a cause for joy rather than sorrow.

This reveals the depth of bitterness that had developed between the two nations. They found happiness in the suffering of others instead of grieving over it.

"Nor should you have spoken proudly in the day of distress."
The phrase suggests boasting, mocking, or speaking arrogantly while Judah suffered. Rather than offering comfort, Edom used Judah's weakness as an opportunity to exalt themselves.

Their words reflected the pride already exposed in verses 3–4. Proud hearts often produce proud speech, especially when others fall.

Remark(s)

There is a clear progression in Edom's sin:

  • They stood by while Judah suffered (v. 11).

  • They looked with satisfaction at the suffering (v. 12a).

  • They rejoiced over it (v. 12b).

  • They boasted and mocked Judah's distress (v. 12c).

Sin rarely remains stationary. A heart that refuses compassion can gradually become a heart that delights in another person's downfall.

This verse calls us to examine our own hearts. It is possible to avoid causing someone's suffering while secretly enjoying their failure. Scripture calls us to the opposite spirit: "Rejoice with those who rejoice, and weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15). God's people are never to celebrate what grieves Him. Even when someone has sinned or fallen under God's discipline, our response should be humility, compassion, and prayer—not satisfaction at their pain.

One of the clearest marks of spiritual maturity is grieving over the failures of others rather than finding pleasure in them.

Obadiah 1:13

NKJV

Obadiah 1:13 You should not have entered the gate of My people In the day of their calamity. Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction In the day of their calamity, Nor laid hands on their substance In the day of their calamity. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:13 You should not have plundered the land of Israelwhen they were suffering such calamity.You should not have gloated over their destructionwhen they were suffering such calamity.You should not have seized their wealthwhen they were suffering such calamity. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom's sin progressed from watching Judah's suffering to actively taking advantage of it by entering the conquered city and plundering its possessions.

Verse Explanation

"You should not have entered the gate of My people..."
The repeated phrase "you should not have" continues God's indictment. Edom's involvement has now intensified. They no longer remain outside watching—they enter Jerusalem after its defeat.

Notice that God calls Judah "My people." Although Judah had come under God's discipline because of its sin, they still belonged to Him. Their discipline did not cancel His covenant relationship with them. Anyone who took advantage of their weakness was opposing God's own people.

"In the day of their calamity..."
This phrase is repeated three times in this verse, emphasizing that Edom exploited Judah at its lowest moment. Rather than showing mercy during a national tragedy, they chose to profit from it.

"Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction..."
The idea from verse 12 is repeated, but now it occurs inside the city. Edom did not simply hear about Jerusalem's suffering from a distance; they entered the ruined city and looked upon the devastation firsthand with satisfaction rather than compassion.

"Nor laid hands on their substance..."
This is outright plunder. The expression "laid hands on" means to seize or take possession of something that belongs to another.

Edom was no longer merely a spectator or an encourager of Babylon's invasion. They became participants by taking Judah's property for themselves. They saw another nation's tragedy as their opportunity for personal gain.

Remark(s)

The progression of Edom's sin continues to unfold:

  • They stood by (v. 11).

  • They looked with satisfaction (v. 12a).

  • They rejoiced (v. 12b).

  • They boasted (v. 12c).

  • They entered the city (v. 13a).

  • They plundered its wealth (v. 13b).

This is an important biblical pattern. Sin that begins in the heart often ends in actions. What started as pride became indifference, then delight in another's suffering, and finally open exploitation.

There is another beautiful truth hidden in this verse. Even while God was disciplining Judah, He still called them "My people." God's correction is never abandonment. He may discipline His children, but He never stops claiming them as His own. Others may mistake God's discipline as permission to mistreat His people, but God does not. Those whom He disciplines remain under His care and protection. That is one of the great comforts of this passage.

Obadiah 1:14

NKJV

Obadiah 1:14 You should not have stood at the crossroads To cut off those among them who escaped; Nor should you have delivered up those among them who remained In the day of distress. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:14 You should not have stood at the crossroads,killing those who tried to escape.You should not have captured the survivorsand handed them over in their terrible time of trouble. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom's betrayal reached its lowest point when it hunted down Judah's refugees and handed survivors over to the enemy.

Verse Explanation

"You should not have stood at the crossroads..."
God's indictment reaches its climax. Edom was no longer simply watching, rejoicing, or plundering. They deliberately positioned themselves where fleeing Judeans would pass in order to intercept them.

The crossroads were strategic intersections where refugees escaping Jerusalem would naturally travel. Edom intentionally took advantage of their desperate situation.

"To cut off those among them who escaped..."
The phrase "cut off" likely means to capture or kill the fugitives attempting to flee. These were not soldiers still fighting in battle but people trying to escape with their lives after Jerusalem had fallen.

Instead of providing refuge to their relatives, Edom became another enemy they had to fear.

"Nor should you have delivered up those among them who remained..."
Some survivors escaped the initial destruction only to be betrayed by Edom. The expression "delivered up" means to hand them over to the conquering army, most likely the Babylonians.

This was an act of deliberate treachery. Rather than protecting vulnerable people, Edom actively cooperated with those who sought to capture them.

"In the day of distress."
Once again, God emphasizes Judah's helpless condition. They were already experiencing the discipline of God through Babylon's invasion. Edom exploited that moment instead of showing mercy.

Verses 11–14 now present a complete picture of Edom's guilt. They progressed from passive indifference to active participation in Judah's destruction.

Remark(s)

The progression of Edom's sin is one of the clearest lessons in the book:

  • They stood by while Jerusalem fell (v. 11).

  • They looked with satisfaction (v. 12).

  • They rejoiced and mocked (v. 12).

  • They entered the city and plundered it (v. 13).

  • They captured and betrayed those trying to escape (v. 14).

Sin rarely remains where it begins. A heart that tolerates indifference can gradually become capable of cruelty. The outward acts in verses 13–14 were the fruit of the proud, hardened heart God exposed in verse 3.

There is also a sobering contrast with God's character. Throughout Scripture, God is repeatedly described as a refuge for those in distress. Edom did the opposite. They turned the place of escape into a place of capture. God's people are called to reflect His heart by protecting the vulnerable, helping those in trouble, and refusing to take advantage of people at their weakest.

This final act explains why God's judgment was so severe. Edom did not merely fail to help—they actively prevented others from escaping suffering.

Obadiah 1:15

NKJV

Obadiah 1:15 "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near; As you have done, it shall be done to you; Your reprisal shall return upon your own head. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:15 “The day is near when I, the LORD,will judge all godless nations!As you have done to Israel,so it will be done to you.All your evil deedswill fall back on your own heads. (NLT)

Main Idea

God expands His message from Edom alone to all nations, declaring that His righteous judgment will repay every person and nation according to their deeds.

Verse Explanation

"For the day of the LORD..."
This marks another major turning point in the book. Up to this point, Obadiah has focused on Edom's sins and coming judgment. Now the prophecy widens to include all the nations.

The Day of the LORD is the time when God openly intervenes in history to judge evil, vindicate His people, and establish His righteous rule. Sometimes the prophets use the phrase for historical acts of judgment, such as the fall of a nation. At other times, it points to the future day when Christ returns to judge the world and establish His kingdom. Here, Edom's judgment becomes an example of a much larger principle that applies to every nation.

"...upon all the nations is near..."
The focus is no longer only on Edom. Every nation is accountable to God. The word "near" emphasizes certainty rather than simply chronology. From God's perspective, His appointed time of judgment is fixed and cannot be delayed or avoided.

"As you have done, it shall be done to you..."
This is one of the Bible's clearest statements of the principle of divine retribution. Edom would experience the very kind of treatment it had inflicted on others.

This is not revenge driven by emotion. It is perfect justice administered by God. Those who showed no mercy would receive no mercy. Those who betrayed others would themselves be betrayed.

The same principle is found throughout Scripture:

  • "Whoever digs a pit will fall into it" (Proverbs 26:27).

  • "Whatever a man sows, that he will also reap" (Galatians 6:7).

"Your reprisal shall return upon your own head."
The word "reprisal" refers to the consequences or repayment for one's actions. The image of something returning upon your own head means that Edom's own deeds would come back upon them. They would reap the harvest of the seeds they had sown.

Remark(s)

This verse explains why God devoted an entire book to Edom. The issue is much bigger than one ancient nation. Edom becomes a living illustration of how God governs His moral universe.

The principle is timeless: what we give to others eventually returns to us under God's righteous government. That should sober us in how we treat people. Mercy, kindness, humility, and generosity matter because God sees them. So do pride, cruelty, betrayal, and indifference.

At the same time, this verse offers tremendous comfort to those who have suffered injustice. They do not have to take revenge into their own hands. God has not forgotten. Every wrong will be answered with perfect justice at the proper time.

For believers, there is one more glorious truth. We deserve the judgment described in this verse, yet at the cross our reprisal fell upon Christ instead of upon us. Jesus bore the judgment we deserved so that all who trust Him might receive mercy instead of condemnation. That does not cancel God's justice—it displays it perfectly while extending grace to sinners.

Obadiah 1:16

NKJV

Obadiah 1:16 For as you drank on My holy mountain, So shall all the nations drink continually; Yes, they shall drink, and swallow, And they shall be as though they had never been.(NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:16 Just as you swallowed up my peopleon my holy mountain,so you and the surrounding nationswill swallow the punishment I pour out on you.Yes, all you nations will drink and staggerand disappear from history. (NLT)

Main Idea

Just as Edom celebrated Judah's fall in Jerusalem, all nations that oppose God will one day drink the full cup of His judgment.

Verse Explanation

"For as you drank on My holy mountain..."
The image of drinking has both a literal and symbolic meaning.

After Jerusalem fell, Edom likely entered the city, celebrated the victory with feasting and drinking, and rejoiced over Judah's humiliation. Their drinking expressed triumph, self-confidence, and delight in another nation's downfall.

At the same time, Scripture often uses the cup as a symbol of God's judgment (Psalm 75:8; Isaiah 51:17; Jeremiah 25:15). Obadiah intentionally moves from Edom's literal celebration to the symbolic cup of divine wrath.

Notice that God calls Jerusalem "My holy mountain." Although He had disciplined Judah, Jerusalem still belonged to Him. Edom celebrated on ground that God claimed as His own.

"So shall all the nations drink continually..."
The prophecy now broadens beyond Edom. The judgment first announced against Edom becomes a pattern for all nations that proudly oppose God and mistreat His people.

This time, however, the nations are not drinking wine in celebration—they are drinking the cup of God's judgment.

"Yes, they shall drink, and swallow..."
The repetition emphasizes the certainty and completeness of the judgment. The nations will not merely taste God's wrath; they will drink it fully. There will be no escape and no possibility of refusing the cup He gives.

"And they shall be as though they had never been."
This describes the complete overthrow of the rebellious nations. Their pride, power, and kingdoms will disappear from history. The nations that exalted themselves against God will have no lasting place in His coming kingdom.

Remark(s)

The image of the cup becomes one of the great themes of Scripture.

In Obadiah, the rebellious nations drink the cup of God's judgment.

But in the Garden of Gethsemane, Jesus prayed, "O My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me" (Matthew 26:39). The cup He spoke of was not physical suffering alone—it was the cup of God's wrath against sin.

On the cross, Jesus drank that cup completely for all who trust in Him. Because He exhausted God's judgment, believers no longer face the cup of wrath but instead receive "the cup of salvation" (Psalm 116:13).

Obadiah's warning therefore points beyond Edom to the gospel itself. Every person will drink one of two cups: either the cup of God's judgment personally, or the salvation purchased because Christ drank that cup in our place.

This verse also reminds us that God has the final celebration. Edom feasted over Jerusalem's fall, believing evil had won. In the end, however, their feast was temporary, while God's kingdom is eternal. Those who rejoice in wickedness will find their joy short-lived, but those who belong to Christ will one day share in the marriage supper of the Lamb.

Obadiah 1:17

NKJV

Obadiah 1:17 "But on Mount Zion there shall be deliverance, And there shall be holiness; The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:17 “But Jerusalem will become a refuge for those who escape;it will be a holy place.And the people of Israel will come backto reclaim their inheritance. (NLT)

Main Idea

After announcing judgment on the nations, God promises that His people will experience deliverance, restoration, and the full recovery of the inheritance He has promised them.

Verse Explanation

"But on Mount Zion..."
The word "But" marks one of the great turning points in Obadiah. After describing the judgment awaiting Edom and the nations (vv. 15–16), God now turns to hope for His people.

Mount Zion represents Jerusalem, God's dwelling place among His people, and ultimately His kingdom. The place where judgment had fallen would also become the place from which restoration would come.

"...there shall be deliverance..."
The word can also mean escape or those who escape. Judah had suffered conquest and exile, but God promises that destruction will not have the final word. A remnant will survive, and God will preserve His people.

Historically, this began with the return from Babylonian exile. Ultimately, it points to the complete salvation God provides through the Messiah and the final restoration of His kingdom.

"And there shall be holiness..."
This is more than the rebuilding of a city. God promises that Zion will once again be set apart for Him.

Holiness speaks of God's presence, His worship, and a people restored to fellowship with Him. The goal of God's deliverance is not simply to rescue His people from danger but to restore them to Himself.

"The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions."
Earlier, Edom helped strip Judah of its land and wealth. Now God promises reversal. His people will recover what rightly belongs to them.

This includes the restoration of Israel's inheritance in the land, but it also reflects a larger biblical theme: God restores what His people lose through judgment and oppression. What God has promised cannot ultimately be stolen away.

Remark(s)

This verse beautifully balances the message of Obadiah. The book is not only about judgment; it is equally about restoration.

Notice the three promises:

  • Deliverance — God rescues His people.

  • Holiness — God restores His relationship with His people.

  • Inheritance — God returns what belongs to His people.

That pattern reaches its fulfillment in Christ. Through Him we are delivered from sin, made holy through His righteousness, and given an inheritance that can never perish or be taken away (1 Peter 1:3–5).

There is another beautiful contrast with Edom. Edom sought to take another's inheritance and lost everything. The house of Jacob waited upon the Lord, and God Himself restored their inheritance. God's way has always been this: we do not secure our future by grasping what is not ours, but by trusting Him to give what He has promised.

This verse also reveals God's heart. His discipline is never His final word to His people. Judgment may come for a season, but for those who belong to Him, restoration is His ultimate purpose. That truth echoes throughout Scripture and reaches its fullest expression in Jesus Christ, who restores far more than sin has taken away.

Obadiah 1:18

NKJV

Obadiah 1:18 The house of Jacob shall be a fire, And the house of Joseph a flame; But the house of Esau shall be stubble; They shall kindle them and devour them, And no survivor shall remain of the house of Esau," For the LORD has spoken. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:18 The people of Israel will be a raging fire,and Edom a field of dry stubble.The descendants of Joseph will be a flameroaring across the field, devouring everything.There will be no survivors in Edom.I, the LORD, have spoken! (NLT)

Main Idea

God promises that the restored people of Israel will become the instrument of His judgment against Edom, bringing the nation's pride to a complete end.

Verse Explanation

"The house of Jacob shall be a fire, and the house of Joseph a flame..."
The imagery changes dramatically. In the previous verse, God promised deliverance, holiness, and restored inheritance. Now He describes His restored people as fire and flame.

The house of Jacob refers primarily to Judah, while the house of Joseph represents the northern tribes of Israel. Together they picture the reunited people of God. The point is not that they possess power in themselves, but that God will work through His restored people to accomplish His purposes.

Fire is often used in Scripture as a symbol of God's judgment because it consumes what cannot endure His holiness.

"But the house of Esau shall be stubble..."
Stubble is the dry stalk left after grain has been harvested. It burns quickly and completely. God contrasts the strength He gives His people with the utter weakness of Edom. The nation that once seemed as secure as mountain fortresses is now compared to dry straw before a fire.

The contrast is intentional:

  • God's people are the fire.

  • Edom is the fuel.

"They shall kindle them and devour them..."
The fire ignites the stubble until nothing remains. Historically, Edom gradually disappeared through conquest and assimilation, eventually vanishing as a distinct nation.

The language also points beyond history to God's final victory over every kingdom that exalts itself against Him.

"And no survivor shall remain of the house of Esau..."
This completes the judgment first announced in verse 10. Edom's national existence would come to an end.

This does not mean every individual descended from Esau died immediately, but that the nation itself would ultimately disappear from history. Unlike Israel, which God repeatedly restored, Edom would never be restored as a nation.

"For the LORD has spoken."
The certainty of the prophecy rests not on military strength or political developments but on the authority of God's word. Because the Lord has spoken, the outcome is certain.

Remark(s)

The imagery of fire and stubble teaches an important biblical principle. The outcome of every conflict ultimately depends on God's presence, not human strength.

Earlier in the chapter, Edom appeared strong and secure while Judah appeared defeated. By the end of the book, those roles are completely reversed. The people God restores become the fire, while the proud nation becomes the stubble.

This reversal also points forward to Christ. The rejected and crucified Messiah became the victorious King through whom God establishes His everlasting kingdom. The God who overturned Edom's expectations ultimately overturned the greatest human expectation of all by bringing life through the cross and the empty tomb.

There is also a Christ-centered fulfillment here. Jesus Christ will one day bring every proud kingdom to an end and establish His own everlasting kingdom. Every power that resists Him will ultimately become like stubble before His righteous judgment. But those who belong to Him are not consumed by that fire because He has already borne God's judgment in their place. The same Lord who judges the proud also saves all who humble themselves and trust in Him.

Obadiah 1:19

NKJV

Obadiah 1:19 The South shall possess the mountains of Esau, And the Lowland shall possess Philistia. They shall possess the fields of Ephraim And the fields of Samaria. Benjamin shall possess Gilead. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:19 “Then my people living in the Negevwill occupy the mountains of Edom.Those living in the foothills of Judahwill possess the Philistine plainsand take over the fields of Ephraim and Samaria.And the people of Benjaminwill occupy the land of Gilead. (NLT)

Main Idea

God promises that His restored people will fully possess the inheritance He has determined for them, reversing the losses they had suffered.

Verse Explanation

"The South shall possess the mountains of Esau..."
The South (the Negev) refers to the southern region of Judah. God promises that those living there will possess the territory once occupied by Edom. This is a direct reversal of Edom's attempt to profit from Judah's downfall. Instead of Edom taking from Judah, Judah will inherit Edom's land.

"And the Lowland shall possess Philistia."
The Lowland (the Shephelah) was the rolling foothill region between Judah's mountains and the Mediterranean coast. The Philistines had long been enemies of Israel. God promises that His people will possess even these long-contested territories.

"They shall possess the fields of Ephraim and the fields of Samaria."
Ephraim and Samaria represent the territory of the former northern kingdom of Israel. After the division of the kingdom following Solomon's reign, Judah and Israel had remained separate for centuries.

This promise looks beyond political division. God foresees a restored people possessing the land together once again. It anticipates the reunification of God's people under His rule.

"Benjamin shall possess Gilead."
Benjamin was the tribe immediately north of Judah. Gilead lay east of the Jordan River and had belonged to several Israelite tribes before foreign powers occupied it.

God is describing a comprehensive restoration. Every direction mentioned—south, west, north, and east—communicates that His people will fully recover the inheritance He intends for them.

The emphasis is not on geography alone but on God's faithfulness to fulfill His promises.

Remark(s)

This verse is about much more than changing national boundaries. It demonstrates a central biblical truth: God restores what belongs to His people.

Throughout Obadiah, Edom sought to enlarge itself by taking advantage of Judah's losses. God's answer is not merely to punish Edom but to restore His people's inheritance.

The repetition of the word "possess" is significant. It appears four times in this single verse. What God's people had temporarily lost would ultimately be recovered because God's promises cannot fail.

The chapter began with Edom trusting what it possessed and losing everything. It now closes by showing God's people, who seemed to have lost everything, receiving back all that God had promised. That is the pattern of God's redemption: what He promises, He ultimately restores.

Obadiah 1:20

NKJV

Obadiah 1:20 And the captives of this host of the children of Israel Shall possess the land of the Canaanites As far as Zarephath. The captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad Shall possess the cities of the South. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:20 The exiles of Israel will return to their landand occupy the Phoenician coast as far north as Zarephath.The captives from Jerusalem exiled in the northwill return home and resettle the towns of the Negev. (NLT)

Main Idea

God promises that even His scattered and exiled people will return and fully possess the inheritance He has prepared for them.

Verse Explanation

"And the captives of this host of the children of Israel..."
The focus shifts to the Israelites who had been taken into exile. Many had been removed from their homeland by foreign powers and appeared to have lost everything. Yet God had not forgotten them.

The word "captives" emphasizes their helpless condition. They had no power to restore themselves. Their future depended entirely on God's faithfulness.

"Shall possess the land of the Canaanites as far as Zarephath."
God promises that the exiles will not merely return—they will possess territory extending northward as far as Zarephath, a town on the Mediterranean coast between Tyre and Sidon.

The emphasis is on the breadth of God's restoration. What seemed permanently lost would be restored according to His promise.

"The captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad..."
The exact location of Sepharad is uncertain. Various suggestions have been made, including a region in Mesopotamia or Asia Minor. The important point is not its precise location but its distance.

Wherever God's people had been scattered, He knew where they were. Distance from Jerusalem did not place them beyond His care or beyond His ability to restore them.

"Shall possess the cities of the South."
Those who had been carried far from home would one day return and possess the cities of the Negev in southern Judah.

This continues the theme of verses 17–19: God's people recover the inheritance He has promised. The exiles are not forgotten, and God's covenant purposes have not failed.

Remark(s)

This verse reveals one of God's most encouraging attributes: He knows where His people are, even when they are scattered.

To human eyes, the exiles seemed forgotten. They were separated from the temple, the land, and one another. Yet God knew their location, remembered His promises, and declared their future long before they returned.

This points to a greater biblical theme. Sin has scattered humanity far from God, but the Good Shepherd gathers His people from every nation. Jesus came "to gather together in one the children of God who were scattered abroad" (John 11:52).

There is also a beautiful pastoral truth here. God's restoration is not limited by geography, circumstances, or the length of time someone has been away. The God who brought Israel home from exile is the same God who seeks the wandering, restores the broken, and finishes what He has promised.

Obadiah is steadily moving toward its final climax. The message is no longer simply that Edom will lose its land, but that God's people will receive their inheritance, and nothing can ultimately prevent Him from fulfilling His word.

Obadiah 1:21

NKJV

Obadiah 1:21 Then saviors shall come to Mount Zion To judge the mountains of Esau, And the kingdom shall be the LORD's. [LORD’S] (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:21 Those who have been rescued will go up to Mount Zion in Jerusalemto rule over the mountains of Edom.And the LORD himself will be king!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Obadiah concludes by declaring that God will establish His righteous rule over all creation, and every opposing kingdom will give way to His everlasting kingdom.

Verse Explanation

"Then saviors shall come to Mount Zion..."
The word "saviors" (or "deliverers") refers to leaders whom God raises up to rescue and govern His people. In Israel's history, judges such as Othniel, Ehud, Gideon, and Samson were sometimes called deliverers because God used them to save Israel from its enemies.

Obadiah is not speaking of many messiahs. These are God's appointed servants who carry out His purposes. Ultimately, they point forward to the one true Savior, Jesus Christ, through whom every act of God's deliverance finds its fulfillment.

"To judge the mountains of Esau..."
The mountains that symbolized Edom's pride and security (vv. 3–4) now become the object of God's judgment.

To judge means to exercise righteous authority, bringing justice where evil once ruled. The place that once appeared untouchable is now fully subject to God's government.

The contrast is striking:

  • Earlier, Edom asked, "Who will bring me down?" (v. 3).

  • Now God's appointed rulers stand over the very mountains Edom trusted.

"And the kingdom shall be the LORD'S."
This is the climax not only of Obadiah but of the entire book's message.

The prophecy begins with one proud nation believing it controls its own destiny. It ends by declaring that God alone is King.

This statement reaches far beyond Edom. Every earthly kingdom rises and falls, but God's kingdom alone is eternal.

Historically, this truth was seen repeatedly as nations came and went while God's purposes continued. Ultimately, it points to the return of Jesus Christ, when every rival kingdom will be brought into submission and He will reign forever.

The final words intentionally leave the reader looking beyond Edom's judgment to God's universal reign.

Remark(s)

This final sentence is one of the greatest declarations in the Old Testament: "The kingdom shall be the LORD'S."

Everything in Obadiah moves toward this conclusion:

  • Pride is judged because the kingdom belongs to the Lord.

  • Injustice is repaid because the kingdom belongs to the Lord.

  • God's people are restored because the kingdom belongs to the Lord.

  • The nations are held accountable because the kingdom belongs to the Lord.

The book begins with Edom asking, "Who will bring me down?" It ends by answering the larger question: Who truly rules the world? The answer is clear: the Lord does.

This verse also points directly to Jesus Christ. After His resurrection, He declared, "All authority has been given to Me in heaven and on earth" (Matthew 28:18). One day, every knee will bow, every tongue will confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, and the prayer He taught His disciples will be fully realized: "Your kingdom come."

Obadiah therefore ends not with judgment, but with hope. The final word of history is not the triumph of proud nations, but the reign of God. Every injustice will be made right, every promise will be fulfilled, and every rival kingdom will pass away. The kingdom shall be the LORD'S.

Overall assessment for MTB

I think Obadiah is one of the strongest chapter studies you've produced so far. A few observations:

  • The progression of Edom's sin (standing by → gazing → rejoicing → boasting → plundering → betraying fugitives) gives the chapter a clear narrative flow rather than treating each verse as isolated.

  • The repeated theme of false securities (position, wealth, alliances, wisdom, military) provides another strong thread through verses 3–9.

  • The transition in verse 15 from Edom to all nations is clearly explained, which is often missed in simpler commentaries.

The only editorial change I would make is to tighten the Christ-centered Remark(s)s in verses 15–21.
