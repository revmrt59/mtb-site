Chapter Study

Obadiah 1

Verse-by-Verse
Obadiah 1:1

NKJV

Obadiah 1:1 The vision of Obadiah. Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom (We have heard a report from the LORD, And a messenger has been sent among the nations, saying, "Arise, and let us rise up against her for battle"): (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:1 This is the vision that the Sovereign LORD revealed to Obadiah concerning the land of Edom. Edom’s Judgment Announced We have heard a message from the LORDthat an ambassador was sent to the nations to say,“Get ready, everyone!Let’s assemble our armies and attack Edom!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Obadiah receives a message from God announcing judgment against Edom. Though Edom feels secure and the nations appear to be acting on their own, the Lord is the One directing events and calling Edom to account.

Verse Explanation

“The vision of Obadiah.”

“Vision” identifies the message as divine revelation rather than Obadiah’s own political analysis or personal opinion. The Hebrew word can refer broadly to a revelation received from God; it does not require that Obadiah saw everything in a literal visual experience. Almost nothing is known about Obadiah himself. His name means “servant of the LORD” or “worshiper of the LORD,” but Scripture gives no additional biographical information that allows us to identify him with certainty.

“Thus says the Lord GOD concerning Edom”

The authority behind the prophecy is immediately clear: “Thus says the Lord GOD.” Obadiah is the messenger; the message belongs to God.

Edom was the nation descended from Esau, Jacob’s brother (Genesis 36:1, 8-9). This family relationship makes what follows especially significant. Edom and Israel were not merely neighboring nations; they were related peoples whose history reached back to two brothers. The rest of Obadiah will expose Edom’s pride, violence, and satisfaction over Judah’s disaster.

“We have heard a report from the LORD”

The plural “we” is not fully explained. It may include Obadiah with God’s people or reflect the prophetic community hearing what God has announced. The important point is the source of the report: it comes “from the LORD.” What is about to happen to Edom is not simply international rumor or political speculation.

“And a messenger has been sent among the nations”

The picture shifts to a messenger moving among the surrounding nations, summoning them to action against Edom. The verse does not identify the messenger, and there is no need to speculate beyond what the text gives us. God is showing that the gathering of the nations against Edom ultimately stands under His sovereign direction.

“Arise, and let us rise up against her for battle”

The nations are called to move against Edom. Edom may appear secure in its mountain strongholds, but its security cannot protect it when God has determined to judge it. This command begins the movement that the following verses will explain: the proud nation that believes itself untouchable will be brought down.

Study Notes

Obadiah is the shortest book in the Old Testament, containing only twenty-one verses, yet its opening immediately establishes the book’s central conflict: the LORD’s judgment of Edom.

The expression “Lord GOD” represents a combination of titles emphasizing God as sovereign Master and the covenant name of the LORD. In English Bibles, the capitalization helps distinguish the divine name from the ordinary title “Lord.”

Obadiah 1:1-4 closely parallels Jeremiah 49:14-16. The relationship between the two passages is clear, although Scripture does not tell us whether one prophet used the other or whether both drew upon prophetic material known to them. The overlap reinforces that Edom’s coming judgment was not an isolated prophetic theme.

Spirit & Life

Edom’s great mistake will be believing that what appears secure to human eyes must actually be secure. Obadiah begins by pulling back the curtain: nations move, messengers travel, and armies gather, but above the visible events stands the Lord GOD. Human strength never places anyone beyond His reach.

There is also something weighty in the simple words “Thus says the Lord GOD.” Before we ask whether God’s Word comforts us, challenges us, or overturns something we have believed, the first question is whether He has spoken. If He has, His Word carries an authority greater than our circumstances, our confidence, or our preferences.

Obadiah 1:2

NKJV

Obadiah 1:2 "Behold, I will make you small among the nations; You shall be greatly despised. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:2 The LORD says to Edom,“I will cut you down to size among the nations;you will be greatly despised. (NLT)

Main Idea

God declares that He will bring proud and seemingly secure Edom down, making the nation small and despised among the surrounding peoples.

Verse Explanation

“Behold, I will make you small among the nations”

“Behold” calls attention to what God is about to declare. Edom may have viewed itself as strong and secure, but the LORD announces a complete reversal: “I will make you small.” The emphasis is on God’s action. Edom’s future will not be determined by how powerful it considers itself but by what God has determined concerning it.

“Small” refers primarily to Edom’s diminished position among the nations. The nation that thought highly of itself would be reduced in power, significance, and standing. This begins a deliberate contrast that continues into the next verses: Edom has an exalted view of itself, but God will bring it low.

“You shall be greatly despised.”

Edom will not merely become weaker; it will lose the honor and standing it once possessed. “Greatly despised” intensifies the reversal. Edom’s pride will be answered by humiliation before the very nations among whom it had sought security and significance.

There is an important irony in the verse: Edom thinks itself great, but God says it will become “small.” Edom possesses a high view of itself, but God says it will become “greatly despised.” God’s assessment, not Edom’s self-assessment, will ultimately prevail.

Study Notes

Obadiah 1:2 is very close to Jeremiah 49:15: “For indeed, I will make you small among nations, despised among men.” This is part of the larger parallel between Obadiah 1:1-4 and Jeremiah 49:14-16 concerning judgment on Edom.

The language of being made “small among the nations” should not be reduced merely to Edom’s physical size or population. In context, the thought includes Edom’s lowered status and significance. Verse 3 immediately identifies the deeper issue behind this judgment: “The pride of your heart has deceived you.”

Spirit & Life

There is a striking difference between being small in our own eyes and being made small by God. Scripture does not condemn humility; it condemns the pride that gives us an inflated view of ourselves and our security. Edom exalted itself, and God announced that He would bring it low.

God sees us as we truly are, not as we have convinced ourselves that we are. That can be sobering, but it is also a mercy. One of the gifts of walking closely with God is allowing His truth to correct our self-perception before pride has to be corrected through humbling circumstances.

Obadiah 1:3

NKJV

Obadiah 1:3 The pride of your heart has deceived you, You who dwell in the clefts of the rock, Whose habitation is high; You who say in your heart, 'Who will bring me down to the ground?' (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:3 You have been deceived by your own pridebecause you live in a rock fortressand make your home high in the mountains.‘Who can ever reach us way up here?’you ask boastfully. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom’s greatest danger was not merely its pride but the deception that pride produced. Its impressive natural defenses made the nation believe it was beyond the reach of judgment.

Verse Explanation

“The pride of your heart has deceived you”

God identifies the heart of Edom’s problem: “pride.” Edom’s confidence had become an exaggerated sense of its own strength and security. But the striking word is “deceived.” Pride did not simply make Edom arrogant; it distorted Edom’s perception of reality. The nation believed something about itself that was not true.

This gives the verse unusual force. Edom’s danger was not only that enemies might attack it. Its deeper danger was that pride had made it incapable of accurately seeing its own vulnerability.

“You who dwell in the clefts of the rock”

Edom occupied the rugged hill country south and southeast of Judah. Its mountainous terrain included steep cliffs, ravines, and naturally defensible positions. “Clefts of the rock” describes the kind of landscape that contributed to Edom’s confidence.

The language should not automatically be treated as a specific reference to Petra. Petra lies within the territory historically associated with Edom and vividly illustrates the region’s rocky terrain, but identifying Obadiah’s words specifically with the later famous city goes beyond what the verse actually says.

“Whose habitation is high”

Edom’s elevated settlements strengthened the impression that it was difficult to attack. Geography had become theology: because Edom occupied strong positions, it began to act as though it were untouchable.

The problem was not the strength of Edom’s defenses. The problem was trusting those defenses as though they placed Edom beyond God’s reach.

“You who say in your heart, ‘Who will bring me down to the ground?’”

Here God exposes what Edom’s pride was saying internally. “Who will bring me down?” is not presented as an honest question but as confident defiance. Edom looked at its position and could not imagine anyone successfully overthrowing it.

The answer comes immediately in verse 4: “I will bring you down,” says the LORD. Edom asks who could possibly do it; God answers that He Himself will.

Study Notes

“The pride of your heart” points beyond outward boasting to an inward attitude. In biblical thought, the “heart” is the inner center of thought, desire, intention, and decision. Edom’s outward confidence grew from an inward conviction about its own security.

Jeremiah 49:16 contains closely parallel language about Edom: “Your fierceness has deceived you, the pride of your heart.” Both passages connect Edom’s pride with the rugged heights it trusted for protection.

Edom’s geography helps the imagery come alive, but the historical setting should not overshadow the verse’s central point. Obadiah is not primarily marveling at Edom’s mountains; he is exposing the false security those mountains helped produce.

Spirit & Life

The penetrating insight in this verse is that pride “has deceived you.” Pride is particularly dangerous because it can hide itself from the person who possesses it. It can make confidence feel like reality, independence feel like strength, and self-sufficiency feel like security.

There is also a warning in Edom turning a genuine strength into a false refuge. Its mountains really were formidable. The deception was not believing the mountains were strong; it was believing their strength made Edom untouchable. Even legitimate gifts, resources, experience, position, or success can become dangerous when our confidence quietly shifts from God to what He has allowed us to possess.

Edom asks, “Who will bring me down to the ground?” The next verse gives the answer. No height is high enough, no refuge strong enough, and no human security great enough to place us beyond the hand of God.

Obadiah 1:4

NKJV

Obadiah 1:4 Though you ascend as high as the eagle, And though you set your nest among the stars, From there I will bring you down," says the LORD. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:4 But even if you soar as high as eaglesand build your nest among the stars,I will bring you crashing down,”says the LORD. (NLT)

Main Idea

No matter how high Edom rises or how secure it believes itself to be, it cannot place itself beyond God’s reach. The LORD Himself will bring the proud nation down.

Verse Explanation

“Though you ascend as high as the eagle”

God takes Edom’s sense of security and pushes it to the extreme. The eagle soars far above the ground and naturally pictures extraordinary height and apparent inaccessibility. Even if Edom could rise that high, it would not escape God.

The imagery also continues the thought of verse 3. Edom lived “in the clefts of the rock” with its “habitation…high.” The nation’s physical elevation had helped produce a false sense of invulnerability. God now declares that even greater heights would make no difference.

“And though you set your nest among the stars”

The language becomes deliberately extravagant. Edom could imagine its “nest” placed among the stars-far beyond the reach of any earthly enemy-and still not be safe from the LORD.

The point is not that Edom literally expected to reach the stars. God is exposing the absurdity of its confidence. There is simply no height to which Edom can climb where God cannot reach it.

“From there I will bring you down”

This directly answers Edom’s boast in verse 3: “Who will bring me down to the ground?” God’s answer is unmistakable: “I will bring you down.”

That contrast is the heart of these verses. Edom says, in effect, “No one can bring us down.” God says, “I can, and I will.” Edom’s security is measured against human enemies; God reminds Edom that its real accountability is to Him.

“says the LORD.”

The judgment rests on the authority of the LORD Himself. This is not Obadiah predicting that Edom’s geography will eventually fail or that another nation will become stronger. God personally declares what He will do.

Study Notes

The eagle and nest imagery is especially fitting after verse 3’s description of Edom’s elevated, rocky dwelling places. Eagles commonly build nests in high, difficult-to-reach locations, making the picture a natural extension of Edom’s confidence in its mountain strongholds.

“Among the stars” is intentional poetic exaggeration. It takes Edom’s confidence to the highest imaginable location in order to make an absolute point: there is nowhere beyond the LORD’s reach.

The wording closely parallels Jeremiah 49:16, where God says concerning Edom, “Though you make your nest as high as the eagle, I will bring you down from there.” Obadiah intensifies the image with “among the stars.”

Spirit & Life

There is a powerful contrast between “you ascend” and “I will bring you down.” Edom can climb, build, fortify, and elevate itself, but human achievement never becomes independence from God.

The verse also exposes the illusion behind self-made security. We naturally look for a place where nothing can touch us-enough money, enough influence, enough preparation, enough control. Those things may have legitimate value, but none can become the ultimate security of our lives. Our safest place is not the height we can reach for ourselves, but humble dependence upon the God whose presence reaches everywhere.

Obadiah 1:5

NKJV

Obadiah 1:5 "If thieves had come to you, If robbers by night"” Oh, how you will be cut off!"” Would they not have stolen till they had enough? If grape-gatherers had come to you, Would they not have left some gleanings? (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:5 “If thieves came at night and robbed you(what a disaster awaits you!),they would not take everything.Those who harvest grapesalways leave a few for the poor.But your enemies will wipe you out completely! (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom’s coming destruction will be unusually thorough. Ordinary thieves or harvesters leave something behind, but the judgment coming upon Edom will leave the nation stripped far more completely.

Verse Explanation

“If thieves had come to you, if robbers by night-Oh, how you will be cut off!”

God begins with the picture of thieves breaking in at night. Even thieves normally take only what they want or can carry. They do not usually remove everything a person owns.

The sudden exclamation “Oh, how you will be cut off!” interrupts the comparison and emphasizes the severity of Edom’s coming destruction. What is coming will be worse than an ordinary robbery. Edom will not merely suffer some loss; its downfall will be devastating.

“Would they not have stolen till they had enough?”

A thief has a natural stopping point. Once the thieves have taken what they want, they leave. Something remains behind.

That ordinary limitation becomes part of God’s point. The forces coming against Edom will not behave like thieves who take enough and move on. The judgment will be far more exhaustive.

“If grape-gatherers had come to you, would they not have left some gleanings?”

God gives a second picture, this time from the vineyard. Even when grape gatherers harvested a vineyard, some grapes would ordinarily remain behind. Under Israel’s law, harvesters were actually commanded not to gather everything, leaving some for the poor and the stranger (Leviticus 19:10; Deuteronomy 24:21).

Whether Obadiah specifically has that law in view or simply the normal realities of harvesting, the image is clear: even a harvested vineyard normally has “some gleanings” left.

Edom’s judgment will be different. Thieves leave something because they eventually have enough; grape gatherers leave something because harvesting is never completely exhaustive. Edom, however, is about to experience a stripping so complete that these ordinary comparisons are inadequate.

Study Notes

“Oh, how you will be cut off!” is placed abruptly within the two comparisons. That interruption gives the line emotional force. Obadiah is not merely explaining the extent of Edom’s losses; he pauses over how devastating its downfall will be.

The vineyard picture continues into verse 6, where Obadiah exclaims, “Oh, how Esau shall be searched out!” Verse 5 establishes the comparison; verse 6 states how thoroughly Edom’s hidden wealth will be uncovered.

Jeremiah 49:9 uses the same two images in its prophecy against Edom: “If grape-gatherers came to you, would they not leave some gleaning grapes? If thieves by night, would they not destroy until they have enough?” The close parallel is another part of the strong relationship between Jeremiah 49:7-22 and Obadiah’s prophecy.

Spirit & Life

There is a sobering progression in verses 3-5. Edom believed its position was secure, God declared that He could bring it down, and now He describes how complete that downfall will be. Pride had not merely caused Edom to think too highly of itself; it had blinded the nation to how vulnerable it actually was before God.

The image of gleanings also makes the severity of the judgment stand out. Normally something remains. Here God is warning Edom that the things it trusted in can be stripped away completely. What we possess can never provide the security that belongs only to God Himself.

Obadiah 1:6

NKJV

Obadiah 1:6 "Oh, how Esau shall be searched out! How his hidden treasures shall be sought after! (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:6 Every nook and cranny of Edomwill be searched and looted.Every treasure will be found and taken. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom will be so completely conquered that even its hidden places and concealed treasures will be discovered and taken. Nothing the nation has stored away will remain safely beyond the reach of God’s judgment.

Verse Explanation

“Oh, how Esau shall be searched out!”

“Esau” refers here to Edom, the nation descended from Esau. Using the ancestor’s name emphasizes the deep historical identity of the people God is addressing.

“Searched out” carries the picture of a thorough investigation. Edom will not simply be defeated on the surface. Its enemies will penetrate its places of security and search through what had been carefully protected.

The exclamation “Oh, how” expresses amazement at the completeness of what will happen. Verse 5 said that ordinary thieves and grape gatherers leave something behind. Verse 6 now explains the contrast: Edom will be searched so thoroughly that what it expected to remain hidden will be found.

“How his hidden treasures shall be sought after!”

Edom’s “hidden treasures” were possessions or valuables stored where they were expected to remain safe. In a rocky, mountainous region, caves, crevices, and other concealed places could provide natural locations for protecting wealth.

Yet concealment will provide no protection. The same Edom that trusted its difficult-to-reach dwelling places in verses 3-4 now discovers that even its hidden wealth can be reached. Its defenses cannot prevent its enemies from finding what it thought was secure.

There is a deliberate movement in these verses: Edom’s high places cannot protect it, and now neither can its hidden places. What is above and what is concealed are equally accessible when God brings judgment.

Study Notes

“Esau” and “Edom” can function almost interchangeably in prophetic passages because Edom descended from Esau. Genesis 36:8 states plainly, “Esau is Edom.” Obadiah repeatedly draws upon this family identity because Edom’s relationship to Jacob and Judah becomes important as the prophecy develops.

The expression translated “hidden treasures” can refer to concealed or stored-up valuables. The precise form those treasures took is not specified, so it is better not to identify particular goods beyond the text.

Verses 5-6 belong together. Verse 5 asks whether thieves or grape gatherers would normally leave something behind; verse 6 answers by describing how unusually exhaustive Edom’s plundering will be.

Spirit & Life

Edom trusted both what could be seen-its impressive position-and what could not be seen-its concealed resources. God reaches both. There is no public strength and no private reserve that can make a person independent of Him.

The language of being “searched out” also carries a broader spiritual reminder. We can hide things from other people, and sometimes even avoid facing them ourselves, but nothing in us is hidden from God. That truth is terrifying when we insist on resisting Him, but precious when we belong to Him. The God who sees completely is also the God before whom we never need to pretend.

Obadiah 1:7

NKJV

Obadiah 1:7 All the men in your confederacy Shall force you to the border; The men at peace with you Shall deceive you and prevail against you. Those who eat your bread shall lay a trap for you. No one is aware of it. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:7 “All your allies will turn against you.They will help to chase you from your land.They will promise you peacewhile plotting to deceive and destroy you.Your trusted friends will set traps for you,and you won’t even know about it. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom’s trusted allies will turn against it. The nation that prided itself on strength and wisdom will be deceived by the very people it considered friends and will fail even to recognize what is happening.

Verse Explanation

“All the men in your confederacy shall force you to the border”

Edom had relationships and agreements with surrounding nations, but those alliances would not save it. “The men in your confederacy” refers to those joined with Edom by treaty or alliance. Instead of defending Edom, they will “force you to the border,” driving Edom away or turning against it.

This adds another layer to Edom’s collapsing security. Its mountain strongholds cannot protect it, its hidden treasures will be discovered, and now its political alliances will fail.

“The men at peace with you shall deceive you and prevail against you.”

These are people Edom regarded as friendly-those with whom it enjoyed peaceful relations. Yet they will “deceive” Edom and gain the advantage over it.

The word “deceive” deliberately echoes verse 3: “The pride of your heart has deceived you.” Edom is first deceived internally by its own pride and then externally by its allies. Its distorted confidence leaves it vulnerable to deception from both directions.

“Those who eat your bread shall lay a trap for you.”

Eating bread together was more than casual dining in the ancient world. Sharing food could express friendship, hospitality, loyalty, and peaceful relationship. The image therefore makes the betrayal especially sharp: people welcomed as friends will secretly work against Edom.

The NKJV’s “trap” describes something placed secretly to bring about Edom’s downfall. Those Edom trusts will be working beneath the surface against it.

“No one is aware of it.”

The final statement exposes how complete Edom’s deception has become. Despite its reputation for wisdom, Edom will not understand what its supposed friends are doing.

That is an important part of the judgment. Edom does not merely lack the strength to stop the conspiracy; it lacks the perception to recognize it. The nation that believed itself exceptionally secure does not even see the danger developing around it.

Study Notes

The three descriptions-“men in your confederacy,” “men at peace with you,” and “those who eat your bread”-may describe overlapping groups rather than three entirely separate sets of people. Together they emphasize relationships Edom had every reason to regard as safe.

The final phrase “No one is aware of it” is difficult in Hebrew and has been translated in several ways. The basic sense is that Edom lacks understanding or awareness of the betrayal being prepared against it. The NKJV captures that central idea well.

Edom had a longstanding reputation for wisdom, something verse 8 will address directly. This makes “No one is aware of it” especially pointed: a people known for wisdom will be unable to perceive its own approaching disaster.

Spirit & Life

The repetition of deception is worth noticing. In verse 3, Edom is deceived by pride; here it is deceived by people. The two belong together. Pride does not necessarily make us more perceptive-it can make us easier to deceive because we begin interpreting everything through an inflated confidence in ourselves.

There is also a sobering picture of misplaced trust. Edom had strong defenses, accumulated wealth, and dependable-looking relationships, yet each source of security fails in succession. God is stripping away every answer Edom might give to the question, “What makes us safe?” Security ultimately cannot rest in what we possess, where we live, what we know, or who stands with us.

Obadiah 1:8

NKJV

Obadiah 1:8 "Will I not in that day," says the LORD, "Even destroy the wise men from Edom, And understanding from the mountains of Esau? (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:8 At that time not a single wise personwill be left in the whole land of Edom,”says the LORD.“For on the mountains of EdomI will destroy everyone who has understanding. (NLT)

Main Idea

God will remove the wisdom and understanding for which Edom was known. When His judgment comes, neither Edom’s strong defenses nor its celebrated wise men will be able to save it.

Verse Explanation

“‘Will I not in that day,’ says the LORD”

The question expects the answer yes. “That day” points to the time when God carries out the judgment already described against Edom. The events are not presented merely as Edom suffering political misfortune; the LORD identifies Himself as the One acting in judgment.

“Even destroy the wise men from Edom”

Edom was associated with wisdom in the ancient world. The best-known biblical example is Eliphaz, one of Job’s friends, who was a Temanite (Job 2:11). Jeremiah also asks concerning Edom, “Is wisdom no more in Teman? Has counsel perished from the prudent?” (Jeremiah 49:7).

God therefore targets something closely associated with Edom’s identity and confidence: “the wise men.” The nation will find that human wisdom cannot devise a way out when God has determined to judge it.

“And understanding from the mountains of Esau?”

“The mountains of Esau” refers to Edom’s mountainous homeland. “Esau” again stands for the nation descended from him.

“Understanding” is closely connected with the “wise men” in the previous phrase. Edom will lose the ability to understand its situation and respond effectively. This fits the end of verse 7, where Edom is betrayed by its allies yet “No one is aware of it.” The nation known for wisdom will find itself unable to perceive or prevent its own downfall.

There is also a continuing dismantling of everything Edom trusted. Its high places cannot save it, its hidden treasures cannot be protected, its allies cannot be trusted, and now its wisdom cannot deliver it.

Study Notes

Teman was an important Edomite region or city and became closely associated with Edom. In verse 9, Obadiah will address “your mighty men, O Teman,” pairing Edom’s renowned wisdom in verse 8 with its military strength in verse 9.

The connection with Jeremiah 49 is especially strong here. Jeremiah 49:7 begins its prophecy concerning Edom by asking, “Is wisdom no more in Teman?” Both prophets highlight the failure of Edom’s celebrated wisdom in the face of divine judgment.

“That day” in this immediate context refers to the coming time of judgment upon Edom. It should not automatically be made identical with every biblical use of “the day of the LORD,” although Obadiah will broaden his vision to “the day of the LORD upon all the nations” in verse 15.

Spirit & Life

Edom’s wisdom was real enough to become part of its reputation, yet even a genuine strength can become a false confidence. Intelligence, experience, good judgment, and careful planning are gifts, but none can make us self-sufficient before God.

There is also a progression worth seeing in verses 3-8. Pride deceives Edom; its allies then deceive Edom; finally, its understanding disappears. Pride can become more than an attitude-it can affect how clearly we see. Humility before God keeps us teachable and willing to see reality as He reveals it rather than merely as we expect it to be.

Obadiah 1:9

NKJV

Obadiah 1:9 Then your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed, To the end that everyone from the mountains of Esau May be cut off by slaughter. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:9 The mightiest warriors of Temanwill be terrified,and everyone on the mountains of Edomwill be cut down in the slaughter. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom’s military strength will fail just as its wisdom has failed. God will leave its warriors terrified and powerless so that the people of Edom can be completely cut down.

Verse Explanation

“Then your mighty men, O Teman, shall be dismayed”

After removing Edom’s wisdom in verse 8, God turns to another source of the nation’s confidence: its “mighty men.” These are Edom’s warriors-the men expected to defend the nation when danger came.

“Teman” was an important part of Edom and can represent Edom more broadly here. The name is especially fitting because the previous verse spoke of Edom’s wisdom, while this verse speaks of its warriors. Neither wise counsel nor military strength will be enough.

“Dismayed” means more than disappointed. The idea is being terrified, shattered in confidence, or thrown into confusion. The men Edom expects to stand firm will themselves lose courage when judgment arrives.

“To the end that everyone from the mountains of Esau may be cut off by slaughter.”

“The mountains of Esau” again refers to Edom’s mountainous homeland. Earlier, those mountains were part of what made Edom feel secure. Now the people inhabiting those same mountains face destruction.

The logic is important: once Edom’s warriors are overwhelmed, the nation is left defenseless. Verse 8 removes its wisdom; verse 9 breaks its military strength. God is systematically dismantling the things Edom trusted to preserve itself.

Study Notes

Teman was associated with Edom and apparently became important enough that its name could be used prominently when addressing the nation. Eliphaz in the book of Job was “the Temanite” (Job 2:11), and Jeremiah 49:7 connects Teman particularly with Edom’s reputation for wisdom.

“Cut off by slaughter” describes violent destruction in the context of national judgment. Some translations connect the wording slightly differently with the following verse, but the basic picture remains clear: Edom’s defenders will fail and devastating destruction will follow.

Verses 8-9 form a deliberate pair. Edom loses its wise men and then its mighty men-the people who might have been expected to provide strategy and strength. Neither wisdom nor power can rescue the nation from the LORD’s judgment.

Spirit & Life

Edom’s securities are being removed one after another: position, wealth, alliances, wisdom, and now military strength. Obadiah is showing how many different things can create the illusion that we are secure apart from God.

There is nothing wrong with strength itself. The danger comes when what God has allowed us to possess becomes what we trust instead of Him. Edom’s mighty men looked formidable until they encountered something beyond their ability to control. Human strength has limits; dependence upon God begins by recognizing them.

Obadiah 1:10

NKJV

Obadiah 1:10 "For violence against your brother Jacob, Shame shall cover you, And you shall be cut off forever. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:10 “Because of the violence you didto your close relatives in Israel,you will be filled with shameand destroyed forever. (NLT)

Main Idea

God now reveals why Edom faces such severe judgment: it acted violently against Judah, a people bound to Edom by the ancient family relationship between Esau and Jacob. Because of this, Edom will experience shame and lasting judgment.

Verse Explanation

“For violence against your brother Jacob”

The word “For” is important. Verses 2-9 have announced Edom’s downfall; now God begins explaining why that judgment is deserved.

Edom’s sin is described as “violence.” As the following verses show, this included more than direct physical attack. Edom stood aloof during Jerusalem’s disaster, rejoiced over Judah’s suffering, took advantage of its calamity, and eventually helped capture those trying to escape.

God calls Jacob “your brother.” Edom descended from Esau, while Israel descended from Esau’s twin brother Jacob. Though centuries had passed, God still viewed Edom’s treatment of Judah in light of that family relationship. Edom had turned against a people to whom it was historically bound.

“Shame shall cover you”

Edom’s pride will end in shame. The nation that considered itself secure and exalted will instead be publicly disgraced.

There is a fitting reversal here. Edom had looked upon Judah’s humiliation and, as verse 12 will show, rejoiced over it. Now shame will “cover” Edom itself.

“And you shall be cut off forever.”

The judgment goes beyond temporary embarrassment or military defeat. Edom will be “cut off,” describing the end of the nation as an independent people.

“Forever” emphasizes the lasting nature of that judgment. Edom would not merely suffer a setback and then return to its former national strength. Its downfall would ultimately be irreversible.

Study Notes

“Violence” translates hamas, a strong term that can include violence, wrongdoing, cruelty, or destructive injustice. The same word is used in Genesis 6:11 to describe the earth as “filled with violence.” Here the following verses define what Edom’s violence looked like in practice.

The identification of “your brother Jacob” reaches back to Genesis 25-36 and the relationship between Esau and Jacob. Elsewhere God explicitly recognizes this continuing kinship; Deuteronomy 23:7 tells Israel, “You shall not abhor an Edomite, for he is your brother.” That makes Edom’s hostility toward Judah especially serious.

Verses 10-14 mark an important shift in Obadiah. The earlier verses primarily announce what will happen to Edom; these verses describe the behavior that brought the judgment. Notice the repeated references to “the day” of Judah’s disaster as Obadiah walks through Edom’s response to it.

Spirit & Life

The words “your brother Jacob” give this judgment a personal weight. God is not only concerned with what Edom did; He is concerned with whom Edom did it to. Relationships carry responsibilities before God, and betrayal becomes especially grievous when it comes from someone who should have acted as a brother.

There is also a warning in the movement from pride to violence. Pride began in Edom’s heart in verse 3, but it did not remain there. Eventually it shaped how Edom treated other people. What rules the heart will ultimately affect how we respond when others are vulnerable, especially when their trouble gives us an opportunity to benefit at their expense.

Obadiah 1:11

NKJV

Obadiah 1:11 In the day that you stood on the other side"” In the day that strangers carried captive his forces, When foreigners entered his gates And cast lots for Jerusalem"” Even you were as one of them. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:11 When they were invaded,you stood aloof, refusing to help them.Foreign invaders carried off their wealthand cast lots to divide up Jerusalem,but you acted like one of Israel’s enemies. (NLT)

Main Idea

When Jerusalem was attacked and plundered, Edom stood aside rather than helping its brother nation. God regarded that deliberate refusal to act as participation with Judah’s enemies.

Verse Explanation

“In the day that you stood on the other side”

Obadiah now describes Edom’s behavior during Judah’s disaster. “Stood on the other side” pictures Edom deliberately remaining apart while violence was being done to Jerusalem. Edom saw what was happening to its brother and chose not to come to his aid.

This is important because Edom’s guilt did not begin with an attack of its own. It began by standing aside. Verses 12-14 will show Edom becoming progressively more involved, but God first calls attention to its refusal to help.

“In the day that strangers carried captive his forces”

“Strangers” refers to foreign enemies who invaded Judah. The phrase translated “carried captive his forces” can also carry the sense of taking away Judah’s wealth or possessions as spoil. The picture is of Jerusalem being overrun and stripped by foreign invaders.

Edom was watching while this happened. The repeated “in the day” emphasizes that Edom was present during Judah’s crisis and had to decide how it would respond.

“When foreigners entered his gates and cast lots for Jerusalem”

The invaders penetrated Jerusalem itself. Entering its “gates” means the city’s defenses had failed and the enemy had gained control.

“Cast lots for Jerusalem” pictures the conquerors dividing what they had taken, whether spoil, territory, captives, or the benefits of conquest. Jerusalem had become something its enemies could divide among themselves.

“Even you were as one of them.”

This is God’s devastating assessment of Edom’s neutrality. Edom may not initially have been one of the invading armies, but by standing aside while its brother was attacked, “you were as one of them.”

The following verses show why the judgment is so strong. Edom did not remain a detached observer. It rejoiced over Judah’s disaster, entered the city during its calamity, took its possessions, blocked escape routes, and handed survivors over to the enemy. Verse 11 introduces that progression by exposing the heart behind it: when Judah needed a brother, Edom chose the side of Judah’s enemies.

Study Notes

The exact historical invasion Obadiah describes has been debated. Many interpreters connect the passage with Babylon’s destruction of Jerusalem in 586 BC, while others propose an earlier attack. The book itself does not name the invading nation or provide a date, so it is wise not to claim greater certainty than the text allows.

The phrase “carried captive his forces” translates wording that can refer to wealth, resources, or possessions rather than specifically military forces. This explains why translations differ here. The central picture is unchanged: foreigners are carrying away what belonged to Judah.

Casting lots was a common means of assigning shares. Here it vividly portrays Jerusalem completely in enemy hands, with the conquerors dividing the gains of their victory.

Spirit & Life

“Even you were as one of them” is the searching line in this verse. Edom apparently could tell itself, at least initially, “We did not do this.” God’s assessment was different. There are moments when refusing to stand with someone who is being wronged becomes a way of standing with those doing the wrong.

The progression is also worth watching carefully. Edom first “stood on the other side.” In the next verses, passive indifference becomes enjoyment, then participation, then exploitation, and finally active betrayal. Sin often moves that way. What we permit ourselves to watch without concern can eventually become something we participate in.

Obadiah 1:12

NKJV

Obadiah 1:12 "But you should not have gazed on the day of your brother In the day of his captivity; Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah In the day of their destruction; Nor should you have spoken proudly In the day of distress. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:12 “You should not have gloatedwhen they exiled your relatives to distant lands.You should not have rejoicedwhen the people of Judah suffered such misfortune.You should not have spoken arrogantlyin that terrible time of trouble. (NLT)

Main Idea

God condemns Edom not only for what it did to Judah, but for the attitude it displayed during Judah’s suffering. Edom watched with satisfaction, rejoiced over its brother’s destruction, and spoke proudly when Judah was in distress.

Verse Explanation

“But you should not have gazed on the day of your brother”

God begins a series of statements describing how Edom should not have responded to Judah’s disaster. “Gazed” means more than simply seeing what happened. In this context, it carries the sense of looking on with satisfaction or malicious interest.

Again God calls Judah “your brother.” The issue is not merely that Edom enjoyed another nation’s misfortune. Edom took pleasure in the calamity of a people with whom it shared an ancient family relationship through Esau and Jacob.

“In the day of his captivity”

Judah’s disaster involved defeat, loss, and people being carried away by their enemies. This was a moment when Judah was vulnerable and humiliated.

The repeated language of “the day” runs through verses 11-14. Obadiah is taking Edom back to the moment of Judah’s calamity and exposing how it responded at each stage.

“Nor should you have rejoiced over the children of Judah in the day of their destruction”

Edom went beyond watching Judah’s suffering; it “rejoiced” over it. Judah’s loss became a source of satisfaction to Edom.

This exposes the heart behind Edom’s actions. Its later participation in Judah’s disaster was not accidental or reluctant. Edom was pleased to see Judah brought low.

“Nor should you have spoken proudly in the day of distress.”

The NKJV’s “spoken proudly” has the sense of making the mouth great-boasting, speaking arrogantly, or gloating over another’s misfortune. Edom used Judah’s humiliation as an occasion to exalt itself.

This connects directly with the pride exposed in verse 3. Edom’s pride was not confined to how it viewed itself; it shaped how Edom responded when its brother fell. Judah’s humiliation became an opportunity for Edom to feel greater.

Study Notes

The repeated “you should not have” language in verses 12-14 creates a deliberate catalogue of Edom’s offenses. The progression moves from looking, to rejoicing, to boasting, and then in verses 13-14 to entering Jerusalem, taking spoil, blocking fugitives, and handing survivors over.

“Spoken proudly” translates a vivid expression literally connected with making one’s mouth large. It describes arrogant or boastful speech, especially appropriate here for Edom’s gloating over Judah’s calamity.

The language of “your brother” continues the emphasis introduced in verse 10. Edom’s guilt is intensified by the fact that the suffering people were not strangers but descendants of Jacob, the brother of Edom’s ancestor Esau.

Spirit & Life

Obadiah reaches beneath Edom’s actions and exposes its reaction to another person’s pain. It is possible to commit no act of violence ourselves and yet reveal something deeply wrong in the heart by secretly enjoying another person’s fall.

The progression from “gazed” to “rejoiced” to “spoken proudly” is especially searching. What we allow ourselves to enjoy inwardly can eventually find expression outwardly. Another person’s failure, loss, or humiliation should never become fuel for our own sense of superiority.

Jesus leads us in exactly the opposite direction. He does not teach us to rejoice when enemies fall, but to love our enemies and pray for those who mistreat us (Matthew 5:44). The heart shaped by Jesus does not ask how another person’s fall makes us look better; it learns to desire mercy even for those with whom there has been conflict.

Obadiah 1:13

NKJV

Obadiah 1:13 You should not have entered the gate of My people In the day of their calamity. Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction In the day of their calamity, Nor laid hands on their substance In the day of their calamity. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:13 You should not have plundered the land of Israelwhen they were suffering such calamity.You should not have gloated over their destructionwhen they were suffering such calamity.You should not have seized their wealthwhen they were suffering such calamity. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom moved from watching and celebrating Judah’s disaster to taking advantage of it. God condemns Edom for entering Jerusalem during its calamity, looking triumphantly upon its suffering, and taking its possessions.

Verse Explanation

“You should not have entered the gate of My people in the day of their calamity.”

Edom’s involvement now becomes active. In verse 11, Edom “stood on the other side.” In verse 12, it watched and rejoiced. Now Edom enters the defeated city itself.

The words “My people” are significant. Judah may be conquered, humiliated, and suffering under judgment, but God still calls them “My people.” Their failure and discipline did not give Edom permission to exploit them.

“Indeed, you should not have gazed on their affliction in the day of their calamity”

The idea from verse 12 is repeated, but now Edom is inside Jerusalem taking advantage of the disaster. “Gazed on their affliction” again carries more than simply witnessing what happened. In this context, Edom is looking upon Judah’s suffering with malicious satisfaction.

The repetition of “in the day of their calamity” keeps emphasizing the timing of Edom’s actions. Judah was already devastated and vulnerable. Rather than showing mercy to its brother, Edom saw an opportunity.

“Nor laid hands on their substance in the day of their calamity.”

Edom finally turns Judah’s suffering into personal gain. “Laid hands on their substance” means taking their wealth or possessions as spoil.

This completes a disturbing progression. Edom first stood aside, then watched, then rejoiced, then boasted, then entered Jerusalem, and now plundered what remained. What began as indifference to a brother’s suffering developed into active exploitation of it.

Study Notes

The threefold repetition of “in the day of their calamity” gives the verse unusual force. Obadiah keeps returning to the fact that Edom chose Judah’s moment of greatest weakness as the opportunity for its actions.

The Hebrew word behind “calamity” can carry the sense of disaster or misfortune. Here it describes the devastating national crisis that had overtaken Judah.

“Their substance” refers to possessions or wealth. The wording does not identify particular items, so the main point is simply that Edom participated in taking what belonged to Judah.

There is also an important theological detail in “My people.” Obadiah does not deny that Judah had experienced severe judgment, but neither does he treat Judah’s judgment as permission for Edom to act wickedly against them. God can discipline His people while still holding others accountable for how they treat them.

Spirit & Life

The words “My people” open something important. Judah is at one of its lowest moments-defeated, plundered, and humiliated-yet God still speaks of them as belonging to Him. Their circumstances might have made it appear that God had abandoned them, but His language says otherwise.

Edom also shows how quickly another person’s weakness can become an opportunity for the wrong kind of heart. God calls us in the opposite direction. When someone is in “the day of their calamity,” their vulnerability is not an opportunity to gain advantage, settle old scores, or feel superior. It is an opportunity for mercy.

Obadiah 1:14

NKJV

Obadiah 1:14 You should not have stood at the crossroads To cut off those among them who escaped; Nor should you have delivered up those among them who remained In the day of distress. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:14 You should not have stood at the crossroads,killing those who tried to escape.You should not have captured the survivorsand handed them over in their terrible time of trouble. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom’s betrayal reached its darkest point when it prevented fleeing Judeans from escaping and handed survivors over to their enemies. Instead of protecting its vulnerable brother, Edom helped complete his destruction.

Verse Explanation

“You should not have stood at the crossroads to cut off those among them who escaped”

As Jerusalem and Judah fell, some people attempted to escape. Edom positioned itself where the fugitives could be intercepted.

“The crossroads” pictures places along the escape routes where Edom could block those fleeing the disaster. The nation that had earlier “stood on the other side” while Judah suffered (verse 11) is now standing in the path of Judah’s survivors.

“Cut off” in this setting carries the idea of intercepting or destroying the fugitives. Rather than opening a path of refuge for its brother, Edom became another obstacle between the survivors and safety.

“Nor should you have delivered up those among them who remained in the day of distress.”

Some Judeans had survived the initial destruction, but Edom “delivered up” these remaining people to the enemy. Those who might otherwise have escaped were betrayed into the hands of their conquerors.

This brings the progression of verses 11-14 to its terrible conclusion. Edom first stood aside, then watched Judah’s suffering, rejoiced over it, boasted, entered the city, took its possessions, blocked those trying to escape, and finally handed survivors over.

The phrase “in the day of distress” again emphasizes Judah’s vulnerability. Edom did not simply oppose Judah when Judah was strong; it turned against its brother when he was already crushed.

Study Notes

The word translated “crossroads” can refer to a road junction or a place where routes divide. The exact geographical location is not identified. The point is that Edom positioned itself along the routes used by those attempting to flee.

“Delivered up” describes handing people over into another’s power. This is more serious than merely refusing refuge: Edom actively assisted Judah’s enemies by surrendering the survivors to them.

Verses 11-14 show a carefully developing pattern of guilt. Edom’s sin moves from passive nonintervention to inward delight, verbal gloating, material exploitation, and finally direct participation in the destruction of Judah’s survivors. Obadiah does not allow Edom to hide behind the claim that it did not begin the original attack.

Spirit & Life

There is a sobering progression from “stood on the other side” in verse 11 to “stood at the crossroads” in verse 14. At first Edom simply refused to help. By the end, Edom was actively preventing others from escaping. Indifference to another person’s suffering can harden into something far worse if the heart is left unchecked.

The crossroads should have been a place of mercy. Edom could have received the fleeing, protected the vulnerable, and acted like a brother. Instead, it turned the escape route into a trap. One measure of what is happening in our hearts is what we do when another person is vulnerable and we have the power either to help them or take advantage of their weakness.

Obadiah 1:15

NKJV

Obadiah 1:15 "For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near; As you have done, it shall be done to you; Your reprisal shall return upon your own head. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:15 “The day is near when I, the LORD,will judge all godless nations!As you have done to Israel,so it will be done to you.All your evil deedswill fall back on your own heads. (NLT)

Main Idea

Edom’s judgment is part of a much larger reality: the day is coming when the LORD will judge all nations. God’s justice will answer human evil so that what people have done will ultimately return upon them.

Verse Explanation

“For the day of the LORD upon all the nations is near”

Obadiah’s vision suddenly widens. Verses 10-14 focused specifically on Edom’s treatment of Judah “in the day” of Judah’s calamity. Now Obadiah points to another day: “the day of the LORD.”

In the prophets, “the day of the LORD” describes a time when God decisively intervenes in judgment and establishes His purposes. Here Edom’s coming judgment becomes part of something larger-God’s judgment “upon all the nations.” Edom is not an isolated case. The LORD is Judge over every nation.

“Near” gives the warning urgency. In Obadiah’s prophetic horizon, God’s intervention is approaching and must be taken seriously. As the remainder of the book unfolds, the prophecy reaches beyond Edom’s immediate downfall toward the LORD’s ultimate rule over the nations.

“As you have done, it shall be done to you”

God now states the principle governing Edom’s judgment. Edom had watched Judah’s disaster, rejoiced over it, plundered its possessions, blocked its fugitives, and handed survivors over. The kind of treatment Edom gave would now return upon Edom.

This is not impersonal karma or an automatic law of the universe. It is divine justice. The LORD sees what has been done and judges accordingly.

“Your reprisal shall return upon your own head.”

The NKJV “reprisal” refers to the consequences or recompense for Edom’s actions. What Edom had inflicted upon others would come back upon “your own head.”

The language is intensely personal. Edom had treated Judah’s calamity as someone else’s problem and even as an opportunity for gain. God declares that Edom will now experience the consequences of its own conduct.

Study Notes

Verse 15 is a major turning point in Obadiah. The prophecy has concentrated on Edom, but “all the nations” broadens the horizon. Edom becomes both the immediate object of judgment and part of the larger judgment of God upon the nations.

The “day of the LORD” is an important prophetic theme. Depending on context, it can refer to historical acts of divine judgment and also point forward toward God’s climactic judgment and reign. Obadiah itself moves in this broader direction, especially in verses 17-21, culminating with “the kingdom shall be the LORD’s.”

There is also a deliberate contrast of “days” in this section. Edom took advantage of Judah “in the day of their calamity” (verse 13) and “in the day of distress” (verse 14). Now Edom must face “the day of the LORD.” Edom had its day over Judah; the LORD will have His day over Edom and the nations.

Spirit & Life

“As you have done, it shall be done to you” is a sobering reminder that God takes seriously how we treat others. Actions that appear forgotten, hidden, or without consequence are not overlooked by Him. His justice means that evil never receives the final word.

For those who belong to Jesus, this verse also makes grace shine more brightly. The gospel does not tell us that our sins were simply ignored. At the cross, Jesus bore judgment in our place. The One who had done no wrong received what our sin deserved so that those who trust in Him could receive what only He deserved-acceptance and life with God.

And Obadiah is moving toward a larger hope. “The day of the LORD” means history does not belong ultimately to proud nations, violent people, or those who exploit the weak. God will have the final word, and verse 21 will bring the book to its great conclusion: “the kingdom shall be the LORD’s.”

Obadiah 1:16

NKJV

Obadiah 1:16 For as you drank on My holy mountain, So shall all the nations drink continually; Yes, they shall drink, and swallow, And they shall be as though they had never been.(NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:16 Just as you swallowed up my peopleon my holy mountain,so you and the surrounding nationswill swallow the punishment I pour out on you.Yes, all you nations will drink and staggerand disappear from history. (NLT)

Main Idea

Obadiah warns Edom that the judgment coming upon Jerusalem will not remain Israel’s story alone. Because Edom had joined in violence against God’s people, the same cup of judgment would come upon Edom and the surrounding nations, until their proud strength was brought to nothing.

Verse Explanation

“For as you drank on My holy mountain,”

Obadiah turns from Edom’s actions against Jerusalem to the judgment those actions would bring. “Drank” most naturally points to Edom and the other enemies celebrating Jerusalem’s downfall, likely including the drinking associated with victory and feasting. “My holy mountain” is Mount Zion in Jerusalem. The striking point is that Edom celebrated on the very place God called His own.

“So shall all the nations drink continually;”

The image now changes from drinking in celebration to drinking the cup of God’s judgment. Throughout the prophets, drinking from a cup can picture experiencing God’s wrath and its consequences (Isaiah 51:17; Jeremiah 25:15-17). Those who drank in triumph over Jerusalem would themselves be made to drink.

“Yes, they shall drink, and swallow,”

The repetition intensifies the picture. The nations will not merely taste judgment; they will have to take it fully. “Swallow” carries the sense of drinking it down completely. The judgment they thought belonged to someone else would become their own experience.

“And they shall be as though they had never been.”

This describes the devastating result of God’s judgment. It does not require the idea that every individual would cease to exist; it is the language of complete overthrow. The nations and powers that seemed secure would be so thoroughly brought down that their former greatness would disappear.

Study Notes

There is a deliberate reversal built around the word “drink.” Edom drank in celebration over Jerusalem; the nations will drink in judgment. The same image carries opposite meanings, turning their celebration into a picture of what is coming upon them.

“All the nations” also widens the horizon beyond Edom. Verse 15 had already declared, “The day of the LORD upon all the nations is near.” Edom is the immediate focus of Obadiah, but Edom becomes part of the larger biblical picture of God ultimately judging the proud and hostile nations.

The “cup” itself is not explicitly mentioned in this verse, but the prophetic background makes the imagery especially vivid. Jeremiah 25:15-29 similarly pictures the nations drinking the wine cup of God’s fury, beginning with Jerusalem and then extending outward to the nations.

Spirit & Life

There is a sobering reversal here: Edom celebrated when judgment fell on someone else, never considering that it could come to them. Scripture repeatedly warns against taking satisfaction in another person’s downfall while remaining blind to our own accountability before God.

The cup of judgment also opens an important line toward Christ. Jesus used this same biblical imagery when He spoke of the “cup” He was about to drink (Matthew 26:39). At the cross, Jesus willingly drank the cup of judgment for His people. Obadiah shows the terrifying reality of that cup; the gospel shows the astonishing grace of the One who drank it in our place.

Obadiah 1:17

NKJV

Obadiah 1:17 "But on Mount Zion there shall be deliverance, And there shall be holiness; The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:17 “But Jerusalem will become a refuge for those who escape;it will be a holy place.And the people of Israel will come backto reclaim their inheritance. (NLT)

Main Idea

After announcing judgment upon the nations, Obadiah turns to hope: Mount Zion will not remain defeated. God will preserve and restore His people, and the inheritance their enemies had taken will once again belong to them.

Verse Explanation

“But on Mount Zion there shall be deliverance,”

The word “But” marks a dramatic reversal from verse 16. The nations that opposed God will face judgment, but Mount Zion will experience deliverance. Zion is Jerusalem, particularly the mountain associated with God’s presence and His rule among His people. Though Jerusalem had been invaded and plundered, its devastation would not be the end of its story.

“Deliverance” carries the idea of escape or survivors. God’s judgment would not completely consume His people. There would be those whom He preserved and through whom His purposes would continue.

“And there shall be holiness;”

Zion would not merely be rescued; it would again be set apart to God. “Holiness” speaks of belonging to Him and being devoted to Him. The restoration Obadiah sees is therefore more than political recovery. God intends Zion to be restored as a place belonging to Him.

“The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions.”

“The house of Jacob” means God’s people, the descendants of Jacob. Earlier, Edom and the other nations had entered Jerusalem, taken its wealth, and helped themselves to what belonged to Judah (vv. 11-14). Now the situation will be reversed. God’s people will again possess what had been taken from them.

The wording is intentionally strong: they will “possess their possessions.” What appeared permanently lost during Jerusalem’s humiliation would not remain in the hands of its enemies. God would restore His people to their inheritance.

Study Notes

“Deliverance” can also carry the sense of “those who escape” or “survivors.” This fits a significant prophetic theme: judgment may be severe, yet God preserves a remnant through whom His purposes continue (Joel 2:32).

Joel 2:32 is especially important because it also connects “Mount Zion,” “deliverance,” and those whom the Lord calls. Peter later quotes Joel 2:32 in Acts 2:21 as he announces salvation through Jesus. Obadiah’s immediate concern is Zion’s restoration, but its language belongs to a larger biblical pattern in which God provides deliverance for His people.

“Possess their possessions” also begins a movement that continues through verses 18-20, where Obadiah describes God’s people taking possession of territories previously controlled by their enemies.

Spirit & Life

The movement from judgment to “deliverance” is one of Scripture’s great patterns. God’s people may pass through severe discipline, loss, and circumstances that appear final, but God’s purposes for them are not exhausted by what they have suffered.

There is also a deeper biblical trajectory in Zion’s deliverance. What Obadiah announces in terms of restored Israel eventually opens into the greater salvation God provides through Jesus. The New Testament takes the prophetic promise that “whoever calls on the name of the LORD shall be saved” and announces its fulfillment in the gospel (Acts 2:21; Romans 10:13). The God who promised deliverance on Zion ultimately provides salvation through His Son.

Obadiah 1:18

NKJV

Obadiah 1:18 The house of Jacob shall be a fire, And the house of Joseph a flame; But the house of Esau shall be stubble; They shall kindle them and devour them, And no survivor shall remain of the house of Esau," For the LORD has spoken. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:18 The people of Israel will be a raging fire,and Edom a field of dry stubble.The descendants of Joseph will be a flameroaring across the field, devouring everything.There will be no survivors in Edom.I, the LORD, have spoken! (NLT)

Main Idea

God will completely reverse the relationship between Israel and Edom. The people Edom helped destroy will become the instrument of Edom’s downfall, and Edom’s judgment will be complete because the Lord Himself has declared it.

Verse Explanation

“The house of Jacob shall be a fire,”

“The house of Jacob” refers to God’s people descended from Jacob. The image of fire pictures the power God will give them against their enemies. Judah had recently appeared weak, defeated, and vulnerable, but that would not remain their condition. God would reverse their humiliation.

“And the house of Joseph a flame;”

“The house of Joseph” most naturally represents the northern tribes of Israel, particularly those associated with Joseph’s sons Ephraim and Manasseh. By placing “the house of Jacob” and “the house of Joseph” together, Obadiah presents a picture that reaches beyond Judah alone toward the reunited people of Israel.

The imagery also intensifies: Jacob is “a fire,” and Joseph “a flame.” Together they become the consuming force in the judgment that follows.

“But the house of Esau shall be stubble;”

Esau was Jacob’s brother and the ancestor of the Edomites, so “the house of Esau” means Edom. Stubble is the dry stalks left after harvest, material that catches fire quickly and offers virtually no resistance to the flames.

The contrast is vivid. Jacob and Joseph are fire and flame; Esau is dry stubble before them. Edom’s mountain strongholds had made the nation feel nearly untouchable (vv. 3-4), but before God’s judgment their supposed strength would prove as helpless as stubble before fire.

“They shall kindle them and devour them,”

The picture moves from potential to action. Israel will not merely survive Edom; Edom will be consumed. This continues Obadiah’s repeated theme of reversal: those who participated in Jerusalem’s destruction will themselves experience destruction.

“And no survivor shall remain of the house of Esau,”

The language emphasizes the completeness of Edom’s downfall as a people and national power. Obadiah is describing the end of the house of Esau as the hostile nation that had opposed Jacob, not necessarily asserting that every individual Edomite would be killed at one moment.

This is especially striking after verse 17, where Mount Zion has “deliverance”-a word that can include the idea of survivors or those who escape. Jacob will have deliverance; Esau will ultimately have no surviving national remnant. The contrast is deliberate and severe.

“For the LORD has spoken.”

This is the foundation beneath the entire prophecy. Edom’s destruction does not depend merely upon Israel becoming stronger or political circumstances changing. It will happen because God has pronounced judgment.

Obadiah ends the declaration with certainty. Edom may look secure and Israel may look devastated, but appearances do not determine the future. “The LORD has spoken.”

Study Notes

The pairing of “the house of Jacob” with “the house of Joseph” is significant. Since Joseph was himself a son of Jacob, the two expressions are not simply two names for exactly the same group. “Joseph” was commonly associated with the northern kingdom, especially Ephraim. The wording therefore appears to envision Judah and the northern tribes together participating in the restoration and victory God promises.

The fire-and-stubble imagery has an Old Testament background. Numbers 24:18, in Balaam’s prophecy, had already foretold that “Edom shall be a possession” and that Israel would act valiantly. Obadiah develops that ancient expectation as part of Edom’s coming judgment.

Historically, Edom did eventually disappear as a distinct nation, although the process unfolded over centuries rather than in one identifiable event immediately following Obadiah.

Spirit & Life

The final words are the controlling truth of the verse: “For the LORD has spoken.” Everything visible seemed to contradict Obadiah’s message. Jerusalem had been devastated, while Edom had taken advantage of its weakness. Yet God’s word, not the present appearance of things, would determine the final outcome.

That remains an important foundation for faith. Biblical faith does not deny what circumstances look like; it gives God’s word greater authority than what circumstances presently seem to say.

Obadiah 1:19

NKJV

Obadiah 1:19 The South shall possess the mountains of Esau, And the Lowland shall possess Philistia. They shall possess the fields of Ephraim And the fields of Samaria. Benjamin shall possess Gilead. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:19 “Then my people living in the Negevwill occupy the mountains of Edom.Those living in the foothills of Judahwill possess the Philistine plainsand take over the fields of Ephraim and Samaria.And the people of Benjaminwill occupy the land of Gilead. (NLT)

Main Idea

God’s restoration will include His people once again possessing the lands around them that had fallen under enemy control. Obadiah describes a broad reversal: the people who had been dispossessed will themselves take possession.

Verse Explanation

“The South shall possess the mountains of Esau,”

“The South” refers to the Negev, the region south of Judah. Its inhabitants would possess “the mountains of Esau,” the mountainous territory of Edom southeast of Judah.

This directly reverses Edom’s earlier advantage over Judah. Edom had exploited Jerusalem’s disaster, but its own territory would eventually come under the possession of God’s restored people.

“And the Lowland shall possess Philistia.”

“The Lowland” is the Shephelah, the rolling foothill region west of Judah between the central hill country and the Mediterranean coastal plain. Philistia lay farther west along the coast.

Obadiah therefore moves geographically from Judah’s south toward its west. Areas associated with Judah would expand into territories held by longtime enemies.

“They shall possess the fields of Ephraim”

Ephraim was one of the dominant tribes of the former northern kingdom of Israel, and its territory lay north of Judah. The prophecy now extends beyond Judah’s immediate neighbors into territory formerly belonging to the northern tribes.

This connects naturally with verse 18, where Obadiah had spoken not only of “the house of Jacob” but also of “the house of Joseph.” God’s restoration reaches beyond Judah alone.

“And the fields of Samaria.”

Samaria was both an important city and the central region of the former northern kingdom. By Obadiah’s time, that kingdom had long since fallen to Assyria. Yet its former territory still had a place in the restoration Obadiah envisioned.

The movement is significant: south toward Edom, west toward Philistia, and north into Ephraim and Samaria. The picture is one of God reversing His people’s losses on every side.

“Benjamin shall possess Gilead.”

Benjamin’s traditional territory was immediately north of Judah, while Gilead lay considerably farther east, across the Jordan River. Benjamin therefore receives territory beyond its former boundaries.

Obadiah closes the verse with the same theme that runs through it: God’s people will “possess” again. Defeat and dispossession will give way to restoration and possession.

Study Notes

This verse can be difficult because it reads almost like a list of unfamiliar place names. A simple mental map makes it much clearer: the Negev is south, Edom southeast, the Shephelah west, Philistia farther west along the coast, Ephraim and Samaria north, and Gilead east across the Jordan. Obadiah is essentially looking outward from Judah in several directions and describing restored possession.

The repeated word “possess” connects directly with verse 17: “The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions.” Verses 19-20 now begin filling in geographically what that promise means.

The verse should first be allowed to carry its concrete geographical meaning. Obadiah is speaking about real peoples and real territories, not merely using the place names as symbols for general spiritual blessing.

Spirit & Life

There is a striking change in vocabulary across the chapter. Earlier, Edom and the nations were taking advantage of Judah’s losses; now God repeatedly speaks of His people possessing again. Judgment had brought genuine loss, but loss was not the final word over God’s people.

This also displays God’s faithfulness across generations. Ephraim and Samaria represented territory that had been lost long before Jerusalem’s recent disaster, yet they still appear within God’s vision of restoration. What human history had scattered and divided was not beyond His ability to gather and restore.

Obadiah 1:20

NKJV

Obadiah 1:20 And the captives of this host of the children of Israel Shall possess the land of the Canaanites As far as Zarephath. The captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad Shall possess the cities of the South. (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:20 The exiles of Israel will return to their landand occupy the Phoenician coast as far north as Zarephath.The captives from Jerusalem exiled in the northwill return home and resettle the towns of the Negev. (NLT)

Main Idea

Obadiah continues the picture of restoration by describing Israelite exiles returning and possessing territories far beyond their former boundaries. The people who had been scattered among the nations would not be forgotten; God would give them a place again among His restored people.

Verse Explanation

“And the captives of this host of the children of Israel shall possess the land of the Canaanites”

Obadiah now turns specifically to Israelites who had been carried away from their homeland. “The captives” are exiles-people removed from their land through conquest and scattered elsewhere.

Their future is described with the same key word that has dominated verses 17-19: they “shall possess.” Those who had lost their homes and inheritance would themselves possess territory again.

“As far as Zarephath.”

Zarephath was a Phoenician town on the Mediterranean coast between Tyre and Sidon, well north of Israel. It is familiar from 1 Kings 17, where Elijah stayed with the widow of Zarephath during the famine.

Its inclusion shows how extensive the restoration will be. Obadiah is looking beyond Israel’s immediate heartland toward the Phoenician coast.

“The captives of Jerusalem who are in Sepharad”

Obadiah then identifies another group: people from Jerusalem who had been carried away and were living in a place called Sepharad.

The precise location of ancient Sepharad is uncertain. Various identifications have been proposed, but none is certain enough to build the interpretation upon. What matters for Obadiah’s message is clear: even Jerusalemites living far from home are included in the promised restoration.

“Shall possess the cities of the South.”

“The South” is again the Negev, the region south of Judah mentioned in verse 19. Jerusalem’s scattered exiles will return to possession rather than remain permanently displaced.

The repeated movement is powerful. They were “captives,” but they “shall possess.” Exile had taken land, homes, and security from them, but captivity would not have the final word.

Study Notes

The first part of this verse is difficult to translate, and English versions differ in how they understand the relationship between “this host,” “the children of Israel,” and “the Canaanites.” The basic picture, however, remains clear: displaced Israelites will possess territory extending toward Zarephath.

“Sepharad” is one of the genuinely uncertain geographical names in Obadiah. Later Jewish tradition used “Sepharad” for Spain, which is why “Sephardic” came to describe Jews associated with Spain and the Iberian world. That later usage should not be assumed to identify the location Obadiah originally had in mind.

Verses 19-20 deliberately broaden the geography of verse 17’s promise that “The house of Jacob shall possess their possessions.” The restoration includes not only those remaining in the land but also people who had been scattered through exile.

Spirit & Life

One of the most encouraging details in the verse is that God’s promise reaches “the captives.” They may have been far from Jerusalem and living among foreign peoples, but distance had not placed them beyond God’s sight or His purposes.

Throughout Scripture, exile can look like the end of the story, but God repeatedly reveals Himself as the One who gathers what has been scattered. Obadiah’s restoration is specifically about Israel and its land, yet it reveals something enduring about God’s character: He does not lose track of His people when circumstances have carried them far from where they once were.

Obadiah 1:21

NKJV

Obadiah 1:21 Then saviors shall come to Mount Zion To judge the mountains of Esau, And the kingdom shall be the LORD's. [LORD’S] (NKJV)

NLT

Obadiah 1:21 Those who have been rescued will go up to Mount Zion in Jerusalemto rule over the mountains of Edom.And the LORD himself will be king!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Obadiah ends with the great reversal toward which the whole prophecy has been moving: those who were oppressed will experience God’s deliverance, Edom’s proud power will be judged, and ultimately the kingdom belongs to the LORD.

Verse Explanation

“Then saviors shall come to Mount Zion”

The “saviors” are human deliverers whom God raises up for His people, not rival saviors alongside God. The Old Testament can use this kind of language for leaders whom God empowers to rescue His people from their enemies, as in the days of the judges (Judges 3:9, 15).

Their coming “to Mount Zion” continues the restoration announced in verse 17. Zion, which had been invaded and humiliated, will once again become the place from which God’s restored rule is expressed.

“To judge the mountains of Esau,”

The deliverers will exercise judgment over Edom. Earlier, Edom looked down from its mountain strongholds with proud confidence, saying, “Who will bring me down to the ground?” (v. 3). Obadiah now gives the final answer: God will.

There is also a fitting geographical reversal. Edom had stood against Mount Zion, but now deliverers come to “Mount Zion” to judge “the mountains of Esau.” The mountain that seemed defeated becomes the place of deliverance, while the mountains that seemed secure come under judgment.

“And the kingdom shall be the LORD’s.”

These final words reach beyond Edom and bring the entire prophecy to its theological climax. Human kingdoms rise, nations boast in their strength, and for a time the wicked may appear to triumph-but none of them possesses ultimate authority. “The kingdom shall be the LORD’s.”

The statement also completes the movement that began with Edom’s pride. Edom believed its position made it secure and untouchable. Obadiah ends by declaring who actually rules. Edom does not have the final word. Babylon does not have the final word. Even Israel’s human deliverers do not have the final word. The kingdom belongs to the LORD.

Study Notes

The word translated “saviors” means deliverers or rescuers in this context. Similar language appears in Judges 3:9, where the LORD raised up a deliverer for Israel, and Nehemiah 9:27, where God gave His people deliverers who saved them from their enemies. God remains the ultimate source of the deliverance even when He accomplishes it through human agents.

The closing declaration “the kingdom shall be the LORD’s” gives the book a horizon larger than Edom alone. Verse 15 had already expanded the prophecy to “the day of the LORD upon all the nations.” The judgment of Edom is one expression of a larger reality: God will ultimately establish His rightful rule over every power that opposes Him.

Spirit & Life

Obadiah begins with a proud nation saying in its heart, “Who will bring me down to the ground?” (v. 3), and ends with “the kingdom shall be the LORD’s.” That is the deepest movement of the book. Human pride assumes that strength, position, wealth, or circumstances determine who ultimately wins. Obadiah says that history belongs to God.

For Christians, that final declaration also points naturally toward Jesus. The New Testament announces the coming day when “The kingdoms of this world have become the kingdoms of our Lord and of His Christ” (Revelation 11:15). Obadiah sees the LORD’s kingdom triumphing over every opposing power; the New Testament reveals that God brings His kingdom to its ultimate fulfillment through Christ.

The book therefore ends not with Edom, but with God. Judgment, deliverance, restoration, and the overthrow of proud human power all lead to the same final reality: “the kingdom shall be the LORD’s.”
