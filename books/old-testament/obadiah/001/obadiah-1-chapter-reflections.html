Chapter Reflections

Obadiah 1

Reflect and Respond
Obadiah 1:1–9 — The Coming Judgment on Edom

Insights

When Strength Becomes False Security

Edom’s cliffs did protect it, its trade did make it wealthy, its treaties were real, and its wisdom was well known (Jeremiah 49:7). Pride did not invent Edom’s strengths. It convinced Edom that those strengths made it secure: “Who will bring me down to the ground?” (v. 3). The same can happen with a gifted church, a family’s savings, or a leader’s record of success. These are good gifts to receive with thanks. The danger comes when we begin to believe those gifts make us safe, or that a decision or a season of life does not really need prayer.

Pride Deceives Before It Destroys

God names Edom’s self-deception before He announces its fall: “The pride of your heart has deceived you” (v. 3), and then, “From there I will bring you down” (v. 4). Pride does more than make us think too highly of ourselves. It blinds us to our true condition, so that we can feel secure and see no problem at all while a fall is already on its way. “Pride goes before destruction, And a haughty spirit before a fall” (Proverbs 16:18).

The Wise Nation That Could Not See

Edom was known for its wisdom (Jeremiah 49:7), yet it could not see what was happening to it. Pride deceived Edom (v. 3). Then its allies deceived it, with the same Hebrew verb, and “no one is aware of it” (v. 7), literally, “there is no understanding in him.” Then the LORD destroyed the wise men and understanding itself (v. 8). Pride began by deceiving Edom about its strength and ended by leaving it unable to see the trap set at its own table. A proud heart gradually loses the ability to recognize the truth, even when it is right in front of it.

Stripped of Every Security

Obadiah never narrates the battle. He names what Edom leaned on and shows each support removed: its high homeland (vv. 3–4), its hidden treasures (vv. 5–6), its allies (v. 7), its wise men (v. 8), and its mighty men (v. 9). One by one, God takes away everything Edom relied on, until it has nothing left to hide behind. When God removes something His people have leaned on in His place, it can feel like pure loss, but it brings the heart back into the open before Him, where the LORD is its only security.

Let Him Who Glories Glory in This

Jeremiah, who also prophesied against Edom (Jeremiah 49:7–22), names three of the same supports Obadiah does: “Let not the wise man glory in his wisdom, Let not the mighty man glory in his might, Nor let the rich man glory in his riches; But let him who glories glory in this, That he understands and knows Me” (Jeremiah 9:23–24). Wisdom, strength, and wealth are not wrong in themselves. The problem is trusting and boasting in them. Edom lost all three (vv. 6, 8–9). The one security that cannot be stripped away is knowing the LORD.

Which Rock Are We Trusting?

Edom lived “in the clefts of the rock” (v. 3). The Hebrew word for rock, sela, is the word David used when he sang, “The LORD is my rock and my fortress and my deliverer” (Psalm 18:2). David’s word for “deliverer” even shares a root with the “deliverance” promised on Mount Zion (v. 17). David and Edom used the same word for very different places of refuge. Edom trusted a stronghold it had climbed into and defended for itself. David trusted the LORD Himself. Everyone takes refuge in something, whether achievement, money, image, or a guarded distance from God and people. The real question is which rock we are trusting.

The Cleft That Saves

Edom’s cleft could not hide it on the day of the LORD. Isaiah says people will flee “into the clefts of the rocks” to hide “from the terror of the LORD” (Isaiah 2:21). Yet Scripture also tells of a cleft that saves. The LORD told Moses, “I will put you in the cleft of the rock, and will cover you with My hand while I pass by” (Exodus 33:22). Moses did not climb there. God placed him there so that he could stand near God’s glory and live. Edom used its rock to keep its distance from God, but the refuge God provides brings us near to Him. Paul writes, “that Rock was Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:4).

Edom Climbed High; Jesus Came Down Low

Edom’s posture was self-exaltation. It lived high in the rock, pictured its nest among the stars, and believed no one could bring it down (vv. 3–4). Jesus moved in the opposite direction. Though He was in the form of God, He “humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death,” and therefore “God also has highly exalted Him” (Philippians 2:6–9). Edom shows where self-exaltation ends. Christ shows the way of humility, surrender, and obedience, and the exaltation that only God can give.

Dwelling in the Word

  1. What strength in my life have I begun to treat as the reason I am safe? (vv. 3–9)

  2. Where am I hiding in a rock of my own making rather than in the LORD? (vv. 3–4)

Obadiah 1:10–14 — The Reason: Edom Mistreated His Brother

Insights

Pride Toward God Becomes Contempt Toward a Brother

In verse 3, “the pride of your heart” lifted Edom above any need for God. In verse 12, the same pride reaches its mouth: “Nor should you have spoken proudly,” literally, “made your mouth great.” When we refuse to humble ourselves before God, we will rarely humble ourselves toward others. Paul makes the same connection: “Let us not become conceited, provoking one another, envying one another” (Galatians 5:26). Pride toward God and contempt toward others come from the same heart.

Pride Rejoices When Others Fall

Esau valued what he could have immediately over the inheritance God had promised (Genesis 25:29–34; Hebrews 12:16–17), and in this sense he pictures the flesh. The flesh compares itself with others, and it can feel better about itself when someone else fails. Judah’s calamity did not create Edom’s hostility; it exposed what was already there (v. 12). Another person’s trouble often reveals more about our hearts than about theirs. When a sibling, a coworker, or a growing church across town stumbles and we feel a flicker of satisfaction, the flesh is at work, and the root is usually a pride we have not yet seen.

Standing Aloof Is Standing Somewhere

Before Obadiah names any blow Edom struck, the LORD says of its standing on the other side, “Even you were as one of them” (v. 11). Refusing to help when we have the opportunity and the responsibility is not neutrality. God sees indifference as clearly as He sees obvious wrongdoing. This applies when a relative knows of abuse and keeps quiet, when church leaders see a wrong and keep their distance to protect themselves, or when friends watch someone being destroyed and say nothing. Standing at a safe distance does not remove responsibility.

Eight Steps Down

Verses 12–14 list eight things Edom “should not have” done. Edom gazed with satisfaction, rejoiced, and spoke proudly (v. 12). It entered the gate, gazed again from inside, and laid hands on Judah’s goods (v. 13). It cut off those who escaped and delivered up those who remained (v. 14). Obadiah may not list these charges in strict chronological order, but taken together they move from watching, to enjoying, to speaking, to taking part, and they show how inward attitudes grow into outward actions. Sin rarely begins with its worst act: “when desire has conceived, it gives birth to sin; and sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death” (James 1:15).

Stop at the First Step

The place to stop sin is the first step, before it reaches the mouth or the hands (vv. 12–14). A church split often begins with people standing apart and saying nothing while a conflict grows. A marriage that ends in betrayal often begins with private satisfaction in the other’s failures, which becomes contempt in conversation long before it becomes anything else. A public pile-on begins with someone enjoying another person’s humiliation. The Holy Spirit is faithful to convict at the first step, and that is where repentance costs the least.

What We Tolerate, We May Eventually Practice

Amos condemned Gaza and Tyre because they “delivered up the whole captivity to Edom” (Amos 1:9; see 1:6). Edom had been the one receiving captives that others handed over. In Obadiah, Edom itself delivers up Judah’s survivors (v. 14). When we become comfortable benefiting from another person’s wrongdoing, we can gradually become willing to do the same thing ourselves. A business that quietly profits from a partner’s dishonesty, a listener who enjoys gossip without repeating it, or a church that accepts the fruit of manipulation can end up doing the very thing it once only benefited from.

A Brother Is Born for Adversity

Each “should not have” implies what Edom should have done. It should have come near, grieved rather than gloated, and kept silent rather than boasting. It should have guarded its brother’s goods, kept the roads open, and sheltered survivors. Obadiah judges Edom by what it did “in the day of their calamity” (v. 13), not by how it behaved in ordinary times. “A friend loves at all times, And a brother is born for adversity” (Proverbs 17:17). Families and churches show what they truly are on someone else’s worst day.

Discipline Is Not Permission

Judah was suffering for its own sins, yet the LORD still called them “My people” (v. 13). His discipline did not mean He had abandoned them, and it gave Edom no right to exploit their weakness. When someone near us faces the consequences of their own failure, such as a moral fall exposed or a ministry lost, God’s correction of someone does not give us permission to condemn, exploit, or abandon them. We can come near without excusing the wrong, keep the way of restoration open, and reaffirm our love (2 Corinthians 2:7–8), because the one under discipline still belongs to God.

When a Brother Is Treated as a Stranger

The word translated “captivity” in verse 12 is related to the word for “foreigner,” and the KJV renders the phrase “in the day that he became a stranger.” On that day “strangers” and “foreigners” carried off Judah’s wealth and entered its gates (v. 11). Edom should have been different. Judah was “your brother Jacob” (v. 10), yet on that day Edom treated its brother like a stranger. When a person’s world is overrun by strangers, he needs someone who still treats him as family. God calls His people to be that someone.

Edom’s Wrongs and Christ’s Suffering

Read alongside the Gospels, several of the wrongs in this section reappear in Christ’s suffering. Foreigners cast lots for Jerusalem (v. 11), and soldiers cast lots for His garments (John 19:24). Edom spoke proudly over Judah (v. 12), and passers-by mocked Him (Matthew 27:39–43). Judah’s survivors were delivered up (v. 14), and Jesus was delivered to Pilate (Mark 15:1). His acquaintances “stood at a distance, watching” (Luke 23:49). In His suffering, Jesus endured many of the same kinds of wrongs, yet He answered them with forgiveness rather than gloating: “Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they do” (Luke 23:34).

Dwelling in the Word

  1. What does my reaction to someone else’s failure reveal about my heart? (vv. 10–12)

  2. Who in my life is facing a day of calamity and needs me to come near? (vv. 11–14)

Obadiah 1:15–21 — The Day of the LORD and the Deliverance of Israel

Insights

We Reap What We Sow

“As you have done, it shall be done to you; Your reprisal shall return upon your own head” (v. 15). God has built a moral order into His world, and what we sow eventually produces a harvest. Edom sowed violence, betrayal, and cruelty, and the harvest returned on its own head. The harvest may not come immediately, but it comes. Paul’s warning even begins where Obadiah began, with deception: “Do not be deceived, God is not mocked; for whatever a man sows, that he will also reap” (Galatians 6:7).

The Cup Christ Drank

Verse 16 pictures the nations drinking the cup of God’s judgment until “they shall be as though they had never been.” In the prophets the cup passes from nation to nation (Jeremiah 25:15–29), and no one in Obadiah escapes it by strength or position. Our deliverance comes through Jesus Christ, who willingly drank the cup of judgment we deserved. In Gethsemane Jesus asked that “this cup” pass from Him, then took it in obedience to the Father (Matthew 26:39). In Obadiah, deliverance on Mount Zion (v. 17) follows the cup of verse 16. For believers, every deliverance rests on the cup Christ drank for us.

What Was Taken Is Answered

The deliverance of verses 17–21 answers the losses of verses 11–14. Captives were carried off (v. 11), and captives return to possess the land (v. 20). Foreigners entered the gates (vv. 11, 13), and Zion will be holy (v. 17). Edom laid hands on Judah’s substance (v. 13), and “the house of Jacob shall possess their possessions” (v. 17). Edom cut off those who escaped (v. 14), and on Mount Zion “there shall be deliverance” (v. 17). Even losses that came through God’s own discipline are not beyond His restoring: “I will restore to you the years that the swarming locust has eaten” (Joel 2:25).

From the Mountains of Esau to Mount Zion

The chapter begins in the mountains of Esau, where a proud nation trusted its height (vv. 3–4), and ends on Mount Zion, where there is deliverance, holiness, inheritance, and the LORD’s rule (vv. 17, 21). Edom exalted itself and was brought down, while God lifted up the people who had been brought low. The movement pictures God’s work in a believer. He does not only rescue us from consequences. He brings us out of a life governed by self into a life under His rule, where we belong wholly to Him.

The Flesh Does Not Win in the End

Obadiah does not end with the house of Esau reformed or strengthened. It is stubble, consumed until no survivor remains (v. 18). With Esau as a picture of the flesh, the lesson is plain: God does not intend to make our self-reliant, self-exalting life strong enough to serve Him. He puts it to death. “And those who are Christ’s have crucified the flesh with its passions and desires” (Galatians 5:24). The flesh may look strong for a season, but it has no future in the kingdom of God.

What Cannot Survive the Fire

Obadiah pictures the house of Esau as stubble, the dry stalks left after harvest, set ablaze and consumed by fire (v. 18). Paul uses the same kind of image for the work of believers. Christ is the only foundation, and whatever is built on Him with “wood, hay, straw” (“stubble” in the KJV) will burn, while gold, silver, and precious stones endure, because “the fire will test each one’s work, of what sort it is” (1 Corinthians 3:11–15). Paul is speaking of the testing of believers’ works, not of a judgment like Esau’s, yet both passages picture material that cannot survive fire. What is built on pride, self-reliance, and human strength will not endure God’s examination, however impressive it looks. What is built on Christ will stand.

Saved, Set Apart, Possessing, Sent

Verses 17–21 unfold step by step. First there is deliverance for those who escape. Then “there shall be holiness.” Then “the house of Jacob shall possess their possessions” (v. 17). Then “saviors shall come to Mount Zion” (v. 21). Over all of it stands the last line: “the kingdom shall be the LORD’s.” These verses reveal a progression in God’s purposes for His people, and that progression does not end with their comfort or even their inheritance. Some believers stop at being saved. Others want to be used without being set apart. God’s purpose leads through holiness and inheritance to usefulness in His kingdom.

Formed Before Sent

Read prophetically, the saviors of verse 21 point to an end-time generation of highly empowered, victorious believers whom God raises up to share in the advance and manifestation of His kingdom. They ascend the same mountain where they were delivered, and their strength comes from what God has done in them, not from ambition or personality. “The people who know their God shall be strong, and carry out great exploits” (Daniel 11:32). They are servants of the Deliverer, not the source of deliverance. Churches should honor the same progression when raising leaders, forming them in holiness before placing them in influence.

Only One Kingdom Remains

Obadiah opens with Edom secure in its heights, asking, “Who will bring me down to the ground?” (v. 3). It ends, “And the kingdom shall be the LORD’s” (v. 21). In between, nations that seemed permanent become “as though they had never been” (v. 16). Human pride rises, God’s people suffer, and evil sometimes seems to win, but God alone determines the final outcome. In Christ we see the King whose kingdom will never pass away (Daniel 7:14; Revelation 11:15). “For He must reign till He has put all enemies under His feet” (1 Corinthians 15:25).

Dwelling in the Word

  1. What loss have I never brought to the Lord by name for His restoration? (vv. 17–20)

  2. Where is God calling me beyond being saved, into holiness, inheritance, or service in His kingdom? (vv. 17, 21)
