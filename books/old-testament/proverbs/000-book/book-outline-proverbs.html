Proverbs Book Outline

Revision 2 • October 31, 2026

Major Movements

I. The Purpose of Proverbs and the Fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:1–7)

The book names its source, “The proverbs of Solomon the son of David, king of Israel” (1:1), and its aim: to give wisdom, instruction, prudence, and discernment to the simple, the young, and even the wise. Its motto governs everything that follows: “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, But fools despise wisdom and instruction” (1:7).

II. A Father’s Call and Wisdom’s Cry (Proverbs 1:8–4:27)

A father urges his son to refuse the enticement of sinners and to listen as Wisdom herself cries out in the streets, warning the simple, the scorners, and the fools who refuse her rebuke. He shows that wisdom must be sought “as silver” and comes from the LORD (2:4–6), calls the son to trust the LORD with all his heart and receive His correction (3:5–12), and praises Wisdom as “a tree of life” (3:18). The movement ends with two paths, the path of the just that “shines ever brighter” and the dark way of the wicked, and the charge, “Keep your heart with all diligence” (4:18–19, 23).

III. Warnings on the Path: Adultery and the Steps into Sin (Proverbs 5:1–7:27)

The father warns against the immoral woman whose lips “drip honey” but whose end is bitter, and commends joyful faithfulness to the wife of one’s youth (5:3–4, 15–19). He adds practical warnings about rash pledges, laziness, the troublemaker, and the seven things the LORD hates (6:1–19), then returns to adultery as fire that cannot be carried without being burned (6:27). Chapter 7 watches a young man “devoid of understanding” walk step by step into the seductress’s house, “the way to hell” (7:7, 27), the book’s clearest picture of how anyone enters the path of sin.

IV. Wisdom Revealed and the Two Invitations (Proverbs 8:1–9:18)

Wisdom speaks openly of her truth, her riches, and her rule, and then of her presence with the LORD before creation, “beside Him as a master craftsman,” rejoicing in His world and delighting in the sons of men (8:30–31); “whoever finds me finds life” (8:35). Wisdom builds her house and spreads her table of bread and wine for the simple (9:1–6), while the foolish woman copies her invitation and offers “stolen water” to guests who end “in the depths of hell” (9:17–18). These chapters bring the prologue to its climax and set the choice before every reader.

V. Solomon’s Proverbs: The Righteous and the Wicked (Proverbs 10:1–15:33)

A new heading, “The proverbs of Solomon” (10:1), begins hundreds of short sayings, most of them sharp contrasts between the wise and the foolish, the righteous and the wicked, the diligent and the lazy. They apply the prologue’s two ways to speech, work, money, family, and friendship. The movement closes with “The fear of the LORD is the instruction of wisdom, And before honor is humility” (15:33).

VI. Solomon’s Proverbs: The LORD Directs the Way (Proverbs 16:1–22:16)

The sayings continue, opening with the LORD’s rule over human plans: “A man’s heart plans his way, But the LORD directs his steps” (16:9). More sayings concern the king, justice, pride and humility, the poor, and the household, and more of them develop one thought across both lines. Wisdom is shown to be lived under the sovereignty of a God who weighs the heart.

VII. The Words of the Wise (Proverbs 22:17–24:34)

A fresh call, “Incline your ear and hear the words of the wise” (22:17), introduces longer instructions on the poor, rulers, wealth, appetite, drink, and the family, with renewed appeals to “My son, give me your heart” (23:26). A short appendix, “These things also belong to the wise” (24:23), adds sayings on justice and honest work and ends at the field of the lazy man, overgrown and broken down (24:30–34).

VIII. Solomon’s Proverbs Copied by Hezekiah’s Men: Wisdom in Pictures (Proverbs 25:1–27:27)

These are proverbs of Solomon “which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied” (25:1), opening with the glory of God to conceal a matter and the glory of kings to search it out (25:2). Many are vivid comparisons from nature and daily life, with clusters on the fool, the lazy man, and the troublemaker (26:1–28) and on friendship and diligence (27:1–27).

IX. Hezekiah’s Collection: The Righteous, the Wicked, and Those Who Rule (Proverbs 28:1–29:27)

The collection returns to direct contrasts between the righteous and the wicked, with special attention to rulers, justice, the poor, confession, and trust in the LORD: “He who covers his sins will not prosper, But whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy” (28:13). It ends with the righteous and the wicked finding each other an abomination (29:27).

X. The Words of Agur (Proverbs 30:1–33)

Agur confesses the limits of human understanding and asks who has ascended into heaven, “What is His name, and what is His Son’s name, If you know?” (30:4). He rests on the purity of God’s word (30:5), prays for neither poverty nor riches, and sets out numerical sayings of things never satisfied, things too wonderful to understand, and small creatures that are exceedingly wise.

XI. The Words of King Lemuel (Proverbs 31:1–9)

A queen mother teaches her royal son not to give his strength to women or to drink, but to use his authority for those who cannot speak for themselves: “Open your mouth, judge righteously, And plead the cause of the poor and needy” (31:9).

XII. The Woman of Valor (Proverbs 31:10–31)

An acrostic poem asks, “Who can find a virtuous wife?” (31:10) and portrays her strength, industry, generosity, wisdom, and trustworthiness. The book closes on the source of her worth: “a woman who fears the LORD, she shall be praised” (31:30), returning to the fear of the LORD with which it began.
