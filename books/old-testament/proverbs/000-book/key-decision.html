Book Questions

Are the proverbs promises? (Proverbs 3:16; 10:27; 15:1; 22:6)

No. Proverbs state what is generally true in God’s ordered world, not guarantees for every case. “A soft answer turns away wrath” (15:1) is wise and usually true, yet Jesus’ gentle answers did not always turn away wrath. Long life is promised to the wise (3:16; 10:27), yet Jesus, who had all wisdom, died young. God’s will stands over any general rule.

This matters most for sayings about raising children (22:6), long life, and wealth. A proverb is never a reason to blame a grieving parent, a sick person, or someone who is poor.

Read: Proverbs 3:13–18, Proverbs 10:27, Proverbs 15:1, Proverbs 26:4–5 · Related: Does “Train up a child” guarantee that children will not stray?, Does Proverbs promise wealth to those who honor God?

Did Solomon write all of Proverbs? (Proverbs 1:1; 22:17; 25:1; 30:1; 31:1)

No, and the book says so. Proverbs is a collection headed by Solomon (1:1; 10:1), with sayings also credited to “the wise” (22:17; 24:23), to Agur (30:1), and to King Lemuel (31:1). “The men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied” one collection of Solomon’s proverbs (25:1). MTB credits Solomon with the collections the text assigns to him, not with the whole book.

Jewish tradition identified Agur and Lemuel with Solomon, but the text does not. MTB understands them to be otherwise unknown wise men.

Read: Proverbs 1:1, Proverbs 10:1, Proverbs 22:17, Proverbs 25:1, Proverbs 30:1, Proverbs 31:1

Does Proverbs promise wealth to those who honor God? (Proverbs 3:9–10; 10:22)

“Honor the LORD with your possessions... so your barns will be filled with plenty” (3:9–10), and “The blessing of the LORD makes one rich” (10:22). These sayings are true, but they are not a formula. Proverbs also warns again and again against greed and trusting in riches (11:28; 23:4–5; 28:20).

MTB reads them with the principle that proverbs are not promises. God promises to meet our needs, and He gives His presence, peace, and power to those who walk His way; He does not promise to make everyone wealthy. Proverbs 3:9 speaks of firstfruits. The tithe may be connected as an application, but Proverbs does not command the tithe.

Read: Proverbs 3:9–10, Proverbs 10:22, Proverbs 11:28, Proverbs 23:4–5, Proverbs 28:20 · Related: Are the proverbs promises?

What does “the fear of the LORD” mean in Proverbs? (Proverbs 1:7; 9:10; 15:33)

It is a right view of God. “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge” (1:7) because once God is seen rightly, everything else comes into focus. It belongs with “the knowledge of the Holy One” (9:10), and God’s holiness means He is unlike any other (Exodus 15:11; Isaiah 6:3).

How it is felt depends on where a person stands. For the unrepentant it is terror: “It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God” (Hebrews 10:31). For everyone it is awe at His glory, majesty, and holiness. It is more than respect, yet it is not cringing dread. It hates evil (8:13), turns from it (3:7), and draws the humble near to a God who is good.

Read: Proverbs 1:7, Proverbs 9:10, Isaiah 6:1–7, Hebrews 12:28–29

I. The Purpose of Proverbs and the Fear of the LORD (Proverbs 1:1–7)

Proverbs 1 · 1 question

Is “I will pour out my spirit on you” a prophecy of Pentecost? (v. 23)

Wisdom calls, “Turn at my rebuke; surely I will pour out my spirit on you; I will make my words known to you” (v. 23). The NKJV uses a lowercase “spirit.” In context, Wisdom promises to pour out her mind and words on those who turn.

The wording anticipates God’s later promise to pour out His Spirit on all flesh (Joel 2:28), fulfilled at Pentecost (Acts 2:17). That is a connection across Scripture, not a direct prophecy of Pentecost.

Read: Proverbs 1:20–33, Joel 2:28–29, Acts 2:16–18

II. A Father’s Call and Wisdom’s Cry (Proverbs 1:8–4:27)

Proverbs 2 · 1 question

Was the “immoral woman” a foreigner? (vv. 16–19)

The NKJV calls her “the immoral woman” and “the seductress” (v. 16). The Hebrew words mean an outsider or foreign woman. She “forsakes the companion of her youth, and forgets the covenant of her God” (v. 17), and later says, “my husband is not at home” (7:19).

MTB understands her by covenant, not necessarily ethnicity: she is a woman who is not committed to keeping covenant with God. She stands outside that covenant or has forsaken it, breaking faith with her husband and with God (v. 17; Malachi 2:14). The Hebrew word for “strange” marks what lies outside God’s appointed bounds (Leviticus 10:1; Numbers 1:51). Whether she was an Israelite or a foreigner, the text’s concern is the broken covenant.

She is first a literal adulterous woman. The young man is fully responsible for his own steps (5:22; 7:8), and Proverbs does not portray women in general as the danger.

Read: Proverbs 2:16–19, Proverbs 5:1–8, Proverbs 7:5, 19, Malachi 2:14

III. Warnings on the Path: Adultery and the Steps into Sin (Proverbs 5:1–7:27)

Proverbs 5 · 1 question

Does Proverbs treat sexual desire as shameful? (vv. 15–23)

No. The father speaks openly: “rejoice with the wife of your youth... always be enraptured with her love” (vv. 18–19). Within marriage, sexual love is God’s good gift for joy as well as for children.

Outside God’s design, it is “fire” in the bosom (6:27) and leads to death (5:5; 7:27). Proverbs keeps both truths together: there is a right and enjoyable way and a wrong and harmful way.

Read: Proverbs 5:1–23, Proverbs 6:27–29, Song of Solomon 8:6–7, Hebrews 13:4 · Related: Is Proverbs 7 only about adultery?

Proverbs 7 · 1 question

Is Proverbs 7 only about adultery? (vv. 6–27)

Proverbs 7 is first a warning against adultery. A simple young man walks near the woman’s corner at twilight, listens to her flattery, and “immediately he went after her, as an ox goes to the slaughter” (v. 22). “Her house is the way to hell, descending to the chambers of death” (v. 27).

MTB also reads Proverbs 7 as showing the pattern by which anyone enters, or refuses, the path of sin: drifting near temptation, lingering in the dark, listening to its flattery, yielding all at once, and not knowing “it would cost his life” (v. 23). “The wages of sin is death” (Romans 6:23). The chapter is still about adultery; the pattern applies to every sin.

Read: Proverbs 7:1–27, James 1:14–15, Romans 6:23 · Related: Does Proverbs treat sexual desire as shameful?

IV. Wisdom Revealed and the Two Invitations (Proverbs 8:1–9:18)

Proverbs 8 · 2 questions

Is the Wisdom who speaks in Proverbs 8 Jesus? (vv. 1–36)

In Proverbs 8, Wisdom speaks as a person present with God before creation: “I was beside Him as a master craftsman; and I was daily His delight” (v. 30). MTB reads Wisdom personified, especially here, as revealing the eternal Son of God. The New Testament calls Christ “the wisdom of God” (1 Corinthians 1:24; Colossians 2:3) and says all things were made through Him (John 1:1–3). Early church writers and later Protestant commentators read Proverbs 8 of the Son.

The poem portrays Wisdom as a woman; the Hebrew word is feminine. That is grammar and poetry. Wisdom is never a goddess, and Christ is not made feminine. Personification alone does not prove a divine person, since Folly is personified too (9:13–18); the identification rests on the New Testament’s witness. Many interpreters read the chapter as a personification of God’s wisdom only.

Read: Proverbs 8:1–36, John 1:1–3, 1 Corinthians 1:24, 30, Colossians 1:15–17, Colossians 2:3 · Related: Does “The LORD possessed me at the beginning” mean the Son was created?

Does “The LORD possessed me at the beginning” mean the Son was created? (vv. 22–23)

No. “The LORD possessed me at the beginning of His way, before His works of old. I have been established from everlasting” (vv. 22–23). The poem’s point is that wisdom has belonged to God from everlasting. In the early church, some used this verse to argue that the Son was created; the church rejected that teaching.

In MTB’s reading, Wisdom reveals the eternal Son, through whom all things were made (John 1:1–3; Colossians 1:16–17). His existence has no beginning. The translation of “possessed” is debated.

Read: Proverbs 8:22–31, John 1:1–3, Colossians 1:15–17, Hebrews 1:2–3 · Related: Is the Wisdom who speaks in Proverbs 8 Jesus?

Proverbs 9 · 1 question

What do Wisdom’s banquet and Folly’s “stolen water” picture? (vv. 1–18)

Wisdom “has built her house, she has hewn out her seven pillars,” prepared her meat and mixed her wine, and sent out her maidens to call: “Come, eat of my bread and drink of the wine I have mixed. Forsake foolishness and live” (vv. 1–6). Folly sits at her own door and calls the same passersby with the same words, offering “stolen water” and “bread eaten in secret,” but her guests “are in the depths of hell” (vv. 13–18).

MTB reads Wisdom’s invitation first as a call to life. At a deeper level, Wisdom is Christ and her house is the church He builds. The meat and wine point to His sacrifice and the Lord’s Supper, where He invites the hungry to His table (John 6:35; Matthew 22:1–10), and the maidens are His messengers.

Folly is the counterfeit way of life: sin, the world, and idolatry, beginning with stolen pleasure. Her “stolen water” echoes the father’s picture of marriage in 5:15–18, and her house is the adulteress’s house (7:27). False teaching is one voice she can speak with (Revelation 2:20; 2 Peter 2:18), but that is an application, not the meaning of the passage.

Read: Proverbs 9:1–18, Proverbs 5:15–18, John 6:35, Matthew 22:1–10 · Related: Is the Wisdom who speaks in Proverbs 8 Jesus? · Is Proverbs 7 only about adultery?

V. Solomon’s Proverbs: The Righteous and the Wicked (Proverbs 10:1–15:33)

Proverbs 13 · 1 question

Do the verses about “the rod” approve harsh punishment? (13:24; 22:15; 23:13–14; 29:15)

“He who spares his rod hates his son, but he who loves him disciplines him promptly” (13:24). Loving, consistent correction is a parent’s duty, and it reflects the Father’s own correction of His children (3:11–12; Hebrews 12:5–11). The rod was the tool of correction in its setting.

These verses never justify harshness, rage, or abuse, which contradict God’s goodness. They are to be applied with care in modern parenting.

Read: Proverbs 3:11–12, Proverbs 13:24, Proverbs 22:15, Proverbs 29:15, Ephesians 6:4, Hebrews 12:5–11

VI. Solomon’s Proverbs: The LORD Directs the Way (Proverbs 16:1–22:16)

Proverbs 18 · 1 question

Who is the “friend who sticks closer than a brother”? (v. 24)

“A man who has friends must himself be friendly, but there is a friend who sticks closer than a brother” (v. 24). In its plain sense, the proverb contrasts many casual friends with one true, loyal friend.

At a deeper level, MTB sees Jesus as the Friend who sticks closer than a brother. He called His disciples friends and laid down His life for them (John 15:13–15).

Read: Proverbs 17:17, Proverbs 18:24, John 15:13–15

Proverbs 22 · 1 question

Does “Train up a child” guarantee that children will not stray? (v. 6)

“Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it” (v. 6). This is a general principle of how character is formed, not a guarantee. It does not promise outcomes, and it does not condemn parents whose children have wandered.

“The way he should go” is debated. It may mean the way of God, or a way suited to the child’s own nature. MTB prefers God’s way.

Read: Proverbs 22:6, Proverbs 22:15, Ezekiel 18:20 · Related: Are the proverbs promises?

VIII. Solomon’s Proverbs Copied by Hezekiah’s Men: Wisdom in Pictures (Proverbs 25:1–27:27)

Proverbs 26 · 1 question

“Answer a fool” or “Do not answer a fool”: does Proverbs contradict itself? (vv. 4–5)

“Do not answer a fool according to his folly, lest you also be like him. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own eyes” (vv. 4–5). The two sayings sit side by side on purpose. Sometimes answering a fool lowers you to his level; sometimes silence leaves him thinking he is wise. Wisdom discerns which situation it is.

This shows that wisdom is skill for living, not a rulebook.

Read: Proverbs 26:1–12 · Related: Are the proverbs promises?

IX. Hezekiah’s Collection: The Righteous, the Wicked, and Those Who Rule (Proverbs 28:1–29:27)

Proverbs 29 · 1 question

Is “Where there is no revelation” about leaders casting vision? (v. 18)

“Where there is no revelation, the people cast off restraint; but happy is he who keeps the law” (v. 18). The KJV reads “vision.” The word refers to prophetic revelation from God, and it is paired with keeping God’s law.

The verse is often used as a slogan for organizational vision-casting. Its point is God’s revealed word and obedience to it.

Read: Proverbs 29:18, 1 Samuel 3:1, Amos 8:11–12

X. The Words of Agur (Proverbs 30:1–33)

Proverbs 30 · 2 questions

“What is His Son’s name?”: is Agur speaking of Christ? (v. 4)

Agur asks, “Who has ascended into heaven, or descended?... What is His name, and what is His Son’s name, if you know?” (v. 4). He presses the limits of human knowledge about the Creator.

MTB reads this as a prophetic witness to the Father and the Son, as many early and Reformed commentators have. Jesus answers the question in His words to Nicodemus: “No one has ascended to heaven but He who came down from heaven, that is, the Son of Man” (John 3:13). Agur asks the name without supplying it.

Read: Proverbs 30:1–6, John 3:13, John 1:18

What are the four things “too wonderful” for Agur? (vv. 18–20)

Agur names four things he does not understand: “the way of an eagle in the air, the way of a serpent on a rock, the way of a ship in the midst of the sea, and the way of a man with a virgin” (v. 19). Each moves without leaving a trail. Verse 20 sets beside them the way of an adulterous woman, who also leaves no trace and says, “I have done no wickedness.”

MTB reads verse 19 as deeper than its surface. The eagle pictures Christ’s ascension; the serpent on the rock, Satan’s fruitless temptation of Christ, the Rock; the ship, the church carried through the world by the Spirit; and the man with a virgin, Christ and His Bride, the “great mystery” Paul applies to Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:32).

This is a layered reading. The plain meaning stands first: some ways are too wonderful to trace, while sin hides its own tracks (v. 20).

Read: Proverbs 30:18–20, Acts 1:9, Ephesians 5:32, 2 Corinthians 11:2 · Related: Is the virtuous wife a real woman, a picture of the church, or an ideal every woman must meet?

XII. The Woman of Valor (Proverbs 31:10–31)

Proverbs 31 · 1 question

Is the virtuous wife a real woman, a picture of the church, or an ideal every woman must meet? (vv. 10–31)

“Who can find a virtuous wife? For her worth is far above rubies” (v. 10). The poem is first a portrait of a real, praiseworthy woman of wisdom. She is praised because she “fears the LORD” (v. 30). She is not a checklist that burdens women.

MTB also reads Proverbs 31 as a portrait of the church as the Bride of Christ, prepared and victorious at the end of the age, a reading found in Augustine and later Christian commentators. The details of verses 15, 18, and 21 do not supply an end-time timetable.

Read: Proverbs 31:10–31, Ephesians 5:25–27, Revelation 19:7–8
