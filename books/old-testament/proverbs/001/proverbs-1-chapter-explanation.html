NLT

Proverbs 1:1 These are the proverbs of Solomon, David’s son, king of Israel. (NLT)

Main Idea

The book opens under the name of Solomon, the son of David and king of Israel, the king to whom God gave wisdom. This heading introduces the collection that follows, though later headings show that other wise men also contributed to the book.

Verse Explanation

The book opens under the name of Solomon, the son of David and king of Israel, the king to whom God gave wisdom. This heading introduces the collection that follows, though later headings show that other wise men also contributed to the book.

“The proverbs of Solomon”

The book opens with a title that tells the reader what kind of writing follows and whose name stands over it. A proverb is a short, memorable saying that states a practical truth about life, often by setting one thing beside another for comparison. Such sayings describe how God’s world generally works; they are meant to be weighed and lived, not treated as promises that guarantee an outcome in every case. Solomon’s name stands first because the LORD gave him wisdom in answer to his prayer for “an understanding heart” (1 Kings 3:9), and Scripture says he “spoke three thousand proverbs” (1 Kings 4:32). This heading introduces the whole book, yet later headings name other contributors as well: the wise (22:17; 24:23), Agur (30:1), and King Lemuel (31:1). The book is best described as the wisdom of Solomon and other wise men gathered under his name.

“the son of David, king of Israel:”

These titles anchor the book in Israel’s history. Solomon was David’s son and heir, king over all Israel in the years of peace and plenty before the kingdom divided. A king who had received his own father’s charge now passes wisdom on in a father’s voice, which suits the “my son” addresses that run through chapters 1–9. As son of David, king of peace, and builder of the temple, Solomon also points beyond himself. Jesus, the greater Son of David, said of Himself, “indeed a greater than Solomon is here” (Matthew 12:42). Scripture also records that Solomon’s heart later turned after other gods (1 Kings 11:4). That sober fact does not weaken his proverbs, since their wisdom comes from God, but it shows that possessing wisdom and walking in it are not the same thing.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “proverbs” covers more than short sayings. It can describe a brief maxim, a byword, a parable or comparison, a poem, and even a prophet’s mocking song, which Isaiah calls a “proverb against the king of Babylon” (Isaiah 14:4). That range explains why the title stands over the longer poems of chapters 1–9 as well as over the short sayings that follow.

Solomon’s name appears again in the headings at 10:1 and 25:1. The second of these adds that the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied further proverbs of Solomon, so the book reached its final form no earlier than Hezekiah’s reign.

Proverbs 1:2 Their purpose is to teach people wisdom and discipline,to help them understand the insights of the wise. (NLT)

Main Idea

The first purpose of the book is that its readers come to know wisdom, the skill of living life God’s way, together with the instruction that trains it. It also aims to sharpen discernment, so that a person can perceive and weigh the words of understanding he hears.

Verse Explanation

The first purpose of the book is that its readers come to know wisdom, the skill of living life God’s way, together with the instruction that trains it. It also aims to sharpen discernment, so that a person can perceive and weigh the words of understanding he hears.

The lines of 1:2–6 form one long purpose statement, with line after line opening with “To.” Before a single proverb is given, the reader is told what the book is meant to accomplish in him.

“To know wisdom and instruction,”

Wisdom in Proverbs is skill for living life God’s way: knowing what to do, how to do it, and why, and then doing it in speech, work, money, marriage, and worship. To know it means more than collecting information about it; it means becoming acquainted with it in practice. Beside wisdom stands instruction, the training and correction that discipline a life. The two belong together, for no one becomes skilled at living without being taught and corrected along the way. Proverbs 1:7 will name exactly this pair as what fools despise.

“To perceive the words of understanding,”

To perceive is to discern, to tell one thing from another. Words of understanding are sayings that carry insight, and the book wants its readers able to weigh what they hear. That skill is tested before the chapter ends, when the smooth pitch of violent men and the public cry of Wisdom compete for the same young man. A person who cannot tell those voices apart will usually follow the louder or more attractive one.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “instruction” means training and discipline, and it includes correction. Proverbs uses the same word for “the chastening of the LORD” in 3:11, so the instruction this book offers corrects as well as informs.

The Hebrew word for “wisdom” is also used for practical skill. When the LORD filled Bezalel “with the Spirit of God, in wisdom” for the work of the tabernacle (Exodus 31:3), the word described the skill of a craftsman.

Proverbs 1:3 Their purpose is to teach people to live disciplined and successful lives,to help them do what is right, just, and fair. (NLT)

Main Idea

Proverbs wants its readers to receive the instruction of wisdom as a disciplined way of life, and it names the fruit that follows: justice, judgment, and equity. The skill it teaches is moral as well as practical, showing a person how to do what is right and fair toward God and neighbor.

Verse Explanation

Proverbs wants its readers to receive the instruction of wisdom as a disciplined way of life, and it names the fruit that follows: justice, judgment, and equity. The skill it teaches is moral as well as practical, showing a person how to do what is right and fair toward God and neighbor.

“To receive the instruction of wisdom,”

To receive is to take in what someone else hands over. Wisdom does not rise up from within a person’s own resources; it is received. The phrase translated instruction of wisdom describes training in wise and prudent conduct, the kind of insight that knows how to act well in real situations. The book assumes a learner who is willing to be taught and to accept discipline, an attitude that the next verses describe as the mark of the wise.

“Justice, judgment, and equity;”

Here the book names the fruit of that training. Justice is what is right by God’s standard; judgment is right decision, the fair verdict that settles a matter; equity is straightness, fair dealing with no hidden crookedness. Together they show that the skill Proverbs teaches is moral as well as practical. A person cannot be wise in the book’s sense and crooked at the same time. The point becomes sharp within a few verses, when a gang proposes to ambush “the innocent without cause” (1:11), the very opposite of justice, judgment, and equity.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “wisdom” in this verse is not the word for wisdom used in 1:2. It comes from a verb meaning to act prudently or with insight, and it describes the good sense that knows how to act well and succeed. ESV renders the phrase instruction in “wise dealing,” and NASB instruction in “wise behavior.”

NKJV’s “Justice” translates the Hebrew noun usually rendered “righteousness,” and ESV and NASB begin the list with “righteousness.” The same three Hebrew nouns return together in 2:9, where NKJV translates them “righteousness and justice, Equity.” There they describe what the son who seeks wisdom will come to understand, so the purpose stated here is the result promised there.

Proverbs 1:4
NKJV

Proverbs 1:4 To give prudence to the simple, To the young man knowledge and discretion"”(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 1:4 These proverbs will give insight to the simple,knowledge and discernment to the young. (NLT)

Main Idea

The book is written for the simple, those who are uncommitted and easily led, to give them prudence, the good sense to see danger coming. It also gives the young man knowledge and discretion as he steps into life, so the inexperienced need not learn everything through painful mistakes.

Verse Explanation

The book is written for the simple, those who are uncommitted and easily led, to give them prudence, the good sense to see danger coming. It also gives the young man knowledge and discretion as he steps into life, so the inexperienced need not learn everything through painful mistakes.

“To give prudence to the simple,”

The book is written first for the simple. In Proverbs the simple person is not unintelligent; he is uncommitted, open in every direction, without settled convictions, and so easily led. He is not yet set against wisdom, but he is not yet committed to it either. Proverbs means to give him prudence, the good sense to see danger coming and act on it. A later saying describes this exactly: “A prudent man foresees evil and hides himself, But the simple pass on and are punished” (22:3). The book starts with the person most at risk, which shows the generosity of its purpose.

“To the young man knowledge and discretion—”

The young man stands at the threshold of adult life, where choices about companions, money, work, and sexuality begin to set the direction of everything after. He is offered knowledge and discretion, the ability to think ahead and plan with purpose. Instruction received in advance spares him from having to learn every lesson through painful mistakes. The opening chapters are full of exactly such forewarning, beginning with the scene in 1:10–19.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “simple” comes from a verb meaning to be open or easily persuaded, and that same verb is translated “entice” in 1:10. The simple are, by the very name Proverbs gives them, the people sinners set out to entice.

The Hebrew noun translated “prudence” comes from the same root as the adjective rendered “prudent” in 22:3, a root meaning to be shrewd. That adjective can describe crafty cunning, as with the serpent in Genesis 3:1, where NKJV renders it “cunning.” Proverbs uses these words in their good sense, for the practical shrewdness that sees danger coming.

Proverbs 1:5
NKJV

Proverbs 1:5 A wise man will hear and increase learning, And a man of understanding will attain wise counsel,(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 1:5 Let the wise listen to these proverbs and become even wiser.Let those with understanding receive guidance (NLT)

Main Idea

Proverbs is not only for beginners, for a wise man will listen and keep adding to what he knows, and a man of understanding will gain wise counsel. A teachable spirit marks the wise, and the wiser a person becomes, the more he wants to hear.

Verse Explanation

Proverbs is not only for beginners, for a wise man will listen and keep adding to what he knows, and a man of understanding will gain wise counsel. A teachable spirit marks the wise, and the wiser a person becomes, the more he wants to hear.

“A wise man will hear and increase learning,”

The purpose statement pauses to say that Proverbs is not only for beginners. The first thing said of the wise man is that he hears. Teachability, not intelligence, is what separates him from the fool of 1:7. Wisdom does not end learning; it adds to it, so that the more a person grows wise, the more he wants to hear. A later verse makes the same point: “Give instruction to a wise man, and he will be still wiser” (9:9). The Hebrew word translated learning is related to the verb behind “To receive” in 1:3; it describes teaching that a person takes in and makes his own.

“And a man of understanding will attain wise counsel,”

The man of understanding does not rely on his own insight alone. He gains wise counsel, the kind of guidance needed to steer decisions well. Proverbs repeatedly connects good outcomes with good counsel and failure with going alone: “Where there is no counsel, the people fall” (11:14). A person who has understanding is the very one who knows he still needs direction, and he seeks it out.

Study Notes

“Wise counsel” translates a Hebrew word for guidance in making plans. It always appears in the plural and only in the wisdom books, and it is used for the planning needed to wage war (20:18; 24:6). The word has often been connected with ropes and the steering of a ship, which would picture counsel as the skill of steering a course, though that origin is not certain.

Proverbs 1:6
Proverbs 1:6
NKJV

Proverbs 1:6 To understand a proverb and an enigma, The words of the wise and their riddles.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 1:6 by exploring the meaning in these proverbs and parables,the words of the wise and their riddles. (NLT)

Main Idea

The book also trains its readers to understand a proverb and an enigma, the sayings and riddles of the wise. Some sayings carry meaning beneath their surface, and the reader is called to search them out rather than skim them.

Verse Explanation

The book also trains its readers to understand a proverb and an enigma, the sayings and riddles of the wise. Some sayings carry meaning beneath their surface, and the reader is called to search them out rather than skim them.

“To understand a proverb and an enigma,”

The purpose statement resumes. A proverb, as in 1:1, is a short saying that states a truth, often by comparison. An enigma is a saying whose meaning is not on the surface and must be worked out. Some proverbs are plain at first hearing; others yield their meaning only to a reader who stops, turns them over, and thinks. The book means to train that kind of reader.

“The words of the wise and their riddles.”

The words of the wise are the sayings of Israel’s teachers, and later headings in the book use the same description for whole collections (22:17; 24:23). Riddles are sayings with a hidden meaning that has to be searched out. The book itself praises such searching: “It is the glory of God to conceal a matter, But the glory of kings is to search out a matter” (25:2). This verse is part of the ground for the deeper, layered readings that Proverbs supports at certain points, such as Wisdom’s banquet in chapter 9 and the woman of valor in chapter 31. Such readings always come after the plain meaning and never replace it.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “enigma” is paired with the word for “proverb” again in Habakkuk 2:6, where NKJV renders it “taunting riddle.”

The Hebrew word for “riddles” is the word used of Samson’s riddle in Judges 14:12 and of the “hard questions” with which the queen of Sheba came to test Solomon in 1 Kings 10:1. In both stories the meaning had to be worked out by someone with insight.

Proverbs 1:7
Proverbs 1:7
NKJV

Proverbs 1:7 The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, But fools despise wisdom and instruction.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 1:7 Fear of the LORD is the foundation of true knowledge,but fools despise wisdom and discipline. (NLT)

Main Idea

Here is the motto of the whole book: the fear of the LORD, a right view of God, is the beginning of knowledge, both its starting point and its chief part. Fools despise wisdom and instruction, which shows that their problem is a refusal to bow before God and receive correction, not a lack of intelligence.

Verse Explanation

Here is the motto of the whole book: the fear of the LORD, a right view of God, is the beginning of knowledge, both its starting point and its chief part. Fools despise wisdom and instruction, which shows that their problem is a refusal to bow before God and receive correction, not a lack of intelligence.

“The fear of the LORD”

Here is the motto of the whole book. The fear of the LORD is a right view of God. LORD translates the personal name by which God revealed Himself to Israel, so the wisdom of Proverbs rests on knowing the God who made Himself known, not on human observation alone. How this fear is expressed depends on where a person stands. For those who defy God it rightly includes terror; for all who see Him it means awe at His glory, majesty, and holiness, for He is unlike any other. This fear is never mere respect, yet it does not drive the humble away. The God who is feared is good, and a right view of Him draws people near.

“is the beginning of knowledge,”

Beginning means both the starting point and the chief or foremost part. Knowledge starts with the fear of the LORD, and the fear of the LORD remains its most important element. Once God is seen rightly, everything else can begin to be seen rightly: oneself, other people, money, work, pleasure, and death. A person may be brilliant and still not know anything rightly if he leaves God out.

“But fools despise wisdom and instruction.”

The second line gives the contrast. The fool in Proverbs is not someone lacking intelligence but someone morally resistant. He despises, treating with contempt what he ought to receive. What he despises is the very pair named in 1:2, wisdom and instruction, so the purpose statement ends where it began. The fool’s problem is a refusal to bow before God and accept correction. The motto recurs at key points in the book (9:10; 15:33), and the final portrait honors “a woman who fears the LORD” (31:30).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “beginning” is the word used for “firstfruits” in 3:9, the first and best portion of the harvest. That use supports the sense of foremost as well as first. The related saying in 9:10, “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom,” uses a different Hebrew word that stresses the starting point.

NKJV’s “fools” in this verse and in 1:22 translates two different Hebrew words. Proverbs has several words for the fool, and the difference cannot be seen in English. In both verses the fool is identified by his attitude toward truth: here he despises wisdom and instruction, and in 1:22 he hates knowledge.

The same conviction appears outside Proverbs in nearly the same words: “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom” (Psalm 111:10), and “Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom” (Job 28:28).

Proverbs 1:8–9 — Hear Your Father and Mother

Proverbs 1:8
Proverbs 1:8
NKJV

Proverbs 1:8 My son, hear the instruction of your father, And do not forsake the law of your mother;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 1:8 My child, listen when your father corrects you.Don’t neglect your mother’s instruction. (NLT)

Main Idea

A father now begins speaking to his son, and he sets the mother’s teaching beside his own: “My son, hear the instruction of your father.” The son is to listen to his father and not abandon what his mother has taught him, for wisdom is first handed down in the home.

Verse Explanation

A father now begins speaking to his son, and he sets the mother’s teaching beside his own: “My son, hear the instruction of your father.” The son is to listen to his father and not abandon what his mother has taught him, for wisdom is first handed down in the home.

“My son,”

With these words a new voice begins, and it will speak through most of chapters 1–9. It is a real father preparing a young man for life, using the warm address “My son” again and again. The address later widens to “my children” (7:24), so every reader who listens may hear himself addressed. The father’s voice also carries God’s own fatherly care as He instructs His children, for Hebrews 12:5–6 quotes the similar address of 3:11–12 as God’s word to believers.

“hear the instruction of your father,”

To hear in this sense is to listen with the intention of obeying. The father’s instruction is the same discipline named in 1:2 and 1:7, the training that wise people receive and fools despise. The son’s first task is not to work out wisdom for himself but to listen to someone who has walked further down the road.

“And do not forsake the law of your mother;”

The mother’s teaching stands beside the father’s, and both parents are honored (compare 6:20; 31:1). Law here means teaching or direction, the guidance she has given her son. To forsake is to abandon or let go. Wisdom is first handed down in the home, from parents who teach their children as they live together, as Israel was commanded: “You shall teach them diligently to your children” (Deuteronomy 6:7).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “law” is torah, the word Israel used for the Law of Moses, but its basic sense is instruction or direction. Proverbs uses it of the mother’s teaching here and in 6:20, and of the woman of valor’s speech in 31:26, “the law of kindness.”

Other ancient wisdom writings were framed the same way. The Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope, for example, is presented as a father’s teaching to his son, so the form of these chapters would have been familiar to their first hearers.

Main Idea

Parental instruction is pictured as a graceful ornament on the head and chains about the neck, signs of honor and beauty. A son who keeps what he was taught wears it openly, and his life displays the dignity his parents’ wisdom gives him.

Verse Explanation

Parental instruction is pictured as a graceful ornament on the head and chains about the neck, signs of honor and beauty. A son who keeps what he was taught wears it openly, and his life displays the dignity his parents’ wisdom gives him.

“For they will be a graceful ornament on your head,”

“For” introduces the reason to listen. The parents’ teaching is pictured as a graceful ornament, a garland or wreath worn on the head at times of honor and celebration. What the son has been taught does not stay hidden; he wears it, and others see the dignity it gives him. Instruction that may feel like restriction to a young man turns out to be his crowning honor.

“And chains about your neck.”

Chains here are necklaces, ornaments of honor. When Pharaoh raised Joseph to authority, he “put a gold chain around his neck” (Genesis 41:42). The picture is of a life that displays its upbringing well. The contrast with the next scene is deliberate: the gang will offer the son spoil and a share in their purse (1:13–14), but the true adornment of his life is the wisdom his father and mother gave him.

Study Notes

The same ornament reappears in 4:9, where Wisdom “will place on your head an ornament of grace.” Proverbs 3:3 and 6:21 also speak of binding wise teaching around the neck.

Proverbs 1:10–19 — Refuse the Enticement of Sinners

Proverbs 1:10
Proverbs 1:10
NKJV

Proverbs 1:10 My son, if sinners entice you, Do not consent.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 1:10 My child, if sinners entice you,turn your back on them! (NLT)

Main Idea

The father’s first warning concerns companions. When sinners try to entice the son, the answer is short and firm, “Do not consent,” with no room left for negotiation.

Verse Explanation

The father’s first warning concerns companions. When sinners try to entice the son, the answer is short and firm, “Do not consent,” with no room left for negotiation.

“My son, if sinners entice you,”

The father’s first warning concerns companions. The sinners here are not people who occasionally stumble; they are men whose way of life is set on wrongdoing, and the next verses show them to be a violent gang. They do not force the son; they entice him. Their method is persuasion, and their target is the simple young man whose heart is open to whatever voice reaches it first. The father assumes that this pressure will come. The question is not whether his son will be tempted but how he will answer.

“Do not consent.”

The answer is short and absolute. In Hebrew it is only two words. The father does not tell his son to listen to the offer, weigh its benefits, and then decide; he tells him to refuse before the conversation goes any further. To consent is to be willing, to give inward agreement. Temptation requires the son’s agreement to succeed, so the responsibility rests with him. A firm no at the beginning spares him from being argued step by step into something he would never have chosen at the outset.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “consent” means to be willing. The same verb returns in 1:25 and 1:30, where those who refuse Wisdom “would have none” of her rebuke and counsel. The son is told not to be willing toward sinners; the scoffers are not willing toward Wisdom.

Proverbs 1:11
NKJV

Proverbs 1:11 If they say, "Come with us, Let us lie in wait to shed blood; Let us lurk secretly for the innocent without cause;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 1:11 They may say, “Come and join us.Let’s hide and kill someone!Just for fun, let’s ambush the innocent! (NLT)

Main Idea

The father voices the gang’s own pitch so the son will recognize it: “Come with us,” they say, inviting him to ambush and murder the innocent without cause. Their appeal begins with belonging and only then reveals the violence behind it.

Verse Explanation

The father voices the gang’s own pitch so the son will recognize it: “Come with us,” they say, inviting him to ambush and murder the innocent without cause. Their appeal begins with belonging and only then reveals the violence behind it.

“Come with us,”

The words begin, “If they say,” and the father now voices the gang’s own words so that his son will recognize them when he hears them. Their pitch opens with belonging: “Come with us.” Before any crime is named, the young man is offered a place in a group. That is often how the path of sin begins, with an appeal to friendship and acceptance that makes the wrong itself seem secondary.

“Let us lie in wait to shed blood;”

Then the plan comes out. To lie in wait is to set an ambush. The gang intends robbery with murder, the kind of highway violence that threatened travelers in ancient Israel. In the days of the judges, the men of Shechem “set men in ambush against him on the tops of the mountains, and they robbed all who passed by them” (Judges 9:25). The gang speaks of shedding blood as casually as if it were an ordinary day’s work.

“Let us lurk secretly for the innocent without cause;”

To lurk secretly is to hide and wait for a victim. Their targets are innocent people who have done them no harm, and the attack is without cause, wholly unprovoked. The words expose what the offer of friendship concealed. Joining these companions means joining in the shedding of innocent blood.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “without cause” means for nothing or for no reason. It can be read with the ambush (attacking for no reason) or with the victims (people who have given no reason to be attacked), and the sense is much the same either way.

Proverbs 1:12
NKJV

Proverbs 1:12 Let us swallow them alive like Sheol, And whole, like those who go down to the Pit;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 1:12 Let’s swallow them alive, like the grave ;let’s swallow them whole, like those who go down to the pit of death. (NLT)

Main Idea

The gang boasts that they will swallow their victims alive and whole, like Sheol, the grave, and like those who go down to the Pit. They speak as if they held the power of death itself, which shows how cruel and arrogant their plan really is.

Verse Explanation

The gang boasts that they will swallow their victims alive and whole, like Sheol, the grave, and like those who go down to the Pit. They speak as if they held the power of death itself, which shows how cruel and arrogant their plan really is.

“Let us swallow them alive like Sheol,”

Sheol is the Hebrew name for the grave, the realm of the dead. Scripture pictures it as a mouth that swallows and is never full. The gang boasts that they will be like Sheol itself, swallowing their victims alive. The words recall the judgment on Korah’s company, when the earth opened and they “went down alive into the pit” (Numbers 16:33). These men speak as if they held the power of death in their own hands.

“And whole, like those who go down to the Pit;”

The Pit stands in parallel with Sheol, another name for the grave. To swallow victims whole is to leave nothing behind. The boast is meant to sound bold and fearless, the kind of talk that can impress a young man who wants to feel strong. The father lets the gang speak in full so that their arrogance and cruelty become plain. Their talk of death will come back on them, for their own lives are what they are really putting at risk (1:18).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “whole” means complete, and NKJV renders the same word “blameless” in 2:21. Here it describes the victims swallowed entire, and it may also hint that they are blameless people, like “the innocent” of 1:11.

In Numbers 16:30 and 16:33 the word NKJV renders “pit” is the Hebrew word Sheol, the same word used here for the grave, the realm of the dead.

Proverbs 1:13

Main Idea

Their real motive comes out: plunder. They promise all kinds of precious possessions and houses filled with spoil, making violent gain sound like quick and easy wealth, and the riches become the bait meant to draw the young man in.

Verse Explanation

Their real motive comes out: plunder. They promise all kinds of precious possessions and houses filled with spoil, making violent gain sound like quick and easy wealth, and the riches become the bait meant to draw the young man in.

“We shall find all kinds of precious possessions,”

The real motive now appears: wealth. The gang promises every sort of valuable thing. Murder is presented as a shortcut to riches, and the reward is described in glowing terms while the bloodshed fades into the background. The bait is the young man’s desire for quick and easy gain.

“We shall fill our houses with spoil;”

Spoil is plunder, what is stripped from victims or taken in war. The gang pictures homes filled with stolen goods. Proverbs will say much about houses and what fills them. Here the house is filled by violence, and a later saying warns that “He who is greedy for gain troubles his own house” (15:27). The riches promised are real enough in the short term, which is what makes the offer tempting; the father’s answer will be that such riches cost the lives of those who take them (1:19).

None.

The pitch closes with an offer of partnership: cast your lot with us and share one purse. Belonging and an equal share of the profits are held out to a young man who wants both, but joining the gang would mean sharing its guilt and its end.

“Cast in your lot among us,”

Casting lots was a common way of making decisions and dividing property in Israel. To cast in one’s lot with others is to join them and share their fortune, whatever it may be. The gang offers the young man full membership. He will no longer stand outside wondering whether he belongs; he will be one of them.

“Let us all have one purse”—

The purse is the common bag that holds the takings. One purse means an equal share for every member, and that promise is attractive to a young man who has little of his own. Belonging and fairness are both offered, though only among thieves and at the expense of innocent victims. The offer hides its real cost. To share the gang’s purse is to share its guilt and its end, and the father will show that the end is death (1:18–19). The dash at the end of the verse marks the moment the father breaks off the gang’s speech and speaks to his son directly.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “purse” is the word used for the merchant’s bag of weights in 16:11, “All the weights in the bag are His work.” There the bag holds honest weights that belong to the LORD; here it holds the takings of robbery.

The lot appears again in 16:33, “The lot is cast into the lap, But its every decision is from the LORD.”

The meaning of this saying is debated. Many take it as above: a bird avoids a net it sees, but the gang does not. Others read it as a bird that sees the net and still flies into it for the bait, so that even plain warning does not stop the greedy. A third view applies it to the son, who has been warned and should not be caught. On each reading 1:18 makes the same point.

The Hebrew word translated “in vain” is the same word rendered “without cause” in 1:11. The gang attacks the innocent for nothing, and their own net is spread for nothing.

“Wisdom calls aloud outside;”

A second voice now rises against the gang’s whispers. Wisdom is pictured as a woman who speaks in person. The gang lurked in secret; Wisdom calls aloud outside, in the open, where anyone can hear. The Hebrew noun for wisdom is feminine, and the poem gives her a woman’s voice. This is personification, a poetic portrait of wisdom as a living person who calls, rebukes, warns, and promises.

“She raises her voice in the open squares.”

The open squares were the broad public places of a town, crowded with people buying, selling, and talking. Wisdom does not hide in a temple court or a scholar’s room. She raises her voice where ordinary people spend their days, and she sounds like one of Israel’s prophets standing in the street. Read in the fuller light of the New Testament, this voice is the voice of Christ, whom Paul calls “the wisdom of God” (1 Corinthians 1:24), the One “in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and knowledge” (Colossians 2:3). That identification rests on the New Testament’s testimony rather than on the personification itself, since Folly is also pictured as a woman who calls (9:13–16). The feminine form belongs to the poem’s language; Wisdom is neither a goddess nor a picture of Christ as feminine. Jesus Himself spoke of “the wisdom of God” sending prophets (Luke 11:49).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “Wisdom” here is in a plural form, yet the verb with it is singular, so one speaker is in view. The same form appears in 9:1. The plural probably expresses the fullness of wisdom; KJV’s margin explains it as “Excellent wisdom.”

The Hebrew verb translated “calls aloud” usually describes a ringing shout, often of joy. Here it describes a loud public cry that summons people to listen.

“For scorners delight in their scorning,”

The scorner is a mocker who enjoys ridiculing correction and the people who give it. His problem is a matter of the will: the fool may lack sense, but the scorner refuses to be wise. The fool goes further than indifference: he hates knowledge. The three types move from loving simplicity, to delighting in mockery, to hating knowledge, which suggests how a person can harden over time. Wisdom addresses all three in one appeal. None of them is beyond her call while she is still calling.

“And did not choose the fear of the LORD,”

The fear of the LORD is presented as something a person chooses. Those who perish did not choose it; they preferred their own way to a right view of God. This takes the reader straight back to the motto in 1:7. Knowledge begins with the fear of the LORD, and ruin follows when a person refuses it. Wisdom’s speech also shows that rejecting her is the same as rejecting the LORD Himself, for her counsel and the fear of the LORD go together. The verse places responsibility squarely on those who refused.

Study Notes

“The fear of the LORD” appears in 1:7 and again in 1:29, so that the chapter’s argument is enclosed between the beginning of knowledge and the refusal that leads to ruin.

Wisdom repeats the charge: they would have none of her counsel and despised every rebuke she gave. The repetition stresses that they were not left without warning; they were corrected again and again and refused each time.

“They would have none of my counsel”

Wisdom repeats the charge of 1:25 almost word for word. The repetition is deliberate. These people were not left without warning or without guidance. Counsel was offered, and they would have none of it.

“And despised my every rebuke.”

To despise is to treat with contempt. This is stronger than the disdain of 1:25, and it was their response to every rebuke, not just one. Correction came again and again, and each time it was spurned. When the end comes, they cannot claim that no one told them. The verse closes the case Wisdom has been building since 1:24.

Verse Explanation

Therefore they will eat the fruit of their own way and be filled to the full with their own fancies. No new punishment needs to be invented, for the schemes they preferred become the harvest they must eat.

“Therefore they shall eat the fruit of their own way,”

“Therefore” brings the conclusion. The judgment Wisdom announces is that they will eat the fruit of their own way. No new punishment has to be invented; the choices they made produce their own harvest. A person who chooses a way chooses its destination. This principle runs throughout Scripture: “whatever a man sows, that he will also reap” (Galatians 6:7). The word way connects the verse with the road picture of 1:15 and 1:19.

“And be filled to the full with their own fancies.”

Their fancies are their schemes, their own plans and ideas about how to live. They refused Wisdom’s counsel and preferred their own; now they will have all of their own they can stomach, and more. To be filled to the full is to be glutted. The gang hoped to fill their houses with spoil (1:13). Those who refuse Wisdom are filled as well, with the bitter results of their own devices.

Study Notes

NKJV’s “fancies” translates a Hebrew word meaning plans or schemes; KJV and ESV render it “devices.” It comes from the same root as the word for “counsel” in 1:25 and 1:30, so those who rejected Wisdom’s counsel are filled with counsels of their own.

Verse Explanation

Wisdom names two ways people perish: the simple are slain by their turning away, and fools are destroyed by their complacency. Neither needs open rebellion to be lost, since drifting from wisdom and careless ease are deadly enough.

“For the turning away of the simple will slay them,”

Wisdom names two ways people perish. The first is the turning away of the simple. Wisdom called them to turn at her rebuke (1:23); instead they turned away. The simple do not have to rebel openly to be lost. Drifting away from wisdom, a little at a time and without settled commitment, is enough to kill them. That is why the book is so urgent for the simple, who are always open to the next voice.

“And the complacency of fools will destroy them;”

The second way is the complacency of fools. Complacency is careless ease, a false sense of security that assumes nothing bad will happen. Fools feel comfortable, and their comfort is what destroys them. Neither line describes a dramatic act of defiance. One is drift and the other is ease, and both are deadly. The danger in each is that it does not feel like danger to the person in it.

Study Notes

The Hebrew noun for “turning away” comes from the verb translated “Turn” in 1:23. Jeremiah uses it for Israel’s backsliding: “Return, you backsliding children, And I will heal your backslidings” (Jeremiah 3:22).

KJV renders “complacency” as “prosperity.” The Hebrew word describes ease or quiet, and here the context shows a careless ease.

Verse Explanation

The speech ends with hope: whoever listens to Wisdom will dwell safely and be secure, without fear of evil. This is the general truth of the wise way, a security that rests on hearing her, set against the ruin just described.

“But whoever listens to me will dwell safely,”

Wisdom’s speech ends with hope. The way out of all she has described is simple: listen to her. To listen is to hear and heed, the same response the wise man gives in 1:5 and the son is asked to give in 1:8. Whoever listens, whether simple, young, or long resistant, will dwell safely. The promise is open to anyone.

“And will be secure, without fear of evil.”

The listener will be secure, at ease, undisturbed by the terror that sweeps over those who refuse Wisdom (1:26–27). This is a different kind of ease from the complacency of fools in 1:32. Theirs was false security that ignored danger; this is true security that rests on hearing wisdom. Like the rest of Proverbs, the line states what is generally true of the wise way, not a promise that the wise will never suffer. The deepest security this line promises is found in the One whom Wisdom pictures. Jesus said, “My sheep hear My voice, and I know them, and they follow Me” (John 10:27).

Study Notes

The promise of security is developed in 3:23–26, where the son who keeps wisdom is told, “Do not be afraid of sudden terror” (3:25).
