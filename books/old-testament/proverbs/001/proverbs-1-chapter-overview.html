Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 1
Main Idea:

Proverbs 1 states the book’s purpose and its motto, that the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, and then sets two voices before a young man: sinners who invite him into violent greed, and Wisdom, who calls him to turn and live.

Setting:

Solomon’s name heads the book (1:1), though later headings name other wise men as well. A father, honoring the mother’s teaching beside his own, prepares his son for the voices he will meet in the streets.

Chapter Outline:

  • Proverbs 1:1–7 — The Purpose of Proverbs and the Fear of the LORD

  • Proverbs 1:8–9 — Hear Your Father and Mother

  • Proverbs 1:10–19 — Refuse the Enticement of Sinners

  • Proverbs 1:20–23 — Wisdom Calls the Simple to Turn

  • Proverbs 1:24–33 — The End of Refusing Wisdom

Key Verse:

“The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, But fools despise wisdom and instruction.” (Proverbs 1:7)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Proverbs 1 is the doorway to the whole book. It offers wisdom, the skill of living life God’s way, along with instruction, prudence, and wise counsel (1:2–5). It is written for “the simple” and “the young man,” yet “A wise man will hear and increase learning” (1:4–5). Then comes the motto that governs everything after it: “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge” (1:7). The fear of the LORD is a right view of God, and once God is seen rightly, everything else can begin to be seen rightly. Fools despise this instruction because their trouble lies in the will, not the mind.

The rest of the chapter sets two voices side by side. A gang of sinners offers the son belonging and easy money, “Let us all have one purse” (1:14), but the father shows that they “lie in wait for their own blood” (1:18). Then Wisdom raises her voice at the city gates, calling the simple, the scorners, and the fools to turn, and warning that those who refuse “shall eat the fruit of their own way” (1:31). The poem pictures Wisdom as a woman calling in public. In the fuller light of the New Testament, this is the voice of Christ, “the wisdom of God” (1 Corinthians 1:24), who still calls people to turn and live.

Chapter Sections

Proverbs 1:1–7 — The Purpose of Proverbs and the Fear of the LORD

The book names Solomon and states its aim: to give wisdom, instruction, and discernment to the simple, the young, and even the wise, and to train readers to search out the riddles of the wise. The introduction closes with the motto of the whole book, that the fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, while fools despise wisdom and instruction.

Proverbs 1:8–9 — Hear Your Father and Mother

A father calls his son to hear his instruction and not to forsake his mother’s teaching. What the son receives at home becomes his honor, like a graceful ornament on his head and chains about his neck.

Proverbs 1:10–19 — Refuse the Enticement of Sinners

The father voices the gang’s invitation to ambush the innocent and share the spoil, so that his son will recognize it, and he answers it with a firm “Do not consent.” Their violence will turn back on them, because greed “takes away the life of its owners” (1:19).

Proverbs 1:20–23 — Wisdom Calls the Simple to Turn

Wisdom cries out in the busiest public places, asking how long the simple will love simplicity, the scorners their scorning, and the fools their hatred of knowledge. To all who turn at her rebuke she promises to pour out her spirit and make her words known, wording that anticipates the LORD’s later promise to pour out His Spirit (Joel 2:28).

Proverbs 1:24–33 — The End of Refusing Wisdom

Because they refused her call and despised her correction, Wisdom will not answer when calamity comes, and they will reap the fruit of their own choices. The chapter ends with two outcomes: the turning away of the simple will slay them, but whoever listens to Wisdom “will dwell safely” (1:33).

Why This Chapter Matters

Proverbs 1 lays the foundation for reading the whole book. Because wisdom begins with the fear of the LORD, the practical counsel that follows about money, speech, work, and family is never mere self-improvement; it is life lived with a right view of God. The sayings ahead describe how God’s world generally works, and they are read as wisdom rather than as promises for every case.

The chapter also shows how people are lost. The gang appeals to belonging and quick gain, and the greatest danger for the simple is drift: “the turning away of the simple will slay them” (1:32). Wisdom’s warning is severe, yet she speaks it while she is still calling, and the Lord behind her call is “not willing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance” (2 Peter 3:9).

Key Themes to Watch For

The fear of the LORD. A right view of God is the beginning of knowledge, and refusing to choose it lies at the root of ruin (1:7, 29).

Two voices and two ways. Sinners and Wisdom both call to the son, and each path leads to its own end (1:10–19, 20–33).

Listening and correction. The wise hear and increase learning, while fools despise instruction and refuse rebuke (1:5, 7, 23–25, 30).

Greed that destroys itself. The violence the gang plans for others returns on its own members (1:11–19).

Wisdom’s public call. Wisdom speaks in the streets and gates where ordinary life is lived, and her call is urgent because the time to answer does not last forever (1:20–28).

Key Words & Concepts

Wisdom (1:2, 20). Skill for living life God’s way in every area. In 1:20–33 Wisdom is pictured as a woman calling in public; the New Testament reveals Christ as “the wisdom of God” (1 Corinthians 1:24).

Proverb (1:1, 6). A short, memorable saying that expresses a practical truth, often by comparison. Proverbs state what is generally true in God’s world, not guarantees for every case.

The fear of the LORD (1:7). A right view of God. For those who defy Him it means terror, and for all it means awe at His glory, majesty, and holiness, for He is unlike any other. It is the “beginning” of knowledge, both its starting point and its chief part.

The simple (1:4, 22, 32). People who are uncommitted and easily led. The book is written to give them prudence, but their drift becomes deadly if they never turn.

Scorners and fools (1:22). Scorners delight in mocking correction, and fools hate knowledge. Both reject wisdom by choice rather than by lack of ability.

Sheol and the Pit (1:12). Names for the grave and the realm of the dead. The gang boasts that it will swallow its victims like the grave itself.
