Proverbs 3:1
Proverbs 3:1
NKJV

Proverbs 3:1 My son, do not forget my law, But let your heart keep my commands;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:1 My child, never forget the things I have taught you.Store my commands in your heart. (NLT)

Main Idea

The father asks his son not to forget his law but to let his heart keep his commands. Obedience here is a matter of the heart’s memory, holding on to instruction from within rather than following it only when someone is watching.

Verse Explanation

The father asks his son not to forget his law but to let his heart keep his commands. Obedience here is a matter of the heart’s memory, holding on to instruction from within rather than following it only when someone is watching.

“My son, do not forget my law,”

The father opens a new appeal with the familiar address. His law is his teaching, the instruction he has been giving since 1:8. To forget it would not be a simple lapse of memory. In Scripture, forgetting often means neglecting, letting something drop out of the place it should hold in one’s life. The son is asked to keep his father’s teaching present and active, the opposite of the immoral woman who “forgets the covenant of her God” (2:17).

“But let your heart keep my commands;”

The commands are to be kept by the heart, the inner person of thought, will, and desire. Obedience here is a matter of the heart’s memory and loyalty, holding on to instruction from within rather than following it only when someone is watching. Israel had been told the same about the LORD’s words: “these words which I command you today shall be in your heart” (Deuteronomy 6:6). The father’s aim is a son whose obedience comes from what he has taken in and values, not from outside pressure.

Study Notes

“Law” translates torah, the Hebrew word for teaching or instruction, as in the mother’s “law” of 1:8.

Proverbs 3:2
Proverbs 3:2
NKJV

Proverbs 3:2 For length of days and long lifeAnd peace they will add to you.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:2 If you do this, you will live many years,and your life will be satisfying. (NLT)

Main Idea

The reason given is that keeping these commands adds length of days, long life, and peace. Proverbs states this as the general fruit of the wise way in God’s world, not a guarantee that every wise person will live to old age.

Verse Explanation

The reason given is that keeping these commands adds length of days, long life, and peace. Proverbs states this as the general fruit of the wise way in God’s world, not a guarantee that every wise person will live to old age.

“For length of days and long life”

The word For introduces the reason to keep the father’s commands. They will add length of days and long life, in Hebrew literally years of life. The wise way is generally the way that preserves life. It avoids the violence, recklessness, and excess that cut lives short, as chapters 1 and 2 have already shown. Proverbs states this as the general fruit of the wise way in God’s world. It is not a guarantee that every wise person will live to old age. Jesus had all wisdom and yet did not live a long life, and many faithful people have died young. The saying describes how things generally go, not a promise that can be claimed against every circumstance.

“And peace they will add to you.”

The Hebrew word translated peace means more than the absence of conflict. It describes wholeness and well-being in every part of life, with God, with others, and within oneself. Length of days without peace would be a poor gift; the father promises both. A life lived by God’s instruction is generally a life at peace, because it is not constantly reaping the conflict and fear that folly produces.

Study Notes

The second phrase in Hebrew is literally years of life. ESV and NASB keep that wording, KJV’s margin notes it, and NKJV renders it “long life.”

Proverbs 3:3
Proverbs 3:3
NKJV

Proverbs 3:3 Let not mercy and truth forsake you; Bind them around your neck, Write them on the tablet of your heart,(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:3 Never let loyalty and kindness leave you!Tie them around your neck as a reminder.Write them deep within your heart. (NLT)

Main Idea

Mercy and truth, steadfast love and faithfulness, must never be allowed to leave the son. He is to bind them around his neck where they are always with him and write them on the tablet of his heart, making them part of who he is.

Verse Explanation

Mercy and truth, steadfast love and faithfulness, must never be allowed to leave the son. He is to bind them around his neck where they are always with him and write them on the tablet of his heart, making them part of who he is.

“Let not mercy and truth forsake you;”

Mercy and truth are a familiar pair in the Old Testament, describing the LORD Himself, who is “abounding in goodness and truth” (Exodus 34:6). Mercy is steadfast love, loyal kindness that keeps faith. Truth is faithfulness, reliability, being true to one’s word. The son is to keep these qualities so close that they never leave him. They describe how he treats God and how he treats people: with loyal love and dependable faithfulness.

“Bind them around your neck,”

To bind them around the neck is to wear them like a necklace, always visible and always present. The picture recalls 1:9, where parental instruction is worn as chains about the neck. Mercy and truth should be the ornaments others see when they look at the son.

“Write them on the tablet of your heart,”

To write them on the tablet of the heart is to make them part of who he is. The tablet recalls the stone tablets on which the commandments were written, and the LORD later promises, “I will put My law in their minds, and write it on their hearts” (Jeremiah 31:33). The two images together, outward and inward, describe a life where faithful love is both seen and real. Binding and writing also echo Israel’s command to bind God’s words on hand and forehead (Deuteronomy 6:8).

Study Notes

The Hebrew words translated “mercy and truth” are the words for steadfast, loyal love and for faithfulness, the same pair used of the LORD in Exodus 34:6. ESV renders the pair with “steadfast love” and “faithfulness,” and the 1984 NIV with “love” and “faithfulness.”

Proverbs 16:6 uses the same pair: “In mercy and truth Atonement is provided for iniquity.”

Proverbs 3:4 And so find favor and high esteem In the sight of God and man.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:4 Then you will find favor with both God and people,and you will earn a good reputation. (NLT)

Main Idea

The person who holds on to mercy and truth will find favor and high esteem in the sight of God and man. A life marked by faithful love wins the approval of the LORD and the respect of people at the same time.

Verse Explanation

The person who holds on to mercy and truth will find favor and high esteem in the sight of God and man. A life marked by faithful love wins the approval of the LORD and the respect of people at the same time.

“And so find favor and high esteem”

The person who holds on to mercy and truth will find favor, the goodwill and approval of others, and high esteem, a good reputation for sound judgment. Faithful love produces trust, and trust produces respect. People are drawn to someone whose kindness is real and whose word can be relied on.

“In the sight of God and man.”

The favor reaches both directions, toward God and toward people. A life marked by mercy and truth wins the approval of the LORD and the respect of others at the same time. Scripture uses similar words of Samuel, who grew “in favor both with the LORD and men” (1 Samuel 2:26), and of Jesus, who “increased in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men” (Luke 2:52). Proverbs is not promising universal popularity; elsewhere it knows that the righteous can be hated. It describes the general esteem that faithful love earns over time.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase rendered “high esteem” combines the word for good with a word for insight or good sense, a word whose related verb can also mean to succeed. KJV has “good understanding,” with “good success” in its margin; ESV reads “good success,” and NASB “good repute.”

Proverbs 3:5–8 — Trust in the LORD with All Your Heart

Proverbs 3:5
Proverbs 3:5
NKJV

Proverbs 3:5 Trust in the LORD with all your heart, And lean not on your own understanding;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:5 Trust in the LORD with all your heart;do not depend on your own understanding. (NLT)

Main Idea

The heart of the chapter’s call is whole-hearted trust: rely on the LORD completely and do not lean on your own understanding for support. The warning is against self-reliance, the habit of putting one’s full weight on personal judgment instead of on God.

Verse Explanation

The heart of the chapter’s call is whole-hearted trust: rely on the LORD completely and do not lean on your own understanding for support. The warning is against self-reliance, the habit of putting one’s full weight on personal judgment instead of on God.

“Trust in the LORD with all your heart,”

Here is the center of the chapter. To trust is to rely on someone, to rest one’s confidence and security on him. The object of trust is the LORD, the God who made Himself known to Israel. The measure of trust is all your heart, the whole inner person without reserve. Trust that holds back part of the heart for self-reliance is not the trust the father means. Jeremiah describes the contrast: “Blessed is the man who trusts in the LORD” and “Cursed is the man who trusts in man” (Jeremiah 17:5–7).

“And lean not on your own understanding;”

To lean is to put one’s weight on something for support. The son is not to put his full weight on his own understanding. This does not set trust against thinking. The whole book commends understanding and urges the son to get it. The warning is against self-reliance, the habit of trusting one’s own judgment as the final word and leaving God out. Human understanding is a good gift, but it cannot bear the weight of a whole life. It must rest on the LORD, who sees what the son cannot see.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb translated “lean” is used of physically resting one’s weight on something, as Saul leaned on his spear (2 Samuel 1:6) and a king leaned on an officer’s hand (2 Kings 5:18; 7:2).

Proverbs 3:6
NKJV

Proverbs 3:6 In all your ways acknowledge Him, And He shall direct your paths.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:6 Seek his will in all you do,and he will show you which path to take. (NLT)

Main Idea

Trust reaches into every area of life, for the son is to acknowledge the LORD in all his ways, and the LORD will direct his paths. A life that recognizes God in every decision is a life He leads along a straight road.

Verse Explanation

Trust reaches into every area of life, for the son is to acknowledge the LORD in all his ways, and the LORD will direct his paths. A life that recognizes God in every decision is a life He leads along a straight road.

“In all your ways acknowledge Him,”

Trust reaches into every area of life. All your ways means every path and decision: work, money, family, friendships, plans. To acknowledge Him is to know Him and recognize Him there, to take the LORD into account in each decision instead of keeping Him confined to religious moments. The Hebrew verb behind acknowledge is the ordinary word for knowing, so the command calls for living with a conscious awareness of God in everything.

“And He shall direct your paths.”

The promise is that He will direct the paths, or make them straight. The picture is of a road cleared and leveled so that the traveler can move forward without being turned aside or tripped. A life that recognizes God in every decision is a life He leads. This does not mean the road will be free of hardship; it means it will lead where it should. The book has already contrasted straight paths with crooked ones (2:13–15), and here the LORD Himself takes responsibility for straightening the way of those who trust Him.

Study Notes

ESV, NASB, and the 1984 NIV render the second line as a promise to make the paths straight. The Hebrew verb means to make straight or level, and KJV and NKJV render it “direct.”

Proverbs 3:7
Proverbs 3:7
NKJV

Proverbs 3:7 Do not be wise in your own eyes; Fear the LORD and depart from evil.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:7 Don’t be impressed with your own wisdom.Instead, fear the LORD and turn away from evil. (NLT)

Main Idea

Trusting God requires humility, so the son must not be wise in his own eyes. Instead he is to fear the LORD and turn away from evil, for a right view of God exposes self-confidence and leads to a changed life.

Verse Explanation

Trusting God requires humility, so the son must not be wise in his own eyes. Instead he is to fear the LORD and turn away from evil, for a right view of God exposes self-confidence and leads to a changed life.

“Do not be wise in your own eyes;”

Trusting God requires humility, and its opposite is being wise in one’s own eyes. Such a person trusts his own judgment so fully that he no longer listens to God or to others. Proverbs treats this as one of the most dangerous conditions a person can be in: “Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him” (26:12). Isaiah pronounces a woe on those who are “wise in their own eyes” (Isaiah 5:21). Self-made wisdom closes the door to the wisdom God gives.

“Fear the LORD and depart from evil.”

The cure for self-confidence is the fear of the LORD, a right view of God. When God is seen as He is, a person’s estimate of his own wisdom shrinks to its proper size. The fear of the LORD then shows itself in a changed life: depart from evil, turn away from it. Job is described the same way, as one who “feared God and shunned evil” (Job 1:1). Humility before God is not only an attitude; it leads to moral action.

Study Notes

Paul echoes this verse in Romans 12:16: “Do not be wise in your own opinion.”

Proverbs 16:6 joins the same two ideas: “by the fear of the LORD one departs from evil.”

Main Idea

Such humble fear of the LORD brings health to the body and strength to the bones. The whole person benefits when pride and evil are put away, though Proverbs speaks here of a general blessing rather than a promise of healing in every case.

Verse Explanation

Such humble fear of the LORD brings health to the body and strength to the bones. The whole person benefits when pride and evil are put away, though Proverbs speaks here of a general blessing rather than a promise of healing in every case.

“It will be health to your flesh,”

The word It refers to the humble fear of the LORD and turning from evil just described. Such a life brings health to the body. Pride, anxiety, and sin take a toll on the whole person, and putting them away brings a measure of healing. Proverbs elsewhere notes the link between the inner life and the body: “A sound heart is life to the body, But envy is rottenness to the bones” (14:30).

“And strength to your bones.”

Bones in Hebrew poetry stand for the deep frame of a person, the core of physical strength. Strength here translates a Hebrew word for drink or refreshment, which suggests moisture, the opposite of dried-up bones. The whole person benefits when pride and evil are put away. Proverbs speaks here of a general blessing that follows the wise way, not a promise of healing in every case. Faithful people still get sick, and their illness is not proof that they lacked trust or humility.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word rendered “flesh” normally means the navel, which KJV keeps (“navel”). ESV and NASB, like NKJV, take it as standing for the whole body.

KJV’s margin explains the Hebrew word behind “health” as medicine and the word behind “strength” as a watering or moistening; ESV and NASB use “refreshment” in the second line.

Proverbs 3:9–10 — Honor the LORD with Your Possessions

Proverbs 3:9
Proverbs 3:9
NKJV

Proverbs 3:9 Honor the LORD with your possessions, And with the firstfruits of all your increase;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:9 Honor the LORD with your wealthand with the best part of everything you produce. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wisdom also governs money, so the son is to honor the LORD with his possessions and with the firstfruits, the first and best, of all his increase. Giving God the first portion declares that everything comes from Him and belongs to Him.

Verse Explanation

Wisdom also governs money, so the son is to honor the LORD with his possessions and with the firstfruits, the first and best, of all his increase. Giving God the first portion declares that everything comes from Him and belongs to Him.

“Honor the LORD with your possessions,”

Wisdom also governs money. To honor the LORD is to give Him weight, to treat Him as important. Possessions are a person’s wealth and goods. How someone uses what he owns shows what he actually values. A person may say he trusts God with all his heart, but his use of money will reveal whether that trust is real.

“And with the firstfruits of all your increase;”

Firstfruits were the first and best portion of the harvest, brought to God before the rest was used. Israel was commanded, “The first of the firstfruits of your land you shall bring into the house of the LORD your God” (Exodus 23:19). Giving God the first portion declares that everything comes from Him and belongs to Him. It is an act of trust as well as worship, because the giver offers the first part before he knows how large the rest will be. This verse speaks of firstfruits, not of the tithe. Giving a tenth may be connected to it as an application, but Proverbs does not command the tithe here.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “firstfruits” is the same word translated “beginning” in 1:7. The fear of the LORD is the beginning and chief part of knowledge, and the firstfruits are the beginning and best part of the harvest.

Main Idea

The result of honoring God this way is full barns and vats overflowing with new wine. In an agricultural setting this pictures God’s generous provision for those who honor Him, a general truth about His blessing and not a formula for wealth.

Verse Explanation

The result of honoring God this way is full barns and vats overflowing with new wine. In an agricultural setting this pictures God’s generous provision for those who honor Him, a general truth about His blessing and not a formula for wealth.

“So your barns will be filled with plenty,”

The result of honoring God this way is full barns. In an agricultural setting, barns were storehouses for grain, and full barns meant security for the year ahead. The picture is of God’s generous provision for those who honor Him with the first of what they have. The one who gives God the first is not left empty.

“And your vats will overflow with new wine.”

Vats were the troughs where juice ran from the winepress. New wine is freshly pressed juice. Overflowing vats picture abundance beyond what is needed. Like the other blessings in this chapter, this states a general truth about God’s blessing and is not a formula for wealth. God promises to meet the needs of His people, and He often blesses generosity with provision, but the verse is not a guarantee that giving will make anyone rich. The book everywhere warns against greed and trust in riches (11:28; 23:4–5). The point is that God is no one’s debtor, and honoring Him with the first is never a loss.

Proverbs 3:11
Proverbs 3:11
NKJV

Proverbs 3:11 My son, do not despise the chastening of the LORD, Nor detest His correction;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:11 My child, don’t reject the LORD’s discipline,and don’t be upset when he corrects you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Tenderly addressing him again as “My son,” the father warns him not to despise the LORD’s chastening or resent His correction. Discipline from God can feel unwelcome, but the wise son refuses to treat it with contempt or bitterness.

Verse Explanation

Tenderly addressing him again as “My son,” the father warns him not to despise the LORD’s chastening or resent His correction. Discipline from God can feel unwelcome, but the wise son refuses to treat it with contempt or bitterness.

“My son, do not despise the chastening of the LORD,”

The father turns from blessing to hardship. Chastening is discipline, the training and correction that may include pain. It is the same Hebrew word used for the father’s own instruction (1:8). Here the discipline comes from the LORD Himself. To despise it is to treat it as worthless or reject it with contempt, as fools despise instruction (1:7). The son is warned not to respond that way when God corrects him.

“Nor detest His correction;”

To detest is to feel loathing or weariness, to resent and recoil from something. Correction can feel unwelcome, and a person may be tempted to grow bitter under it or to give up. The wise son refuses both contempt and bitterness. He receives God’s correction as something good, even when it hurts. This verse does not say that every hardship is a punishment for sin; it addresses how to respond when God does correct.

Study Notes

Hebrews 12:5 quotes this verse in the wording of the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament: “My son, do not despise the chastening of the LORD, Nor be discouraged when you are rebuked by Him.” Its second line differs from the Hebrew, which NKJV follows.

Job 5:17 contains the same command: “Therefore do not despise the chastening of the Almighty.”

Proverbs 3:12
Proverbs 3:12
NKJV

Proverbs 3:12 For whom the LORD loves He corrects, Just as a father the son in whom he delights.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:12 For the LORD corrects those he loves,just as a father corrects a child in whom he delights. (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD corrects the one He loves, just as a father corrects the son in whom he delights. God’s correction is proof of His love and of belonging to Him, never a sign of rejection, as Hebrews 12:5–6 later explains to believers.

Verse Explanation

The LORD corrects the one He loves, just as a father corrects the son in whom he delights. God’s correction is proof of His love and of belonging to Him, never a sign of rejection, as Hebrews 12:5–6 later explains to believers.

“For whom the LORD loves He corrects,”

The word For gives the reason not to despise God’s correction: it comes from love. The LORD corrects the one He loves. Discipline is evidence of His care, not of His rejection. A God who did not correct would be a God who did not care what became of His children. Hebrews applies this verse directly to believers, explaining that discipline proves sonship and that it “yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it” (Hebrews 12:11).

“Just as a father the son in whom he delights.”

The comparison is to a father who corrects the son he delights in. A good father does not correct out of anger or dislike but because he wants his son to grow. God’s delight in His child is the reason for His correction, not a contradiction of it. The risen Christ speaks the same way to His church: “As many as I love, I rebuke and chasten” (Revelation 3:19). Divine correction is never rejection; it is the affection of the Bridegroom who wants His people whole. This verse must never be used to justify harshness or cruelty in human discipline, which contradicts the very love it describes.

Study Notes

Hebrews 12:6 also follows the Septuagint, whose second line reads “And scourges every son whom He receives.” The Greek translators understood the Hebrew words for “as a father” as a verb meaning that the LORD makes a son suffer; the two readings differ only slightly in the Hebrew letters. NKJV follows the standard Hebrew text.

Proverbs 3:13–18 — Wisdom Is a Tree of Life

Proverbs 3:13
Proverbs 3:13
NKJV

Proverbs 3:13 Happy is the man who finds wisdom, And the man who gains understanding;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:13 Joyful is the person who finds wisdom,the one who gains understanding. (NLT)

Main Idea

A new note of praise begins: “Happy is the man who finds wisdom,” and the man who gains understanding. The happiness described here belongs to the one who has discovered and taken hold of something better than anything else he could gain.

Verse Explanation

A new note of praise begins: “Happy is the man who finds wisdom,” and the man who gains understanding. The happiness described here belongs to the one who has discovered and taken hold of something better than anything else he could gain.

“Happy is the man who finds wisdom,”

A new section begins with a burst of praise. Happy describes the deep well-being of a life that is right with God. It translates the same Hebrew word that opens the Psalms: “Blessed is the man” (Psalm 1:1). The happy man is the one who finds wisdom. Chapter 2 described the search, seeking wisdom like silver and hidden treasure; now the father celebrates the finding. Wisdom is pictured again as something to be discovered and possessed, a treasure worth every effort spent on the search.

“And the man who gains understanding;”

The parallel line speaks of gaining understanding. The Hebrew verb behind gains means to bring out or obtain, as a person draws something out for himself. The two lines describe the same person from two sides: he has found wisdom and he has gained understanding. The happiness described here is not a passing mood. It belongs to the one who has taken hold of something better than anything else he could gain, as the next verses go on to explain.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “Happy” here and the word for “happy” in 3:18 come from the same root, so the praise of wisdom in 3:13–18 begins and ends on the same note.

Proverbs 3:14
Proverbs 3:14
NKJV

Proverbs 3:14 For her proceeds are betterthan the profits of silver, And her gain than fine gold.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 3:14 For wisdom is more profitable than silver,and her wages are better than gold. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wisdom’s returns are better than the profits of silver, and her gain is better than fine gold. Measured as an investment, wisdom outperforms the most valued treasures people work for.

Verse Explanation

Wisdom’s returns are better than the profits of silver, and her gain is better than fine gold. Measured as an investment, wisdom outperforms the most valued treasures people work for.

“For her proceeds are better than the profits of silver,”

The word For explains why the finder of wisdom is happy. The language is borrowed from the marketplace. Proceeds and profits describe the return a merchant gets from trade. Silver was the common currency of the ancient world, and trading in it could bring great gain. Wisdom’s return is better. Measured as an investment, wisdom outperforms the very thing people most often work for.

“And her gain than fine gold.”

The comparison rises from silver to fine gold, the most precious metal. Her gain, what wisdom yields to the one who has her, surpasses even that. The father is not dismissing money; the book will say much about earning and using it well. He is ranking values. A person who gains wealth without wisdom has gained less than someone who has wisdom without wealth. Job makes the same comparison: wisdom “cannot be purchased for gold, Nor can silver be weighed for its price” (Job 28:15).

Study Notes

KJV renders the first line with the word “merchandise,” reflecting the trading sense of the Hebrew noun, which means profit gained from trade.

NLT

Main Idea

She is more precious than rubies, and nothing a person could desire compares with her. The praise climbs past every precious thing to say that no wish or ambition is worth more than wisdom itself.

Verse Explanation

She is more precious than rubies, and nothing a person could desire compares with her. The praise climbs past every precious thing to say that no wish or ambition is worth more than wisdom itself.

“She is more precious than rubies,”

The praise climbs higher. Rubies here stand for the costliest gems a person could own. Wisdom is more precious, more valuable and more rare, than any jewel. The same comparison returns later in the book, both for Wisdom herself (8:11) and for the virtuous wife, whose “worth is far above rubies” (31:10). The woman of valor at the end of the book is valued in the same terms as Wisdom at its beginning.

“And all the things you may desire cannot compare with her.”

The second line reaches past every precious stone to everything a person could want. All the things you may desire cannot compare with her. No ambition, possession, achievement, or pleasure is worth more than wisdom itself. The verse asks the reader to look honestly at his own desires and ask whether anything in them is valued above the skill of living God’s way. Wisdom is the one possession that makes every other one safe to have.

Study Notes

The precise stone named by the Hebrew word behind “rubies” is uncertain; red coral is one suggestion. ESV and NASB render it “jewels.” Proverbs 8:11 repeats this verse almost word for word.

Main Idea

Wisdom is pictured holding gifts in both hands: length of days in her right hand, riches and honor in her left. These are the general fruits of the wise life, offered by wisdom herself rather than pursued for their own sake.

Verse Explanation

Wisdom is pictured holding gifts in both hands: length of days in her right hand, riches and honor in her left. These are the general fruits of the wise life, offered by wisdom herself rather than pursued for their own sake.

“Length of days is in her right hand,”

Wisdom is pictured as a woman holding gifts in both hands. In her right hand, the hand of strength and honor, is length of days. The picture recalls 3:2, where keeping the father’s commands adds length of days. Wisdom generally preserves life, because it avoids the paths that lead to early death.

“In her left hand riches and honor.”

In her left hand are riches and honor. Wisdom does not ignore material well-being or reputation, and the wise life often brings both. Yet the gifts are in her hands; they come with her and from her. The order matters. A person who chases riches and honor for their own sake may lose them along with wisdom. The person who seeks wisdom finds that she brings her gifts with her. These are the general fruits of the wise life, not a promise of wealth, and they are always held under the warnings the book gives against greed and trusting in riches. Wisdom says later, “Riches and honor are with me, Enduring riches and righteousness” (8:18).

Proverbs 3:17 Her ways are ways of pleasantness, And all her paths are peace.(NKJV)

Main Idea

Her ways are ways of pleasantness, and all her paths are peace. The road of wisdom is not a joyless burden but a pleasant and peaceful way to live.

Verse Explanation

Her ways are ways of pleasantness, and all her paths are peace. The road of wisdom is not a joyless burden but a pleasant and peaceful way to live.

“Her ways are ways of pleasantness,”

Wisdom’s ways are ways of pleasantness. Many people imagine that a wise, godly life must be dull, joyless, or burdensome, a long list of things to avoid. The father insists on the reverse. The road of wisdom is pleasant to walk. It is folly that promises pleasure and then delivers bitterness, as the immoral woman will show (5:3–4).

“And all her paths are peace.”

All her paths are peace. Every road wisdom leads down ends in peace, the wholeness and well-being described in 3:2. This does not mean that the wise never face conflict or hardship. It means that the way itself is good, and the person walking it is not constantly at war with God, with others, and with his own conscience. The verse balances the strong demands of the earlier part of the chapter. Trust, humility, generosity, and accepting correction are not burdens imposed by a harsh master; they are the pathways of a pleasant and peaceful life.

Verse Explanation

She is a tree of life to those who take hold of her, and those who keep holding her are happy. The image recalls the tree in Eden; wisdom gives life to those who cling to her, and in Christ the way to the tree of life is opened again (Revelation 2:7).

“She is a tree of life to those who take hold of her,”

The praise reaches its climax. The tree of life stood in the garden of Eden (Genesis 2:9), and after the fall the way to it was guarded by cherubim and a flaming sword (Genesis 3:24). The image speaks of life in its fullness, life as God intended. Wisdom is a tree of life to those who take hold of her. She gives life to those who grasp her firmly and do not let go. The book will use the same image three more times (11:30; 13:12; 15:4). In Christ the way to the tree of life is opened again: “To him who overcomes I will give to eat from the tree of life” (Revelation 2:7). The wisdom that gives life points to the One who is the life, and the tree that was lost in Eden is restored in Him (Revelation 22:2).

“And happy are all who retain her.”

To retain her is to keep holding on. Finding wisdom (3:13) is the beginning; continuing to hold her is the life of it. The section that began with “Happy is the man who finds wisdom” ends with happy are all who retain her. The repetition frames the poem and shows that the happiness belongs to those who keep wisdom, not only to those who once encountered it.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb in the first line, “take hold of,” means to seize or grasp firmly. ESV and NASB render the second line as those who hold her fast, stressing a continuing grip.

Proverbs 3:19–20 — By Wisdom the LORD Founded the Earth

Proverbs 3:19
Proverbs 3:19
NKJV

Proverbs 3:19 The LORD by wisdom founded the earth; By understanding He established the heavens;(NKJV)

Main Idea

The LORD used wisdom to found the earth and understanding to establish the heavens. The same wisdom offered to the son is the wisdom by which God made the world, a truth chapter 8 develops and the New Testament fulfills in the Son through whom all things were made (John 1:3).

Verse Explanation

The LORD used wisdom to found the earth and understanding to establish the heavens. The same wisdom offered to the son is the wisdom by which God made the world, a truth chapter 8 develops and the New Testament fulfills in the Son through whom all things were made (John 1:3).

“The LORD by wisdom founded the earth;”

The father now shows how great this wisdom is. The LORD used wisdom to found the earth, laying its foundations like a builder setting the base of a house. The wisdom offered to the son is the same wisdom by which God made the world. That is why it works: it fits the way the world was built. Living by God’s wisdom means living with the grain of creation rather than against it. The psalmist praises the same truth: “In wisdom You have made them all” (Psalm 104:24).

“By understanding He established the heavens;”

By understanding He established the heavens, setting them firmly in place. Earth below and heavens above were both made by God’s wisdom and understanding. Here wisdom is the means by which God created, not yet a person speaking. Chapter 8 develops the same truth with Wisdom speaking as one present at creation, “beside Him as a master craftsman” (8:30). The New Testament fulfills it in the eternal Son: “All things were made through Him” (John 1:3), and “by Him all things were created that are in heaven and that are on earth” (Colossians 1:16). This connection is a canonical link that chapter 8 develops more fully, not a direct statement of this verse.

Study Notes

Jeremiah 10:12 uses similar language of the LORD: “He has established the world by His wisdom.”

Verse Explanation

By His knowledge the depths were broken open and the clouds drop dew. God’s wisdom not only made the world but still waters it, from the deep springs below to the dew that falls each morning.

“By His knowledge the depths were broken up,”

The depths are the deep waters beneath the earth. By God’s knowledge they were broken up, split open so that their waters flowed. The line may look back to God’s ordering of the waters at creation, when He gathered the seas and let springs and rivers break out to water the land. The same Hebrew verb is used in Genesis 7:11 of the flood, when “all the fountains of the great deep were broken up,” but in this verse the setting is God’s wise provision, not judgment.

“And clouds drop down the dew.”

From above, the clouds drop dew. In the dry summer months of Israel, dew was a vital source of moisture for crops, and Scripture often treats it as a sign of God’s blessing (Genesis 27:28). God’s wisdom did not only make the world; it still waters it, from the springs below to the dew that falls each morning. The God who sustains creation so carefully is the God who offers His wisdom to the son. The point of 3:19–20 is to show the reader the size and reliability of the wisdom he is being urged to take hold of.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb translated “broken up” is the same verb used in Genesis 7:11. Interpreters differ on whether 3:20 refers to creation, to the flood, or to God’s ongoing provision of springs and rain; the context of creation and provision favors the first or third.

The father turns from praising wisdom back to direct appeal, with the familiar “My son.” The word them points ahead to what the second line names, as the NKJV dash shows. Study Notes

The first line does not say what must not depart from the son’s sight. The second line names it: sound wisdom and discretion. They are the things the son must not let slip away. The Hebrew verb behind “depart” means to turn aside. Another form of the same verb describes the “devious” in 2:15 and the “perverse person” in 3:32.

Verse Explanation

Wisdom now turns to the neighbor: do not withhold good from those to whom it is due when it is in your power to do it. Whether a debt, a wage, or help that is owed, the ability to do good creates a duty to do it.

“Do not withhold good from those to whom it is due,”

Wisdom now turns to the neighbor. After all the praise of wisdom’s worth, the father shows what it looks like on an ordinary day. The son must not withhold good from those to whom it is due. This may be a debt he owes, wages he has promised, or help that someone has a rightful claim to. Holding it back is a quiet form of injustice, and it often passes unnoticed by everyone except the person who is waiting.

“When it is in the power of your hand to do so.”

The condition is ability. When it is in the power of your hand to do so, the duty becomes clear. Wisdom does not demand what a person cannot give, but it does not let him excuse himself when he can. The ability to do good creates the obligation to do it. Israel’s law was specific about this in the case of hired workers: “The wages of him who is hired shall not remain with you all night until morning” (Leviticus 19:13). The fear of the LORD shows itself in paying what is owed, promptly and fully.

Study Notes

The phrase “those to whom it is due” is literally its owners in Hebrew, as KJV’s margin notes. It is the same Hebrew word rendered “owners” in 1:19. The good being withheld already belongs, in a sense, to the person who is owed it.

“Do not say to your neighbor, ‘Go, and come back,’”

The second command addresses delay. The son must not send a neighbor away with the words, “Go, and come back.” The neighbor here is anyone nearby, someone who lives and works close at hand. Telling him to return later may sound reasonable and even kind, but it can be a polite way of refusing.

“And tomorrow I will give it,”

The promise of tomorrow puts off what could be done today. For a poor laborer waiting on his wages, one more day without them could mean a day without food. Israel’s law required that a worker be paid the same day, “for he is poor and has set his heart on it” (Deuteronomy 24:15). Wisdom does not make others wait for what can be given now.

“When you have it with you.”

The verse closes with the decisive point: when you have it with you. The problem is not inability but reluctance. The son has what is needed in his hand. James later describes the same failure of love, saying to the needy “Depart in peace, be warmed and filled” while giving them nothing (James 2:16). The command presses wisdom down into the practical details of everyday honesty and kindness.

“For the perverse person is an abomination to the LORD,”

The word For introduces the reason behind the commands of 3:27–31, drawn from how the LORD Himself deals with people. The perverse person is the devious one, whose ways bend and twist. “But His secret counsel is with the upright.”

The contrast is striking. With the upright, those who walk straight, the LORD shares His secret counsel. The Hebrew word describes a private circle of trusted friends or the intimate confidence shared among them. God takes the upright into His confidence, sharing His heart with them. The psalmist says, “The secret of the LORD is with those who fear Him” (Psalm 25:14). Jesus spoke the same way to His disciples: “I have called you friends, for all things that I heard from My Father I have made known to you” (John 15:15). The crooked may gain worldly advantage, but the upright gain friendship with God.

The Hebrew word for “perverse” is a form of the same verb rendered “devious” in 2:15 and “depart” in 3:21, a verb meaning to turn aside.

The blessing does not depend on the size of the dwelling but on the character of those who live there and the God who watches over it.

The Hebrew word rendered “home” can mean the abode of shepherds and their flocks, but in poetry it often means simply a dwelling or habitation, and that is its sense here.

Main Idea

He scorns the scornful but gives grace to the humble. God meets the proud on their own terms and pours out favor on the lowly, a truth the New Testament repeats (James 4:6; 1 Peter 5:5).

Verse Explanation

He scorns the scornful but gives grace to the humble. God meets the proud on their own terms and pours out favor on the lowly, a truth the New Testament repeats (James 4:6; 1 Peter 5:5).

“Surely He scorns the scornful,”

The scornful are the scorners of 1:22, mockers who delight in ridiculing correction. The LORD scorns them; He meets them on their own terms. Those who mock His wisdom will find that He treats their pride with the contempt it deserves. This is not petty retaliation but justice. God resists those who set themselves against Him.

“But gives grace to the humble.”

To the humble He gives grace, favor that is undeserved and freely given. The humble are those who know their need and do not exalt themselves. This verse is quoted twice in the New Testament in the form “God resists the proud, But gives grace to the humble” (James 4:6; 1 Peter 5:5). The New Testament wording follows the Greek Septuagint, which renders the first line as God resisting the proud; the Hebrew says He scorns the scornful. Both versions agree on the main truth: God opposes those who exalt themselves and favors those who humble themselves. Christ is the supreme example, for “He humbled Himself and became obedient to the point of death” (Philippians 2:8), and God exalted Him.

Study Notes

The Hebrew text has two forms of the last word. The letters written in the text spell the word for “afflicted,” which NASB follows, while the traditional reading marked by the scribes gives the word for “humble,” which NKJV, ESV, and the 1984 NIV follow. The two words are closely related in form and meaning.

ESV renders “grace” as “favor.”

Proverbs 3:35 The wise inherit honor,but fools are put to shame! (NLT)

Main Idea

The chapter ends with two legacies: the wise will inherit glory, but fools will be left with shame. Each way of life leaves its own inheritance, honor for those who walk with wisdom and disgrace for those who refuse it.

Verse Explanation

The chapter ends with two legacies: the wise will inherit glory, but fools will be left with shame. Each way of life leaves its own inheritance, honor for those who walk with wisdom and disgrace for those who refuse it.

“The wise shall inherit glory,”

The chapter ends with two legacies. The wise shall inherit glory. An inheritance is what a person receives and keeps, what belongs to him permanently. The glory the wise inherit is honor and weight, a lasting good name before God and people. It is the end of the road that began with keeping the father’s commands (3:1).

“But shame shall be the legacy of fools.”

Shame shall be the legacy of fools. Their legacy is disgrace, the opposite of glory. Each way of life leaves its own inheritance, honor for those who walk with wisdom and dishonor for those who refuse it. The two lines sum up the chapter’s contrast. The person who trusts the LORD, honors Him, receives His correction, holds on to wisdom, and treats his neighbor rightly will be remembered with honor. The one who despises all this will leave nothing behind but shame.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of the second line is difficult. Its verb can mean to lift up or to carry off, so the line may mean that fools carry off shame or that shame lifts fools up for all to see. KJV’s margin gives the literal sense as shame exalting the fools, and ESV renders it with the word “disgrace.”
