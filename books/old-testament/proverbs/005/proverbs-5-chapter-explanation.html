Proverbs 5

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 5:1
Proverbs 5:1
NKJV

Proverbs 5:1 My son, pay attention to my wisdom; Lend your ear to my understanding,(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:1 My son, pay attention to my wisdom;listen carefully to my wise counsel. (NLT)

Main Idea

The father opens another appeal, asking his son to pay attention to his wisdom and lend his ear to his understanding. What follows is frank teaching on sexual temptation, and the son must be listening before the danger arrives.

Verse Explanation

The father opens another appeal, asking his son to pay attention to his wisdom and lend his ear to his understanding. What follows is frank teaching on sexual temptation, and the son must be listening before the danger arrives.

“My son, pay attention to my wisdom;”

The father speaks again in the voice of chapters 1–4, addressing “my son” as he did at 4:20, and the call to attention opens a new unit of teaching. What follows is his most direct talk about sexual temptation. He calls it “my wisdom,” wisdom he has learned and tested and now hands on: skill for living God’s way, applied here to desire, marriage and the body. A father who speaks this openly is doing what wise parents do. He does not leave his son to learn about sex from silence, from rumor, or from the woman waiting at the corner.

“Lend your ear to my understanding,”

The second line repeats the request with a physical picture. To lend the ear is to lean toward the speaker so as to catch every word. “Understanding” is the ability to see into a situation and discern what is really happening, and the son will need exactly that, because the danger described in 5:3 looks sweet on the surface. The father claims his son’s ear before temptation ever speaks. Whoever has the son’s attention first will help him weigh every voice that comes after. The ear returns at the close of the section, where the ruined man admits in 5:13 that he never inclined his ear to those who taught him.

Study Notes

“Lend your ear” in 5:1 and “inclined my ear” in 5:13 translate the same Hebrew verb (natah), so the warning section opens with an ear requested and closes, in the ruined man’s lament, with an ear withheld.

Proverbs 5:2
Proverbs 5:2
NKJV

Proverbs 5:2 That you may preserve discretion, And your lips may keep knowledge.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:2 Then you will show discernment,and your lips will express what you’ve learned. (NLT)

Main Idea

The purpose of listening is that the son may hold on to discretion and that his own lips may keep knowledge. Good judgment and truthful speech will be his defense against the smooth words described in the next verse.

Verse Explanation

The purpose of listening is that the son may hold on to discretion and that his own lips may keep knowledge. Good judgment and truthful speech will be his defense against the smooth words described in the next verse.

“That you may preserve discretion,”

The father now states the purpose of listening. “Discretion” is careful, forward-looking judgment, the ability to think ahead and choose wisely before a situation turns dangerous. To “preserve” it is to guard it and keep watch over it. Discretion is not gained once and then left on a shelf. A young man can know what is right in a calm hour and lose hold of it when desire grows loud, so his judgment must be kept under guard, and the father’s teaching is the means of keeping it.

“And your lips may keep knowledge.”

The second line moves from inner judgment to speech. The son’s own lips are to hold on to knowledge, the true understanding of God’s ways that the father has been teaching him. The phrase prepares a deliberate contrast with the next verse, where the immoral woman’s lips “drip honey” (5:3). Two sets of lips will compete for the son: his own, stocked with truth, and hers, full of sweet persuasion. Knowledge kept ready on his lips is his answer to her smooth words. The pairing shows that the struggle in this chapter begins with words long before it becomes a matter of actions.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 5:3
Proverbs 5:3
NKJV

Proverbs 5:3 For the lips of an immoral woman drip honey, And her mouth is smoother than oil;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:3 For the lips of an immoral woman are as sweet as honey,and her mouth is smoother than oil. (NLT)

Main Idea

The immoral woman’s danger lies in her speech: her lips drip honey and her mouth is smoother than oil. Her words are sweet and easy to accept, and that is exactly what makes them deadly.

Verse Explanation

The immoral woman’s danger lies in her speech: her lips drip honey and her mouth is smoother than oil. Her words are sweet and easy to accept, and that is exactly what makes them deadly.

“For the lips of an immoral woman drip honey,”

“For” gives the reason the son needs guarded discretion. The NKJV’s “immoral woman” renders a Hebrew word that describes her as a strange or outsider woman: outside the young man’s home and outside the boundaries God has set. Chapter 2 identified her as a wife who “forsakes the companion of her youth” (2:17), so the subject here is literal adultery. The text does not settle her nationality, and that is not its concern. Nor does the father present women in general as the danger. He warns about one woman’s chosen path and about his son’s own steps. Her lips “drip honey”: her words are sweet, pleasant and easy to take in. Honey was the sweetest thing an Israelite knew, and the image says that temptation does not arrive looking bitter.

“And her mouth is smoother than oil;”

Oil was used to soften the skin and soothe wounds, and something smoother than oil meets no resistance at all. Her speech flatters, reassures and removes objections before they can be raised. Chapter 2 had already described her as the seductress “who flatters with her words” (2:16), and in chapter 7 the son will hear her actual speech (7:14–20). In each place her power lies in what she says. That is why the father has asked for his son’s ear and stocked his lips with knowledge. Words that slide down easily are seldom weighed, and the father wants his son to weigh them.

Study Notes

The Hebrew wording behind “drip honey” closely matches the bridegroom’s praise of his bride in the Song of Songs: “Your lips, O my spouse, Drip as the honeycomb” (Song of Songs 4:11). The same sweetness that is celebrated within marriage there is the bait of adultery here.

“Immoral woman” here and in 5:20, and “seductress” in 5:20, translate two Hebrew words (zarah and nokriyah) that describe a woman as a stranger or outsider. In chapters 2, 5 and 7 they mark her as outside the son’s marriage and outside God’s ways; they do not by themselves settle whether she was an Israelite.

Proverbs 5:4
Proverbs 5:4
NKJV

Proverbs 5:4 But in the end she is bitter as wormwood, Sharp as a two-edged sword.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:4 But in the end she is as bitter as poison,as dangerous as a double-edged sword. (NLT)

Main Idea

In the end she is as bitter as wormwood and as sharp as a two-edged sword. The father sets the honey-sweet beginning of sin beside its bitter, wounding end so the son can see the whole road before he takes the first step.

Verse Explanation

In the end she is as bitter as wormwood and as sharp as a two-edged sword. The father sets the honey-sweet beginning of sin beside its bitter, wounding end so the son can see the whole road before he takes the first step.

“But in the end she is bitter as wormwood,”

The word “But” turns from the first taste to the last. “In the end” points to the outcome, what a relationship with her finally produces. Wormwood is a plant known for its intensely bitter taste, and in Deuteronomy 29:18 and Lamentations 3:19 it stands for bitterness and grief. The father sets the honey of 5:3 beside the wormwood of 5:4 so that his son can taste both ends of the road at once. Temptation offers its beginning and keeps its ending out of sight. Wisdom judges a path by where it finishes, even while the path still looks pleasant.

“Sharp as a two-edged sword.”

A two-edged sword cuts on both sides and wounds deeply. The smooth mouth of 5:3 has become a blade, and what felt soothing proves to be the instrument of injury. The father is not exaggerating for effect. The rest of the chapter describes the wounds in plain terms: lost honor and years (5:9), wealth drained away (5:10), a body worn out (5:11) and public disgrace (5:14). Chapter 7 carries the same road to its end, a young man who “did not know it would cost his life” (7:23). The image is meant to let the son feel the cost before he ever pays it.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase translated “two-edged sword” uses the plural of the ordinary Hebrew word for “mouth,” so it reads literally a sword “of mouths.” Hebrew used “mouth” for the edge of a blade, and a sword “of mouths” is a sword with edges. Her “mouth” in 5:3 translates a different Hebrew word, literally her palate, so the link between the two verses lies in the picture rather than in a repeated word: speech that felt smooth turns out to cut.

Proverbs 5:5
Proverbs 5:5
NKJV

Proverbs 5:5 Her feet go down to death, Her steps lay hold of hell.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:5 Her feet go down to death;her steps lead straight to the grave. (NLT)

Main Idea

Her feet go down to death, and her steps take hold of hell, the realm of the dead. Whoever walks with her is walking downhill toward the grave.

Verse Explanation

Her feet go down to death, and her steps take hold of hell, the realm of the dead. Whoever walks with her is walking downhill toward the grave.

“Her feet go down to death,”

The father now follows her feet. Her path does not run level; it slopes downward, and death lies at the bottom. The line restates the warning of chapter 2, “her house leads down to death” (2:18), and stands against the son’s own feet, which he was told to keep on a level, established path (4:26–27). Two walkers are heading in two directions. Death does not ambush her road from somewhere outside it. Death is where the road itself goes, and every step taken with her goes lower.

“Her steps lay hold of hell.”

“Hell” here translates the Hebrew word Sheol, the Old Testament name for the realm of the dead and the grave. In Proverbs it names death’s domain and its grip, the place from which, as 2:19 says, those who go to her do not return. To “lay hold of” something is to grasp it firmly. Her steps take hold of Sheol the way a hand closes on something it means to keep. The man who walks with her is not strolling near the edge of ruin; he keeps company with someone whose footsteps are fastened to it. Read with 5:3–4, the verse traces sin’s course from a sweet start to a bitter end and then to death, a sequence the father sets out step by step in chapter 7, the book’s fullest picture of how a person enters the path of sin.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb rendered “lay hold of” means to grasp and hold firmly, and it is the same verb the father used at 4:4, “Let your heart retain my words.” The son is told to hold fast to his father’s words; her steps hold fast to Sheol, the Hebrew name for the realm of the dead.

Proverbs 5:6
Proverbs 5:6
NKJV

Proverbs 5:6 Lest you ponder her path of life"” Her ways are unstable; You do not know them.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:6 For she cares nothing about the path to life.She staggers down a crooked trail and doesn’t realize it. (NLT)

Main Idea

The father warns that the son might weigh her road as though it were the path of life, yet her ways are unstable and shifting. “You do not know them,” he says; whoever is drawn to her cannot see where her road actually leads.

Verse Explanation

The father warns that the son might weigh her road as though it were the path of life, yet her ways are unstable and shifting. “You do not know them,” he says; whoever is drawn to her cannot see where her road actually leads.

“Lest you ponder her path of life—”

The NKJV reads the first line as a warning addressed to the son: the father describes her ways so that the son will not begin to weigh her road as though it were the path of life. The Hebrew verb behind “ponder” can mean to weigh carefully, as goods are weighed on a scale, or to level a road and prepare it for walking. The danger is that the son starts treating her road as a real option, measuring it, wondering whether it might lead somewhere good. A man who begins to weigh temptation as a possible way of life has already moved toward it. The same Hebrew verb appears at 4:26, “Ponder the path of your feet,” and again at 5:21, where the LORD “ponders all his paths.” The son is to weigh his own path, not hers.

“Her ways are unstable;”

Her paths wander and shift. They have no fixed direction, no firm footing and no settled destination that she will admit. A road like that cannot be weighed honestly because it keeps moving under the traveler’s feet. What looked like a pleasant lane turns and twists until the one following it cannot tell where he is.

“You do not know them.”

In the NKJV the father again speaks to the son: he cannot know her ways or see where they lead. Her road is hidden from the very person being drawn along it, and that blindness is part of its danger. The father, who can see the end of the road, describes it so that his son can see it too before he sets foot on it. The Hebrew of this verse can be read in more than one way, but the NKJV’s reading fits the father’s purpose throughout 5:1–6: to give his son discernment about a road that cannot be read from the inside.

Study Notes

In Hebrew the verb form meaning “you” (spoken to a man) and the form meaning “she” are spelled the same way, so the verbs in the first and last lines of 5:6 can be read either way. The NKJV and KJV take them as addressed to the son. The ESV, NASB, the 1984 NIV and NLT make the woman the subject of both lines, and the AMP makes her the subject of the first line; the NLT reads, “For she cares nothing about the path to life. She staggers down a crooked trail and doesn’t realize it.” On that alternative reading the woman herself never considers the path of life and does not see how unstable her own ways are. Both readings fit the context.

Proverbs 5:7–14 — Stay Far from Her Door

Proverbs 5:7
Proverbs 5:7
NKJV

Proverbs 5:7 Therefore hear me now, my children, And do not depart from the words of my mouth.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:7 So now, my sons, listen to me.Never stray from what I am about to say: (NLT)

Main Idea

Turning to “my children,” the father calls them to hear him now and not to depart from the words of his mouth. The appeal broadens to all who will listen, because the danger he describes is common to every generation.

Verse Explanation

Turning to “my children,” the father calls them to hear him now and not to depart from the words of his mouth. The appeal broadens to all who will listen, because the danger he describes is common to every generation.

“Therefore hear me now, my children,”

“Therefore” draws a conclusion from 5:3–6: since her words are sweet and her road ends in death, listen now. The address widens from “my son” to “my children,” as it did at 4:1. The father speaks to every young person who will hear him, because the danger he describes is not peculiar to one household; it waits for every generation. “Now” adds urgency. The time to listen is before temptation speaks, while the mind is still clear and the feet are still free.

“And do not depart from the words of my mouth.”

To depart from his words is to turn aside from the road they mark out. The father sets the words of his mouth against her mouth, which is “smoother than oil” (5:3). The son will listen to one mouth or the other. The first step toward her house is seldom a loud rejection of the father’s teaching. More often it is a quiet turning aside from it, one small step at a time, and the father asks his children to stay on the line his words have drawn.

None.

Proverbs 5:8
Proverbs 5:8
NKJV

Proverbs 5:8 Remove your way far from her, And do not go near the door of her house,(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:8 Stay away from her!Don’t go near the door of her house! (NLT)

Main Idea

The father’s plainest command is to keep far away from her and not even go near the door of her house. Wisdom does not test its strength against temptation at close range; it keeps its distance.

Verse Explanation

The father’s plainest command is to keep far away from her and not even go near the door of her house. Wisdom does not test its strength against temptation at close range; it keeps its distance.

“Remove your way far from her,”

Here is the father’s plainest command in the chapter. He does not tell his son to approach temptation and resist it bravely at close range. He tells him to put distance between his road and hers. “Your way” is the son’s own route through life, the streets he chooses to walk and the places he chooses to go. The outcome of temptation is usually settled long before the decisive moment, by the road a man has chosen. Distance is not weakness. It is the sensible response to a danger that is stronger up close than it looks from far away. Joseph showed this wisdom when he “fled and ran outside” (Genesis 39:12), and Paul gives the same counsel: “Flee sexual immorality” (1 Corinthians 6:18).

“And do not go near the door of her house,”

The father names the boundary precisely: her door. The son is to stay away from the threshold itself. In chapter 7 the young man’s fall begins at exactly this point, when he “took the path to her house” (7:8) as evening fell. A man who never approaches the door never has to fight the battle inside the house. The line ends with a comma in the NKJV because the sentence runs on; 5:9–14 will set out what lies beyond that door.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 5:9
Proverbs 5:9
NKJV

Proverbs 5:9 Lest you give your honor to others, And your years to the cruel one;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:9 If you do, you will lose your honorand will lose to merciless people all you have achieved. (NLT)

Main Idea

Going near her risks giving away one’s honor to others and one’s years to a cruel one. Adultery robs a man of his dignity and of the best years of his life, handing them to those who will show him no mercy.

Verse Explanation

Going near her risks giving away one’s honor to others and one’s years to a cruel one. Adultery robs a man of his dignity and of the best years of his life, handing them to those who will show him no mercy.

“Lest you give your honor to others,”

“Lest” introduces the consequences of ignoring 5:8, and a chain of them follows through 5:14. “Honor” here means a man’s dignity and vigor, the strength and splendor of his life at its best. The verb is “give.” No one tears his honor from him; he hands it over himself, a choice at a time. The “others” who receive it are people outside his home who have no right to it. The adulterer imagines he is getting something, and the father shows him that he is giving himself away.

“And your years to the cruel one;”

He also gives away his years, the prime of a life that should have been spent building a home and serving God. They go to “the cruel one,” someone who will show him no mercy. The text does not identify this person. It may be the wronged husband, whose fury 6:34–35 describes, or it may be those who take advantage of the adulterer and drain him. Either way, his best years end up in hostile hands. The cost of adultery is counted in time that cannot be given back, as well as in money.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “honor” is used elsewhere for splendor and majesty. Here it refers especially to a man’s vigor, and the NASB renders it “your vigor,” which brings out that sense of personal strength.

Who “the cruel one” is remains uncertain. Suggestions include the injured husband of 6:34–35, the woman herself, and those who exploit the man once he is exposed. The ESV’s “the merciless” keeps the description general, as the Hebrew does.

Proverbs 5:10
Proverbs 5:10
NKJV

Proverbs 5:10 Lest aliens be filled with your wealth, And your labors go to the house of a foreigner;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:10 Strangers will consume your wealth,and someone else will enjoy the fruit of your labor. (NLT)

Main Idea

Strangers could also be filled with his wealth, and the fruit of his labor could end up in a foreigner’s house. What he worked to build for his own family is drained away to people outside it.

Verse Explanation

Strangers could also be filled with his wealth, and the fruit of his labor could end up in a foreigner’s house. What he worked to build for his own family is drained away to people outside it.

“Lest aliens be filled with your wealth,”

“Aliens” are strangers, people outside his family. The irony is sharp. He went after the strange woman of 5:3, and strangers end up feeding on what he has. “Wealth” includes his strength and resources, the productive power of his whole life, and “filled” pictures others eating their fill of what he earned. The text does not stop to explain the means by which it happens. It shows the result: what was meant to sustain his own household flows out to people who have no claim on it.

“And your labors go to the house of a foreigner;”

“Labors” are the fruit of hard and often painful toil. He worked to build a house, and what he built goes to “the house of a foreigner.” The contrast with 5:8 is deliberate. He would not keep away from her house, and now his labor ends up in someone else’s. What God gave him to provide for his wife and children is drained away to those outside the home.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word rendered “wealth” basically means strength or power. The ESV and NASB translate it “strength,” and the KJV margin notes that the Hebrew reads “thy strength.” The word therefore covers more than money: it includes the vigor and working capacity of a man’s life.

Proverbs 5:11
Proverbs 5:11
NKJV

Proverbs 5:11 And you mourn at last, When your flesh and your body are consumed,(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:11 In the end you will groan in anguishwhen disease consumes your body. (NLT)

Main Idea

At the end of that road he will groan when his flesh and body are worn out. The pleasure that seemed so sweet leaves him spent and broken, mourning a loss he cannot undo.

Verse Explanation

At the end of that road he will groan when his flesh and body are worn out. The pleasure that seemed so sweet leaves him spent and broken, mourning a loss he cannot undo.

“And you mourn at last,”

“At last” echoes “in the end” from 5:4. The father moves his son forward in imagination to the end of the road and lets him hear the sound he would make there. “Mourn” is a strong word for groaning, a deep cry of anguish rather than a quiet sigh. The pleasure has gone and only the consequences remain. Regret does come, but it comes late, when it can no longer change what has been lost.

“When your flesh and your body are consumed,”

The loss reaches the body itself. “Flesh” and “body” together describe the whole physical person, worn down and used up. The father does not name a single cause. The wasting could come from disease, from exhaustion, from the strain of a double life, or from the long toll of years spent badly. The text leaves the means open and fixes the son’s attention on the result. Sexual sin does not stay in the realm of feelings and secrets; it spends the body God gave, and the father wants his son to count that cost while he is still young and strong.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “mourn” means to groan, and it is a strong word. A noun from the same Hebrew root describes “the roaring of a lion” at 19:12 and 20:2, which shows how deep a cry the word can describe.

Proverbs 5:12
Proverbs 5:12
NKJV

Proverbs 5:12 And say: "How I have hated instruction, And my heart despised correction!(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:12 You will say, “How I hated discipline!If only I had not ignored all the warnings! (NLT)

Main Idea

In his regret he cries, “How I have hated instruction, And my heart despised correction!” He now sees that his ruin began long before the act, when he refused to be taught and resented being corrected.

Verse Explanation

In his regret he cries, “How I have hated instruction, And my heart despised correction!” He now sees that his ruin began long before the act, when he refused to be taught and resented being corrected.

“How I have hated instruction,”

The ruined man now speaks, and the father quotes his lament in advance. “Instruction” is discipline and training, the teaching that forms a person and sometimes corrects him. The man does not say that no one told him or that he did not know. He says he hated the very teaching that would have saved him. His trouble was never a lack of information; it was a settled resentment toward being taught. The book’s motto had said that “fools despise wisdom and instruction” (1:7), and this man has become what the motto described.

“And my heart despised correction!”

Correction is reproof, the word that tells a person he is going the wrong way. The man’s heart despised it and treated it as worthless. He now sees that his ruin began long before the act of adultery, in a heart that refused to be told it was wrong. The father puts these words in his son’s ears so that the son will never have to say them himself. To hear the end of the wrong road while there is still time to choose another is itself a mercy.

Study Notes

The Hebrew noun translated “instruction,” a word for discipline and training, returns in 5:23, where the man dies “for lack of instruction.” The chapter therefore closes on the very thing he says he hated.

Proverbs 5:13
Proverbs 5:13
NKJV

Proverbs 5:13 I have not obeyed the voice of my teachers, Nor inclined my ear to those who instructed me!(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:13 Oh, why didn’t I listen to my teachers?Why didn’t I pay attention to my instructors? (NLT)

Main Idea

He confesses that he did not obey the voice of his teachers or listen to those who instructed him. The warnings were there all along; he simply would not hear them.

Verse Explanation

He confesses that he did not obey the voice of his teachers or listen to those who instructed him. The warnings were there all along; he simply would not hear them.

“I have not obeyed the voice of my teachers,”

The man confesses that he had teachers. He was not left without guidance; he heard the voice of those who taught him and did not obey it. The Hebrew verb behind “obeyed” is the ordinary word for hearing, and to hear someone’s voice in this sense means to heed it and act on it. He heard the words, but he did not hear in the way that obeys. The plural “teachers” points to more than one voice: parents, elders and others who spoke God’s ways into his life. Their faithfulness makes his refusal plainer.

“Nor inclined my ear to those who instructed me!”

He also did not incline his ear, the very thing the father asked for at the start of the chapter (5:1). The section that began with a father asking his son to lend his ear ends with a man admitting that he never did. Being near good teaching did not protect him; leaning toward it would have. What separates the son of 5:1 from the man of 5:13 is the response each gives to the same wisdom.

Proverbs 5:14
Proverbs 5:14
NKJV

Proverbs 5:14 I was on the verge of total ruin, In the midst of the assembly and congregation."(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:14 I have come to the brink of utter ruin,and now I must face public disgrace.” (NLT)

Main Idea

He was on the verge of total ruin in the midst of the assembly and congregation, his disgrace exposed before the whole community. Secret sin ended in public shame among the very people with whom he worshiped and lived.

Verse Explanation

He was on the verge of total ruin in the midst of the assembly and congregation, his disgrace exposed before the whole community. Secret sin ended in public shame among the very people with whom he worshiped and lived.

“I was on the verge of total ruin,”

“On the verge” means at the very edge, almost all the way there. “Total ruin” is complete disaster, trouble of every kind come upon him at once. The man looks back and sees how close he came to losing everything. The verse describes a man brought to the brink; it does not say whether he went over. Either way, the father’s point is clear: adultery carries a man right up to the edge of complete collapse.

“In the midst of the assembly and congregation.”

His ruin took place in public, in the middle of the gathered community. “Assembly” and “congregation” are words for Israel’s people gathered together, both for worship and for the public life of the nation. Two things follow. The sin that began in secret, with smooth words, ended exposed before everyone. And it happened among the people of God. Being in the congregation did not protect a heart that despised correction. The text does not say what form the public disgrace took. It says that his disgrace was public and that it came upon him among the very people with whom he worshiped and lived.

Study Notes

“Assembly” and “congregation” translate two different Hebrew words that are both used of Israel gathered as a community. Placed side by side, they stress that his disgrace took place before the whole people. The NLT brings out the public shame directly: “I have come to the brink of utter ruin, and now I must face public disgrace.”

Proverbs 5:15–20 — Rejoice with the Wife of Your Youth

Proverbs 5:15
Proverbs 5:15
NKJV

Proverbs 5:15 Drink waterfrom your own cistern, And running water from your own well.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 5:15 Drink water from your own well —share your love only with your wife. (NLT)

Main Idea

Using the picture of water, the father urges his son to drink from his own cistern and from the running water of his own well. A man’s thirst for love and pleasure is to be satisfied within his own marriage.

Verse Explanation

Using the picture of water, the father urges his son to drink from his own cistern and from the running water of his own well. A man’s thirst for love and pleasure is to be satisfied within his own marriage.

“Drink water from your own cistern,”

After fourteen verses of warning, the father turns to what his son should do, and his first word is “Drink.” Desire is not the enemy; it has a proper home. In a dry land like Israel water was precious and had to be caught and stored. A cistern was a pit or tank, often cut into rock, that held rainwater for a household, and to have one was to have a guarded supply of life. The father uses the picture for a man’s own wife. His thirst for love and sexual delight is real, and God means it to be satisfied, at home and with her.

“And running water from your own well.”

A well gave running water, fresh water flowing up from a spring below. The second line moves from stored water to living, flowing water, and so pictures the goodness and refreshment of married love. The repeated “your own” sets these images against the stranger, the aliens and the foreigner of 5:3–10. The father’s answer to temptation is a full yes to the wife God has given, and not only a no to the woman he must refuse. The yes protects the son as much as the warning does.

Study Notes

The Song of Songs uses the same family of images for the bride: “A garden enclosed Is my sister, my spouse, A spring shut up, A fountain sealed” (Song of Songs 4:12). It also calls her “A well of living waters” (Song of Songs 4:15).

The water images run without a break from 5:15 through 5:18: cistern, well, fountains, streams and fountain. The address to “the wife of your youth” at 5:18 makes their meaning plain.

A question follows: should his springs be scattered abroad, streams of water flowing in the streets? Sexual love poured out carelessly is like precious water wasted in public places, where it is lost and defiled.

“Should your fountains be dispersed abroad,”

The NKJV reads the verse as a question expecting the answer no. “Fountains” are springs, sources of water, and here they stand for the man’s sexual love and its life-giving power. To have them “dispersed abroad” is to let them spill out in every direction, poured out on anyone and kept for no one.

“Streams of water in the streets?”

Water running in the streets is wasted. It is trampled, dirtied and lost in public places where it does no one any good. The streets and open squares are also where the immoral woman lurks; chapter 7 places her “in the open square, Lurking at every corner” (7:12). A man’s love does not grow by being spread around. It is cheapened and lost. The father asks rather than commands so that his son will see the folly for himself and agree with the answer.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of 5:16 contains no word or prefix that marks it as a question, so translators must decide from the context whether the verse asks or states. The NKJV, ESV, NASB, the 1984 NIV and AMP read a question expecting “no.” The KJV and YLT read the line as a statement (KJV: ‘Let thy fountains be dispersed abroad’). The context favors the question, because 5:15 has just told the son to drink from his own well and 5:17 insists that these waters be “only your own.”

Some interpreters also see children in the scattered fountains, and the AMP adds “children” in parentheses after “springs.” The verse itself does not require that reading.

“Let them be only your own,”

The exclusiveness is part of the gift, the condition under which the joy of 5:18–19 can flourish.

“And not for strangers with you.”

“Strangers” carries on the stranger language that runs through the chapter, from the strange woman of 5:3 to the aliens and foreigner of 5:10. The words “with you” picture others sharing what should be private, as though strangers were drinking from his well beside him. What is shared with strangers is no longer the couple’s own. The father wants his son to guard his marriage the way an Israelite guarded his water supply, because once it is fouled it cannot easily be made clean again.

“Let your fountain be blessed,”

The father blesses what God created. “Your fountain” is the man’s own source of love and life, the same image as 5:15–16, now spoken over with a blessing. From the first chapter of the Bible, “God blessed them” (Genesis 1:28), the man and the woman He had made, and married love still stands under that blessing. “And rejoice with the wife of your youth.”

“Rejoice” is a command. The father wants more than a faithful marriage; he wants a glad one. “The wife of your youth” is the woman a man married when he was young, and the phrase looks across the years to the same woman, still delighted in, when both are older. Ecclesiastes gives the same counsel: “Live joyfully with the wife whom you love all the days of your vain life” (Ecclesiastes 9:9). Gladness in marriage is one of the father’s chief defenses against the stranger, because a man who keeps delighting in his wife has little room left for counterfeit delight. The phrase also turns the immoral woman’s betrayal around: she is the one who “forsakes the companion of her youth” (2:17).

Main Idea

With frank and tender language, the father describes the wife as a loving deer and a graceful doe, and he urges the son to be satisfied with her body at all times and always enraptured with her love. Sexual delight within marriage is a good gift from God to be enjoyed, not merely tolerated.

Verse Explanation

With frank and tender language, the father describes the wife as a loving deer and a graceful doe, and he urges the son to be satisfied with her body at all times and always enraptured with her love. Sexual delight within marriage is a good gift from God to be enjoyed, not merely tolerated.

“As a loving deer and a graceful doe,”

The father describes the wife with images of grace and beauty from the animal world. The deer and the doe were admired for their gentle eyes, light movement and sure-footed beauty, and the adjectives “loving” and “graceful” add affection and charm. The Song of Songs draws on the same world in Song of Songs 4:5, where the bridegroom praises his bride. The father speaks of his son’s wife tenderly, as a woman to be admired and cherished, not merely possessed.

“Let her breasts satisfy you at all times;”

The father speaks plainly about physical love. Scripture is not embarrassed by the body or by sexual delight within marriage. “Satisfy” continues the water imagery of 5:15, because the Hebrew verb behind it means to drink one’s fill, to be refreshed and fully satisfied. “At all times” means this satisfaction is not meant to fade after the early years of marriage. God intends a husband’s desire to find its full answer in his own wife. The New Testament affirms the same goodness: “Marriage is honorable among all, and the bed undefiled” (Hebrews 13:4).

“And always be enraptured with her love.”

“Enraptured” means carried away, overwhelmed with delight, almost intoxicated. The father does not ask his son to deaden the capacity to be swept away by love. He directs it toward his wife. “Always” matches “at all times” in the line before, and married love is to remain a source of delight for a lifetime. The father is speaking to a son who will marry, and he sets out God’s good design. His words give no grounds for shaming readers who are single, widowed or in a painful marriage; they show what the gift is meant to be and why trading it away would be folly.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “doe” in “graceful doe” is the feminine form of the word for the wild mountain goat, or ibex, a sure-footed climber of rocky slopes.

The KJV margin renders the Hebrew verb behind “satisfy” literally as “water thee.” That literal rendering shows how closely the line is tied to the water images of 5:15–18.

The Hebrew verb translated “be enraptured” appears again in 5:20 (“be enraptured”) and in 5:23 (“go astray”). Its basic sense is to wander or stray, and it can describe reeling or staggering, and the KJV margin gives the literal sense as “err thou.” The repetition links delight in a wife, infatuation with a seductress and the final straying of the fool.

The language of embrace is set deliberately beside the wife’s love in 5:19, so the same desire appears first as a gift and then as a counterfeit. “For the ways of man are before the eyes of the LORD,”

“For” gives the deepest reason behind everything the father has said. “And He ponders all his paths.”

“Ponders” is the same Hebrew verb used in 4:26 and 5:6. At 4:26 the son was told to weigh his own path; at 5:6 he was warned not to weigh her road as though it were the path of life; here the LORD weighs every path. He examines and evaluates each one, as one weighs on a scale or surveys a road. His sight is more than observation. He knows where each path leads and judges it rightly, and His seeing gives weight to every command the father has given. The God who sees the road to her door also sees the husband who keeps his vows when no one else is watching.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb at the end of 5:21 can mean to weigh or to make level, and translations vary among those and related senses: the NKJV and ESV read “ponders,” the 1984 NIV “examines,” and the NASB “watches.”

“His own iniquities entrap the wicked man,”

The focus moves from God’s sight to sin’s own consequences. The wicked man is caught by his own iniquities. No one else springs the trap. Sin promised freedom and pleasure, but it works like a snare that closes on the one who set it. The word “own” places the responsibility squarely on him. He cannot blame the woman’s smooth words, the teachers he ignored or his circumstances; his choices built the trap that holds him.

“And he is caught in the cords of his sin.”

Cords are ropes, the kind used to bind a prisoner or tie down an animal. Each sin may feel like a free choice, but sins repeated become ropes that bind tighter with every turn. What he thought he controlled now controls him. Jesus said the same: “whoever commits sin is a slave of sin” (John 8:34). Yet the verse describes how sin works; it does not close the door on those who turn back. Proverbs itself promises, “whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy” (28:13). The cords of sin can be loosed by the mercy of God.

The chapter lets this warning stand as its last word.
