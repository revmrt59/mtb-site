Proverbs 6

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 6:1
Proverbs 6:1
NKJV

Proverbs 6:1 My son, if you become surety for your friend, If you have shaken hands in pledge for a stranger,(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 6:1 My child, if you have put up security for a friend’s debtor agreed to guarantee the debt of a stranger — (NLT)

Main Idea

The father raises a practical danger: becoming surety, guaranteeing a friend’s debt or shaking hands on a pledge for a stranger. A quick promise to cover someone else’s obligation can tie a person to a debt he does not control.

Verse Explanation

The father raises a practical danger: becoming surety, guaranteeing a friend’s debt or shaking hands on a pledge for a stranger. A quick promise to cover someone else’s obligation can tie a person to a debt he does not control.

“My son, if you become surety for your friend,”

The father turns to a new danger, one that has to do with money and promises. To become “surety” was to guarantee another person’s debt: if the borrower failed to pay, the one who had pledged became liable for it. The nearest modern equivalent is cosigning a loan. “Your friend” translates the ordinary Hebrew word for a neighbor or companion, someone the son knows. That is part of the danger. A rash pledge usually begins with goodwill: a friend is in need, a lender wants security, and the son steps in with his name.

“If you have shaken hands in pledge for a stranger,”

In Israel an agreement of this kind was sealed by striking hands, a public gesture that bound a man to his word, and Proverbs returns to the practice more than once (17:18; 22:26). The “stranger” is someone outside the son’s circle, whether the lender to whom he is now bound or another party whose debts he has agreed to carry. Either way, he has tied his future to the faithfulness of someone he cannot control. Proverbs commends generosity elsewhere: “He who has pity on the poor lends to the LORD” (19:17). The father is not forbidding help. He is warning against a hasty guarantee that hands one’s security to another person’s choices. Wisdom gives freely what it can afford to lose and avoids promises it may not be able to keep.

Study Notes

Whether the “friend” and the “stranger” of 6:1 are the same person, or the friend is the borrower and the stranger the creditor, is debated. The ESV and NASB render “friend” as “neighbor,” which shows the general sense of the Hebrew word.

“Shaken hands” renders a Hebrew phrase that means to strike the palms, the gesture that sealed the pledge. The KJV keeps the picture of a hand “stricken” with a stranger.

Proverbs 6:2

Proverbs 6:2 You are snared by the words of your mouth; You are taken by the words of your mouth.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 6:2 if you have trapped yourself by your agreementand are caught by what you said — (NLT)

Main Idea

By that pledge the son is snared and caught by the words of his own mouth. The repeated line stresses that his own promise, spoken too quickly, has become the trap that holds him.

Verse Explanation

By that pledge the son is snared and caught by the words of his own mouth. The repeated line stresses that his own promise, spoken too quickly, has become the trap that holds him.

“You are snared by the words of your mouth;”

The trap was not set by an enemy. The son is snared by his own words. A promise spoken in a moment of sympathy, pressure or pride has become a snare that holds him. The father wants his son to see that words create obligations. What comes out of his mouth can bind his hands and his future for years.

“You are taken by the words of your mouth.”

The second line repeats the first almost exactly, changing only the verb. “Taken” means captured, caught like an animal in a trap or a city in war. The repetition presses the point home: it was his own mouth. A single pledge has put him in the power of others. The father does not tell him to treat his word as if it meant nothing. Because his word is real and binding, he must now take honorable steps to be released from it.

Proverbs 6:3
Proverbs 6:3
NKJV

Proverbs 6:3 So do this, my son, and deliver yourself; For you have come into the hand of your friend: Go and humble yourself; Plead with your friend.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 6:3 follow my advice and save yourself,for you have placed yourself at your friend’s mercy.Now swallow your pride;go and beg to have your name erased. (NLT)

Main Idea

The remedy is urgent and humbling: “So do this, my son, and deliver yourself,” for he is now in his friend’s power. He must go, swallow his pride, and press his friend to release him from the pledge.

Verse Explanation

The remedy is urgent and humbling: “So do this, my son, and deliver yourself,” for he is now in his friend’s power. He must go, swallow his pride, and press his friend to release him from the pledge.

“So do this, my son, and deliver yourself;”

The father moves from diagnosis to remedy, and his tone becomes urgent. To “deliver yourself” is to rescue yourself and get free. He does not tell the son to quietly break his word or to wait and hope that the borrower pays. He tells him to act.

“For you have come into the hand of your friend:”

“Into the hand of” means into someone’s power. By pledging, the son has placed his security under another person’s control. Whether this friend is the borrower whose debt he guaranteed or the one who now holds his pledge, the son is no longer free to manage his own affairs.

“Go and humble yourself;”

The way out requires humility. The son must set aside his pride, admit that he promised too quickly, and seek release. Pride would rather stay trapped than confess a mistake, and many people remain in difficulties they could have left because they would not humble themselves and ask.

“Plead with your friend.”

To “plead” here is to press hard, to ask persistently and refuse to accept a casual no. The father does not counsel a polite inquiry. He counsels earnest, repeated appeal until the son is released from the obligation. The energy that was missing when the promise was made must now go into getting free of it.

Study Notes

The two Hebrew verbs behind “humble yourself” and “plead” are rare. The first appears elsewhere only in Psalm 68:30, and its basic sense is to stamp or trample, which here suggests a man lowering himself before another. The second means to press or urge hard. Translations therefore vary: the NKJV and NASB read “humble yourself,” the ESV has “hasten” and “plead urgently,” and the NASB “importune.” All of them stress humble, persistent pressing.

Verse Explanation

He is not to give sleep to his eyes or rest to his eyelids until the matter is settled. Delay only tightens the trap, so getting free must come before comfort.

“Give no sleep to your eyes,”

The urgency becomes concrete. The son is not to go to bed with the matter unsettled. A debt guaranteed for someone else can grow, and a creditor’s patience can run out. Every night of delay tightens the trap, so getting free must come before comfort.

“Nor slumber to your eyelids.”

The second line repeats the first for emphasis: rest must wait until he is free. The same pair of Hebrew words, translated sleep and slumber, returns a few lines later in the sluggard’s request for “A little sleep, a little slumber” (6:10). The son caught by a rash pledge must give up sleep for a time; the sluggard who gives himself to sleep loses everything. Sleep is good in its place, and wisdom knows when it must wait.

Proverbs 6:5
Proverbs 6:5
NKJV

Proverbs 6:5 Deliver yourself like a gazelle from the hand of the hunter, And like a bird from the hand of the fowler.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 6:5 Save yourself like a gazelle escaping from a hunter,like a bird fleeing from a net. (NLT)

Main Idea

The son must free himself like a gazelle escaping the hunter’s hand or a bird escaping the fowler. The images picture a desperate, all-out effort to get loose, the opposite of the careless way he got caught.

Verse Explanation

The son must free himself like a gazelle escaping the hunter’s hand or a bird escaping the fowler. The images picture a desperate, all-out effort to get loose, the opposite of the careless way he got caught.

“Deliver yourself like a gazelle from the hand of the hunter,”

A gazelle caught by a hunter does not relax or wait to see what will happen. It struggles with all its strength to get loose. The gazelle was proverbial for speed; Scripture says of one fast runner that he “was as fleet of foot as a wild gazelle” (2 Samuel 2:18). The father wants the same quick, all-out effort from his son.

“And like a bird from the hand of the fowler.”

A fowler was a bird-catcher who used nets and snares. A bird caught in his trap beats its wings until it escapes. The two pictures together describe a creature that knows it is caught and will not rest until it is free. The careless way the son got into the trap, with a handshake and a few words, stands in sharp contrast to the desperate effort needed to get out. A psalm uses the same picture for rescue: “Our soul has escaped as a bird from the snare of the fowlers” (Psalm 124:7).

Proverbs 6:6
Proverbs 6:6
NKJV

Proverbs 6:6 Go to the ant, you sluggard! Consider her ways and be wise,(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 6:6 Take a lesson from the ants, you lazybones.Learn from their ways and become wise! (NLT)

Main Idea

The father sends the sluggard to a tiny teacher: “Go to the ant, you sluggard!” The lazy man is told to study the ant’s ways and become wise, for even an insect can shame the one who will not work.

Verse Explanation

The father sends the sluggard to a tiny teacher: “Go to the ant, you sluggard!” The lazy man is told to study the ant’s ways and become wise, for even an insect can shame the one who will not work.

“Go to the ant, you sluggard!”

The father turns to a new figure, the sluggard. The NKJV uses this word only here and in 6:9; elsewhere Proverbs speaks of the lazy man. The sluggard is not someone who cannot work because of illness, disability or genuine hardship. He is someone who will not work. He lacks self-discipline, keeps putting off what must be done, and loves his sleep. The father sends him to school, and the teacher is an ant. There is gentle mockery in the command: a man made in God’s image is told to go and learn from one of the smallest creatures he could step on.

“Consider her ways and be wise,”

To “consider” is to look carefully and study. The ant’s “ways” are her habits of life. The goal is that the sluggard will “be wise,” gaining the skill for living that he lacks. Wisdom in Proverbs is practical, and here it means the skill of working steadily and preparing ahead. God has built lessons into His creation, and the humble can learn from them. Work itself belonged to God’s good design before sin entered the world, for the LORD put the man in the garden “to tend and keep it” (Genesis 2:15). The sluggard’s laziness quietly refuses that design.

Study Notes

“Sluggard” appears in the NKJV of Proverbs only at 6:6 and 6:9; the same Hebrew word is rendered “lazy man” elsewhere in the book (for example 13:4 and 26:13–16).

Verse Explanation

What makes the ant remarkable is that she has no captain, overseer, or ruler driving her. She works without anyone watching or commanding, which is the self-discipline the sluggard lacks.

“Which, having no captain, Overseer or ruler,”

The ant’s remarkable trait is that she works without a boss. “Captain,” “Overseer” and “ruler” are three titles of authority: a chief who commands, an officer who supervises and a ruler who governs. The ant has none of them standing over her, and still she works. The sluggard needs to be pushed, reminded and driven, and even then he does little. The ant needs no one to watch her. This is self-discipline: doing what needs to be done because it needs to be done, without waiting for someone to enforce it. The lesson does not condemn leaders or supervision. It exposes the person who works only when he is watched.

Verse Explanation

Still she gathers her supplies in summer and her food at harvest, working while the time allows. The ant prepares ahead of time, while the sluggard lets the opportunity pass.

“Provides her supplies in the summer,”

Summer is the season of warmth and growth, when food is available. The ant uses it. She gathers while there is something to gather, before the season changes and food is scarce.

“And gathers her food in the harvest.”

The harvest is the time of gathering crops, a window that does not stay open long. The ant works ahead of her need and stores what she will eat later. The lesson is foresight: wisdom recognizes the season and uses it. The sluggard lets the opportunity pass and finds himself without supply after the season has turned. Some opportunities come only once a year, and some only once in a lifetime; the ant does not waste hers.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Two questions confront the sluggard: “How long will you slumber, O sluggard?” and when will he rise from his sleep? The father addresses him directly, exposing a habit of putting off what must be done.

“How long will you slumber, O sluggard?”

The father now speaks to the sluggard directly. “How long” implies that the sleeping has already gone on far too long. The question is a rebuke, but it also calls him to get up, and it assumes that he still can.

“When will you rise from your sleep?”

The second question presses further. “When” asks for a time, and the sluggard never has one. He always intends to rise later. These questions expose the habit of putting things off. The sluggard rarely refuses to work outright; he simply never finds the right moment to begin, and the moment he is waiting for never comes.

Main Idea

The sluggard’s reply is pictured in his own drowsy words: a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands. Each delay seems small and harmless, but together they become a way of life.

Verse Explanation

The sluggard’s reply is pictured in his own drowsy words: a little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands. Each delay seems small and harmless, but together they become a way of life.

“A little sleep, a little slumber,”

The father now voices the sluggard’s own reply. Each request is small: just a little more sleep, just a little more rest. No single delay seems harmful, and that is the trap. The repeated “a little” shows how the sluggard thinks. He never asks for a ruined life. He only asks for a few more minutes, again and again.

“A little folding of the hands to sleep—”

Folded hands are hands at rest, doing nothing, the opposite of the ant’s busy work. The dash at the end of the NKJV line shows that the thought runs straight into 6:11, where all these little delays add up to something large. A way of life is built from small choices repeated, and the sluggard’s life is built from many small postponements.

Study Notes

6:10–11 return almost word for word in 24:33–34, as the lesson drawn from the overgrown field of the lazy man (24:30–34). The NKJV reads “to sleep” in 6:10 and “to rest” in 24:33.

Proverbs 6:11
NKJV

Proverbs 6:11 So shall your poverty come on you like a prowler, And your need like an armed man.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 6:11 then poverty will pounce on you like a bandit;scarcity will attack you like an armed robber. (NLT)

Main Idea

The result is poverty that comes on him like a prowler and need like an armed man. Ruin arrives suddenly and overpowers him, though it has been building quietly through every small delay.

Verse Explanation

The result is poverty that comes on him like a prowler and need like an armed man. Ruin arrives suddenly and overpowers him, though it has been building quietly through every small delay.

“So shall your poverty come on you like a prowler,”

The consequence arrives. Poverty comes like a prowler, someone who moves about quietly and arrives unexpectedly. The sluggard does not see poverty approaching. It slips up on him while he sleeps. Each small delay seemed harmless, but together they have opened the door.

“And your need like an armed man.”

“Need” is want, the lack of what is necessary. It comes like an armed man, someone who cannot be resisted. When poverty finally arrives, the sluggard has no defense against it. The two pictures move from stealth to force: consequences gather quietly and then overwhelm. This describes where persistent idleness usually leads. Proverbs does not teach that all poverty comes from laziness, and nothing here permits the reader to blame the sick, the disabled or the needy for their circumstances. The father is warning a capable son about his own habits.

Study Notes

“Prowler” renders a Hebrew expression for one who walks about or travels, and the KJV keeps that sense: ‘one that travelleth.’ Other versions bring out the threat: the NASB has “vagabond,” the ESV “robber,” and the current NIV “thief.” “Armed man” is literally, in Hebrew, ‘a man of the shield,’ a soldier equipped for battle.

Proverbs 6:12–15 — The Worthless Troublemaker

Proverbs 6:12
Proverbs 6:12
NKJV

Proverbs 6:12 A worthless person, a wicked man, Walks with a perverse mouth;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 6:12 What are worthless and wicked people like?They are constant liars, (NLT)

Main Idea

A new figure appears: the worthless person, a wicked man who goes about with a perverse mouth. His twisted speech is the first mark of a life given over to trouble.

Verse Explanation

A new figure appears: the worthless person, a wicked man who goes about with a perverse mouth. His twisted speech is the first mark of a life given over to trouble.

“A worthless person, a wicked man,”

The father turns to another figure, and this one is worse than the sluggard. The sluggard harms himself by doing nothing; this man harms others by what he does. “A worthless person” translates a Hebrew expression for a man of no value and real wickedness, someone destructive as well as useless. “A wicked man” adds that his whole life is set against what is right.

“Walks with a perverse mouth;”

To “walk” is to live, to go about one’s life. His speech is “perverse,” twisted and crooked, the opposite of honest and straight. Deceitful talk is not an occasional slip for him; it is the way he goes through life. The father describes him from the outside in, beginning with the mouth, moving through his body, and reaching his heart in 6:14. Wisdom learns to recognize such a person before being drawn into his schemes.

Study Notes

“A worthless person” is literally, in Hebrew, ‘a man of belial.’ Here belial is not a personal name but the Hebrew word for worthlessness, describing someone good for nothing and destructive. The KJV and other older versions left the word untranslated, which is why it can sound like a name. The same word describes Eli’s sons in 1 Samuel 2:12, where the KJV has ‘sons of Belial’ and the NKJV renders it “corrupt.”

Proverbs 6:13
NKJV

Proverbs 6:13 He winks with his eyes, He shuffles his feet, He points with his fingers;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 6:13 signaling their deceit with a wink of the eye,a nudge of the foot, or the wiggle of fingers. (NLT)

Main Idea

He also speaks with his body, winking, shuffling his feet, and pointing with his fingers. These secret signals show a man who deceives and schemes even when he says nothing aloud.

Verse Explanation

He also speaks with his body, winking, shuffling his feet, and pointing with his fingers. These secret signals show a man who deceives and schemes even when he says nothing aloud.

“He winks with his eyes,”

The troublemaker communicates without words. A wink is a secret signal, a way of letting an accomplice know that what is being said is not what is meant, or of mocking someone behind his back.

“He shuffles his feet,”

Movements of the feet serve as further signals. Like the wink, they send hidden messages, perhaps to coordinate with others or to mislead the person being deceived.

“He points with his fingers;”

Pointing fingers can direct attention, accuse, or signal to a partner. Together, eyes, feet and fingers show a man whose whole body serves his deceit. His mouth says one thing while his body says another. Winking and gesturing are not wrong in themselves. A deceitful heart simply puts every part of the person to work for its schemes. The signals may fool the people around him, but they do not hide anything from God.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “shuffles his feet” is usually understood as scraping or rubbing the feet, a movement used to make secret signs; some scholars instead connect it with a verb meaning to speak. The KJV’s “speaketh” expresses that signalling sense, and the ESV and NASB read “signals.”

Main Idea

Perversity fills his heart, he keeps plotting evil, and he sows discord among others. His mouth and gestures flow from a corrupt heart, and the harvest he plants is strife.

Verse Explanation

Perversity fills his heart, he keeps plotting evil, and he sows discord among others. His mouth and gestures flow from a corrupt heart, and the harvest he plants is strife.

“Perversity is in his heart,”

Now the source appears. His twisted mouth and secret signals flow from a heart full of perversity, a deliberate crookedness that turns what is straight. Proverbs treats the heart as the inner person, the place of thought, will and desire, and teaches that the whole course of a life comes from it: “Keep your heart with all diligence, For out of it spring the issues of life” (4:23).

“He devises evil continually,”

To “devise” is to plot and plan. He does not stumble into evil; he thinks it up on purpose. “Continually” shows that this is his steady occupation. His mind is always at work on some new harm.

“He sows discord.”

“Discord” is strife and quarreling between people. To “sow” it is to plant it on purpose and let it grow, as a farmer scatters seed. The troublemaker spreads conflict among people who would otherwise live in peace. His rumors, half-truths and whispered suggestions do their work long after he has moved on. The phrase links his portrait directly to the list that follows, which ends with “one who sows discord among brethren” (6:19).

Proverbs 6:15
NKJV

Proverbs 6:15 Therefore his calamity shall come suddenly; Suddenly he shall be broken without remedy.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 6:15 But they will be destroyed suddenly,broken in an instant beyond all hope of healing. (NLT)

Main Idea

Therefore calamity will come on him suddenly, and he will be broken beyond remedy. The one who caused others to fall will himself be shattered without warning or repair.

Verse Explanation

Therefore calamity will come on him suddenly, and he will be broken beyond remedy. The one who caused others to fall will himself be shattered without warning or repair.

“Therefore his calamity shall come suddenly;”

“Therefore” ties his end to his way of life. He devised evil “continually,” but his ruin comes “suddenly.” The man who spent his life arranging trouble for others does not see his own trouble coming. Calamity arrives without warning.

“Suddenly he shall be broken without remedy.”

The word “Suddenly” is repeated for emphasis. “Broken” means shattered, as a clay pot is smashed. “Without remedy” means there is no healing and no repair. The schemer who broke the peace of others is himself broken beyond mending. The verse does not say when this will happen, and it may be long delayed, but the end is certain. A wicked man may seem untouchable for years. God’s justice can wait and then arrive in a moment, and delay is no guarantee of safety.

Study Notes

In Hebrew “without remedy” reads literally ‘and there is no healing.’ The noun for healing is the same word rendered “health” where the father says his words are “health to all their flesh” (4:22). The schemer is left without the healing that wisdom gives.

Proverbs 6:16–19 — Seven Things the LORD Hates

Proverbs 6:16
Proverbs 6:16
NKJV

Proverbs 6:16 These six things the LORD hates, Yes, seven are an abomination to Him:(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 6:16 There are six things the LORD hates —no, seven things he detests: (NLT)

Main Idea

The father introduces a list with a counting pattern: six things the LORD hates, and seven that are an abomination to Him. The form builds toward the seventh item for emphasis, showing what God finds detestable.

Verse Explanation

The father introduces a list with a counting pattern: six things the LORD hates, and seven that are an abomination to Him. The form builds toward the seventh item for emphasis, showing what God finds detestable.

“These six things the LORD hates,”

The father now speaks of what the LORD Himself hates. The word is strong, and it is meant to be. God is not neutral toward evil that destroys people. His hatred of these things comes from His goodness and holiness: He hates what wounds the people He loves and what contradicts His own character. The list describes ways of acting, and every item on it is something a person can turn from.

“Yes, seven are an abomination to Him:”

The pattern of six and then seven is a Hebrew way of building a list toward a climax. Agur uses the same form: “There are three things which are too wonderful for me, Yes, four which I do not understand” (30:18). The form does not mean that the first six are hated less, and nothing should be built on the difference between six and seven. It gives the final item special emphasis. An “abomination” is something God finds detestable, utterly offensive to Him. In Proverbs the word often stands opposite what is “His delight” (11:20). The list moves through the body, eyes, tongue, hands, heart and feet, and ends with the sin that destroys community.

Study Notes

The same rising-number pattern appears in the prophets, “For three transgressions of Damascus, and for four” (Amos 1:3), and repeatedly in 30:15–31.

Proverbs 6:17
Proverbs 6:17

Main Idea

The first three are a proud look, a lying tongue, and hands that shed innocent blood. The list begins with pride, then moves through the body from the eyes to the tongue to the hands, from arrogance to deceit to violence.

Verse Explanation

The first three are a proud look, a lying tongue, and hands that shed innocent blood. The list begins with pride, then moves through the body from the eyes to the tongue to the hands, from arrogance to deceit to violence.

“A proud look,”

The list begins with pride. “A proud look” describes eyes lifted up in arrogance, looking down on others. Pride heads the list because so much else grows from it; the one who thinks himself above others and above correction is ready to lie, harm and divide. Wisdom says the same in chapter 8: “Pride and arrogance and the evil way And the perverse mouth I hate” (8:13).

“A lying tongue,”

The second item is deceitful speech. A lying tongue says what is false to gain an advantage or avoid exposure. The worthless man of 6:12 walked with a perverse mouth, and here God names lying as something He hates. God is truthful, and lies attack the trust that every relationship depends on.

“Hands that shed innocent blood,”

The third item is violence against the innocent: killing or harming someone who has done nothing to deserve it. The list moves from arrogance in the eyes to deceit on the tongue to violence in the hands, showing how sin spreads through a person and grows worse as it goes. Scripture treats the shedding of innocent blood with the utmost seriousness, because every human being bears God’s image: “For in the image of God He made man” (Genesis 9:6).

Study Notes

In Hebrew “a proud look” is literally ‘eyes raised high.’ The ESV and NASB render it “haughty eyes,” and the KJV margin gives the same literal sense: ‘Heb. Haughty eyes.’

The only safe course is the one he has just given: keep the commandment as a lamp, guard the heart and the eyes, and stay away from a fire that no one can carry without being burned.

Study Notes

The NASB renders “recompense” as “ransom.” In Hebrew the line reads literally that he will not ‘lift the face’ of any ransom, which the KJV margin gives as ‘He will not accept the face of any ransom.’ The picture is of a man who will not even look with favor on the payment held out to him, which is why the KJV can say ‘He will not regard any ransom’ and the NKJV simply that he will accept no recompense.
