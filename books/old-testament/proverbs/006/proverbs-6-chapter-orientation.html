Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 6
Main Idea:

Proverbs 6 warns a son against traps that catch a careless life, a rash pledge, lazy sleep, and the lying, discord-sowing ways the LORD hates, and then holds up his parents’ commandment as a lamp that keeps him from the fire of adultery.

Setting:

The chapter stands between the warning against the immoral woman in chapter 5 and the full scene of chapter 7. Four short warnings come first (6:1–19), and then the father’s “My son” at 6:20 leads back to adultery.

Chapter Outline:

  • Proverbs 6:1–5 — Get Free from a Rash Pledge

  • Proverbs 6:6–11 — Go to the Ant, You Sluggard

  • Proverbs 6:12–15 — The Worthless Troublemaker

  • Proverbs 6:16–19 — Seven Things the LORD Hates

  • Proverbs 6:20–23 — The Commandment Is a Lamp

  • Proverbs 6:24–35 — Fire in the Bosom

Key Verse:

“Can a man take fire to his bosom, And his clothes not be burned?” (Proverbs 6:27)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Proverbs 6 pauses in the father’s warnings about adultery to name other traps, and each begins small. A son shakes hands on a friend’s debt and is “snared by the words of your mouth” (6:2). A sluggard asks for “A little sleep, a little slumber” (6:10), and poverty arrives “like a prowler” (6:11). Wisdom, the skill of living life God’s way, is shown here in what a man promises and how he works. The ant has no overseer, yet she gathers her food in harvest, and the sluggard is told, “Consider her ways and be wise” (6:6).

The chapter then turns to wickedness and shows what God thinks of it. The worthless man winks, shuffles, points, and “sows discord” (6:14). Next comes the list of what “the LORD hates” (6:16), which opens with “A proud look” and saves for last “one who sows discord among brethren” (6:17, 19). His hatred of these things comes from His goodness, because each one wounds people He loves.

When “My son” returns, the father answers temptation with teaching bound on the heart, a lamp for every hour. His question, “Can a man take fire to his bosom, And his clothes not be burned?” (6:27), tells the son that adultery cannot be handled safely, because the man who commits it “destroys his own soul” (6:32).

Chapter Sections

Proverbs 6:1–5 — Get Free from a Rash Pledge

A son who has guaranteed a neighbor’s debt has been caught by his own quick words, so the father tells him to set his pride aside and plead for release before he lets himself sleep. The counsel guards against careless pledges without forbidding generosity, which the book commends elsewhere (11:24–25).

Proverbs 6:6–11 — Go to the Ant, You Sluggard

The sluggard is sent to learn from the ant, which works with no one standing over her and gathers food while the season allows. His own drowsy excuses show how small delays add up until need overtakes him like an armed man.

Proverbs 6:12–15 — The Worthless Troublemaker

The worthless man deceives with his whole person, through a twisted mouth, a wink, a shuffle of the feet, and a pointing finger, because his heart keeps plotting evil and planting strife. The ruin he works on others comes back on him suddenly and cannot be repaired.

Proverbs 6:16–19 — Seven Things the LORD Hates

A counting pattern builds to seven things the LORD finds detestable, beginning with a proud look and tracing sin through the eyes, tongue, hands, heart, and feet. The list ends with the one who sows discord among brethren, the same sin that marked the troublemaker just before it.

Proverbs 6:20–23 — The Commandment Is a Lamp

The father calls his son back to both parents’ teaching and tells him to bind it on his heart, where it will guide him as he walks, guard him as he sleeps, and speak with him when he wakes. Even their correction lights the way of life.

Proverbs 6:24–35 — Fire in the Bosom

That light keeps the son from the seductress, and the warning starts with desire in the heart and the pull of her eyes, before any act. Questions about fire and hot coals, and a comparison with a hungry thief, show that adultery costs a man his own soul and stirs a husband’s anger that no gift can quiet.

Why This Chapter Matters

Proverbs 6 brings wisdom down to the details of an ordinary week: a signature on someone else’s debt, a morning slept away, a wink across the room. The father treats these as real dangers because small, repeated choices decide where a life ends up. His portrait is of a man who will not work, never of one who cannot; illness, disability, and genuine need lie outside this warning.

The chapter also shows how much God cares about the peace of His people. Sowing discord stands beside lying and the shedding of innocent blood among the things He hates, and Jesus later blesses the peacemakers (Matthew 5:9). The warning about adultery locates the battle in the heart (6:25; compare Matthew 5:28). Its reproach among people lasts (6:33), yet Proverbs also promises that “whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy” (28:13).

Key Themes to Watch For

Traps that start small. A quick handshake, a little more sleep, and a lingering look each lead to losses far larger than the first step (6:1–2, 10–11, 25–26).

Urgency to get free. The son is to give his eyes no sleep until he is released from the pledge, escaping like a gazelle from the hunter or a bird from the fowler (6:3–5).

Sin in the whole person. Mouth, eyes, feet, fingers, hands, and heart all appear in the troublemaker and in the list of what the LORD hates, because sin starts inside and comes out through the body (6:12–14, 17–18).

Discord among brethren. Sowing discord marks the worthless man and closes the LORD’s list, showing how seriously God takes the breaking of peace (6:14, 19).

Teaching kept in the heart. Parents’ commands bound on the heart lead, keep, and counsel the son, and they are what guard him from the seductress (6:20–24).

Key Words & Concepts

Surety (6:1). A person who guarantees someone else’s debt, much like cosigning a loan today. A handshake sealed the pledge, and the one who gave it became liable if the borrower failed.

Sluggard (6:6, 9). The NKJV uses this word only here; elsewhere Proverbs speaks of the lazy man. He lacks self-discipline and keeps putting off what must be done, which the ant’s steady work exposes.

A worthless person (6:12). A wicked man who uses his mouth, gestures, and heart to stir up trouble. His winks and signals are the outward show of a scheming heart.

Abomination (6:16). Something the LORD finds detestable. The pattern of six and then seven builds toward the last item for emphasis and does not set six against seven.

Fire in the bosom (6:27). The bosom is the fold of a garment at the chest. Carrying fire there means certain harm, the father’s picture of what adultery does to the man who commits it.

Restore sevenfold (6:31). Most likely full repayment rather than a fixed legal amount. The thief may lose everything in his house, yet his debt can be paid, while the wronged husband “will accept no recompense” (6:35).
