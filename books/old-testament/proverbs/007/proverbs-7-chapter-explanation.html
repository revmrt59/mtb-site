Proverbs 7:1
Proverbs 7:1
NKJV

Proverbs 7:1 My son, keep my words, And treasure my commands within you.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:1 Follow my advice, my son;always treasure my commands. (NLT)

Main Idea

The father begins his last warning about adultery by urging his son to keep his words and treasure his commands within. Before telling the story that follows, he points to the first defense: a heart that values his instruction and stores it up.

Verse Explanation

The father begins his last warning about adultery by urging his son to keep his words and treasure his commands within. Before telling the story that follows, he points to the first defense: a heart that values his instruction and stores it up.

“My son, keep my words,”

The father opens his last and fullest warning about adultery the way he opened the others, with “My son” and a call to keep his words. Before he tells the story that fills most of the chapter, he names his son’s first defense. To “keep” is to guard and hold on to something, the way a person keeps what is valuable from being lost or stolen. The father’s words are not meant to be heard once and set aside.

“And treasure my commands within you.”

To “treasure” is to store up and hide away as something precious, the way valuables are put where they will be safe. “Within you” places the commands inside the son, in his heart and memory, ready when he needs them. The father speaks before the woman of this chapter ever appears. The words a person stores up when life is quiet are the words he hears when pressure comes. The whole chapter turns on a contest of voices, her flattering words against the father’s, and the father makes sure his voice is inside his son first. Memorized words do not protect anyone automatically; what protects is a heart that values the father’s instruction enough to keep it.

Study Notes

The second line repeats the second line of 2:1 exactly, “And treasure my commands within you,” linking this final warning to the father’s first appeal to receive his words.

Proverbs 7:2
Proverbs 7:2
NKJV

Proverbs 7:2 Keep my commands and live, And my law as the apple of your eye.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:2 Obey my commands and live!Guard my instructions as you guard your own eyes. (NLT)

Main Idea

Keeping these commands means life, and the son is to guard the father’s law as the apple of his eye, the pupil that a person instinctively protects. The instruction is that precious, so the son is to keep it with the same quick and careful attention.

Verse Explanation

Keeping these commands means life, and the son is to guard the father’s law as the apple of his eye, the pupil that a person instinctively protects. The instruction is that precious, so the son is to keep it with the same quick and careful attention.

“Keep my commands and live,”

Keeping the commands means life. The father’s teaching is not a set of restrictions laid on top of life; it is the way life is found and kept. The chapter that begins with “live” ends with “the chambers of death” (7:27), and the story between them shows what happens to a young man who did not keep these words.

“And my law as the apple of your eye.”

“The apple of your eye” is the pupil, the small dark center of the eye. A person protects it by instinct: the eyelid closes and the hand comes up before any thought is formed. The son is to guard his father’s law, his instruction, with the same quick and careful protection. The picture tells him how precious the teaching is and how fast his response must be when anything threatens it. Temptation will not wait while he decides whether the father’s words still matter.

Study Notes

The same expression describes God’s care for His people: “He kept him as the apple of His eye” (Deuteronomy 32:10).

Proverbs 7:3
Proverbs 7:3
NKJV

Proverbs 7:3 Bind them on your fingers; Write them on the tablet of your heart.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:3 Tie them on your fingers as a reminder.Write them deep within your heart. (NLT)

Main Idea

He is to bind these commands on his fingers and write them on the tablet of his heart. Instruction must be close at hand in daily action and written deep within, where it governs desire.

Verse Explanation

He is to bind these commands on his fingers and write them on the tablet of his heart. Instruction must be close at hand in daily action and written deep within, where it governs desire.

“Bind them on your fingers;”

Fingers are what a person acts with. The hand reaches, takes and does. To bind the commands on the fingers pictures instruction close at hand in daily action, a constant prompt at the point where choices become deeds. The image recalls Moses’ command to bind God’s words “as a sign on your hand” (Deuteronomy 6:8).

“Write them on the tablet of your heart.”

A tablet was a writing surface of stone, clay or wood, and words written on it stayed. The heart is the inner person, where desire, thought and decision arise. The commands are to be written there, deep within, where they govern what the son wants as well as what he does. The father asked the same at 3:3. Hands and heart together cover the whole person, outward action and inward desire. In the story that follows, the young man “devoid of understanding” (7:7) shows what happens when nothing has been written there.

Proverbs 7:4
Proverbs 7:4
NKJV

Proverbs 7:4 Say to wisdom, "You are my sister," And call understanding your nearest kin,(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:4 Love wisdom like a sister;make insight a beloved member of your family. (NLT)

Main Idea

The son is told to say to wisdom, “You are my sister,” and to call understanding his nearest kin. Wisdom is to be a close and familiar companion, as dear and trusted as family, so that he is not looking elsewhere for intimacy.

Verse Explanation

The son is told to say to wisdom, “You are my sister,” and to call understanding his nearest kin. Wisdom is to be a close and familiar companion, as dear and trusted as family, so that he is not looking elsewhere for intimacy.

“Say to wisdom, ‘You are my sister,’”

In Israel “sister” could be a word of deep affection, and in the Song of Songs the bridegroom calls his bride “my sister, my spouse” (Song of Songs 4:12). The father tells his son to speak to wisdom with that kind of closeness. Wisdom is to be a familiar and dearly loved companion, someone he lives with and trusts. The pairing with the seductress is deliberate. A young man who already has a close and loving bond with wisdom is not looking elsewhere for intimacy. The best protection against a false closeness is a true one.

“And call understanding your nearest kin,”

“Nearest kin” is a close relative, a member of the family a person can count on. Understanding, the ability to see what is really happening, is to be that near. The young man in the story is “devoid of understanding” (7:7); the son is to have understanding as family. The plain point of the verse is intimacy with what is right, established before any temptation arrives.

Proverbs 7:5 That they may keep you from the immoral woman, (H802) From the seductress who flatters with her words.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:5 Let them protect you from an affair with an immoral woman,from listening to the flattery of a promiscuous woman. (NLT)

Main Idea

Such closeness to wisdom will keep him from the immoral woman, the seductress who flatters with her words. The danger is again her speech, and the son’s protection is a heart already filled with a better voice.

Verse Explanation

Such closeness to wisdom will keep him from the immoral woman, the seductress who flatters with her words. The danger is again her speech, and the son’s protection is a heart already filled with a better voice.

“That they may keep you from the immoral woman,”

“They” are wisdom and understanding, the sister and the near relative of 7:4. The father explains the purpose of this closeness: it will keep the son from the immoral woman. The Hebrew verb behind “keep” is the one used in 7:2 for the son guarding his father’s commands, and now it describes what those words do for him. What he guards will guard him. As in chapters 2 and 5, the immoral woman is an outsider to his home and to God’s ways, and 7:19 will reveal that she is married.

“From the seductress who flatters with her words.”

Her danger is again her speech. She flatters with her words; she makes them smooth. The line repeats the second line of 2:16 exactly, tying this chapter to the father’s earlier warnings. The story that follows gives her words more space than anything else, a speech that fills 7:14–20 and then the persuasion that finally wins in 7:21. The son’s protection is a heart already filled with a better voice.

The father now tells what he saw from the window of his house, looking out through the lattice. He becomes a watchman, describing a scene so his son can learn from another man’s ruin rather than his own.

Verse Explanation

The father now tells what he saw from the window of his house, looking out through the lattice. He becomes a watchman, describing a scene so his son can learn from another man’s ruin rather than his own.

“For at the window of my house”

The father now tells what he saw. The father’s window is covered with a lattice, a screen through which a person inside can look out at the street. From there he watches the street below.

“I looked through my lattice,”

He becomes a watchman, seeing a scene that the young man in it cannot see whole. From the window the father can see the street, the corner, the woman and where her house leads; the young man sees only the next step in front of him. The father tells the story so that his son can learn from another man’s ruin without suffering his own. The text presents it as something the father saw, and its details are true to life in every generation.

Proverbs 7:7
Proverbs 7:7
NKJV

Proverbs 7:7 And saw among the simple, I perceived among the youths, A young man devoid of understanding,(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:7 I saw some naive young men,and one in particular who lacked common sense. (NLT)

Main Idea

Among the simple youths he noticed “A young man devoid of understanding.” In the story, trouble starts with a young man drifting without wisdom, and this pictures how the path of sin commonly begins for anyone: with a heart not yet given to what is right.

Verse Explanation

Among the simple youths he noticed “A young man devoid of understanding.” In the story, trouble starts with a young man drifting without wisdom, and this pictures how the path of sin commonly begins for anyone: with a heart not yet given to what is right.

“And saw among the simple,”

The father sees a group of “the simple.” In Proverbs the simple are the uncommitted: young or inexperienced people who have not set themselves against wisdom but have not given themselves to it either. They are open, easily persuaded and easily led. The book was written in part “To give prudence to the simple” (1:4), and both Wisdom and Folly call to them by name in chapter 9.

“I perceived among the youths,”

“Youths” are young men. The father picks out one face in a crowd. Among his friends this young man probably seemed ordinary, and nothing about him stood out as wicked. That is part of the warning. The path into sin is often walked by people who look like everyone else.

“A young man devoid of understanding,”

The young man lacks understanding; he lacks the inner sense and conviction that would anchor him. He is not described as wicked or rebellious. He is empty, and an empty heart does not stay empty for long. The scene that follows is first a story about adultery, told as a warning to a son. It is also the book’s clearest picture of how anyone enters the path of sin, whose wages are death, and read at that level the path begins here, with an uncommitted heart not yet given to what is right. The father’s appeal in 7:1–4 was meant to fill exactly that emptiness.

Study Notes

In Hebrew “devoid of understanding” is literally “lacking heart,” where the heart is the seat of understanding and judgment. The same Hebrew expression is used of the adulterer in 6:32 and of those Wisdom and Folly both call in 9:4 and 9:16.

Proverbs 7:8
Proverbs 7:8
NKJV

Proverbs 7:8 Passing along the street near her corner; And he took the path to her house(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:8 He was crossing the street near the house of an immoral woman,strolling down the path by her house. (NLT)

Main Idea

The young man was passing along the street near her corner and took the path toward her house. His fall began with coming close, putting himself near temptation by choices that seemed harmless.

Verse Explanation

The young man was passing along the street near her corner and took the path toward her house. His fall began with coming close, putting himself near temptation by choices that seemed harmless.

“Passing along the street near her corner;”

The young man is not yet doing anything forbidden. He is walking along a street. But it is the street near her corner, and he has put himself where she is likely to be. Nothing in the text says she dragged him there. He walked there himself. In the pattern of the path of sin this is the second step: coming close, placing oneself near temptation by choices that seem harmless.

“And he took the path to her house”

Now he turns. “He took the path” means he stepped onto the road that leads to her door. In chapter 5 the father had commanded, “do not go near the door of her house” (5:8), and this young man does exactly what was forbidden. Each step is only a little different from the one before, but the direction is unmistakable. The woman will entice him, but he chose where to walk before she said a word, and he is responsible for his own feet.

NLT

Proverbs 7:9 It was at twilight, in the evening,as deep darkness fell. (NLT)

Main Idea

It was twilight, then evening, then the black darkness of night. The deepening dark matches the young man’s growing danger, and it shows how sin seeks the cover of darkness, where it hopes to go unseen.

Verse Explanation

It was twilight, then evening, then the black darkness of night. The deepening dark matches the young man’s growing danger, and it shows how sin seeks the cover of darkness, where it hopes to go unseen.

“In the twilight, in the evening,”

The time is twilight, then evening, and the light is fading. The young man chose this hour, or at least did not leave when it came.

“In the black and dark night.”

Then it is fully night, black and dark. The four phrases of the verse trace the light disappearing while he keeps walking, and the deepening darkness matches his growing danger. Sin looks for darkness because it hopes to go unseen; this is the third step of the pattern, the cover of darkness. Yet the father at the window has seen everything, and the LORD sees more, for “the ways of man are before the eyes of the LORD” (5:21). Darkness hides nothing from Him.

Study Notes

In Hebrew “black” in “the black and dark night” is the same word rendered “apple” in 7:2, a word for the pupil of the eye. Used of the night, it means the middle of the night, and the NASB translates it that way: ‘the middle of the night.’

Proverbs 7:10 And there a woman met him, With the attire of a harlot, and a crafty heart.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:10 The woman approached him,seductively dressed and sly of heart. (NLT)

Main Idea

There a woman met him, dressed like a harlot and with a crafty heart. Opportunity now meets the young man who placed himself in its way, and her outward dress hides an inward plan.

Verse Explanation

There a woman met him, dressed like a harlot and with a crafty heart. Opportunity now meets the young man who placed himself in its way, and her outward dress hides an inward plan.

“And there a woman met him,”

“There,” at the corner he walked to and the hour he chose, a woman meets him. The meeting is not an accident of fate. Opportunity is waiting at the place where he put himself. In the pattern this is the fourth step: opportunity meets the one who has placed himself in its way.

“With the attire of a harlot, and a crafty heart.”

Her clothing is the clothing of a prostitute, meant to attract and to signal that she is available. She is dressed like a prostitute, and 7:19 reveals that she is a married woman. Her outward dress says one thing, and her heart hides another. “A crafty heart” describes a heart that keeps its purposes hidden. She knows what she wants and has planned how to get it. The young man brings no wise resolve to meet her.

Study Notes

In Hebrew “crafty heart” is literally ‘guarded of heart.’ The word comes from the same Hebrew verb translated “Keep” in “Keep your heart with all diligence” (4:23). The son is to guard his heart for life; she guards hers to conceal her purpose.

Proverbs 7:11
Proverbs 7:11
NKJV

Proverbs 7:11 She was loud and rebellious, Her feet would not stay at home.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:11 She was the brash, rebellious type,never content to stay at home. (NLT)

Main Idea

She was loud and rebellious, and her feet would not stay at home. She is restless and defiant, a married woman who has abandoned the place and the commitments that belong to her.

Verse Explanation

She was loud and rebellious, and her feet would not stay at home. She is restless and defiant, a married woman who has abandoned the place and the commitments that belong to her.

“She was loud and rebellious,”

The father describes her character. “Loud” means noisy, restless and stirred up. “Rebellious” means defiant and stubborn, refusing restraint. She will not accept the boundaries that belong to her marriage. The same Hebrew word behind “loud” later describes Folly herself: “A foolish woman is clamorous” (9:13).

“Her feet would not stay at home.”

Her feet will not stay in her own house. She is restless, always out and always moving. The line does not make a rule about women leaving the house. It describes a married woman who has abandoned the place and the commitments that belong to her and whose restlessness takes her into the street in search of someone else. Her feet stand opposite the son’s feet, which the father wants kept on the right path.

Study Notes

The NASB renders the Hebrew word behind “loud” and “clamorous” as “boisterous” in both verses. Its use for both women is one of several details that link this woman with Folly’s portrait in 9:13–18.

Proverbs 7:12
NKJV

Proverbs 7:12 At times she was outside, at times in the open square, Lurking at every corner.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:12 She is often in the streets and markets,soliciting at every corner. (NLT)

Main Idea

At times she was in the street, at times in the open square, lurking at every corner. She is a hunter in search of prey, and the young man has walked straight into her territory.

Verse Explanation

At times she was in the street, at times in the open square, lurking at every corner. She is a hunter in search of prey, and the young man has walked straight into her territory.

“At times she was outside, at times in the open square,”

She moves through the public places of the city, the street outside and the open square where people gather. These are the places where Wisdom calls: “Wisdom calls aloud outside; She raises her voice in the open squares” (1:20). Two voices compete in the same streets, and the young man will choose which one to follow.

“Lurking at every corner.”

“Lurking” means lying in wait, the posture of a hunter waiting for prey. The young man passed “her corner” (7:8), but in fact she is at every corner. Temptation is not confined to one spot that is easy to avoid. The same Hebrew verb, meaning to lie in wait, described the violent gang of chapter 1, who said, “Let us lie in wait to shed blood” (1:11). Seduction and violence use the hunter’s same patience. The young man thinks he is out for an evening walk; he has walked into her hunting ground.

Proverbs 7:13 So she caught him and kissed him; With an impudent face she said to him:(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:13 She threw her arms around him and kissed him,and with a brazen look she said, (NLT)

Main Idea

She seized him and kissed him, and with a brazen face she began to speak. Her boldness overwhelms a young man who came without resolve, and enticement begins with touch and words together.

Verse Explanation

She seized him and kissed him, and with a brazen face she began to speak. Her boldness overwhelms a young man who came without resolve, and enticement begins with touch and words together.

“So she caught him and kissed him;”

She acts first and boldly. She seizes him and kisses him in public before she says a word. Touch comes before talk, and the young man who came without resolve is overwhelmed by her boldness. Here the fifth step of the pattern begins: enticement, working through the body and the imagination together.

“With an impudent face she said to him:”

“Impudent” means shameless and brazen. Her face shows no embarrassment; she has hardened herself against shame. A person who no longer blushes at sin has already gone a long way down the road. The colon at the end of the line introduces her speech, which runs through 7:20. The father lets his son hear exactly how temptation talks.

Study Notes

In Hebrew “with an impudent face” reads literally that she made her face strong, or firm; the KJV margin gives ‘she strengthened her face.’ To make the face strong was to put on a bold look that refuses to show shame, which is why the NASB renders it ‘a brazen face.’

Proverbs 7:14
Proverbs 7:14
NKJV

Proverbs 7:14 "I have peace offerings with me; Today I have paid my vows.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:14 “I’ve just made my peace offeringsand fulfilled my vows. (NLT)

Main Idea

She claims, “I have peace offerings with me; Today I have paid my vows.” Peace offerings provided a shared meal, so she offers him a feast and implies her account with God is settled, wrapping sin in religious language to excuse it.

Verse Explanation

She claims, “I have peace offerings with me; Today I have paid my vows.” Peace offerings provided a shared meal, so she offers him a feast and implies her account with God is settled, wrapping sin in religious language to excuse it.

“I have peace offerings with me;”

Her first words are about worship. Peace offerings were sacrifices in which part of the animal was returned to the worshiper to be eaten in a shared meal, and the Law required the meat to be eaten within a day or two, as Leviticus 7:15–16 directs. She is telling him that she has fresh meat at home, a feast ready to share. She is also wrapping her proposal in religious language.

“Today I have paid my vows.”

A vow was a promise made to God, often fulfilled by bringing an offering. She has paid her vows today. The implication is that her religious duties are done and her account with God is settled, so she is free. This is the sixth step of the pattern: excuses and justification, even religious ones. Religious words and activity can sit right beside serious sin and even be used to excuse it. Her words teach nothing about sacrifice. They are self-justification, using the outward forms of worship to quiet the conscience while the heart pursues adultery.

Main Idea

She tells him she came out especially to meet him, diligently seeking his face, and has found him. Flattery makes him feel chosen and wanted, as though the encounter were meant to be.

Verse Explanation

She tells him she came out especially to meet him, diligently seeking his face, and has found him. Flattery makes him feel chosen and wanted, as though the encounter were meant to be.

“So I came out to meet you,”

“So” links her worship to her pursuit: because the feast is ready, she came out looking for him. She tells him that he was the one she wanted.

“Diligently to seek your face,”

“Diligently to seek” describes eager, earnest searching. The language of seeking is the language Wisdom uses of those who love her: “those who seek me diligently will find me” (8:17). The seductress borrows the vocabulary of something good to draw him in.

“And I have found you.”

She makes him feel chosen and wanted, as though the meeting were meant to be. Flattery works because people long to be noticed and desired, and the young man, lacking heart, has no settled sense of worth that would let him see through it. In reality she was lurking at every corner (7:12). She would have said the same to anyone.

Study Notes

“Diligently to seek” here and “seek me diligently” in 8:17 translate the same Hebrew verb, so the seductress’s words echo Wisdom’s promise.

Proverbs 7:18
Proverbs 7:18
NKJV

Proverbs 7:18 Come, let us take our fill of love until morning; Let us delight ourselves with love.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:18 Come, let’s drink our fill of love until morning.Let’s enjoy each other’s caresses, (NLT)

Main Idea

Then she says, “Come, let us take our fill of love until morning,” and calls what she offers delight in love. Sin is given a pleasant name, as adultery is dressed up as love to hide what it really is.

Verse Explanation

Then she says, “Come, let us take our fill of love until morning,” and calls what she offers delight in love. Sin is given a pleasant name, as adultery is dressed up as love to hide what it really is.

“Come, let us take our fill of love until morning;”

Now she states the offer plainly. The Hebrew verb behind “take our fill” means to drink deeply, to be saturated, and it is the same verb used of a husband’s delight in his wife in 5:19, “Let her breasts satisfy you,” turned here to an adulterous night. “Until morning” promises a whole night, with no hurry and no fear.

“Let us delight ourselves with love.”

She calls it love, twice. She never says adultery, and she never uses a word that would name what she proposes truthfully. This is the eighth step of the pattern: euphemism, calling sin love. Giving sin a pleasant name does not change what it is. The father calls things by their right names, and at the end of the chapter he names her house “the way to hell” (7:27).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “love” in the first line is a plural form used in the Song of Songs for the delights of love between bride and bridegroom (for example Song of Songs 1:2, “your love is better than wine”). Here the same word dresses up an adulterous night.

Proverbs 7:19
Proverbs 7:19
NKJV

Proverbs 7:19 For my husband is not at home; He has gone on a long journey;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 7:19 for my husband is not home.He’s away on a long trip. (NLT)

Main Idea

Her husband, she says, is not at home but has gone far away on a long trip. She assures the young man that no one will know, removing the fear of being caught.

Verse Explanation

Her husband, she says, is not at home but has gone far away on a long trip. She assures the young man that no one will know, removing the fear of being caught.

“For my husband is not at home;”

Her reassurance shows that she is married. She does not argue that adultery is right. She argues that it is safe. This is the seventh step of the pattern: the assurance that no one will know. The question she raises is whether they will be caught, never whether it is right.

“He has gone on a long journey;”

Her husband is far away and will not walk in on them. She removes the fear of being caught, and the young man is given every reason to think there will be no consequences. But her husband is not the only one who sees, and the rest of the chapter shows that the true cost does not depend on whether anyone finds out.

Study Notes

In Hebrew “my husband” is literally “the man.” She speaks of her own husband without calling him hers, a detached way of referring to him; the KJV’s “the goodman” keeps that impersonal wording.

“He has taken a bag of money with him,”

“And will come home on the appointed day.”

He will come home on a set day, and she knows when it is. She speaks as though she controls the consequences: there is time, and nothing to fear. With her enticing speech she caused him to yield, and with her flattering lips she seduced him. Words carried the young man all the way, as the father warned they would, and his resistance gave way.

“With her enticing speech she caused him to yield,”

Her words do the work. “Enticing speech” is persuasive talk, a great deal of it, and it overcomes his resistance. “She caused him to yield” means she bent him, wearing him down until he gave way. The father had warned at the start that her danger lay in her words, and the warning comes true. The young man never says a word in the whole chapter. He only listens, and listening is how he falls.

“With her flattering lips she seduced him.”

“Flattering lips” are smooth lips, words that slide easily into the heart. “Seduced” means she drove him along, compelling him by persuasion. The two lines say the same thing twice for emphasis: words carried him all the way. Desire that her touch and her description of the bed had stirred now gives way entirely. James describes the same movement: “each one is tempted when he is drawn away by his own desires and enticed” (James 1:14).

Study Notes

The Hebrew noun behind “enticing speech” is the word that 1:5 renders “learning” and 4:2 “doctrine.” Here it is used for the persuasive force of her words; the ESV has “seductive speech” and the NASB “many persuasions.”

He went after her immediately, like an ox going to the slaughter or a fool to the correction of the stocks. The yielding seems sudden, but it is the end of many earlier steps, and he walks to destruction as unaware as an animal led to be killed.

“Immediately he went after her, as an ox goes to the slaughter,”

After a long, slow approach, the fall is sudden. This is the tenth step of the pattern, sudden yielding. The decision looks abrupt, but it completes what every earlier step began: the empty heart, the street near her corner, the darkness, the meeting, the kiss, the excuses and the promises. He follows her as an ox goes to the slaughter. An ox led to the butcher does not know where it is going; it walks along calmly, perhaps expecting food. The young man thinks he is going to a feast and a night of love. The father sees an animal walking to its death.

“Or as a fool to the correction of the stocks,”

The second picture is of a fool walking to punishment, to the restraint of the stocks or fetters, as though he were going somewhere pleasant. The Hebrew of this line is difficult and translations render it differently, but its sense in context is clear: the young man walks into his own punishment without understanding it. He thinks he is free, and he is going to be bound.

Study Notes

The NKJV and KJV read “as a fool to the correction of the stocks,” and the NASB ‘as one in fetters to the discipline of a fool.’ The ESV, the NLT and the 1984 NIV picture a stag or deer caught in a trap instead, reflecting a different understanding of the difficult Hebrew. The Hebrew word that the NKJV renders “stocks” refers to an ankle chain, which explains the NASB’s “fetters.” All the readings describe a creature walking unaware into restraint or death.

“Till an arrow struck his liver.”

The pictures continue. The liver is a vital organ, and an arrow there means a deadly wound. The image may continue the picture of the ox or describe a hunted animal; either way, the end comes suddenly and it is fatal. “Till” sets a limit on the pleasure. She promised love “until morning” (7:18); it lasted only until the arrow struck.

“As a bird hastens to the snare,”

A bird sees the bait and flies straight to it, unaware of the trap. It hurries, as though afraid of missing something. The young man rushes into his ruin with the same eager blindness.

“He did not know it would cost his life.”

He did not know the price. The father’s son, and every reader of this book, has been warned again and again in chapters 5, 6 and now 7. The young man in the story walks on without counting the cost, and not knowing does not keep a person from paying. Forgetting God’s word and the consequences of sin is part of the ninth step of the pattern, and here its cost comes due. The cost is “his life,” which in Proverbs means more than the end of the body; it is the ruin of the whole life that follows sin’s path. Paul names both the wage and the gift: “the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Romans 6:23). For anyone who has already walked this road, the blood of Christ cleanses: “If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness” (1 John 1:9).

“Do not let your heart turn aside to her ways,”

The way of escape begins where the young man’s ruin began, in the heart. To “turn aside” is to swerve, to veer off the road. The heart turns first, and the feet follow. The young man of 7:7 lacked heart, and his empty heart drifted. The father’s children are to guard their hearts so that desire does not begin to lean toward her ways. “Do not stray into her paths;”

To “stray” is to wander and drift off course. “Her paths” are plural; there is more than one road into her house. No one can control every temptation that comes his way, but everyone can choose which roads he walks. The father does not counsel his children to resist bravely once they are inside her door. He counsels them never to start down her road. Paul gives the same counsel, “Flee also youthful lusts” (2 Timothy 2:22), and he adds that God Himself provides the way out, for with the temptation He “will also make the way of escape” (1 Corinthians 10:13).

“Her house is the way to hell,”

The chapter ends at the destination. Her house, which the young man took for a place of feasting and pleasure, is “the way to hell,” where “hell” translates the Hebrew word Sheol, the realm of the dead. Her house stands opposite the house Wisdom builds: “Wisdom has built her house” (9:1). The book sets two women and two houses before every reader, and each house leads to an end, life or death. In chapter 9 Folly repeats the same lie with her own call to the simple, and of her guests the book says, “he does not know that the dead are there” (9:18).

“Descending to the chambers of death.”

“Chambers” are inner rooms, like the bedroom she prepared with linen and perfume. Her inner rooms are in truth the chambers of death, and her house is a stairway that leads only downward. The verse is first a literal warning about adultery and the ruin it brings. Read as the pattern of the path of sin, it also shows where every such path ends. The New Testament carries the image into a person’s relationship with God, for James calls those who befriend the world “Adulterers and adulteresses” (James 4:4). The father has told the story so that his children will never enter that house, and the gospel stands ready for any who already have.
