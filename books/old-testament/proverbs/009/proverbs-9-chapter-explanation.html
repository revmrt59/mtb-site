Proverbs 9

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 9:1
Proverbs 9:1
NKJV

Proverbs 9:1 Wisdom has built her house, She has hewn out her seven pillars;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:1 Wisdom has built her house;she has carved its seven columns. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wisdom, pictured as a noble woman, has finished building a large house with seven pillars cut and set in place, a home made ready to receive guests. At a deeper, spiritual level, Wisdom is Christ and her house is the Church He builds, and the seven pillars may illustrate the fullness of the Spirit resting on Him (Isaiah 11:2).

Verse Explanation

Wisdom, pictured as a noble woman, has finished building a large house with seven pillars cut and set in place, a home made ready to receive guests. At a deeper, spiritual level, Wisdom is Christ and her house is the Church He builds, and the seven pillars may illustrate the fullness of the Spirit resting on Him (Isaiah 11:2).

“Wisdom has built her house,”

The chapter opens with Wisdom’s work already finished. The house stands before a single guest has been called. Wisdom is pictured as a noble woman, the same figure who cried out in the streets (1:20–21) and took her stand at the city gates (8:1–3). In chapter 8 she spoke at the doorways; now the reader sees what lies behind her door. This matters because the prologue has warned again and again about another house, the immoral woman’s, which “leads down to death” (2:18). After all those warnings, the son is finally shown a house he may safely enter, a place to sit down, eat, and stay.

“She has hewn out her seven pillars;”

To hew is to cut stone. Wisdom has not pitched a tent; she has quarried and set pillars that will stand. Pillars held up the roof of a large hall or the covered sides of a courtyard, so a house with seven of them is a spacious home, ready to receive many guests. Seven often signals completeness in Scripture, and here it suggests a house fully finished and lacking nothing. The verse does not explain the number, and the plain picture is enough: Wisdom’s house is solid, generous, and ready.

At a deeper, spiritual level, Wisdom personified is Christ, whom the New Testament calls “the wisdom of God” (1 Corinthians 1:24), and her house is the Church He builds. Jesus said, “I will build My church” (Matthew 16:18). Within that layered reading, the seven pillars may illustrate the fullness of the Spirit resting on Him, “The Spirit of wisdom and understanding, The Spirit of counsel and might” (Isaiah 11:2). The number pictures that fullness; the verse does not give each pillar a separate meaning.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “Wisdom” here is a plural form, the same form used in 1:20, even though the verbs that follow, “has built” and “has hewn,” are singular, and so is the word “her.” The plural does not mean many wisdoms. Brown-Driver-Briggs lists this form as an emphatic plural, a way of stressing wisdom in her fullness, so the grammar presents Wisdom as one person who possesses wisdom in full measure.

Proverbs 9:2
Proverbs 9:2
NKJV

Proverbs 9:2 She has slaughtered her meat, She has mixed her wine, She has also furnished her table.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:2 She has prepared a great banquet,mixed the wines, and set the table. (NLT)

Main Idea

The feast is fully prepared: the meat slaughtered, the wine mixed, and the table set, so that the guest brings nothing but an appetite. The rich meal pictures the full and satisfying life Wisdom gives, and at the layered level the slaughtered meat and mixed wine point to Christ’s sacrifice and the Lord’s Supper.

Verse Explanation

The feast is fully prepared: the meat slaughtered, the wine mixed, and the table set, so that the guest brings nothing but an appetite. The rich meal pictures the full and satisfying life Wisdom gives, and at the layered level the slaughtered meat and mixed wine point to Christ’s sacrifice and the Lord’s Supper.

“She has slaughtered her meat,”

Meat was not everyday food in ancient Israel. Slaughtering an animal meant a feast and honored guests. Wisdom is not setting out leftovers; she has prepared a costly meal, and the Hebrew stresses the point by pairing a verb with a noun formed from it: she has slaughtered her slaughter.

“She has mixed her wine,”

Wine was often mixed before serving, whether with water or with spices. In the setting of a feast, the point is careful preparation by the hostess herself. The same detail returns when she speaks to her guests of “the wine I have mixed” (9:5).

“She has also furnished her table.”

The table is set and nothing remains to be done. Count the completed actions so far: built, hewn, slaughtered, mixed, furnished. Five finished works come before one word is spoken to a guest. Everything also belongs to Wisdom: her house, her pillars, her meat, her wine, her table. The guest brings nothing but hunger. This order matters for the whole chapter, because Folly, as 9:13–18 will show, prepares nothing at all.

At the layered level the slaughtered meat and mixed wine point to Christ’s sacrifice and to the Lord’s Supper, where He gave the bread and the cup to His disciples (1 Corinthians 11:24–25). In Jesus’ parable of the wedding feast, the king’s message comes in the same order as Wisdom’s work: “my oxen and fatted cattle are killed, and all things are ready” (Matthew 22:4).

Study Notes

The 1611 King James Version translates the object of the verb as “her beastes,” but its marginal note gives the Hebrew as “her killing.” The note tells English readers that the Hebrew object of the verb is not a word for animals but a noun meaning slaughter, so the line repeats the idea of slaughter in both its verb and its object.

The NLT renders the line “She has prepared a great banquet,” which gives the sense of the slaughtered meat without keeping the image itself.

Proverbs 9:3
Proverbs 9:3
NKJV

Proverbs 9:3 She has sent out her maidens, She cries out from the highest places of the city,(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:3 She has sent her servants to invite everyone to come.She calls out from the heights overlooking the city. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wisdom does not wait for guests to find her house. She sends her maidens out to call the guests and cries out herself from the city’s highest places, where everyone can hear; read spiritually, the maidens picture Christ’s messengers carrying His call to the feast (Matthew 22:9).

Verse Explanation

Wisdom does not wait for guests to find her house. She sends her maidens out to call the guests and cries out herself from the city’s highest places, where everyone can hear; read spiritually, the maidens picture Christ’s messengers carrying His call to the feast (Matthew 22:9).

“She has sent out her maidens,”

Like a great lady of a household, Wisdom has young women who serve her, and she sends them into the town. In the ancient world a host sent servants to tell invited guests that the meal was ready (compare Luke 14:17). The detail shows Wisdom’s initiative. She does not wait for people to stumble onto her house; she sends for them.

“She cries out from the highest places of the city,”

Wisdom does not leave the calling to her maidens. She raises her own voice from the heights of the city, where everyone can hear, just as she earlier took “her stand on the top of the high hill” (8:2). Her call is public and open. That matters because Folly will take a seat “by the highest places of the city” (9:14) as well, so the two rival calls go out over the same public space. The difference between them is not location but what each house offers, and Wisdom has nothing to hide.

Read spiritually, the maidens picture Christ’s messengers carrying His call to the feast. In the parable of the wedding, the king tells his servants, “go into the highways, and as many as you find, invite to the wedding” (Matthew 22:9). This spiritual sense follows naturally from the plain picture of servants sent out to gather guests.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “city” in this verse is not the common word for a city but a rarer poetic one. It occurs only five times in the Old Testament, and three of them come in these scenes: at 8:3, where Wisdom cries out “at the entry of the city”; here, where she calls from the city’s heights; and at 9:14, where Folly sits “by the highest places of the city.” Verses 3 and 14 also share the same Hebrew wording for “the highest places.” Because the two invitations are described in the same uncommon words, the reader hears both calls going out from the same heights over the same town.

Proverbs 9:4
Proverbs 9:4
NKJV

Proverbs 9:4 "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!" As for him who lacks understanding, she says to him,(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:4 “Come in with me,” she urges the simple.To those who lack good judgment, she says, (NLT)

Main Idea

Her call goes first to the simple, the uncommitted who are easily led, and to the one who lacks understanding: “Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!” Wisdom seeks out exactly the people who most need her and asks only that they turn aside and come in.

Verse Explanation

Her call goes first to the simple, the uncommitted who are easily led, and to the one who lacks understanding: “Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!” Wisdom seeks out exactly the people who most need her and asks only that they turn aside and come in.

“Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!”

The simple are the uncommitted. They are not set against wisdom, but they have not chosen it either, so they are easily led: “The simple believes every word” (14:15). Wisdom’s first call goes straight to them, the very people who most need her. To “turn in” is the language of hospitality, turning aside from the road and coming through the door. Wisdom does not require the simple to become wise before they come; she asks them to come so that they can become wise.

“As for him who lacks understanding, she says to him,”

In Hebrew the second description reads literally one who lacks heart. In Proverbs the heart is the inner person, the place of thought, judgment, and will. The same phrase described the young man of 7:7, “A young man devoid of understanding,” who walked into the immoral woman’s house. Wisdom calls exactly that kind of person, the one most at risk, before Folly can reach him. The line also introduces the words she speaks to him in 9:5–6.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase meaning “lacking heart” is rendered “devoid of understanding” at 7:7 and “lacks understanding” here and at 9:16. The NKJV’s varied English hides the fact that all three verses use the same Hebrew words.

Wisdom’s call here picks up her earlier address in 8:5, “O you simple ones, understand prudence.” Folly will repeat the words of this verse almost exactly at 9:16, so that the two calls sound alike by design.

Proverbs 9:5
Proverbs 9:5
NKJV

Proverbs 9:5 "Come, eat of my bread And drink of the wine I have mixed.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:5 “Come, eat my food,and drink the wine I have mixed. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wisdom speaks to her guests directly, urging them to come and eat her bread and drink the wine she has mixed herself. To eat at her table is to take her instruction in until it becomes part of a person, and at the spiritual level this table points to Christ, the bread of life, who invites the hungry to Himself (John 6:35).

Verse Explanation

Wisdom speaks to her guests directly, urging them to come and eat her bread and drink the wine she has mixed herself. To eat at her table is to take her instruction in until it becomes part of a person, and at the spiritual level this table points to Christ, the bread of life, who invites the hungry to Himself (John 6:35).

“Come, eat of my bread”

Wisdom now speaks to her guests directly. “Come” calls them to draw near, and “eat of my bread” offers them food from her own table. In Proverbs, eating and drinking can picture taking in instruction until it becomes part of a person. The words of the wise are meant to be received the way food is received: “My son, eat honey because it is good,” says 24:13, and the next line adds, “So shall the knowledge of wisdom be to your soul” (24:14). Wisdom’s bread is her teaching, offered for the guest’s life and strength.

“And drink of the wine I have mixed.”

She repeats what she prepared in 9:2, now with the personal touch, “the wine I have mixed.” The guest is not asked to buy anything or bring anything. Wisdom gives her best freely to people who lack understanding.

At the spiritual level, this table points to Christ, the bread of life, who invites the hungry to Himself. In John 6:35 Jesus says, “I am the bread of life. He who comes to Me shall never hunger.” The plain sense remains Wisdom feeding the simple with her instruction; the deeper, spiritual sense shows where that table finally leads.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “bread” is often used for food in general, and the NLT reflects that wider sense with “Come, eat my food.”

Proverbs 9:6
Proverbs 9:6
NKJV

Proverbs 9:6 Forsake foolishness and live, And go in the way of understanding.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:6 Leave your simple ways behind, and begin to live;learn to use good judgment.” (NLT)

Main Idea

Her call carries both a demand and a promise: “Forsake foolishness and live.” Coming to Wisdom’s table means leaving the old ways of folly behind and walking a new road, “the way of understanding,” and the result is life itself.

Verse Explanation

Her call carries both a demand and a promise: “Forsake foolishness and live.” Coming to Wisdom’s table means leaving the old ways of folly behind and walking a new road, “the way of understanding,” and the result is life itself.

“Forsake foolishness and live,”

Wisdom’s call has two sides. “Forsake” means to leave behind, and “live” is itself a command as well as a promise. No one can sit at Wisdom’s table and keep the habits of the simple at the same time. The word “foolishness” here is closely tied to the word “simple” in 9:4: the simple are being told to leave their simpleness, the aimless drift that “will slay them” (1:32). “Live” answers Wisdom’s great promise from the previous chapter, “whoever finds me finds life” (8:35), and it prepares the reader for the end of this chapter, where Folly’s guests are found among “the dead” (9:18).

“And go in the way of understanding.”

Proverbs pictures life as a walk on one of two paths: “the path of the just” that grows brighter and “the way of the wicked” that is like darkness (4:18–19). Wisdom’s guests do not just enjoy a meal and go home the way they came. They leave her house on a different road, “the way of understanding.” Coming to her table changes the direction of a life.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “foolishness” is the plural of the word translated “simple” in 9:4, and its plain sense is “simple ones.” The KJV takes it that way, “the foolish,” meaning leave the company of the simple, and Brown-Driver-Briggs also lists this verse among its references to simple persons. The NKJV and most modern versions translate it as the condition itself; the NLT reads “Leave your simple ways behind.” Either way, the word ties the command to the invitation of 9:4: the people Wisdom calls simple are told to leave simpleness behind.

The Hebrew verb translated “go” means to walk straight ahead or keep advancing; Brown-Driver-Briggs explains this verse as a command to go straight on in the way of understanding. In other forms the same Hebrew root means to call someone happy or blessed, and Young’s Literal Translation reads “be happy” here, drawing on that second sense. Most translations take it as walking, which fits the picture of a road.

Proverbs 9:7–9 — Correcting the Scoffer, Teaching the Wise

Proverbs 9:7
Proverbs 9:7
NKJV

Proverbs 9:7 "He who corrects a scoffer gets shame for himself, And he who rebukes a wicked man only harms himself.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:7 Anyone who rebukes a mocker will get an insult in return.Anyone who corrects the wicked will get hurt. (NLT)

Main Idea

Still speaking, Wisdom turns to those who will not come. Anyone who tries to correct a scoffer, a person who mocks instruction, gets shame for his trouble, and rebuking a wicked man only brings harm back on the one who rebukes.

Verse Explanation

Still speaking, Wisdom turns to those who will not come. Anyone who tries to correct a scoffer, a person who mocks instruction, gets shame for his trouble, and rebuking a wicked man only brings harm back on the one who rebukes.

“He who corrects a scoffer gets shame for himself,”

Wisdom is still speaking, but she turns from the guests who will come to the people who will not. The scoffer is the mocker who treats instruction with contempt. He has already appeared among those Wisdom rebuked in 1:22, and Proverbs says plainly that “a scoffer does not listen to rebuke” (13:1). His trouble is not a lack of information but a settled refusal; he will not be taught. So the person who tries to correct him receives shame for his effort. The scoffer answers correction with ridicule and insult, and the one who spoke ends up dishonored.

“And he who rebukes a wicked man only harms himself.”

The second line restates the first with a parallel figure. The “wicked man” stands beside the scoffer, and the result is harm rather than shame. The rebuker comes away with a stain or injury, whether to his name or his person. These sayings explain why Wisdom’s call does not win everyone. The problem does not lie in her message or her table; it lies in the hearer who has hardened himself against correction.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “harms himself” means a blemish or defect, whether physical or moral. The line reads literally “his blemish,” as Young’s Literal Translation renders it, and the KJV has “a blot.” Brown-Driver-Briggs places this verse among the word’s figurative uses for the disgrace that comes from being rebuffed, which matches the “shame” of the first line.

Verses 7–12 read like a small collection of sayings set between the two banquets. The NKJV punctuation keeps them inside Wisdom’s speech and closes her quotation at the end of 9:12. Wisdom also speaks of herself in the first person in 9:11, “For by me your days will be multiplied.”

Proverbs 9:8
Proverbs 9:8
NKJV

Proverbs 9:8 Do not correct a scoffer, lest he hate you; Rebuke a wise man, and he will love you.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:8 So don’t bother correcting mockers;they will only hate you.But correct the wise,and they will love you. (NLT)

Main Idea

The warning becomes counsel: do not waste correction on a scoffer, who will only hate you for it, but rebuke a wise man and he will love you for it. The difference lies in the hearer’s will, so the verse calls for discernment about who will receive correction rather than giving an excuse never to speak the truth.

Verse Explanation

The warning becomes counsel: do not waste correction on a scoffer, who will only hate you for it, but rebuke a wise man and he will love you for it. The difference lies in the hearer’s will, so the verse calls for discernment about who will receive correction rather than giving an excuse never to speak the truth.

“Do not correct a scoffer, lest he hate you;”

The observation of 9:7 now becomes counsel. Correcting a scoffer does not produce repentance; it produces hatred. “A scoffer does not love one who corrects him” (15:12), because he takes every correction as an attack. The verse does not forbid ever speaking the truth. It calls for discernment about who is ready to hear it. Where a rebuke will only harden a mocker and turn him against the speaker, the words are wasted.

“Rebuke a wise man, and he will love you.”

The same action meets the opposite response. A wise man receives rebuke as a gift and loves the one who gives it, because he values the truth more than his pride. Proverbs says elsewhere, “Faithful are the wounds of a friend” (27:6). The contrast between hatred and love shows that the decisive difference lies in the person being corrected, not in the correction. Part of the skill of wisdom is knowing when to speak and to whom.

Study Notes

In Hebrew both lines of this verse use the same verb, which the NKJV renders first “correct” and then “rebuke.” The identical action draws opposite responses, which sharpens the contrast between the scoffer and the wise.

Proverbs 9:9
Proverbs 9:9
NKJV

Proverbs 9:9 Give instruction to a wise man, and he will be still wiser; Teach a just man, and he will increase in learning.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:9 Instruct the wise,and they will be even wiser.Teach the righteous,and they will learn even more. (NLT)

Main Idea

Instruction given to the wise is never wasted, because he grows wiser still, and the just man who is taught keeps increasing in learning. Teachability is itself a mark of wisdom, and the one who already has some will gain more (1:5), the very opposite of the scoffer.

Verse Explanation

Instruction given to the wise is never wasted, because he grows wiser still, and the just man who is taught keeps increasing in learning. Teachability is itself a mark of wisdom, and the one who already has some will gain more (1:5), the very opposite of the scoffer.

“Give instruction to a wise man, and he will be still wiser;”

The wise man is not finished learning; he is the one most ready to learn more. Whatever is given to him, he takes in and grows by it. This is the opposite of the scoffer, who closes himself to correction. Wisdom never becomes a possession that makes a person proud and unteachable. It makes him hungrier. The opening of the book said the same thing: “A wise man will hear and increase learning” (1:5).

“Teach a just man, and he will increase in learning.”

The parallel line pairs the wise man with the “just man,” the righteous person. In Proverbs wisdom is never only a matter of intelligence; it belongs to people who live rightly before God. The righteous person is teachable, and his learning keeps increasing. The two lines together describe the kind of guest Wisdom’s table is for, one who keeps receiving and keeps growing.

Study Notes

The first line in Hebrew says only, Give to a wise man. The word “instruction” is not in the Hebrew text; translators supply it to complete the sense. The second line shows what kind of gift is meant, because its verb means to make known, which the NKJV translates “Teach.”

The Hebrew words behind “increase in learning” are the same pair used in 1:5, where the NKJV has “increase learning”; the verb means to add. That same Hebrew verb returns two verses later, where years of life “will be added” (9:11), so the wise man who keeps adding learning is also the one promised added years.

Proverbs 9:10–12 — The Fear of the LORD Is the Beginning of Wisdom

Proverbs 9:10
Proverbs 9:10
NKJV

Proverbs 9:10 "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom, And the knowledge of the Holy One is understanding.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:10 Fear of the LORD is the foundation of wisdom.Knowledge of the Holy One results in good judgment. (NLT)

Main Idea

The book’s motto from 1:7 returns at the close of the prologue, now as “the beginning of wisdom.” A right view of God is where all wisdom starts, and the second line joins that fear to knowing “the Holy One,” the God who is unlike any other.

Verse Explanation

The book’s motto from 1:7 returns at the close of the prologue, now as “the beginning of wisdom.” A right view of God is where all wisdom starts, and the second line joins that fear to knowing “the Holy One,” the God who is unlike any other.

“The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom,”

Near the close of the prologue, the book’s motto returns. Proverbs 1:7 called the fear of the LORD “the beginning of knowledge”; here it is “the beginning of wisdom.” The two statements frame chapters 1–9 like bookends. The fear of the LORD is a right view of God. It includes awe at His glory and holiness, and it “is to hate evil” (8:13). It is the beginning in the sense of the starting point and foundation; no one skips past it and still arrives at wisdom. Its place here is significant. Standing between Wisdom’s table and Folly’s, it is what enables a person to tell the two feasts apart.

“And the knowledge of the Holy One is understanding.”

The second line matches the first. “The knowledge of the Holy One” stands parallel to “the fear of the LORD,” and “understanding” stands parallel to “wisdom.” To know God is the substance of understanding. This knowledge is more than information about Him; it is acquaintance with the God who is holy, set apart and unlike any other. The title “the Holy One” keeps that holiness in view. True wisdom therefore begins with God Himself and keeps returning to Him. It is never only a technique for living well.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “the Holy One” is plural in form, here and at 30:3. Brown-Driver-Briggs lists both verses under the divine title and calls the form an intensive plural: it refers to the one God and stresses His holiness rather than counting holy beings. Joshua 24:19 offers a parallel, for in “He is a holy God” both the Hebrew word for God and the word for holy are plural in form. The KJV renders the phrase “the holy,” and Young’s Literal Translation reads Holy Ones.

Two different Hebrew words stand behind “beginning” in 1:7 and in this verse. The word in 1:7 means the first part or start of something, and elsewhere it can also mean the chief or choicest part. The word here is a different noun that Brown-Driver-Briggs glosses as beginning or first principle. Both verses therefore present the fear of the LORD as the starting point of wisdom and knowledge; the change of word varies the expression rather than the idea. The first line of this verse matches Psalm 111:10 word for word in the NKJV, though the Psalm uses the same Hebrew word for “beginning” as Proverbs 1:7.

Proverbs 9:11
Proverbs 9:11
NKJV

Proverbs 9:11 For by me your days will be multiplied, And years of life will be added to you.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:11 Wisdom will multiply your daysand add years to your life. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wisdom speaks in the first person: “For by me your days will be multiplied.” Life lived her way tends toward length of days and added years, which is the general blessing of the wise way rather than a guarantee that every wise person will live long.

Verse Explanation

Wisdom speaks in the first person: “For by me your days will be multiplied.” Life lived her way tends toward length of days and added years, which is the general blessing of the wise way rather than a guarantee that every wise person will live long.

“For by me your days will be multiplied,”

Wisdom speaks in the first person, “by me,” which shows that she is still the speaker. The word “For” ties this promise to the motto just stated. Because wisdom begins with a right view of God, it leads toward life. The wise way tends to multiply a person’s days. It avoids the violence, vice, and reckless choices that cut lives short, and it walks in the fear of the LORD, which “prolongs days” (10:27).

“And years of life will be added to you.”

The second line repeats the promise with the language of adding, as the father said earlier: “the years of your life will be many” (4:10). This is the general blessing of the wise way, set against the house of Folly, whose guests are already among the dead (9:18). It is not a guarantee that every wise person will live long. Proverbs describes how God’s world generally works. Godly people sometimes die young, and Jesus Himself, who had all wisdom, did not live a long life. God’s purposes stand above any general rule, and the verse should never be used to explain an early death as a lack of wisdom.

Study Notes

The second line uses the same Hebrew words as 3:2, where the father promises, “For length of days and long life And peace they will add to you.” The NKJV’s “long life” there and “years of life” here translate the same Hebrew phrase, and both verses use the same verb for adding, so Wisdom repeats in her own voice the promise the father gave at the start of the prologue.

Proverbs 9:12
Proverbs 9:12
NKJV

Proverbs 9:12 If you are wise, you are wise for yourself, And if you scoff, you will bear it alone."(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:12 If you become wise, you will be the one to benefit.If you scorn wisdom, you will be the one to suffer. (NLT)

Main Idea

Her speech closes by making the choice personal. The wise man’s wisdom profits him, and the scoffer will carry the cost of his scorn alone, for no one can be wise on another’s behalf or shift the weight of his own folly onto someone else.

Verse Explanation

Her speech closes by making the choice personal. The wise man’s wisdom profits him, and the scoffer will carry the cost of his scorn alone, for no one can be wise on another’s behalf or shift the weight of his own folly onto someone else.

“If you are wise, you are wise for yourself,”

Wisdom closes her speech by making the choice personal. The person who becomes wise receives the profit of his own wisdom. This is not selfishness; it is ownership. Wisdom cannot be inherited or borrowed. Parents can teach and Wisdom can call, but no one can be wise on another person’s behalf. Each guest must turn in for himself.

“And if you scoff, you will bear it alone.”

The scoffer carries the cost of his scorn himself. The verb bear means to carry a load. He mocked the one who tried to correct him (9:7–8), but he cannot shift the consequences of his choice back onto that person or onto anyone else. Folly does hurt others, as Proverbs says elsewhere (10:1), so the point is not that a scoffer’s life touches no one. The point is that the outcome of scoffing lands on the scoffer and cannot be handed off. With this, Wisdom’s speech ends and the chapter turns to her rival.

Study Notes

The Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, contains lines after 9:12 and after 9:18 that are not in the Hebrew text. In Brenton’s English translation of the Septuagint (1851), the three lines added after verse 12 describe a man who leans on falsehoods, tries to rule the winds, and gathers barrenness in a dry land. The four lines added after verse 18 urge the reader to hurry away from Folly’s place and not to drink from a stranger’s fountain. The NKJV and the other English versions compared in this study follow the Hebrew and do not include these lines.

Proverbs 9:13–18 — Folly’s Counterfeit Invitation

Proverbs 9:13
Proverbs 9:13
NKJV

Proverbs 9:13 A foolish womanis clamorous; She is simple, and knows nothing.(NKJV)

Proverbs 9:13 The woman named Folly is brash.She is ignorant and doesn’t know it. (NLT)

Main Idea

The scene turns to Wisdom’s rival. Folly, also pictured as a woman, is loud and restless, and although she calls to the simple, she is simple herself and “knows nothing.”

Verse Explanation

The scene turns to Wisdom’s rival. Folly, also pictured as a woman, is loud and restless, and although she calls to the simple, she is simple herself and “knows nothing.”

“A foolish woman is clamorous;”

The scene turns to Wisdom’s rival. The Hebrew calls her a woman of folly, so she is Folly personified, set beside Wisdom as a second hostess. She is “clamorous,” loud and restless. The word links her with the seductress of chapter 7, who “was loud and rebellious” (7:11). Wisdom also raised her voice, but with a message; Folly makes noise. The chapter wants the reader to recognize her as the same kind of figure the father warned about, now speaking in the language of a banquet.

“She is simple, and knows nothing.”

Here is the irony of her call. Folly invites the simple, yet she is simple herself. She has nothing to teach, no understanding to give, and no knowledge of where her house leads. Her ignorance prepares for the last verse of the chapter, where her guest “does not know” what waits inside (9:18). A teacher who knows nothing can only lead her pupils into what she does not understand.

Study Notes

The Hebrew behind “foolish woman” reads literally woman of folly. The second word is an abstract noun meaning folly or stupidity, and it occurs only here in the Old Testament. Because the woman is named by the quality itself rather than simply described as foolish, the NLT can render the phrase “The woman named Folly.”

The Hebrew word behind “clamorous” is the same word rendered “loud” in 7:11, so the link between Folly and the seductress of chapter 7 rests on the wording itself.

The ESV reads “seductive” where the NKJV has “simple.” The Hebrew noun occurs only here. Strong’s relates it to the word for “simple” and defines it as silliness, that is, being easily enticed, while Brown-Driver-Briggs records a scholarly proposal by C. H. Toy to read a slightly different form meaning one who entices. The ESV’s rendering leans toward the enticing sense; the NKJV, KJV, and NASB keep the sense of simpleness, the NASB with “naive.”

Folly sits. Wisdom built, hewed, slaughtered, mixed, furnished, sent, and cried out (9:1–3). Folly’s only described action is sitting at her door. “On a seat by the highest places of the city,”

She does not hide in a back alley. Folly takes a prominent seat by “the highest places of the city,” the very place from which Wisdom called (9:3). Her strategy is imitation. She sets herself up in public, where Wisdom speaks, so that her voice can compete with Wisdom’s for the same listeners. The Hebrew word for “seat” is the ordinary word for a throne, which may suggest that she claims a place of honor that she has not earned.

Study Notes

Brown-Driver-Briggs defines the Hebrew word for “seat” as a seat of honor or throne, and elsewhere in the Old Testament it is usually translated “throne.” Young’s Literal Translation renders it “throne” here.

“To call to those who pass by,”

Folly’s audience is passersby, people walking past her door. She does not go out after them as Wisdom’s maidens did; she calls out from where she sits and waits for them to turn aside. Her call depends on catching people in passing, when they have not thought carefully about where they are going.

“Who go straight on their way:”

These people are not wandering. They are going straight, keeping to their path and minding their own business. That is the target. Folly wants to pull people off a straight road they are already walking. Temptation does not come only to those who are already lost; it comes to people who are headed the right way. For the simple, who have not settled their commitment, a voice from the high places of the city can be enough to turn them aside.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase behind “go straight on their way” is literally about people who make their paths straight, as the NASB reflects when it speaks of people making their paths straight. It uses the same Hebrew verb and noun as 3:6, “He shall direct your paths.” The passersby Folly targets are already walking the kind of straight path that 3:6 describes.

The Hebrew verb behind “call” in this verse is the same verb used for Wisdom’s crying out in 9:3. It also lies behind “her guests” in 9:18, which is literally her called ones, the people who answered the call Folly gives here.

“And as for him who lacks understanding, she says to him,”

Folly speaks to the same person Wisdom addressed, the one who lacks heart, sense, and judgment. Wisdom called him in so that he would gain understanding and leave his simpleness behind (9:6). Proverbs 9:17
NKJV

Proverbs 9:17 "Stolen water is sweet, And bread eaten in secret is pleasant."(NKJV)

Proverbs 9:17 “Stolen water is refreshing;food eaten in secret tastes the best!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Folly has no feast she prepared, only pleasure taken: “Stolen water is sweet, And bread eaten in secret is pleasant.” She sells forbidden pleasure as sweeter because it is stolen and hidden, the same lie the seductress told in chapter 7, and at the layered level her table pictures the false pleasures of sin, the world, and idolatry.

Verse Explanation

Folly has no feast she prepared, only pleasure taken: “Stolen water is sweet, And bread eaten in secret is pleasant.” She sells forbidden pleasure as sweeter because it is stolen and hidden, the same lie the seductress told in chapter 7, and at the layered level her table pictures the false pleasures of sin, the world, and idolatry.

“Stolen water is sweet,”

Folly’s offer sounds like a proverb, and that is part of its danger. In Proverbs, water from one’s own well pictures the joys of married love: “Drink water from your own cistern” (5:15). Stolen water is pleasure taken from what belongs to someone else. In the setting of chapters 5–7 that means adultery first of all, though the principle reaches to any forbidden pleasure. Folly claims that the stealing itself makes it sweeter. Its appeal lies in its being forbidden.

“And bread eaten in secret is pleasant.”

Secrecy adds to the appeal. This is the same lie the seductress told: “my husband is not at home” (7:19), so no one will know. The contrast with Wisdom is complete. Wisdom prepared her own bread at her own cost and offered it from the high places for all to see (9:2–5). Folly has prepared nothing. Her bread is taken, not given, and it must be eaten where no one is looking. At the layered level, her table pictures the false pleasures of sin, the world, and idolatry, the counterfeit set against Wisdom’s house. The literal warning about sexual sin remains fully in place.

Study Notes

The word “eaten” is supplied in translation. The Hebrew reads literally bread of secrecies, and the 1611 King James Version points this out in its margin, giving the Hebrew as “of secrecies.”

A later saying uses the same picture and shows its outcome: “Bread gained by deceit is sweet to a man, But afterward his mouth will be filled with gravel” (20:17).

Proverbs 9:18
Proverbs 9:18
NKJV

Proverbs 9:18 But he does not know that the dead are there, That her guests are in the depths of hell.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 9:18 But little do they know that the dead are there.Her guests are in the depths of the grave. (NLT)

Main Idea

What the guest does not know is the truth about her house: “the dead are there,” and her guests are “in the depths of hell.” Folly’s house looks like a place of pleasure, but it ends in death, and the prologue closes with that choice set plainly before every reader.

Verse Explanation

What the guest does not know is the truth about her house: “the dead are there,” and her guests are “in the depths of hell.” Folly’s house looks like a place of pleasure, but it ends in death, and the prologue closes with that choice set plainly before every reader.

“But he does not know that the dead are there,”

The chapter turns from what Folly says to what she hides. The guest who turns in “does not know.” He thinks he has found a place of pleasure, but “the dead are there.” The Hebrew word translated “the dead” refers to the shades of the departed, the same Hebrew word used when the father warned that the immoral woman’s “paths” lead “to the dead” (2:18). The simple man walks in expecting a meal, and he is walking into the company of the dead. Folly, who “knows nothing” (9:13), has led him into what neither of them understood.

“That her guests are in the depths of hell.”

In Hebrew “Her guests” is literally those she has called, so the word looks back to her calling in 9:15. They are “in the depths of hell,” the lowest part of Sheol, the realm of the dead. Sheol is the Hebrew word behind the NKJV’s “hell,” and that English word reflects how seriously the verse treats Folly’s end. The prologue ends here, with the same verdict given on the seductress’s house: “Her house is the way to hell, Descending to the chambers of death” (7:27). Wisdom’s guests were told to “live” (9:6); Folly’s guests are found among the dead. With that choice set plainly before the reader, the book turns to the proverbs of Solomon (10:1).

Study Notes

Brown-Driver-Briggs defines the Hebrew word translated “the dead” as shades or ghosts, a name for the dead in Sheol, the Hebrew term for the realm of the dead. Besides 2:18, Proverbs uses the same word at 21:16, where the man who wanders from understanding “Will rest in the assembly of the dead.”

The NKJV and KJV read “hell” for the Hebrew Sheol; the ESV and NASB keep “Sheol,” and the NLT has “the grave.” Sheol is the Old Testament term for the realm of the dead, and this verse does not describe that realm beyond placing Folly’s guests in its depths.
