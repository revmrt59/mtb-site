Proverbs 10

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 10:1
Proverbs 10:1
NKJV

Proverbs 10:1 The proverbs of Solomon: A wise son makes a glad father, But a foolish son is the grief of his mother.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:1 The proverbs of Solomon: A wise child brings joy to a father;a foolish child brings grief to a mother. (NLT)

Main Idea

A new heading, “The proverbs of Solomon,” opens the first collection of short sayings, and the first one returns to the home of the prologue. A wise son makes his father glad, while a foolish son brings grief to his mother, so the path a son chooses is felt by the whole family.

Verse Explanation

A new heading, “The proverbs of Solomon,” opens the first collection of short sayings, and the first one returns to the home of the prologue. A wise son makes his father glad, while a foolish son brings grief to his mother, so the path a son chooses is felt by the whole family.

“The proverbs of Solomon:”

This is the first new heading since 1:1, and it changes the form of the book. The long speeches of the father and of Wisdom give way to short sayings, most only two lines long. This first collection runs to 22:16. In chapter 10 nearly every saying is a contrast: the second line, usually beginning with “But,” sets its picture against the first. The reader learns to read the two lines against each other, asking what each side shows about the other. The sayings are still part of the same book. They carry the two ways of chapters 1–9 into the home, the field, the market, and the mouth, and they are grouped by repeated words and subjects rather than thrown together at random.

“A wise son makes a glad father,”

The first saying goes straight back into the home of the prologue, where a father spoke to “my son.” Wisdom and folly are never private matters. A son who walks wisely brings real gladness to his father.

“But a foolish son is the grief of his mother.”

The contrast is grief, a heavy and lasting sorrow. The foolish son in Proverbs is not slow of mind; he is morally careless and despises instruction. Hebrew poetry often spreads one thought across both lines, so the point is not that only fathers rejoice and only mothers grieve. Both parents share both outcomes, just as both were honored in the father’s first appeal: “hear the instruction of your father, And do not forsake the law of your mother” (1:8). The path a son chooses is felt by the whole household.

Study Notes

The collection that begins here runs from 10:1 to 22:16 and contains 375 verses in the NKJV numbering.

The first line of 15:20 repeats the first line of this verse word for word in Hebrew, “A wise son makes a father glad,” and 17:25 returns to the grief a foolish son brings to his parents.

Proverbs 10:2
Proverbs 10:2
NKJV

Proverbs 10:2 Treasures of wickedness profit nothing, But righteousness delivers from death.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:2 Tainted wealth has no lasting value,but right living can save your life. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wealth gained by wickedness may look like security, but it profits nothing in the end. Righteousness, by contrast, delivers from death, which is something no treasure can do.

Verse Explanation

Wealth gained by wickedness may look like security, but it profits nothing in the end. Righteousness, by contrast, delivers from death, which is something no treasure can do.

“Treasures of wickedness profit nothing,”

These are riches gained by wicked means: theft, fraud, oppression, and violence. The gang of 1:10–19 promised exactly this kind of treasure: “We shall fill our houses with spoil” (1:13). Such wealth looks like security, and it can buy a great deal, but when life itself is at stake it profits nothing.

“But righteousness delivers from death.”

Righteousness, living rightly before God, does what no treasure can do. In the setting of Proverbs, the wicked way leads to an early and violent end; the gang that grasped for gain was lying in wait “for their own blood” (1:18). Righteousness keeps a person off that road and out of that end. The verse does not explain how deliverance comes in every case, and Proverbs speaks here in its usual general terms. Its contrast is still sharp: one kind of gain cannot save a life, and the other preserves it. Placed right after the first saying, it shows what a wise son is choosing when he refuses wicked gain.

Study Notes

The second line is repeated word for word in 11:4, where it answers a different first line: “Riches do not profit in the day of wrath.”

Proverbs 10:3
Proverbs 10:3
NKJV

Proverbs 10:3 The LORD will not allow the righteous soulto famish, But He casts away the desire of the wicked.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:3 The LORD will not let the godly go hungry,but he refuses to satisfy the craving of the wicked. (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD Himself stands behind the contrast: He will not let the righteous starve, and He pushes away what the wicked crave. This is a general truth about God’s care and His opposition to evil, not a promise that a righteous person will never know want.

Verse Explanation

The LORD Himself stands behind the contrast: He will not let the righteous starve, and He pushes away what the wicked crave. This is a general truth about God’s care and His opposition to evil, not a promise that a righteous person will never know want.

“The LORD will not allow the righteous soul to famish,”

This is the first time the LORD is named in Solomon’s collection, and He stands behind the contrast between the two ways. The “soul” here is the whole living person, including the appetite and its need for food. To “famish” is to go hungry. The LORD cares for the righteous and provides for them. This is a general truth about God’s care, not a promise that a righteous person will never know want. Faithful people in Scripture have gone hungry; Paul could describe his ministry as lived “in hunger and thirst” (2 Corinthians 11:27).

“But He casts away the desire of the wicked.”

The wicked crave, and the LORD thrusts their craving aside. What they reach for, He does not allow them to secure. The verse shows that God is not neutral between the two ways. He sustains the one and resists the other, which is why “Treasures of wickedness profit nothing” in the end (10:2).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “soul” can refer to the appetite as well as to the whole living person, which fits the image of going hungry.

The Hebrew word behind “desire” in the second line is difficult because it has two quite different meanings. It can mean a craving, usually a bad one, or it can mean ruin and destruction. Brown-Driver-Briggs places this verse under the first meaning, and the ESV agrees with “craving.” The KJV has “substance,” and the 1611 edition offers in its margin an alternative rendering that speaks of the wicked being cast away for their wickedness. The NKJV’s “desire” follows the sense of craving: the LORD pushes aside what the wicked long for.

Proverbs 10:4
Proverbs 10:4
NKJV

Proverbs 10:4 He who has a slack hand becomes poor, But the hand of the diligent makes rich.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:4 Lazy people are soon poor;hard workers get rich. (NLT)

Main Idea

Work habits have consequences: a slack, careless hand leads to poverty, while the diligent hand produces wealth. The saying commends diligence as wisdom applied to work and describes the usual result rather than a formula for riches, and it does not blame those who are poor through inability or hardship.

Verse Explanation

Work habits have consequences: a slack, careless hand leads to poverty, while the diligent hand produces wealth. The saying commends diligence as wisdom applied to work and describes the usual result rather than a formula for riches, and it does not blame those who are poor through inability or hardship.

“He who has a slack hand becomes poor,”

The hand stands for a person’s work. A slack hand is loose, careless, and negligent; it does the job halfway or not at all. Over time such habits lead to poverty. Proverbs treats this as one of the plain patterns of life in God’s world.

“But the hand of the diligent makes rich.”

The diligent person works with focus and steady effort, and the usual result is plenty. This is wisdom applied to work, the skill of doing the right thing with care and persistence. The saying describes how work generally pays; it is not a formula that guarantees riches. Nor does it blame everyone who is poor. Proverbs itself knows that poverty can come from injustice: “Much food is in the fallow ground of the poor, And for lack of justice there is waste” (13:23). This verse addresses the poverty that comes from neglect, and it calls the reader to diligent hands. It also prepares for 10:22, where the same verb, “makes rich,” describes the blessing of the LORD.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “the diligent” returns in 12:24, 13:4, and 21:5, each time set against the lazy or the hasty, so this verse begins a thread the collection keeps following.

Proverbs 10:5
Proverbs 10:5
NKJV

Proverbs 10:5 He who gathers in summer is a wise son; He who sleeps in harvest is a son who causes shame.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:5 A wise youth harvests in the summer,but one who sleeps during harvest is a disgrace. (NLT)

Main Idea

The harvest season makes the contrast concrete. The son who gathers while the crops are ready is wise, but the one who sleeps through harvest brings shame on his family; he is the lazy man of 6:6–11, missing a moment that will not come again.

Verse Explanation

The harvest season makes the contrast concrete. The son who gathers while the crops are ready is wise, but the one who sleeps through harvest brings shame on his family; he is the lazy man of 6:6–11, missing a moment that will not come again.

“He who gathers in summer is a wise son;”

In Israel the summer months brought the harvest of grain and fruit, the season when the year’s food had to be gathered. The son who works then is called wise, the same description used in 10:1. Here wisdom is a matter of timing: doing the work while the season is open. He is like the ant, which “Provides her supplies in the summer” (6:8).

“He who sleeps in harvest is a son who causes shame.”

The other son sleeps through the harvest. He is the lazy man of 6:6–11, whose motto is “A little sleep, a little slumber” (6:10). The harvest does not wait for him; when the season passes, the opportunity is gone and the family goes short. His laziness is not only his own loss. He brings shame on his household, the same kind of shame and grief the first verse described. Together 10:1 and 10:5 frame the opening section with a wise son and a foolish one.

Study Notes

The phrase “a son who causes shame” translates the same Hebrew wording in 17:2 and 19:26.

Proverbs 10:6–11 — Blessing on the Righteous, Violence in the Mouth of the Wicked

Proverbs 10:6
Proverbs 10:6
NKJV

Proverbs 10:6 Blessings are on the head of the righteous, But violence covers the mouth of the wicked.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:6 The godly are showered with blessings;the words of the wicked conceal violent intentions. (NLT)

Main Idea

Blessings rest on the head of the righteous like a crown, openly given and received. The mouth of the wicked, however, is covered by violence, his speech hiding the harm in his heart, and the line returns in verse 11 to frame this section.

Verse Explanation

Blessings rest on the head of the righteous like a crown, openly given and received. The mouth of the wicked, however, is covered by violence, his speech hiding the harm in his heart, and the line returns in verse 11 to frame this section.

“Blessings are on the head of the righteous,”

In Scripture a blessing was often pictured as resting on the head, as when Jacob laid his hands on the heads of Joseph’s sons (Genesis 48:14) and spoke of blessings “on the head of Joseph” (Genesis 49:26). The righteous wear their blessings openly. Good gifts from God, and the goodwill of others, rest on them where everyone can see.

“But violence covers the mouth of the wicked.”

The Hebrew is compact and can be read two ways. It may mean that the mouth of the wicked conceals violence, his speech covering the harm he intends, or that his own violence finally covers his mouth and silences him. The NKJV keeps the wording open. In this verse the contrast of head and mouth points to the first sense: what rests on the righteous is blessing, while what lies behind the words of the wicked is violence. The same line returns in 10:11, framing the section with two sayings about the mouth of the wicked.

Study Notes

The ESV and NASB take the first sense, saying that the mouth of the wicked “conceals violence,” and the NLT reads “the words of the wicked conceal violent intentions.” The 1984 NIV takes the other sense, “violence overwhelms.”

The Hebrew verb behind “covers” is a catchword in this part of the chapter, a repeated word that links neighboring sayings. It recurs in 10:11, in “love covers all sins” (10:12), and in “Whoever hides hatred” (10:18), where the NKJV translates it “hides.” Following the word through these verses shows the same act of covering put to opposite uses: the wicked cover violence, love covers sins, and the liar covers hatred.

Proverbs 10:7
Proverbs 10:7
NKJV

Proverbs 10:7 The memory of the righteous is blessed, But the name of the wicked will rot.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:7 We have happy memories of the godly,but the name of a wicked person rots away. (NLT)

Main Idea

The contrast reaches past death. The righteous are remembered with blessing and their names spoken with gratitude, but the name of the wicked rots like something dead until no one wants to say it.

Verse Explanation

The contrast reaches past death. The righteous are remembered with blessing and their names spoken with gratitude, but the name of the wicked rots like something dead until no one wants to say it.

“The memory of the righteous is blessed,”

The contrast now reaches beyond death. When the righteous are gone, they are remembered with blessing. People speak their names with gratitude, and their memory continues to do good. The blessings that rested on their heads in life (10:6) carry on in the way they are remembered.

“But the name of the wicked will rot.”

A name in Scripture is a person’s reputation and the memory of who he was. The name of the wicked rots like something dead, decaying until no one wants to speak it. The picture is vivid and unpleasant on purpose. Whatever the wicked gained or built, what remains of him spoils. The verse calls the reader to think about what a life leaves behind.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of the first line reads literally, the memory of the righteous is for a blessing, and the ESV renders it “a blessing.” The wording suggests that the memory of the righteous becomes a blessing to others, not only that it is spoken of well.

The Hebrew verb “rot” is related to the noun translated “rottenness” in 12:4 and 14:30, where it describes decay in the bones. The same picture of slow decay lies behind what happens to the name of the wicked here.

Proverbs 10:8
Proverbs 10:8
NKJV

Proverbs 10:8 The wise in heart will receive commands, But a prating fool will fall.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:8 The wise are glad to be instructed,but babbling fools fall flat on their faces. (NLT)

Main Idea

A wise heart is teachable and receives commands, while “a prating fool,” one who chatters without listening, will fall. His own talking keeps him from hearing the instruction that would have kept him standing.

Verse Explanation

A wise heart is teachable and receives commands, while “a prating fool,” one who chatters without listening, will fall. His own talking keeps him from hearing the instruction that would have kept him standing.

“The wise in heart will receive commands,”

The wise in heart are wise inwardly, not merely in appearance. Their wisdom shows in teachability: they receive commands. They listen, take instruction, and let it direct them. Wisdom begins with receiving before it begins with speaking.

“But a prating fool will fall.”

A “prating fool” is a chatterer; the Hebrew says literally a fool of lips. He talks so much that he never listens, so the instruction that could have kept him upright never reaches him. He “will fall,” brought down by his own folly. The contrast is between the heart and the lips, between receiving and talking. The line is repeated at 10:10.

Study Notes

The 1611 King James Version notes in its margin that the Hebrew behind “a prating fool” is literally a fool of lips. Its second note offers be beaten only as an alternative rendering of “fall”; the Hebrew verb means to be thrown or thrust down. The ESV calls him “babbling.”

Proverbs 10:9
Proverbs 10:9
NKJV

Proverbs 10:9 He who walks with integrity walkssecurely, But he who perverts his ways will become known.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:9 People with integrity walk safely,but those who follow crooked paths will be exposed. (NLT)

Main Idea

Integrity gives a person secure footing, because he has nothing to hide and no fear of being found out. The one who twists his ways may get by for a while, but he will become known, because crooked paths are eventually exposed.

Verse Explanation

Integrity gives a person secure footing, because he has nothing to hide and no fear of being found out. The one who twists his ways may get by for a while, but he will become known, because crooked paths are eventually exposed.

“He who walks with integrity walks securely,”

Integrity is wholeness, a life without a hidden side. The person who walks with integrity walks with confidence, because he has nothing to cover up and no fear of being found out. His security does not come from cleverness or luck but from the plainness of his ways.

“But he who perverts his ways will become known.”

The one who twists his ways, bending them toward deceit, may get by for a time, but he “will become known.” Crooked paths are eventually exposed. Moses warned Israel in Numbers 32:23, “be sure your sin will find you out.” The contrast is between living securely and living in danger of exposure. Integrity is not only right; it is also the safer way to live.

Study Notes

A later saying sets the same pair side by side: “Whoever walks blamelessly will be saved, But he who is perverse in his ways will suddenly fall” (28:18).

Proverbs 10:10
Proverbs 10:10
NKJV

Proverbs 10:10 He who winks with the eye causes trouble, But a prating fool will fall.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:10 People who wink at wrong cause trouble,but a bold reproof promotes peace. (NLT)

Main Idea

The one who winks with the eye, signaling mischief behind someone’s back, causes trouble for others. The second line repeats verse 8, linking the sly signaler with the careless talker as two ways of misusing what should communicate truth.

Verse Explanation

The one who winks with the eye, signaling mischief behind someone’s back, causes trouble for others. The second line repeats verse 8, linking the sly signaler with the careless talker as two ways of misusing what should communicate truth.

“He who winks with the eye causes trouble,”

The wink here is a secret signal of malice, not a friendly gesture. It says one thing behind what is openly said, a sign between people plotting against someone. The worthless man of 6:12–14 “winks with his eyes” as part of his scheming (6:13), and in Psalm 35:19 David prays, “Nor let them wink with the eye who hate me without a cause.” Such a person causes trouble, pain and grief for others, without ever saying a word that could be held against him.

“But a prating fool will fall.”

The second line repeats 10:8. The sly signaler and the careless talker stand side by side as two ways of misusing what should communicate truth. One harms others quietly; the other ruins himself loudly. Both end badly, and placing the familiar line here invites the reader to compare them.

Study Notes

The NLT’s second line, “but a bold reproof promotes peace,” matches the Greek Septuagint, which in Brenton’s English translation (1851) says that the one who reproves boldly is a peacemaker. The Hebrew text, followed by the NKJV, ESV, and NASB, repeats the second line of 10:8.

Where the NKJV has “But,” the ESV reads “and,” since the Hebrew connective can mean either.

Proverbs 10:11
Proverbs 10:11
NKJV

Proverbs 10:11 The mouth of the righteous is a well of life, But violence covers the mouth of the wicked.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:11 The words of the godly are a life-giving fountain;the words of the wicked conceal violent intentions. (NLT)

Main Idea

Righteous speech is “a well of life,” a spring that refreshes and sustains those who drink from it. The wicked man’s mouth hides violence, as verse 6 already said, and the repeated line closes this section by setting life-giving words against words that cover harm.

Verse Explanation

Righteous speech is “a well of life,” a spring that refreshes and sustains those who drink from it. The wicked man’s mouth hides violence, as verse 6 already said, and the repeated line closes this section by setting life-giving words against words that cover harm.

“The mouth of the righteous is a well of life,”

A well or spring in a dry land meant life itself. The words of the righteous are like that: a source that refreshes, sustains, and keeps giving to those who draw from it. Proverbs uses the same image for the teaching of the wise and for the fear of the LORD (13:14; 14:27). The God who is “the fountain of life” (Psalm 36:9) makes the speech of His people a source of life to others.

“But violence covers the mouth of the wicked.”

The line from 10:6 returns, now set against a different first line. In 10:6 the contrast was head and mouth; here it is mouth and mouth. The same organ can pour out life or hide violence. The repetition closes the section and points ahead to the next one, where the chapter gathers many sayings about the use of words.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase behind “a well of life” is the one the NKJV renders “a fountain of life” in 13:14 and 14:27.

Proverbs 10:12–21 — Words That Wound and Words That Feed

Proverbs 10:12
Proverbs 10:12
NKJV

Proverbs 10:12 Hatred stirs up strife, But love covers all sins.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:12 Hatred stirs up quarrels,but love makes up for all offenses. (NLT)

Main Idea

Hatred keeps old quarrels alive and stirs up fresh strife, but love covers all sins, refusing to broadcast another’s wrongs and choosing to forgive (1 Peter 4:8). Covering sin in love does not mean hiding abuse or avoiding needed correction, but it does mean refusing to keep a conflict burning.

Verse Explanation

Hatred keeps old quarrels alive and stirs up fresh strife, but love covers all sins, refusing to broadcast another’s wrongs and choosing to forgive (1 Peter 4:8). Covering sin in love does not mean hiding abuse or avoiding needed correction, but it does mean refusing to keep a conflict burning.

“Hatred stirs up strife,”

Hatred does not let a quarrel rest. It wakes up old grievances, retells old injuries, and keeps conflict alive. Where hatred lives, strife keeps breaking out again, because hatred wants the offense remembered.

“But love covers all sins.”

Love does the opposite. To cover a sin is to refuse to expose it, broadcast it, or keep bringing it up; it is the choice to forgive and let the offense go. Proverbs says the same elsewhere: “He who covers a transgression seeks love” (17:9). Peter takes up this line for the church: “love will cover a multitude of sins” (1 Peter 4:8). Covering sin in love does not mean concealing abuse or crime, enabling ongoing wrongdoing, or avoiding needed correction. Proverbs also says, “Open rebuke is better Than love carefully concealed” (27:5), and it warns that “He who covers his sins will not prosper” (28:13). The love in this verse covers the offenses of others so that peace can be restored. It does not hide one’s own sin from God or shield wrongdoing from justice.

Study Notes

For “all sins” the ESV reads “all offenses.” The Hebrew word is the one often translated transgression, a wrong that breaks faith with another person or rebels against God. It is the same word translated “sin” in 10:19.

Proverbs 10:13
Proverbs 10:13
NKJV

Proverbs 10:13 Wisdom is found on the lips of him who has understanding, But a rod is for the back of him who is devoid of understanding.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:13 Wise words come from the lips of people with understanding,but those lacking sense will be beaten with a rod. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wisdom shows up on the lips of the person who understands, so his words carry help to others. The one who lacks understanding will not learn from words and needs the rod on his back, correction he could have avoided by listening.

Verse Explanation

Wisdom shows up on the lips of the person who understands, so his words carry help to others. The one who lacks understanding will not learn from words and needs the rod on his back, correction he could have avoided by listening.

“Wisdom is found on the lips of him who has understanding,”

Understanding shows up in what a person says. Wisdom can be “found” on his lips, so others who listen to him find help and direction. The understanding person carries wisdom in his words and passes it on.

“But a rod is for the back of him who is devoid of understanding.”

The one “devoid of understanding,” in Hebrew literally lacking heart, is the same kind of person as the young man of 7:7. He will not learn from words, so he learns from the rod. In its setting the rod is the tool of correction, and the saying is plain about the result: what a person refuses to receive through his ears he may have to receive on his back. Proverbs says it again: “a rod for the fool’s back” (26:3). The contrast of lips and back shows that correction will come one way or the other. It is far better to receive it through words.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase behind “devoid of understanding” is literally lacking heart, the same phrase used in 9:4 and 7:7; the person lacks the inner sense and judgment that the heart stands for in Proverbs. The 1611 King James Version translates the phrase void of understanding and adds the marginal note “Hebr. heart.” That note replaces only the word “understanding” with the Hebrew word heart; the idea of lacking is already in the English text. The same Hebrew expression lies behind “lack of wisdom” in 10:21.

Proverbs 10:14
Proverbs 10:14
NKJV

Proverbs 10:14 Wise people store up knowledge, (H1847) But the mouth of the foolish is near destruction.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:14 Wise people treasure knowledge,but the babbling of a fool invites disaster. (NLT)

Main Idea

The wise store up knowledge like provisions, holding it until the right time to use it. The fool’s mouth works the other way, pouring out whatever he thinks and bringing him close to ruin with every careless word.

Verse Explanation

The wise store up knowledge like provisions, holding it until the right time to use it. The fool’s mouth works the other way, pouring out whatever he thinks and bringing him close to ruin with every careless word.

“Wise people store up knowledge,”

To store up is to lay something away as a treasure or a provision, kept safe until it is needed. The wise do this with knowledge. They gather it, keep it, and hold it ready for the right moment. They do not need to spend every thought as soon as it comes.

“But the mouth of the foolish is near destruction.”

The fool works the other way. Whatever comes into his head comes out of his mouth, and every careless word brings him a step closer to ruin. Destruction is “near,” close at hand. The contrast is between storing and spending: the wise keep knowledge in reserve, while the fool’s talk is a constant danger to himself.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “destruction” is a catchword in this chapter. It appears again in 10:15, “The destruction of the poor,” and in 10:29, “destruction will come to the workers of iniquity.”

The Hebrew verb behind “store up” is the one used in 2:7, where the LORD “stores up sound wisdom for the upright.” It means to hide something away as a treasure. The wise do with knowledge what the LORD does with wisdom: they keep it in reserve until it is needed.

Proverbs 10:15
Proverbs 10:15
NKJV

Proverbs 10:15 The rich man's wealth is his strong city; The destruction of the poor is their poverty.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:15 The wealth of the rich is their fortress;the poverty of the poor is their destruction. (NLT)

Main Idea

Here is a plain observation of life: the rich man’s wealth works like a fortified city around him, while poverty leaves the poor exposed to ruin. The saying describes what money does in a fallen world without praising it, and later Proverbs adds that this fortress is “a high wall in his own esteem” (18:11).

Verse Explanation

Here is a plain observation of life: the rich man’s wealth works like a fortified city around him, while poverty leaves the poor exposed to ruin. The saying describes what money does in a fallen world without praising it, and later Proverbs adds that this fortress is “a high wall in his own esteem” (18:11).

“The rich man’s wealth is his strong city;”

This is a plain observation of life. Wealth works like a fortified city around a rich man. It shields him from many of the troubles that would overwhelm others, such as a bad harvest, a sudden expense, or a strong enemy. The saying describes what money does in a fallen world without praising it.

“The destruction of the poor is their poverty.”

The poor have no such walls. Poverty leaves them exposed, so that a small setback can become ruin. The saying is not admiration of the rich or contempt for the poor; it states how things are. Proverbs later adds an important correction to the first line: “The rich man’s wealth is his strong city, And like a high wall in his own esteem” (18:11). That fortress is partly imagined, and the verse just before it names the only true refuge: “The name of the LORD is a strong tower” (18:10). The book also insists that how a person treats the poor matters to God (14:31).

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 10:16
Proverbs 10:16
NKJV

Proverbs 10:16 The labor of the righteous leads to life, The wages of the wicked to sin.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 10:16 The earnings of the godly enhance their lives,but evil people squander their money on sin. (NLT)

Main Idea

Both the righteous and the wicked work and are paid. The righteous man’s labor serves life, while the wicked man’s earnings only feed more sin, so the direction of a person’s gain matters more than its size.

Verse Explanation

Both the righteous and the wicked work and are paid. The righteous man’s labor serves life, while the wicked man’s earnings only feed more sin, so the direction of a person’s gain matters more than its size.

“The labor of the righteous leads to life,”

Both the righteous and the wicked work and are paid. The labor of the righteous, the earnings that come from his work, serves life. It sustains his household, supplies others, and supports the way of life. His gain stays in the service of what is good.

“The wages of the wicked to sin.”

The wicked man earns too, but his earnings only feed more sin. What he gains, he spends on the way of death. The saying does not condemn wealth or wages as such; it looks at where each person’s gain goes. The direction of a person’s income matters more than its size. This saying follows naturally from the observation about wealth and poverty in 10:15, which described what money does; this one asks where it goes.

Study Notes

The two lines use different Hebrew nouns. The first means work or the wages that work earns; the second means produce or yield, a word often used of crops and income. The ESV distinguishes them as “wage” and “gain,” and the KJV as “labour” and “fruit.”

Proverbs 10:22
Proverbs 10:22
NKJV

Proverbs 10:22 The blessing of the LORD makes a person rich,and he adds no sorrow with it. (NLT)

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

Real riches come as a gift: “The blessing of the LORD makes one rich.” What He gives comes without the sorrow that grasping or ill-gotten wealth brings, and read with the rest of Proverbs, the saying names the LORD as the source of true blessing rather than promising wealth to all who serve Him.

“The blessing of the LORD makes one rich,”

This saying names the source of true riches. The same verb, “makes rich,” described the diligent hand in 10:4. Diligence matters, but it works under the blessing of the LORD; He is the One who finally gives. The book also warns against trusting in riches: “He who trusts in his riches will fall” (11:28). So this verse is not a formula that promises wealth to everyone who serves God. God promises to meet the needs of His people, and His blessing takes many forms. What the verse insists on is that every good gift comes from Him.

“And He adds no sorrow with it.”

What the LORD gives comes without the sorrow that grasping or ill-gotten wealth brings. Riches gained by greed bring anxiety, conflict, and loss; greed “takes away the life of its owners” (1:19). Proverbs says later, “Better is a little with the fear of the LORD, Than great treasure with trouble” (15:16). The LORD’s gifts do not carry that hidden cost. Some translators read the line differently, as saying that toil adds nothing to it, because the Hebrew word for “sorrow” can also mean painful labor. Either way, the LORD is the source, and His blessing is not something human strain can produce.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of the second line can be read with “sorrow” as the object (“He adds no sorrow”) or as the subject (painful toil adds nothing). The NKJV, ESV, NASB, and NLT take the first reading.

The Hebrew word translated “sorrow” here is the same word used in Genesis 3:16, “In pain you shall bring forth children,” and it is closely related to the words for “sorrow” in that verse and “toil” in Genesis 3:17. The word covers both pain and painful labor. That range explains why the second line can be read either as no sorrow added by the LORD or as human toil adding nothing to His blessing.
