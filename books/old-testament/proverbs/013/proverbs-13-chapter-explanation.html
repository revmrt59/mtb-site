Chapter Study

Proverbs 13

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 13:1
Proverbs 13:1
NKJV

Proverbs 13:1 A wise son heeds his father's instruction, But a scoffer does not listen to rebuke.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:1 A wise child accepts a parent’s discipline;a mocker refuses to listen to correction. (NLT)

Main Idea

A wise son shows his wisdom by listening when his father instructs him. The scoffer will not even hear a rebuke, and that refusal to listen is the heart of his problem (9:7–8).

Verse Explanation

A wise son shows his wisdom by listening when his father instructs him. The scoffer will not even hear a rebuke, and that refusal to listen is the heart of his problem (9:7–8).

“A wise son heeds his father’s instruction,”

Proverbs 13 opens in the home, just as the whole collection of Solomon’s sayings did: “A wise son makes a glad father” (10:1). The father’s instruction is his training and correction, the patient work of forming a son for life. What marks the son as wise is not a natural gift or a quick mind but what he does when his father speaks. He heeds. Wisdom in Proverbs is skill for living God’s way, and it begins with the humility to be taught. The son is wise because he receives.

“But a scoffer does not listen to rebuke.”

The scoffer is the most stubborn figure in the book’s gallery of fools. A rebuke is sharper than instruction, the kind of correction that names a fault plainly, and the scoffer will not even hear it. His problem is not a lack of ability; it is a settled refusal. Earlier the father warned, “He who corrects a scoffer gets shame for himself” (9:7). The two lines set one son who grows because he listens beside another who stays where he is because he will not. Proverbs still holds the door open, for Wisdom’s call to the scornful was, “Turn at my rebuke” (1:23).

Study Notes

The first line of the Hebrew has no verb. It sets the two phrases side by side, as Young’s Literal Translation shows by joining them with a dash. Translators supply the verb from context; NKJV has “heeds,” ESV “hears,” KJV “heareth,” and NASB adds “accepts” in brackets.

The Hebrew word behind “instruction” in 13:1 is a broad word for a father’s training, discipline, and correction. The same Hebrew word returns in 13:18, where the NKJV renders it “correction,” and in 13:24, where it stands behind “disciplines.” The chapter therefore begins and ends with a father’s training of his son.

Proverbs 13:2
Proverbs 13:2
NKJV

Proverbs 13:2 A man shall eat wellby the fruit of his mouth, But the soul of the unfaithful feeds on violence.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:2 Wise words will win you a good meal,but treacherous people have an appetite for violence. (NLT)

Main Idea

A man’s words bear fruit he will enjoy, so good speech feeds the speaker as well as others. The unfaithful have a different appetite, for what their souls hunger after is violence.

Verse Explanation

A man’s words bear fruit he will enjoy, so good speech feeds the speaker as well as others. The unfaithful have a different appetite, for what their souls hunger after is violence.

“A man shall eat well by the fruit of his mouth,”

Words are pictured as a crop, and the speaker eventually eats what his words produce. Kind, true, and timely speech brings back trust, peace, and goodwill, so the one who speaks well is himself fed by it. Proverbs said nearly the same thing a chapter earlier: “A man will be satisfied with good by the fruit of his mouth” (12:14). The point is practical. A person’s words do not vanish once spoken; he lives with what they grow.

“But the soul of the unfaithful feeds on violence.”

The second line keeps the picture of eating but moves from the mouth to the appetite. The “soul” here is the inner person with its cravings and desires. The unfaithful are the treacherous, people who betray the trust others place in them, and what they hunger for is violence, harm done to others for their own gain. The father described the same appetite in the violent men of chapter 4: “they eat the bread of wickedness, And drink the wine of violence” (4:17). Set beside each other, the two lines show what feeds two kinds of people. One man is nourished by good words; the other craves what destroys.

Study Notes

The words “feeds on” are supplied by the NKJV. The second line in Hebrew has no verb at all; it simply sets violence beside the soul of the unfaithful, leaving the reader to supply the link. ESV and NASB render “soul” here as “desire.”

The Hebrew word behind “soul” in 13:2 also stands behind “life” in 13:3 and “soul” twice in 13:4. This repeated word ties the opening sayings together around appetite, speech, and life.

Proverbs 13:3
Proverbs 13:3
NKJV

Proverbs 13:3 He who guards his mouth preserves his life, But he who opens wide his lips shall have destruction.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:3 Those who control their tongue will have a long life;opening your mouth can ruin everything. (NLT)

Main Idea

Guarding the mouth is a way of guarding life itself, because careless words can bring real ruin. The one who opens his lips wide, saying whatever comes to mind, invites destruction.

Verse Explanation

Guarding the mouth is a way of guarding life itself, because careless words can bring real ruin. The one who opens his lips wide, saying whatever comes to mind, invites destruction.

“He who guards his mouth preserves his life,”

The mouth is pictured as a gate with a watchman posted at it. The one who keeps watch over what passes through his lips is keeping watch over his own life. A later saying puts it the same way: “Whoever guards his mouth and tongue Keeps his soul from troubles” (21:23). Guarding the mouth does not mean silence. Proverbs praises the right word at the right time; the guarded mouth is one that thinks before it speaks and lets nothing out that should have stayed in.

“But he who opens wide his lips shall have destruction.”

The opposite picture is a mouth flung open with no one at the gate. This person says whatever comes to mind, whether boasting, gossip, rash promises, or angry words. Destruction follows, and it lands on the speaker himself. A careless sentence can ruin a reputation, a friendship, or a livelihood, and it cannot be called back. Proverbs warned earlier, “In the multitude of words sin is not lacking” (10:19). Coming right after 13:2, the saying adds a second lesson about speech: words feed the speaker, and unguarded words can also bring him down.

Study Notes

The 1984 NIV renders the second line “speaks rashly,” which captures the sense of the picture. KJV, ESV, and NASB keep the image of opening the lips wide.

The same Hebrew verb stands behind “guards” in 13:3 and “guards” in 13:6. The repetition links the first and last sayings of the opening section: the man who guards his mouth is matched by righteousness that guards the blameless.

Proverbs 13:4
Proverbs 13:4
NKJV

Proverbs 13:4 The soul of a lazy man desires, and has nothing; But the soul of the diligent shall be made rich.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:4 Lazy people want much but get little,but those who work hard will prosper. (NLT)

Main Idea

The lazy man wants things as much as anyone, but wanting without working leaves him with nothing. The diligent soul is the one that ends up satisfied and made rich.

Verse Explanation

The lazy man wants things as much as anyone, but wanting without working leaves him with nothing. The diligent soul is the one that ends up satisfied and made rich.

“The soul of a lazy man desires, and has nothing;”

The lazy man is not a man without desires. His soul wants things as much as anyone’s does, perhaps more. His trouble is that wanting is all he does. Desire never becomes effort, so the craving stays and the hands stay empty. Proverbs has already drawn his portrait in the sluggard who needs the ant to teach him (6:6–8), and here it shows the inside of that life: a soul full of longing and a life with nothing to show for it.

“But the soul of the diligent shall be made rich.”

The diligent person also has a soul with appetites, but his desire is joined to steady work, and his soul is the one that ends up satisfied. The picture is of a well-fed soul, filled and content. The father said earlier, “the hand of the diligent makes rich” (10:4). This is a general pattern in God’s world, not a promise that every hard worker will prosper. The saying describes laziness, not poverty as such; illness, inability, and injustice (13:23) can leave diligent people in need, and Proverbs never treats them as lazy.

Study Notes

“Made rich” translates a Hebrew verb that means to be made fat, the picture of a well-fed body. In Proverbs it describes a person who is satisfied and prospering. KJV, NASB, and YLT keep “made fat,” and ESV has “richly supplied.” The same Hebrew verb appears in 11:25, “The generous soul will be made rich.”

The Hebrew verb behind “desires” in 13:4 is closely related to the Hebrew noun behind “desire” in 13:12 and 13:19. The shared word family links the lazy man’s empty craving in the first section with the two sayings about fulfilled desire that frame the third.

The NKJV uses “lazy man” here and “sluggard” only in 6:6 and 6:9, though the Hebrew word is the same.

Proverbs 13:5
Proverbs 13:5
NKJV

Proverbs 13:5 A righteous man hates lying, But a wicked man is loathsome and comes to shame.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:5 The godly hate lies;the wicked cause shame and disgrace. (NLT)

Main Idea

A righteous man hates lying, in himself and in others. The wicked man becomes loathsome to those around him, and his falseness brings him to shame.

Verse Explanation

A righteous man hates lying, in himself and in others. The wicked man becomes loathsome to those around him, and his falseness brings him to shame.

“A righteous man hates lying,”

The righteous man does more than avoid lies; he hates them. Righteousness here is a matter of the heart, a settled dislike of falsehood in himself and in others. In this he shares the LORD’s own response, for among the things the LORD hates is “A lying tongue” (6:17), and “Lying lips are an abomination to the LORD” (12:22). A person who has learned to hate lying will not easily be drawn into it, because truth has become part of who he is.

“But a wicked man is loathsome and comes to shame.”

The wicked man does not merely tell lies; he becomes offensive. “Loathsome” pictures something that gives off a bad smell, and his falseness eventually becomes as obvious to others as an odor in the room. What he hoped to keep hidden is exposed, and he “comes to shame.” The two lines are not exactly parallel. The first describes what the righteous man hates; the second describes what the wicked man becomes. The one who does not hate lying ends up becoming repulsive himself.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “is loathsome” comes from a root meaning to smell bad, and figuratively to become offensive. The same root is used in the manna story when leftover manna “bred worms and stank” (Exodus 16:20). The picture explains why the NKJV speaks of the wicked man as loathsome: his falseness becomes as offensive as a bad smell.

In Hebrew both verbs in the second line are causative in form; that is, they describe the wicked man causing something, not only experiencing it. Read that way, he spreads offense and brings shame and disgrace, as ESV renders the line. The NKJV, with KJV, describes what the wicked man becomes: loathsome, and brought to shame.

Proverbs 13:6
Proverbs 13:6
NKJV

Proverbs 13:6 Righteousness guards him whose way is blameless, But wickedness overthrows the sinner.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:6 Godliness guards the path of the blameless,but the evil are misled by sin. (NLT)

Main Idea

Righteousness stands like a guard around the person whose way is blameless, keeping him safe as he walks. Wickedness overthrows the sinner, so each person’s way either protects him or brings him down.

Verse Explanation

Righteousness stands like a guard around the person whose way is blameless, keeping him safe as he walks. Wickedness overthrows the sinner, so each person’s way either protects him or brings him down.

“Righteousness guards him whose way is blameless,”

Righteousness is pictured as a sentry standing watch over a traveler. The person in view is one “whose way is blameless,” someone whose life holds together with integrity. Blameless in Proverbs does not mean sinless; it describes a whole and undivided way of life, a person who is the same in private as in public. His righteousness becomes his protection. The man who guarded his mouth in 13:3 now finds that he himself is guarded.

“But wickedness overthrows the sinner.”

Wickedness is also given a role, and it is the role of an enemy. It throws down the very person who practices it. The sinner is not overthrown by an outside force so much as by his own way of living, which turns on him. Proverbs said it earlier: “the wicked will fall by his own wickedness” (11:5). The saying closes the first section by showing that each person’s way either keeps him or brings him down. Choices do not simply pass away; they become either a guard or a trap.

Study Notes

The original 1611 edition of the KJV notes in its margin that the Hebrew behind “the sinner” is literally “sin.” That Hebrew word can also mean a sin offering, which is how YLT renders it, but the setting of moral ruin in this verse supports the NKJV’s reading.

ESV reverses the subjects of the second line, making sin the force that overthrows the wicked; the sense is much the same.

Proverbs 13:7–11 — Riches, Pride, and Honest Gain

Proverbs 13:7
Proverbs 13:7
NKJV

Proverbs 13:7 There is one who makes himself rich, yet has nothing; And one who makes himself poor, yet has great riches.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:7 Some who are poor pretend to be rich;others who are rich pretend to be poor. (NLT)

Main Idea

Appearances about wealth can deceive. One man puts on a show of riches but actually has nothing, while another seems poor yet has great riches, so outward show is a poor measure of what a person truly has.

Verse Explanation

Appearances about wealth can deceive. One man puts on a show of riches but actually has nothing, while another seems poor yet has great riches, so outward show is a poor measure of what a person truly has.

“There is one who makes himself rich, yet has nothing;”

The wording describes a person who acts rich. He presents himself as wealthy, with the clothes, the spending, and the confidence that go with it, and yet behind the display he has nothing. The saying does not say why he does it; it simply observes that the show and the reality do not match. In a world where people were judged by what they seemed to have, this was a needed warning against judging by appearance.

“And one who makes himself poor, yet has great riches.”

The second man does the opposite. He lives plainly and seems to have little, yet he holds great riches. He may be cautious, modest, or simply private; again the saying does not judge his motive. Together the two lines teach the reader to look past surfaces, a lesson Proverbs has already given in 12:9, where the man who is slighted but has a servant is better off than the one who honors himself but lacks bread. Outward show is a poor measure of what a person truly has, and the person who measures others, or himself, by appearances will be misled in both directions.

Study Notes

In Hebrew both verbs, “makes himself rich” and “makes himself poor,” are reflexive in form, meaning the person does the action to himself: he makes himself out to be rich or poor. That is why ESV, NASB, and AMP render both with “pretends.”

Some older commentators, such as Joseph Benson and the Pulpit Commentary, also allow a reading of the second line in terms of generosity: a person becomes poor by giving freely and yet is truly rich, in the spirit of 11:24. The parallel with pretended wealth in the first line favors the reading of pretense that most translations adopt.

Proverbs 13:8
Proverbs 13:8
NKJV

Proverbs 13:8 The ransom of a man's lifeis his riches, But the poor does not hear rebuke.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:8 The rich can pay a ransom for their lives,but the poor won’t even get threatened. (NLT)

Main Idea

Riches can buy a man’s life back when he is threatened, paying his ransom. The poor man does not even hear such threats, because no one troubles to demand a ransom from a man with nothing, so wealth brings dangers of its own.

Verse Explanation

Riches can buy a man’s life back when he is threatened, paying his ransom. The poor man does not even hear such threats, because no one troubles to demand a ransom from a man with nothing, so wealth brings dangers of its own.

“The ransom of a man’s life is his riches,”

Riches have real uses, and one of them is to buy a life back. In the ancient world a wealthy man could be seized and held for payment, and his money could set him free. The Law of Moses also allowed a man whose life was forfeit to “pay to redeem his life” (Exodus 21:30). The saying does not despise wealth. It admits plainly that money can rescue a person from serious danger.

“But the poor does not hear rebuke.”

The second line notices the other side of that fact. The rich man needs a ransom because he is worth threatening. The poor man never hears such threats, because no kidnapper or extortionist troubles himself with a man who has nothing to take. Wealth that can protect a life also exposes it to dangers the poor never face. The saying is realistic about money in both directions, and it does not make poverty a blessing; 13:23 shows how hard the life of the poor can be.

Study Notes

The second line repeats the exact Hebrew words of 13:1 that the NKJV renders “does not listen to rebuke,” but in a new sense. In 13:1 the scoffer refuses to hear correction; here the poor man is simply never threatened. ESV renders the Hebrew word for rebuke here as “no threat.”

The line is brief, and its exact sense is debated. The reading that the poor are spared threats fits the parallel with ransom best and is followed by most modern translations.

Proverbs 13:9
Proverbs 13:9
NKJV

Proverbs 13:9 The light of the righteous rejoices, But the lamp of the wicked will be put out.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:9 The life of the godly is full of light and joy,but the light of the wicked will be snuffed out. (NLT)

Main Idea

The light of the righteous shines brightly and joyfully, a picture of a life that flourishes. The lamp of the wicked will be put out, leaving his house in darkness and his life without a future.

Verse Explanation

The light of the righteous shines brightly and joyfully, a picture of a life that flourishes. The lamp of the wicked will be put out, leaving his house in darkness and his life without a future.

“The light of the righteous rejoices,”

Light in Proverbs pictures life, safety, and well-being. Here the light of the righteous is said to rejoice, as if the light itself were glad, shining brightly and with joy. The life of the righteous is not grim endurance; it has gladness in it and grows stronger. The father said, “the path of the just is like the shining sun, That shines ever brighter unto the perfect day” (4:18).

“But the lamp of the wicked will be put out.”

A burning lamp in an Israelite house was a sign that the household was alive and continuing. For the lamp of the wicked to go out is to picture his life ending and his house left in darkness, with no future. Proverbs uses the same image for the one who curses his parents: “His lamp will be put out in deep darkness” (20:20). The contrast is between a light that keeps shining with joy and a small flame that will finally be extinguished.

Study Notes

The second line is repeated word for word in 24:20, where it follows “there will be no prospect for the evil man.”

The 1984 NIV renders the first line “shines brightly,” taking “rejoices” as a picture of a bright, lively flame; ESV and NASB keep “rejoices,” and KJV “rejoiceth.”

Proverbs 13:10
Proverbs 13:10
NKJV

Proverbs 13:10 By pride comes nothing but strife, But with the well-advised is wisdom.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:10 Pride leads to conflict;those who take advice are wise. (NLT)

Main Idea

Pride produces nothing but strife, because the proud cannot yield or admit that they might be wrong. Wisdom belongs to the well-advised, those humble enough to seek counsel and take it.

Verse Explanation

Pride produces nothing but strife, because the proud cannot yield or admit that they might be wrong. Wisdom belongs to the well-advised, those humble enough to seek counsel and take it.

“By pride comes nothing but strife,”

Pride produces one crop only, and that crop is strife. The proud person cannot yield, cannot admit he might be wrong, and cannot receive correction, so every disagreement hardens into a quarrel. Pride is close kin to presumption: presuming that I am right, that others owe me, or that God will approve whatever I have decided. When quarrels keep growing, the issue being argued is often not the root; pride is.

“But with the well-advised is wisdom.”

The well-advised are people who take counsel, who talk things through with others and listen. Wisdom is found with them. The implied contrast is humility. The proud will not be told, so they fight; the humble ask and listen, so they grow wise and avoid strife. The line echoes an earlier saying, “When pride comes, then comes shame; But with the humble is wisdom” (11:2), where the humble stand in the place the well-advised stand here. Like the scoffer of 13:1, the proud person closes his ear, and the result is conflict instead of growth.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “pride” here describes insolence or presumption, a self-assertion that will not yield. The same word stands behind “When pride comes” in 11:2, and in Deuteronomy 18:22 a false prophet is said to have spoken “presumptuously” using the same word. ESV and NASB render it “insolence,” and AMP adds “presumption” beside pride.

“Nothing but” renders a Hebrew word meaning only, which KJV places at the start of the line with “Only.”

Proverbs 13:11
Proverbs 13:11
NKJV

Proverbs 13:11 Wealth gained by dishonesty will be diminished, But he who gathers by labor will increase.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:11 Wealth from get-rich-quick schemes quickly disappears;wealth from hard work grows over time. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wealth gained by dishonesty tends to dwindle away. What is gathered steadily by honest labor grows over time, so the way money comes in affects whether it lasts.

Verse Explanation

Wealth gained by dishonesty tends to dwindle away. What is gathered steadily by honest labor grows over time, so the way money comes in affects whether it lasts.

“Wealth gained by dishonesty will be diminished,”

The saying looks at how money comes in. Wealth gained by dishonesty, through fraud, schemes, or getting something for nothing, tends to dwindle. It has no foundation in real work or honest exchange, and it slips away as easily as it came. Proverbs repeats the warning: “An inheritance gained hastily at the beginning Will not be blessed at the end” (20:21).

“But he who gathers by labor will increase.”

The contrast is the person who gathers by labor, adding a little at a time through honest work. His wealth increases. The picture is gradual and steady rather than sudden. The two lines teach that the way money is gained affects whether it lasts. This is a general pattern in God’s ordered world, not a guarantee of wealth to every honest worker, and it sits beside the book’s many warnings against greed and haste (28:20).

Study Notes

The Hebrew of the first line speaks of wealth “from vapor,” using the word familiar from Ecclesiastes as “vanity,” something empty and without substance. KJV reads “by vanity” and YLT “from vanity.” The NKJV and NASB take that emptiness as dishonesty or fraud. ESV’s “gained hastily” reflects a slightly different reading of the Hebrew word, one that the Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon notes has been proposed.

In the second line the Hebrew behind “by labor” is literally “by the hand,” as the margin of the original 1611 KJV notes. The phrase pictures wealth gathered by hand, little by little.

Proverbs 13:12–19 — Hope Fulfilled and the Fountain of Life

Proverbs 13:12
Proverbs 13:12
NKJV

Proverbs 13:12 Hope deferred makes the heart sick, But when the desire comes, it is a tree of life.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:12 Hope deferred makes the heart sick,but a dream fulfilled is a tree of life. (NLT)

Main Idea

The saying honors the real pain of waiting: hope that is put off again and again makes the heart sick. When the longing is finally fulfilled, it is a tree of life, refreshing and renewing, the image Proverbs used for Wisdom herself in 3:18 and one that is finally restored in Christ (Revelation 22:2).

Verse Explanation

The saying honors the real pain of waiting: hope that is put off again and again makes the heart sick. When the longing is finally fulfilled, it is a tree of life, refreshing and renewing, the image Proverbs used for Wisdom herself in 3:18 and one that is finally restored in Christ (Revelation 22:2).

“Hope deferred makes the heart sick,”

Hope that is put off, stretched out and delayed again and again, makes the heart sick. The saying does not scold the person who feels this way or tell him to try harder to be patient. It simply tells the truth about waiting. Long delay wears down the inner person, and Scripture is honest enough to say so. Anyone who has waited years for a prayer to be answered, a child to come home, or a sorrow to lift will recognize the ache this line describes.

“But when the desire comes, it is a tree of life.”

When the longing is finally fulfilled, it is “a tree of life,” refreshing and renewing the one who waited. The image reaches back to Eden, where “The tree of life was also in the midst of the garden” (Genesis 2:9), the life humanity lost when Adam and Eve were driven out. Proverbs first used the image for Wisdom herself: “She is a tree of life to those who take hold of her” (3:18). Here it describes the life that floods back into a heart when a long-delayed hope arrives.

At a deeper level, this is one of the passages where Proverbs invites a layered reading. The tree of life lost in Eden appears again at the end of Scripture, where the risen Christ promises, “To him who overcomes I will give to eat from the tree of life” (Revelation 2:7), and its leaves are “for the healing of the nations” (Revelation 22:2). The deepest hope deferred in the human story is the hope of returning to the life of God, and that desire is fulfilled in Christ.

Study Notes

The word “desire” in 13:12 returns in 13:19, “A desire accomplished is sweet to the soul,” so the two sayings about fulfilled longing frame the third section of the chapter.

“Deferred” translates a Hebrew word meaning drawn out or prolonged, a hope stretched over a long time. YLT renders it “prolonged.”

“Tree of life” appears four times in the NKJV of Proverbs: 3:18; 11:30; 13:12; and 15:4.

Proverbs 13:13
Proverbs 13:13
NKJV

Proverbs 13:13 He who despises the word will be destroyed, But he who fears the commandment will be rewarded.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:13 People who despise advice are asking for trouble;those who respect a command will succeed. (NLT)

Main Idea

How a person treats God’s word decides where he ends. The one who despises the word brings destruction on himself, while the one who fears the commandment and takes it seriously will be rewarded.

Verse Explanation

How a person treats God’s word decides where he ends. The one who despises the word brings destruction on himself, while the one who fears the commandment and takes it seriously will be rewarded.

“He who despises the word will be destroyed,”

The word here, parallel to “the commandment” in the next line, is the instruction God has given and the wise have handed on. To despise it is to treat it as worthless, to hear it and wave it away. The result is destruction, and it is self-inflicted. The person who scorns the word that could have kept him safe brings ruin on himself. This is the scoffer’s attitude of 13:1 carried to its end.

“But he who fears the commandment will be rewarded.”

To fear the commandment is to take it seriously, with the reverence due to the One who gave it. This is the fear of the LORD, a right view of God, showing itself in how a person treats what God says. Such a person “will be rewarded,” repaid with good. The two lines put the matter simply: how a person treats God’s word decides where he ends. The saying stands at the head of a short run of sayings about the life-giving power of instruction (13:13–14).

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “will be destroyed” can also mean to be held in pledge, like property held as security for a debt; the Brown-Driver-Briggs lexicon lists this verse under both meanings. NASB follows the pledge sense with “in debt”: the one who despises the word becomes bound to pay the penalty of what he ignored. ESV, like the NKJV, speaks of destruction.

The margin of the original 1611 KJV offers “in peace” as an alternative to “rewarded,” because the Hebrew verb comes from the same root as shalom, “peace.” The context of repayment favors the NKJV’s rendering.

Proverbs 13:14
Proverbs 13:14
NKJV

Proverbs 13:14 The law of the wise is a fountain of life, To turn one away from the snares of death.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:14 The instruction of the wise is like a life-giving fountain;those who accept it avoid the snares of death. (NLT)

Main Idea

The teaching of the wise is “a fountain of life,” a source that keeps flowing and refreshing. Its purpose is to turn a person away from the snares of death, and 14:27 says the same of the fear of the LORD.

Verse Explanation

The teaching of the wise is “a fountain of life,” a source that keeps flowing and refreshing. Its purpose is to turn a person away from the snares of death, and 14:27 says the same of the fear of the LORD.

“The law of the wise is a fountain of life,”

The “law” here is teaching, the instruction the wise give, which in Proverbs flows from God’s own word (3:1). It is called a fountain of life, a spring of fresh water in a dry land. A fountain does not run out after one drink; it keeps flowing and keeps refreshing everyone who comes to it. The teaching of the wise does the same for those who take it in, giving life again and again.

“To turn one away from the snares of death.”

The second line states the purpose of that teaching. Death is pictured as a hunter who has set snares along the road, hidden traps that catch a person before he sees them. The teaching of the wise turns a person aside before he steps into one. In 14:27 the same words are said of the fear of the LORD: “The fear of the LORD is a fountain of life, To turn one away from the snares of death” (14:27). The teaching of the wise and the fear of the LORD flow from the same source and lead to the same life.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “law” here is torah, which means instruction or teaching. Good news faithfully carried can mend relationships, settle disputes, and refresh those who receive it. A later saying compares a faithful messenger to “the cold of snow in time of harvest” (25:13). The contrast shows how much depends on trustworthiness. The same message can heal or harm depending on who carries it.

“But it is an abomination to fools to depart from evil.”

The second line turns sharply. “Abomination” is a strong word, usually used of what the LORD finds detestable (15:8). Fools may want the sweetness of fulfilled desire as much as anyone, yet refuse to leave the evil that keeps them from it. Companions influence the road a person takes: whoever walks with wise men becomes wise. The one who keeps company with fools will be destroyed with them, because people tend to become like those they walk beside.

“He who walks with wise men will be wise,”

“But the companion of fools will be destroyed.”

The companion of fools comes to harm. People tend to become like those they walk beside, and the one who keeps company with fools shares their road and their end. Paul quotes a similar saying: “Evil company corrupts good habits” (1 Corinthians 15:33). The proverb concerns those whose company influences a person’s path, his close friends and chosen companions. It does not tell the believer to withdraw from all contact with unbelievers or the foolish, but to be careful about whom he allows to lead him.

In Hebrew, the word for “companion” (roʿeh) and the verb behind “will be destroyed” (yeroaʿ) sound alike, so the line plays on their sound: the one who keeps company with fools comes to harm. The margin of the original 1611 KJV gives the literal sense of the verb as “broken,” and ESV and NASB speak of suffering “harm.”

“A good man leaves an inheritance to his children’s children,”

In Israel an inheritance was usually the family’s land, held and passed down from generation to generation as a gift from God. Naboth would not sell his vineyard because it was “the inheritance of my fathers” (1 Kings 21:3). A good man lives in a way that blesses not only his children but his grandchildren. His honest work, careful stewardship, and faithful life leave something that lasts past his own lifetime. That legacy includes material goods, but it also includes a good name and an example of walking with God.

“But the wealth of the sinner is stored up for the righteous.”

The sinner may gather much, but he cannot keep it. His wealth is “stored up,” laid aside, and under God’s rule it eventually passes to others who will use it rightly. Proverbs says the same of the one who gains by extortion: he “Gathers it for him who will pity the poor” (28:8). This is a general reversal under God’s rule, not a promise that believers will receive a transfer of wealth from unbelievers. The righteous do not scheme for the sinner’s money; they simply outlast him, while what he hoarded slips from his hands.

Proverbs 13:24
Proverbs 13:24
NKJV

Proverbs 13:24 He who spares his rod hates his son, But he who loves him disciplines him promptly.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 13:24 Those who spare the rod of discipline hate their children.Those who love their children care enough to discipline them. (NLT)

Main Idea

Withholding correction is not kindness; a father who spares his rod, the ordinary tool of correction in that setting, acts as if he hates his son. Love takes the trouble to discipline him promptly, meaning active, patient training toward the son’s good, as the Father trains His children (Hebrews 12:10–11), never an outlet for anger or harshness (Ephesians 6:4).

Verse Explanation

Withholding correction is not kindness; a father who spares his rod, the ordinary tool of correction in that setting, acts as if he hates his son. Love takes the trouble to discipline him promptly, meaning active, patient training toward the son’s good, as the Father trains His children (Hebrews 12:10–11), never an outlet for anger or harshness (Ephesians 6:4).

“He who spares his rod hates his son,”

To spare the rod is to hold back correction. In Israel’s world the rod was a staff or stick, the ordinary tool of correction in a household, and Proverbs speaks of it in several places (22:15; 29:15). The word “hates” is deliberately strong. A father who never corrects his son may tell himself he is being kind, but in effect he is treating the boy as if he did not care what becomes of him. He leaves the son to a course that will harm him. The contrast is not between a harsh father and a gentle one; it is between love that takes responsibility and neglect that walks away from it. Scripture gives a sobering example in Eli, whose sons did evil “and he did not restrain them” (1 Samuel 3:13).

“But he who loves him disciplines him promptly.”

Love shows itself in action. The Hebrew word for discipline is the one translated “instruction” in 13:1, and it covers the whole work of teaching, guiding, correcting, and training a child toward maturity. Corrective consequences have a place within that work, but punishment alone does not fulfill it. “Promptly” describes a father who is attentive and diligent, who does not wait until a problem has grown large, and who starts early. Loving discipline is active, patient involvement in a child’s life for the child’s good. It explains the right way, sets clear expectations, and gives a child room to practice what is right, while still correcting what is wrong.

This is how God Himself treats His children. “For whom the LORD loves He corrects, Just as a father the son in whom he delights” (3:12). The letter to the Hebrews draws out the purpose of the Father’s training: He disciplines “for our profit, that we may be partakers of His holiness,” and though no chastening seems joyful at the time, “afterward it yields the peaceable fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it” (Hebrews 12:10–11). Earthly parents aim at the same goal: character, wisdom, love for God and neighbor, and growing responsibility, not mere compliance or parental convenience.

The New Testament also sets the manner of discipline. “And you, fathers, do not provoke your children to wrath, but bring them up in the training and admonition of the Lord” (Ephesians 6:4); “Fathers, do not provoke your children, lest they become discouraged” (Colossians 3:21). Paul’s description of how Scripture works, “for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness” (2 Timothy 3:16), offers a helpful pattern for parents: teach the right way, show what is wrong, help make the correction, and train in what is right. That pattern applies the wider teaching of Scripture to parenting; it is not a list that this proverb itself contains. The rod saying never permits harshness, rage, humiliation, or abuse, all of which contradict the goodness of the Father whose discipline it reflects. Nor does it promise how a child will turn out; it describes a parent’s loving responsibility.

Study Notes

“Promptly” translates a Hebrew verb formed from the word for dawn. It carries the sense of seeking early or earnestly, like someone who rises at dawn to attend to a task. KJV renders it “betimes,” ESV “diligent,” and NASB “diligently.” The same verb describes seeking Wisdom: “those who seek me diligently will find me” (8:17).

The author of this study, in his 1997 Child Discipline Workshop, holds that the rod of this verse includes spanking, while discipline itself is far broader than punishment. His teaching places correction inside a whole process of formation: teaching before correcting, clear expectations, explaining the reasons behind a direction, and encouraging good character. He distinguishes deliberate wrongdoing from honest mistakes, lack of understanding, tiredness, and ordinary immaturity, and he counsels restraint, the least force needed, judgment suited to a child’s age, and never correcting in anger.

In that teaching the aim of correction is restorative: a change of heart and direction, not retaliation or the release of a parent’s frustration. Correction is followed by forgiveness and reassurance of love, so that a child is not left under a permanent label of shame, in the spirit of “whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy” (28:13). The workshop also calls parents to admit their own wrongs and ask forgiveness. As practical counsel, not as a command of Proverbs, the workshop also preferred that parents not use the hand.

These pastoral positions should be distinguished from what the verse itself states and from claims about child development or physical safety. Proverbs speaks of the rod in its historical setting and of a parent’s responsibility to train; it does not establish a medically safe technique or guarantee a child’s response. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends nonphysical forms of discipline, and the author’s view is a theological and pastoral position, not a medical consensus.

The chapter begins and ends with eating. In 13:2 a man eats well by the fruit of his mouth, and in 13:25 the righteous eats to the satisfying of his soul, while the stomach of the wicked is left wanting.
