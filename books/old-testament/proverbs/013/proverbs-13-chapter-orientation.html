Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 13
Main Idea:

Proverbs 13 sets the son who heeds instruction against the scoffer who will not hear rebuke, and follows that contrast into speech, money, longing, companions, and a father’s discipline, showing that instruction received is a fountain of life.

Setting:

The chapter continues Solomon’s first collection of two-line sayings (10:1–22:16). Chapter 12 ended on the way of righteousness, where life is found (12:28); chapter 13 begins with the father’s voice that teaches a son to walk it.

Chapter Outline:

  • Proverbs 13:1–6 — The Teachable Son and the Guarded Mouth

  • Proverbs 13:7–11 — Riches, Pride, and Honest Gain

  • Proverbs 13:12–19 — Hope Fulfilled and the Fountain of Life

  • Proverbs 13:20–25 — Companions, Inheritance, and Discipline

Key Verse:

“The law of the wise is a fountain of life, To turn one away from the snares of death.” (Proverbs 13:14)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Proverbs 13 opens on the question of whether a person will listen: “A wise son heeds his father’s instruction, But a scoffer does not listen to rebuke” (13:1). Rebuke reaches the scoffer, yet he refuses to hear it, because his trouble lies in his will (9:7–8). The contrast returns in 13:18, and 13:24 shows instruction from the father’s side, as love that takes the trouble to discipline.

In between, the sayings test whether instruction has taken hold: a mouth that is guarded (13:3), work that turns desire into satisfaction (13:4), money gained honestly instead of quickly (13:11), and friends who make a person wise (13:20). Even poverty is weighed fairly, for some of it comes “for lack of justice” (13:23).

At the center stand two images of life. Hope that drags on “makes the heart sick,” and the saying honors that ache without scolding it; when the longing is met, “it is a tree of life” (13:12). Then the source of life is named. The teaching of the wise is “a fountain of life” (13:14), and the one who “fears the commandment will be rewarded” (13:13). To fear God’s word is to take the LORD Himself seriously, and the chapter ends with the righteous eating “to the satisfying of his soul” (13:25).

Chapter Sections

Proverbs 13:1–6 — The Teachable Son and the Guarded Mouth

The chapter starts with a son who listens and a scoffer who will not, and then shows what listening produces: lips that are watched, a soul that works instead of only wanting, and a righteous hatred of lies. Righteousness itself stands guard over the blameless, while wickedness topples the one who practices it.

Proverbs 13:7–11 — Riches, Pride, and Honest Gain

These sayings look behind the appearance of wealth, where a show of riches can hide an empty purse and plain living can hide great riches, and they note that a fortune can ransom a life while drawing threats the poor never hear. Pride stirs up quarrels that good counsel would have settled, and money gained by dishonesty dwindles, while what is gathered steadily by labor grows.

Proverbs 13:12–19 — Hope Fulfilled and the Fountain of Life

Two sayings about longing frame this section, the sickness of hope delayed and the sweetness of a desire accomplished (13:12, 19). Between them, the word and the teaching of the wise give life to those who take them seriously, and the prudent man, the faithful ambassador, and the one who accepts rebuke all show what that teaching produces.

Proverbs 13:20–25 — Companions, Inheritance, and Discipline

The last section follows a life outward into its friendships and its legacy: the company a person keeps, the inheritance a good man leaves to his grandchildren, and the poor man whose harvest is swept away by injustice. A father’s love shows itself in the work of training and correcting his son, and the chapter closes at the table, where the righteous are satisfied and the wicked go hungry.

Why This Chapter Matters

Proverbs 13 makes teachability the hinge of a life. Those who heed instruction, regard rebuke, and walk with the wise all grow, while the scoffer and the companion of fools are undone by what they refuse and whom they choose (13:1, 18, 20). It also refuses quick judgments about money. Outward show can hide emptiness (13:7), the sinner’s wealth stored up for the righteous describes God’s rule in general rather than a promised transfer of riches (13:22), and some poverty comes from wrongs done to the poor (13:23).

The saying about the rod (13:24) presents discipline as love that takes responsibility instead of neglecting a child. The Father trains His children this way, “for our profit, that we may be partakers of His holiness” (Hebrews 12:10), and fathers are told, “do not provoke your children to wrath” (Ephesians 6:4).

Key Themes to Watch For

Hearing correction. The wise son heeds instruction and the one who regards rebuke is honored, while the scoffer will not listen and the one who disdains correction comes to poverty and shame (13:1, 18).

Guarded speech. Good words feed the speaker, a watched mouth preserves life, and the righteous man hates lying (13:2–3, 5).

The true measure of wealth. Appearances deceive, riches carry dangers of their own, dishonest gain dwindles, and honest labor and a good man’s inheritance last (13:7–8, 11, 22).

Longing and life. Hope delayed and desire accomplished frame the word and the teaching of the wise as a source of life (13:12–14, 19).

Companions and discipline. The people a person walks with and the correction a father gives both influence the road a son takes (13:20, 24).

Key Words & Concepts

The scoffer (13:1). The most stubborn figure in Proverbs’ gallery of fools. He cannot be corrected because he will not be taught (9:7–8), a refusal of the will rather than a lack of ability. The book still holds out hope that any person can turn and become teachable.

The lazy man and the diligent (13:4). The lazy man wants as much as anyone, but desire without work leaves his soul with nothing, while the diligent soul is satisfied. The NKJV uses “lazy man” here for the figure called the sluggard in 6:6–11, and Proverbs never confuses laziness with illness or genuine need.

Pride and the well-advised (13:10). The NKJV reads “pride.” Pride presumes it is right and will not yield, so quarrels follow, while wisdom belongs to those humble enough to ask for counsel and take it.

A tree of life (13:12). The image Proverbs first used for Wisdom (3:18). Here it pictures the renewal that comes when a long-delayed desire is finally met. Read in the light of the whole Bible, the deepest longing of God’s people is answered in Christ, who promises the overcomer “the tree of life” (Revelation 2:7).

The word and the commandment (13:13–14). The instruction God has given, handed on by the wise. To “fear” the commandment is to treat it with the seriousness its Giver deserves. In 14:27 the same words, “a fountain of life,” describe the fear of the LORD.

Promptly (13:24). The loving father does not wait for neglect to let a problem grow; the word may also carry the sense of diligently or early. Discipline in Proverbs is the whole work of training a son toward maturity, and correction is one part of it.
