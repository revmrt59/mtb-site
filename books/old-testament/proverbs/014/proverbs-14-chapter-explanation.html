Proverbs 14

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 14:1
Proverbs 14:1
NKJV

Proverbs 14:1 The wise woman builds her house, But the foolish pulls it down with her hands.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:1 A wise woman builds her home,but a foolish woman tears it down with her own hands. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wisdom builds and folly tears down, pictured here in two women and their homes: the wise woman builds her house, while the foolish one pulls hers down with her own hands. The saying recalls Wisdom building her house in 9:1 and looks ahead to the woman of valor in 31:10–31, and whatever is built in a life bears the signature of the wisdom or folly behind it.

Verse Explanation

Wisdom builds and folly tears down, pictured here in two women and their homes: the wise woman builds her house, while the foolish one pulls hers down with her own hands. The saying recalls Wisdom building her house in 9:1 and looks ahead to the woman of valor in 31:10–31, and whatever is built in a life bears the signature of the wisdom or folly behind it.

“The wise woman builds her house,”

The chapter opens with a woman at work on her household. Her “house” is more than a building; it is the home and family she tends, its peace, its provision, and its future. Her wisdom shows in what she builds. Readers of Proverbs have heard this language before: “Wisdom has built her house” (9:1). The wise woman does in a single home what Wisdom does on a grand scale, and the book will close with a portrait of such a woman, who “watches over the ways of her household” (31:27). Whatever is built in a life, a marriage, a family, finances, a ministry, bears the signature of the wisdom behind it.

“But the foolish pulls it down with her hands.”

The foolish woman also has a house, and she tears it down. No enemy has to break in; she does the damage “with her hands,” through her own words, habits, and choices. The contrast stands within the book’s larger picture of two women and two houses, Wisdom and Folly, each calling to the simple. The saying is not a judgment on women in general, nor does it blame every wife or mother for every troubled home. It holds up a mirror to every reader, man or woman, and asks what his or her own hands are doing to the house God has given.

Study Notes

The Hebrew opens with a plural, literally the wise ones of women, which is why ESV has “wisest” and KJV and YLT add “Every.” The NKJV’s singular gives the same sense: the line describes the kind of woman who builds her house.

The second line in Hebrew begins with the ordinary noun for folly rather than a word for a foolish woman. That is why ESV reads the line as folly itself tearing the house down, a reading that strengthens the echo of Wisdom and Folly in chapter 9.

Proverbs 14:2
Proverbs 14:2
NKJV

Proverbs 14:2 He who walks in his uprightness fears the LORD, But he who is perverse in his ways despises Him.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:2 Those who follow the right path fear the LORD;those who take the wrong path despise him. (NLT)

Main Idea

A person’s daily walk shows what he thinks of God. The one who lives uprightly fears the LORD, while the one whose ways are crooked despises Him, whatever he may claim with his lips.

Verse Explanation

A person’s daily walk shows what he thinks of God. The one who lives uprightly fears the LORD, while the one whose ways are crooked despises Him, whatever he may claim with his lips.

“He who walks in his uprightness fears the LORD,”

A person’s walk is his daily conduct, the way he lives when no one is checking. The one who walks in uprightness, straight and honest in his dealings, fears the LORD. The saying works in both directions. The fear of the LORD, a right view of God, produces upright living, and upright living shows that the fear of the LORD is really there. Reverence for God is not hidden in the heart only; it comes out in the feet.

“But he who is perverse in his ways despises Him.”

The perverse person is crooked and devious in his ways. Whatever he may say about God, his life despises Him. He treats God’s commands and God Himself as if they did not matter. The two lines make a person’s conduct the clearest testimony to what he truly thinks of God. A crooked way of life is not merely a moral failing; at its root it is contempt for the LORD.

Study Notes

ESV and NASB render “perverse” as “devious,” describing a person whose ways turn aside from what is straight.

Proverbs 14:3
Proverbs 14:3
NKJV

Proverbs 14:3 In the mouth of a fool is a rod of pride, But the lips of the wise will preserve them.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:3 A fool’s proud talk becomes a rod that beats him,but the words of the wise keep them safe. (NLT)

Main Idea

In a fool’s mouth pride becomes a rod, and his arrogant words end up bringing punishment on himself. The lips of the wise protect them, because careful speech keeps them out of harm.

Verse Explanation

In a fool’s mouth pride becomes a rod, and his arrogant words end up bringing punishment on himself. The lips of the wise protect them, because careful speech keeps them out of harm.

“In the mouth of a fool is a rod of pride,”

The fool’s mouth holds a rod, and the rod is made of pride. His arrogant, boastful words become a stick that strikes others and eventually brings punishment back on him. Proverbs speaks elsewhere of the rod that falls on the fool: “a rod is for the back of him who is devoid of understanding” (10:13). Here the fool supplies that rod himself. His proud talk is the very thing that will bring correction down on his own back.

“But the lips of the wise will preserve them.”

The wise also have mouths, but their lips protect them. Careful, humble speech keeps them out of the trouble that the fool’s pride invites. The contrast continues the theme of guarded speech from 13:3. The fool is undone by what he says; the wise are kept safe by what they say and by what they refrain from saying.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “rod” here is not the word for rod used in 13:24. It is a different word that refers to a shoot or branch rather than a staff, so the fool’s proud words are pictured as a stick that grows out of his own mouth.

Several modern translations read the first line as a rod for the fool’s “back” (ESV, NASB), following a slightly different understanding of the Hebrew. The NKJV, with KJV and YLT, reads “a rod of pride.”

Proverbs 14:4
Proverbs 14:4
NKJV

Proverbs 14:4 Where no oxen are, the trough is clean; But much increase comes by the strength of an ox.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:4 Without oxen a stable stays clean,but you need a strong ox for a large harvest. (NLT)

Main Idea

Where there are no oxen, the feeding trough stays clean, but much increase comes by the strength of an ox. Oxen must be fed and cleaned up after, yet their strength makes a far larger harvest possible, and wisdom accepts the costs that come with productive work.

Verse Explanation

Where there are no oxen, the feeding trough stays clean, but much increase comes by the strength of an ox. Oxen must be fed and cleaned up after, yet their strength makes a far larger harvest possible, and wisdom accepts the costs that come with productive work.

“Where no oxen are, the trough is clean;”

A farmer without oxen has a clean feeding trough. There is no feed to buy, no stall to muck out, no animal to tend. The line is a plain observation from farm life, and it is meant to be smiled at: the cleanest barn belongs to the farmer who is not producing much.

“But much increase comes by the strength of an ox.”

The second line gives the other side. Oxen are messy and expensive, but their strength pulls the plow and brings in a much larger harvest. The saying teaches that productive work carries costs, and wisdom accepts them. A person who avoids every mess and every expense may keep things tidy, but he will also have little to show. The proverb fits far beyond farming: any household, business, or ministry that grows will have its untidy and costly side, and the wise do not mistake a clean trough for success.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “clean” can also mean empty, and the 1984 NIV renders it “empty”: without oxen the manger has no grain in it. Either way the picture is of a farm that produces little. ESV, NASB, and KJV read “manger” or “crib” for “trough.”

Proverbs 14:5
Proverbs 14:5
NKJV

Proverbs 14:5 A faithful witness does not lie, But a false witness will utter lies.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:5 An honest witness does not lie;a false witness breathes lies. (NLT)

Main Idea

Truth can be expected from a faithful witness. A false witness pours out lies, so the character of each shows itself in what he says when the truth matters most.

Verse Explanation

Truth can be expected from a faithful witness. A false witness pours out lies, so the character of each shows itself in what he says when the truth matters most.

“A faithful witness does not lie,”

The setting is the court, where justice depended on the testimony of witnesses. A faithful witness is a reliable one, and his reliability shows itself in the simplest way: he does not lie. When the truth matters most and lying might help a friend or harm an enemy, he tells what he knows. The Law forbade false witness (Exodus 20:16), and Proverbs treats honest testimony as a mark of character.

“But a false witness will utter lies.”

The false witness pours out lies as naturally as breathing. Among the things the LORD hates is “A false witness who speaks lies” (6:19). The two lines are close to saying the same thing twice, and that is the point: what a person is shows in what he says. A faithful man produces true testimony and a false man produces lies, and the court, and everyone else, can know the tree by its fruit. The theme returns at 14:25, where true testimony is shown to save lives.

Study Notes

The Hebrew behind “will utter lies” is literally breathes out lies, as ESV and YLT render it, picturing lies that come as easily as breath. Proverbs uses the same Hebrew expression in 6:19, 14:25, 19:5, and 19:9, where the NKJV has “speaks lies.”

Proverbs 14:6
Proverbs 14:6
NKJV

Proverbs 14:6 A scoffer seeks wisdom and does not find it, But knowledge is easy to him who understands.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:6 A mocker seeks wisdom and never finds it,but knowledge comes easily to those with understanding. (NLT)

Main Idea

The scoffer may go looking for wisdom, but his contempt for correction keeps him from ever finding it. To the person who understands, knowledge comes easily, because a teachable heart opens the door that scorn keeps shut.

Verse Explanation

The scoffer may go looking for wisdom, but his contempt for correction keeps him from ever finding it. To the person who understands, knowledge comes easily, because a teachable heart opens the door that scorn keeps shut.

“A scoffer seeks wisdom and does not find it,”

The scoffer may actually go looking for wisdom. He may want the advantages it brings or the reputation of being wise. Yet he does not find it. The reason lies in what he is. Wisdom begins with the fear of the LORD and grows through correction, and the scoffer has contempt for both (13:1). He wants wisdom on his own terms, without humility, and on those terms it cannot be had.

“But knowledge is easy to him who understands.”

To the person with understanding, knowledge comes easily. He is teachable, so each lesson builds on the last. The contrast is not between a clever person and a dull one. It is between a heart that is open and a heart that is closed. The scoffer’s search is endless and empty because his scorn shuts the door; the understanding person finds the door already open.

Study Notes

The NKJV supplies “find it” in the first line; the Hebrew says simply that the scoffer seeks wisdom and there is none, as YLT shows. ESV reads “in vain.”

Proverbs 14:7
Proverbs 14:7
NKJV

Proverbs 14:7 Go from the presence of a foolish man, When you do not perceive in him the lips of knowledge.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:7 Stay away from fools,for you won’t find knowledge on their lips. (NLT)

Main Idea

When a man’s lips show no knowledge, the son is told to leave his presence. Staying near such a fool brings nothing good, so wisdom knows when to walk away.

Verse Explanation

When a man’s lips show no knowledge, the son is told to leave his presence. Staying near such a fool brings nothing good, so wisdom knows when to walk away.

“Go from the presence of a foolish man,”

The saying is a command, the father’s voice speaking directly to his son. Leave the fool’s company. The fool here is the one whose life and talk are set against wisdom. Staying near him will teach nothing good and may draw the son into his ways, as 13:20 warned: “the companion of fools will be destroyed.”

“When you do not perceive in him the lips of knowledge.”

The second line gives the reason and the test. When a man’s words show no knowledge, when a son has listened long enough to see that nothing true or wise is coming, it is time to go. Wisdom does not require endless argument with someone who will not learn. It knows when to walk away. The verse closes the first section with practical counsel about the company a person keeps.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of the second line is compressed, and translations handle it differently. NASB reads it as a consequence of staying, warning that the son will not discern words of knowledge, and ESV as a reason for leaving. The NKJV, with KJV, makes it a condition: leave when you see no knowledge in him.

Proverbs 14:8–15 — The Way That Seems Right

Proverbs 14:8
Proverbs 14:8
NKJV

Proverbs 14:8 The wisdom of the prudent is to understand his way, But the folly of fools is deceit.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:8 The prudent understand where they are going,but fools deceive themselves. (NLT)

Main Idea

The prudent man’s wisdom lies in understanding his own way, thinking about where his steps are taking him. Fools are marked by deceit, misleading others and themselves about the road they are on.

Verse Explanation

The prudent man’s wisdom lies in understanding his own way, thinking about where his steps are taking him. Fools are marked by deceit, misleading others and themselves about the road they are on.

“The wisdom of the prudent is to understand his way,”

The prudent person’s wisdom is pointed at his own path. He thinks about where his steps are taking him, why he is going that way, and where the road ends. This is wisdom turned inward, the honest self-examination that keeps a person from drifting. It opens a section of the chapter that keeps returning to the idea of a way and its end (14:12, 14, 15).

“But the folly of fools is deceit.”

Fools are marked by deceit. They mislead others, and, more dangerously, they mislead themselves about the road they are on. The contrast with the first line suggests that self-deception is especially in view: where the prudent man looks honestly at his way, the fool tells himself a comfortable story. That is how a person can walk confidently down a path that ends in death, the warning that comes four verses later (14:12).

Study Notes

The NLT draws out the self-deception the contrast implies: “fools deceive themselves.” ESV renders the last word “deceiving.”

Proverbs 14:9
Proverbs 14:9
NKJV

Proverbs 14:9 Fools mock at sin, But among the upright there is favor.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:9 Fools make fun of guilt,but the godly acknowledge it and seek reconciliation. (NLT)

Main Idea

Fools treat sin as a joke and mock at the guilt it brings. Among the upright there is favor, the goodwill that grows where people deal honestly and make things right.

Verse Explanation

Fools treat sin as a joke and mock at the guilt it brings. Among the upright there is favor, the goodwill that grows where people deal honestly and make things right.

“Fools mock at sin,”

Fools treat sin as a joke. They laugh off wrongdoing and the guilt it brings, and they make light of any effort to make things right. Mockery of this kind stands in the way of the very things that would help: honestly admitting the wrong and making amends for it. The fool’s laughter keeps him exactly where he is.

“But among the upright there is favor.”

Among the upright there is favor, goodwill and acceptance. Upright people take wrongdoing seriously, admit their faults, and make amends, and so relationships among them can be restored. The contrast is between a company where guilt is laughed off and trust erodes and a company where guilt is faced and goodwill grows. Favor here may include God’s favor as well as human goodwill, for the upright are “His delight” (12:22).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “sin” here is the word used for the guilt offering, the sacrifice brought to make amends for wrongdoing. ESV and YLT render it as the guilt offering, and the 1984 NIV speaks of making amends for sin. Read that way, the fool mocks the very means of setting things right.

The Hebrew of the first line is grammatically unusual: the subject, “fools,” is plural, but the verb “mock” is singular. Because of this, the translators’ notes in the NET Bible discuss a proposal to reverse the line so that the guilt offering is the subject and it mocks fools. The NKJV, like the other translations compared here, keeps fools as the ones who mock.

Proverbs 14:10
Proverbs 14:10
NKJV

Proverbs 14:10 The heart knows its own bitterness, And a stranger does not share its joy.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:10 Each heart knows its own bitterness,and no one else can fully share its joy. (NLT)

Main Idea

Every heart knows its own bitterness in a way no one else can fully share, and its deepest joys are just as private. The saying recognizes an inner life that others cannot see, which calls for compassion and humility in judging another person.

Verse Explanation

Every heart knows its own bitterness in a way no one else can fully share, and its deepest joys are just as private. The saying recognizes an inner life that others cannot see, which calls for compassion and humility in judging another person.

“The heart knows its own bitterness,”

Every heart carries griefs that only it fully knows. Others may see the outward circumstances, but the bitterness itself, the particular weight of a loss or a wound, is known from the inside. The saying does not tell a person to keep his sorrows to himself; it simply recognizes that there is an inner life no one else can completely enter.

“And a stranger does not share its joy.”

The same is true of joy. The deepest gladness of a heart cannot be fully handed to someone else. A stranger, someone outside, can rejoice with a person but cannot feel exactly what he feels. The verse calls for humility in judging others, since we never see the whole of another person’s heart, and for compassion, since everyone carries more than is visible. Only the One who searches hearts knows them completely (1 Samuel 16:7).

Study Notes

Behind “its own bitterness” the Hebrew reads literally the bitterness of its soul, describing grief felt at the deepest level of a person. In the second line KJV and YLT say a stranger does not “intermeddle” with the heart’s joy.

Proverbs 14:11
Proverbs 14:11
NKJV

Proverbs 14:11 The house of the wicked will be overthrown, But the tent of the upright will flourish.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:11 The house of the wicked will be destroyed,but the tent of the godly will flourish. (NLT)

Main Idea

The wicked may live in a house that looks solid, but it will be overthrown. The upright may live in a mere tent, yet it will flourish, so outward strength is no measure of what will last.

Verse Explanation

The wicked may live in a house that looks solid, but it will be overthrown. The upright may live in a mere tent, yet it will flourish, so outward strength is no measure of what will last.

“The house of the wicked will be overthrown,”

The wicked man lives in a house, a solid, permanent dwelling that seems built to last. Yet it will be overthrown. The saying looks past the present appearance to the end. Wickedness undermines what it builds, and a house founded on it cannot stand. An earlier saying put it the same way: “The wicked are overthrown and are no more, But the house of the righteous will stand” (12:7).

“But the tent of the upright will flourish.”

The upright may live in a mere tent, a modest and temporary dwelling, and yet it will flourish. The word “flourish” pictures something growing and blossoming. The irony is deliberate: the stronger-looking dwelling falls and the weaker-looking one thrives. Outward strength is no measure of what will last. The saying continues the chapter’s interest in houses that began in 14:1.

Study Notes

KJV renders “tent” as “tabernacle,” the older English word for a tent.

Proverbs 14:12
Proverbs 14:12
NKJV

Proverbs 14:12 There is a way that seems right toa man, But its end is the way of death.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:12 There is a path before each person that seems right,but it ends in death. (NLT)

Main Idea

A path can seem right to a man and still end in death. Sincerity is no guarantee of safety, and the same saying returns word for word at 16:25, a warning Jesus echoes when He speaks of the broad way that leads to destruction (Matthew 7:13).

Verse Explanation

A path can seem right to a man and still end in death. Sincerity is no guarantee of safety, and the same saying returns word for word at 16:25, a warning Jesus echoes when He speaks of the broad way that leads to destruction (Matthew 7:13).

“There is a way that seems right to a man,”

The saying begins with a road that looks good. It seems right, straight and sensible from where a man is standing. He is not consciously choosing evil; he is following what feels right to him. That is what makes the way so dangerous. A path chosen by human judgment alone, apart from God’s word, can look entirely reasonable and still be wrong.

“But its end is the way of death.”

The end of that road is death. Sincerity is no guarantee of safety, and a person can be sincerely mistaken all the way to destruction. The saying sits at the center of a section about ways and steps (14:8–15), and its warning explains why the prudent must understand their way (14:8) and consider their steps (14:15). The same saying returns word for word in 16:25, so Proverbs states this warning twice. The verse does not dismiss conscience; it warns against trusting one’s own sense of what is right apart from God. Jesus spoke of the same danger in His picture of the broad way, “wide is the gate and broad is the way that leads to destruction” (Matthew 7:13).

Study Notes

Proverbs 16:25 repeats this verse word for word in the NKJV.

The Hebrew behind “seems right” is literally straight before a man, that is, straight in his own eyes. “Its end” translates a Hebrew word for the latter end or outcome, the same word used in 14:13. The Hebrew also has a plural, ways of death, which KJV and YLT preserve.

Proverbs 14:13
Proverbs 14:13
NKJV

Proverbs 14:13 Even in laughter the heart may sorrow, And the end of mirth may be grief.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:13 Laughter can conceal a heavy heart,but when the laughter ends, the grief remains. (NLT)

Main Idea

Laughter can cover a sorrowing heart, and mirth can end in grief. Outward cheer is no proof that all is well inside, which is another reason to treat others with care.

Verse Explanation

Laughter can cover a sorrowing heart, and mirth can end in grief. Outward cheer is no proof that all is well inside, which is another reason to treat others with care.

“Even in laughter the heart may sorrow,”

Laughter is not always what it seems. A person can laugh with friends while his heart aches. Outward cheer may cover private grief, and those around him may never know. The saying, like 14:10, recognizes the hidden inner life and warns against assuming that a smiling face means all is well.

“And the end of mirth may be grief.”

Mirth itself can end in grief. Joy in this life is mixed and temporary; the party ends and the sorrow that waited outside comes back in. The verse is not cynical about joy, and Proverbs elsewhere praises “A merry heart” (15:13). It is honest about the limits of earthly happiness. Read with 14:10, it calls for compassion toward others, whose laughter may be covering more than we know, and for realism about one’s own heart.

Study Notes

“End” here translates the same Hebrew word for the latter end or outcome used in 14:12, a verbal link between the way that ends in death and the mirth that ends in grief.

Proverbs 14:14
Proverbs 14:14
NKJV

Proverbs 14:14 The backslider in heart will be filled with his own ways, But a good man will be satisfied from above.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:14 Backsliders get what they deserve;good people receive their reward. (NLT)

Main Idea

The backslider in heart, the one who has turned away inwardly, will be filled with the fruit of his own ways and get exactly what he chose. A good man is satisfied too, but with what comes from above.

Verse Explanation

The backslider in heart, the one who has turned away inwardly, will be filled with the fruit of his own ways and get exactly what he chose. A good man is satisfied too, but with what comes from above.

“The backslider in heart will be filled with his own ways,”

The backslider in heart is a person who has turned away inwardly from God and from what is right. His outward life may not yet show it, but his heart has already gone back. The judgment on him is fitting: he “will be filled with his own ways.” He gets exactly what he chose. The ways he preferred to God’s become his whole diet, and he will have his fill of them, along with their consequences.

“But a good man will be satisfied from above.”

A good man is satisfied too, but the source of his satisfaction is different. The NKJV’s “from above” points beyond the man himself to the good that comes to him from God. The two lines set two kinds of fullness side by side. One person is stuffed with the fruit of his own turning away; the other is filled with good that he did not have to produce for himself. Both reap what their lives have sown, and the contrast lies in what each has chosen to live on.

Study Notes

The final phrase in Hebrew is brief, literally from upon him. KJV renders it “from himself,” and NASB and ESV take it to mean that the good man is filled with the fruit of his own good ways, matching the first line. The NKJV’s “from above” is an interpretive rendering that names God as the source.

“Backslider” translates a Hebrew word for one who turns back or turns away; “in heart” shows that the turning has happened inwardly.

Proverbs 14:15
Proverbs 14:15
NKJV

Proverbs 14:15 The simple believes every word, But the prudent considers well his steps.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:15 Only simpletons believe everything they’re told!The prudent carefully consider their steps. (NLT)

Main Idea

The simple, uncommitted and easily led, believe every word they hear. The prudent consider their steps with care and test what they hear before acting on it, which is the very prudence Proverbs was written to give the simple (1:4).

Verse Explanation

The simple, uncommitted and easily led, believe every word they hear. The prudent consider their steps with care and test what they hear before acting on it, which is the very prudence Proverbs was written to give the simple (1:4).

“The simple believes every word,”

The simple person in Proverbs is uncommitted and easily led. He has not set himself against wisdom, but he has not committed himself to it either, and so he believes whatever he hears. Every word sounds convincing to him, the salesman’s pitch, the gossip’s story, the tempter’s promise. His credulity is not innocence; it leaves him exposed to every voice that wants to lead him somewhere.

“But the prudent considers well his steps.”

The prudent person tests what he hears before acting on it. He considers where each step will lead, much like the prudent man of 14:8 who understands his way. The contrast is between drifting and walking with care. Proverbs holds out real hope for the simple, for the book was written “To give prudence to the simple” (1:4). The section ends where it began, with the need to look carefully at one’s path, which is how a person avoids the way that only seems right (14:12).

Study Notes

ESV and NASB render “every word” as “everything.” KJV says the prudent man “looketh well” to his going.

Proverbs 14:16–25 — Prudence, Mercy to the Poor, and a True Witness

Proverbs 14:16
Proverbs 14:16
NKJV

Proverbs 14:16 A wise man fears and departs from evil, But a fool rages and is self-confident.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:16 The wise are cautious and avoid danger;fools plunge ahead with reckless confidence. (NLT)

Main Idea

A wise man fears evil and turns away from it. The fool charges ahead, raging and sure of himself, blind to the danger he refuses to fear.

Verse Explanation

A wise man fears evil and turns away from it. The fool charges ahead, raging and sure of himself, blind to the danger he refuses to fear.

“A wise man fears and departs from evil,”

The wise man is cautious about evil. He takes its danger seriously, and that healthy fear leads him to turn away from it. Proverbs joins the two elsewhere: “Fear the LORD and depart from evil” (3:7). The wise do not test how close they can come to sin without being harmed; they see where it leads and keep their distance.

“But a fool rages and is self-confident.”

The fool does the opposite. He “rages,” charging ahead with angry, careless energy, and he is self-confident, sure that nothing bad will happen to him. His confidence is not courage; it is blindness to a danger he refuses to fear. The contrast sets humility that sees danger against arrogance that ignores it. This saying, which opens a new section, returns to the theme of fear: the right kind of fear makes a person safe, and the lack of it makes him reckless.

Study Notes

In this form the Hebrew verb behind “rages” can mean to be arrogant or to become furious. Here it stands opposite the wise man’s caution: the fool pushes ahead in proud self-assurance. YLT renders it “transgressing,” NASB “arrogant,” and ESV “reckless.” The 1984 NIV supplies “the Lord” as the object of the wise man’s fear in the first line, while the other translations leave the object unstated.

Proverbs 14:17
Proverbs 14:17
NKJV

Proverbs 14:17 A quick-tempered man acts foolishly, And a man of wicked intentions is hated.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 14:17 Short-tempered people do foolish things,and schemers are hated. (NLT)

Main Idea

Acting in the heat of the moment, a quick-tempered man does foolish things. The man who plots wickedness in cold blood is hated, so both hot anger and calculated malice are condemned.

Verse Explanation

Acting in the heat of the moment, a quick-tempered man does foolish things. The man who plots wickedness in cold blood is hated, so both hot anger and calculated malice are condemned.

“A quick-tempered man acts foolishly,”

The quick-tempered man flares up and does foolish things in the heat of the moment. His anger runs ahead of his judgment, and he says and does what he will later regret. Proverbs repeatedly praises the opposite quality, being “slow to anger” (15:18), and this saying shows why. Anger that comes quickly leaves no room for wisdom to speak.

“And a man of wicked intentions is hated.”

The second man is different. He is not hot-tempered but calculating, a man who plots and schemes. People come to hate him, because his malice is deliberate. The two lines are not a simple contrast between good and bad; they set two kinds of wrongdoing side by side. One sins in the heat of the moment, the other in cold blood, and both are condemned. If anything, the second is worse, for the quick-tempered man is called foolish while the schemer is hated.

Study Notes

“Quick-tempered” is literally short of nostrils in Hebrew. The same Hebrew word means both nostrils and anger, so the phrase describes anger that flares quickly. Its opposite appears in 14:29, where “slow to wrath” is literally long of nostrils.

The Hebrew word behind “wicked intentions” means schemes or plans. It can be used positively, as in 1:4, where the NKJV translates it “discretion,” but here it describes plotting.

“The king’s favor is toward a wise servant,”

A king looks favorably on a servant who acts wisely. In the royal court, skill, good judgment, and faithfulness were noticed and rewarded. The saying applies the book’s teaching about wisdom to the world of work and service under authority. A wise servant brings honor to his master and receives honor in return.

“But his wrath is against him who causes shame.”

The king’s anger falls on the servant who causes shame, the one who acts foolishly or dishonestly and so disgraces his master. The chapter that began with a wise woman building her house closes with a wise servant who wins his king’s favor. Faithfulness and good sense matter in every place of service. As a canonical comparison, Jesus’ parable of the talents pictures a master who says, “Well done, good and faithful servant” (Matthew 25:21).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “wise” here describes a servant who acts with insight and prudence, the kind of good sense that serves a master well. ESV and NASB read “deals wisely” or “acts wisely.”
