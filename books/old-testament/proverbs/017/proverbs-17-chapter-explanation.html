Proverbs 17

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 17:1
Proverbs 17:1
NKJV

Proverbs 17:1 Better is a dry morsel with quietness, Than a house full of feasting with strife.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:1 Better a dry crust eaten in peacethan a house filled with feasting — and conflict. (NLT)

Main Idea

A plain, dry meal eaten in peace is better than a house full of feasting where people quarrel. The chapter opens by setting quietness above abundance, since plenty cannot make up for a home torn by strife.

Verse Explanation

A plain, dry meal eaten in peace is better than a house full of feasting where people quarrel. The chapter opens by setting quietness above abundance, since plenty cannot make up for a home torn by strife.

“Better is a dry morsel with quietness,”

A “dry morsel” is a crust of bread with nothing to soften it: no oil, no wine, no stew to dip it in. It is the plain fare of a poor or simple table. Yet the saying sets it beside “quietness,” the settled ease of a home where no one is bracing for the next argument. The bread is not praised for being dry, and poverty is not held up as a virtue. What gives this meal its worth is the peace around it.

“Than a house full of feasting with strife.”

The other house has everything a table could hold. The word behind “feasting” is the ordinary Hebrew word for sacrifices, which points to the meat of offerings that a worshiper and his household shared at a celebration meal. Such a house might look both prosperous and devout. Then the line ends on the two words that undo it all: “with strife.” Quarreling spoils the abundance, and even a religious feast is no compensation for a home at war with itself. Proverbs has weighed peace against plenty before, but this opening saying adds that outward devotion cannot make up for strife either. The chapter will come back to strife in 17:14 and 17:19.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “feasting” is zebach, the ordinary word for a sacrifice, especially the kind of sacrifice whose meat was eaten at a feast. The KJV keeps the literal word “sacrifices” in its text, with a margin reading “good cheer.” Most modern versions, like the NKJV, render the word “feasting,” since a sacrificial meal was the great feast of an ordinary household. The law describes the worshiper eating the meat of his peace offering: “The flesh of the sacrifice of his peace offering for thanksgiving shall be eaten the same day it is offered” (Leviticus 7:15).

Two earlier “better” sayings make a similar comparison: “Better is a little with the fear of the LORD, Than great treasure with trouble” (15:16) and “Better is a dinner of herbs where love is, Than a fatted calf with hatred” (15:17).

Proverbs 17:2
Proverbs 17:2
NKJV

Proverbs 17:2 A wise servant will rule over a son who causes shame, And will share an inheritance among the brothers.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:2 A wise servant will rule over the master’s disgraceful sonand will share the inheritance of the master’s children. (NLT)

Main Idea

Character can overturn the expected order in a household: a wise servant may come to rule over a son who brings shame, and even share the inheritance with the brothers. Birth does not guarantee a son’s place, and faithful wisdom can win what shameful conduct throws away.

Verse Explanation

Character can overturn the expected order in a household: a wise servant may come to rule over a son who brings shame, and even share the inheritance with the brothers. Birth does not guarantee a son’s place, and faithful wisdom can win what shameful conduct throws away.

“A wise servant will rule over a son who causes shame,”

In an Israelite household the son was the heir and the servant worked under the family, so the expected order placed the son above the servant. This saying reverses that order. The servant is “wise,” prudent and capable in the way he handles affairs, while the son is one “who causes shame,” disgracing his family by the way he lives. Over time the faithful servant can come to manage the household and even hold authority over the son who has wasted his position.

“And will share an inheritance among the brothers.”

The second line goes further. The servant not only rules; he receives a portion of the family estate as though he were one of the brothers. Scripture shows that such a thing could happen. Before Isaac was born, Abram expected his property to pass to a servant: “the heir of my house is Eliezer of Damascus” (Genesis 15:2). The proverb is not a rule of inheritance law, and it does not promise that every faithful servant will be rewarded this way. It observes that birth gives a person a place, but character determines whether he keeps it. A son can throw away by shameful living what a servant gains by wise and faithful service.

Study Notes

The phrase “a son who causes shame” also describes the lazy son of harvest time: “He who sleeps in harvest is a son who causes shame” (10:5). A similar phrase describes the son who mistreats his parents in 19:26.

Proverbs 17:3
Proverbs 17:3
NKJV

Proverbs 17:3 The refining pot is for silver and the furnace for gold, But the LORD tests the hearts.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:3 Fire tests the purity of silver and gold,but the LORD tests the heart. (NLT)

Main Idea

Silver is proved in the refining pot and gold in the furnace, and in the same way the LORD tests human hearts. Only He can examine the inner person, and His testing brings out what is really there and purifies it, as fire does with precious metal.

Verse Explanation

Silver is proved in the refining pot and gold in the furnace, and in the same way the LORD tests human hearts. Only He can examine the inner person, and His testing brings out what is really there and purifies it, as fire does with precious metal.

“The refining pot is for silver and the furnace for gold,”

The first line comes from a metalworker’s shop. Silver was heated in a refining pot, or crucible, and gold in a furnace. The heat did two things at once: it separated the dross from the metal, and it proved whether the metal was genuine. The metals in view are silver and gold, precious materials worth the refiner’s effort.

“But the LORD tests the hearts.”

The word “But” turns from the workshop to God. What fire does for metal, the LORD does for “the hearts,” the inner person with its thoughts, motives, and desires. Human refiners can test silver and gold, but no human tool can reach a heart. Only the LORD can examine it, and He does. His testing brings out what is really there, and it purifies, as the refiner’s fire does. The picture is of a refiner who values what he works with and tests it to prove and purify it. Not every hardship a person faces is God testing him for sin; the verse speaks of God’s searching knowledge and refining work. Set near the start of a chapter about households, friendships, and courts, the saying places every relationship that follows under the eyes of the LORD who sees the heart.

Study Notes

The first line is repeated word for word at 27:21, where it is completed differently: “And a man is valued by what others say of him” (27:21). There praise becomes the test that reveals a person.

The Hebrew verb behind “tests” is bachan, which means to examine something closely in order to prove what it really is. It is used elsewhere for God’s examination of people, as in “For the righteous God tests the hearts and minds” (Psalm 7:9). Here the word links the refiner’s fire in the first line with God’s searching of the heart in the second. The image differs from 16:2, where the LORD “weighs the spirits” as on a scale; here He tests them as with fire.

Proverbs 17:4
Proverbs 17:4
NKJV

Proverbs 17:4 An evildoer gives heed to false lips; A liar listens eagerly to a spiteful tongue.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:4 Wrongdoers eagerly listen to gossip;liars pay close attention to slander. (NLT)

Main Idea

People are drawn to words that match their own hearts: the evildoer pays attention to false lips, and the liar listens eagerly to a spiteful tongue. What a person enjoys hearing exposes what he is, so a taste for slander is itself a mark of wickedness.

Verse Explanation

People are drawn to words that match their own hearts: the evildoer pays attention to false lips, and the liar listens eagerly to a spiteful tongue. What a person enjoys hearing exposes what he is, so a taste for slander is itself a mark of wickedness.

“An evildoer gives heed to false lips;”

Proverbs often warns against the person who speaks lies. This saying turns to the person who listens. An “evildoer” is someone who habitually does wrong, and he “gives heed” to “false lips,” paying close attention to deceptive talk. He listens because the lies serve him. They confirm his suspicions, feed his schemes, or excuse his own conduct. Falsehood needs an audience to spread, and he supplies one.

“A liar listens eagerly to a spiteful tongue.”

The second line sharpens the first. The listener is now called “a liar,” and he “listens eagerly,” leaning in to catch every word of “a spiteful tongue,” speech meant to wound and ruin. The pairing is deliberate. A man who deals in lies enjoys hearing malicious words because they match his own heart. What a person loves to hear reveals what he is. The verse makes the listener responsible as well as the speaker, and it exposes the appetite that keeps slander moving from mouth to ear. The LORD who tests the hearts in 17:3 sees not only what people say but what they are eager to hear.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 17:5
Proverbs 17:5
NKJV

Proverbs 17:5 He who mocks the poor reproaches his Maker; He who is glad at calamity will not go unpunished.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:5 Those who mock the poor insult their Maker;those who rejoice at the misfortune of others will be punished. (NLT)

Main Idea

Mocking the poor is an insult aimed at God, because the poor man bears the work of his Maker (compare 14:31). The second line widens the warning to anyone who is glad when disaster strikes another; that kind of cruelty will not go unpunished.

Verse Explanation

Mocking the poor is an insult aimed at God, because the poor man bears the work of his Maker (compare 14:31). The second line widens the warning to anyone who is glad when disaster strikes another; that kind of cruelty will not go unpunished.

“He who mocks the poor reproaches his Maker;”

To “mock” the poor is to laugh at them, ridicule their condition, or treat them with contempt. The saying declares that this “reproaches his Maker.” It insults the God who made that poor person. The LORD made rich and poor alike, as Proverbs says elsewhere: “The LORD is the maker of them all” (22:2). Contempt for the poor therefore lands on Him. An earlier saying spoke of the one who “oppresses” the poor (14:31); this one shows that no money or rights need be taken. Ridicule alone is enough to insult God.

“He who is glad at calamity will not go unpunished.”

The second line moves from contempt to gloating. “Calamity” is disaster, the day ruin falls on someone. A person can be “glad” about it without saying a word, quietly pleased when a rival or an unfortunate neighbor suffers. The verse declares that such a person “will not go unpunished,” which implies that God takes notice and will hold him to account. The two lines describe the same kind of heart, one that lifts itself by looking down on others, first on their poverty and then on their pain. The God who tests hearts sees the secret gladness as clearly as the open sneer.

Study Notes

Proverbs gives a direct command on the same subject: “Do not rejoice when your enemy falls, And do not let your heart be glad when he stumbles” (24:17).

Proverbs 17:6
Proverbs 17:6
NKJV

Proverbs 17:6 Children's children are the crown of old men, And the glory of children is their father.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:6 Grandchildren are the crowning glory of the aged;parents are the pride of their children. (NLT)

Main Idea

Grandchildren are a crown that honors the old, and children find their glory in their father. The saying pictures a family in which honor flows both ways across the generations, each giving the other something to be proud of.

Verse Explanation

Grandchildren are a crown that honors the old, and children find their glory in their father. The saying pictures a family in which honor flows both ways across the generations, each giving the other something to be proud of.

“Children’s children are the crown of old men,”

A “crown” is a mark of honor worn where everyone can see it. For the old, grandchildren are that crown. In Israel, living to see a third generation was counted a blessing from God; Psalm 128 closes with the wish, “Yes, may you see your children’s children” (Psalm 128:6). A growing family meant that a man’s name and household would continue after him. The line assumes a family in which the younger generation brings credit to the older.

“And the glory of children is their father.”

The second line runs in the other direction. Children take pride in their father. His name, character, and reputation are their “glory,” something they can hold their heads up about. Together the two lines picture honor flowing both down and up the generations, each giving the other reason to be glad. The second line also carries a quiet expectation: a father ought to live in a way his children are glad to claim. That prepares for the later sayings in this chapter, at 17:21 and 17:25, where a foolish son brings his parents sorrow instead of joy. The proverb describes God’s good design for families. It does not measure the worth of those who have no grandchildren or whose fathers gave them little to be proud of.

Proverbs 17:7
Proverbs 17:7
NKJV

Proverbs 17:7 Excellent speech is not becoming to a fool, Much less lying lips to a prince.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:7 Eloquent words are not fitting for a fool;even less are lies fitting for a ruler. (NLT)

Main Idea

Fine, noble speech does not suit a fool, and lying lips suit a ruler far less. The argument moves from the lesser to the greater: if eloquence seems out of place in a fool’s mouth, falsehood is even more out of place in one entrusted with leading others.

Verse Explanation

Fine, noble speech does not suit a fool, and lying lips suit a ruler far less. The argument moves from the lesser to the greater: if eloquence seems out of place in a fool’s mouth, falsehood is even more out of place in one entrusted with leading others.

“Excellent speech is not becoming to a fool,”

“Excellent speech” is lofty, polished language, the kind of words that belong to a person of dignity. “Becoming” means fitting or suitable. Fine words in a fool’s mouth sound hollow because his life contradicts them. The fool in this verse is the coarse, godless kind, the man who has no regard for God or for others, and noble words from his mouth are contradicted by the way he lives.

“Much less lying lips to a prince.”

“Much less” signals an argument from the lesser to the greater. If eloquence is out of place in a fool, lying is far more out of place in a “prince,” a nobleman or ruler entrusted with leadership and judgment. People depend on a ruler’s word, so when he lies, the harm reaches everyone under his authority. The saying ties speech to character and calling: words should fit the person, and the higher a person’s responsibility, the more a lie betrays it. The chapter uses the same word for nobles again at 17:26, where upright princes are wrongly punished.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “fool” here is nabal. It describes a person who is senseless toward God and coarse toward others, and it is the name of the man of whom Abigail said, “Nabal is his name, and folly is with him!” (1 Samuel 25:25). The Hebrew word for “prince” is nadib, which describes a person who is noble in rank and also noble and generous in character. Isaiah sets the same two words side by side: “The foolish person will no longer be called generous” (Isaiah 32:5). The pairing helps explain the verse: the nabal and the nadib are opposites in character as well as in station.

The KJV margin gives the literal Hebrew behind “excellent speech” as ‘a lip of excellency,’ that is, speech of abundance or superiority. Most versions take it as fine or eloquent speech, while the 1984 NIV reads “Arrogant lips,” understanding the excess as pride.

Proverbs 17:8
Proverbs 17:8
NKJV

Proverbs 17:8 A present is a precious stone in the eyes of its possessor; Wherever he turns, he prospers.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:8 A bribe is like a lucky charm;whoever gives one will prosper! (NLT)

Main Idea

Here a gift or bribe is seen through the eyes of the one who uses it: to him it is like a precious stone, a charm that seems to open every door. The proverb observes how people trust in gifts to get their way; it does not approve bribery, which verse 23 plainly condemns.

Verse Explanation

Here a gift or bribe is seen through the eyes of the one who uses it: to him it is like a precious stone, a charm that seems to open every door. The proverb observes how people trust in gifts to get their way; it does not approve bribery, which verse 23 plainly condemns.

“A present is a precious stone in the eyes of its possessor;”

The Hebrew word translated “present” is the same word rendered “bribe” in 17:23. The word by itself names a gift given to gain something, but here the setting shows what kind of gift is meant: a payment used to buy influence, as 17:23 makes plain. The key phrase is “in the eyes of its possessor.” The verse reports how the gift looks to the person who holds it and hands it out. To him it is “a precious stone,” a gem or charm that seems to carry good fortune with it. He trusts his gift the way someone else might trust a lucky stone.

“Wherever he turns, he prospers.”

From his point of view, it works. Every door he approaches seems to open, and every official he pays seems to cooperate. Proverbs is honest that corrupt methods often appear to succeed for a time. Yet the verse describes how a briber sees things; it does not approve of what he does. The same Hebrew word returns in 17:23, where the chapter names the bribe plainly and states its purpose: “To pervert the ways of justice.” Read together, the two sayings give both sides, the briber’s confidence and the damage his payment does. A gift freely given in generosity is a different matter, and Proverbs can speak of it without condemnation: “A man’s gift makes room for him” (18:16).

Study Notes

Both “present” here and “bribe” in 17:23 translate the Hebrew word shochad, a gift or payment given to win someone’s favor. Most often it names a bribe, as in “you shall take no bribe” (Exodus 23:8). In 6:35, though, the same word describes gifts offered to a wronged husband: “Nor will he be appeased though you give many gifts” (6:35). The setting, not the word alone, shows when a corrupt payment is meant. In this chapter, 17:23 makes the corrupt purpose clear.

The Hebrew behind “precious stone” is literally ‘a stone of favor,’ and the KJV margin renders it ‘a stone of grace.’ Many interpreters understand it as a charm or lucky stone believed to bring good fortune, and the NASB and the 1984 NIV render it “a charm.” That reading suits the verse, since the briber expects his gift to win favor wherever he goes.

Proverbs 17:9
Proverbs 17:9
NKJV

Proverbs 17:9 He who covers a transgression seeks love, But he who repeats a matter separates friends.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:9 Love prospers when a fault is forgiven,but dwelling on it separates close friends. (NLT)

Main Idea

Love looks for ways to cover an offense, forgiving it and not spreading it, and so it keeps friendship whole. The one who keeps bringing the matter up, repeating it to others or raising it again, drives close friends apart (compare 10:12); covering a personal wrong is not the same as hiding abuse or crime.

Verse Explanation

Love looks for ways to cover an offense, forgiving it and not spreading it, and so it keeps friendship whole. The one who keeps bringing the matter up, repeating it to others or raising it again, drives close friends apart (compare 10:12); covering a personal wrong is not the same as hiding abuse or crime.

“He who covers a transgression seeks love,”

A “transgression” is an offense, a wrong done against someone. To “cover” it is to let it rest: to forgive it, to refuse to broadcast it, and to stop holding it over the offender. The verb “seeks” shows that covering is active. The one who covers is going after love, valuing the friendship more than winning the point. Proverbs has already said that “love covers all sins” (10:12); this saying shows covering as the way love pursues and keeps a relationship. Covering a personal wrong in this sense never means concealing abuse or crime, hiding danger from those who need protection, or shielding wrongdoing from proper authority. The verse speaks of offenses between friends, and it gives no cover to an abuser.

“But he who repeats a matter separates friends.”

To “repeat” a matter can mean two things. A person may repeat it to others, spreading the story until everyone knows. Or he may keep bringing it up with the friend himself, refusing to let it rest. Either way the result is the same: he “separates friends.” The whisperer of chapter 16 does the same damage, for he “separates the best of friends” (16:28). Repeating an offense, whether to others or to the friend himself, keeps the wound open and can drive even close friends apart.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “repeats” means to do or say something a second time, which allows both senses: telling the matter to others and raising it again with the offender.

Proverbs 17:10
Proverbs 17:10
NKJV

Proverbs 17:10 Rebuke is more effective for a wise man Than a hundred blows on a fool.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:10 A single rebuke does more for a person of understandingthan a hundred lashes on the back of a fool. (NLT)

Main Idea

One rebuke goes deeper into a wise man than a hundred blows into a fool. The wise person takes correction to heart, while the fool, whose problem is moral and not mental, can be beaten again and again and still refuse to learn.

Verse Explanation

One rebuke goes deeper into a wise man than a hundred blows into a fool. The wise person takes correction to heart, while the fool, whose problem is moral and not mental, can be beaten again and again and still refuse to learn.

“Rebuke is more effective for a wise man”

A “rebuke” is a word of correction, telling someone plainly that he is wrong. “More effective” translates a Hebrew verb that means to go down or sink in; the rebuke goes deep into a wise man. The wise person is not someone who never needs correction. He is someone a word can reach. He hears the rebuke, takes it to heart, and changes.

“Than a hundred blows on a fool.”

“A hundred blows” is deliberate exaggeration. A fool can be beaten again and again and still learn nothing. The difference lies in the heart that receives the correction, not in its strength. The fool’s problem is moral, not mental: he refuses instruction, so neither words nor force get through to him. The verse does not commend harsh treatment. It measures the distance between a teachable heart, which one word can turn, and a hardened one that a hundred blows cannot.

Study Notes

The law of Moses limited a court-ordered beating: “Forty blows he may give him and no more” (Deuteronomy 25:3). A hundred blows is far beyond any lawful punishment, which marks the number as hyperbole.

Proverbs 17:11
Proverbs 17:11
NKJV

Proverbs 17:11 An evil man seeks only rebellion; Therefore a cruel messenger will be sent against him.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:11 Evil people are eager for rebellion,but they will be severely punished. (NLT)

Main Idea

Bent on evil, a man looks for nothing but rebellion, and so a cruel messenger will be sent against him. The one who rises against rightful authority will eventually meet stern judgment, pictured as a harsh officer sent to deal with the rebel.

Verse Explanation

Bent on evil, a man looks for nothing but rebellion, and so a cruel messenger will be sent against him. The one who rises against rightful authority will eventually meet stern judgment, pictured as a harsh officer sent to deal with the rebel.

“An evil man seeks only rebellion;”

The evil man here has one aim. “Seeks only” describes a settled direction of life, not a single misstep. His goal is “rebellion,” defiance of rightful authority. Placed right after the saying about the wise man whom a rebuke can reach, this man is the opposite: he has refused correction and set himself against those placed over him.

“Therefore a cruel messenger will be sent against him.”

“Therefore” ties the consequence to the cause. Because he pursues rebellion, “a cruel messenger will be sent against him.” In the setting of a kingdom, this pictures an officer dispatched by the king to deal with a rebel, harsh and unsparing in carrying out his task. The words “will be sent” do not say who sends him, but they make clear that someone with authority acts. Behind every rightful authority stands the LORD who judges, and the man who keeps choosing rebellion will finally meet a severity he has brought on himself. The verse states a general truth about settled defiance; it does not mean that every hardship is a sign of rebellion.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “messenger” is the ordinary word for a messenger and also the word used for angels. Some readers hear an angel of judgment here, but a royal officer fits the immediate picture, as in “As messengers of death is the king’s wrath” (16:14).

The Hebrew of the first line can be read with either word as the subject. The NKJV, ESV and KJV read the evil man seeking rebellion, while the NASB and AMP read ‘A rebellious man seeks only evil.’ The sense is close either way.

Proverbs 17:12
Proverbs 17:12
NKJV

Proverbs 17:12 Let a man meet a bear robbed of her cubs, Rather than a fool in his folly.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:12 It is safer to meet a bear robbed of her cubsthan to confront a fool caught in foolishness. (NLT)

Main Idea

It would be safer to run into a mother bear robbed of her cubs than to meet a fool in the middle of his folly. The startling picture warns that a fool acting out his folly is more dangerous and less predictable than an enraged animal.

Verse Explanation

It would be safer to run into a mother bear robbed of her cubs than to meet a fool in the middle of his folly. The startling picture warns that a fool acting out his folly is more dangerous and less predictable than an enraged animal.

“Let a man meet a bear robbed of her cubs,”

A mother bear robbed of her cubs was a familiar picture of savage fury in Israel. Bears lived in the land, and shepherds knew the danger; David told Saul of the time “when a lion or a bear came and took a lamb out of the flock” (1 Samuel 17:34). Hushai described David’s men as enraged “like a bear robbed of her cubs in the field” (2 Samuel 17:8). The saying says it would be better to run into such an animal.

“Rather than a fool in his folly.”

The comparison is startling on purpose. A fool “in his folly,” caught up in the full flow of his foolishness, is more dangerous than an enraged bear. The bear’s fury is terrible, yet the verse ranks the fool as the greater danger. The fool acting out his folly listens to no reason, and the damage he does can spread through a household, a business, or a community. The verse also implies the wise course: some meetings should be avoided, and wisdom recognizes when to stay out of a fool’s path. As elsewhere in Proverbs, the “fool” is someone who morally refuses wisdom, and the saying is no insult to people of limited ability.

Study Notes

The LORD uses the same image of His own judgment on Israel: “I will meet them like a bear deprived of her cubs” (Hosea 13:8). In the NKJV both verses use the verb “meet.”

Proverbs 17:13
Proverbs 17:13
NKJV

Proverbs 17:13 Whoever rewards evil for good, Evil will not depart from his house.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:13 If you repay good with evil,evil will never leave your house. (NLT)

Main Idea

Whoever repays good with evil brings lasting trouble on his own household, for evil will not leave his house. Ingratitude of this kind invites harm that stays, reaching beyond the man himself to the people who live under his roof.

Verse Explanation

Whoever repays good with evil brings lasting trouble on his own household, for evil will not leave his house. Ingratitude of this kind invites harm that stays, reaching beyond the man himself to the people who live under his roof.

“Whoever rewards evil for good,”

To “reward” here is to repay. This man answers kindness with harm. Someone treated him well, helped him, or trusted him, and he paid that goodness back with injury. Repaying evil for evil is sin; repaying evil for good is worse, because it turns another person’s kindness into the occasion for hurting him. It is ingratitude in its ugliest form.

“Evil will not depart from his house.”

The consequence reaches beyond the man to his “house,” his household and family. The evil he sent out comes back and settles in. David’s life shows the pattern. He repaid the loyal Uriah with adultery and murder, and the LORD told him, “the sword shall never depart from your house” (2 Samuel 12:10). That account illustrates what the verse states as a general rule. Like other proverbs, this one describes how God’s ordered world tends to work. It does not explain every trouble that comes to a family, and it gives no one the right to trace a household’s suffering to a hidden sin.

Study Notes

The psalmist uses the same expression of his enemies: “They reward me evil for good, To the sorrow of my soul” (Psalm 35:12).

Proverbs 17:14–20 — Strife, Justice, and the Faithful Friend

Proverbs 17:14
Proverbs 17:14
NKJV

Proverbs 17:14 The beginning of strife is like releasing water; (H4325) Therefore stop contention before a quarrel starts.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 17:14 Starting a quarrel is like opening a floodgate,so stop before a dispute breaks out. (NLT)

Main Idea

Starting a quarrel is like opening a small breach in a dam: once the water begins to flow, it widens and is hard to stop. So the wise course is to drop the matter before the quarrel breaks out, because strife is far easier to prevent than to contain.

Verse Explanation

Starting a quarrel is like opening a small breach in a dam: once the water begins to flow, it widens and is hard to stop. So the wise course is to drop the matter before the quarrel breaks out, because strife is far easier to prevent than to contain.

“The beginning of strife is like releasing water;”

The picture is of water held back by a dam or stored in a cistern. Open a small breach and the water begins to trickle out; then the flow wears the opening wider until it cannot be stopped. A quarrel works the same way. The first sharp word or the first refusal to let something pass seems small, yet once strife begins, it gathers force and widens the breach between people.

“Therefore stop contention before a quarrel starts.”

“Therefore” draws the practical lesson. The time to stop strife is at the beginning, when a quiet word or a decision to drop the matter can still end it. To “stop contention” is to leave it, to walk away from the dispute before “a quarrel starts” and breaks out into open conflict. Letting a matter go at that point takes wisdom and humility, and it is far easier than trying to hold back the flood later. This saying opens a group of verses on strife, justice, and friendship, and the cluster closes with another word on strife at 17:19.

Study Notes

The LORD finds both kinds of injustice detestable, declaring the guilty innocent and condemning the innocent. Twisting a verdict in either direction offends the righteous Judge, which is why the gospel shows God justifying sinners only through Christ, so that He remains “just and the justifier” (Romans 3:26).

“He who justifies the wicked,”

To “justify” is a courtroom word. It means to declare someone in the right, to acquit him. Justifying the wicked means declaring a guilty person innocent and letting him go free when the evidence condemns him. A judge might do it for a bribe, for friendship, or out of fear. The LORD described His own justice in exactly these terms: “For I will not justify the wicked” (Exodus 23:7).

“and he who condemns the just,”

The opposite error is condemning the just, declaring an innocent person guilty and punishing him. One wrong lets the guilty loose on others; the other crushes the innocent. People tend to excuse one of these, depending on whom they favor. The verse puts both in the same sentence.

“Both of them alike are an abomination to the LORD.”

“Both of them alike” refuses to rank them. Each is “an abomination to the LORD,” something He finds detestable, because each turns truth upside down in the place where truth matters most. The saying applies first to judges, but it reaches anyone who must decide between people. It also raises a question the gospel answers. Paul says God “justifies the ungodly” (Romans 4:5), and He does so without breaking this proverb. In Christ the righteous One bore the condemnation sinners deserved, so that God remains “just and the justifier of the one who has faith in Jesus” (Romans 3:26). The proverb itself speaks of human judgment, but the wider message of Scripture shows why mercy through the cross never bends justice.

A wise friend may give what he is able and willing to lose, but he does not bind his household to a debt he cannot carry.
