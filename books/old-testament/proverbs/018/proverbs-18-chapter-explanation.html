Proverbs 18:1
Proverbs 18:1
NKJV

Proverbs 18:1 A man who isolates himself seeks his own desire; He rages against all wise judgment.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:1 Unfriendly people care only about themselves;they lash out at common sense. (NLT)

Main Idea

The man who cuts himself off from others does so to pursue his own desires, and in that isolation he lashes out against every sound counsel. The warning concerns self-willed withdrawal that refuses correction, not solitude, quiet temperament, or grief.

Verse Explanation

The man who cuts himself off from others does so to pursue his own desires, and in that isolation he lashes out against every sound counsel. The warning concerns self-willed withdrawal that refuses correction, not solitude, quiet temperament, or grief.

“A man who isolates himself seeks his own desire;”

The man in this verse separates himself from others, and the reason is given at once: he “seeks his own desire.” He withdraws so that no one can question what he wants. The problem is the motive. Scripture does not fault a person for seeking quiet, for a reserved temperament, or for the solitude of grief, and Jesus Himself often withdrew to pray. This man pulls away to follow his own wishes without the check of other voices.

“He rages against all wise judgment.”

The second line shows where that path leads. The man who began by withdrawing ends by fighting. He “rages against,” or breaks out in quarrel against, “all wise judgment,” every piece of sound counsel that might correct him. Desire that is never challenged does not stay harmless; it turns hostile to the wisdom it needs. Proverbs values counsel highly, and here a man cuts himself off from it on purpose and then resents it when it reaches him. The verse opens a chapter much concerned with speech and relationships, and it begins with a man who wants neither.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of this verse is difficult, and translations differ. The ESV and NASB are close to the NKJV, while the current NIV reads ‘An unfriendly person’ in the first line. The KJV takes the verse quite differently, saying the separated man ‘intermeddleth with all wisdom.’ Its margin offers an alternative: ‘He that separateth himself seeketh according to his desire, and intermeddleth in every business.’ That marginal rendering comes closer to the NKJV’s picture of a man who withdraws in order to pursue his own desire.

The Hebrew verb behind “rages against” describes a dispute breaking out into the open. It occurs only three times in the Old Testament, all in Proverbs: here, at 17:14, where a quarrel “starts,” and at 20:3, where it is rendered “Since any fool can start a quarrel” (20:3). In each place it pictures conflict bursting out, so the man of 18:1 is not merely disagreeing with sound counsel but quarreling against it.

Proverbs 18:2
Proverbs 18:2
NKJV

Proverbs 18:2 A fool has no delight in understanding, But in expressing his own heart.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:2 Fools have no interest in understanding;they only want to air their own opinions. (NLT)

Main Idea

A fool takes no pleasure in understanding anything; what he enjoys is airing his own opinions. Opening the chapter’s cluster on the fool’s mouth, the saying shows that his trouble lies in what he loves, since he would rather talk than learn.

Verse Explanation

A fool takes no pleasure in understanding anything; what he enjoys is airing his own opinions. Opening the chapter’s cluster on the fool’s mouth, the saying shows that his trouble lies in what he loves, since he would rather talk than learn.

“A fool has no delight in understanding,”

The fool here is not said to be unable to understand. He “has no delight” in it. Understanding gives him no pleasure, so he does not pursue it. As elsewhere in Proverbs, the fool’s trouble is moral and lies in what he loves. A person grows wise by wanting to learn, and the fool does not want to.

“But in expressing his own heart.”

What he enjoys instead is “expressing his own heart,” laying out his own opinions and putting himself on display. He would rather talk than learn. The result is that nothing new is ever added to him; he keeps showing the same contents because he never takes anything in. This saying opens the chapter’s cluster on the fool’s mouth, which runs through 18:8. It pairs naturally with 18:1. One man withdraws so that no one can correct him, and the other talks so much that no one can teach him.

Proverbs 18:3
NKJV

Proverbs 18:3 When the wicked comes, contempt comes also; And with dishonor comes reproach.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:3 Doing wrong leads to disgrace,and scandalous behavior brings contempt. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wherever wickedness arrives, contempt comes with it, and dishonor brings disgrace in its train. Sin does not travel alone; it draws scorn and shame after it, both for the wicked person and for those around him.

Verse Explanation

Wherever wickedness arrives, contempt comes with it, and dishonor brings disgrace in its train. Sin does not travel alone; it draws scorn and shame after it, both for the wicked person and for those around him.

“When the wicked comes, contempt comes also;”

Wickedness never arrives alone. When the wicked person comes, “contempt comes also,” scorn for what is right and scorn for others. The line may describe the contempt the wicked man shows or the contempt that falls on him, and both are true to life. Wrongdoing brings a habit of looking down on people, and in time it brings looking down on the wrongdoer as well.

“And with dishonor comes reproach.”

The second line repeats the idea with different words. “Dishonor,” conduct that is shameful, brings “reproach,” disgrace and open blame. The pairs travel together: wickedness and contempt, dishonor and reproach. Proverbs often speaks of honor and shame as the weight a life carries before God and people. This chapter returns to the theme in 18:12, where humility comes before honor, and in 18:13, where answering too quickly brings shame.

Proverbs 18:4
Proverbs 18:4
NKJV

Proverbs 18:4 The words of a man's mouth are deep waters; (H4325) The wellspring of wisdom is a flowing brook.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:4 Wise words are like deep waters;wisdom flows from the wise like a bubbling brook. (NLT)

Main Idea

What comes out of a person’s mouth can run as deep as hidden waters, drawn from the depths of the heart. When wisdom is the source, those words flow like a fresh, running brook that refreshes and gives life to those who hear.

Verse Explanation

What comes out of a person’s mouth can run as deep as hidden waters, drawn from the depths of the heart. When wisdom is the source, those words flow like a fresh, running brook that refreshes and gives life to those who hear.

“The words of a man’s mouth are deep waters;”

Words come from deep inside a person. “Deep waters” suggests depth and hiddenness, water drawn from far below the surface. What a man says rises from the hidden places of his heart, and its meaning is not always plain at first. Proverbs uses the same picture elsewhere: “Counsel in the heart of man is like deep water, But a man of understanding will draw it out” (20:5). A person’s words can carry more than appears on the surface.

“The wellspring of wisdom is a flowing brook.”

The second line describes words drawn from wisdom. A “wellspring” is a spring, a source that keeps producing because it is fed from below. When wisdom is the source, words run like “a flowing brook,” fresh, moving, and life-giving to those who hear. A brook is not a stagnant pool. Wise speech keeps flowing because the source behind it keeps supplying. In the middle of the chapter’s sayings about the fool’s mouth, this verse shows what speech can be when it springs from wisdom.

Study Notes

Interpreters differ on how the two lines relate. Some read them as parallel, so that a man’s words are deep and wisdom’s words flow freely. Others read a contrast between hidden, hard-to-fathom words and the clear, open flow of wisdom. The NKJV’s semicolon allows either reading.

Proverbs 18:5
Proverbs 18:5
NKJV

Proverbs 18:5 It is not good to show partiality to the wicked, Or to overthrow the righteous in judgment.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:5 It is not right to acquit the guiltyor deny justice to the innocent. (NLT)

Main Idea

Favoring the guilty is not good, and neither is pushing aside the righteous when a case is judged. The proverb names two sides of the same corruption, for justice is perverted whether the wicked are excused or the innocent are denied their right.

Verse Explanation

Favoring the guilty is not good, and neither is pushing aside the righteous when a case is judged. The proverb names two sides of the same corruption, for justice is perverted whether the wicked are excused or the innocent are denied their right.

“It is not good to show partiality to the wicked,”

The Hebrew behind “show partiality” is literally ‘to lift up the face’ of someone. The expression describes looking on a person with favor because of who he is rather than what he has done. Showing such favor “to the wicked” means excusing a guilty person because of his wealth, rank, or connections. The verse calls it “not good,” a restrained phrase that carries real weight in Proverbs. The law commanded judges, “You shall not show partiality in judgment; you shall hear the small as well as the great” (Deuteronomy 1:17).

“Or to overthrow the righteous in judgment.”

The other side of the same corruption is to “overthrow the righteous in judgment,” to push an innocent person aside, deny him his right, and let his case fail. Favoring the wicked and overturning the righteous usually go together, because whoever bends the verdict toward one bends it away from the other. The saying echoes 17:15, and it reminds every judge, leader, or parent who must decide between people that judgment is a trust from God. Bending it misrepresents Him.

Study Notes

Proverbs repeats this warning in nearly the same words at 24:23 and 28:21: “To show partiality is not good” (28:21).

Proverbs 18:6
NKJV

Proverbs 18:6 A fool's lips enter into contention, And his mouth calls for blows.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:6 Fools’ words get them into constant quarrels;they are asking for a beating. (NLT)

Main Idea

When a fool opens his mouth, he walks straight into quarrels, and his words practically invite a beating. His careless speech stirs up conflict that comes back on his own head.

Verse Explanation

When a fool opens his mouth, he walks straight into quarrels, and his words practically invite a beating. His careless speech stirs up conflict that comes back on his own head.

“A fool’s lips enter into contention,”

The fool’s lips go first, and the fool follows them into “contention.” He does not set out to quarrel, perhaps, but his careless words walk him straight into one. Unguarded speech has a way of finding fights.

“And his mouth calls for blows.”

The second line goes further. His mouth “calls for blows,” as though his words were an invitation to be struck. The Hebrew verb behind “calls” is the one used when Wisdom cries out in the city in 1:21. The fool’s mouth also calls, but what it summons is trouble. The verse describes the result of his speech, not a justification for violence. Insults and provocations tend to draw a response, and the fool keeps provoking until the response comes back on his own head. This saying and the next form a pair about the fool’s mouth: here it harms him through others, and in 18:7 it ruins him from within.

Proverbs 18:7 A fool's mouth is his destruction, And his lips are the snare of his soul.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:7 The mouths of fools are their ruin;they trap themselves with their lips. (NLT)

Main Idea

Moving from the harm a fool’s words cause others to the harm they do to him, this saying calls his mouth his ruin and his lips a trap for his own life. The fool is caught in the snare his own speech has set.

Verse Explanation

Moving from the harm a fool’s words cause others to the harm they do to him, this saying calls his mouth his ruin and his lips a trap for his own life. The fool is caught in the snare his own speech has set.

“A fool’s mouth is his destruction,”

The previous verse showed the fool’s words drawing trouble from others. This one says his mouth is “his destruction.” His own words are what ruin him. The tool he trusts most, his ready tongue, is the very thing that brings him down.

“And his lips are the snare of his soul.”

A “snare” is a trap set for birds or animals, hidden until it springs. The fool’s lips set such a trap, and it catches “his soul,” meaning his life, his whole person. He is caught in the snare his own speech has laid. Proverbs repeatedly ties a person’s safety to his mouth: “He who guards his mouth preserves his life, But he who opens wide his lips shall have destruction” (13:3). The fool never learns to guard it, and so his words become the cords that bind him.

Proverbs 18:8
Proverbs 18:8
NKJV

Proverbs 18:8 The words of a talebearer are like tasty trifles, And they go down into the inmost body.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:8 Rumors are dainty morselsthat sink deep into one’s heart. (NLT)

Main Idea

Gossip goes down as easily as tasty bits of food, and like food it settles deep inside the one who takes it in. The talebearer’s words are pleasant to receive, yet they lodge in the heart and do their damage long after they are swallowed.

Verse Explanation

Gossip goes down as easily as tasty bits of food, and like food it settles deep inside the one who takes it in. The talebearer’s words are pleasant to receive, yet they lodge in the heart and do their damage long after they are swallowed.

“The words of a talebearer are like tasty trifles,”

A “talebearer” is a whisperer, someone who carries gossip from one person to another. His words are “like tasty trifles,” choice bits of food that are easy to swallow and pleasant to the taste. That is the danger. Gossip is welcome; people enjoy hearing it. The verse speaks to the listener as much as to the speaker, because tasty morsels are eaten only by someone who wants them.

“And they go down into the inmost body.”

Once swallowed, the words “go down into the inmost body.” They settle deep inside, in the innermost rooms of a person. Gossip does not pass through and disappear. It lodges in the heart and colors how the hearer sees the person talked about, long after the conversation is over. What tasted pleasant in the moment does lasting damage once it is taken in. Proverbs portrays the talebearer as a breaker of friendships, and this saying shows why his work is so effective: people take in his words gladly and keep them.

Study Notes

This verse is repeated word for word at 26:22.

The KJV reads the first line very differently, “as wounds,” with a margin offering ‘like as when men are wounded.’ Modern versions follow the sense of delicacies or choice morsels, as the NKJV does.

The Hebrew phrase behind “inmost body” is literally ‘the chambers of the belly,’ a picture of the innermost part of a person, and the KJV margin notes the word ‘chambers.’ The same expression appears at 20:27, where the NKJV renders it “all the inner depths of his heart” (20:27). The phrase shows how deep gossip goes once it is taken in.

Proverbs 18:9–12 — The Strong Tower and the Strong City

Proverbs 18:9
Proverbs 18:9
NKJV

Proverbs 18:9 He who is slothful in his work Is a brother to him who is a great destroyer.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:9 A lazy person is as bad assomeone who destroys things. (NLT)

Main Idea

The man who is slack in his work is close kin to the one who destroys. Neglect and laziness bring ruin as surely as deliberate destruction does, only more slowly.

Verse Explanation

The man who is slack in his work is close kin to the one who destroys. Neglect and laziness bring ruin as surely as deliberate destruction does, only more slowly.

“He who is slothful in his work”

The man in view is slack in his work. He does not attack anything or set out to do harm; he simply lets his work slide, doing it carelessly or not at all. Proverbs gives a full portrait of the lazy man elsewhere, and this saying adds one sharp observation about the results of slackness.

“Is a brother to him who is a great destroyer.”

He is “a brother” to the destroyer. The word ties them by family likeness: one ruins by doing harm, the other by doing nothing, but both end in ruin. A neglected field, a neglected house, or a neglected trust falls apart as surely as one that someone tears down, only more slowly. Neglect can feel innocent because nothing is done on purpose, but the proverb places it in the same family as destruction. The saying concerns slackness in work a person is able to do. Inability, illness, or real need are different matters, and the verse does not condemn them.

Study Notes

The word “brother” links several sayings in this chapter. It appears here, in the offended brother of 18:19, and in the friend who sticks closer than a brother in 18:24.

Proverbs 18:10
Proverbs 18:10
NKJV

Proverbs 18:10 The name of the LORD is a strong tower; The righteous run to it and are safe.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:10 The name of the LORD is a strong fortress;the godly run to him and are safe. (NLT)

Main Idea

The name of the LORD, meaning His revealed character and His presence with His people, is a strong tower, and the righteous run into it and are kept safe. The picture is of a high fortress in time of danger, and the safety is real because the LORD Himself is the refuge, not because His name is a formula to be spoken.

Verse Explanation

The name of the LORD, meaning His revealed character and His presence with His people, is a strong tower, and the righteous run into it and are kept safe. The picture is of a high fortress in time of danger, and the safety is real because the LORD Himself is the refuge, not because His name is a formula to be spoken.

“The name of the LORD is a strong tower;”

In Scripture, a name stands for the person as he is known. “The name of the LORD” is the LORD as He has revealed Himself: His character, His faithfulness, and His presence with His people. When the LORD passed before Moses, He “proclaimed the name of the LORD” (Exodus 34:5) and then described Himself as “merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abounding in goodness and truth” (Exodus 34:6). That revealed character is the “strong tower.” In the ancient world a tower was the strongest point of a city’s defenses, the place people fled when an enemy came. David spoke of the LORD the same way: “A strong tower from the enemy” (Psalm 61:3).

“The righteous run to it and are safe.”

The righteous do not admire the tower from a distance. They “run to it,” moving toward the LORD in trust when danger comes. There they “are safe,” set high out of reach. The safety is real because the LORD Himself is the refuge. His name is not a formula or a word to be recited for protection; it is who He is, and trusting Him is what running to the tower means. The “righteous” are those who walk with Him, and this is where they turn when they are threatened. The next verse sets beside this tower another refuge that only looks strong.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “are safe” means to be set on high, out of reach, the picture of someone secure at the top of a tower. The KJV margin renders it ‘set aloft.’ The same verb describes the rich man’s “high wall” in the next verse, which sharpens the contrast between the two refuges: the righteous are truly set high in the LORD, while the rich man’s wall is high only in his own mind.

Proverbs 18:11
Proverbs 18:11
NKJV

Proverbs 18:11 The rich man's wealth is his strong city, And like a high wall in his own esteem.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:11 The rich think of their wealth as a strong defense;they imagine it to be a high wall of safety. (NLT)

Main Idea

Set beside the strong tower of verse 10, the rich man’s wealth is his strong city, and he imagines it as a high wall. Money can give some real protection (10:15), but only in his own esteem is it the safe refuge that the name of the LORD truly is.

Verse Explanation

Set beside the strong tower of verse 10, the rich man’s wealth is his strong city, and he imagines it as a high wall. Money can give some real protection (10:15), but only in his own esteem is it the safe refuge that the name of the LORD truly is.

“The rich man’s wealth is his strong city,”

This line repeats 10:15 word for word: “The rich man’s wealth is his strong city” (10:15). Wealth does give some real protection. It can buy food in a famine, pay a debt, or secure help. In 10:15 the saying simply observes this. Placed here, right after the strong tower of 18:10, it invites a comparison: the righteous run to the LORD’s name, and the rich man runs to his money.

“And like a high wall in his own esteem.”

The second line qualifies everything. His wealth is like a high wall “in his own esteem,” in his imagination. He pictures it as an unclimbable defense, but that picture exists mainly in his mind. Wealth has limits that no amount of it can overcome: it cannot stop sickness or death, cannot buy a clear conscience, and cannot stand before God’s judgment. The verse does not say money is worthless. It says the rich man trusts it far beyond what it can actually do. Set side by side, 18:10 and 18:11 show two refuges, one that is as strong as it looks and one that is strong mainly in the eyes of its owner.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “esteem” refers to an image or imagined picture, and the ESV and NASB render it “imagination.” The word shows that the rich man’s high wall exists mainly in his own mind.

Proverbs 18:12
Proverbs 18:12
NKJV

Proverbs 18:12 Before destruction the heart of a man is haughty, And before honor is humility.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:12 Haughtiness goes before destruction;humility precedes honor. (NLT)

Main Idea

Before a man’s downfall comes a haughty heart, and before honor comes humility. The saying explains why the rich man’s imagined wall cannot save him: pride leads to destruction, while the humble are the ones God raises up.

Verse Explanation

Before a man’s downfall comes a haughty heart, and before honor comes humility. The saying explains why the rich man’s imagined wall cannot save him: pride leads to destruction, while the humble are the ones God raises up.

“Before destruction the heart of a man is haughty,”

Pride begins in “the heart,” inside a person, long before it shows outwardly, and it comes “before destruction.” A haughty heart is lifted up, sure of its own strength and standing. The saying follows naturally after the rich man of 18:11, whose wall stood high only in his imagination. The man who trusts his own height is already on the way down. Proverbs said it earlier: “Pride goes before destruction, And a haughty spirit before a fall” (16:18).

“And before honor is humility.”

The second line gives the other order. Humility comes “before honor.” The humble person does not grasp for a high place, and in God’s ordered world that is the path to being honored. These words repeat the close of 15:33: “And before honor is humility” (15:33). Read with 18:10–11, the verse shows two kinds of height. The height the LORD gives to those who run to Him is safe, while the height a person gives himself is the step before a fall.

Proverbs 18:13
NKJV

Proverbs 18:13 He who answers a matter before he hears it, It is folly and shame to him.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:13 Spouting off before listening to the factsis both shameful and foolish. (NLT)

Main Idea

Answering a matter before hearing it out is foolish and brings shame on the one who does it. Wisdom listens fully before it speaks, and this saying opens a section on fair hearing and settling disputes.

Verse Explanation

Answering a matter before hearing it out is foolish and brings shame on the one who does it. Wisdom listens fully before it speaks, and this saying opens a section on fair hearing and settling disputes.

“He who answers a matter before he hears it,”

This saying opens a section on fair hearing and settling disputes. The man in view answers “before he hears,” responding to a matter before he has listened to it fully. He assumes he already knows what the other person means, or he is simply eager to speak. Either way, he replies to what he imagines rather than to what is actually being said.

“It is folly and shame to him.”

Such answering is “folly,” foolishness, because it responds without knowledge, and it is “shame to him,” because answering without hearing is disgraceful in itself. Even if he happens to guess correctly, he has not given the matter or the person the hearing they deserve. Listening fully before replying is wisdom, and it is also fairness. The person speaking deserves to be heard, and a matter cannot be judged rightly until it is understood. In disputes, in leadership, in families, and in friendships, the quick reply that cuts someone off usually reveals pride. The New Testament gives the same counsel: “let every man be swift to hear, slow to speak, slow to wrath” (James 1:19).

Proverbs 18:14
Proverbs 18:14
NKJV

Proverbs 18:14 The spirit of a man will sustain him in sickness, But who can bear a broken spirit?(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:14 The human spirit can endure a sick body,but who can bear a crushed spirit? (NLT)

Main Idea

The human spirit can carry a person through physical sickness, but when the spirit itself is crushed, who can bear it? Inner strength sustains the body in illness, while a broken spirit takes away that very strength, so the one who carries it needs compassion, not blame.

Verse Explanation

The human spirit can carry a person through physical sickness, but when the spirit itself is crushed, who can bear it? Inner strength sustains the body in illness, while a broken spirit takes away that very strength, so the one who carries it needs compassion, not blame.

“The spirit of a man will sustain him in sickness,”

“The spirit of a man” is his inner strength, the courage and resolve that hold him up. That inner strength can “sustain him in sickness,” carrying a person through physical illness. Many people endure great pain because their spirit remains whole. The body may weaken, and the spirit can still bear it.

“But who can bear a broken spirit?”

The question turns the matter around. When the spirit itself is crushed, what is left to carry the person? A “broken spirit” takes away the very strength that would have sustained him. The question admits that some burdens are too heavy for human resources. It does not tell the crushed person to try harder or cheer up, and it does not rank one kind of suffering above another. It recognizes that a wound in the spirit is a real wound, often harder to bear than a sickness of the body, and so the one who carries it needs compassion and care, never blame. The verse leaves its question open, and Scripture elsewhere answers it with the God who heals the brokenhearted.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “broken” in “broken spirit” means stricken or wounded. The same phrase for a stricken spirit appears at 15:13 and 17:22, and in each place it describes an inner life crushed by sorrow or despair.

Proverbs 18:16
Proverbs 18:16
NKJV

Proverbs 18:16 A man's gift makes room for him, And brings him before great men.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:16 Giving a gift can open doors;it gives access to important people! (NLT)

Main Idea

A gift given to the right person opens doors and brings the giver into the presence of important people. The proverb observes how presents smooth a person’s way in the world; it describes a common practice without approving the bribe that perverts justice (17:23).

Verse Explanation

A gift given to the right person opens doors and brings the giver into the presence of important people. The proverb observes how presents smooth a person’s way in the world; it describes a common practice without approving the bribe that perverts justice (17:23).

“A man’s gift makes room for him,”

A “gift” here is a present given to someone. It “makes room” for the giver, opening a way where none seemed to be. In the ancient world, as now, a gift could smooth the way into someone’s favor and show respect, as when Jacob sent gifts ahead to Esau in Genesis 32 or the Queen of Sheba came to Solomon with presents in 1 Kings 10.

“And brings him before great men.”

The gift brings the giver “before great men,” into the presence of people of rank and influence. The proverb observes how the world works. It describes a common practice without approving the bribe that perverts justice, which the previous chapter condemned in 17:23. A courteous gift that opens a door is different from a secret payment that bends a verdict. The verse is about a material present, not a person’s talents or spiritual gifts, and it makes no promise that a gift will always bring a person into the company of the powerful.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “gift” here is mattan, a general word for a present. It is different from shochad, the word for a bribe used in 17:8 and 17:23, which supports reading this verse as a description of ordinary gift-giving rather than bribery.

Main Idea

The first person to state his case always sounds right until the other side comes and questions him. Fair judgment hears both parties, because one account alone, however convincing, rarely tells the whole story.

“The first one to plead his cause seems right,”

The picture is from a dispute or a court case. The first person to tell his side always “seems right.” His account is clear, his reasons sound good, and no one has yet contradicted him. He may be entirely sincere, and his story may still be incomplete.

“Until his neighbor comes and examines him.”

Then the other party arrives and “examines him,” questioning his account and bringing out what was left unsaid. The first story that seemed so convincing now looks different. Fair judgment hears both sides before deciding. Even godly people can be misled by hearing only one account. When Ziba accused Mephibosheth, David answered at once, “Here, all that belongs to Mephibosheth is yours” (2 Samuel 16:4), and only later did Mephibosheth tell the other side: “my servant deceived me” (2 Samuel 19:26). The verse guards against quick verdicts in every setting where one person tells another’s story.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “examines” means to search something out thoroughly. It is the verb of “the glory of kings is to search out a matter” (25:2), so the neighbor’s questioning is pictured as a careful investigation, not a casual objection.

When a dispute cannot be settled any other way, casting lots brings the quarrel to an end and keeps strong rivals from fighting it out. Since the decision of the lot belongs to the LORD (16:33), both sides can accept the outcome without a contest of strength.

“Casting lots causes contentions to cease,”

Some disputes cannot be settled by evidence or argument. Casting lots was a recognized way in Israel of making a decision that neither side could control. Land was divided this way, and duties were assigned. When a quarrel reached a deadlock, the lot could bring it to an end, because the decision no longer depended on either party.

“And keeps the mighty apart.”

The lot “keeps the mighty apart,” separating powerful rivals who might otherwise settle the matter by force. Without such a means, the stronger party would usually win. Israel trusted that the outcome of the lot belonged to God: “The lot is cast into the lap, But its every decision is from the LORD” (16:33). Both sides could therefore accept the result without a contest of strength. The principle reaches beyond the lot itself. When two strong wills cannot agree, accepting a fair process and trusting God with the result can bring peace where fighting would only deepen the breach.

“A brother offended is harder to win than a strong city,”

A “brother” here is a close relative or near companion, someone bound to the offender by family or deep friendship. When such a person has been wronged, he is “harder to win than a strong city.” A fortified city could hold out against a siege for months; a wounded brother may hold out longer. The closer the relationship, the deeper the wound, and the harder it is to reach the person again. The strong city of 18:11 returns here, now as a picture of how hard a broken relationship is to restore.

“And contentions are like the bars of a castle.”

The quarrels that follow such an offense are “like the bars of a castle,” the heavy bolts that lock a fortress gate. Each new contention adds another bar, and the gate grows harder to open. The verse is a sober warning. Offenses between people who are close should be avoided, and when they happen, they need to be handled with great care. It does not minimize real hurts, and it does not blame the wounded brother for guarding himself. Healing such a breach takes patience, humility, and often the help of God, who can open what people cannot.

Study Notes

The KJV prints the words ‘is harder to be won’ in italics to mark them as supplied by the translators. The Hebrew line is compressed and has no verb, reading literally ‘a brother offended than a strong city,’ so every translation must supply the comparison. The YLT renders the line ‘A brother transgressed against is as a strong city,’ and the ESV and the 1984 NIV read “more unyielding” instead.

Proverbs 18:20–24 — The Tongue, a Wife, and a Faithful Friend

Proverbs 18:20 Wise words satisfy like a good meal;the right words bring satisfaction. (NLT)

Main Idea

From the fruit of his own mouth a person will be filled, and the harvest of his lips will satisfy him. Words are like seed that a person will eventually eat, so what he says comes back to him, for better or worse.

Verse Explanation

From the fruit of his own mouth a person will be filled, and the harvest of his lips will satisfy him. Words are like seed that a person will eventually eat, so what he says comes back to him, for better or worse.

“A man’s stomach shall be satisfied from the fruit of his mouth;”

The picture is of a harvest. Words are like seed, and the “fruit” they produce is food the speaker himself will eat. A man’s “stomach shall be satisfied” from what his mouth has produced. Over time, what a person says becomes what he lives on. Proverbs has used this image before: “A man will be satisfied with good by the fruit of his mouth” (12:14).

“From the produce of his lips he shall be filled.”

The second line repeats the thought with “produce” and “filled.” The verse is neutral about what kind of harvest comes in. It does not say the man is filled with good things, only that he is filled with what his lips have grown. Kind, truthful, wise words bring a harvest of trust and peace; harsh, deceitful, or careless words bring a harvest of conflict and regret. Either way, the speaker eats what he sowed. This saying prepares directly for 18:21, which states the same truth in its strongest form.

Proverbs 18:21
Proverbs 18:21
NKJV

Proverbs 18:21 Death and life are in the power of the tongue, And those who love it will eat its fruit.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:21 The tongue can bring death or life;those who love to talk will reap the consequences. (NLT)

Main Idea

Death and life are in the power of the tongue, for words can build, heal, and encourage, or wound, deceive, and destroy, and those who love to use the tongue will eat what it produces. Speech carries real weight before God and people, yet the saying gives no formula by which spoken words create the outcomes a person wants.

Verse Explanation

Death and life are in the power of the tongue, for words can build, heal, and encourage, or wound, deceive, and destroy, and those who love to use the tongue will eat what it produces. Speech carries real weight before God and people, yet the saying gives no formula by which spoken words create the outcomes a person wants.

“Death and life are in the power of the tongue,”

This is one of the strongest statements about speech in the Bible. “Death and life” are held in the tongue’s “power.” The Hebrew says literally that they are in the hand of the tongue, picturing the tongue as holding death and life in its grip. Words can build, encourage, comfort, instruct, and heal. They can also wound, slander, deceive, inflame conflict, and destroy trust. A word can lift someone who is ready to give up, and a word can crush a person for years. In particular cases words carry literal life-and-death weight: false testimony in a court, a lie that sends someone into danger, or counsel that turns a person back from ruin. The saying is far from mere etiquette. Speech has real moral weight before God and people, and because the tongue holds life as well as death, wisdom means more than avoiding harmful words. It also means speaking the truth, encouragement, and counsel another person needs.

“And those who love it will eat its fruit.”

The second line returns to the harvest of 18:20. “Those who love it,” who love to use the tongue, “will eat its fruit.” What a person habitually loves to do with his words, whether to build or to tear down, comes back to him in his own life and relationships. This is wisdom about speech and its consequences. It does not promise that kind words will control another person’s response or automatically repair a relationship, and it does not make a mistreated person responsible for someone else’s wrongdoing. Nor does it teach that spoken words create their own reality. A person who honestly says he is sick is not keeping himself sick by saying so, and no one can speak a desired possession into existence.

The New Testament develops the spiritual weight of speech further, and its teaching helps explain why the tongue matters so much. Paul joins honest speech, unresolved anger, and spiritual danger: “do not let the sun go down on your wrath, nor give place to the devil” (Ephesians 4:26–27). He calls believers to speak “what is good for necessary edification, that it may impart grace to the hearers” (Ephesians 4:29), and he warns against bitterness and evil speaking because they grieve the Holy Spirit. James calls the tongue “a fire” (James 3:6) and grieves that with the same mouth “we bless our God and Father, and with it we curse men” (James 3:9). Speech can also be a real act of faith and repentance: “with the mouth confession is made unto salvation” (Romans 10:10), and “If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins” (1 John 1:9).

In that light, the words people settle on about themselves and others deserve attention. Inner vows such as ‘I will never’ or ‘I will always,’ and settled judgments such as ‘you are’ spoken over another person, can express fear, bitterness, self-protection, or a refusal to remain open to God’s direction. Not every firm promise or boundary is sinful, and such statements do not hold automatic supernatural power. Yet they can harden the heart and wound others, and they belong before God, to be examined in the light of Scripture, repented of where they express sin, and replaced with His truth. James warns against presuming to control the future and teaches instead, “If the Lord wills, we shall live and do this or that” (James 4:15).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word yad, “hand,” is often used for power or control, as when someone is said to be in another person’s hand. The NKJV’s “power” expresses that sense.

The pronoun “it” in the second line is usually taken to refer to the tongue, so that those who love to use the tongue eat its fruit. The NLT renders the line “those who love to talk will reap the consequences.”

Verse Explanation

Finding a wife is finding something good, and the man who does so has received favor from the LORD. A good marriage is a gift from His hand, and the wording echoes Wisdom’s promise that whoever finds her “obtains favor from the LORD” (8:35).

“He who finds a wife finds a good thing,”

A wife is called “a good thing.” The word “good” recalls the beginning of Scripture, where God said, “It is not good that man should be alone” (Genesis 2:18), and then made the woman. Marriage is part of God’s good design, and the man who finds a wife has found something genuinely good. The context of Proverbs makes clear that the good thing is a good wife. Other sayings warn that a contentious marriage is a misery: “Better to dwell in a corner of a housetop, Than in a house shared with a contentious woman” (21:9). Marriage at any cost is not what is praised here.

“And obtains favor from the LORD.”

Finding a good wife is a sign that the man has received “favor from the LORD.” She is a gift from God’s hand, as another proverb says: “a prudent wife is from the LORD” (19:14). The wording also echoes Wisdom’s own promise: “For whoever finds me finds life, And obtains favor from the LORD” (8:35). Finding a good wife and finding wisdom are described in the same terms, and the book closes with the question, “Who can find a virtuous wife?” (31:10). The verse celebrates marriage as God’s gift. It does not mean that the unmarried lack God’s favor. Single men and women can walk fully in His favor, and Scripture honors singleness as a gift as well.

Study Notes

The Hebrew text says simply “a wife,” and the NKJV and other major English versions follow it. The Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation, reads ‘a good wife,’ and the Clementine edition of the Latin Vulgate likewise reads mulierem bonam, ‘a good woman.’ Those versions make explicit what the context of Proverbs already implies.

Proverbs 18:24
Proverbs 18:24
NKJV

Proverbs 18:24 A man who has friends must himself be friendly, But there is a friend who sticks closer than a brother.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 18:24 There are “friends” who destroy each other,but a real friend sticks closer than a brother. (NLT)

Main Idea

Anyone who wants friends must show himself friendly, but there is one friend who sticks closer than a brother, a loyal companion more faithful than family. At a deeper, layered level, that friend is Christ, who calls His disciples friends and lays down His life for them (John 15:13).

Verse Explanation

Anyone who wants friends must show himself friendly, but there is one friend who sticks closer than a brother, a loyal companion more faithful than family. At a deeper, layered level, that friend is Christ, who calls His disciples friends and lays down His life for them (John 15:13).

“A man who has friends must himself be friendly,”

In the NKJV the first line states a practical principle: anyone who wants friends must show himself friendly. Friendship is given as well as received. A person who waits for others to reach out, while offering nothing himself, will find himself alone. Other versions read the line very differently, as a warning that a man with many companions may come to ruin, because casual friends cannot be relied on when trouble comes. Either way, the line prepares for the contrast in the second half, between ordinary friendships and one friend of a different kind.

“But there is a friend who sticks closer than a brother.”

Some friendships prove stronger than family ties. “There is a friend,” a loyal companion, “who sticks closer than a brother.” The verb “sticks” describes clinging, the holding fast of loyal love. Brothers share blood but can drift apart, and this friend stays when others leave. The natural meaning is a word of praise for a faithful friend whose loyalty exceeds even that of family, a fitting close to a chapter that warns of the offended brother in 18:19.

At a deeper, layered level, the friend who sticks closer than a brother is Christ. He is the friend who never leaves, and He showed the full measure of His friendship by laying down His life: “Greater love has no one than this, than to lay down one’s life for his friends” (John 15:13). He also said, “I have called you friends” (John 15:15), and He promised, “I will never leave you nor forsake you” (Hebrews 13:5). This layered reading does not replace the proverb’s plain praise of loyal human friendship. It shows where that loyalty is found in its fullest form.

Study Notes

The first line is a well-known translation difficulty. The KJV, like the NKJV, reads a man with friends who must show himself friendly. The ESV reads ‘A man of many companions may come to ruin,’ the NASB (1995 edition) ‘A man of too many friends comes to ruin,’ and the current NIV ‘One who has unreliable friends soon comes to ruin.’ The difference turns on how the Hebrew verb in the first line is understood: the KJV and NKJV take it as showing oneself friendly, while the modern versions take it from a verb meaning to be broken or ruined.

The Hebrew word behind “sticks” means clinging or holding fast. It comes from the verb used for a man being “joined to his wife” (Genesis 2:24) and for Ruth who “clung to her” mother-in-law (Ruth 1:14), so the friend’s loyalty is described in the language of the closest human bonds.
