Proverbs 19

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 19:1
Proverbs 19:1
NKJV

Proverbs 19:1 Better is the poor who walks in his integrity Than one who is perverse in his lips, and is a fool.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:1 Better to be poor and honestthan to be dishonest and a fool. (NLT)

Main Idea

A poor man who walks with integrity is better off than a fool whose speech is twisted, whatever the fool may have. The comparison weighs character against advantage, and integrity wins even when it comes without wealth.

Verse Explanation

A poor man who walks with integrity is better off than a fool whose speech is twisted, whatever the fool may have. The comparison weighs character against advantage, and integrity wins even when it comes without wealth.

“Better is the poor who walks in his integrity”

The chapter opens with a comparison, and the first figure is a poor man. What sets him apart is that he “walks in his integrity.” Integrity is wholeness, a life that is the same inside and out, and “walks” makes it the steady course of his days. He may have little, but no loss of money can take this from him.

“Than one who is perverse in his lips, and is a fool.”

The reader might expect the other figure to be a rich man, and a near-twin saying does contrast the upright poor man with a crooked rich one: “Better is the poor who walks in his integrity Than one perverse in his ways, though he be rich” (28:6). Here the comparison is with a man who is “perverse in his lips,” whose speech is twisted and deceitful, and who “is a fool.” Whatever advantages the fool may have, the poor man of integrity is better off. The verse weighs character against advantage and finds character heavier. It does not make poverty itself a virtue. It says that honest wholeness before God is worth more than anything a crooked mouth can gain.

None.

Proverbs 19:2
Proverbs 19:2
NKJV

Proverbs 19:2 Also it is not good for a soul to be without knowledge, And he sins who hastens with his feet.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:2 Enthusiasm without knowledge is no good;haste makes mistakes. (NLT)

Main Idea

Being without knowledge is not good for a person, and the one who rushes ahead with hasty feet sins by missing the way. Joined by Also to verse 1, the saying adds that eager energy needs understanding to guide it, or it carries a person into wrong before he has stopped to think.

Verse Explanation

Being without knowledge is not good for a person, and the one who rushes ahead with hasty feet sins by missing the way. Joined by Also to verse 1, the saying adds that eager energy needs understanding to guide it, or it carries a person into wrong before he has stopped to think.

“Also it is not good for a soul to be without knowledge,”

“Also” links this saying to 19:1 and adds another judgment of wisdom, this time stated directly: something is “not good.” A “soul” here is a person, especially in his desires and drives. To be “without knowledge” is to act on those desires without understanding. Energy, eagerness, and sincere zeal are not enough; desire that runs ahead of knowledge goes wrong. The line does not condemn desire. It warns against desire with no understanding to guide it.

“And he sins who hastens with his feet.”

The second line pictures a man in a hurry. He “hastens with his feet,” rushing ahead before he knows where he is going, and so “he sins.” The Hebrew verb behind “sins” is chata’, which can describe missing a target or missing the way, which fits the picture of hasty feet that miss the path. Yet the NKJV’s “sins” keeps the moral weight: rushing ahead without stopping to understand is not just a practical mistake. Haste that refuses to learn carries a person into wrong before he has stopped to think. Slowing down to understand is often an act of wisdom and faith.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb rendered “sins,” chata’, is used in its basic sense of missing a target in Judges 20:16, where the slingers “could sling a stone at a hair’s breadth and not miss” (Judges 20:16). The ESV renders this line with the hasty man who ‘misses his way,’ and the AMP reads ‘sins (misses the mark),’ keeping both senses together.

The Hebrew word behind “soul” is nefesh, which can mean a person, a life, or a person’s appetite and desire. The ESV renders the first line ‘Desire without knowledge is not good,’ taking the word in that last sense, which fits the picture of eager feet that rush ahead.

Proverbs 19:3
Proverbs 19:3
NKJV

Proverbs 19:3 The foolishness of a man twists his way, And his heart frets against the LORD.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:3 People ruin their lives by their own foolishnessand then are angry at the LORD. (NLT)

Main Idea

Through his own foolishness a man wrecks the course of his life, and then his heart rages against the LORD for what has happened. The saying exposes the folly of blaming God for trouble one has caused, which is different from the honest lament Scripture welcomes.

Verse Explanation

Through his own foolishness a man wrecks the course of his life, and then his heart rages against the LORD for what has happened. The saying exposes the folly of blaming God for trouble one has caused, which is different from the honest lament Scripture welcomes.

“The foolishness of a man twists his way,”

A man’s own “foolishness” is the cause here. It “twists his way,” wrecking or overturning the course of his life. The trouble that follows is not mysterious; it grows from his own choices. Proverbs consistently traces ruin back to folly rather than to blind fate.

“And his heart frets against the LORD.”

Then comes the strange turn. Having ruined his own way, the man’s “heart frets against the LORD.” He rages at God for the results of what he himself did. The cause is in the first line, and the anger is aimed in the second. The verse exposes the habit of blaming God for self-made trouble rather than owning it. This is different from the honest lament Scripture welcomes. The Psalms are full of believers pouring out pain, confusion, and questions before God, often in times when they had done nothing to cause their suffering. What this saying warns against is turning against the LORD over a ruin one’s own folly produced. The right response to such trouble is to own it and turn back to Him.

Proverbs 19:4
Proverbs 19:4
NKJV

Proverbs 19:4 Wealth makes many friends, But the poor is separated from his friend.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:4 Wealth makes many “friends”;poverty drives them all away. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wealth attracts plenty of friends, while the poor man finds even his friend pulling away. The proverb states plainly how people tend to behave around money; it describes the world as it is without approving it.

Verse Explanation

Wealth attracts plenty of friends, while the poor man finds even his friend pulling away. The proverb states plainly how people tend to behave around money; it describes the world as it is without approving it.

“Wealth makes many friends,”

Money attracts people. “Wealth makes many friends,” or at least many people who act like friends. They gather around what the rich man has, and they enjoy what he can give them.

“But the poor is separated from his friend.”

The poor man has the opposite experience. Not only does he lack a crowd; he is “separated from his friend,” losing even the one companion he had. The two lines are a plain observation about how people tend to behave around money. The proverb describes the world as it is without approving it. Proverbs elsewhere condemns oppressing the poor and fawning over the rich, and this saying does not endorse favoring wealth. Its honesty protects the reader from naivety about the crowd that gathers around money, and it quietly exposes how shallow such friendships are. Proverbs made a similar observation earlier: “The poor man is hated even by his own neighbor, But the rich has many friends” (14:20). The theme returns in 19:6–7.

Proverbs 19:5
Proverbs 19:5
NKJV

Proverbs 19:5 A false witness will not go unpunished, And he who speaks lies will not escape.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:5 A false witness will not go unpunished,nor will a liar escape. (NLT)

Main Idea

The false witness will not escape punishment, and the one who breathes out lies will not get away. Courts may be fooled for a time, but this saying insists that perjury will be answered.

Verse Explanation

The false witness will not escape punishment, and the one who breathes out lies will not get away. Courts may be fooled for a time, but this saying insists that perjury will be answered.

“A false witness will not go unpunished,”

The scene shifts to the court. A “false witness” gives untrue testimony against someone, a serious crime in Israel because a person’s property, freedom, or life could depend on the word of witnesses. The law commanded that a false witness receive the penalty he had tried to bring on his neighbor. The saying declares that such a person “will not go unpunished.” A court may be fooled for a time, but the matter is not finished.

“And he who speaks lies will not escape.”

The second line widens the warning to anyone who “speaks lies.” The liar often uses his lie as an escape, a way out of trouble or a way to gain an advantage. The verse turns that upside down: he “will not escape.” What he meant as a way out becomes a trap. This saying is repeated almost exactly in 19:9, and the two warnings enclose the sayings of 19:6–8 within the chapter’s opening section.

Study Notes

The law set the penalty for a false witness: “then you shall do to him as he thought to have done to his brother” (Deuteronomy 19:19).

Proverbs 19:6
Proverbs 19:6
NKJV

Proverbs 19:6 Many entreat the favor of the nobility, And every man is a friend to one who gives gifts.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:6 Many seek favors from a ruler;everyone is the friend of a person who gives gifts! (NLT)

Main Idea

Many people seek the favor of those in high places, and everyone wants to be friends with a man who gives gifts. Returning to the theme of verse 4, the saying observes how generosity and rank draw a crowd of hangers-on.

Verse Explanation

Many people seek the favor of those in high places, and everyone wants to be friends with a man who gives gifts. Returning to the theme of verse 4, the saying observes how generosity and rank draw a crowd of hangers-on.

“Many entreat the favor of the nobility,”

Many people seek the favor of “the nobility,” those of high rank and influence. To “entreat the favor” is to court someone, to try to win his goodwill. People flock to the powerful because of what the powerful can do for them.

“And every man is a friend to one who gives gifts.”

The second line states the motive plainly. “Every man is a friend” to the person who gives gifts. Such friendship is commercial; it lasts as long as the gifts keep coming. Returning to the theme of 19:4, the saying observes how generosity and rank draw a crowd. It does not condemn generosity or deny that giving can build goodwill. It exposes how much friendship in the world is really attachment to what another person supplies. The next verse shows the other side, when the gifts stop.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase behind “entreat the favor” joins a verb meaning to soothe or entreat with the word for “face.” It describes seeking someone’s goodwill by appealing to him in person. The same expression is used of prayer: “I entreated Your favor with my whole heart” (Psalm 119:58).

The Hebrew word rendered “nobility” is nadib, which can describe a person who is noble in rank or noble and generous in character. The ESV and NASB read ‘a generous man,’ which connects the first line closely to the gift-giver of the second.

Proverbs 19:7
Proverbs 19:7
NKJV

Proverbs 19:7 All the brothers of the poor hate him; How much more do his friends go far from him! He may pursue them with words, yet they abandon him.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:7 The relatives of the poor despise them;how much more will their friends avoid them!Though the poor plead with them,their friends are gone. (NLT)

Main Idea

Even the poor man’s own brothers turn against him, and his friends keep their distance all the more. He chases after them with pleading words, yet they are gone, a painful picture of how poverty can strip a person of support.

Verse Explanation

Even the poor man’s own brothers turn against him, and his friends keep their distance all the more. He chases after them with pleading words, yet they are gone, a painful picture of how poverty can strip a person of support.

“All the brothers of the poor hate him;”

This verse is unusual in Proverbs because it has three lines instead of two. It begins with family. “All the brothers of the poor hate him.” “Hate” in Proverbs can mean to reject or turn away from someone, and here even his own relatives keep their distance. Poverty strips away the support a person would most expect to keep.

“How much more do his friends go far from him!”

If brothers, bound by blood, turn away, “how much more” will friends who have no such tie. They “go far from him,” putting distance between themselves and his need.

“He may pursue them with words, yet they abandon him.”

The third line shows the poor man still trying. He “may pursue them with words,” pleading, calling after them, asking for help, “yet they abandon him.” His words reach no one. This is one of the most painful pictures of loneliness in Proverbs. Like 19:4 and 19:6, it describes what happens without approving it, and it lets the reader feel the cost of poverty from the inside. God sees this loneliness and has written it into His Word, and His law repeatedly commands His people to open their hands to the poor rather than walk away from them.

Study Notes

The third line is difficult in Hebrew. The written Hebrew text reads a word meaning “not,” while the reading noted in the margin of the Hebrew manuscripts has a word that sounds the same but means “to him.” Translations therefore differ. The NKJV describes the friends abandoning him, the ESV reads ‘but does not have them,’ the NASB and AMP ‘they are gone,’ and the YLT ‘they are not!’ Every reading leaves the poor man alone.

Proverbs 19:8
Proverbs 19:8
NKJV

Proverbs 19:8 He who gets wisdomloves his own soul; He who keeps understanding will find good.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:8 To acquire wisdom is to love yourself;people who cherish understanding will prosper. (NLT)

Main Idea

Whoever gains wisdom shows love for his own life, and whoever holds on to understanding will find good. Seeking wisdom is not selfish in a wrong sense; it is the truest care a person can show for his own soul.

Verse Explanation

Whoever gains wisdom shows love for his own life, and whoever holds on to understanding will find good. Seeking wisdom is not selfish in a wrong sense; it is the truest care a person can show for his own soul.

“He who gets wisdom loves his own soul;”

To get wisdom is to acquire it, as one would acquire a valuable possession. The person who does this “loves his own soul,” showing true care for his own life. Seeking wisdom is not selfish in a wrong sense, and it is not a burden laid on happiness. It is the deepest good a person can do for himself, because wisdom leads to life. The fool who refuses wisdom is in effect his own enemy.

“He who keeps understanding will find good.”

The second line moves from getting to keeping. It is not enough to acquire understanding once; a person must hold on to it, guard it, and live by it. The one who “keeps understanding will find good.” This is the general pattern of God’s ordered world: wisdom kept and practiced tends to bring good. Getting must become keeping, as the father urged elsewhere: “Take firm hold of instruction, do not let go; Keep her, for she is your life” (4:13).

Study Notes

Where the NKJV has “He who gets wisdom,” the Hebrew words qoneh lev say literally ‘He who gets heart,’ so the whole first line reads ‘He who gets heart loves his own soul.’ The KJV margin notes that “wisdom” is literally ‘an heart,’ and the YLT reads ‘Whoso is getting heart is loving his soul.’ In Hebrew the heart is the seat of thought and judgment, not only of feeling, so to get a heart means to gain understanding. The expression is the opposite of the young man “devoid of understanding,” literally lacking heart, in 7:7.

Proverbs 19:9
Proverbs 19:9
NKJV

Proverbs 19:9 A false witness will not go unpunished, And he who speaks lies shall perish.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:9 A false witness will not go unpunished,and a liar will be destroyed. (NLT)

Main Idea

The warning of verse 5 returns almost word for word, except that now the liar will perish. The repetition frames the opening section and strengthens the sentence: deceit that harms others ends in the liar’s own ruin.

Verse Explanation

The warning of verse 5 returns almost word for word, except that now the liar will perish. The repetition frames the opening section and strengthens the sentence: deceit that harms others ends in the liar’s own ruin.

“A false witness will not go unpunished,”

The opening line of 19:5 returns word for word. The repetition is deliberate. Together with 19:5 it encloses the sayings of 19:6–8 within the chapter’s opening section, and it presses the warning home. When Proverbs says something twice, it wants the reader to be sure.

“And he who speaks lies shall perish.”

The second line changes one word. In 19:5 the liar “will not escape”; here he “shall perish.” The sentence grows heavier. The lie that harms others ends in the liar’s own ruin. Between the two sayings stand verses about the poor man abandoned by brothers and friends, the kind of person most vulnerable to false testimony. The frame suggests that God is especially watchful when lies are told against those who have no one to speak for them. The verse does not say how or when the liar will perish, but it is certain that he will not finally get away.

Proverbs 19:10
Proverbs 19:10
NKJV

Proverbs 19:10 Luxury is not fitting for a fool, Much less for a servant to rule over princes.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:10 It isn’t right for a fool to live in luxuryor for a slave to rule over princes! (NLT)

Main Idea

Luxury does not suit a fool, and in the social order of Solomon’s day it was even more out of place for a servant to rule over princes. The point is the disorder that follows when comfort or power arrives without the wisdom to handle it, not a judgment on servants, for a wise servant can rightly rule (17:2).

Verse Explanation

Luxury does not suit a fool, and in the social order of Solomon’s day it was even more out of place for a servant to rule over princes. The point is the disorder that follows when comfort or power arrives without the wisdom to handle it, not a judgment on servants, for a wise servant can rightly rule (17:2).

“Luxury is not fitting for a fool,”

“Luxury” is comfort, delight, and plenty. It “is not fitting for a fool” because he lacks the wisdom to handle it. Comfort does not make a person; it reveals him, and in a fool’s hands it feeds his folly and makes him worse. Proverbs elsewhere lists “A fool when he is filled with food” among the things the earth cannot bear (30:22).

“Much less for a servant to rule over princes.”

“Much less” argues from the lesser to the greater. In the social order of Solomon’s day, a servant suddenly raised to rule over princes was an even greater disruption, especially when he came to power without preparation or character. Proverbs lists the same picture: “For a servant when he reigns” (30:22). The point is the disorder that follows when power arrives without the wisdom to use it. The verse passes no judgment on servants as such. Proverbs has already praised “A wise servant” who rules over a shameful son (17:2), and Scripture tells of Joseph, sold as a slave, who ruled Egypt well. The danger lies in the unprepared and unwise, whatever their station.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 19:11
Proverbs 19:11
NKJV

Proverbs 19:11 The discretion of a man makes him slow to anger, And his glory is to overlook a transgression.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:11 Sensible people control their temper;they earn respect by overlooking wrongs. (NLT)

Main Idea

Good sense makes a person slow to anger, and it is his glory to overlook an offense. Patience and the willingness to let a personal wrong go are marks of wisdom, though overlooking an offense does not mean tolerating abuse or ignoring sin that must be corrected.

Verse Explanation

Good sense makes a person slow to anger, and it is his glory to overlook an offense. Patience and the willingness to let a personal wrong go are marks of wisdom, though overlooking an offense does not mean tolerating abuse or ignoring sin that must be corrected.

“The discretion of a man makes him slow to anger,”

“Discretion” is good sense, insight into people and situations. It “makes him slow to anger.” The Hebrew says literally that it makes his anger long, giving it a long fuse. A person who understands more of what is happening, and why, has room to be patient. Quick anger usually reacts to the surface; discretion sees deeper and waits.

“And his glory is to overlook a transgression.”

The second line goes further. A man’s “glory,” his honor and beauty, is “to overlook a transgression,” to pass over an offense done against him rather than nurse it or repay it. The world often sees this as weakness. Proverbs calls it glory. In this, a person acts like God, who is praised as “Pardoning iniquity And passing over the transgression of the remnant of His heritage” (Micah 7:18). Overlooking a personal wrong does not mean tolerating abuse, ignoring harm done to others, or leaving sin uncorrected when correction is needed. It means choosing not to hold onto a personal slight, and it is a mark of wisdom and strength.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase behind “overlook” is literally ‘pass over.’ Micah uses the same verb with the same word for “transgression” when he praises God for “passing over the transgression” (Micah 7:18), so the person who overlooks an offense is acting in a way Scripture also ascribes to God.

Proverbs 19:12
Proverbs 19:12
NKJV

Proverbs 19:12 The king's wrath is like the roaring of a lion, But his favor is like dew on the grass.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:12 The king’s anger is like a lion’s roar,but his favor is like dew on the grass. (NLT)

Main Idea

The king’s anger is as fearsome as a roaring lion, but his favor is as refreshing as dew on the grass. Those who live under a ruler’s authority should take both seriously, since he holds the power to threaten or to bless.

Verse Explanation

The king’s anger is as fearsome as a roaring lion, but his favor is as refreshing as dew on the grass. Those who live under a ruler’s authority should take both seriously, since he holds the power to threaten or to bless.

“The king’s wrath is like the roaring of a lion,”

The roar of a lion is a fearsome sound, and the “king’s wrath” is like that roar. A king held the power of life and death, and his anger was dangerous to anyone who provoked it. Proverbs uses the same picture in 20:2: “The wrath of a king is like the roaring of a lion” (20:2).

“But his favor is like dew on the grass.”

The second line gives the opposite picture. The king’s “favor is like dew on the grass.” In a dry land, dew kept vegetation alive through rainless seasons, falling quietly and refreshing everything it touched. A king’s favor is like that: gentle, life-giving, and welcome. Those who live under a ruler’s authority are wise to take both seriously, avoiding what provokes his anger and valuing his favor. The saying also shows the weight carried by anyone in authority. Anger from above lands hard, and kindness from above refreshes.

Study Notes

Proverbs describes royal favor with a similar image of moisture: “his favor is like a cloud of the latter rain” (16:15).

Proverbs 19:13
Proverbs 19:13
NKJV

Proverbs 19:13 A foolish son is the ruin of his father, And the contentions of a wife are a continual dripping.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:13 A foolish child is a calamity to a father;a quarrelsome wife is as annoying as constant dripping. (NLT)

Main Idea

A foolish son brings ruin on his father, and quarreling in a marriage is like a steady drip that never stops. Both lines describe pain inside the home, the second pointing to strife that wears down a household over time.

Verse Explanation

A foolish son brings ruin on his father, and quarreling in a marriage is like a steady drip that never stops. Both lines describe pain inside the home, the second pointing to strife that wears down a household over time.

“A foolish son is the ruin of his father,”

The verse turns to pain inside the home. A “foolish son” is “the ruin of his father,” bringing calamity on him, whether through disgrace, wasted resources, or broken hopes. Proverbs is not sentimental about family life. It names the grief a wayward child can bring. Yet, as in 17:21 and 17:25, the saying describes the father’s loss without blaming him for his son’s choices.

“And the contentions of a wife are a continual dripping.”

The second line names another pain: “the contentions of a wife” are “a continual dripping,” like water dripping steadily through a leaking roof. One drop is nothing; endless drops wear on a person until he can find no rest. The picture is of strife that never stops, wearing down a household over time. This is a portrait of strife in a home, not a joke about wives, and the same truth applies to a quarrelsome husband. The verse shows how much the peace of a home depends on everyday words. Proverbs uses the same image in 27:15, and the very next verse in this chapter, 19:14, sets the prudent wife beside the contentious one.

Study Notes

The image recurs in 27:15: “A continual dripping on a very rainy day And a contentious woman are alike” (27:15).

Proverbs 19:14
Proverbs 19:14
NKJV

Proverbs 19:14 Houses and riches are an inheritance from fathers, But a prudent wife is from the LORD.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:14 Fathers can give their sons an inheritance of houses and wealth,but only the LORD can give an understanding wife. (NLT)

Main Idea

A man may inherit houses and wealth from his father, but a wise and prudent wife comes from the LORD. Possessions can be passed down, while a good spouse is a gift only God can give, in contrast to the quarreling of verse 13.

Verse Explanation

A man may inherit houses and wealth from his father, but a wise and prudent wife comes from the LORD. Possessions can be passed down, while a good spouse is a gift only God can give, in contrast to the quarreling of verse 13.

“Houses and riches are an inheritance from fathers,”

Some things can be passed down. “Houses and riches” come as “an inheritance from fathers,” handed from one generation to the next. A man may receive property without doing anything to earn it, simply because of the family he was born into.

“But a prudent wife is from the LORD.”

Other gifts cannot be inherited. “A prudent wife,” one who is wise and discerning, “is from the LORD.” No father can bequeath her, and no amount of property can buy her character. She is a gift only God can give. The verse stands in contrast to the quarreling of 19:13 and echoes 18:22, which calls a wife “a good thing” and says the man who finds her “obtains favor from the LORD.” It points to God’s hand in the most personal parts of life. It is not a promise that every marriage will be easy, and it does not suggest that a hard marriage means God gave the wrong gift. It honors a wise spouse as one of the LORD’s best gifts.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “prudent” describes insight and good sense. It comes from the same root as the word used in 19:11 for the “discretion” that makes a man slow to anger, so the wife of 19:14 has the same quality of wise insight that 19:11 praises.

Proverbs 19:15–21 — Pity for the Poor and the LORD’s Counsel

Proverbs 19:15
Proverbs 19:15
NKJV

Proverbs 19:15 Laziness casts one into a deep sleep, And an idle person will suffer hunger.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:15 Lazy people sleep soundly,but idleness leaves them hungry. (NLT)

Main Idea

Laziness drops a person into deep sleep, and the idle soul will go hungry. The lazy man’s drowsiness is not harmless rest, for it leads straight to want.

Verse Explanation

Laziness drops a person into deep sleep, and the idle soul will go hungry. The lazy man’s drowsiness is not harmless rest, for it leads straight to want.

“Laziness casts one into a deep sleep,”

Laziness here acts on the person. It “casts one into a deep sleep,” pulling him down into a heavy drowsiness. The more a person gives in to slackness, the harder it is to wake up. What begins as a small indulgence becomes a weight. The “deep sleep” is not refreshing rest but dullness that settles over a life until a person no longer notices what needs doing.

“And an idle person will suffer hunger.”

The second line shows the result. “An idle person,” in Hebrew literally a slack soul, meaning a person whose will has gone limp, “will suffer hunger.” The lazy man’s drowsiness leads straight to want. He sleeps while work waits, and so there is nothing to eat. The verse addresses settled unwillingness to work. It does not condemn rest, which God gives as a good gift, and it does not describe those who cannot work because of illness, grief, exhaustion, or disability. Proverbs portrays the lazy man fully elsewhere; this saying shows the downward pull of laziness and its plain consequence.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “deep sleep” is tardemah, a heavy sleep from which a person is not easily roused. It is the same word used when “the LORD God caused a deep sleep to fall on Adam” (Genesis 2:21). The comparison concerns the depth of the sleep, not its source.

Proverbs 19:16
Proverbs 19:16
NKJV

Proverbs 19:16 He who keeps the commandment keeps his soul, But he who is careless of his ways will die.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 19:16 Keep the commandments and keep your life;despising them leads to death. (NLT)

Main Idea

Whoever keeps the commandment guards his own life, but the one who is careless about how he lives will die. Obedience to God’s instruction protects the soul, and indifference to one’s ways is deadly.

Verse Explanation

Whoever keeps the commandment guards his own life, but the one who is careless about how he lives will die. Obedience to God’s instruction protects the soul, and indifference to one’s ways is deadly.

“He who keeps the commandment keeps his soul,”

The verb “keeps” appears twice. The one who keeps “the commandment,” God’s instruction, “keeps his soul,” guarding his own life. Obeying God is not a fence that hems in a person’s good; it is the wall that protects it. The “commandment” here may refer to God’s law or to the wise instruction that applies it, and in Proverbs the two run together.

“But he who is careless of his ways will die.”

The opposite is the one who is “careless of his ways,” who does not care how he lives or where his life is going. The Hebrew word behind “careless” means to despise or hold in contempt, and the KJV and ESV read “despises.” Not caring about one’s ways is a quiet way of despising them. Such a person “will die.” Indifference to the direction of life is deadly, while attention to God’s instruction protects it. As with other proverbs, this states the general pattern of God’s ordered world rather than a timetable for every life.

Proverbs 19:18
Proverbs 19:18
NKJV

NLT

Proverbs 19:18 Discipline your children while there is hope.Otherwise you will ruin their lives. (NLT)

Main Idea

Parents are told to correct their son while there is still hope, during the years when correction can still turn him. They must never aim at the child’s ruin, whether by withholding needed discipline or by punishing in anger, and the verse gives no license for harshness or abuse.

Verse Explanation

Parents are told to correct their son while there is still hope, during the years when correction can still turn him. They must never aim at the child’s ruin, whether by withholding needed discipline or by punishing in anger, and the verse gives no license for harshness or abuse.

“Chasten your son while there is hope,”

To “chasten” is to discipline, and in Proverbs discipline is the whole work of teaching, guiding, correcting, and training a child toward maturity. It includes correction and fitting consequences, but it is much broader than punishment. Proverbs has already said that loving parents do not neglect this work: “he who loves him disciplines him promptly” (13:24). This verse adds urgency. Discipline belongs to the time “while there is hope,” the years when a child is still teachable and correction can still turn him. Loving discipline is an act of hope. It says the child can still change, and it does the work now rather than giving up or waiting until it is too late. The verse does not guarantee how a child will turn out; it urges parents to use the season they have.

“And do not set your heart on his destruction.”

The second line sets a boundary, and its exact sense is debated. It may warn parents not to make themselves party to a child’s ruin by withholding needed discipline, leaving him to a path that ends in death. Or it may warn parents not to let correction become destructive, disciplining in anger or to the point of harm. On either reading, the parent’s aim must be the child’s life and good, never his ruin, and the verse gives no license for harshness, rage, or abuse. The New Testament keeps the same balance: “fathers, do not provoke your children to wrath, but bring them up in the training and admonition of the Lord” (Ephesians 6:4). Behind every parent stands the LORD Himself, who corrects His children because He loves them (3:12) and whose discipline aims at their holiness and “the peaceable fruit of righteousness” (Hebrews 12:11).

Study Notes

Translations of the second line vary widely. The NKJV reads “do not set your heart on his destruction,” the ESV ‘do not set your heart on putting him to death,’ and the NASB (1995 edition) ‘do not desire his death.’ The KJV reads ‘let not thy soul spare for his crying,’ and its margin offers ‘to his destruction’ or ‘to cause him to die’ as alternatives. The Hebrew can be read as a warning against neglect or against excess, which is why both readings are given above.
