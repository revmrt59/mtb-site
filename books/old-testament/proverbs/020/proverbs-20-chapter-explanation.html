Proverbs 20:1
Proverbs 20:1
NKJV

Proverbs 20:1 Wine is a mocker, Strong drink is a brawler, And whoever is led astray by it is not wise.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:1 Wine produces mockers; alcohol leads to brawls.Those led astray by drink cannot be wise. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wine mocks and strong drink brawls, as if alcohol itself were a scoffer and a troublemaker, and whoever lets it lead him astray is not wise. The warning is against being carried off by drink, which deceives the one who trusts it and stirs up strife (compare 23:29–35).

Verse Explanation

Wine mocks and strong drink brawls, as if alcohol itself were a scoffer and a troublemaker, and whoever lets it lead him astray is not wise. The warning is against being carried off by drink, which deceives the one who trusts it and stirs up strife (compare 23:29–35).

“Wine is a mocker,”

The chapter opens by giving wine a character. It is “a mocker,” the same figure as the scoffer Proverbs warns against, the one who laughs at correction and sneers at what is right. Wine comes looking like a friend, but in the end it makes a fool of the one who trusts it, and it can turn the drinker himself into a mocker.

“Strong drink is a brawler,”

“Strong drink” is a brawler, noisy and quarrelsome. It stirs up trouble, loosens the tongue, and starts fights. The two lines personify alcohol as two of the troublemakers Proverbs describes most often, the scoffer and the man who loves strife.

“And whoever is led astray by it is not wise.”

The third line gives the verdict. Whoever is “led astray by it,” carried off, staggering, deceived into folly, “is not wise.” The warning is against being led astray by drink, letting it take control and lead a person where he would not go with a clear head. The verse warns against that loss of control; it does not lay down a further rule. Proverbs gives a much fuller portrait of the drunkard in 23:29–35, ending with wine that “bites like a serpent” (23:32), and Lemuel’s mother applies the warning to rulers in 31:4–5. The New Testament gives the same warning with its positive counterpart: “do not be drunk with wine, in which is dissipation; but be filled with the Spirit” (Ephesians 5:18).

Study Notes

The KJV renders the second line ‘strong drink is raging,’ and it reads “deceived” where the NKJV has “led astray.” The NASB reads ‘whoever is intoxicated by it.’

Proverbs 20:2
Proverbs 20:2
NKJV

Proverbs 20:2 The wrath of a king is like the roaring of a lion; Whoever provokes him to anger sins against his own life.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:2 The king’s fury is like a lion’s roar;to rouse his anger is to risk your life. (NLT)

Main Idea

A king’s anger is as terrifying as a lion’s roar, and the one who provokes him puts his own life at risk. Following the brawler of verse 1, the saying warns that some quarrels are deadly, especially with those who hold power.

Verse Explanation

A king’s anger is as terrifying as a lion’s roar, and the one who provokes him puts his own life at risk. Following the brawler of verse 1, the saying warns that some quarrels are deadly, especially with those who hold power.

“The wrath of a king is like the roaring of a lion;”

The roar of a lion is a terrifying sound, and the “wrath of a king” is like that roar: terrifying, because the king holds the power of life and death. Proverbs used nearly the same picture in 19:12, and here it follows the brawler of 20:1. Some quarrels are far more dangerous than others.

“Whoever provokes him to anger sins against his own life.”

To provoke the king is to bring his anger on oneself, and the one who does it “sins against his own life.” He may think he is striking at the king’s pride, but the harm comes back on him. The verse calls this sin, not just foolishness: rash provocation of rightful authority wrongs one’s own life. The next verse draws the lesson for every kind of conflict: it is honorable to stop.

None.

Proverbs 20:3
Proverbs 20:3
NKJV

Proverbs 20:3 It is honorable for a man to stop striving, Since any fool can start a quarrel.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:3 Avoiding a fight is a mark of honor;only fools insist on quarreling. (NLT)

Main Idea

It brings a man honor to step back from a fight, since any fool can start a quarrel. Real strength shows in ending strife; picking a fight takes no wisdom at all.

Verse Explanation

It brings a man honor to step back from a fight, since any fool can start a quarrel. Real strength shows in ending strife; picking a fight takes no wisdom at all.

“It is honorable for a man to stop striving,”

“Honorable” here means bringing glory or credit. It is to a man’s honor “to stop striving,” to step back from a quarrel, to sit apart from strife. The world often counts backing down as weakness, but Proverbs calls it honor. Real strength shows itself in ending a conflict.

“Since any fool can start a quarrel.”

The reason is simple: “any fool can start a quarrel.” Picking a fight takes no wisdom, skill, or courage at all. Anyone can do it. Ending strife, or never starting it, takes self-control, humility, and a willingness to let one’s pride go unsatisfied. Following the warnings about the brawling drink of 20:1 and the provoked king of 20:2, this saying gives the wise response to conflict. It echoes 17:14, which urged stopping contention before a quarrel starts.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “start a quarrel” describes a dispute breaking out into the open. It occurs only three times in the Old Testament, all in Proverbs: here, in 17:14 (“before a quarrel starts”), and in 18:1 (“rages against”). Each time it pictures conflict bursting out, which is what the fool is quick to start and the honorable man refuses to continue.

Proverbs 20:4
Proverbs 20:4
NKJV

Proverbs 20:4 The lazy man will not plow because of winter; He will beg during harvest and have nothing.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:4 Those too lazy to plow in the right seasonwill have no food at the harvest. (NLT)

Main Idea

The lazy man will not plow when the cold season comes, so at harvest time he looks for a crop and finds nothing. Excuses at planting time lead to emptiness at reaping time, and he ends up begging.

Verse Explanation

The lazy man will not plow when the cold season comes, so at harvest time he looks for a crop and finds nothing. Excuses at planting time lead to emptiness at reaping time, and he ends up begging.

“The lazy man will not plow because of winter;”

In Israel, plowing and sowing were done in the cooler, wetter months, after the early rains softened the ground. That season could be cold and unpleasant. The lazy man uses it as his excuse: he “will not plow because of winter.” The discomfort is real, but it does not cancel the work. Waiting for comfortable conditions means the work never gets done.

“He will beg during harvest and have nothing.”

The consequence comes months later. When harvest arrives, he “will beg,” looking for a crop or asking others for food, and he will “have nothing.” Nothing grows where nothing was planted. The empty harvest is not bad luck; it is the fruit of the skipped season. Proverbs elsewhere gives a full portrait of the lazy man, and this saying shows his excuse and its end. It concerns a person able to work who refuses because the work is unpleasant. It says nothing against those prevented from working by illness, inability, or real hardship.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word rendered “winter” refers to the autumn and winter season, the time after the early rains when the ground was plowed. The ESV reads ‘in the autumn,’ and the KJV text reads ‘by reason of the cold,’ with a margin reading “winter.”

Proverbs 20:5
Proverbs 20:5
NKJV

Proverbs 20:5 Counsel in the heart of man is like deep water, (H4325) But a man of understanding will draw it out.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:5 Though good advice lies deep within the heart,a person with understanding will draw it out. (NLT)

Main Idea

The purposes in a person’s heart are like water deep in a well, hidden from view, but a man of understanding knows how to draw them out. Wisdom includes the patient skill of listening and asking until what lies beneath comes to the surface.

Verse Explanation

The purposes in a person’s heart are like water deep in a well, hidden from view, but a man of understanding knows how to draw them out. Wisdom includes the patient skill of listening and asking until what lies beneath comes to the surface.

“Counsel in the heart of man is like deep water,”

“Counsel” here means purpose or plan, the intentions and thoughts a person carries inside. They are “like deep water,” lying far below the surface in a well, hidden from view. People carry much more than they say. Their real purposes, fears, and understanding often lie deep.

“But a man of understanding will draw it out.”

“A man of understanding will draw it out,” as someone draws water from a deep well with a bucket and rope. He does not force or drill; he listens, asks, and waits patiently until what lies beneath comes to the surface. This is part of wisdom: the skill of understanding people, helping them bring their thoughts into the open, and discerning what is really going on. The verse applies to counselors, parents, friends, and leaders, and also to the wise person drawing out the counsel of quiet people who know more than they say. Proverbs used the image of deep waters for words in 18:4.

Study Notes

The ESV renders “counsel” as ‘purpose’ and the NASB as ‘A plan,’ which brings out the sense of hidden intentions.

Proverbs 20:6
Proverbs 20:6
NKJV

Proverbs 20:6 Most men will proclaim each his own goodness, But who can find a faithful man?(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:6 Many will say they are loyal friends,but who can find one who is truly reliable? (NLT)

Main Idea

Plenty of people will announce their own goodness and loyalty, but a truly faithful man is hard to find. Talk of kindness is cheap; steady faithfulness is rare and precious.

Verse Explanation

Plenty of people will announce their own goodness and loyalty, but a truly faithful man is hard to find. Talk of kindness is cheap; steady faithfulness is rare and precious.

“Most men will proclaim each his own goodness,”

The Hebrew word behind “goodness” is chesed, loyal, faithful love. Many people “proclaim” their own goodness, announcing how loyal, kind, and dependable they are. Talk of such love is easy and common.

“But who can find a faithful man?”

The question exposes the gap between talk and reality. “Who can find a faithful man,” someone who actually proves dependable over time? Such a person is rare. Faithfulness is proved in action, not proclaimed in words, and many who announce their loyalty do not keep it when it costs them. The verse does not deny that faithful people exist; it says they are hard to find and precious when found. The same kind of question will be asked in 31:10 about the virtuous wife, and here it is asked about the faithful man. The question invites the reader to be the kind of person whose faithfulness others can find rather than only claim it.

Study Notes

The ESV renders the first line ‘Many a man proclaims his own steadfast love,’ showing that the Hebrew word behind “goodness” is chesed, the word for loyal love. That word makes the contrast with the “faithful man” of the second line sharper: many claim loyal love, but few prove faithful.

Proverbs 20:7–12 — Who Can Say His Heart Is Clean?

Proverbs 20:7

Proverbs 20:7 The righteous man walksin his integrity; His children are blessed after him.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:7 The godly walk with integrity;blessed are their children who follow them. (NLT)

Main Idea

The righteous man lives with integrity, and his children are blessed after him. A godly life leaves an inheritance of example and blessing that reaches the next generation, as a general pattern of God’s ways.

Verse Explanation

The righteous man lives with integrity, and his children are blessed after him. A godly life leaves an inheritance of example and blessing that reaches the next generation, as a general pattern of God’s ways.

“The righteous man walks in his integrity;”

The righteous man “walks in his integrity,” living with wholeness and honesty day after day. As in 19:1, integrity is a walk, the steady course of a life. His righteousness is not a claim to sinlessness; it is a way of life before God that includes humility and turning from sin when he fails.

“His children are blessed after him.”

The effect of his life reaches beyond him: “His children are blessed after him.” A godly life leaves an inheritance of example, reputation, and blessing that reaches the next generation. Children who grow up seeing integrity lived out receive something of great worth. This describes the general pattern of God’s ways, not a guarantee that every child of a righteous parent will prosper or follow the Lord, and it gives no ground for blaming parents whose children turn away. It says that a faithful walk today matters beyond one’s own lifetime.

Proverbs 20:8
Proverbs 20:8
NKJV

Proverbs 20:8 A king who sits on the throne of judgment Scatters all evil with his eyes.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:8 When a king sits in judgment, he weighs all the evidence,distinguishing the bad from the good. (NLT)

Main Idea

When a king sits on his judgment seat, his searching gaze scatters evil like chaff. A just ruler’s attention alone makes wrongdoers scatter, because they know he sees.

Verse Explanation

When a king sits on his judgment seat, his searching gaze scatters evil like chaff. A just ruler’s attention alone makes wrongdoers scatter, because they know he sees.

“A king who sits on the throne of judgment”

The verse pictures a king seated to hear cases, exercising his role as judge. In Israel the king was the highest court of appeal, and his attention to justice set the moral tone of the whole kingdom.

“Scatters all evil with his eyes.”

The king “scatters all evil with his eyes.” The Hebrew verb behind “scatters” is the verb for winnowing, tossing grain into the wind so that the chaff blows away. The just king’s searching, discerning look sees through the schemes of wrongdoers and brings their evil into the open, so that it cannot hide before his judgment seat. What the king discerns, he can then judge justly. Under a just ruler, the righteous have nothing to fear and evil cannot hold its ground. The next verse turns from the king’s gaze to a deeper question: if a just king’s eyes expose evil, who can stand before the eyes of God and claim a clean heart?

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb rendered “scatters” means to scatter or to winnow, and it appears again in 20:26, where the wise king “sifts out the wicked.” The ESV reads “winnows” in both verses, which keeps the farming picture of separating chaff from grain.

Proverbs 20:9
Proverbs 20:9
NKJV

Proverbs 20:9 Who can say, "I have made my heart clean, I am pure from my sin"?(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:9 Who can say, “I have cleansed my heart;I am pure and free from sin”? (NLT)

Main Idea

Who can honestly claim to have made his own heart clean and to be free from sin? The question expects the answer no one, for no person can purify himself; that need is met only by God, who creates a clean heart and cleanses those who confess (Psalm 51:10; 1 John 1:9).

Verse Explanation

Who can honestly claim to have made his own heart clean and to be free from sin? The question expects the answer no one, for no person can purify himself; that need is met only by God, who creates a clean heart and cleanses those who confess (Psalm 51:10; 1 John 1:9).

“Who can say, ‘I have made my heart clean,’”

The verse is a question that expects the answer “no one.” Who can honestly say, “I have made my heart clean”? The claim is not merely that the heart is clean, but that the person made it so by his own effort. No human being can do this. The heart, the inner person, carries sin that no amount of self-improvement can remove.

“‘I am pure from my sin’?”

The second part of the claim goes further: “I am pure from my sin.” No one can say this truthfully. The question exposes the pride that would claim self-made purity and the honesty that admits the need for cleansing. This does not mean that the righteous of Proverbs are hypocrites. Their righteousness is real, but it is a way of life before God that includes humility and confession. As 28:13 puts it, “whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy” (28:13). The question prepares for the answer Scripture gives elsewhere. What no one can do for himself, God does. David prayed, “Create in me a clean heart, O God” (Psalm 51:10), and the New Testament promises, “If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness” (1 John 1:9). Cleansing comes from God, not from the self.

Study Notes

The verse stands right after the king who scatters evil with his eyes in 20:8, and just before the false weights of 20:10, so the question of the clean heart sits between royal justice and everyday honesty.

Proverbs 20:10
Proverbs 20:10
NKJV

Proverbs 20:10 Diverse weights and diverse measures, They are both alike, an abomination to the LORD.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:10 False weights and unequal measures —the LORD detests double standards of every kind. (NLT)

Main Idea

Two sets of weights and two sets of measures, one for buying and one for selling, are equally detestable to the LORD. Cheating in trade is not a small matter of business; it offends the God who sees every transaction (compare 11:1).

Verse Explanation

Two sets of weights and two sets of measures, one for buying and one for selling, are equally detestable to the LORD. Cheating in trade is not a small matter of business; it offends the God who sees every transaction (compare 11:1).

“Diverse weights and diverse measures,”

Trade in Israel used stone weights in a balance and standard containers for measuring grain. “Diverse weights and diverse measures” means two sets of each, one heavy and one light, one large and one small. A dishonest merchant would use the heavy weight when buying and the light one when selling, cheating in both directions. The law forbade it plainly: “You shall not have in your bag differing weights, a heavy and a light” (Deuteronomy 25:13).

“They are both alike, an abomination to the LORD.”

Both the false weights and the false measures “are both alike, an abomination to the LORD.” He finds them detestable. Cheating in trade is not a small matter of business practice; it offends the God who sees every transaction. Proverbs already said, “Dishonest scales are an abomination to the LORD, But a just weight is His delight” (11:1). The doubleness itself is the sin: one standard for oneself and another for others. The saying returns almost word for word in 20:23. Placed right after the question of 20:9, it shows that the heart no one can cleanse alone reveals itself in such small, everyday dishonesty.

Study Notes

The Hebrew is literally ‘a stone and a stone, an ephah and an ephah.’ The stone was a weight used in a balance, and an ephah was a standard dry measure for grain. The doubled words describe two different weights and two different measures used for the same purpose.

Proverbs 20:11
Proverbs 20:11
NKJV

Proverbs 20:11 Even a child is known by his deeds, Whether what he does is pure and right.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:11 Even children are known by the way they act,whether their conduct is pure, and whether it is right. (NLT)

Main Idea

Even a child shows by what he does whether his conduct is pure and right. Character shows itself early in actions, so behavior, not claims, reveals what is in the heart.

Verse Explanation

Even a child shows by what he does whether his conduct is pure and right. Character shows itself early in actions, so behavior, not claims, reveals what is in the heart.

“Even a child is known by his deeds,”

“Even a child,” a young person, “is known by his deeds.” Character shows itself early. A child’s actions reveal what kind of person he is becoming, long before he is grown. Behavior, not claims, reveals what is in the heart.

“Whether what he does is pure and right.”

His deeds show “whether what he does is pure and right.” Even in childhood, conduct can be clean and upright or not. The saying takes the young seriously. What children do matters, and it already shows who they are becoming. Coming right after the question of 20:9, which no one can answer with a self-made clean heart, the verse also shows that the call to pure and right conduct remains. Knowing that only God can cleanse the heart does not cancel the call to live purely.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “is known” is a form of the verb to recognize, and the ESV and YLT render it ‘makes himself known.’ The same verb form can elsewhere mean to disguise oneself, so the line may also hint that a child can mask his character for a time. The main versions follow the sense of being recognized by conduct.

Proverbs 20:12 The hearing ear and the seeing eye, The LORD has made them both.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:12 Ears to hear and eyes to see —both are gifts from the LORD. (NLT)

Main Idea

The ear that hears and the eye that sees were both made by the LORD. Since He gave these faculties, He hears and sees all things Himself, and people are accountable to use their ears and eyes as He intends.

Verse Explanation

The ear that hears and the eye that sees were both made by the LORD. Since He gave these faculties, He hears and sees all things Himself, and people are accountable to use their ears and eyes as He intends.

“The hearing ear and the seeing eye,”

The verse names two faculties: “the hearing ear and the seeing eye.” In Proverbs the hearing ear is often the listening ear, the one that receives instruction, and the seeing eye perceives what is true. These are the senses through which a person takes in wisdom.

“The LORD has made them both.”

“The LORD has made them both.” Ears and eyes are His gifts and His design. Two truths follow. First, the God who made the ear hears, and the God who made the eye sees; nothing escapes Him. Second, people are accountable to use their ears and eyes as He intended, to listen to wisdom and to see rightly. The verse follows sayings about dishonest weights and a child’s deeds, where hidden things are exposed, and it reminds the reader that all of life is lived before the One who sees and hears everything. It prepares for the next verse, which tells the reader to open his eyes.

Proverbs 20:13 Do not love sleep, lest you come to poverty; Open your eyes, and you will be satisfied with bread.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:13 If you love sleep, you will end in poverty.Keep your eyes open, and there will be plenty to eat! (NLT)

Main Idea

Do not love sleep, or poverty will follow; keep your eyes open and you will have enough bread. The command sets drowsy self-indulgence against alert diligence, and each brings its own result.

Verse Explanation

Do not love sleep, or poverty will follow; keep your eyes open and you will have enough bread. The command sets drowsy self-indulgence against alert diligence, and each brings its own result.

“Do not love sleep, lest you come to poverty;”

The command is not “do not sleep.” Rest is God’s good gift. The warning is against loving sleep, making comfort and ease a person’s settled desire. The one who loves sleep comes “to poverty.” Drowsy self-indulgence that puts rest ahead of responsibility leads to want.

“Open your eyes, and you will be satisfied with bread.”

The opposite is to “open your eyes,” to stay awake, alert, and ready to work. The one who does “will be satisfied with bread,” having enough to eat. The command follows directly after 20:12, where the LORD made the seeing eye; here the reader is told to open it. Diligence is partly waking up to what God has already given and using it. Like other proverbs about work, this describes the general pattern of God’s world. Poverty has many causes besides laziness, and this saying does not blame every poor person for loving sleep.

Main Idea

The buyer complains that the goods are worthless, then walks away and brags about the bargain he got. With a touch of humor, the proverb exposes the small dishonesty of haggling that runs down a product only to win a better price.

The buyer complains that the goods are worthless, then walks away and brags about the bargain he got. With a touch of humor, the proverb exposes the small dishonesty of haggling that runs down a product only to win a better price.

“‘It is good for nothing,’ cries the buyer;”

The scene is a market. The buyer examines the goods and complains loudly: “It is good for nothing.” He runs down the item, pointing out every flaw, hoping to drive the price down.

“But when he has gone his way, then he boasts.”

Once the deal is done and he “has gone his way,” the buyer “boasts” about the fine bargain he got. The thing he called worthless was worth much more than he paid. With a touch of humor, the proverb exposes the small dishonesty of haggling that uses words as tools rather than truth. His complaint was a pose, and his boasting shows what he really thought. The verse sits among sayings about honest trade and guarded speech, and it shows that dishonesty can hide even in ordinary bargaining.

In Hebrew the buyer’s complaint is the word for “bad” said twice, ‘Bad, bad,’ as the ESV and NASB render it.

Proverbs 20:15
Proverbs 20:15
NKJV

Proverbs 20:15 There is gold and a multitude of rubies, But the lips of knowledge are a precious jewel.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:15 Wise words are more valuablethan much gold and many rubies. (NLT)

Main Idea

Gold and many rubies exist, but lips that speak with knowledge are a rarer jewel. Wise speech is worth more than wealth because it is harder to find and does more good.

Verse Explanation

Gold and many rubies exist, but lips that speak with knowledge are a rarer jewel. Wise speech is worth more than wealth because it is harder to find and does more good.

“There is gold and a multitude of rubies,”

The verse opens almost casually: “There is gold and a multitude of rubies.” Gold and precious stones exist in the world, and people can find and own them. They are valuable, but they are not rare in the sense the next line means.

“But the lips of knowledge are a precious jewel.”

What is truly rare is “the lips of knowledge,” speech that comes from real understanding. Such lips are “a precious jewel,” worth more than gold and rubies because they are harder to find and do more good. A person who speaks true knowledge, wisely and at the right time, is a treasure. Wise speech can guide, warn, comfort, and teach in ways no amount of wealth can. In a section about honest dealing and guarded lips, this verse sets the value of wise words above material riches.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase behind “precious jewel” is literally ‘a vessel of preciousness,’ an object of great value, and the YLT renders it ‘a precious vessel.’ After the gold and rubies of the first line, the wording sets wise speech among a person’s treasured possessions, something to be valued and kept.

Proverbs 20:16
Proverbs 20:16
NKJV

Proverbs 20:16 Take the garment of one who is surety for a stranger, And hold it as a pledge when it is for a seductress.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:16 Get security from someone who guarantees a stranger’s debt.Get a deposit if he does it for foreigners. (NLT)

Main Idea

A lender should take the garment of anyone who guarantees a stranger’s debt and hold it as security when he has pledged on behalf of a seductress. A man that careless is a poor risk, so the saying warns, with dry realism, against rash surety (compare 6:1–5).

Verse Explanation

A lender should take the garment of anyone who guarantees a stranger’s debt and hold it as security when he has pledged on behalf of a seductress. A man that careless is a poor risk, so the saying warns, with dry realism, against rash surety (compare 6:1–5).

“Take the garment of one who is surety for a stranger,”

The saying speaks to a lender. If a man becomes “surety for a stranger,” guaranteeing the debt of someone he does not know, the lender should “take the garment” of the guarantor as security. A man who pledges for strangers is careless with his commitments, and the lender is wise to protect himself. Proverbs has warned the guarantor before, in 6:1–5, 11:15 and 17:18; here the warning comes from the lender’s side.

“And hold it as a pledge when it is for a seductress.”

If the guarantor has pledged on behalf of “a seductress,” an immoral woman, he is an even poorer risk. A man that rash with his commitments and his company cannot be trusted to pay, so the lender should hold the pledge firmly. The saying is dry and realistic. It warns against rash surety by showing how others will see it, and it implies that a person known for careless pledges loses the trust of those who deal with him. The law also limited such pledges with mercy, requiring a garment taken in pledge to be returned before sunset, as Exodus 22:26 directs. The saying warns against rash pledges; it does not forbid all help to others.

Study Notes

This verse is repeated almost exactly at 27:13.

The Hebrew text has two readings in the second line. The written text has ‘foreigners,’ while the reading noted in the margin of the Hebrew manuscripts has ‘a foreign woman.’ The NKJV and KJV follow the marginal reading, and the ESV and NASB follow the written text. At 27:13, where the saying is repeated, the Hebrew has only the feminine form, and the NKJV reads “seductress” there as well.

Proverbs 20:17
Proverbs 20:17
NKJV

Proverbs 20:17 Bread gained by deceit is sweet to a man, But afterward his mouth will be filled with gravel.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 20:17 Stolen bread tastes sweet,but it turns to gravel in the mouth. (NLT)

Main Idea

Bread gained by deceit tastes sweet at first, but afterward the mouth is filled with gravel. Dishonest gain delights for a moment and then turns bitter and painful.

Verse Explanation

Bread gained by deceit tastes sweet at first, but afterward the mouth is filled with gravel. Dishonest gain delights for a moment and then turns bitter and painful.

“Bread gained by deceit is sweet to a man,”

Gain by dishonesty tastes good at first. “Bread gained by deceit,” food or income obtained through lying or cheating, “is sweet to a man.” The pleasure is real, and part of what makes deceit tempting is that it often works for a while.

“But afterward his mouth will be filled with gravel.”

The second line turns on the word “afterward.” The same mouth that tasted sweetness will be “filled with gravel,” grit that breaks the teeth and fills the mouth with pain.
