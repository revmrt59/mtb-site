Chapter Study

Proverbs 21

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 21:1
Proverbs 21:1
NKJV

Proverbs 21:1 The king's heart is in the hand of the LORD, Like the rivers of water; He turns it wherever He wishes.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:1 The king’s heart is like a stream of water directed by the LORD;he guides it wherever he pleases. (NLT)

Main Idea

In the LORD’s hand the king’s heart is turned wherever He wishes, the way a farmer guides water through irrigation channels. Even the mightiest ruler is under God’s direction, which gives His people reason to trust Him above every throne.

Verse Explanation

In the LORD’s hand the king’s heart is turned wherever He wishes, the way a farmer guides water through irrigation channels. Even the mightiest ruler is under God’s direction, which gives His people reason to trust Him above every throne.

“The king’s heart is in the hand of the LORD,”

Solomon begins with the most powerful man in his world. A king’s heart was where wars were declared, taxes set, and justice given or denied for a whole nation. In Proverbs the “heart” is the inner person, the place of thought, will, and desire, and this saying places that center of royal decision “in the hand of the LORD.” The hand pictures control and keeping. No throne is so high that it stands outside God’s reach, and Solomon, himself a king, writes as one whose own heart answered to Someone greater.

“Like the rivers of water;”

The “rivers of water” here are most naturally the channels a farmer cut to carry water across his fields. In a dry land water was guided through small ditches, and with a few strokes of a hoe a farmer could open one channel and close another. The water flows freely and follows its own nature, yet it goes where the farmer sends it. That is the force of the picture. It does not explain everything about how God’s rule and a king’s choices fit together, but it shows both at work: the king really decides, and God really directs, without a struggle and without destroying the king’s own will.

“He turns it wherever He wishes.”

The LORD is the one who turns, and He turns the king’s heart “wherever He wishes.” Scripture shows Him doing this with rulers who did not know Him, as when “the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia” to send the exiles home (Ezra 1:1). The saying does not excuse a ruler’s injustice, and it gives no reason to stop praying for those in authority. It is the reason such prayer makes sense (1 Timothy 2:1–2). God’s people can trust Him above every throne, because He governs the hearts of those who sit on them.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “rivers” in “rivers of water” is the one used in Psalm 1:3 of the tree “Planted by the rivers of water” (Psalm 1:3). It means a channel or stream, often a channel cut for irrigation, rather than a great river. That is why the ESV and NLT render it “stream.”

The chapter opens and closes with the LORD’s rule over the two great powers of Solomon’s world, the throne (21:1) and the army (21:30–31).

Proverbs 21:2
Proverbs 21:2
NKJV

Proverbs 21:2 Every way of a man is right in his own eyes, But the LORD weighs the hearts.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:2 People may be right in their own eyes,but the LORD examines their heart. (NLT)

Main Idea

Every person thinks his own way is right, but the LORD weighs the heart. Self-approval proves nothing, since God looks beneath the choices to the motives that drive them.

Verse Explanation

Every person thinks his own way is right, but the LORD weighs the heart. Self-approval proves nothing, since God looks beneath the choices to the motives that drive them.

“Every way of a man is right in his own eyes,”

The saying widens from the king to every person. A man’s “way” is his course of conduct, the choices and habits that make up a life. “Right in his own eyes” describes self-assessment: from the inside, our own path almost always looks straight and justified. Israel’s darkest period is summed up in the same words: “everyone did what was right in his own eyes” (Judges 21:25). People are rarely the villains of their own stories. We explain, excuse, and approve ourselves almost by instinct, which is why our own verdict cannot settle the matter.

“But the LORD weighs the hearts.”

Against that self-approval stands the LORD who “weighs.” The picture comes from the marketplace scale, where goods were tested against honest weights. God does not stop at the visible path; He measures the heart, the motives and desires that drive the choices. Two people can do the same thing for very different reasons, and God weighs the difference. The line humbles the person who is sure of himself, and it also gives every reader somewhere to go. The One who measures the heart accurately is the One who can correct what He finds.

Study Notes

The verse nearly repeats 16:2, “All the ways of a man are pure in his own eyes, But the LORD weighs the spirits” (16:2). Such repetitions with small changes of wording are common in Solomon’s collection.

The same description of God, “He who weighs the hearts” (24:12), returns in the words of the wise, where it warns against pleading ignorance.

Proverbs 21:3
Proverbs 21:3
NKJV

Proverbs 21:3 To do righteousness and justice Is more acceptable to the LORD than sacrifice.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:3 The LORD is more pleased when we do what is right and justthan when we offer him sacrifices. (NLT)

Main Idea

Doing what is right and just pleases the LORD more than offering sacrifice. Worship is not set aside, but worship cut off from obedience cannot take the place of a righteous life (compare 1 Samuel 15:22).

Verse Explanation

Doing what is right and just pleases the LORD more than offering sacrifice. Worship is not set aside, but worship cut off from obedience cannot take the place of a righteous life (compare 1 Samuel 15:22).

“To do righteousness and justice”

“Righteousness” is right living measured by God’s standard, and “justice” is fair dealing, giving others what is due to them in court, in business, and in daily life. Together the two words describe a whole way of life toward God and neighbor. They name what the LORD chose Abraham’s family to practice, “that they keep the way of the LORD, to do righteousness and justice” (Genesis 18:19). The weight falls on the verb “to do.” The verse is about righteousness practiced, not merely admired.

“Is more acceptable to the LORD than sacrifice.”

Sacrifice was commanded by God and stood at the center of Israel’s worship, so the comparison is striking. It is more striking from Solomon, who at the temple’s dedication offered sheep and oxen “that could not be counted or numbered for multitude” (1 Kings 8:5). The saying does not cancel worship; it ranks it. An offering cannot stand in for a just life or cover a settled pattern of injustice. Samuel told Saul, “to obey is better than sacrifice” (1 Samuel 15:22), and the prophets said the same (Hosea 6:6; Micah 6:8). Coming right after 21:2, the saying follows naturally from it. The God who weighs hearts is not satisfied with ritual that leaves the heart and the life unchanged.

Study Notes

Sacrifice appears near the beginning and the end of the chapter. Here righteousness and justice please the LORD more than sacrifice, and in 21:27 the sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination.

Proverbs 21:4–8 — Pride, Haste, and Ill-Gotten Treasure

Proverbs 21:4
Proverbs 21:4
NKJV

Proverbs 21:4 A haughty look, a proud heart, (H3820) And the plowing of the wicked are sin.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:4 Haughty eyes, a proud heart,and evil actions are all sin. (NLT)

Main Idea

Haughty eyes, a proud heart, and even the plowing of the wicked are all sin. When pride sits at the center of a life, even ordinary work done from that heart is tainted by it.

Verse Explanation

Haughty eyes, a proud heart, and even the plowing of the wicked are all sin. When pride sits at the center of a life, even ordinary work done from that heart is tainted by it.

“A haughty look, a proud heart,”

The verse names pride twice, once on the face and once within. In Hebrew, “a haughty look” is literally height of eyes, the raised eyes of someone who looks down on others. “A proud heart” is the source of that look, a heart swollen with its own importance. The look shows the heart, and the heart produces the look. “A proud look” heads the list of things the LORD hates (6:17). Proverbs treats pride as a matter of the heart that the fear of the LORD exposes, because a right view of God leaves no room for an inflated view of self.

“And the plowing of the wicked are sin.”

The third item is unexpected. The “plowing of the wicked” is his ordinary work, the field he cultivates to make a living. Plowing is not wrong in itself, yet when it comes from a proud and godless heart, even everyday labor is counted as sin, because it is done without reference to God and for self alone. The verse names a look and a heart before any outward deed and calls all of it “sin.” Pride does not have to do anything dramatic to be evil in God’s sight. It colors everything a person does, including the work that looks perfectly normal.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word rendered “plowing” can also be read as “lamp,” and the ESV and the NASB (1995) translate it that way. On that reading the line speaks of the outward prosperity of the wicked, since a burning lamp pictures life and well-being in Proverbs (13:9). The KJV, like the NKJV, has “plowing,” with “light” in its margin.

Proverbs 21:5
Proverbs 21:5
NKJV

Proverbs 21:5 The plans of the diligent lead surely to plenty, But those of everyone who is hasty, surely to poverty.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:5 Good planning and hard work lead to prosperity,but hasty shortcuts lead to poverty. (NLT)

Main Idea

The careful plans of the diligent lead to plenty, but everyone who rushes ahead in haste comes to poverty. Patient, thoughtful work builds over time, while impatience cuts corners and loses what it grasps for.

Verse Explanation

The careful plans of the diligent lead to plenty, but everyone who rushes ahead in haste comes to poverty. Patient, thoughtful work builds over time, while impatience cuts corners and loses what it grasps for.

“The plans of the diligent lead surely to plenty,”

The diligent person in Proverbs is the steady, careful worker, the counterpart of the lazy man. Here the emphasis falls on his “plans,” his forethought. Diligence joins thinking to steady effort. He considers what needs doing, and then he keeps at it. Such plans “lead surely to plenty,” which describes the normal direction careful work moves in. Like the book’s other sayings about wealth, this states how God’s world generally works. It does not promise that every diligent person will become rich.

“But those of everyone who is hasty, surely to poverty.”

The contrast is not the one we expect. Proverbs usually sets the diligent against the lazy (10:4), but here the opposite is “everyone who is hasty.” The hasty person may be busy and may work hard, but he rushes ahead without thinking, grabs at quick returns, and cuts corners. The repeated “surely” shows how dependable both outcomes are. Hurry can empty a life as surely as idleness, and elsewhere the book warns that “he who hastens to be rich will not go unpunished” (28:20). The skill taught here is patience joined to planning.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 21:6
Proverbs 21:6
NKJV

Proverbs 21:6 Getting treasures by a lying tongue Is the fleeting fantasy of those who seek death.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:6 Wealth created by a lying tongueis a vanishing mist and a deadly trap. (NLT)

Main Idea

Treasure gained by a lying tongue is a vapor that vanishes, the chasing of those who are really seeking death. Wealth won through deceit does not last, and the pursuit of it leads toward ruin.

Verse Explanation

Treasure gained by a lying tongue is a vapor that vanishes, the chasing of those who are really seeking death. Wealth won through deceit does not last, and the pursuit of it leads toward ruin.

“Getting treasures by a lying tongue”

The verse looks at wealth gained through deceit: false claims, misleading promises, dishonest contracts. “Treasures” suggests more than a living; it is wealth stored up. The “lying tongue” is one of the things the LORD hates (6:17), and here it becomes a method of getting rich.

“Is the fleeting fantasy”

A “fleeting fantasy” has no substance and is quickly gone, like a breath of vapor on a cold morning that the wind carries off. Dishonest gain looks solid while it is being counted. It has no lasting foundation, and it vanishes.

“of those who seek death.”

The final phrase exposes the irony. The liar thinks he is pursuing treasure, but the verse counts him among “those who seek death.” He does not intend to die, yet the method he has chosen moves him toward ruin. Proverbs often describes sin as a road whose end is hidden from the one walking it, and this saying names the end plainly. The chapter returns to roads that end in death at 21:16 and 21:25.

Study Notes

Behind “fleeting fantasy” stand two Hebrew words. The first means vapor or breath and is the word Ecclesiastes uses for “vanity” (Ecclesiastes 1:2). The second means driven away, so the picture is a breath of vapor blown off by the wind.

The ESV closes the verse with a snare of death instead of seekers of death. The ancient Greek translation (the Septuagint) and the Latin Vulgate read “snares of death,” and some Hebrew manuscripts have the Hebrew word for “snares,” which differs by one letter from the word for “those who seek.” Most Hebrew manuscripts, followed by the NKJV and KJV, read “those who seek death.”

Proverbs 21:7
Proverbs 21:7
NKJV

Proverbs 21:7 The violence of the wicked will destroy them, Because they refuse to do justice.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:7 The violence of the wicked sweeps them away,because they refuse to do what is just. (NLT)

Main Idea

Violence carried out by the wicked will sweep them away, because they refuse to do what is just. The harm they plan for others turns back on them, and their own refusal of justice is the cause.

Verse Explanation

Violence carried out by the wicked will sweep them away, because they refuse to do what is just. The harm they plan for others turns back on them, and their own refusal of justice is the cause.

“The violence of the wicked will destroy them,”

“Violence” covers robbery, oppression, and every kind of harm done by force to gain an advantage over others. The wicked use violence as a tool, but the verse says it “will destroy them.” The Hebrew verb behind “destroy” means to drag or sweep away, so the picture is of the wicked being hauled off like a catch in a net. The harm they plan for others turns back and carries them off. Proverbs said the same of the violent gang in its first chapter: “they lie in wait for their own blood” (1:18).

“Because they refuse to do justice.”

The second line gives the reason. The wicked are not ignorant of what is right; they “refuse to do justice.” Their ruin is the outcome of a settled choice rather than an accident. The Hebrew verb translated “refuse” returns at 21:25, where the lazy man’s hands “refuse to labor.” In both sayings the trouble lies in the will. The verse is the reverse side of 21:3. The LORD prizes doing justice above sacrifice, and those who refuse to do it bring destruction on themselves.

Study Notes

The ESV and NLT render the verb “sweep” and “sweeps,” closer to the picture in the Hebrew than the NKJV’s “destroy.”

Proverbs 21:8
Proverbs 21:8
NKJV

Proverbs 21:8 The way of a guilty man is perverse; But as for the pure, his work is right.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:8 The guilty walk a crooked path;the innocent travel a straight road. (NLT)

Main Idea

Crooked is the way of the guilty man, but the conduct of the pure is upright. What a person is inside shows in how he lives, whether twisted or straight.

Verse Explanation

Crooked is the way of the guilty man, but the conduct of the pure is upright. What a person is inside shows in how he lives, whether twisted or straight.

“The way of a guilty man is perverse;”

A man’s “way” is his conduct, the course of his life. The guilty man’s way is “perverse,” twisted and crooked, full of turns and evasions. Guilt does not produce straight living. It produces concealment, and one cover-up leads to another. A person with something to hide cannot walk in a straight line, because he must keep adjusting his story and his path.

“But as for the pure, his work is right.”

The “pure” person is clean in heart and conduct before God. His “work,” what he does day by day, “is right,” straight and upright. The line is plain and undramatic, and that fits its meaning, since purity shows itself in ordinary honest work. The saying answers 21:2. There a man’s way is “right in his own eyes”; here the pure man’s work actually is right. The outward life reveals the inner condition, twisted or straight, and the God who weighs the heart sees both.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “guilty” occurs only here in the Old Testament, and its meaning has been debated. The KJV rendered the line with “froward” and “strange,” while modern versions agree with the NKJV’s “guilty.”

The word “right” in 21:8 is the same Hebrew word as in 21:2, so the contrast between seeming right and being right is verbal as well as conceptual.

Proverbs 21:9–19 — Justice and the Cry of the Poor

Proverbs 21:9
Proverbs 21:9
NKJV

Proverbs 21:9 Better to dwell in a corner of a housetop, Than in a house shared with a contentious woman.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:9 It’s better to live alone in the corner of an atticthan with a quarrelsome wife in a lovely home. (NLT)

Main Idea

It is better to live cramped in a corner of the rooftop than to share a whole house with a quarrelsome wife. The proverb pictures how strife in a home can make a spacious house unbearable, a warning that applies to husbands as well as wives.

Verse Explanation

It is better to live cramped in a corner of the rooftop than to share a whole house with a quarrelsome wife. The proverb pictures how strife in a home can make a spacious house unbearable, a warning that applies to husbands as well as wives.

“Better to dwell in a corner of a housetop,”

Houses in Israel had flat roofs used for work, storage, drying produce, and sleeping in hot weather. A “corner of a housetop” would be a cramped and exposed spot, open to sun, wind, and rain. This is a “better than” saying, and it names the lesser good first: a tiny, uncomfortable corner outside.

“Than in a house shared with a contentious woman.”

The alternative is a whole house, with all its room and comfort, shared with a “contentious woman,” a wife given to constant quarreling. The comparison shows that strife can make a spacious home unbearable. The worth of a home lies in its peace more than in its size. The saying speaks from a husband’s side because the book addresses a son, but it portrays strife in a home and is no joke about wives. Proverbs names the “contentious man” as well (26:21), and the warning applies to husbands as much as wives. Every member of a household either builds its peace or wears it down.

Study Notes

The saying is repeated almost word for word in 25:24, and 21:19 restates it with a harsher picture. The two contentious-woman sayings, 21:9 and 21:19, open and close this section of the chapter.

Proverbs 21:10
Proverbs 21:10
NKJV

Proverbs 21:10 The soul of the wicked desires evil; (H7451) His neighbor finds no favor in his eyes.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:10 Evil people desire evil;their neighbors get no mercy from them. (NLT)

Main Idea

Evil is what the wicked person’s soul craves, and he shows no kindness even to his neighbor. His desires are so bent toward harm that those closest to him receive no mercy.

Verse Explanation

Evil is what the wicked person’s soul craves, and he shows no kindness even to his neighbor. His desires are so bent toward harm that those closest to him receive no mercy.

“The soul of the wicked desires evil;”

“Soul” here means the inner self with its appetites and longings. The wicked person does not simply fall into evil now and then. He craves it. His desires have been bent until harm itself is what he wants. Proverbs often traces conduct back to desire, and this verse goes to the root: what a person longs for determines how he lives.

“His neighbor finds no favor in his eyes.”

The result shows up close to home. The “neighbor” is the person who lives beside him, someone he deals with every day. Even this person “finds no favor in his eyes”; he receives no kindness, pity, or grace. A heart that hungers for evil cannot spare mercy even for the man next door. The saying stands in a section about justice and the poor, and it describes the kind of heart that shuts out the needs of others, the same hardness 21:13 goes on to warn against. Wickedness begins as an appetite and shows itself as hardness toward the people nearest at hand.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 21:11
Proverbs 21:11
NKJV

Proverbs 21:11 When the scoffer is punished, the simple is made wise; But when the wise is instructed, he receives knowledge.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:11 If you punish a mocker, the simpleminded become wise;if you instruct the wise, they will be all the wiser. (NLT)

Main Idea

When the scoffer is punished, the simple learn wisdom by watching; when the wise is taught, he gains knowledge directly. The scoffer’s punishment serves as a lesson to others, while the wise person learns from instruction itself (compare 19:25).

Verse Explanation

When the scoffer is punished, the simple learn wisdom by watching; when the wise is taught, he gains knowledge directly. The scoffer’s punishment serves as a lesson to others, while the wise person learns from instruction itself (compare 19:25).

“When the scoffer is punished, the simple is made wise;”

The scoffer is the proud mocker who will not accept correction (9:7–8; 13:1). His problem lies in the will: he will not be wise. When he is punished he may learn nothing, but someone else does. The “simple” person, uncommitted and easily led, watches what happens and gains wisdom from it. Judgment on one man teaches others. The simple stands at a crossroads, and the scoffer’s punishment can turn him toward the right path.

“But when the wise is instructed, he receives knowledge.”

The wise person learns another way. He needs no painful example. When he “is instructed,” he “receives knowledge” directly. The verse sets three people side by side by how they learn. The scoffer may not learn even from his own punishment, the simple learns by watching, and the wise learns from a word. The same order appears in 19:25. The better course is to be the kind of person who can be taught by instruction and does not need hard lessons to see what is true.

Study Notes

Proverbs 19:25 makes the same point with different wording: “Strike a scoffer, and the simple will become wary” (19:25).

The Hebrew verb behind “instructed” in 21:11 is the same verb translated “wisely considers” in 21:12, and “understanding” in 21:16 is a form of that verb used as a noun. The verb has to do with insight, so the repeated word links these sayings: the wise gain insight when instructed, the Righteous One considers the house of the wicked with full insight, and the wanderer leaves the way of insight.

Proverbs 21:12
Proverbs 21:12
NKJV

Proverbs 21:12 The righteous God wisely considers the house of the wicked, Overthrowing the wicked for their wickedness.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:12 The Righteous One knows what is going on in the homes of the wicked;he will bring disaster on them. (NLT)

Main Idea

God in His righteousness takes careful notice of the house of the wicked and overthrows the wicked for their evil. He is not unaware of what goes on in their homes, and His judgment falls in its time.

Verse Explanation

God in His righteousness takes careful notice of the house of the wicked and overthrows the wicked for their evil. He is not unaware of what goes on in their homes, and His judgment falls in its time.

“The righteous God wisely considers the house of the wicked,”

The NKJV identifies the subject as “The righteous God,” the Righteous One who sees all. He “wisely considers” the house of the wicked, looking carefully and with full understanding at what goes on inside it. The “house” is both the dwelling and the household, the family and fortune the wicked man has built. It may look secure and prosperous from the street, but nothing in it escapes God’s notice.

“Overthrowing the wicked for their wickedness.”

God’s careful consideration leads to action. He overthrows the wicked “for their wickedness,” so the judgment matches the guilt. He is never hasty or careless in judgment; He considers before He overthrows. Believers troubled by the apparent success of the wicked can rest in this. The house is being weighed even while it seems to stand firm. The verse does not say when the overthrow comes. It says that the Righteous One sees, and that His judgment is just.

Study Notes

The Hebrew has no separate word for “God” in the first line. It uses “righteous” as a title, the Righteous One, and the NKJV supplies “God” to make the subject clear. The KJV instead reads the first line of a righteous man who considers the house of the wicked, and supplies God as the subject of the second line. The NKJV reading fits the second line, since overthrowing the wicked is God’s work.

Proverbs 21:13
Proverbs 21:13
NKJV

Proverbs 21:13 Whoever shuts his ears to the cry of the poor Will also cry himself and not be heard.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:13 Those who shut their ears to the cries of the poorwill be ignored in their own time of need. (NLT)

Main Idea

Whoever closes his ears to the cry of the poor will one day cry out himself and not be heard. Hardness toward the needy closes a person off from the mercy he will someday need; the warning targets a hard heart, not every unanswered prayer.

Verse Explanation

Whoever closes his ears to the cry of the poor will one day cry out himself and not be heard. Hardness toward the needy closes a person off from the mercy he will someday need; the warning targets a hard heart, not every unanswered prayer.

“Whoever shuts his ears to the cry of the poor”

To shut one’s ears is a deliberate act. The man hears the cry and chooses not to listen. The “cry of the poor” is the call for help from those without money or power, often in the face of injustice. The picture is of a settled habit, a hardened refusal of mercy. God’s own character stands behind the warning. He is the God who hears the poor, and of the poor man who cries to Him He said, “I will hear, for I am gracious” (Exodus 22:27).

“Will also cry himself and not be heard.”

The positions reverse. The man who would not listen becomes the one crying out, and now no one hears him. The ones who do not hear him may be other people who remember his hardness, or it may be God, whose mercy he refused to imitate. James states the principle: “judgment is without mercy to the one who has shown no mercy” (James 2:13). The verse warns against a hard heart. It does not turn every unanswered prayer into punishment for neglecting the poor. What it does say clearly is that no one can expect mercy while refusing to show it.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 21:14
Proverbs 21:14
NKJV

Proverbs 21:14 A gift in secret pacifies anger, And a bribe behind the back, strong wrath.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:14 A secret gift calms anger;a bribe under the table pacifies fury. (NLT)

Main Idea

A secret gift calms anger, and a bribe slipped under the cloak quiets fierce wrath. The proverb observes how hidden payments work to buy peace, without approving bribery, which Proverbs condemns when it perverts justice (17:23).

Verse Explanation

A secret gift calms anger, and a bribe slipped under the cloak quiets fierce wrath. The proverb observes how hidden payments work to buy peace, without approving bribery, which Proverbs condemns when it perverts justice (17:23).

“A gift in secret pacifies anger,”

The verse observes how life works. A gift given quietly calms someone’s anger. An angry person often cannot back down in public without losing face, and a private gift lets him lay his anger aside. Jacob sent gifts ahead to Esau for this reason, saying, “I will appease him with the present that goes before me” (Genesis 32:20). A quiet gift can be a wise way to make peace.

“And a bribe behind the back, strong wrath.”

The second line moves into darker territory. A “bribe behind the back,” hidden in the fold of a garment, quiets even “strong wrath.” The saying states a fact of life: hidden payments buy peace and influence. It does not approve the practice. The same expression appears where “A wicked man accepts a bribe behind the back To pervert the ways of justice” (17:23). Whether a gift is wise or wicked depends on its purpose. A gift that restores peace without twisting justice can be right, while a bribe that buys a verdict or excuses wrongdoing is condemned. Placed in a section about justice and the poor, the observation also shows how easily the powerful can be bought.

Study Notes

“Behind the back” renders a Hebrew word for the bosom, the fold of the robe where something could be carried out of sight. The NASB and KJV translate it “bosom.”

Proverbs 21:15
Proverbs 21:15
NKJV

Proverbs 21:15 It is a joy for the just to do justice, But destruction will come to the workers of iniquity.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 21:15 Justice is a joy to the godly,but it terrifies evildoers. (NLT)

Main Idea

Doing justice is a joy to the just, but it brings terror to those who practice evil. The same act of justice that gladdens the righteous is ruin to the wicked.

Verse Explanation

Doing justice is a joy to the just, but it brings terror to those who practice evil. The same act of justice that gladdens the righteous is ruin to the wicked.

“It is a joy for the just to do justice,”

For the just person, doing right is not a burden carried reluctantly. It is a “joy.” Such joy shows what God does in a heart. The just person takes pleasure in fair dealing, in giving people their due, and in setting wrong things right. The line also includes justice carried out in the courts, which gladdens the righteous because it protects them and vindicates what is good.

“But destruction will come to the workers of iniquity.”

The same justice that gladdens the just brings “destruction” to those who practice evil. The Hebrew word translated “destruction” usually means ruin, and many translations render it “terror” here, the dismay of evildoers when justice arrives. Justice does not change; what changes is a person’s relation to it. For the righteous, the arrival of justice is good news. For the “workers of iniquity,” those who make a practice of wrongdoing, it means exposure and ruin.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of the first line can be read as the just person’s own delight in doing justice, as in the NKJV, or as a statement about what happens when justice is done in the land. The ESV takes the second view, with “joy” for the righteous and “terror” for evildoers.

The second line is identical in the NKJV to the second line of 10:29, “But destruction will come to the workers of iniquity” (10:29).
