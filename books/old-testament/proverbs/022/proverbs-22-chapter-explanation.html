Proverbs 22

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 22:1
Proverbs 22:1
NKJV

Proverbs 22:1 A good name is to be chosen rather than great riches, Loving favor rather than silver and gold.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:1 Choose a good reputation over great riches;being held in high esteem is better than silver or gold. (NLT)

Main Idea

A good name, a reputation built on character, is worth choosing over great riches, and being well thought of is better than silver and gold. What people can trust in a person outlasts and outweighs anything he owns.

Verse Explanation

A good name, a reputation built on character, is worth choosing over great riches, and being well thought of is better than silver and gold. What people can trust in a person outlasts and outweighs anything he owns.

“A good name is to be chosen rather than great riches,”

A “name” in Scripture is more than a label. It is a person’s reputation, what others know and say about his character. A “good name” belongs to someone whose word can be relied on and whose dealings are honest. The saying is a choice between two treasures, and it says this one is “to be chosen,” deliberately preferred, over “great riches.” Money can be lost in a day, and it cannot buy back trust once trust is gone. A good name is built slowly through faithful living, and it outlasts whatever a person owns.

“Loving favor rather than silver and gold.”

The second line restates the first. “Loving favor” is the goodwill and esteem of others, being well thought of because of how one lives. It is worth more than “silver and gold,” the most precious metals of the ancient world. The verse does not despise money. It sets money in its place beneath character. Proverbs repeatedly warns that wealth gained by deceit or oppression destroys the very things this verse values, and the chapter will return to rich and poor in the very next saying, 22:2.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of the first line says simply “name”; the NKJV, with most English versions, adds “good” to bring out the sense. The KJV prints the supplied word in italics.

Ecclesiastes uses the same comparison: “A good name is better than precious ointment” (Ecclesiastes 7:1).

Proverbs 22:2
Proverbs 22:2
NKJV

Proverbs 22:2 The rich and the poor have this in common, The LORD is the maker of them all.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:2 The rich and poor have this in common:The LORD made them both. (NLT)

Main Idea

Rich and poor share one thing in common: the LORD made them all. Every person, whatever his wealth, stands before God as His creature with equal dignity, and that truth calls the rich to treat the poor with respect.

Verse Explanation

Rich and poor share one thing in common: the LORD made them all. Every person, whatever his wealth, stands before God as His creature with equal dignity, and that truth calls the rich to treat the poor with respect.

“The rich and the poor have this in common,”

The rich and the poor seem to have little in common. They live in different houses, eat different food, and stand in very different places in society. Yet the verse says they share one thing, and it is the most important thing about them.

“The LORD is the maker of them all.”

Both were made by the LORD. Every person, whatever his wealth or poverty, is God’s creature, and that gives each one dignity before Him. The truth works in two directions. It humbles the rich, who owe their life to the same Maker as the poorest beggar, and it lifts the poor, who are not lesser people in God’s sight. Proverbs draws the practical conclusion elsewhere: “He who oppresses the poor reproaches his Maker” (14:31). This verse does not explain away inequality or approve the way the rich sometimes treat the poor. It grounds equal dignity and equal responsibility before God, and it prepares for the warnings about the poor that run through the rest of the chapter (22:7, 9, 16, 22–23).

Study Notes

A similar saying appears in 29:13, “The poor man and the oppressor have this in common: The LORD gives light to the eyes of both” (29:13).

Proverbs 22:3
Proverbs 22:3
NKJV

Proverbs 22:3 A prudent man foresees evil and hides himself, But the simple pass on and are punished.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:3 A prudent person foresees danger and takes precautions.The simpleton goes blindly on and suffers the consequences. (NLT)

Main Idea

Seeing trouble coming, the prudent person takes cover, but the simple keep walking straight into it and pay the price. Wisdom includes foresight, and the uncommitted, careless person suffers for his lack of it.

Verse Explanation

Seeing trouble coming, the prudent person takes cover, but the simple keep walking straight into it and pay the price. Wisdom includes foresight, and the uncommitted, careless person suffers for his lack of it.

“A prudent man foresees evil and hides himself,”

The “prudent” man has practical good sense. He looks ahead and “foresees evil,” meaning trouble or danger coming his way, whether a bad deal, a dangerous companion, or the consequences of a foolish course. Seeing it, he “hides himself,” taking cover or changing direction before the trouble arrives. Wisdom includes foresight. The prudent person does not wait to learn everything by painful experience.

“But the simple pass on and are punished.”

The “simple” are the uncommitted and easily led, people who have not set their hearts on wisdom. They “pass on,” walking straight ahead without looking, and they “are punished,” suffering the consequences they could have avoided. Their problem is carelessness more than malice. They never stop to think where the road leads. The book’s opening lines promise “To give prudence to the simple” (1:4), and this saying shows why they need it.

Study Notes

The saying appears again, almost word for word, in 27:12.

Proverbs 22:4
Proverbs 22:4
NKJV

Proverbs 22:4 By humility and the fear of the LORD Are riches and honor and life.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:4 True humility and fear of the LORDlead to riches, honor, and long life. (NLT)

Main Idea

Humility and the fear of the LORD, a right view of God, bring riches, honor, and life. These blessings flow from a life bowed before God, as a general pattern of His goodness and not a formula for wealth.

Verse Explanation

Humility and the fear of the LORD, a right view of God, bring riches, honor, and life. These blessings flow from a life bowed before God, as a general pattern of His goodness and not a formula for wealth.

“By humility and the fear of the LORD”

Two qualities belong together here. “Humility” is a lowly view of oneself, the opposite of the pride Proverbs condemns. “The fear of the LORD” is a right view of God, seeing Him in His holiness and greatness and responding with awe, trust, and obedience. The two are closely linked. A person who sees God rightly cannot think too highly of himself, and humility is the soil in which reverence for God grows.

“Are riches and honor and life.”

From that root come “riches and honor and life.” These are the blessings God delights to give those who walk humbly before Him, and Proverbs speaks of them often. Like the book’s other sayings about wealth, this describes a general pattern of God’s goodness in His ordered world. It is not a formula that promises money to everyone who is humble. Many faithful people remain poor, and the book warns sharply against trusting riches, as in 23:4–5. “Riches” here is best understood as God’s blessing and provision broadly. The honor and life He gives last longer than any fortune.

Study Notes

The first Hebrew word of the verse, which the NKJV renders “By,” is a noun meaning consequence or reward. Read that way, riches, honor, and life are what humility and the fear of the LORD lead to, and the ESV and the NASB (1995) render the verse with “reward.” The KJV margin offers the same reading.

Proverbs 22:5
Proverbs 22:5
NKJV

Proverbs 22:5 Thorns and snares are in the way of the perverse; He who guards his soul will be far from them.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:5 Corrupt people walk a thorny, treacherous road;whoever values life will avoid it. (NLT)

Main Idea

Thorns and traps line the path of the crooked, but the one who guards his life keeps far away from them. The perverse walk into needless pain, while careful living avoids it.

Verse Explanation

Thorns and traps line the path of the crooked, but the one who guards his life keeps far away from them. The perverse walk into needless pain, while careful living avoids it.

“Thorns and snares are in the way of the perverse;”

The “perverse” are the crooked, those whose lives twist away from what is right. Their path is lined with “thorns and snares,” painful obstacles and hidden traps. Thorns scratch and slow the traveler; snares catch him without warning. The crooked person’s way is full of needless pain because he keeps choosing paths that lead into it.

“He who guards his soul will be far from them.”

The contrast is the person who “guards his soul,” watching over his own life with care. He “will be far from them,” keeping his distance from the crooked road and its traps. This is the same practical wisdom as 22:3: seeing danger ahead and avoiding it. The guarding is active. A person protects his life by choosing his path carefully, by refusing crooked ways, and by staying out of places where snares are set.

Proverbs 22:6
Proverbs 22:6
NKJV

Proverbs 22:6 Train up a child in the way he should go, And when he is old he will not depart from it.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:6 Direct your children onto the right path,and when they are older, they will not leave it. (NLT)

Main Idea

Parents are to train a child in the way he should go, God’s way, so that when he is old he will not turn from it. The saying describes how early formation usually bears fruit for a lifetime; it is a principle to live by, not a guarantee, and never a verdict against parents whose children stray.

Verse Explanation

Parents are to train a child in the way he should go, God’s way, so that when he is old he will not turn from it. The saying describes how early formation usually bears fruit for a lifetime; it is a principle to live by, not a guarantee, and never a verdict against parents whose children stray.

“Train up a child”

To “train up” a child is to prepare him from his earliest years for the life ahead. The Hebrew verb behind “train up” is the one used elsewhere for dedicating a new house or the temple, so it carries the sense of starting something on its intended course. Training includes teaching, example, correction, and patient practice over many years. The “child” may be anyone from an infant to a young man, so the work begins early and continues through growing up.

“in the way he should go,”

The phrase “the way he should go” has been understood in two ways. The preferred reading takes it as God’s way, the path of wisdom and righteousness that Proverbs describes throughout. Parents are to set their child on that road. Another reading takes it as the way suited to the child’s own nature and calling, training that fits who he is. There is truth in that second idea, since wise parents pay attention to each child, but the book’s constant concern with “the way” of wisdom favors the first.

“And when he is old he will not depart from it.”

The second line describes the fruit of such training. Early formation usually lasts a lifetime. Habits, convictions, and loves learned in childhood tend to stay. This is a general principle, not a guarantee. Children grow into adults who make their own choices, and some who were trained well still wander. The verse must never be used to condemn parents whose grown children have strayed, and it gives no ground for treating a child’s choices as merely the parents’ doing. What it gives parents is a charge to train faithfully and the hope that such training usually bears fruit.

Study Notes

Apart from this verse, the Hebrew verb behind “train up” is used only of dedicating a new house in Deuteronomy 20:5 and the temple in 1 Kings 8:63 and 2 Chronicles 7:5. In those passages it marks the beginning of a building’s intended use, and that background fits the sense of starting a child on his course.

The Hebrew wording behind “in the way he should go” is shorter than the English, literally in his way, as the KJV margin notes. The Hebrew does not say whose standard defines “his way,” and that is the reason for the two readings of the line.

The KJV margin also offers “Catechise” as an alternative to “Train,” reflecting the older understanding of training as instruction.

Proverbs 22:7
Proverbs 22:7
NKJV

Proverbs 22:7 The rich rules over the poor, And the borrower is servant to the lender.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:7 Just as the rich rule the poor,so the borrower is servant to the lender. (NLT)

Main Idea

The rich rule over the poor, and the borrower becomes the servant of the lender. Debt places a person under another’s control, and this plain observation counsels caution about borrowing without forbidding it altogether.

Verse Explanation

The rich rule over the poor, and the borrower becomes the servant of the lender. Debt places a person under another’s control, and this plain observation counsels caution about borrowing without forbidding it altogether.

“The rich rules over the poor,”

The first line states a plain fact of life in Solomon’s world and in ours. Those with money have power over those without it. The verse does not say this is how things ought to be. It describes how they are, and Proverbs elsewhere warns the rich not to abuse that power (22:16, 22–23).

“And the borrower is servant to the lender.”

The second line explains one way the rich come to rule. Debt places a person under another’s control. The borrower becomes the servant of the lender, bound by obligations he cannot easily escape. In the ancient world that could become literal servitude, when a debtor who could not pay was forced into service or could lose his children to the creditor as slaves (2 Kings 4:1). The saying is practical wisdom. It counsels caution about debt, especially for things a person does not need, and it encourages paying what is owed. It does not forbid all borrowing, and the law of Moses itself regulated lending to the poor. The chapter returns to the danger of debt in its warning against surety (22:26–27).

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 22:8
Proverbs 22:8
NKJV

Proverbs 22:8 He who sows iniquity will reap sorrow, And the rod of his anger will fail.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:8 Those who plant injustice will harvest disaster,and their reign of terror will come to an end. (NLT)

Main Idea

Whoever sows wrongdoing will reap trouble, and the rod he used in anger against others will fail. The oppressor’s power to strike will come to an end, and the harvest of his injustice will be his own sorrow.

Verse Explanation

Whoever sows wrongdoing will reap trouble, and the rod he used in anger against others will fail. The oppressor’s power to strike will come to an end, and the harvest of his injustice will be his own sorrow.

“He who sows iniquity will reap sorrow,”

The picture comes from farming. What a person plants determines what he harvests. The one who “sows iniquity,” planting injustice and wrongdoing in his dealings with others, “will reap sorrow,” trouble and grief. He may expect a profit, but the crop that comes up is misery. Scripture states the principle in many places, and Paul puts it simply: “whatever a man sows, that he will also reap” (Galatians 6:7).

“And the rod of his anger will fail.”

The “rod of his anger” is the power the unjust man uses to strike others, his means of oppressing and punishing those beneath him. That rod “will fail,” coming to an end. His power to harm does not last. In a section about rich and poor, the verse warns the oppressor that his ability to beat down the weak is temporary, and it comforts the oppressed that the tyrant’s rod will break.

Study Notes

The second line is difficult, and translations vary. The NKJV, ESV, and KJV say the rod “will fail,” and the NASB (1995) that it “will perish.” The KJV margin offers a different construction, that he will be consumed with the rod of his anger.

The ancient Greek translation (the Septuagint) adds a line after this verse saying that God blesses a cheerful and generous man. Paul’s words in 2 Corinthians 9:7, “God loves a cheerful giver” (2 Corinthians 9:7), closely resemble that added line and may echo it. The added line is not part of the Hebrew text that the NKJV translates.

Proverbs 22:9
Proverbs 22:9
NKJV

Proverbs 22:9 He who has a generous eye will be blessed, For he gives of his bread to the poor.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:9 Blessed are those who are generous,because they feed the poor. (NLT)

Main Idea

The person with a generous eye will be blessed, because he shares his bread with the poor. A heart that looks for ways to give receives blessing from the God who cares for the needy.

Verse Explanation

The person with a generous eye will be blessed, because he shares his bread with the poor. A heart that looks for ways to give receives blessing from the God who cares for the needy.

“He who has a generous eye will be blessed,”

In Hebrew, “a generous eye” is literally a good eye. The eye shows what the heart is looking for, and this person looks for ways to help. He notices need and responds to it. Such a person “will be blessed.” The verse does not say how, but Proverbs consistently teaches that God takes notice of generosity toward the poor and does not forget it.

“For he gives of his bread to the poor.”

The second line gives the reason and the evidence. He “gives of his bread,” sharing from his own food, the most basic provision. Bread here is not a symbol for something vague. It is daily food, and he shares it with “the poor” who lack it. Generosity in Proverbs is concrete. It costs something and meets a real need. Set beside 22:7 and 22:16, the verse shows another way to use wealth: the rich can rule over the poor, oppress them, or share with them, and only the last brings blessing.

Study Notes

The opposite Hebrew expression, a bad or evil eye, describes a stingy, grasping person in 23:6 and 28:22, where the NKJV translates it “miser” and “evil eye.” The pair shows that in Proverbs the eye stands for a person’s attitude toward what he has and toward others’ needs.

Proverbs 22:10
Proverbs 22:10
NKJV

Proverbs 22:10 Cast out the scoffer, and contention will leave; Yes, strife and reproach will cease.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:10 Throw out the mocker, and fighting goes, too.Quarrels and insults will disappear. (NLT)

Main Idea

Drive out the scoffer, and quarreling goes with him; strife and insults come to an end. Sometimes peace requires removing the person who keeps stirring up contempt and conflict.

Verse Explanation

Drive out the scoffer, and quarreling goes with him; strife and insults come to an end. Sometimes peace requires removing the person who keeps stirring up contempt and conflict.

“Cast out the scoffer, and contention will leave;”

The scoffer is the proud mocker who will not accept correction and who stirs up trouble wherever he goes. When he cannot be taught, the verse gives a hard but necessary counsel: “Cast out the scoffer.” Remove him from the group, the household, or the assembly. When he goes, “contention will leave” with him. The quarreling followed him in, and it follows him out.

“Yes, strife and reproach will cease.”

The second line strengthens the first. “Strife,” the ongoing conflict, and “reproach,” the insults and shame that the scoffer’s contempt produces, “will cease.” Some troublemakers cannot be reasoned with, and peace in a community sometimes requires removing a person who keeps stirring up contempt and conflict. The verse does not encourage quick expulsion of anyone who disagrees. It speaks of the scoffer, the person who has already shown that he will not be corrected, as 9:7–8 describes. Patient correction comes first, but there comes a point when protecting the peace of the many is right.

Proverbs 22:11
Proverbs 22:11
NKJV

Proverbs 22:11 He who loves purity of heart And has grace on his lips, The king will be his friend.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:11 Whoever loves a pure heart and gracious speechwill have the king as a friend. (NLT)

Main Idea

Whoever loves purity of heart and speaks with grace will have the king as his friend. Inner integrity joined to kind speech earns trust even in the highest places.

Verse Explanation

Whoever loves purity of heart and speaks with grace will have the king as his friend. Inner integrity joined to kind speech earns trust even in the highest places.

“He who loves purity of heart”

The first quality is inward. This person “loves purity of heart.” He does not merely avoid obvious sin; he values a clean heart, sincere motives, and integrity that runs all the way through.

“And has grace on his lips,”

The second quality is outward. He “has grace on his lips,” speaking with kindness, wisdom, and courtesy. His words are pleasant and fitting. Inner purity and gracious speech belong together, because the mouth speaks from what fills the heart.

“The king will be his friend.”

The result is favor in high places. “The king will be his friend.” A wise ruler values people he can trust and whose words are both honest and gracious. Such people are rare in a royal court, where flattery and scheming are common. The saying describes the honor integrity often brings. As elsewhere in Proverbs, it states what is generally true, and it also shows what kind of person a good king should want near him.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of this verse is compact, and the KJV construes it differently: that the king is his friend because of the grace of his lips. The KJV margin gives the reading the NKJV follows.

Proverbs 22:12 The LORD preserves those with knowledge,but he ruins the plans of the treacherous. (NLT)

Main Idea

Knowledge is kept safe under the LORD’s watchful eyes, but He overturns the words of the faithless. Truth is under God’s protection, while the speech of the treacherous will not stand.

Verse Explanation

Knowledge is kept safe under the LORD’s watchful eyes, but He overturns the words of the faithless. Truth is under God’s protection, while the speech of the treacherous will not stand.

“The eyes of the LORD preserve knowledge,”

The “eyes of the LORD” picture His watchful attention over all things. Those eyes “preserve knowledge,” keeping true knowledge safe and guarding the people who hold it. Truth is under God’s protection. Whatever people may do to suppress or distort it, the LORD watches over what is true, and in the end it will stand.

“But He overthrows the words of the faithless.”

The contrast is the “faithless,” the treacherous people who break trust and twist the truth. God “overthrows” their words, frustrating their lies and schemes so that they do not stand. Their clever speech may succeed for a while, but it will not last. The verse fits the chapter’s concern with the poor and the powerful. Those who use deceit to gain an advantage over others will find that God Himself works against their words.

Proverbs 22:13
Proverbs 22:13
NKJV

Proverbs 22:13 The lazy man says, "There is a lion outside! I shall be slain in the streets!"(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:13 The lazy person claims, “There’s a lion out there!If I go outside, I might be killed!” (NLT)

Main Idea

Afraid of nothing but work, the lazy man insists there is a lion outside and that he will be killed in the streets. The absurd excuse exposes his habit of inventing dangers so he can stay at home, a mark of the lazy man throughout Proverbs.

Verse Explanation

Afraid of nothing but work, the lazy man insists there is a lion outside and that he will be killed in the streets. The absurd excuse exposes his habit of inventing dangers so he can stay at home, a mark of the lazy man throughout Proverbs.

“The lazy man says, ‘There is a lion outside!’”

The lazy man always has an excuse, and this one is absurd. He claims, “There is a lion outside!” He is not really afraid of a lion. He is afraid of work, and he invents a danger so he can stay home.

“I shall be slain in the streets!”

He adds drama to the excuse: “I shall be slain in the streets!” The exaggeration exposes him. The lazy man’s imagination is very active when it comes to reasons not to do what needs doing. Proverbs paints him the same way in 26:13, where he says, “There is a lion in the road!” (26:13). Laziness hides behind excuses that sound like caution. The verse is not about genuine danger or legitimate fear. It describes the habit of inventing obstacles to avoid responsibility, a habit Proverbs treats as both foolish and harmful.

Proverbs 22:14
Proverbs 22:14
NKJV

Proverbs 22:14 The mouth of an immoral woman is a deep pit; He who is abhorred by the LORD will fall there.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:14 The mouth of an immoral woman is a dangerous trap;those who make the LORD angry will fall into it. (NLT)

Main Idea

An immoral woman’s mouth is a deep pit, and the one under the LORD’s displeasure falls into it. Her seductive words are a trap, and the man who has already turned from God is the one who tumbles in (compare 7:21–23).

Verse Explanation

An immoral woman’s mouth is a deep pit, and the one under the LORD’s displeasure falls into it. Her seductive words are a trap, and the man who has already turned from God is the one who tumbles in (compare 7:21–23).

“The mouth of an immoral woman is a deep pit;”

The “immoral woman” is the adulteress of the book’s opening chapters, an outsider to the young man’s home and to God’s ways. Her “mouth” is her seductive speech: flattery, enticement, and promises of pleasure. That mouth “is a deep pit,” like the pits dug to trap animals, easy to fall into and almost impossible to climb out of. Chapter 7 showed her words at work: “With her enticing speech she caused him to yield” (7:21). The danger begins with listening.

“He who is abhorred by the LORD will fall there.”

The second line is sobering. The one who falls into the pit is “abhorred by the LORD,” a man under His displeasure. The line does not mean that God pushes an innocent man in. The man who has already turned his back on God, rejecting His word and His ways, has no protection against her words. His fall is both the result of turning from God and a judgment on it. The man who fears the LORD and keeps His word has a guard against the snare, as the father taught in chapters 5–7.

Study Notes

Proverbs uses the same image in 23:27, “For a harlot is a deep pit, And a seductress is a narrow well” (23:27).

The Hebrew word behind “immoral woman” is plural here, literally strange women, which the KJV (“strange women”) and ESV (“forbidden women”) reflect. The NKJV and the NASB (1995) render it as singular, treating the plural as a description of a type of woman.

Proverbs 22:15
Proverbs 22:15
NKJV

Proverbs 22:15 Foolishness is bound up in the heart of a child; The rod of correction will drive it far from him.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:15 A youngster’s heart is filled with foolishness,but physical discipline will drive it far away. (NLT)

Main Idea

Foolishness is tied tight in the heart of a child, but the rod of correction will drive it far away. Children need loving, consistent correction because folly comes naturally to them; the verse never justifies harshness, rage, or abuse.

Verse Explanation

Foolishness is tied tight in the heart of a child, but the rod of correction will drive it far away. Children need loving, consistent correction because folly comes naturally to them; the verse never justifies harshness, rage, or abuse.

“Foolishness is bound up in the heart of a child;”

Proverbs does not picture children as naturally wise. “Foolishness,” the moral folly that resists instruction and goes its own way, “is bound up in the heart of a child.” It is tied tight, knotted in, part of him from the start. Children do not need lessons in selfishness or defiance. That is why training and correction are needed, as 22:6 has already said. A child left to himself will not grow into wisdom on his own, as the book says elsewhere: “a child left to himself brings shame to his mother” (29:15).

“The rod of correction will drive it far from him.”

The “rod of correction” is the tool of discipline in its biblical setting, and the verse says it “will drive” foolishness “far from him.” The rod includes physical correction, yet the discipline Proverbs speaks of is broader than punishment. It is the whole work of teaching, guiding, correcting, and training a child toward maturity, and it reflects the way the LORD corrects the son in whom He delights, as 3:11–12 describes. As at 13:24, correction aims at restoration, turning a child from a harmful course toward what is right. It is calm, consistent, and given with love. It is never an outlet for a parent’s anger or frustration, and it never justifies harshness, rage, or abuse. The goal is a child set free from the folly that would harm him, one who has learned the right way and not merely learned to fear punishment.

Study Notes

Proverbs speaks of the rod in the correction of children at 13:24, 22:15, 23:13–14, and 29:15. The fuller discussion of discipline belongs to 13:24, and each later saying adds its own emphasis. Here the stress falls on the folly already present in a child’s heart.

Proverbs 22:16
Proverbs 22:16
NKJV

Proverbs 22:16 He who oppresses the poor to increase his riches, And he who gives to the rich, will surely come to poverty.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 22:16 A person who gets ahead by oppressing the pooror by showering gifts on the rich will end in poverty. (NLT)

Main Idea

Whoever oppresses the poor to build his wealth, or gives gifts to the rich to win favor, will surely come to poverty himself. Whether he squeezes the weak or flatters the strong, the man who chases riches in these ways ends up with less, closing Solomon’s collection on the theme of rich and poor.

Verse Explanation

Whoever oppresses the poor to build his wealth, or gives gifts to the rich to win favor, will surely come to poverty himself. Whether he squeezes the weak or flatters the strong, the man who chases riches in these ways ends up with less, closing Solomon’s collection on the theme of rich and poor.

“He who oppresses the poor to increase his riches,”

The first figure squeezes the weak to make himself richer. He “oppresses the poor,” taking advantage of people who cannot fight back, through unfair wages, crushing interest, or seizure of their goods, all “to increase his riches.”

“And he who gives to the rich,”

The second figure flatters the strong. He “gives to the rich,” offering gifts to powerful people to win favor or gain an advantage. One exploits those beneath him, and the other courts those above him. Both are using people to get ahead.

“will surely come to poverty.”

Both “will surely come to poverty.” The man who chases riches in these ways ends up with less. Oppressing the poor sets him against God, who defends them in 22:22–23, and gifts to the rich are often wasted on people who need nothing and give nothing back. The saying closes Solomon’s long collection on the theme of rich and poor that opened this chapter at 22:2 and returned at 22:7. Like other sayings about money, it states a general pattern and sets no timetable, but it warns that wealth gained by using people does not last.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of this verse is compressed, and translations arrange it differently. The 1984 NIV adds “both” to make clear that the two figures share one end.

With this saying the collection headed “The proverbs of Solomon” (10:1) comes to an end, and a new call to listen begins at 22:17.

Proverbs 22:20
NKJV

Proverbs 22:20 I have written thirty sayings for you,filled with advice and knowledge. (NLT)

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

“Have I not written to you excellent things”

The teacher asks a question that expects the answer yes. He has “written” these things, so the instruction is careful, preserved teaching rather than a passing remark. He calls them “excellent things,” words of high value and noble quality. The question underlines their worth and calls the son to take them seriously.

“Of counsels and knowledge,”

The content is “counsels and knowledge,” advice for living and understanding of what is true. Counsel tells a person what to do, and knowledge helps him see why. Both are needed, and both are given here for the son’s good. The sentence runs on into 22:21, which states what these writings are meant to accomplish.

Study Notes

The word translated “excellent things” is one of the hardest in the chapter. The letters written in the Hebrew text spell a word that literally means three days ago, the day before yesterday, and it is used in expressions meaning formerly or previously. A note in the margin of Hebrew Bibles directs the reader to a different word, which the NKJV, the NASB (1995), and the KJV understand as excellent or noble things. The ancient Greek, Syriac, Aramaic, and Latin translations have three times instead, and the YLT also renders “three times.” Many modern scholars read the margin’s word with different vowels as thirty, and the ESV and NLT translate “thirty sayings.”

The “thirty” reading is tied to the Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope, a wisdom text arranged in thirty chapters and preserved on a papyrus in the British Museum. E. A. Wallis Budge published the text in 1922 and 1923, and in 1924 Adolf Erman pointed out close parallels between it and the sayings that follow, especially 22:17–23:11, and proposed reading “thirty” here. Many scholars have discussed the parallels since. How the two are related, and in which direction any influence ran, is debated. Whatever their history, these sayings stand in Scripture under the purpose stated in 22:19.

“Do not rob the poor because he is poor,”

The first instruction of the new collection goes straight to the poor. “Because he is poor” names the robber’s motive. The poor man is easy to take from. “Nor oppress the afflicted at the gate;”

The “gate” of an Israelite city was where the elders sat and cases were judged. To “oppress the afflicted at the gate” is to crush the poor through the courts, by bribery, false testimony, or legal maneuvering. Injustice in the very place meant to provide justice is especially wicked. The “afflicted” are those already pressed down by poverty or trouble, and the command forbids pressing them further.

Proverbs 22:28

Proverbs 22:28 Do not remove the ancient landmark Which your fathers have set.(NKJV)

Proverbs 22:28 Don’t cheat your neighbor by moving the ancient boundary markersset up by previous generations. (NLT)

Main Idea

Do not move the ancient boundary stone that your fathers set in place. Shifting a landmark was a quiet way of stealing a neighbor’s land and inheritance, and the command protects what God gave each family (compare Deuteronomy 19:14).

Verse Explanation

Do not move the ancient boundary stone that your fathers set in place. Shifting a landmark was a quiet way of stealing a neighbor’s land and inheritance, and the command protects what God gave each family (compare Deuteronomy 19:14).

“Do not remove the ancient landmark”

A “landmark” was a boundary stone marking the edge of a family’s land. In Israel the land was divided among the tribes and families as their inheritance from the LORD, and the boundary stones recorded those divisions. Moving one, even a short distance, quietly took land from a neighbor. Because the stones were “ancient,” set long ago, few people might remember exactly where they belonged, and a dishonest man could take advantage of that. The law of Moses forbade the practice: “You shall not remove your neighbor’s landmark, which the men of old have set” (Deuteronomy 19:14).

“Which your fathers have set.”

The boundary was set by “your fathers,” the earlier generations who received the land. To move it was to steal both property and inheritance, the portion God had given a family to pass down. That is why Naboth refused to sell his vineyard to the king: “The LORD forbid that I should give the inheritance of my fathers to you!” (1 Kings 21:3). The command protects the weak, whose land was most easily taken, and it honors God’s gift to each family. The warning returns in 23:10 with special concern for the fatherless.

Study Notes

In Israel, moving a neighbor’s boundary stone fell under a public curse, pronounced before all the people: “Cursed is the one who moves his neighbor’s landmark” (Deuteronomy 27:17).
