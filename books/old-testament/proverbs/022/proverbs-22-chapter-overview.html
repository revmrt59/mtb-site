Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 22
Main Idea:

Proverbs 22 places rich and poor alike under the LORD who made them, prizing a good name above riches and forbidding the exploitation of the weak, and at 22:17 it opens the words of the wise, given so that the son’s trust may be in the LORD.

Setting:

Verses 1–16 close the collection headed “The proverbs of Solomon” (10:1). At 22:17 a new call to hear “the words of the wise” begins longer instructions addressed personally to the son.

Chapter Outline:

  • Proverbs 22:1–5 — A Good Name and the Maker of All

  • Proverbs 22:17–21 — The Words of the Wise

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Proverbs 22 opens by weighing two kinds of wealth. “A good name is to be chosen rather than great riches” (22:1), for the trust a man earns outlasts what he owns. Then every purse is set beside its owner’s Maker: “The rich and the poor have this in common, The LORD is the maker of them all” (22:2). The pair returns when “The rich rules over the poor” (22:7), and again with the man who squeezes the poor or courts the rich and “will surely come to poverty” (22:16).

Within those sayings stands the child. He is to be trained “in the way he should go” (22:6), God’s way, and the foolishness “bound up in the heart of a child” needs loving correction (22:15). Verse 6 describes how early formation usually bears fruit; it is no guarantee and never a verdict against parents whose children stray.

At verse 17 a teacher calls the son to “hear the words of the wise” and states his purpose: “So that your trust may be in the LORD” (22:19). Wisdom, the skill of living life God’s way, begins in leaning on Him. The first instruction returns to the poor, and now the LORD enters the courtroom: “the LORD will plead their cause” (22:23). The Maker of rich and poor is the advocate of the weakest.

Chapter Sections

Proverbs 22:1–5 — A Good Name and the Maker of All

A good name built on character outweighs great riches, and because the LORD made both rich and poor, every person stands before Him with the same dignity. The prudent see trouble coming and hide while the simple walk on into it, thorns and snares line the way of the perverse, and humility with the fear of the LORD leads to riches, honor, and life as a pattern of God’s goodness, not a formula for wealth.

Proverbs 22:6–16 — Training a Child and Justice for the Poor

A child trained in God’s way and corrected with love appears among sayings about the borrower’s bondage, the generous eye, and the oppressor whose rod of anger will fail. The scoffer must be driven out before strife will cease, the lazy man pleads an imaginary lion to stay home, the immoral woman’s mouth is a pit for the man the LORD abhors, and the section closes as it began, with the rich and the poor (22:7, 16).

Proverbs 22:17–21 — The Words of the Wise

A teacher calls the son to bend his ear, keep these sayings within him, and have them ready on his lips, so that his trust may be in the LORD. He has written excellent counsel so that the son may know what is certainly true and bring back a true answer to those who send him.

Proverbs 22:22–29 — Do Not Rob the Poor or Move the Landmark

The first warnings of the new collection forbid robbing the poor or crushing the afflicted at the gate, where cases were judged, because the LORD Himself will take up their cause against those who plunder them. The son must also keep away from the angry man whose ways he would learn, refuse pledges that could cost him his bed, and leave the ancient landmark where his fathers set it, while the man who excels in his work will stand before kings.

Why This Chapter Matters

Proverbs 22 puts money in its place. A good name is worth more than riches (22:1), and debt and surety are real forms of bondage that can take the bed from under a man (22:7, 27). That counsel calls for caution without forbidding every loan, and the riches tied to humility in 22:4 describe God’s general goodness to the humble, not a guarantee of wealth.

The chapter also shows how seriously God takes the poor. They share the same Maker as the rich (22:2), and He takes up their case against those who rob them (22:23), so cheating the weak sets a man against God Himself. For parents, 22:6 is a charge to train faithfully, not a promise that makes them answerable for every choice a grown child makes.

Key Themes to Watch For

Rich and poor before their Maker. The LORD made them both, the generous share with the poor, and those who rob the poor will meet Him as their defender (22:2, 7, 9, 16, 22–23).

A good name. Character and gracious speech are worth more than silver and gold and win the friendship even of a king (22:1, 11).

Training and correction. A child is trained in the way he should go, and loving correction drives out the folly bound up in his heart (22:6, 15).

Trust in the LORD. The LORD’s eyes preserve knowledge, and the words of the wise are written so that the son’s trust may rest in Him (22:12, 19).

Snares for the soul. Thorns and snares lie in the way of the perverse (22:5), and friendship with an angry man can “set a snare for your soul” (22:24–25).

Key Words & Concepts

A good name (22:1). A reputation built on character, so that others can trust what a person says and does. Its partner line, “Loving favor,” means being well thought of, and both are worth more than silver and gold.

Train up a child (22:6). To prepare a child from his earliest years for the life ahead. The phrase “in the way he should go” is debated; it most likely means the way of God, though some read it as the way that fits the child’s own nature. The saying is a principle of formation, not a guarantee.

The words of the wise (22:17). The opening of a new collection of longer instructions to the son. Its “excellent things” (22:20) translate a difficult word that some read as thirty, referring to thirty sayings; either way, the teacher points to the worth of what he has written.

Surety (22:26). Pledging oneself, by a handshake, to pay another person’s debt. The words of the wise repeat the warning of 6:1–5, for a pledge a man cannot pay may cost him his own bed (22:27).

The ancient landmark (22:28). A boundary stone marking a family’s land. Moving it quietly stole a neighbor’s inheritance, which the law of Moses also forbade (Deuteronomy 19:14).
