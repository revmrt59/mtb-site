Proverbs 23

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 23:1
Proverbs 23:1
NKJV

Proverbs 23:1 When you sit down to eat with a ruler, Consider carefully what is before you;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:1 While dining with a ruler,pay attention to what is put before you. (NLT)

Main Idea

When the son sits down to eat with a ruler, he is told to consider carefully what is set before him. A great man’s table is not simply a meal; it is a setting that calls for watchfulness and discernment.

Verse Explanation

When the son sits down to eat with a ruler, he is told to consider carefully what is set before him. A great man’s table is not simply a meal; it is a setting that calls for watchfulness and discernment.

“When you sit down to eat with a ruler,”

The teacher pictures a young man invited to a powerful man’s table. In the ancient world, a meal with a ruler was an honor and an opportunity, and also a test. Rulers used hospitality to judge people, win their loyalty, and place them under obligation. The son might be there to seek favor, to serve, or simply because he was invited, but in every case the setting calls for care.

“Consider carefully what is before you;”

The command is to pay close attention. In Hebrew, “Consider carefully” repeats the verb for understanding, literally understanding, understand. The doubling presses the son to give the scene his full attention, so he must not eat absent-mindedly. “What is before you” is first of all the food spread on the table, and it also includes the whole situation: the host, his purposes, and what the meal might cost. A great man’s table is not simply a meal. The son must keep his wits about him, notice what is being offered and why, and not let the splendor of the occasion dull his judgment.

Study Notes

The Hebrew phrase rendered “what is before you” can also mean who is before you. Most English versions, including the ESV and KJV, translate it as what, and both senses fit the scene.

Proverbs 23:2
Proverbs 23:2
NKJV

Proverbs 23:2 And put a knife to your throat If you are a man given to appetite.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:2 If you are a big eater,put a knife to your throat; (NLT)

Main Idea

Put a knife to your throat if you are a man ruled by appetite. The startling picture is deliberate overstatement, calling for decisive self-restraint at a table where indulgence could cost a person dearly.

Verse Explanation

Put a knife to your throat if you are a man ruled by appetite. The startling picture is deliberate overstatement, calling for decisive self-restraint at a table where indulgence could cost a person dearly.

“And put a knife to your throat”

The language is startling on purpose. To “put a knife to your throat” is not a literal command. It is deliberate overstatement, a vivid way of saying, restrain yourself as decisively as if your life depended on it. The throat is where food goes down, and the picture is of cutting off appetite before it can carry a person away. Proverbs uses strong images like this to make a point stick.

“If you are a man given to appetite.”

The warning is aimed at the man “given to appetite,” someone whose desires easily take control. In Hebrew he is literally a master of appetite, though in truth his appetite masters him. At a ruler’s table, such a man is in danger. Rich food and fine wine can make him forget himself, say too much, or put himself in the host’s debt. The person who knows he has a weakness in this area must take especially firm action. Self-control is part of wisdom, and here it is applied to the most ordinary of desires.

Study Notes

The word rendered “appetite” is the common Hebrew word often translated “soul.” It can name a person’s appetite or desire, and here it is the hunger that the knife at the throat is meant to restrain. The word for “throat” in the first line is a different Hebrew word.

Proverbs 23:3
Proverbs 23:3
NKJV

Proverbs 23:3 Do not desire his delicacies, For they are deceptive food.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:3 don’t desire all the delicacies,for he might be trying to trick you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Craving the ruler’s delicacies is dangerous, because they are deceptive food. The rich dishes may be offered for the host’s own purposes, so the guest who wants them too much can be drawn in and used.

Verse Explanation

Craving the ruler’s delicacies is dangerous, because they are deceptive food. The rich dishes may be offered for the host’s own purposes, so the guest who wants them too much can be drawn in and used.

“Do not desire his delicacies,”

The “delicacies” are the choice dishes of a ruler’s table, rich food the son would rarely taste. The command is not merely “do not eat,” but “Do not desire.” The danger lies in craving them, letting the appetite fix on them until it governs his behavior.

“For they are deceptive food.”

The reason is that they are “deceptive food,” in Hebrew literally bread of lies. The meal looks like favor and generosity, but it may not be what it seems. A ruler might use a lavish table to flatter, to obligate, to loosen tongues, or to discover a guest’s weaknesses. The food is real, but the message it sends may be false. The son who wants the delicacies too much can be drawn in and used. Wisdom sees past the surface of an offer to the purpose behind it. This is the first of several tables in the chapter where the food is not what it appears to be, and the miser’s table follows in 23:6–8.

Proverbs 23:4
Proverbs 23:4
NKJV

Proverbs 23:4 Do not overwork to be rich; Because of your own understanding, cease!(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:4 Don’t wear yourself out trying to get rich.Be wise enough to know when to quit. (NLT)

Main Idea

Do not wear yourself out trying to get rich; have the good sense to stop. Diligent work is commended throughout Proverbs, but exhausting oneself to make riches the goal is folly.

Verse Explanation

Do not wear yourself out trying to get rich; have the good sense to stop. Diligent work is commended throughout Proverbs, but exhausting oneself to make riches the goal is folly.

“Do not overwork to be rich;”

The second instruction moves from food to money. The son must not “overwork to be rich,” wearing himself out in the pursuit of wealth. Proverbs praises hard work throughout, so this is not a warning against diligence. The problem is making riches the goal and spending one’s strength, health, and attention chasing them. A person can work hard for good reasons, to provide for his family and to give to others, without exhausting himself for wealth as an end in itself.

“Because of your own understanding, cease!”

The second line calls the son to stop, and the NKJV ties that stopping to his “own understanding.” The most likely sense is that he should have the good sense to quit, using the discernment he has to recognize that the endless pursuit of wealth is folly. Money is a useful tool, but it is not everything, and the wise person knows when enough is enough. God promises to meet needs. He does not promise to make everyone rich, and the person who wears himself out for riches has misunderstood what life is for.

Study Notes

The second line is difficult and has been read in two ways. The NKJV, ESV, and NLT understand it as using good sense to stop. The NASB (1995) reads it as a command to cease from one’s consideration of wealth, that is, to stop thinking about it. The KJV reads it as a command to cease from one’s own wisdom, that is, to stop relying on one’s own cleverness to get rich, which recalls “lean not on your own understanding” (3:5).

Proverbs 23:5
Proverbs 23:5
NKJV

Proverbs 23:5 Will you set your eyes on that which is not? For riches certainly make themselves wings; They fly away like an eagle toward heaven.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:5 In the blink of an eye wealth disappears,for it will sprout wingsand fly away like an eagle. (NLT)

Main Idea

Will you fix your eyes on something that is here and then gone? Riches sprout wings and fly off like an eagle into the sky, so setting the heart on them means trusting what will not stay.

Verse Explanation

Will you fix your eyes on something that is here and then gone? Riches sprout wings and fly off like an eagle into the sky, so setting the heart on them means trusting what will not stay.

“Will you set your eyes on that which is not?”

The teacher asks a question that exposes the folly of chasing wealth. To “set your eyes on” something is to fix your attention and desire on it. Riches are “that which is not,” something that seems solid but has no lasting substance. The question asks whether the son really wants to give his heart to something that can disappear in a moment.

“For riches certainly make themselves wings;”

The answer comes in a striking image. Riches “make themselves wings,” as if money could suddenly sprout feathers. One day it is there, and the next it has flown. Markets fail, thieves steal, disasters strike, and wealth is gone. The word “certainly” stresses that this is no rare accident. It is the nature of riches.

“They fly away like an eagle toward heaven.”

The picture is completed. Like an eagle soaring upward until it is out of sight, wealth flies away beyond recovery. An eagle in flight cannot be caught or called back. Setting the heart on riches means trusting what will not stay. The saying does not condemn possessions or honest gain, but it warns against making them the anchor of life.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 23:6
Proverbs 23:6
NKJV

Proverbs 23:6 Do not eat the bread of a miser, Nor desire his delicacies;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:6 Don’t eat with people who are stingy;don’t desire their delicacies. (NLT)

Main Idea

The son should not eat the food of a stingy man or long for his fine dishes. After the ruler’s table comes the miser’s table, another meal where the food is not what it seems.

Verse Explanation

The son should not eat the food of a stingy man or long for his fine dishes. After the ruler’s table comes the miser’s table, another meal where the food is not what it seems.

“Do not eat the bread of a miser,”

After the ruler’s table comes another meal. In Hebrew the “miser” is literally a man with an evil eye, the opposite of the “generous eye” praised in 22:9. He is stingy, grudging, and always counting the cost. The son is told not to eat his “bread,” not to accept his hospitality.

“Nor desire his delicacies;”

As at the ruler’s table, the warning includes desire. The miser may set out fine dishes, but the son should not want them. The repetition of “desire his delicacies” from 23:3 links the two scenes. In both, the food is not what it seems, and the person who craves it walks into an uncomfortable trap. The miser’s food comes with strings: resentment, calculation, and an expectation of return. The next verses explain why.

Study Notes

The Hebrew behind “miser” is literally one who is evil of eye, the same expression translated “evil eye” in 28:22, where such a man “hastens after riches” (28:22).

Proverbs 23:7
Proverbs 23:7
NKJV

Proverbs 23:7 For as he thinks in his heart, so is he. "Eat and drink!" he says to you, But his heart is not with you.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:7 They are always thinking about how much it costs.“Eat and drink,” they say, but they don’t mean it. (NLT)

Main Idea

As he calculates in his heart, so he really is. He says, “Eat and drink!” but his heart is not with you, so his hospitality is a show that hides a grudging, counting spirit.

Verse Explanation

As he calculates in his heart, so he really is. He says, “Eat and drink!” but his heart is not with you, so his hospitality is a show that hides a grudging, counting spirit.

“For as he thinks in his heart, so is he.”

The key to the miser is what goes on inside him. “As he thinks in his heart, so is he” means that his true character is found in his private calculations, not in his public words. The context suggests a man who is inwardly counting every bite his guest takes. His real self is the grudging man inside, whatever face he shows outside.

“Eat and drink!” he says to you,

Outwardly he plays the generous host. He says, “Eat and drink!” and urges his guest to enjoy the meal. The words sound warm and welcoming, but they do not match his heart.

“But his heart is not with you.”

The truth is that “his heart is not with you.” His hospitality is a show. He resents what the guest eats and keeps a mental account of what it costs him. Eating at such a table puts the son in an awkward and false position, enjoying what is given without real goodwill. The verse teaches the son to look past words to the heart, and to recognize that real generosity is a matter of the heart, not just the hand.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb translated “thinks” appears only here in the Old Testament, and its sense has been debated. It is usually understood as calculating or reckoning, and several modern versions take it that way; the ESV has “calculating,” and the NLT speaks of thinking about the cost. That sense fits the miser who counts what his guest eats. The NKJV and KJV keep the more general “thinks.”

The line is often quoted on its own as a statement about thought determining character. In context it describes the miser whose inward calculation reveals what he really is.

Proverbs 23:8
Proverbs 23:8
NKJV

Proverbs 23:8 The morsel you have eaten, you will vomit up, And waste your pleasant words.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:8 You will throw up what little you’ve eaten,and your compliments will be wasted. (NLT)

Main Idea

The bite you have eaten you will vomit up, and your pleasant words of thanks will be wasted. Discovering the host’s real attitude turns the meal sour, and the polite conversation spent on him comes to nothing.

Verse Explanation

The bite you have eaten you will vomit up, and your pleasant words of thanks will be wasted. Discovering the host’s real attitude turns the meal sour, and the polite conversation spent on him comes to nothing.

“The morsel you have eaten, you will vomit up,”

The end of the meal is unpleasant. The “morsel” is the small bite the guest ate, and he “will vomit up” what he has eaten. The language may describe real sickness, or it may picture the disgust a guest feels when he realizes his host begrudged every mouthful. Either way, what seemed like a pleasant meal turns sour. Food eaten at a grudging table does not sit well.

“And waste your pleasant words.”

The “pleasant words” are the polite conversation and thanks the guest offered his host. They are wasted, spent on a man who never meant his welcome. The guest tried to be gracious, but his kindness was poured out on someone with no goodwill toward him. The lesson is to be discerning about whose hospitality one accepts. Some offers are worth refusing, because what is given with a grudging heart costs more than it is worth.

Proverbs 23:9
Proverbs 23:9
NKJV

Proverbs 23:9 Do not speak in the hearing of a fool, For he will despise the wisdom of your words.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:9 Don’t waste your breath on fools,for they will despise the wisest advice. (NLT)

Main Idea

Speaking wisdom in the hearing of a fool is wasted, because he will scorn the wisdom of your words. Some people will only despise good counsel, and wisdom knows when to keep silent.

Verse Explanation

Speaking wisdom in the hearing of a fool is wasted, because he will scorn the wisdom of your words. Some people will only despise good counsel, and wisdom knows when to keep silent.

“Do not speak in the hearing of a fool,”

The “fool” in Proverbs is not someone who lacks intelligence. He is morally deficient, someone who despises wisdom and instruction. The son is told not to “speak in the hearing of” such a person, meaning not to pour out wise counsel on someone who will not receive it.

“For he will despise the wisdom of your words.”

The reason is that the fool “will despise the wisdom of your words.” He will not just ignore them; he will treat them with contempt. Wise words spent on a fool are wasted and may even provoke him. Jesus gave a similar warning about casting pearls before swine in Matthew 7:6. Wisdom includes knowing when to speak and when to keep silent. That is not a refusal to love the fool. Some people are not ready to listen, and pressing wise words on them may only draw out their contempt. Proverbs elsewhere shows that there are times to answer a fool and times not to, as 26:4–5 shows, and discerning which is which is itself part of wisdom.

Proverbs 23:10
Proverbs 23:10
NKJV

Proverbs 23:10 Do not remove the ancient landmark, Nor enter the fields of the fatherless;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:10 Don’t cheat your neighbor by moving the ancient boundary markers;don’t take the land of defenseless orphans. (NLT)

Main Idea

Do not move the ancient boundary stone or encroach on the fields of the fatherless. Repeating 22:28, the warning now names the most vulnerable victims, orphans with no one to protect their family land.

Verse Explanation

Do not move the ancient boundary stone or encroach on the fields of the fatherless. Repeating 22:28, the warning now names the most vulnerable victims, orphans with no one to protect their family land.

“Do not remove the ancient landmark,”

The command repeats 22:28. The “ancient landmark” is the boundary stone that marked a family’s inherited land, set long ago and protected by the law of Moses in Deuteronomy 19:14. Moving it was a quiet way to steal land.

“Nor enter the fields of the fatherless;”

This time the warning names the most likely victims. The “fatherless” were orphans with no father to defend their family’s land. To “enter” their fields was to encroach on them, taking land a strip at a time, knowing no one would object. The fatherless had no one to protect them and no power to resist. The land-grabber assumed that no one would come to their defense. The command forbids taking advantage of the most vulnerable, and God’s law repeatedly singles out the fatherless for His special protection: “He administers justice for the fatherless and the widow” (Deuteronomy 10:18).

None.

Proverbs 23:11
Proverbs 23:11
NKJV

Proverbs 23:11 For their Redeemer is mighty; He will plead their cause against you.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:11 For their Redeemer is strong;he himself will bring their charges against you. (NLT)

Main Idea

Their Redeemer is mighty, and He will plead their case against you. The fatherless may have no human kinsman to defend them, but the LORD Himself takes up that role, the Redeemer whom the rest of Scripture reveals fully in Christ.

Verse Explanation

Their Redeemer is mighty, and He will plead their case against you. The fatherless may have no human kinsman to defend them, but the LORD Himself takes up that role, the Redeemer whom the rest of Scripture reveals fully in Christ.

“For their Redeemer is mighty;”

The reason for the command is that the fatherless are not alone. They have a “Redeemer.” In Israel the redeemer was the nearest kinsman, the relative whose duty was to buy back family land that had been sold, to free a relative who had fallen into servitude, and to defend the cause of a family member who could not defend himself, as Leviticus 25:25 describes. Boaz acted as redeemer for Ruth and Naomi. The orphan whose land is being stolen seems to have no such kinsman. The verse says he does, and his Redeemer “is mighty.” The LORD Himself takes up the role of next of kin for those who have no one else.

“He will plead their cause against you.”

The Redeemer acts as advocate. He “will plead their cause against you,” taking the case of the fatherless to court and arguing it against the one who wronged them. The same language described the LORD’s defense of the poor in 22:23. The person who moves an orphan’s boundary stone has not escaped notice. He has made an enemy of God. Job confessed, “For I know that my Redeemer lives” (Job 19:25), and the rest of Scripture reveals the Redeemer fully in Christ, who became our kinsman by sharing our flesh and who defends those who have no other defender.

Study Notes

“Redeemer” translates the Hebrew go’el, the kinsman-redeemer of Leviticus 25 and the Book of Ruth. The word names the close relative whose family duty was to buy back lost land, free a relative from servitude, and take up his cause. Applied to the LORD here, it means that the fatherless, who have no such relative, have God Himself acting as their next of kin.

Jeremiah uses closely similar words of the LORD’s defense of His exiled people: “Their Redeemer is strong; The LORD of hosts is His name. He will thoroughly plead their case” (Jeremiah 50:34).

Proverbs 23:12
Proverbs 23:12
NKJV

Proverbs 23:12 Apply your heart to instruction, And your ears to words of knowledge.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:12 Commit yourself to instruction;listen carefully to words of knowledge. (NLT)

Main Idea

Apply your heart to instruction and your ears to words of knowledge. Between warnings, the call to listen returns, for wisdom must be received with both heart and ear.

Verse Explanation

Apply your heart to instruction and your ears to words of knowledge. Between warnings, the call to listen returns, for wisdom must be received with both heart and ear.

“Apply your heart to instruction,”

Between warnings, the call to listen returns, much like the opening call of the collection in 22:17. “Apply your heart” means to bring the whole inner person to the task of learning. “Instruction” is discipline and training, the teaching that corrects and forms a person. The son is to set his heart on receiving it.

“And your ears to words of knowledge.”

The second line adds the ears. Wisdom must be received with both heart and ear: the ear hears the words, and the heart takes them in and acts on them. This brief call reminds the son that the warnings he is hearing are not random rules. They are instruction from a teacher who wants him to grow wise. It also prepares for the next section, where instruction takes the form of a parent’s correction (23:13–14).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “instruction” here, a word for discipline and training, is the same word translated “correction” in 23:13, which links the call to listen with the saying on discipline that follows.

Proverbs 23:13–18 — Correction, a Rejoicing Father, and a Sure Hope

Proverbs 23:13
Proverbs 23:13
NKJV

Proverbs 23:13 Do not withhold correction from a child, For if you beat him with a rod, he will not die.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:13 Don’t fail to discipline your children.The rod of punishment won’t kill them. (NLT)

Main Idea

Correction is not to be held back from a child, for if you discipline him with the rod, he will not die. Loving correction will not destroy a child, whereas the lack of it may lead him toward ruin; the verse never justifies harshness, rage, or abuse.

Verse Explanation

Correction is not to be held back from a child, for if you discipline him with the rod, he will not die. Loving correction will not destroy a child, whereas the lack of it may lead him toward ruin; the verse never justifies harshness, rage, or abuse.

“Do not withhold correction from a child,”

The command is to give correction and not hold it back. In Hebrew, “correction” is the same word translated “instruction” in 23:12, and it covers the whole work of training, teaching, and correcting a child. A parent may hold back out of busyness, a wish to avoid conflict, or a mistaken idea of kindness. The verse says that is not love. The book has already said, “he who loves him disciplines him promptly” (13:24).

“For if you beat him with a rod, he will not die.”

The second line reassures the parent: correction with the rod “will not” kill the child. Loving correction does not destroy a child, while the lack of it may lead him toward ruin. Parents who fear that correcting their child will harm him are told that the greater harm lies in neglecting it. The rod is the tool of correction in its biblical setting, and it includes physical correction. Yet the verse gives no license for harshness. As at 13:24 and 22:15, discipline in Proverbs is restorative. It is given calmly and with love, never in rage or frustration, and it never justifies abuse. Its aim is to turn the child from a harmful course toward what is right.

Study Notes

Where the NKJV and KJV have “beat,” the ESV and the NASB (1995) have “strike.” The Hebrew verb is the common word for striking or hitting, used of blows of every kind.

Proverbs 23:14
Proverbs 23:14
NKJV

Proverbs 23:14 You shall beat him with a rod, And deliver his soul from hell.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:14 Physical disciplinemay well save them from death. (NLT)

Main Idea

By correcting him with the rod you will rescue his soul from hell, here meaning the grave and the realm of death. Discipline turns a child from the path of folly that ends in death; it does not itself save the soul.

Verse Explanation

By correcting him with the rod you will rescue his soul from hell, here meaning the grave and the realm of death. Discipline turns a child from the path of folly that ends in death; it does not itself save the soul.

“You shall beat him with a rod,”

The first line restates the parent’s responsibility. Correction is to be given and not avoided. The repetition from 23:13 underlines the duty without making it a call to severity. It asks for the faithful correction that flows from love and aims at the child’s good.

“And deliver his soul from hell.”

The goal is rescue. “Hell” here translates the Hebrew word Sheol, the grave and the realm of death. To “deliver his soul” is to snatch his life away from the road that ends there. Proverbs repeatedly shows folly as a path that leads to death, “Descending to the chambers of death” (7:27), and loving correction turns a child off that path before it is too late. The verse does not mean that physical discipline saves a child’s soul or earns eternal life; salvation is God’s work. What correction does is pull a young person back from the way of folly and point him toward the way of life. It is an act of rescue, and like the book’s other sayings it describes what correction aims at, not a guarantee of how every child will turn out.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “hell” is Sheol, the Old Testament name for the realm of the dead. The ESV and the NASB (1995) keep “Sheol,” and the NLT renders it “death.”

Proverbs 23:15
Proverbs 23:15
NKJV

Proverbs 23:15 My son, if your heart is wise, My heart will rejoice"”indeed, I myself;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:15 My child, if your heart is wise,my own heart will rejoice! (NLT)

Main Idea

My son, if your heart is wise, my own heart will rejoice. The father opens a fresh appeal by telling the son how much his wisdom means to him personally.

Verse Explanation

My son, if your heart is wise, my own heart will rejoice. The father opens a fresh appeal by telling the son how much his wisdom means to him personally.

“My son, if your heart is wise,”

A fresh appeal begins with “My son.” The father speaks warmly and personally. His concern is the son’s “heart,” the inner person, and he hopes that heart will be “wise.” Wisdom here is more than knowledge in the head. It is skill for living, rooted in the heart.

“My heart will rejoice—indeed, I myself;”

The father’s response is joy. “My heart will rejoice” answers “your heart” in the first line, so a wise heart in the son produces a glad heart in the father. The added words “indeed, I myself” make it emphatic and personal. The father is not merely pleased with a well-behaved child; he himself is glad. Solomon’s collection opened on the same note: “A wise son makes a glad father” (10:1). By beginning this appeal with his own delight rather than with a threat, the father gives the son a reason to listen that is stronger than fear.

None.

Proverbs 23:16
Proverbs 23:16
NKJV

Proverbs 23:16 Yes, my inmost being will rejoice When your lips speak right things.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:16 Everything in me will celebratewhen you speak what is right. (NLT)

Main Idea

My inmost being will exult when your lips speak what is right. The father’s joy goes deep, and it is the son’s upright speech, the fruit of a wise heart, that brings it.

Verse Explanation

My inmost being will exult when your lips speak what is right. The father’s joy goes deep, and it is the son’s upright speech, the fruit of a wise heart, that brings it.

“Yes, my inmost being will rejoice”

The father’s joy goes deeper still. “My inmost being” stands for the depths of a person, the seat of the most personal feelings. His gladness is not polite approval. It reaches the core of who he is.

“When your lips speak right things.”

What brings this joy is the son’s speech, “When your lips speak right things.” Upright speech is the fruit of a wise heart, so the heart of 23:15 shows itself through the lips of 23:16. Later in the chapter the drunkard’s heart “will utter perverse things” (23:33), and the contrast is plain. Words reveal what is inside, and a father rejoices when his son’s words are right.

Study Notes

“Inmost being” is literally kidneys, which the KJV renders “reins.” In Hebrew idiom the kidneys represent the deepest emotions and thoughts.

Proverbs 23:17
Proverbs 23:17
NKJV

Proverbs 23:17 Do not let your heart envy sinners, But be zealous for the fear of the LORD all the day;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 23:17 Don’t envy sinners,but always continue to fear the LORD. (NLT)

Main Idea

Do not let your heart envy sinners, but be zealous for the fear of the LORD all day long. Instead of looking enviously at those who seem to prosper through sin, the son is to keep his passion fixed on a right view of God.

Verse Explanation

Do not let your heart envy sinners, but be zealous for the fear of the LORD all day long. Instead of looking enviously at those who seem to prosper through sin, the son is to keep his passion fixed on a right view of God.

“Do not let your heart envy sinners,”

The son might look at sinners who seem to prosper and feel envy. They enjoy comfort, success, and freedom from the restraints he lives under. Envy begins in the heart, and the father warns against letting it take root. Envy of the wicked is a recurring danger in the words of the wise (24:1, 19), because it can lead a person to leave the right path in order to imitate those who seem to be getting ahead.

“But be zealous for the fear of the LORD all the day;”

In place of envy, the son is to be zealous for “the fear of the LORD,” a right view of God that produces awe, trust, and obedience. Zeal is passionate devotion, and it is to last “all the day,” without interruption. The cure for envy is more than ceasing to want what sinners have. Now the drinker speaks. He boasts that he was struck and not hurt. He has been in a fight or beaten, but the drink numbed him to the pain, and he takes his numbness as proof that nothing is wrong.

“They have beaten me, but I did not feel it.”

He says it again: he was beaten and did not feel it. The drink has dulled his senses so that he cannot feel the harm being done to him. The numbness that seems like a mercy is itself part of the danger.

“When shall I awake, that I may seek another drink?”

The poem ends with his question. Bruised and dazed, his first thought on waking is to drink again. This is the voice of bondage. He is enslaved to the very thing that is ruining him. The portrait is sober and true, and it is given so that the son will see where the road leads before he takes it.

It is not the final word on those who are enslaved. Paul names drunkards among those who will not inherit the kingdom of God, and then he writes, “And such were some of you. But you were washed, but you were sanctified, but you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus and by the Spirit of our God” (1 Corinthians 6:11). No bondage is beyond God’s power to wash and set free.
