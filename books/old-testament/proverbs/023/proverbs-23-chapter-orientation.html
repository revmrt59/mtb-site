Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 23
Main Idea:

Proverbs 23 warns the son against tables, riches, and wine that promise more than they give, and sets in their place a father’s plea for his whole heart and the LORD, the mighty Redeemer of the fatherless.

Setting:

The chapter continues “the words of the wise” begun at 22:17, longer instructions in which a teacher speaks as a father to “my son.” Fresh appeals open at verses 15 and 19, and a closing poem portrays the drunkard.

Chapter Outline:

  • Proverbs 23:1–3 — Restraint at a Ruler’s Table

  • Proverbs 23:4–8 — Riches That Fly Away and the Miser’s Table

  • Proverbs 23:9–12 — The Fatherless and Their Mighty Redeemer

  • Proverbs 23:13–18 — Correction, a Rejoicing Father, and a Sure Hope

  • Proverbs 23:19–28 — My Son, Give Me Your Heart

  • Proverbs 23:29–35 — Who Has Woe?

Key Verse:

“My son, give me your heart, And let your eyes observe my ways.” (Proverbs 23:26)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Much of Proverbs 23 happens at the table. The son sits before a ruler’s delicacies, then before a miser whose welcome hides the fact that “his heart is not with you” (23:7), then among “winebibbers” (23:20), and finally before a cup of wine that “sparkles” and “swirls around smoothly” (23:31). Each offer looks generous and hides a cost. Riches belong in the same company, for they “make themselves wings” (23:5). Here wisdom, the skill of living life God’s way, looks like self-control: desire for food, money, or drink must be ruled, or it will rule the man.

Against these false hosts the chapter sets two who can be trusted. One is the LORD. The fatherless may have no kinsman left, yet “their Redeemer is mighty” (23:11). The other is the father, whose inmost being rejoices when his son’s “lips speak right things” (23:16). He calls the son away from envying sinners to zeal for the fear of the LORD, a right view of God, because “your hope will not be cut off” (23:18). His plea, “My son, give me your heart” (23:26), asks for what the ruler, the miser, the harlot, and the wine all try to capture. Through a human father’s voice, God’s own fatherly care asks for the whole heart.

Chapter Sections

Proverbs 23:1–3 — Restraint at a Ruler’s Table

A guest at a ruler’s table is told to notice what is set before him and to curb his appetite as firmly as if he held a knife to his own throat, a deliberate overstatement. The rich dishes are “deceptive food” (23:3), served for the host’s purposes, so the man who craves them is easily used.

Proverbs 23:4–8 — Riches That Fly Away and the Miser’s Table

The son is told to stop wearing himself out for wealth, because riches sprout wings and are gone like an eagle into the sky; the warning is against making money the goal, not against honest work. A second table follows, where a stingy host urges his guest to eat while privately counting the cost, and the meal ends in nausea and wasted thanks.

Proverbs 23:9–12 — The Fatherless and Their Mighty Redeemer

Wise words spent on a fool are lost, because he will only despise them. The ancient landmark of 22:28 returns with orphans named as the ones robbed when it is moved, and the warning rests on the LORD Himself, their mighty Redeemer, who will take up their case; then the son is called once more to bring heart and ears to instruction.

Proverbs 23:13–18 — Correction, a Rejoicing Father, and a Sure Hope

Loving correction will not destroy a child but turns him from the path that ends in the grave, and these verses never license harshness, rage, or abuse. The father then speaks of the deep gladness a wise heart and upright speech give him, and he tells his son to trade envy of sinners for zeal for the fear of the LORD all day long, because the hope of the righteous will not be cut off.

Proverbs 23:19–28 — My Son, Give Me Your Heart

A second appeal asks the son to steer his heart in the right way, to keep clear of heavy drinkers and gluttons who end in rags, and to listen to the father who begot him and the mother who bore him, even in her old age. Truth is worth buying at any price and must never be sold, and the father asks for his son’s heart because the harlot is a pit that is easy to fall into and hard to climb out of.

Proverbs 23:29–35 — Who Has Woe?

Six questions about woe, quarrels, wounds, and bloodshot eyes find one answer: those who linger long at the wine. The poem moves from the wine’s red sparkle to its serpent’s bite, to a mind that sees strange things and a body reeling as if at sea, and it ends with the drinker, beaten and numb, already wanting another drink.

Why This Chapter Matters

Proverbs 23 treats appetite as a spiritual matter. Gluttony, the love of money, and drunkenness appear as one danger: a desire that promises satisfaction and then leaves a man sick, in rags, or enslaved (23:8, 21, 35). The drunkard’s portrait ends in his own words, so the reader hears how bondage talks, numb to its injuries. Scripture elsewhere shows that such bondage is not beyond God’s power to wash and set free (1 Corinthians 6:10–11).

The chapter also shows whose side God takes. An orphan whose boundary stone is moved has a mighty Redeemer, the kinsman-defender whom the rest of Scripture reveals fully in Christ. The rod of 23:13–14 belongs beside a father who delights in his son, so correction aims at rescue from the path of death and never excuses harshness, rage, or abuse.

Key Themes to Watch For

Appetite under rule. Food, wealth, and wine all promise more than they give, and the wise restrain desire before it masters them (23:1–5, 20–21, 31–35).

What a host really thinks. The ruler’s food is deceptive (23:3) and the miser’s welcome is empty (23:6–8), for as a man “thinks in his heart, so is he” (23:7).

The LORD defends the vulnerable. The fatherless have a Redeemer who pleads their cause against anyone who takes their land (23:10–11).

A heart given to the father. A wise heart and right speech bring parents joy, and the father asks for the son’s heart and attention to his ways (23:15–16, 19, 24–26).

Hope instead of envy. Zeal for the fear of the LORD replaces envy of sinners, because the righteous have a future that will not fail (23:17–18).

Key Words & Concepts

Deceptive food (23:3). Rich food set out by a ruler for his own ends. The meal looks like favor, but it may be a lure, which is why the guest must “Consider carefully what is before you” (23:1).

Redeemer (23:11). The Hebrew go’el is the near kinsman who defends and restores a vulnerable relative. When the fatherless have no human kinsman to stand up for them, the LORD takes that role Himself (compare Job 19:25), and the canon reveals the Redeemer fully in Christ.

Hell (23:14). Here the word means Sheol, the grave and the realm of death. Correction rescues a child from the path of folly that leads there; it does not itself save the soul.

Hereafter (23:18). A future or outcome. The righteous have an end ahead of them that will not fail, while the sinner’s success has no future. Whether the word reaches beyond death is debated.

Harlot and seductress (23:27). Two names for the woman who ensnares men, pictured as “a deep pit” and “a narrow well” (23:27). The seductress is the same outsider to the young man’s home and to God’s ways met in chapters 5–7, and the son remains responsible for guarding his own heart (23:26).
