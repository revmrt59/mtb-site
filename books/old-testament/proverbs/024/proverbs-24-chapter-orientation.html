Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 24
Main Idea:

Proverbs 24 sets a house built by wisdom beside a field ruined by neglect, and between them calls the son to rescue the perishing, rise again after trouble, refuse gloating and revenge, and fear the LORD.

Setting:

Verses 1–22 finish “the words of the wise” begun at 22:17, still addressed to “My son” (24:13, 21). A short appendix, “These things also belong to the wise” (24:23), ends at the lazy man’s field.

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Proverbs 24 opens with a house and closes with a field. “Through wisdom a house is built” (24:3), and knowledge fills its rooms “With all precious and pleasant riches” (24:4). At the other end lies a vineyard where “Its stone wall was broken down” (24:31). No enemy did that damage; a little folding of the hands did. Between them stands the wisdom that builds: skill for living life God’s way, a strength that holds in adversity and moves to “Deliver those who are drawn toward death” (24:10–11).

The chapter keeps the son’s eyes on God. Whoever pleads, “Surely we did not know this,” faces “He who weighs the hearts” (24:12), who will “render to each man according to his deeds.” So the son must not gloat over a fallen enemy, “Lest the LORD see it” (24:18), or say, “I will render to the man according to his work” (24:29), because repaying belongs to God. The righteous may fall seven times and rise again (24:16), and the fear of the LORD, a right view of God, frees him from envying the wicked, whose lamp will be put out (24:19–21).

The appendix applies this wisdom to the courtroom and the field, ending with a wise man who learns from another’s ruin: “I considered it well” (24:32).

Chapter Sections

Proverbs 24:1–6 — Wisdom Builds a House

The son is told not to envy evil men, whose hearts and lips are busy with violence and trouble, because wisdom builds a house, understanding makes it secure, and knowledge fills its rooms with what is precious and pleasant. The same wisdom is strength, for a man wages war by wise counsel and finds safety among many counselors rather than going it alone.

Proverbs 24:7–12 — Strength in Adversity and Rescue for the Perishing

Three short sayings name the fool who has nothing to say at the city gate, the plotter who earns the name of schemer, and the scoffer whom people find detestable. Then the son is tested: strength that faints in adversity is small, and he must rescue those being dragged toward death, because the God who weighs the hearts sees through the excuse “Surely we did not know this” (24:12) and will repay each man for his deeds.

Proverbs 24:13–16 — Sweet Wisdom and the Righteous Who Rise Again

Honey is good and sweet to the taste, and the knowledge of wisdom is just as good to the soul, bringing a future and a hope that will not be cut off (compare 23:18). The wicked man is warned to his face not to raid the home of the righteous, for the righteous may be knocked down by trouble seven times and still rise, while the wicked are brought down by calamity.

Proverbs 24:17–22 — Do Not Gloat or Fret; Fear the LORD

The son must not be glad when his enemy stumbles, because gloating displeases the LORD, and he must not fret over or envy evildoers, whose lamp will be put out. He is to fear the LORD and the king and keep away from “those given to change” (24:21), most likely people bent on overturning order, whose ruin can come without warning.

Proverbs 24:23–29 — Further Sayings of the Wise

A new heading adds sayings that also belong to the wise: partiality in judgment is not good, and whoever calls the wicked righteous is cursed by the people and abhorred by nations, while honest rebuke brings blessing and a right answer is like a kiss on the lips. The son is to prepare his fields before he builds his house, to refuse to testify against a neighbor without cause, and to leave repayment to God instead of vowing to treat others as they treated him.

Proverbs 24:30–34 — The Field of the Lazy Man

The wise man walks past a lazy man’s field and vineyard, sees thorns, nettles, and a broken stone wall, and takes the lesson to heart. The ruin came through small, repeated rests, and want arrives as suddenly as an armed man; the warning concerns willful neglect, not need that comes from inability or genuine hardship.

Why This Chapter Matters

Proverbs 24 will not let wisdom stay at home. The chapter that praises a well-furnished house also sends the son to rescue those stumbling to the slaughter (24:11) and removes the excuse of not knowing, because God weighs the heart behind it. Strength that faints when rescue grows costly is small (24:10).

The chapter also guards the heart toward enemies. Envy, fretting, gloating, and revenge all let the wicked set the terms of a life, and Proverbs 24 forbids each (24:1, 17–19, 29). Jesus carries this further in commanding love for enemies (Matthew 5:44), and Paul writes, “Repay no one evil for evil” (Romans 12:17). The neglected field adds a quieter warning: ruin seldom comes in a day, though Proverbs never confuses laziness with genuine inability or hardship.

Key Themes to Watch For

Building and ruin. Wisdom builds and furnishes a house, and work is put in order before the house is built, while neglect leaves a wall broken down (24:3–4, 27, 30–31).

Nothing to envy in the wicked. Evil men plot violence and have no future, so neither their company nor their success is worth wanting (24:1–2, 19–20).

Courage to rescue. Adversity tests a person’s strength, and the God who weighs the hearts does not accept ignorance as a reason for standing by (24:10–12).

Rising again. Trouble may knock the righteous down again and again without finishing him, while calamity brings the wicked down (24:15–16).

Justice without favoritism or revenge. The wise judge fairly, rebuke the wicked honestly, refuse false witness, and do not pay back what was done to them (24:23–26, 28–29).

Key Words & Concepts

Schemer (24:8). A person known for plotting harm. What a man keeps planning in secret eventually becomes the name others give him.

An abomination to men (24:9). Proverbs often names what is “an abomination to the LORD” (11:1; 15:8). Here it is people who find the scoffer detestable, because his mockery of correction makes him repugnant even to others.

He who weighs the hearts (24:12). A picture of God measuring motives as if on a scale (compare 21:2). He knows whether “we did not know” is true, and He will repay accordingly.

Seven times (24:16). A way of saying again and again. In context the falls are troubles the wicked bring on the righteous, and the saying offers no excuse for repeated sin.

The fear of the LORD (24:21). A right view of God: terror for those who defy Him, and awe for all at His glory, majesty, and holiness, for He is unlike any other. It comes before respect for the king and never requires obeying a ruler who commands what God forbids (Acts 5:29).

The lazy man (24:30). The NKJV’s word here; it uses “sluggard” only in 6:6 and 6:9. He is also called “the man devoid of understanding” (24:30), because his trouble is a lack of sense and self-discipline, not a lack of ability.
