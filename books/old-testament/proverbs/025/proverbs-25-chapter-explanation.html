Proverbs 25

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 25:1
Proverbs 25:1
NKJV

Proverbs 25:1 These also are proverbs of Solomon which the men of Hezekiah kingof Judah copied:(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:1 These are more proverbs of Solomon, collected by the advisers of King Hezekiah of Judah. (NLT)

Main Idea

A fresh collection begins here: more proverbs of Solomon, copied and preserved by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah more than two centuries after Solomon’s reign. The heading shows the book being gathered over time, as a God-fearing king saw to it that Solomon’s wisdom was handed on to a new generation.

Verse Explanation

A fresh collection begins here: more proverbs of Solomon, copied and preserved by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah more than two centuries after Solomon’s reign. The heading shows the book being gathered over time, as a God-fearing king saw to it that Solomon’s wisdom was handed on to a new generation.

“These also are proverbs of Solomon” The small word “also” ties what follows to the Solomon collection already given in 10:1–22:16. A new gathering begins here and runs through the end of chapter 29, and it carries the same name and the same authority. Solomon “spoke three thousand proverbs” (1 Kings 4:32), so Scripture holds only a selection of what he said, and this heading tells us that a second selection was made long after his death.

“which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied:” Hezekiah reigned in Judah about 715–686 BC, more than two centuries after Solomon. Scripture remembers him as a king who “trusted in the LORD God of Israel” (2 Kings 18:5) and who reopened and repaired the house of the LORD in the first year of his reign (2 Chronicles 29:3). Men in his service took sayings of Solomon that had been preserved and copied them into this collection. The verse does not name these men, describe their sources, or say when in Hezekiah’s reign they did the work. What it does show is that the book of Proverbs was gathered over time, and that God used faithful servants in a later generation to hand Solomon’s wisdom on. Their work as copyists takes nothing away from the authority of the sayings; it is part of the way the LORD gave His word to His people.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb translated “copied” basically means to move or transfer something. Here it describes transcribing, carrying sayings over into a new written collection. The text does not say whether Hezekiah’s men worked from older written collections, from sayings handed down by memory, or from both.

This is the third heading in the book to carry Solomon’s name (1:1; 10:1; 25:1). Many sayings in chapters 25–27 are built as comparisons with “like” or “as,” while chapters 28–29 return to the righteous-and-wicked contrasts that dominate 10:1–22:16.

Proverbs 25:2
Proverbs 25:2
NKJV

Proverbs 25:2 It is the glory of God to conceal a matter, But the glory of kings is to search out a matter.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:2 It is God’s privilege to conceal thingsand the king’s privilege to discover them. (NLT)

Main Idea

God’s glory lies in concealing a matter, while a king’s glory lies in searching one out. The LORD’s ways hold depths no one can exhaust, and a good ruler honors his office by digging patiently for the truth before he judges, which sets the tone for a collection that rewards those who search.

Verse Explanation

God’s glory lies in concealing a matter, while a king’s glory lies in searching one out. The LORD’s ways hold depths no one can exhaust, and a good ruler honors his office by digging patiently for the truth before he judges, which sets the tone for a collection that rewards those who search.

“It is the glory of God to conceal a matter,” Glory is the honor or weight that makes someone worthy of respect. God’s glory includes what He keeps hidden. His ways and purposes run deeper than any creature can trace, and the fact that He does not explain everything is no flaw in Him. It belongs to His greatness, and it puts every human mind, including a king’s, in its proper place before Him.

“But the glory of kings is to search out a matter.” A king, by contrast, is honored when he investigates. In Israel the king served as the final judge in hard cases, as Solomon did in 1 Kings 3:16–28 when two women came before him with one living child. He had to examine the facts, test the claims, and find the truth before he ruled. A king who decided without searching dishonored his office.

The two lines set God and king side by side, each with his own glory. The king’s honor lies in diligent searching; God’s honor lies in depths that even the most careful searcher will never exhaust. Placed at the head of a collection full of sayings about kings, the verse puts the human ruler under God from the start. Because it honors searching, it also encourages patient study of God’s ways and of the sayings that follow. It does not turn every proverb into a riddle with a hidden meaning. The saying is first about God’s greatness and a ruler’s duty to find out the truth before he judges.

Study Notes

“Glory” translates the same Hebrew word in both lines. The chapter returns to it in 25:27, where seeking one’s own glory “is not glory.”

The verb “search out” comes from the same Hebrew root as “unsearchable” in 25:3. The wordplay binds the two sayings: kings search matters out, yet their own hearts cannot be searched.

Proverbs 25:3
Proverbs 25:3
NKJV

Proverbs 25:3 As the heavens for height and the earth for depth, So the heart of kings is unsearchable.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:3 No one can comprehend the height of heaven, the depth of the earth,or all that goes on in the king’s mind! (NLT)

Main Idea

Kings search matters out, yet a king’s own heart is beyond his subjects’ searching, as high as the heavens and as deep as the earth. Those who serve a ruler cannot fully read his mind, so they must carry themselves with care and humility in his presence.

Verse Explanation

Kings search matters out, yet a king’s own heart is beyond his subjects’ searching, as high as the heavens and as deep as the earth. Those who serve a ruler cannot fully read his mind, so they must carry themselves with care and humility in his presence.

“As the heavens for height and the earth for depth,” The saying measures with the two greatest dimensions an Israelite could imagine, the sky overhead and the depths of the earth below. No one could climb to the top of the one or dig to the bottom of the other. The comparison is meant to stretch the imagination as far as it will go.

“So the heart of kings is unsearchable.” In Proverbs the heart is the inner person: thoughts, intentions, plans and motives. A king’s heart lies beyond his subjects’ reach. They cannot know everything he knows, everything he weighs, or why he decides as he does. Coming right after 25:2, the saying has an edge to it. The king is honored for searching out matters, yet he himself cannot be searched out by the people under him. The king’s hidden counsel calls for care, respect and humility at court, which 25:6–7 will spell out in practical terms.

The verse does not place the king beyond God. Earlier the book said, “The king’s heart is in the hand of the LORD” (21:1). What is hidden from subjects lies open to the LORD, who weighs every heart, a king’s included.

Study Notes

“Unsearchable” renders a Hebrew phrase that means, quite literally, that there is no searching.

Proverbs 25:4
Proverbs 25:4
NKJV

Proverbs 25:4 Take away the dross from silver, And it will go to the silversmith for jewelry.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:4 Remove the impurities from silver,and the sterling will be ready for the silversmith. (NLT)

Main Idea

Silver has to be purged of its dross before the silversmith can make anything fine from it. The picture from the refiner’s fire prepares for the next saying: what is valuable becomes useful only when what corrupts it is taken away.

Verse Explanation

Silver has to be purged of its dross before the silversmith can make anything fine from it. The picture from the refiner’s fire prepares for the next saying: what is valuable becomes useful only when what corrupts it is taken away.

“Take away the dross from silver,” Silver had to be heated in the refiner’s fire so that its impurities, the dross, could be separated from it before the metal could be worked. Refining was a familiar picture in Israel; the book has already said, “The refining pot is for silver” (17:3). The process takes away what does not belong so that what is valuable can show itself.

“And it will go to the silversmith for jewelry.” Once purified, the silver passes to the craftsman, who makes something fine from it. The silver was never the problem; the dross was. Remove the corrupting element, and what had value all along becomes useful.

Taken by itself the verse is simply a fact from the workshop. Its purpose appears in 25:5, which repeats the opening command and applies it to the royal court. The two sayings belong together, the picture first and then its meaning, and the reader is meant to carry the image of the refiner straight into the king’s presence.

Study Notes

The NKJV’s “jewelry” renders a general Hebrew word for an object, article or vessel; the ESV has “a vessel.” The KJV and YLT read the second line as a vessel coming forth for the refiner, but every translation keeps the same picture of purified silver becoming something useful.

Proverbs 25:5
Proverbs 25:5
NKJV

Proverbs 25:5 Take away the wicked from before the king, And his throne will be established in righteousness.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:5 Remove the wicked from the king’s court,and his reign will be made secure by justice. (NLT)

Main Idea

The refiner’s image now applies to the royal court. When the wicked are removed from the king’s presence, his throne will be “established in righteousness,” because a ruler’s justice depends on the people he keeps close to him.

Verse Explanation

The refiner’s image now applies to the royal court. When the wicked are removed from the king’s presence, his throne will be “established in righteousness,” because a ruler’s justice depends on the people he keeps close to him.

“Take away the wicked from before the king,” The line repeats the opening command of 25:4, and the parallel makes the meaning plain: wicked officials and advisers are the dross in a kingdom. “Before the king” means in his presence, among those who stand near him, counsel him and carry out his orders. A ruler can mean well and still be bent by corrupt people around him, because he sees much of his kingdom through their eyes.

“And his throne will be established in righteousness.” To be established is to be made firm and secure. When the wicked are removed, justice can flow from the throne, and a throne grounded in righteousness stands. Proverbs has said the same thing in other words: “a throne is established by righteousness” (16:12).

The saying describes the way God has ordered His world. Righteousness gives a government stability, while wickedness eats away at it from within. Like the other proverbs, this is a general pattern; it does not promise that every righteous ruler will reign long or that every corrupt court will fall at once. It does show that the people a leader keeps close to him matter a great deal.

Study Notes

Other sayings picture the king himself removing evil: he “Scatters all evil with his eyes” (20:8) and “sifts out the wicked” (20:26). The refining image of 25:4–5 makes the same point with the picture of silver and dross.

Proverbs 25:6
Proverbs 25:6
NKJV

Proverbs 25:6 Do not exalt yourself in the presence of the king, And do not stand in the place of the great;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:6 Don’t demand an audience with the kingor push for a place among the great. (NLT)

Main Idea

Solomon warns against pushing yourself forward in the king’s presence or claiming a place among the great. Seizing honor at court is presumption, and the next verse gives the reason it is foolish.

Verse Explanation

Solomon warns against pushing yourself forward in the king’s presence or claiming a place among the great. Seizing honor at court is presumption, and the next verse gives the reason it is foolish.

“Do not exalt yourself in the presence of the king,” To exalt yourself is to put yourself forward, claiming honor or attention you have not been given. At court this meant pushing toward the king, displaying yourself, or acting as though you belonged among his closest men. The warning matters because the king’s presence is exactly where such self-promotion is most tempting and most dangerous.

“And do not stand in the place of the great;” The great are the nobles and high officials, and their place was the position near the king reserved for men of rank. To stand there uninvited is to assign yourself a status the king has not granted. The verse forbids presumption, deciding your own rank in a setting where only the king has the right to decide it.

The thought is unfinished, and 25:7 supplies the reason. Behind the court setting stands a truth found throughout Proverbs: “before honor is humility” (15:33), while pride goes before a fall (16:18). The humility asked for here is a matter of the heart, a willingness to let honor come from the one who has the right to give it.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 25:7
Proverbs 25:7
NKJV

Proverbs 25:7 For it is betterthat he say to you, "Come up here," Than that you should be put lower in the presence of the prince, Whom your eyes have seen.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:7 It’s better to wait for an invitation to the head tablethan to be sent away in public disgrace. Just because you’ve seen something, (NLT)

Main Idea

It is far better to be invited higher by the king than to be moved down in front of a noble you have seen with your own eyes, where the shame is public. Jesus drew on this wisdom in His parable of the wedding seats (Luke 14:8–11), where the lower seat reflects a humble heart rather than a tactic for getting honor.

Verse Explanation

It is far better to be invited higher by the king than to be moved down in front of a noble you have seen with your own eyes, where the shame is public. Jesus drew on this wisdom in His parable of the wedding seats (Luke 14:8–11), where the lower seat reflects a humble heart rather than a tactic for getting honor.

“For it is better that he say to you, ‘Come up here,’” Here is the reason for humility. It is far better to take a lower place and then be called up by the king himself, whose word confers honor in front of everyone present. Honor that is given is real honor.

“Than that you should be put lower in the presence of the prince,” The alternative is public demotion: to have claimed a place near the king and then be moved down while a noble looks on. The shame is the sharper because it happens openly, in front of the very people one hoped to impress. “Whom your eyes have seen.” In the NKJV the last line describes the prince. The demotion happens in front of a noble you have seen with your own eyes, so the shame is witnessed and public rather than a private correction. Honor seized can be taken away in a moment.

Jesus drew on this wisdom in His parable of the wedding guests. He told those who chose the best seats to take the lowest place instead, so that the host may say, “Friend, go up higher” (Luke 14:10), and He gave the principle behind it: “whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted” (Luke 14:11). Jesus used the proverb to expose a proud heart. The lower seat is the place of someone who leaves his honor in the hands of the one who has the right to give it; it is never a clever tactic for getting the higher one.

Study Notes

The ESV and the 1984 NIV join the final line to 25:8, so that it becomes the object of the warning about going to court: do not rush to bring before a judge what you have seen with your own eyes. The NKJV, NASB and KJV keep it with 25:7 as a description of the prince. The words can be read either way, and the NKJV follows the traditional verse division.

Proverbs 25:8–10 — Settle Disputes Discreetly

Proverbs 25:8
Proverbs 25:8
NKJV

Proverbs 25:8 Do not go hastily to court; For what will you do in the end, When your neighbor has put you to shame?(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:8 don’t be in a hurry to go to court.For what will you do in the endif your neighbor deals you a shameful defeat? (NLT)

Main Idea

Rushing a quarrel into court is unwise, because the case may turn, and you may end up the one put to shame when your neighbor answers you. A hasty accuser has not thought about where the matter will end.

Verse Explanation

Rushing a quarrel into court is unwise, because the case may turn, and you may end up the one put to shame when your neighbor answers you. A hasty accuser has not thought about where the matter will end.

“Do not go hastily to court;” The picture is someone who rushes out to bring a complaint or legal accusation against a neighbor the moment he feels wronged. In Israel such cases were heard publicly, often by the elders at the city gate. The problem the verse names is haste: acting before thinking the matter through and before hearing the other side.

“For what will you do in the end,” The question forces the angry person to look ahead to the outcome. Anger and offense want action now; wisdom asks where the action will lead. “When your neighbor has put you to shame?” The accuser may discover, in front of everyone, that he was wrong, that his evidence was weak, or that his neighbor has a better answer. Proverbs has already warned, “The first one to plead his cause seems right, Until his neighbor comes and examines him” (18:17).

The word “neighbor” matters too. The opponent is someone who will still live nearby after the case is over. A quarrel pressed too fast can cost a person his good name and his relationship with the one next door, even if he wins the point.

Study Notes

The Hebrew behind “go hastily to court” is a verb meaning to contend or quarrel, and the same verb is used for pleading a case before judges. The KJV translates it “to strive.” The NKJV’s “court” brings out the legal setting, while the Hebrew word itself covers any quarrel that is pressed too quickly.

Shame links this small group of sayings with the one before it: the proud guest may be “put lower” (25:7), the hasty accuser may be put to shame (25:8), and the betrayer of a secret may have his shame exposed (25:10).

Proverbs 25:9
Proverbs 25:9
NKJV

Proverbs 25:9 Debate your case with your neighbor, And do not disclose the secret to another;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:9 When arguing with your neighbor,don’t betray another person’s secret. (NLT)

Main Idea

Instead of going to court, settle the matter privately with your neighbor himself. In the course of that dispute, do not break a confidence by revealing someone else’s secret to win your argument.

Verse Explanation

Instead of going to court, settle the matter privately with your neighbor himself. In the course of that dispute, do not break a confidence by revealing someone else’s secret to win your argument.

“Debate your case with your neighbor,” The wiser course is to argue the matter out with the neighbor himself, privately and directly, instead of hauling him before the elders. Going straight to the person gives the dispute its best chance of being settled quietly and keeps it from becoming public. Directness here is a kindness to both sides.

“And do not disclose the secret to another;” In the heat of an argument a person is tempted to grab any weapon that might help, including something told to him in confidence. The verse forbids that. A secret entrusted to you is not yours to spend in winning a quarrel, even a quarrel in which you are right. Faithfulness is tested most when we are at odds with someone. Proverbs commends the one who is “of a faithful spirit” and “conceals a matter” (11:13). The verse is unfinished, and 25:10 gives the consequence of betraying a confidence.

Study Notes

The ESV, the 1984 NIV and NLT take the second line to mean another person’s secret, so that the forbidden act is betraying a confidence that belongs to someone else, whether the neighbor or a third party. The NKJV’s wording can carry the same sense.

Proverbs 25:10
Proverbs 25:10
NKJV

Proverbs 25:10 Lest he who hears it expose your shame, And your reputation be ruined.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:10 Others may accuse you of gossip,and you will never regain your good reputation. (NLT)

Main Idea

Betraying a confidence backfires on the one who does it. Whoever hears the secret may turn and expose your own shame, and the damage to your good name may never be undone.

Verse Explanation

Betraying a confidence backfires on the one who does it. Whoever hears the secret may turn and expose your own shame, and the damage to your good name may never be undone.

“Lest he who hears it expose your shame,” The person who hears the betrayed secret, whether the neighbor or a bystander, may turn on the one who told it. He now knows the teller cannot be trusted with a confidence, and he may expose him publicly. The one who tried to shame another with a secret ends up shamed himself.

“And your reputation be ruined.” The damage lasts. A person who betrays confidences in a quarrel becomes known as a betrayer, and that name follows him long after the dispute is forgotten. Together, 25:8–10 give one piece of counsel for conflict: do not rush to court, settle the matter directly with your neighbor, and never win by betrayal. Haste and betrayal both end in the shame of the one who chose them, while patient, honest dealing protects both the relationship and one’s own good name.

Study Notes

The second line uses a Hebrew word for an evil report, the damaging talk that spreads about a person, and says literally that this report will not turn back. The YLT keeps that wording, and the ESV renders the sense as ill repute that will have “no end.” Either way, the betrayer cannot call back the damage to his name.

Proverbs 25:11–15 — A Word Fitly Spoken

Proverbs 25:11
Proverbs 25:11
NKJV

Proverbs 25:11 A word fitly spoken is like apples of gold In settings of silver.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:11 Timely advice is lovely,like golden apples in a silver basket. (NLT)

Main Idea

A word spoken at the right time and in the right way is as lovely and valuable as golden apples set in silver. The fitness of the word, its timing and its setting, is what makes it so precious.

Verse Explanation

A word spoken at the right time and in the right way is as lovely and valuable as golden apples set in silver. The fitness of the word, its timing and its setting, is what makes it so precious.

“A word fitly spoken” A fitting word suits its moment, its hearer and its manner. The right truth spoken at the wrong time, too harshly, or to someone not ready to hear it can wound instead of help. Proverbs has already celebrated “a word spoken in due season” (15:23); this saying gives that truth a picture.

“is like apples of gold In settings of silver.” The image comes from fine craftsmanship: golden fruit, or ornaments shaped like fruit, set off by a frame or filigree of silver. The exact object is uncertain, but the point is clear. Gold is precious in itself, and it is more lovely still when set in a frame that suits it. So it is with speech. What is said matters, and so do when and how it is said. A wise person weighs both the content of his words and their setting.

This saying opens a short group (25:11–15) about speech and service that help: a fitting word, a rebuke well received, a faithful messenger, and patient, gentle persuasion. The next saying keeps the jewelry picture and applies it to correction.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “fitly” is rare, and its meaning is debated. It appears to come from a root meaning to turn, and a note in the KJV margin connects the phrase with wheels, as though the word runs smoothly in its proper course. Other translations take it as the word’s proper time or circumstance: the YLT has “fit times” and the NASB “right circumstances.” All of these renderings agree that the saying is about timeliness and suitability.

The Hebrew word rendered “settings” elsewhere refers to a carved figure or image, so the picture may be of carved silver work that frames the golden fruit. The KJV has “pictures,” and the NLT has “a silver basket.” Each rendering keeps the same image of gold set off by silver.

Proverbs 25:12

Proverbs 25:12 Like an earring of gold and an ornament of fine gold Is a wise rebuker to an obedient ear.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:12 To one who listens, valid criticismis like a gold earring or other gold jewelry. (NLT)

Main Idea

Rebuke can be as fine as an earring of gold, but only when a wise rebuker meets an obedient ear. The saying honors both people: the one who corrects with wisdom and the one who listens and receives it.

Verse Explanation

Rebuke can be as fine as an earring of gold, but only when a wise rebuker meets an obedient ear. The saying honors both people: the one who corrects with wisdom and the one who listens and receives it.

“Like an earring of gold and an ornament of fine gold” Two pieces of jewelry are named: a gold ring and an ornament of fine gold. Such things were valuable, worn with pride, and noticed by others. The saying chooses something people gladly put on.

“Is a wise rebuker to an obedient ear.” Correction is compared to jewelry, which may surprise anyone who has felt the sting of being corrected. The saying names two conditions. The rebuker must be wise, correcting with truth, good timing and care rather than irritation. The ear must be obedient, willing to listen and to act on what it hears. When the two meet, correction stops being an injury and becomes an ornament that adds honor to the one who receives it. The jewelry is pictured on the ear itself, the very place where the rebuke was heard.

The saying honors both people in the exchange. A foolish rebuker wounds without helping, and a closed ear wastes even the best correction. Proverbs repeatedly praises the one who receives correction (12:1; 15:31), and here that receiving is pictured as something fine.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “earring” is also used for a nose ring, as in Genesis 24:47, where the NKJV has “nose ring.”

Earlier the father said that the instruction of father and mother would be “a graceful ornament on your head” (1:9). Here the ornament belongs to the ear that receives a wise rebuke.

Proverbs 25:13
Proverbs 25:13
NKJV

Proverbs 25:13 Like the cold of snow in time of harvest Is a faithful messenger to those who send him, For he refreshes the soul of his masters.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:13 Trustworthy messengers refresh like snow in summer.They revive the spirit of their employer. (NLT)

Main Idea

Like the coolness of snow in the heat of harvest, a faithful messenger brings welcome relief to those who sent him. Someone who carries out his errand reliably “refreshes the soul of his masters.”

Verse Explanation

Like the coolness of snow in the heat of harvest, a faithful messenger brings welcome relief to those who sent him. Someone who carries out his errand reliably “refreshes the soul of his masters.”

“Like the cold of snow in time of harvest” The grain harvest in Israel came in the warm, dry months of spring and early summer, and workers in the fields longed for anything cold. Snow actually falling at harvest would harm the crop, and 26:1 uses that as a picture of something out of place. The picture is probably of snow carried down from the mountains to chill a drink, which would have been a rare and welcome relief in the heat. Whatever the exact custom, the point is cold refreshment in the hottest season.

“Is a faithful messenger to those who send him,” Senders depended on reliable messengers, because a messenger carried the sender’s words, business and reputation. Everything depended on whether he went promptly, said exactly what he was told, and brought back a true report. “For he refreshes the soul of his masters.” A reliable messenger brings the ones who sent him the same kind of relief a cold drink brings a harvester. They can rest, knowing the work has been done well.

The opposite appears in 10:26, where the lazy man is “As vinegar to the teeth and smoke to the eyes” to those who send him. Dependability can look like a plain virtue, but this saying ranks it among the things that revive the people who rely on us.

Study Notes

The Greek writer Xenophon, in his Memorabilia, mentions people hunting for snow in summer to chill their wine. His remark shows that cooling drinks with snow was known in the ancient world, though the proverb itself does not describe the custom.

“Refreshes the soul” translates a Hebrew expression that means to bring back or restore the soul, used of reviving someone who is exhausted.

Proverbs 25:14
Proverbs 25:14
NKJV

Proverbs 25:14 Whoever falsely boasts of giving Is like clouds and wind without rain.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:14 A person who promises a gift but doesn’t give itis like clouds and wind that bring no rain. (NLT)

Main Idea

A man who boasts of gifts he never gives is like clouds and wind that promise rain and bring none. In a dry land, that kind of empty show raises hopes only to disappoint them.

Verse Explanation

A man who boasts of gifts he never gives is like clouds and wind that promise rain and bring none. In a dry land, that kind of empty show raises hopes only to disappoint them.

“Whoever falsely boasts of giving” This person talks about gifts he means to give, or presents himself as generous, and then never gives. His generosity exists only in his words. He wants the credit of giving without its cost.

“Is like clouds and wind without rain.” In a dry land where every farmer depended on rain, building clouds and rising wind lifted everyone’s hopes. When the clouds passed over and nothing fell, the disappointment was bitter. The boaster does the same thing to people. He raises expectations and leaves others worse off than if he had promised nothing, because they planned on his gift and waited for it.

The saying exposes more than stinginess. It names the habit of using the promise of generosity to win admiration while keeping one’s hands closed. Coming right after the faithful messenger of 25:13, it sets dependable words beside empty ones: one man refreshes like cold water at harvest, the other disappoints like a sky that never rains.

Study Notes

The KJV margin gives the Hebrew of the first line literally as boasting in a gift of falsehood, that is, a gift that is never actually given.

Proverbs 25:15
Proverbs 25:15
NKJV

Proverbs 25:15 By long forbearance a ruler is persuaded, And a gentle tongue breaks a bone.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:15 Patience can persuade a prince,and soft speech can break bones. (NLT)

Main Idea

Patient restraint can persuade even a ruler, and a gentle tongue can break down the hardest resistance. The surprising image of soft words breaking a bone shows gentleness accomplishing what force cannot.

Verse Explanation

Patient restraint can persuade even a ruler, and a gentle tongue can break down the hardest resistance. The surprising image of soft words breaking a bone shows gentleness accomplishing what force cannot.

“By long forbearance a ruler is persuaded,” Long forbearance is patience that does not lose its temper or give up. A ruler cannot be forced or bullied, since he holds the power. Yet patient, calm persistence can win him over where pressure and anger would fail. The phrase describes a person who is slow to anger, the quality that 16:32 ranks above military strength.

“And a gentle tongue breaks a bone.” The tongue is soft and bone is hard, yet soft speech breaks the bone. The image is meant to surprise. Gentle words can wear down the hardest resistance, a stubborn will or a hardened position, that harsh words would only stiffen. Proverbs said earlier that “A soft answer turns away wrath” (15:1); here gentleness does more, for it changes a mind.

Like all proverbs, this is a general truth and not a guarantee. Patience and gentleness do not always succeed. They remain the way of wisdom, and they often accomplish what force cannot.

Study Notes

“Long forbearance” translates a Hebrew phrase built from the words for “length” and “anger,” describing a long delay before anger rises. A closely related phrase is rendered “slow to wrath” or “slow to anger” elsewhere in Proverbs (14:29; 15:18; 16:32).

“Have you found honey?” Honey was a delight in Israel, sometimes found wild in unexpected places, as when Samson found bees and honey in the lion’s carcass in Judges 14:8, or when Jonathan found it dripping in the woods in 1 Samuel 14:25–27. The question opens with a gift. Proverbs itself says, “eat honey because it is good” (24:13).

“Eat only as much as you need,” The command concerns measure, not refusal. Eat enough to satisfy you. The moment of unexpected abundance is often the moment restraint is hardest, which is why the saying speaks to the finder of honey. “Lest you be filled with it and vomit.” Too much honey makes a person sick, and the sweetness he enjoyed comes back up. A good thing becomes a misery through excess.

The saying teaches self-control with good gifts. The danger lies in an appetite that will not stop, never in the honey itself. God’s gifts are meant to be enjoyed with thanks and within limits. The next saying applies the same lesson to friendship, and 25:27 returns to honey to make a point about seeking glory.

Study Notes

“Filled with it” in this verse and “weary of you” in 25:17 translate the same Hebrew verb, which means to be satisfied or full. The shared word links the two sayings: too much honey and too much of a visitor produce the same overfullness.

The command is to make your visits rare enough that they stay welcome. Wisdom pays attention to other people’s time, space and limits, and it does not assume that what one enjoys the other enjoys just as much.

“Lest he become weary of you and hate you.” The neighbor may grow tired of you, and weariness can turn into resentment. “Hate” is strong language here for the dislike that grows when affection has been overused. Like honey in 25:16, a neighbor’s company is a good thing that turns sour through excess. The two sayings stand together as a pair: the same principle of measure governs the table and the friendship. A visit that is not constant keeps its value, and a welcome that is not worn out stays warm.

Proverbs 25:21–22 — Kindness to an Enemy

Proverbs 25:21
Proverbs 25:21
NKJV

Main Idea

When your enemy is hungry or thirsty, you are to give him bread to eat and water to drink. Wisdom commands practical kindness toward the very person who opposes you, meeting his real needs instead of waiting for him to change first.

Verse Explanation

When your enemy is hungry or thirsty, you are to give him bread to eat and water to drink. Wisdom commands practical kindness toward the very person who opposes you, meeting his real needs instead of waiting for him to change first.

“If your enemy is hungry, give him bread to eat;” The command is concrete. An enemy is someone who opposes you, perhaps someone who has wronged you. When he is in need, you are to meet the need with real food. “And if he is thirsty, give him water to drink;” The second line repeats the first with water, the most basic need in a dry land. Nothing is said about whether the enemy has apologized or changed.

Proverbs has already forbidden revenge: “Do not say, ‘I will recompense evil’” (20:22), and “Do not say, ‘I will do to him just as he has done to me’” (24:29). This saying goes further. Wisdom does more than refrain from paying back evil; it actively does good to the one who has done harm. Kindness toward an enemy is already part of the Law, which required an Israelite to bring back an enemy’s straying ox or donkey and help with his fallen animal (Exodus 23:4–5). Such kindness meets the enemy’s actual needs instead of waiting for him to change first. The verse is incomplete by itself; 25:22 gives its reason and result.

None.

Main Idea

Such kindness will “heap coals of fire on his head,” most likely the burning shame that unearned goodness stirs, which may turn an enemy toward repentance, and the LORD Himself will reward the one who shows it. Verse Explanation

Such kindness will “heap coals of fire on his head,” most likely the burning shame that unearned goodness stirs, which may turn an enemy toward repentance, and the LORD Himself will reward the one who shows it. Paul quotes this saying and concludes that we overcome evil with good (Romans 12:20–21), the way Christ loved His enemies.

“For so you will heap coals of fire on his head,” The picture is startling, and it is clearly figurative. Burning coals on the head would be painful, and the pain here most likely belongs to the enemy’s conscience. He expected hostility and received bread and water instead. Unearned goodness stirs a burning shame or remorse in him, and that shame can lead him to see his wrong and turn from it. The exact meaning of the coals is debated, but the saying cannot be read as a disguised form of revenge, a way of hurting an enemy under cover of helping him. The kindness of 25:21 is real kindness, and its hoped-for fruit is the enemy’s change of heart.

“And the LORD will reward you.” The final line turns attention from the enemy to God. Whether or not the enemy responds, the LORD sees and repays the one who does good. The person who shows kindness can leave the outcome, and any justice still owed, in God’s hands.

Paul quotes this saying when he teaches believers not to avenge themselves, and he draws the conclusion, “overcome evil with good” (Romans 12:21). Christ Himself acted in this way toward us, for “when we were enemies we were reconciled to God through the death of His Son” (Romans 5:10).

Study Notes

Paul places this saying immediately after his call to leave vengeance to God (Romans 12:19). In Romans 12:20 he quotes the commands of 25:21, with slightly different wording, “feed him” and “give him a drink,” and he also quotes the coals clause of 25:22. He leaves out only the closing line about the LORD’s reward.

Some early interpreters, including John Chrysostom, took the coals as a sign of God’s judgment, since burning coals elsewhere picture His punishment, as in “Let burning coals fall upon them” (Psalm 140:10). On that reading, kindness leaves a stubborn enemy more accountable before God. Paul’s conclusion, “overcome evil with good” (Romans 12:21), favors the restorative reading.

Proverbs 25:23
Proverbs 25:23
NKJV

Proverbs 25:23 The north wind brings forth rain, And a backbiting tongue an angry countenance.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 25:23 As surely as a north wind brings rain,so a gossiping tongue causes anger! (NLT)

Main Idea

As surely as the north wind brings rain, a backbiting tongue produces angry faces. Slander spoken behind someone’s back does not stay hidden; it stirs up open anger.

Verse Explanation

As surely as the north wind brings rain, a backbiting tongue produces angry faces. Slander spoken behind someone’s back does not stay hidden; it stirs up open anger.

“The north wind brings forth rain,” The first line states a weather pattern as a sure cause and effect: when this wind blows, rain follows. The point of the comparison lies in that certainty, one thing reliably producing another.

“And a backbiting tongue an angry countenance.” A backbiting tongue speaks against people secretly, behind their backs. The saying declares that it produces angry faces as surely as the wind brings rain. The slanderer thinks his words are hidden, but they come out, and when they do they bring open anger: in the person slandered, in hearers who are offended, and in relationships that are spoiled. What is said in secret does not stay secret in its effects. The verse joins the book’s earlier warnings about the talebearer whose words go “into the inmost body” (18:8) and who “reveals secrets” (20:19).

Study Notes

In the land of Israel rain usually comes from the west, off the Mediterranean (compare 1 Kings 18:44 and Luke 12:54), and the north wind is more often linked with cool, clear weather. Some commentators, among them Keil and Delitzsch, therefore suggest that “north” here may include the northwest, the direction from which rain does come.

The KJV text reverses the comparison: the north wind “driveth away” rain, and an angry countenance drives away a backbiting tongue. The KJV margin gives the alternative that the wind brings forth rain, which the NKJV and most modern translations follow. On the KJV’s reading, an angry face silences the slanderer; on the NKJV’s, the slanderer produces angry faces.

In Hebrew, “a backbiting tongue” is literally a tongue of secrecy, a tongue that slanders in secret; the YLT has “secret tongue.” The Hebrew word for secrecy comes from the same root as the verb “conceal” in 25:2, so the chapter sets God’s glorious concealing beside the slanderer’s harmful secrecy.
