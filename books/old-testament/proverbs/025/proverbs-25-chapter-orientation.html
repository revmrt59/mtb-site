Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 25
Main Idea:

Proverbs 25 opens a new collection with God’s glory in concealing and the king’s glory in searching, then uses vivid word pictures to teach humility before the great, discretion in disputes, fitting speech, restraint, kindness to an enemy, and rule over one’s own spirit.

Setting:

Verse 1 heads a collection that runs through chapter 29: more proverbs of Solomon, copied by the men of Hezekiah king of Judah, who reigned more than two centuries after Solomon.

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

The men of Hezekiah open their collection of Solomon’s sayings (25:1) with a word about God Himself: “It is the glory of God to conceal a matter, But the glory of kings is to search out a matter” (25:2). The LORD’s ways hold depths no one exhausts, and a good king, like a good reader of Proverbs, digs patiently for the truth. Even a king’s heart is beyond his subjects’ reach (25:3), so the wise wait humbly to hear, “Come up here” (25:7).

From the court the sayings move into ordinary life, nearly all of them pictures, and wisdom, the skill of living life God’s way, shows itself in what fits. A fitting word is like golden apples in silver (25:11), and a gentle tongue breaks a bone (25:15). Cheerful songs to a heavy heart are like taking away a coat in the cold (25:20), and even honey sickens when there is too much of it (25:16, 27).

Near the end comes the hardest kindness: bread and water for a hungry enemy, with the promise that “the LORD will reward you” (25:22). The chapter closes on the man who cannot rule his own spirit, a city with its walls broken down (25:28).

Chapter Sections

Proverbs 25:1–7 — The Glory of Kings and the Lower Seat

Hezekiah’s men preserve more of Solomon’s sayings, and the collection opens with God who conceals, kings who search, a royal heart no subject can measure, and a throne made secure when the wicked are removed as dross is taken from silver. The section ends at court, where it is far better to be called up to a higher place than to claim one and be sent down while the prince looks on.

Proverbs 25:8–10 — Settle Disputes Discreetly

A quarrel rushed into court can end with the accuser shamed once his neighbor answers. The wiser course is to settle the matter with the neighbor himself and never to win by betraying someone else’s secret, a betrayal that can leave the betrayer’s own reputation in ruins.

Proverbs 25:11–15 — A Word Fitly Spoken

Speech is prized here for its timing and faithfulness: the right word at the right moment is like gold set in silver, and a wise rebuke received by a listening ear is as fine as an earring of gold. A reliable messenger refreshes like the cool of snow at harvest, a man who boasts of gifts he never gives disappoints like clouds without rain, and patient gentleness can win over even a ruler.

Proverbs 25:16–20 — Moderation, False Witness, and the Heavy Heart

Honey and a neighbor’s company are both good, yet too much of either turns sweetness into sickness and welcome into weariness. The sayings then name people who hurt when they should help: the false witness who wounds like a weapon, the unfaithful man who fails like a bad tooth in time of trouble, and the one who sings cheerful songs to a grieving heart.

Proverbs 25:21–22 — Kindness to an Enemy

Wisdom answers an enemy’s hunger and thirst with bread and water, and such kindness heaps coals of fire on his head, most often understood as the burning remorse that undeserved goodness can awaken and that may lead him to repent. The LORD Himself rewards the one who does it, and Paul quotes the saying in his call to leave vengeance to God (Romans 12:19–20).

Proverbs 25:23–28 — Good News and Self-Rule

Hidden slander brings open anger as surely as the north wind brings rain, life with a contentious woman can make a house less bearable than a corner of its roof (compare 21:9), and good news from far away revives like cold water. A righteous man who gives way before the wicked fouls a spring others drink from, and the chapter closes by warning against seeking one’s own glory and by picturing the man without self-rule as a city with its walls broken down.

Why This Chapter Matters

Proverbs 25 shows God at work in the way Scripture reached His people. Solomon’s sayings lost none of their authority by waiting for Hezekiah’s men; a later king who “trusted in the LORD God of Israel” (2 Kings 18:5) saw that they were handed on. Because God’s glory is to conceal, searching out His ways is an honor fit for kings.

The chapter also stands behind two of the New Testament’s plainest commands. Jesus’ word that “he who humbles himself will be exalted” (Luke 14:11) grows from the lower seat of 25:6–7, and Paul builds “overcome evil with good” on the coals of fire (Romans 12:20–21). Christ lived out both, humbling Himself and dying for us “when we were enemies” (Romans 5:10). Humility aimed at promotion and kindness meant as revenge miss what this chapter teaches.

Key Themes to Watch For

Humility before the great. Claiming a place at court invites public shame, and seeking one’s own glory is no glory at all (25:6–7, 27).

Words that fit. Timing, faithfulness, and gentleness make speech valuable, while slander, false witness, and empty boasting do real harm (25:11–15, 18, 23).

Faithful and faithless people. A faithful messenger refreshes those who send him, while an unfaithful man and a righteous man who gives way before the wicked fail the people who depend on them (25:13, 19, 26).

Restraint and self-rule. Honey and a neighbor’s company are good gifts best enjoyed in measure, and a person without rule over his own spirit has no defense (25:16–17, 27–28).

Good for evil. Wisdom meets an enemy’s need with bread and water and leaves the reward to the LORD (25:21–22).

Key Words & Concepts

The men of Hezekiah (25:1). Men in the service of Hezekiah, the reforming king of Judah (about 715–686 BC), who copied these proverbs of Solomon. Their work shows the book being gathered over time, and it is no less God’s word for that.

To conceal a matter (25:2). God’s glory includes depths in His ways that no one can fully search, while a king is honored by searching out the truth. The verse is one reason Proverbs rewards careful and deeper reading, though it does not license finding hidden meanings in every saying.

Dross (25:4). The impurities a refiner removes from melted silver before the silversmith can work it. Verse 5 applies the picture to the court: remove the wicked, and the king’s throne “will be established in righteousness.”

Apples of gold in settings of silver (25:11). A picture of something precious set off by a fitting frame. A word is valuable for what it says and also for when and how it is said.

Coals of fire (25:22). The meaning is debated. Most understand the coals as the burning shame or remorse that kindness stirs in an enemy, which may turn him toward repentance. Paul’s use rules out reading the kindness as a disguised form of revenge (Romans 12:20–21).

A city broken down, without walls (25:28). Ancient cities depended on their walls for safety. A person without self-control lies open to anger, appetite, and impulse. Paul names self-control among the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22–23).
