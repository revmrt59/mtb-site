Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 26:1
Proverbs 26:1
NKJV

Proverbs 26:1 As snow in summer and rain in harvest, So honor is not fitting for a fool.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:1 Honor is no more associated with foolsthan snow with summer or rain with harvest. (NLT)

Main Idea

Snow in summer and rain at harvest are out of season and can ruin a crop, and honor given to a fool is just as out of place. This opening picture sets the theme for the portrait of the fool that runs through verse 12: honor does not fit him.

Verse Explanation

Snow in summer and rain at harvest are out of season and can ruin a crop, and honor given to a fool is just as out of place. This opening picture sets the theme for the portrait of the fool that runs through verse 12: honor does not fit him.

“As snow in summer and rain in harvest,” Both pictures describe weather that comes at the wrong time. In Israel the summer months were dry, and the grain harvest needed clear days so the cut grain could be gathered and threshed. Snow in summer was almost unheard of, and rain at harvest could flatten the standing grain or spoil what was cut. What is good in its season does damage out of season.

“So honor is not fitting for a fool.” Honor means public respect, position or praise. The fool in Proverbs is a person whose problem is moral, not mental: he despises instruction and keeps choosing his own way. Giving such a person honor is as out of place as snow in summer. It does not suit him, and it does harm, because honor gives influence, and a fool with influence spreads his folly further. Honoring him also teaches onlookers that folly is rewarded.

This verse opens the chapter’s long portrait of the fool (26:1–12). Its key idea is fitness. The sayings that follow ask, in one picture after another, what happens when honor, responsibility or even wise words are put into a fool’s hands.

Study Notes

Eleven of the twelve sayings in 26:1–12 mention the fool by name; the exception is 26:2. The repetition gathers the book’s scattered sayings about the fool into one concentrated portrait.

Snow appeared at 25:13 as a welcome coolness at harvest. Here snow and rain out of season picture what is unfitting.

Proverbs 26:2
Proverbs 26:2
NKJV

Proverbs 26:2 Like a flitting sparrow, like a flying swallow, So a curse without cause shall not alight.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:2 Like a fluttering sparrow or a darting swallow,an undeserved curse will not land on its intended victim. (NLT)

Main Idea

A curse spoken without cause is like a sparrow or a swallow darting through the air that never comes to rest. An undeserved curse has nowhere to land, so the one who has done no wrong need not live in fear of it.

Verse Explanation

A curse spoken without cause is like a sparrow or a swallow darting through the air that never comes to rest. An undeserved curse has nowhere to land, so the one who has done no wrong need not live in fear of it.

“Like a flitting sparrow, like a flying swallow,” Sparrows and swallows dart and swoop, rarely settling anywhere for long. Anyone who has watched a swallow over a field knows how restless its flight is. The picture is of something that flies around and never lands.

“So a curse without cause shall not alight.” A curse in the ancient world was a spoken wish or pronouncement of harm, often thought to carry real power. The saying deals with a curse “without cause,” one spoken against someone who has done nothing to deserve it. Such a curse is like a bird that flutters about and never comes to rest. It has nowhere to land, because there is no just cause for it, and the LORD, not the one who speaks, governs what comes to pass. When Balaam was hired to curse Israel he had to confess, “How shall I curse whom God has not cursed?” (Numbers 23:8).

The saying frees the innocent from fear of undeserved words spoken against them. It is a single proverb about baseless curses, not a full teaching on curses, and it should not be stretched beyond that.

Study Notes

The Hebrew text handed down by the Jewish scribes carries a note on one small word in the last line. The word written in the text means “not,” while the reading noted in the margin is a word that sounds the same but means “to him.” On that marginal reading, some have taken the line to mean that a causeless curse comes back on the one who spoke it. The NKJV, like the KJV, ESV and NASB, follows the written word, “not.”

Proverbs 26:3
Proverbs 26:3
NKJV

Proverbs 26:3 A whip for the horse, A bridle for the donkey, And a rod for the fool's back.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:3 Guide a horse with a whip, a donkey with a bridle,and a fool with a rod to his back! (NLT)

Main Idea

Each kind of creature needs its own kind of control: the whip for the horse, the bridle for the donkey, and the rod for the fool’s back. A fool who will not be guided by wise words ends up having to be driven by pain.

Verse Explanation

Each kind of creature needs its own kind of control: the whip for the horse, the bridle for the donkey, and the rod for the fool’s back. A fool who will not be guided by wise words ends up having to be driven by pain.

“A whip for the horse, A bridle for the donkey,” Each animal needs its own kind of control. A horse is urged on with a whip, and a donkey is steered with a bridle. Neither responds to reasoning; each must be directed from outside.

“And a rod for the fool’s back.” The fool belongs on the same list. Because he will not be guided by wise words, he ends up being driven by pain. Proverbs has said the same before: “a rod is for the back of him who is devoid of understanding” (10:13). The saying is not a lesson on raising children; it describes the adult fool who has rejected every gentler means of correction and is left to learn through hard consequences, whether through discipline, the courts, or the results of his own choices.

The verse also continues the theme of 26:1. Honor does not fit a fool; the rod does. In Proverbs the rod is a tool of correction in its setting, and nothing here justifies harshness, rage or abuse. The sad point is that a person made to be guided by wisdom has made himself like an animal that must be driven.

None.

Proverbs 26:4
Proverbs 26:4
NKJV

Proverbs 26:4 Do not answer a fool according to his folly, Lest you also be like him.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:4 Don’t answer the foolish arguments of fools,or you will become as foolish as they are. (NLT)

Main Idea

Do not answer a fool on his own terms, matching his foolishness with your own, or you will become like him. Some arguments are best left alone, because entering them drags the wise down to the fool’s level.

Verse Explanation

Do not answer a fool on his own terms, matching his foolishness with your own, or you will become like him. Some arguments are best left alone, because entering them drags the wise down to the fool’s level.

“Do not answer a fool according to his folly,” To answer a fool according to his folly is to answer him on his own terms: to match his insults with insults, his shouting with shouting, his foolish argument with a foolish argument. The fool sets the rules of the exchange, and the one who answers accepts them.

“Lest you also be like him.” The danger is that the wise person ends up looking and acting like the fool. Arguing with a fool on his terms drags you down to his level. Onlookers may not be able to tell the two of you apart, and you may lose your own temper, dignity and clear thinking in the process. Some quarrels are best left alone, and refusing to engage can be the wisest response. Proverbs has already said, “any fool can start a quarrel” (20:3).

This saying stands back to back with 26:5, which gives what looks like the opposite counsel. The pair is deliberate. Read together, they show that wisdom does not apply rules mechanically; it reads the situation and discerns which response fits.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 26:5
Proverbs 26:5
NKJV

Proverbs 26:5 Answer a fool according to his folly, Lest he be wise in his own eyes.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:5 Be sure to answer the foolish arguments of fools,or they will become wise in their own estimation. (NLT)

Main Idea

There are also times to answer a fool according to his folly, exposing it plainly, so that he does not walk away convinced of his own wisdom. Set back to back with verse 4, the pair shows that wisdom is skill in reading the situation, not a rulebook to apply mechanically.

Verse Explanation

There are also times to answer a fool according to his folly, exposing it plainly, so that he does not walk away convinced of his own wisdom. Set back to back with verse 4, the pair shows that wisdom is skill in reading the situation, not a rulebook to apply mechanically.

“Answer a fool according to his folly,” Here the phrase has a different sense. To answer a fool according to his folly is to answer him in the way his folly calls for: to expose it plainly, name it for what it is, and refuse to let it stand unchallenged. Answering this way does not stoop to his level; it meets his folly with a clear reply.

“Lest he be wise in his own eyes.” If no one ever answers him, the fool may conclude that he is right and that his foolishness is wisdom. Silence can confirm him in his error and leave others thinking his view has gone unanswered. Sometimes love for the fool, and for those listening, requires a plain answer.

Placed beside 26:4, the saying shows how wisdom works. There is a time to stay silent so as not to become like the fool, and a time to speak so that the fool does not become wise in his own eyes. No rule can decide every case in advance. Wisdom is skill: knowing the person, the moment, the stakes and the listeners, and choosing the response that fits. The two sayings belong together, and neither cancels the other.

Study Notes

The phrase “wise in his own eyes” returns in 26:12 and 26:16, linking the unanswered fool, the self-satisfied man and the lazy man. The book has already warned, “Do not be wise in your own eyes” (3:7).

The apparent tension was noticed early. The Babylonian Talmud (Shabbat 30b) discusses it and resolves it by applying “answer a fool” to matters of Torah, God’s law, and “do not answer” to ordinary worldly affairs. That is the Talmud’s own way of dividing the cases, but it agrees with the basic point that each saying fits a different situation.

Proverbs 26:6
Proverbs 26:6
NKJV

Proverbs 26:6 He who sends a message by the hand of a fool Cuts off his own feet and drinks violence.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:6 Trusting a fool to convey a messageis like cutting off one’s feet or drinking poison! (NLT)

Main Idea

Sending a message by a fool is like cutting off your own feet and drinking violence. He is the opposite of the faithful messenger of 25:13, and whoever trusts him with an errand cripples his own business and invites harm on himself.

Verse Explanation

Sending a message by a fool is like cutting off your own feet and drinking violence. He is the opposite of the faithful messenger of 25:13, and whoever trusts him with an errand cripples his own business and invites harm on himself.

“He who sends a message by the hand of a fool” A messenger in the ancient world carried more than a letter. He spoke for the sender, handled business on his behalf and brought back a report. Trusting such an errand to a fool means trusting your words and your interests to someone careless, unreliable or dishonest.

“Cuts off his own feet and drinks violence.” The messenger was supposed to be the sender’s feet, extending his reach to places he could not go. Sending a fool is like cutting those feet off: the errand goes nowhere, or goes badly. To drink violence means to take in harm, to swallow trouble that comes back on oneself. The sender’s own foolish choice brought the trouble on him, though the fool still answers for his own misconduct.

The fool here is the opposite of the faithful messenger of 25:13, who refreshes the soul of his masters. The saying carries the chapter’s theme of fitness into everyday business. Responsibility, like honor, belongs with those whose character matches it.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 26:7
Proverbs 26:7
NKJV

Proverbs 26:7 Like the legs of the lame that hang limp Is a proverb in the mouth of fools.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:7 A proverb in the mouth of a foolis as useless as a paralyzed leg. (NLT)

Main Idea

A proverb in a fool’s mouth is as useless as the limp legs of a lame man. He can repeat the words, but they will not carry him anywhere, because he does not live what he quotes.

Verse Explanation

A proverb in a fool’s mouth is as useless as the limp legs of a lame man. He can repeat the words, but they will not carry him anywhere, because he does not live what he quotes.

“Like the legs of the lame that hang limp” The picture is of legs that are present but cannot carry their owner. They are there, yet they do not do what legs are for. The image is about uselessness, not about the person who is lame; Scripture uses it only to make the comparison vivid.

“Is a proverb in the mouth of fools.” A fool can learn a proverb and repeat it, but in his mouth it does not work. He can quote the words without understanding when they apply, and he does not live by them. A proverb is meant to carry a person along the path of wisdom, but a proverb the speaker does not live goes nowhere.

The saying is striking in a book made of proverbs. It warns that collecting wise sayings is not the same as being wise. Wisdom, skill for living life God’s way, is shown in how a person lives, and the fool shows that a person can know the right words and still walk the wrong path. The same second line returns in 26:9 with a more dangerous picture.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb in the first line is uncertain. The KJV speaks of legs that are not equal, while the NKJV and ESV picture legs that hang limp or useless. The comparison’s point is the same in each rendering.

Proverbs 26:8
Proverbs 26:8
NKJV

Proverbs 26:8 Like one who binds a stone in a sling Is he who gives honor to a fool.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:8 Honoring a foolis as foolish as tying a stone to a slingshot. (NLT)

Main Idea

Giving honor to a fool is as senseless as tying a stone fast in a sling, which defeats the whole purpose of the sling. The saying returns to the theme of verse 1, that honor and the fool do not belong together.

Verse Explanation

Giving honor to a fool is as senseless as tying a stone fast in a sling, which defeats the whole purpose of the sling. The saying returns to the theme of verse 1, that honor and the fool do not belong together.

“Like one who binds a stone in a sling” A sling was a leather pouch on two cords. The slinger placed a stone in the pouch, whirled it, and released one cord so the stone flew out. If someone tied the stone fast in the pouch, the sling could not throw it. The weapon would be useless, or the stone would swing back and strike the slinger.

“Is he who gives honor to a fool.” Giving honor to a fool is just as senseless. Honor is meant to do something: to reward good character, to give influence to those who will use it well, and to hold up an example. Bestowed on a fool, it accomplishes none of these and may come back on the one who gave it, as the fool misuses the position or praise he was handed.

The saying returns to the theme of 26:1, that honor and the fool do not belong together. Together with 26:6, it warns those who appoint, promote and praise others to look at character before they hand out honor or responsibility.

Study Notes

The KJV margin records an older understanding of the line as putting a precious stone into a heap of stones, which would picture honor wasted on a fool. The NKJV and most modern translations read the picture of a sling.

Proverbs 26:9
Proverbs 26:9
NKJV

Proverbs 26:9 Like a thorn that goes into the hand of a drunkard Is a proverb in the mouth of fools.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:9 A proverb in the mouth of a foolis like a thorny branch brandished by a drunk. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wise words in a fool’s mouth are like a thorn stuck in the hand of a drunkard, who cannot feel it or deal with it sensibly. Verse 7 showed the proverb hanging useless; here it becomes something that can wound.

Verse Explanation

Wise words in a fool’s mouth are like a thorn stuck in the hand of a drunkard, who cannot feel it or deal with it sensibly. Verse 7 showed the proverb hanging useless; here it becomes something that can wound.

“Like a thorn that goes into the hand of a drunkard” A drunk man with a thorn in his hand may not feel it, cannot remove it skillfully, and may swing his hand around and hurt himself or others. The thorn is in his hand, yet he is in no condition to deal with it wisely.

“Is a proverb in the mouth of fools.” A proverb in a fool’s mouth can wound. He uses wise words carelessly, applies them to the wrong situations, or wields them as weapons against others while ignoring them himself. True words, misused, can do real harm.

The second line is the same as in 26:7, but the picture has changed. There, the proverb in the fool’s mouth was useless, like legs that hang limp. Here it is dangerous, like a thorn in a drunkard’s hand. The fool cannot be trusted with honor, with errands, or even with wise sayings, because he lacks the judgment to use them rightly.

Study Notes

The NKJV pictures a thorn that goes into the drunkard’s hand, and the ESV a thorn that “goes up” into it, as the KJV also reads. The NLT has “a thorny branch brandished by a drunk.” Each rendering keeps the point that something hurtful is in hands that cannot manage it.

Proverbs 26:10
Proverbs 26:10
NKJV

Proverbs 26:10 The great God who formed everything Gives the fool his hire and the transgressor his wages.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:10 An employer who hires a fool or a bystanderis like an archer who shoots at random. (NLT)

Main Idea

The great Creator who formed everything gives the fool and the transgressor the wages they have earned. Folly does not escape the notice of the God who made the world, and in His time each one receives his due.

Verse Explanation

The great Creator who formed everything gives the fool and the transgressor the wages they have earned. Folly does not escape the notice of the God who made the world, and in His time each one receives his due.

“The great God who formed everything” In the NKJV the verse opens by naming God as the great Creator who formed all things. He made the world and set its order, including the order by which actions bring their results. The fool lives in a world he did not make, under a God he cannot escape.

“Gives the fool his hire and the transgressor his wages.” Hire and wages are what a worker earns. The Creator pays the fool and the transgressor what they have earned. Folly and sin are not overlooked by the God who formed everything; in His time each receives his due. Within the fool portrait, the verse sets all the sayings about the fool under God’s rule. Honor does not suit the fool, and the God who made the world sees that the fool’s path brings its own wages.

The wording of this verse is one of the hardest in Proverbs, and the NKJV represents one way of reading it. The point it makes in the NKJV, that the Creator repays folly and transgression, agrees with what the book teaches elsewhere, and it is best held simply and without building a major teaching on this verse alone.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of 26:10 is notoriously difficult, and translations differ widely. The word “God” is not present in the Hebrew; the KJV marks it as supplied. The NLT compares an employer who hires a fool or a bystander to an archer “who shoots at random,” and the ESV and NASB similarly speak of an “archer” and of one who hires a fool or passers-by.

The differences arise in the Hebrew itself. The first word of the verse can be read as “great” or as “archer,” and the verb that follows can be read either as forming or producing something, or as wounding. The NKJV follows the line of the KJV and YLT. The alternative reading fits the theme of 26:6, the folly of entrusting work to a fool.

Proverbs 26:11
Proverbs 26:11
NKJV

Proverbs 26:11 As a dog returnsto his own vomit, So a fool repeats his folly.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:11 As a dog returns to its vomit,so a fool repeats his foolishness. (NLT)

Main Idea

Just as a dog goes back to its own vomit, a fool goes back to the folly that sickened him. The repulsive image shows folly as a habit the fool will not break, and Peter applies it to those who return to the corruption they once escaped (2 Peter 2:20–22).

Verse Explanation

Just as a dog goes back to its own vomit, a fool goes back to the folly that sickened him. The repulsive image shows folly as a habit the fool will not break, and Peter applies it to those who return to the corruption they once escaped (2 Peter 2:20–22).

“As a dog returns to his own vomit,” Dogs in ancient Israel were mostly scavengers, not household pets, and the picture is deliberately disgusting. A dog vomits what made it sick and then goes back and eats it again. Nothing has been learned.

“So a fool repeats his folly.” The fool goes back to the very folly that sickened him. He suffers the consequences of his foolish choices, and then makes the same choices again. Folly for him is a habit he will not break. The repulsive image shows how unnatural and degrading that cycle is, and it explains why honor, errands and proverbs are wasted on him: he does not learn from experience.

Peter quotes this saying of people who had “escaped the pollutions of the world through the knowledge of the Lord and Savior Jesus Christ” and then turned back to them (2 Peter 2:20). The proverb gave him a ready picture of a person returning to what he once rejected. The warning applies to anyone tempted to go back to a sin he has already seen for what it is.

Study Notes

Peter cites the saying as a “true proverb” and joins it with a second saying about a washed sow returning to the mire (2 Peter 2:22). The second saying is not found in Proverbs.

Proverbs 26:12
Proverbs 26:12
NKJV

Proverbs 26:12 Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:12 There is more hope for foolsthan for people who think they are wise. (NLT)

Main Idea

There is more hope for a fool than for a man wise in his own eyes. The self-satisfied man is worse off than the fool, because he is convinced he has nothing left to learn, and the opening question turns the warning toward every reader.

Verse Explanation

There is more hope for a fool than for a man wise in his own eyes. The self-satisfied man is worse off than the fool, because he is convinced he has nothing left to learn, and the opening question turns the warning toward every reader.

“Do you see a man wise in his own eyes?” The question turns from the fool to another kind of man and draws the reader in. To be wise in one’s own eyes is to be confident in one’s own judgment, satisfied that one already knows what is right, and closed to correction. Proverbs has warned, “Do not be wise in your own eyes; Fear the LORD and depart from evil” (3:7).

“There is more hope for a fool than for him.” After eleven sayings about the fool, this is a shock. The self-satisfied man is in worse shape than the fool. The fool may at least be corrected by hard consequences, as 26:3 suggested. The man wise in his own eyes is convinced he has nothing left to learn, so he will not seek wisdom, receive counsel, or admit his need. Pride closes the door that even a fool might someday walk through.

The question also turns the warning toward every reader. It is easy to see the fool in others; it is harder to see self-satisfaction in oneself. The fear of the LORD, a right view of God, is the remedy, because seeing God rightly humbles a person’s view of his own wisdom.

Study Notes

The same comparison appears in 29:20, where a man hasty in his words is placed below the fool. Both sayings measure a person against the fool to show how serious his condition is.

Proverbs 26:13–16 — The Lazy Man

Proverbs 26:13
Proverbs 26:13
NKJV

Proverbs 26:13 The lazy man says, "There is a lion in the road! A fierce lion is in the streets!"(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:13 The lazy person claims, “There’s a lion on the road!Yes, I’m sure there’s a lion out there!” (NLT)

Main Idea

The lazy man invents danger to stay at home, crying out that a fierce lion prowls the road and the streets. His alarm sounds urgent, but it is an excuse for avoiding the work that waits outside (compare 22:13); the portrait is of a man who will not work, not of someone kept from work by illness, disability, or real need.

Verse Explanation

The lazy man invents danger to stay at home, crying out that a fierce lion prowls the road and the streets. His alarm sounds urgent, but it is an excuse for avoiding the work that waits outside (compare 22:13); the portrait is of a man who will not work, not of someone kept from work by illness, disability, or real need.

“The lazy man says,” The portrait of the lazy man (26:13–16) begins with his words. He does not say he does not want to work; he gives a reason, and the reason sounds urgent. “There is a lion in the road! A fierce lion is in the streets!” Lions did live in the land in Bible times, and David told Saul in 1 Samuel 17:34–36 that he had fought them while keeping sheep. But a lion prowling the town streets was hardly likely. The lazy man invents or exaggerates a danger so he can stay home. His alarm sounds like prudence, and that is what makes it a good excuse.

The saying echoes 22:13, where the lazy man cries, “There is a lion outside! I shall be slain in the streets!” Here the doubled cry heightens the drama: a lion, a fierce lion, in the road and in the streets. The more vivid the danger, the more reasonable staying in bed appears. Laziness is often eloquent.

The portrait describes a man who will not work, not someone kept from work by illness, disability or real need. Real dangers and genuine limits are not laziness. The target of the saying is the invented excuse that covers an unwillingness to do what one can and should do.

Study Notes

The NKJV’s “a lion” and “A fierce lion” render two different Hebrew words for lion, which adds to the drama of the excuse.

The NKJV uses “lazy man” in all four sayings of 26:13–16, while the ESV and NASB use “sluggard.” The NKJV reserves “sluggard” for 6:6 and 6:9.

Proverbs 26:14
Proverbs 26:14
NKJV

Proverbs 26:14 As a door turns on its hinges, So does the lazy man on his bed.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:14 As a door swings back and forth on its hinges,so the lazy person turns over in bed. (NLT)

Main Idea

As a door swings back and forth on its hinges without going anywhere, the lazy man turns over and over in his bed. He moves, but he never gets up.

Verse Explanation

As a door swings back and forth on its hinges without going anywhere, the lazy man turns over and over in his bed. He moves, but he never gets up.

“As a door turns on its hinges,” A door swings back and forth on its hinges, opening and closing, moving a great deal and going nowhere. It stays attached to the same post. “So does the lazy man on his bed.” The lazy man turns over in bed the same way: from one side to the other, again and again, without ever getting up. He is not still; he moves, but the movement accomplishes nothing.

The picture is gently comic, and that is part of its force. A reader can smile at it and then recognize the pattern: motion without progress, a day spent turning over instead of rising. The book has already pictured the same man pleading, “A little sleep, a little slumber, A little folding of the hands to sleep” (6:10), and it has shown where that road leads, to poverty that comes “like a prowler” (6:11). The door image adds that the lazy man is fastened to his bed as surely as a door is fastened to its frame.

Study Notes

None.

Proverbs 26:15
Proverbs 26:15
NKJV

Proverbs 26:15 The lazy man buries his hand in the bowl; It wearies him to bring it back to his mouth.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:15 Lazy people take food in their handbut don’t even lift it to their mouth. (NLT)

Main Idea

The lazy man buries his hand in the bowl and then finds it too tiring to lift the food to his mouth. The exaggeration is comic, yet it exposes a man who will not finish even what would feed him (compare 19:24).

Verse Explanation

The lazy man buries his hand in the bowl and then finds it too tiring to lift the food to his mouth. The exaggeration is comic, yet it exposes a man who will not finish even what would feed him (compare 19:24).

“The lazy man buries his hand in the bowl;” Meals were eaten with the hands from a common dish. The lazy man reaches into the bowl, and there his hand stays. “It wearies him to bring it back to his mouth.” Lifting the food the short distance to his mouth is too much effort. He has started to eat and cannot be bothered to finish.

The exaggeration is deliberate and comic, and the picture makes the point unforgettable: the lazy man will not finish even what would feed him. He begins things and leaves them undone. His weariness is not the tiredness that follows hard work; it is the heaviness of a will that does not want to make any effort at all.

The saying nearly repeats 19:24. Its repetition here, in the fuller portrait of the lazy man, joins the excuse of 26:13, the bed of 26:14 and the self-deception of 26:16. The book does not mock people who are genuinely weak or ill; it exposes a habit of mind that refuses effort.

Study Notes

Where the NKJV has “bowl,” the KJV reads “bosom,” picturing a man with his hands tucked into the folds of his garment. Most modern translations, like the NKJV, read a dish or bowl.

Proverbs 26:16
Proverbs 26:16
NKJV

Proverbs 26:16 The lazy man is wiser in his own eyes Than seven men who can answer sensibly.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:16 Lazy people consider themselves smarterthan seven wise counselors. (NLT)

Main Idea

Worst of all, the lazy man considers himself wiser than seven men who can give a sensible answer. His laziness is protected by self-deception, which links him with the man wise in his own eyes in verse 12.

Verse Explanation

Worst of all, the lazy man considers himself wiser than seven men who can give a sensible answer. His laziness is protected by self-deception, which links him with the man wise in his own eyes in verse 12.

“The lazy man is wiser in his own eyes” The last stroke of the portrait is the most serious. The lazy man does not see himself as lazy. In his own eyes he is wise; his excuses look like good sense to him, and his inactivity looks like prudence. “Than seven men who can answer sensibly.” Seven was a number of fullness. He thinks himself wiser than a whole council of sensible people who could give a sound reason for what they do. If they urged him to work, he would have an answer for all of them.

Self-deception protects his laziness. Because he is convinced he is right, he cannot be corrected. The phrase “wise in his own eyes” links him with 26:12, where such a man has less hope than a fool, and with 26:5, where the fool left unanswered becomes wise in his own eyes. Laziness that cannot see itself is the hardest kind to cure, and the portrait ends there, at the point where the lazy man most needs to look honestly at himself.

Proverbs 26:17 Interfering in someone else’s argumentis as foolish as yanking a dog’s ears. (NLT)

Main Idea

Meddling in a quarrel that is not your own is like grabbing a passing dog by the ears: you cannot easily let go, and you are likely to be bitten. The saying warns against interfering in other people’s fights; it does not forbid honest peacemaking.

Verse Explanation

Meddling in a quarrel that is not your own is like grabbing a passing dog by the ears: you cannot easily let go, and you are likely to be bitten. The saying warns against interfering in other people’s fights; it does not forbid honest peacemaking.

“He who passes by and meddles in a quarrel not his own” The man in this saying is walking past. He has no part in the quarrel, no responsibility for it, and no relationship that gives him a place in it. Yet he stops and jumps in, taking sides or adding his own heat to a dispute that belongs to someone else.

“Is like one who takes a dog by the ears.” Dogs in Israel were mostly half-wild street animals. Grabbing one by the ears is easy to do and very hard to undo. Once you have hold, letting go means getting bitten, and holding on means a struggle you did not need. So it is with meddling. The passerby quickly finds himself caught in a quarrel he cannot easily leave, and he is likely to get hurt.

The saying warns against meddling; it does not forbid peacemaking. Jesus said, “Blessed are the peacemakers” (Matthew 5:9). The difference lies in whether the matter is truly one’s concern, whether one has been asked or has a rightful place, and whether one comes to bring peace or to take sides and stir things further. A busybody adds fuel; a peacemaker helps put out the fire.

Study Notes

In Hebrew, the words for “passes by” and “meddles” are built on the same three letters and sound alike. The repetition carries the point of the saying: the man who was only passing by becomes, in the very next word, the man caught up in someone else’s quarrel. The second word can also suggest becoming worked up or angry, which is why the KJV margin gives “is enraged” as an alternative for “meddleth,” and the YLT speaks of the passer-by making himself angry over strife not his own.

The fire language here also prepares for the sayings that follow. Fire and its fuel run through 26:18–21, and the chapter keeps returning to the way careless or cruel words set things burning.

That madman is the man who deceives his neighbor and then brushes it off by saying, “I was only joking!” Harm done by a lie is not made harmless by calling it a joke, any more than a burning arrow is harmless because it was thrown carelessly.

“Is the man who deceives his neighbor,” The madman of 26:18 turns out to be an ordinary-looking neighbor who deceives the person next door. He lies, misleads or tricks someone who trusted him. “And says, ‘I was only joking!’” When he is caught, or when the harm becomes clear, he brushes it off. It was a joke, he says; no harm meant.

The comparison refuses the excuse. A firebrand thrown carelessly burns just as much as one thrown on purpose, and an arrow shot for fun still wounds. A deception does real damage whatever the deceiver claims about his intentions afterward. Calling it a joke does not undo the harm. It adds a second offense, because it treats the neighbor’s hurt as unimportant and avoids honest confession.

The saying exposes the habit of hiding cruelty or dishonesty behind humor. Wisdom takes responsibility for the effects of its words instead of hiding behind a laugh.

In Hebrew the excuse is a question, asking in effect whether one was not just playing, as the KJV, NASB and YLT render it. The NKJV, ESV and NLT turn it into a statement.

The LORD hates “one who sows discord among brethren” (6:19), and this saying shows how that discord is kept alive.

Proverbs 26:23–28 — Hidden Hatred Revealed

Proverbs 26:23
Proverbs 26:23
NKJV

Proverbs 26:23 Fervent lips with a wicked heartAre like earthenware covered with silver dross.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 26:23 Smooth words may hide a wicked heart,just as a pretty glaze covers a clay pot. (NLT)

Main Idea

Fervent, warm words covering a wicked heart are like a cheap clay pot glazed with silver dross. The shine is only on the surface, and what lies beneath is common and worthless.

Verse Explanation

Fervent, warm words covering a wicked heart are like a cheap clay pot glazed with silver dross. The shine is only on the surface, and what lies beneath is common and worthless.

“Fervent lips with a wicked heart” Fervent lips speak warmly and earnestly. They sound sincere, affectionate, even passionate. The heart behind them, however, is wicked. The words and the heart do not match. “Are like earthenware covered with silver dross.” Earthenware was ordinary clay pottery, cheap and common. Silver dross is the worthless residue left over when silver is refined. The NKJV’s wording pictures a common clay pot overlaid with that dross so that it gleams like silver on the outside. It looks like something precious, but underneath it is still common clay.

So it is with warm words over a wicked heart. The shine is only on the surface. Anyone who judged by the coating would be deceived about what lay beneath. The silver dross recalls 25:4, where the dross was removed so that the silver could become something fine. Here the dross itself is pictured as a cover, a cheap imitation of value.

This verse opens the last section of the chapter (26:23–28), which traces hatred hidden behind pleasant speech. The fire language of 26:18–21 continues in the burning or fervent lips, but here the warmth is a disguise.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “fervent” means burning or glowing, and the KJV, NASB and YLT render it more literally as “burning lips.”

The ESV and NLT have “glaze” in place of “silver dross.” They follow a proposal by the scholar H. L. Ginsberg to read the Hebrew letters behind “silver dross” as a single word meaning glaze. The proposal rests on a word for glaze found in Ugaritic, an ancient language related to Hebrew, with a parallel word in Hittite. It is a scholarly rereading of the letters, not a Hebrew word attested elsewhere in the Bible, and the NKJV keeps the traditional reading. Either way, the picture is a cheap pot with a shiny surface.

“Though his hatred is covered by deceit,” For a time his hatred stays hidden. The verse describes the way God has ordered His world. Deceit does not hold forever. Study Notes

Both sayings warn that what a person hides does not stay hidden or bring him good.
