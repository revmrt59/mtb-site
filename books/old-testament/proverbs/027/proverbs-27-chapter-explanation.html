Proverbs 27:1
Proverbs 27:1
NKJV

Proverbs 27:1 Do not boast about tomorrow, For you do not know what a day may bring forth.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:1 Don’t brag about tomorrow,since you don’t know what the day will bring. (NLT)

Main Idea

Do not boast about what you will do tomorrow, because no one knows what a single day may bring. The warning targets presumption about the future, not careful planning, and James draws on the same wisdom (James 4:13–14).

Verse Explanation

Do not boast about what you will do tomorrow, because no one knows what a single day may bring. The warning targets presumption about the future, not careful planning, and James draws on the same wisdom (James 4:13–14).

“Do not boast about tomorrow,” To boast about tomorrow is to speak as though the future were already in your hands: what you will do, what you will gain, how things will turn out. The boast assumes a control no human being has. “For you do not know what a day may bring forth.” A single day can bring what no one expected, whether loss, illness, opportunity or death. The future belongs to God, and every person lives one day at a time under His hand.

The saying warns against presumption, not against planning. Proverbs elsewhere commends careful preparation: “The plans of the diligent lead surely to plenty” (21:5). The difference lies in the heart behind the plan. The wise person plans and works while holding tomorrow humbly, aware that the outcome is not his to command. The presumptuous person talks as if tomorrow were guaranteed.

James draws on the same wisdom when he rebukes those who say, “Today or tomorrow we will go to such and such a city,” reminding them, “you do not know what will happen tomorrow,” and teaching them instead to say, “If the Lord wills, we shall live and do this or that” (James 4:13–15).

Study Notes

“Bring forth” translates the ordinary Hebrew verb for giving birth. The day is pictured as about to deliver something no one has yet seen.

The verb “boast” here and the verb “praise” in 27:2 come from the same Hebrew root, so the two sayings belong together: do not praise your own future, and do not praise yourself.

Proverbs 27:2
Proverbs 27:2
NKJV

Proverbs 27:2 Let another man praise you, and not your own mouth; A stranger, and not your own lips.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:2 Let someone else praise you, not your own mouth —a stranger, not your own lips. (NLT)

Main Idea

Praise should come from someone else, even a stranger, and never from your own mouth. Self-praise is empty, while the honest recognition of others carries a weight that boasting can never earn.

Verse Explanation

Praise should come from someone else, even a stranger, and never from your own mouth. Self-praise is empty, while the honest recognition of others carries a weight that boasting can never earn.

“Let another man praise you, and not your own mouth;” Praise means something when it comes from someone else, because it is not your own verdict on yourself. Praise from your own mouth is worth nothing; it only tells people what you think of yourself, and it usually lowers their opinion of you.

“A stranger, and not your own lips.” The second line strengthens the first. Praise should come from outside yourself, even from a stranger, and not from your own lips. The saying does not promise that every word of praise from others is sincere; it forbids supplying your own.

Following 27:1, the saying continues the theme of humility. The first verse forbids boasting about the future; this one forbids boasting about oneself. Both describe a person who tries to secure by his own words what only God and others can give. The wise person does his work faithfully and leaves his reputation in other hands, as 25:27 has already warned that seeking one’s own glory “is not glory.”

Study Notes

The Hebrew words behind “another man” and “A stranger” often describe outsiders or foreigners. Here they simply mean someone other than yourself.

Proverbs 27:3
Proverbs 27:3
NKJV

Proverbs 27:3 A stone is heavy and sand is weighty, But a fool's wrath is heavier than both of them.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:3 A stone is heavy and sand is weighty,but the resentment caused by a fool is even heavier. (NLT)

Main Idea

Stone and sand are heavy loads, yet a fool’s wrath is heavier than both. His anger is a crushing burden for everyone around him, and there is no setting it down.

Verse Explanation

Stone and sand are heavy loads, yet a fool’s wrath is heavier than both. His anger is a crushing burden for everyone around him, and there is no setting it down.

“A stone is heavy and sand is weighty,” Stone and sand are heavy loads that ordinary people knew well, stones for building and sand carried by the load. Anyone who had hauled them knew how much they could tire the back.

“But a fool’s wrath is heavier than both of them.” A fool’s anger, his vexation and resentment, is a heavier load still. Those who live or work with him have to carry it. He takes offense easily, nurses grudges, and makes everyone around him bear the weight of his moods. Unlike a stone, his wrath cannot be set down when the job is done, because it keeps returning.

The saying contrasts physical weight with emotional weight and declares the second heavier. A wise person learns to avoid provoking a fool and to be wary of becoming one, since unreasonable anger is a burden imposed on others.

Proverbs 27:4
Proverbs 27:4
NKJV

Proverbs 27:4 Wrath is cruel and anger a torrent, But who is able to stand before jealousy?(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:4 Anger is cruel, and wrath is like a flood,but jealousy is even more dangerous. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wrath is cruel and anger overflows like a flood, yet jealousy is harder still to withstand. Anger may spend itself, but jealousy settles in and keeps working, and no one can stand before it.

Verse Explanation

Wrath is cruel and anger overflows like a flood, yet jealousy is harder still to withstand. Anger may spend itself, but jealousy settles in and keeps working, and no one can stand before it.

“Wrath is cruel and anger a torrent,” Wrath is cruel because it wounds without mercy, and anger is like a torrent, a flood that overflows its banks and sweeps away what stands in its path. Both are destructive forces. Yet a flood eventually subsides, and an outburst of anger can pass.

“But who is able to stand before jealousy?” Jealousy is worse. It is the burning resentment that someone else has what one wants, or the fierce, possessive anger of one who believes he has been wronged in love or honor. It does not burst out and then die down; it settles in, keeps working and plots revenge. The question expects the answer, “No one.”

Proverbs has shown jealousy’s power before: “jealousy is a husband’s fury; Therefore he will not spare in the day of vengeance” (6:34). The saying warns the reader both to guard his own heart against jealousy and to recognize how dangerous it is when it rises in someone else.

Proverbs 27:5
Proverbs 27:5
NKJV

Proverbs 27:5 Open rebuke is betterThan love carefully concealed.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:5 An open rebukeis better than hidden love! (NLT)

Main Idea

An open rebuke does more good than love that is carefully kept hidden. Love that never speaks up when a friend is going wrong gives him no help, while honest correction can turn him around.

Verse Explanation

An open rebuke does more good than love that is carefully kept hidden. Love that never speaks up when a friend is going wrong gives him no help, while honest correction can turn him around.

“Open rebuke is better” An open rebuke is honest, direct correction spoken to a person’s face. It is not pleasant to give or receive. “Than love carefully concealed.” Love carefully concealed is affection that never speaks up. It sees a friend going wrong and says nothing, perhaps to keep the peace, avoid discomfort, or protect the friendship.

The saying ranks the uncomfortable rebuke above the silent love. Love that never acts when a friend is in danger gives him no help. A rebuke, however painful, can turn him around. Real love is willing to risk the friend’s displeasure for his good.

This does not mean every rebuke is wise. The openness here is honesty, not public shaming. The verse prepares for 27:6, which explains why a friend’s wounds can be trusted, and it fits the praise in 25:12 for a wise rebuke received by an obedient ear.

Proverbs 27:6
Proverbs 27:6
NKJV

Proverbs 27:6 Faithful are the wounds of a friend, But the kisses of an enemy are deceitful.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:6 Wounds from a sincere friendare better than many kisses from an enemy. (NLT)

Main Idea

The wounds a friend gives are faithful because they come from love and aim at his good, while an enemy’s kisses are deceitful, however warm they seem. Truth that hurts from a friend is better than affection that hides hostility.

Verse Explanation

The wounds a friend gives are faithful because they come from love and aim at his good, while an enemy’s kisses are deceitful, however warm they seem. Truth that hurts from a friend is better than affection that hides hostility.

“Faithful are the wounds of a friend,” A friend sometimes wounds: he tells you a hard truth, corrects you, or opposes a foolish plan. The wounds hurt, but they are faithful. They can be trusted because they come from someone who loves you and aims at your good. A friend who is willing to hurt you with the truth proves his loyalty.

“But the kisses of an enemy are deceitful.” A kiss was a common greeting and a sign of affection. An enemy may greet you warmly, flatter you and seem to be on your side, while his heart is set against you. His kisses look like love but serve his own purposes. Warmth on the surface proves nothing.

The two lines set substance against appearance. The friend’s words feel like hostility but are love; the enemy’s kisses feel like love but hide hostility. Wisdom learns to value the first and see through the second. The faithful wounds come from love, so the saying never justifies harshness or cruelty presented as honesty.

Study Notes

Translations differ on the last word. The Hebrew word behind “deceitful” comes from a verb that Hebrew lexicons define as to be abundant or plentiful, and the ESV reads “profuse,” the YLT “abundant,” and the 1984 NIV says an enemy “multiplies” kisses. The NKJV, NASB and KJV read “deceitful,” and the KJV margin offers “earnest” or “frequent.” Either way, the enemy’s kisses are excessive and cannot be trusted.

Proverbs 27:7–14 — Friends, Neighbors, and Prudence

Proverbs 27:7
Proverbs 27:7
NKJV

Proverbs 27:7 A satisfied soulloathes the honeycomb, But to a hungry soul every bitter thing is sweet.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:7 A person who is full refuses honey,but even bitter food tastes sweet to the hungry. (NLT)

Main Idea

Hunger and fullness change a person’s taste: one who is satisfied refuses even honeycomb, while to a hungry soul even bitter things seem sweet. Need makes people glad to receive what comfort would turn away.

Verse Explanation

Hunger and fullness change a person’s taste: one who is satisfied refuses even honeycomb, while to a hungry soul even bitter things seem sweet. Need makes people glad to receive what comfort would turn away.

“A satisfied soul loathes the honeycomb,” The soul here is the whole person with his appetites. Someone who has just eaten his fill turns away even from honeycomb, a prized sweet food. Fullness dulls desire, and what would be a delight to others becomes unwelcome.

“But to a hungry soul every bitter thing is sweet.” A hungry person will eat almost anything gladly. Even bitter food tastes good when the stomach is empty. Need changes taste.

The saying describes how hunger and fullness affect what people value. A person with plenty can become indifferent to good gifts, while a person in need is grateful for small things. The saying can describe hunger for food, for kindness, or for counsel. It warns the comfortable against the dullness that comes with having too much, and it reminds the reader that need often makes people more grateful and more receptive than abundance does. It also sits naturally beside 27:9, where a friend’s counsel is sweet to those who will receive it.

Proverbs 27:8
Proverbs 27:8
NKJV

Proverbs 27:8 Like a bird that wanders from its nest Is a man who wanders from his place.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:8 A person who strays from homeis like a bird that strays from its nest. (NLT)

Main Idea

A man who wanders from his place is like a bird that has strayed from its nest, rootless and unprotected. Leaving home and the people and duties that belong to it leaves a person without shelter.

Verse Explanation

A man who wanders from his place is like a bird that has strayed from its nest, rootless and unprotected. Leaving home and the people and duties that belong to it leaves a person without shelter.

“Like a bird that wanders from its nest” A bird that leaves its nest and wanders loses its shelter. Away from the nest it is exposed to danger, and the nest itself, with any eggs or young in it, is left unprotected. “Is a man who wanders from his place.” A man’s place is his home, his land, his family, his work and his responsibilities. A man who wanders away from them, whether from restlessness or from avoiding his duties, is in the same situation. He has no settled shelter, and those who depended on him are left without his care.

The saying does not forbid every move; people in Scripture moved for good reasons and at God’s direction. It warns against rootless wandering, the restless leaving of one’s proper place and duties without good cause. Stability, faithfulness to one’s home and people, and staying with one’s responsibilities are part of wisdom, and the man who drifts away from them loses more than he expects.

Proverbs 27:9
Proverbs 27:9
NKJV

Proverbs 27:9 Ointment and perfume delight the heart, And the sweetness of a man's friend gives delight by hearty counsel.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:9 The heartfelt counsel of a friendis as sweet as perfume and incense. (NLT)

Main Idea

As ointment and perfume delight the heart, so a friend’s sincere counsel brings sweet delight. Advice that comes from the heart of a true friend is as refreshing as fragrance.

Verse Explanation

As ointment and perfume delight the heart, so a friend’s sincere counsel brings sweet delight. Advice that comes from the heart of a true friend is as refreshing as fragrance.

“Ointment and perfume delight the heart,” In a hot, dusty land, fragrant oils and perfumes were a real pleasure. They were used for hospitality, celebration and refreshment, and their scent lifted the spirits of those who received them.

“And the sweetness of a man’s friend gives delight by hearty counsel.” A friend’s heartfelt counsel brings the same kind of delight. Hearty counsel is advice that comes from the heart, honest, caring and personal. When a true friend gives it, it refreshes the soul as perfume refreshes the senses. The sweetness lies both in the advice itself and in the friendship it expresses.

The verse belongs to the chapter’s thread on friendship. After the faithful wounds of 27:6, it shows the gentler side of the same friendship. A true friend can correct and can comfort, and either way his counsel comes from a heart that wants your good.

Study Notes

The KJV margin gives the Hebrew behind “hearty counsel” as counsel of the soul. The NASB takes the line differently, as a man’s counsel being sweet to his friend, but the main translations agree that a friend’s sincere advice is the source of delight.

Proverbs 27:10
Proverbs 27:10
NKJV

Proverbs 27:10 Do not forsake your own friend or your father's friend, Nor go to your brother's house in the day of your calamity; Better is a neighbor nearby than a brother far away.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:10 Never abandon a friend —either yours or your father’s.When disaster strikes, you won’t have to ask your brother for assistance.It’s better to go to a neighbor than to a brother who lives far away. (NLT)

Main Idea

Keep faith with your own friends and your family’s old friends, and do not count on a distant brother when disaster strikes. A neighbor close at hand can help more than a relative far away, so long-standing friendships are worth guarding.

Verse Explanation

Keep faith with your own friends and your family’s old friends, and do not count on a distant brother when disaster strikes. A neighbor close at hand can help more than a relative far away, so long-standing friendships are worth guarding.

“Do not forsake your own friend or your father’s friend,” Old friendships are worth keeping, including those passed down from one’s father. A family friend has known the household for years, has proven his loyalty, and often has wisdom and experience to share. To abandon such friendships carelessly is foolish.

“Nor go to your brother’s house in the day of your calamity;” When disaster strikes, a person naturally turns to family. Yet the saying warns against counting on a brother who may be distant, whether in miles or in heart. “Better is a neighbor nearby than a brother far away.” A neighbor close at hand can help at once, while a brother far away cannot. Nearness, both in place and in commitment, matters more in a crisis than family ties alone.

The saying does not discount family; Proverbs says that “a brother is born for adversity” (17:17). It recognizes that a faithful friend or neighbor who is present and willing may help more than a relative who is absent. Wisdom keeps old friendships in good repair, because those are the people who will stand with you when trouble comes.

Study Notes

This saying has three lines rather than the usual two. The NLT reads the middle line as a reassurance (“When disaster strikes, you won’t have to ask your brother for assistance”), while the NKJV, ESV and NASB read it as a warning against going to a brother’s house.

NLT

Proverbs 27:11 Be wise, my child, and make my heart glad.Then I will be able to answer my critics. (NLT)

Main Idea

The father’s voice returns: “My son, be wise, and make my heart glad.” A wise son brings his father joy and gives him an answer for anyone who criticizes him, because the son’s life proves the father’s teaching.

Verse Explanation

The father’s voice returns: “My son, be wise, and make my heart glad.” A wise son brings his father joy and gives him an answer for anyone who criticizes him, because the son’s life proves the father’s teaching.

“My son, be wise, and make my heart glad,” The father’s voice, so frequent in chapters 1–9 and heard again in 23:15, returns briefly here. He urges his son to be wise, because a wise son brings his father joy. Proverbs has said this from the beginning of Solomon’s sayings: “A wise son makes a glad father” (10:1).

“That I may answer him who reproaches me.” A father is often judged by how his children turn out. If the son is foolish, critics may scorn the father and his teaching. If the son is wise, the father has an answer for anyone who reproaches him: his son’s life proves the value of what he taught.

The appeal is personal and warm. The father does not only command; he tells his son how much his wisdom will mean to him. The son’s choices affect more than himself, because his life can honor or shame those who raised him. Placed among sayings about friends and neighbors, the verse reminds the reader that the whole collection is, at heart, a father’s instruction.

Proverbs 27:12
NKJV

Proverbs 27:12 A prudent man foresees evil and hides himself; The simple pass on and are punished.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:12 A prudent person foresees danger and takes precautions.The simpleton goes blindly on and suffers the consequences. (NLT)

Main Idea

The prudent man sees trouble coming and takes cover, while the simple keep going and pay for it. The saying, nearly identical to 22:3, sets foresight against the careless drift of the uncommitted.

Verse Explanation

The prudent man sees trouble coming and takes cover, while the simple keep going and pay for it. The saying, nearly identical to 22:3, sets foresight against the careless drift of the uncommitted.

“A prudent man foresees evil and hides himself;” A prudent man looks ahead. He sees trouble coming, whether danger, temptation or a bad situation, and takes cover. He changes course, prepares, or stays out of harm’s way. Prudence is practical foresight.

“The simple pass on and are punished.” The simple are the uncommitted, the easily led, those who drift without thinking about where they are going. They walk straight into the trouble the prudent man saw and avoided, and they pay for it. Their problem is not that they could not see the danger; they did not stop to look.

The saying repeats 22:3 almost word for word. Its return here, in a chapter about friendship and wise living, underlines the importance of foresight. The book offers the simple a better way: Proverbs was written “To give prudence to the simple” (1:4).

Study Notes

The only difference from 22:3 in the NKJV is the opening of the second line: there it begins with “But.” The heading in 25:1 presents these chapters as a further collection of Solomon’s proverbs, so it is natural for a saying from the earlier collection to appear again here.

Proverbs 27:13
Proverbs 27:13
NKJV

Proverbs 27:13 Take the garment of him who is surety for a stranger, And hold it in pledge when he is surety for a seductress.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:13 Get security from someone who guarantees a stranger’s debt.Get a deposit if he does it for foreigners. (NLT)

Main Idea

Take the garment of someone who guarantees a stranger’s debt, and hold it as a pledge when he backs a seductress. A man that careless with his promises is a poor risk, so the lender should protect himself (compare 20:16).

Verse Explanation

Take the garment of someone who guarantees a stranger’s debt, and hold it as a pledge when he backs a seductress. A man that careless with his promises is a poor risk, so the lender should protect himself (compare 20:16).

“Take the garment of him who is surety for a stranger,” A surety was someone who pledged himself to pay another person’s debt if that person failed to pay. To act as surety for a stranger, someone whose character and ability to repay are unknown, is reckless. The saying tells the lender to take the surety’s garment as security, because a man that careless with his promises is a poor risk.

“And hold it in pledge when he is surety for a seductress.” If he is guaranteeing the debts of a seductress, an immoral woman, he has shown even worse judgment. The lender should hold the pledge firmly. A man who has entangled himself with such a woman cannot be trusted to keep his word.

The saying nearly repeats 20:16 and continues the book’s steady warning about surety (6:1–5). It counsels the lender to protect himself, and it warns the reader not to become the kind of person whose rash pledges make him a bad risk. The warnings on surety are practical counsel, not a ban on helping others; they caution against binding oneself rashly for those who cannot be trusted.

Study Notes

Taking a garment as security was a recognized practice. The Law required that a neighbor’s garment taken as a pledge be returned before sundown, since it might be his only covering at night (Exodus 22:26–27). The saying applies the practice to the reckless surety.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

A friend who blesses loudly early in the morning will find his greeting counted as a curse. Praise that is overdone and badly timed grates on the one it is meant to honor and makes its sincerity suspect.

“He who blesses his friend with a loud voice, rising early in the morning,” Picture a man who wakes his friend at dawn with loud, effusive blessings and greetings. He may think he is being friendly, but the timing and volume are all wrong. Early morning is not the moment for noisy praise, and the overdone display raises questions about his motive.

“It will be counted a curse to him.” His blessing will be received as a curse. It annoys rather than honors, and its excess makes it seem insincere. People suspect flattery when praise is too loud, too eager, or too badly timed.

The saying fits the chapter’s concern with honest friendship. After the faithful wounds of 27:6 and the hearty counsel of 27:9, this verse shows a false kind of friendliness. Sincere affection is shown with sensitivity to the person and the moment, and a blessing that ignores both can do more harm than good.

Main Idea

Verse Explanation

A contentious woman is like the steady dripping of a leaking roof on a day of heavy rain. The image captures strife that wears a home down by its constancy (compare 19:13).

“A continual dripping on a very rainy day” In a long, heavy rain a roof could leak, and the dripping would go on hour after hour, steady and hard to ignore. “And a contentious woman are alike;” A contentious woman, one given to quarreling and complaint, has the same effect on a home. Her contention does not come in a single storm but in a steady drip that wears everyone down by its constancy.

The saying echoes an earlier proverb that compares “the contentions of a wife” to “a continual dripping” (19:13). Like 21:9 and 25:24, it is a portrait of strife in a home, not a joke about wives or a statement about women in general. Proverbs gives the same warning about the contentious man (26:21). The point is the corrosive effect of constant quarreling on the people who live with it. The verse is unfinished, and 27:16 completes the picture.

Proverbs 27:16
Proverbs 27:16
NKJV

Proverbs 27:16 Whoever restrains her restrains the wind, And grasps oil with his right hand.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:16 Stopping her complaints is like trying to stop the windor trying to hold something with greased hands. (NLT)

Main Idea

Trying to restrain her is like trying to hold back the wind or grip oil in your hand. Strife of this kind cannot be contained by force, and the verse completes the picture begun in verse 15.

Verse Explanation

Trying to restrain her is like trying to hold back the wind or grip oil in your hand. Strife of this kind cannot be contained by force, and the verse completes the picture begun in verse 15.

“Whoever restrains her restrains the wind,” Trying to restrain the contentious woman of 27:15 is like trying to hold back the wind. No one can grab the wind or stop it by force. “And grasps oil with his right hand.” It is also like trying to grip oil in your hand; the harder you squeeze, the more it slips through your fingers. The right hand was the hand of strength, yet even the strong hand cannot hold oil.

The two pictures make the same point. Strife of this kind cannot be controlled by force or pressure. Attempts to suppress it only show how impossible the task is. The saying does not tell anyone how to fix such a situation; it describes how hard it is, and in doing so it warns against becoming the kind of person whose contention no one can restrain.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of this verse is difficult. The verb in the first line usually means to hide or store up, which is why the KJV speaks of one who “hideth” her as hiding the wind, and of ointment in the right hand that betrays itself. The NKJV, ESV and NASB take the verb in the sense of holding back, and agree on the picture of restraining the wind and grasping oil.

Proverbs 27:17
Proverbs 27:17
NKJV

Proverbs 27:17 As iron sharpens iron, So a man sharpens the countenance of his friend.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:17 As iron sharpens iron,so a friend sharpens a friend. (NLT)

Main Idea

As one iron blade sharpens another, so one friend sharpens another. Friends who press on each other with honest counsel and correction make each other keener and more useful, and the faithful wounds of verse 6 are part of that sharpening.

Verse Explanation

As one iron blade sharpens another, so one friend sharpens another. Friends who press on each other with honest counsel and correction make each other keener and more useful, and the faithful wounds of verse 6 are part of that sharpening.

“As iron sharpens iron,” An iron tool or blade could be sharpened by working it against another piece of iron, and the friction made the edge keener. “So a man sharpens the countenance of his friend.” Friends do the same for each other. In honest conversation, counsel, disagreement and encouragement, they press on one another and make each other sharper, wiser and more effective. The countenance, or face, stands for the whole person as he meets others, his alertness, outlook and character.

Sharpening involves friction. Friends who never challenge each other do not sharpen each other. The faithful wounds of 27:6 and the open rebuke of 27:5 are part of this process, and so is the hearty counsel of 27:9. The verse pictures friends helping one another, with counsel that goes both ways rather than one person doing all the work. It also suggests that a person who isolates himself loses the edge that friendship provides. The New Testament urges believers to “consider one another in order to stir up love and good works” (Hebrews 10:24).

Study Notes

The NKJV, KJV and YLT keep the Hebrew word for “face,” translating it “countenance” or “face,” while the ESV and NASB render the line more generally as one man sharpening another.

Proverbs 27:18
Proverbs 27:18
NKJV

Proverbs 27:18 Whoever keeps the fig tree will eat its fruit; So he who waits on his master will be honored.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 27:18 As workers who tend a fig tree are allowed to eat the fruit,so workers who protect their employer’s interests will be rewarded. (NLT)

Main Idea

The one who tends the fig tree eats its fruit, and the servant who faithfully waits on his master will be honored. Patient, faithful service ordinarily brings its reward the way a well-kept tree yields fruit, though the saying commends such service without guaranteeing that every earthly master will honor it.

Verse Explanation

The one who tends the fig tree eats its fruit, and the servant who faithfully waits on his master will be honored. Patient, faithful service ordinarily brings its reward the way a well-kept tree yields fruit, though the saying commends such service without guaranteeing that every earthly master will honor it.

“Whoever keeps the fig tree will eat its fruit;” A fig tree had to be looked after and protected, and the one who kept it faithfully would in time eat its fruit. The reward came after patient work.

“So he who waits on his master will be honored.” A servant who faithfully attends to his master, serving him, looking after his interests and being dependable, will in time be honored. Faithful service, like faithful care of a fig tree, ordinarily brings its reward.

The saying commends patient diligence in service. It describes the way things usually go in a well-ordered world, not a guarantee that every master will reward every faithful servant. Some faithful servants are overlooked in this life, and Scripture holds that God sees and rewards what people do not. The point is that faithful care, whether of a tree or of a master’s interests, is the path that leads to fruit and honor.

Study Notes

The two Hebrew verbs behind “keeps” and “waits on” both mean to guard or watch over. The parallel ties the tending of a tree to the attentive service of a master.

Verse Explanation

Know the condition of your flocks and give careful attention to your herds. The closing poem calls for steady, personal care of what has been entrusted to you, the daily diligence of a good shepherd.

“Be diligent to know the state of your flocks,” The chapter, and the whole collection of comparisons in chapters 25–27, closes with a short poem about a farmer’s care of his animals (27:23–27). Flocks of sheep and goats were the living wealth of many Israelite households. The owner is told to know their condition firsthand: which animals are healthy or sick, where the pasture is good, which ewes are about to lamb, what dangers threaten. Distant management, leaving everything to hired hands, is not enough.

“And attend to your herds;” To attend is to give careful, continuing attention. The line repeats the first with a second Hebrew word for the animals in one’s care, a word that can mean either a flock or a herd. Together the two lines describe steady, personal diligence: knowing, watching and caring for what has been entrusted to you.

The poem teaches stewardship. Wisdom shows itself in faithful attention to ordinary work and to the resources God has placed in one’s hands. The same attentiveness can rightly be applied, by extension, to those who care for people, as Peter tells elders, “Shepherd the flock of God which is among you” (1 Peter 5:2); but the poem’s own subject is the household’s flocks and herds.

Study Notes

“Be diligent to know” translates Hebrew that repeats the verb “know,” literally knowing, know. Hebrew repeats a verb this way for emphasis, so the line stresses careful, attentive knowledge of the flock. The word behind “state” is literally “face,” and “attend to” is literally to set one’s heart on something, as the KJV margin notes.

The five verses of 27:23–27 form a closing poem. They move from command (27:23) through reason (27:24) and the cycle of the seasons (27:25) to the provision the flocks supply (27:26–27).

A flock cared for diligently keeps producing year after year, while a pile of riches may vanish. The wise person does not rely on what he has accumulated; he faithfully tends what continues to provide. The verse warns against trusting in riches without promising that diligence will always prevent loss.
