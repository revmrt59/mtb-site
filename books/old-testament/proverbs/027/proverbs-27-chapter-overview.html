Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 27
Main Idea:

Proverbs 27 calls for humility before a tomorrow no one can see, prizes the friend whose wounds are faithful and whose counsel sharpens, exposes a heart that reveals the man and eyes that are never satisfied, and ends by urging steady care of one’s flocks because riches and crowns do not last.

Setting:

This is the last chapter of word-picture sayings in the proverbs of Solomon copied by Hezekiah’s men (25:1). Friends and neighbors fill its middle, and the father’s “My son” returns once (27:11).

Chapter Outline:

  • Proverbs 27:1–6 — Humility and Faithful Wounds

  • Proverbs 27:7–14 — Friends, Neighbors, and Prudence

  • Proverbs 27:15–22 — Iron Sharpens Iron

  • Proverbs 27:23–27 — Know the State of Your Flocks

Key Verse:

“As iron sharpens iron, So a man sharpens the countenance of his friend.” (Proverbs 27:17)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Proverbs 27 begins by taking tomorrow out of human hands: “Do not boast about tomorrow, For you do not know what a day may bring forth” (27:1). Verse 2 takes praise out of our own mouths. That humility clears the ground for what the chapter values most, the honest word of a friend. “Faithful are the wounds of a friend” (27:6), because they aim at his good, while an enemy’s kisses merely look like love.

The word “friend” appears in five verses, making friendship the chapter’s thread. A friend’s counsel is as sweet as perfume (27:9), and a neighbor nearby helps more than a brother far away (27:10). “As iron sharpens iron, So a man sharpens the countenance of his friend” (27:17), and the faithful wounds of verse 6 are part of that sharpening. The chapter also looks inward: the heart shows the man as water shows a face (27:19), and human eyes, like Hell and Destruction, are never full (27:20).

The closing poem moves from the heart to the pasture. Riches and crowns pass, but flocks known and tended keep clothing and feeding a household (27:23–27). The chapter never names the LORD, yet wisdom, skill for living God’s way, appears here as patient attention to what He has entrusted, within the order of grass and harvest He keeps.

Chapter Sections

Proverbs 27:1–6 — Humility and Faithful Wounds

The chapter opens by forbidding boasts about a day no one has seen and praise that comes from one’s own lips, then weighs a fool’s wrath as heavier than stone and sand and jealousy as a force no one can stand against. It closes by ranking open rebuke above hidden love, and the honest wounds of a friend above the warm but deceitful kisses of an enemy.

Proverbs 27:7–14 — Friends, Neighbors, and Prudence

Hunger and fullness change what tastes sweet, a man who leaves his place is as exposed as a bird away from its nest, and a friend’s heartfelt counsel refreshes like perfume, while a neighbor close by can do more in a crisis than a brother far off. Alongside the father’s renewed appeal to his son, the prudent man takes cover from trouble he sees coming, the careless surety is treated as the poor risk he is, and a loud blessing at dawn rings false.

Proverbs 27:15–22 — Iron Sharpens Iron

A quarrelsome home is pictured as rain dripping through the roof all day, strife that can no more be held than wind or oil. Then come iron sharpening iron, the fig tree that feeds its keeper and the servant honored for waiting on his master, the face mirrored in water, eyes that never have enough, praise that tests a man like a refiner’s fire, and a fool whose folly survives even the mortar.

Proverbs 27:23–27 — Know the State of Your Flocks

A short closing poem tells the owner to know his flocks and herds firsthand, since wealth and even royal power can slip away. As hay is cut and new grass returns, the lambs, goats, and milk keep supplying clothing, land, and food for the whole household, down to the maidservants.

Why This Chapter Matters

Proverbs 27 gives a test of friendship that flattery cannot pass. Love that stays “carefully concealed” (27:5) helps no one, and a blessing shouted at the wrong moment can be counted a curse (27:14). The friend worth keeping will say the hard thing, and his words can be trusted because they come from love, not harshness.

The chapter also guards against presumption. James draws on the same wisdom, telling merchants who plan a year of profit to say, “If the Lord wills, we shall live and do this or that” (James 4:15). The closing poem sets steady care of ordinary work above trust in riches, though it describes usual provision, not a shield against every loss. By extension, the same attentiveness belongs to those called to “Shepherd the flock of God” (1 Peter 5:2).

Key Themes to Watch For

Humility about tomorrow and about oneself. No one knows what a day will bring, so boasting about the future and praising oneself are both out of place, and the praise of others tests a person as fire tests metal (27:1–2, 21).

Honest friendship. True friends rebuke openly, wound faithfully, and give heartfelt counsel, while an enemy’s kisses and an overdone blessing ring false (27:5–6, 9, 14).

Friends who stay near and sharpen. Old friendships, including a father’s friend, are worth keeping, and friends who press on one another make each other keener (27:10, 17).

The revealing and restless heart. The heart shows who a person is, and desire left to itself is never satisfied, just as hunger and fullness change what tastes sweet (27:7, 19–20).

Faithful care of what is entrusted. The keeper of the fig tree, the servant who waits on his master, and the owner who knows his flocks show steady attention that ordinarily bears fruit (27:18, 23–27).

Key Words & Concepts

Faithful wounds (27:6). Painful words from a friend that can be trusted because they aim at the other’s good. The verse sets them against an enemy’s kisses, which feel warm and deceive.

The simple (27:12). The uncommitted person who drifts on without looking ahead. The saying nearly repeats 22:3, setting the prudent man’s foresight against that careless drift and its cost.

Surety (27:13). One who pledges to cover another person’s debt. A man who does this for a stranger, or for a seductress, has shown such poor judgment that the lender should hold his garment as security (compare 20:16).

The contentious woman (27:15–16). A portrait of constant quarreling in a home, compared to a roof leaking through a long rain and to wind no one can hold back. The book uses the dripping image in 19:13, and it is a sober picture of strife, not a joke about wives.

Hell and Destruction (27:20). Names for the realm of the dead, which keeps receiving and is never full. The same pair lies open before the LORD in 15:11; here they picture human eyes that always want more.

Mortar and pestle (27:22). Tools for pounding grain by hand. Pounding a fool along with the grain would still leave his foolishness in place, because he holds on to it by choice.
