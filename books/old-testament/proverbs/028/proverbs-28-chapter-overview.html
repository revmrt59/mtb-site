Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 28
Main Idea:

Proverbs 28 sets the bold righteous against the fleeing wicked in how they treat God’s law, use power over the poor, and pursue riches, and at its center it promises mercy to the sinner who stops covering his sins.

Setting:

Chapter 28 begins the last part of the proverbs of Solomon copied by Hezekiah’s men (25:1–29:27). The word pictures of chapters 25–27 give way here to direct contrasts between the righteous and the wicked, with rulers and the poor often in view.

Chapter Outline:

  • Proverbs 28:1–11 — Keeping the Law and Walking in Integrity

  • Proverbs 28:12–18 — Confession, Reverence, and Rulers

  • Proverbs 28:19–28 — Hastening to Be Rich and Trusting the LORD

Key Verse:

“He who covers his sins will not prosper, But whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy.” (Proverbs 28:13)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Proverbs 28 opens with two very different men: “The wicked flee when no one pursues, But the righteous are bold as a lion” (28:1). Guilt makes a person afraid of shadows, while a clear conscience before God gives courage. The sayings that follow tie that difference to God’s law. Those who forsake it praise the wicked, “But those who seek the LORD understand all” (28:5), and the man who will not hear the law finds that “Even his prayer is an abomination” (28:9).

At the center stands the plainest word of mercy in Proverbs: “He who covers his sins will not prosper, But whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy” (28:13). Verse 14 adds a lasting reverence, the fear of the LORD that keeps a heart from hardening. The God who refuses the prayer of a man who will not hear His law meets the confessing sinner with mercy.

Around that center, a wicked ruler over poor people is “a roaring lion and a charging bear” (28:15), and the man who hastens after riches does not consider that poverty is coming (28:22). Hiding runs through the chapter: people hide when the wicked rise (28:12, 28), eyes are hidden from the poor (28:27), and the sinner covers his sins (28:13). Mercy comes only when what is covered is confessed.

Chapter Sections

Proverbs 28:1–11 — Keeping the Law and Walking in Integrity

The section begins with the fear that guilt produces and the courage of a clear conscience, then shows how a person’s response to God’s law comes out in whom he admires, what he understands, the company he keeps, and even his prayers. Among these sayings, a poor man’s integrity outweighs crooked wealth, a poor man who oppresses the poor is like rain that flattens the crops, usury’s gains pass to one who pities the poor, and the man who leads the upright astray falls into his own pit.

Proverbs 28:12–18 — Confession, Reverence, and Rulers

After a saying about how a whole community celebrates or hides depending on who rises to power, the section promises mercy to the one who confesses and forsakes his sins and calls the always-reverent man happy while warning that a hardened heart falls into calamity. It then turns to rulers who are wicked or lack understanding and so prey on the poor, to the bloodguilty man whom no one should shield, and to the safety of the one who walks blamelessly.

Proverbs 28:19–28 — Hastening to Be Rich and Trusting the LORD

Steady work and faithfulness are set against frivolous pursuits, a judgment that can be bought for bread, and the grasping eye that rushes after riches without considering that poverty is coming. The closing sayings value honest rebuke above flattery, expose the son who robs his parents and says, “It is no transgression” (28:24), set pride and self-trust against trust in the LORD, and commend giving to the poor, before the last verse echoes verse 12 and adds that when the wicked perish, the righteous increase.

Why This Chapter Matters

Proverbs 28 answers a question the book’s earlier warnings raise: what hope is there for someone already on the wrong path? For anyone who has followed the young man of chapter 7 into sin, the way back is confession that forsakes the sin, met by mercy (28:13). David knew it: “my iniquity I have not hidden,” “And You forgave the iniquity of my sin” (Psalm 32:5). In Christ the promise is complete: “If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins” (1 John 1:9). This mercy is God’s gift, received in confession, never earned by it.

The chapter also speaks to anyone who holds authority, from a king to a parent or employer. Power that serves greed preys on the weak, while a ruler who “hates covetousness will prolong his days” (28:16).

Key Themes to Watch For

Guilt and boldness. A guilty conscience runs from shadows and drives the bloodguilty man toward the pit, while the righteous stand without fear (28:1, 17).

Hearing God’s law. Keeping the law shows in whom a person praises or resists, what he understands, the company he keeps, and whether his prayer is acceptable (28:4–5, 7, 9).

Covering and confessing. Hidden sin does not prosper, but confession joined to forsaking receives mercy, and constant reverence keeps the heart soft (28:13–14).

Rulers and the people under them. A rebellious land churns through rulers, and wicked rule sends people into hiding and turns power against the poor, while understanding and a hatred of covetousness bring stability (28:2, 12, 15–16, 28).

Integrity, haste, and generosity. Integrity outweighs riches, haste for wealth ends in want, and the one who gives to the poor will not lack, a description of God’s ways rather than a formula for financial return (28:6, 8, 20, 22, 27).

Key Words & Concepts

The law (28:4, 7, 9). The Hebrew word is torah. In this collection it means God’s instruction, and keeping it marks a son as discerning. Refusing to hear it turns even prayer into an abomination, though God never turns away the cry of the repentant (compare 15:8).

Always reverent (28:14). A steady fear of the LORD, a right view of God that brings awe at His holiness and keeps a person tender toward Him. Its opposite is the heart that hardens and falls into calamity.

Usury and extortion (28:8). Charging interest to the needy and taking what belongs to others by pressure or fraud. The saying insists that wealth gathered this way will pass to someone who pities the poor.

An evil eye (28:22). A grasping, greedy outlook that hurries after riches. The book’s portrait of the greedy, the one who “hastens to be rich” (28:20), is completed here by a man so focused on gain that he does not consider the poverty coming toward him.

Trusts in his own heart (28:26). Relying on one’s own inner impulses as a guide. Proverbs calls the heart something to be kept “with all diligence” (4:23) and tells the reader to “lean not on your own understanding” (3:5); here the self-trusting man is called a fool, while “whoever walks wisely,” with skill for living God’s way, “will be delivered.”
