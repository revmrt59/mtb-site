Proverbs 29

Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 29:1
Proverbs 29:1
NKJV

Proverbs 29:1 He who is often rebuked, and hardens his neck, Will suddenly be destroyed, and that without remedy.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:1 Whoever stubbornly refuses to accept criticismwill suddenly be destroyed beyond recovery. (NLT)

Main Idea

The man who is rebuked again and again yet stiffens his neck will be broken suddenly, beyond any remedy. The ruin falls suddenly after many warnings have been refused, as Wisdom warned at the book’s opening (1:24–31); while the rebuke is still being given, there is mercy for the one who turns (28:13).

Verse Explanation

The man who is rebuked again and again yet stiffens his neck will be broken suddenly, beyond any remedy. The ruin falls suddenly after many warnings have been refused, as Wisdom warned at the book’s opening (1:24–31); while the rebuke is still being given, there is mercy for the one who turns (28:13).

“He who is often rebuked,”

This man has not lacked warning. Rebuke has come to him again and again, through parents, friends, the word of God, and the consequences of his own choices. In Proverbs a rebuke is a gift. “Whom the LORD loves He corrects” (3:12), and “he who heeds rebuke gets understanding” (15:32). The point of “often” is that he has had many openings to turn, and each one was mercy.

“and hardens his neck,”

The picture is an ox stiffening its neck against the yoke, refusing to be turned. The LORD used the same image of Israel at the golden calf: “it is a stiff-necked people!” (Exodus 32:9). The verse places the hardening in the man himself. No one becomes hard all at once; each refused correction makes the next refusal easier.

“Will suddenly be destroyed, and that without remedy.”

The end comes suddenly, yet only after a long history of patience. When it comes, it is beyond repair. Wisdom warned at the book’s opening that those who “despised my every rebuke” (1:30) would “eat the fruit of their own way” (1:31). The warning is urgent because the door is still open while the rebuke is being given: “whoever confesses and forsakes them will have mercy” (28:13). The verse does not describe a person who has stumbled and turned back, but one who has refused, and keeps refusing, until there is no more time.

Study Notes

In Hebrew the verse opens with a man of reproofs, a way of describing someone marked by correction received again and again. The 1611 KJV gives this literal wording in its margin. The NKJV’s “often rebuked” brings out the repetition the phrase implies.

The second line closely resembles the end of the troublemaker in 6:15: “Suddenly he shall be broken without remedy” (6:15). The ESV and YLT also use “broken” in 29:1.

Proverbs 29:2
Proverbs 29:2
NKJV

Proverbs 29:2 When the righteous are in authority, the people rejoice; But when a wicked man rules, the people groan.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:2 When the godly are in authority, the people rejoice.But when the wicked are in power, they groan. (NLT)

Main Idea

When the righteous hold authority, the people rejoice, but under a wicked ruler they groan. The sayings on rulers that run through this chapter begin with the effect leadership has on ordinary people.

Verse Explanation

When the righteous hold authority, the people rejoice, but under a wicked ruler they groan. The sayings on rulers that run through this chapter begin with the effect leadership has on ordinary people.

“When the righteous are in authority, the people rejoice;”

The first of the chapter’s sayings on rulers measures leadership by its effect on ordinary people. When righteous men and women grow in number and influence, the mood of a whole people changes, and they rejoice. Righteous authority serves others, protects the weak, and judges fairly, so those under it can live in peace. Chapter 28 made the same observation from other angles (28:12, 28). The saying applies to every place where some people have authority over others, from a nation to a household, and it is not a slogan for one party or cause.

“But when a wicked man rules, the people groan.”

A wicked ruler uses power for himself, and the people under him pay for it. “Groan” is the sigh of people burdened and oppressed. The verse does not list the wicked ruler’s achievements; it listens to the sound of his people. Scripture shows that such groaning does not go unheard: when Israel cried out under Pharaoh, “God heard their groaning” (Exodus 2:24).

Study Notes

The NKJV’s “are in authority” interprets a Hebrew phrase that more literally means when the righteous increase. The ESV and NASB read “increase,” and the KJV margin offers “increased.” The same expression describes the wicked being “multiplied” in 29:16, setting the two sayings side by side in this chapter.

Proverbs 29:3
Proverbs 29:3
NKJV

Proverbs 29:3 Whoever loves wisdom makes his father rejoice, But a companion of harlots wastes his wealth.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:3 The man who loves wisdom brings joy to his father,but if he hangs around with prostitutes, his wealth is wasted. (NLT)

Main Idea

A son who loves wisdom brings joy to his father, but one who keeps company with harlots squanders his wealth. The saying joins a father’s gladness over a wise son with the earlier warnings that sexual sin wastes a man’s substance.

Verse Explanation

A son who loves wisdom brings joy to his father, but one who keeps company with harlots squanders his wealth. The saying joins a father’s gladness over a wise son with the earlier warnings that sexual sin wastes a man’s substance.

“Whoever loves wisdom makes his father rejoice,”

The son in view does more than possess some wisdom; he loves it. What a person loves directs his steps, and a son who loves wisdom chooses its way. Wisdom herself said, “I love those who love me” (8:17). His choices reach beyond himself to his family. A father’s joy over a wise son is a familiar theme in Proverbs, beginning with “A wise son makes a glad father” (10:1).

“But a companion of harlots wastes his wealth.”

The second line names a different love and a different company. A man who keeps company with harlots squanders his wealth, and with it his strength, honor, and years. The father of chapters 5–7 warned that the immoral woman would leave “aliens” to be “filled with your wealth” (5:10) and that “by means of a harlot A man is reduced to a crust of bread” (6:26). In Jesus’ parable the younger son “wasted his possessions with prodigal living” (Luke 15:13). Later his angry older brother accuses him of having “devoured your livelihood with harlots” (Luke 15:30). That charge is the brother’s accusation within the story, not the narrator’s report, yet the parable pictures the kind of waste this proverb warns against. The verse sets the two loves side by side, an echo of the two women who call to the young man throughout the opening chapters of the book: Wisdom and the immoral woman.

Study Notes

Proverbs 28:7 makes a closely related contrast: “Whoever keeps the law is a discerning son, But a companion of gluttons shames his father” (28:7). In both sayings a son’s companions show where his heart is and decide whether his father rejoices or is shamed.

Proverbs 29:4
Proverbs 29:4
NKJV

Proverbs 29:4 The king establishes the landby justice, But he who receives bribes overthrows it.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:4 A just king gives stability to his nation,but one who demands bribes destroys it. (NLT)

Main Idea

A king who rules with justice makes the land stable, but one who takes bribes tears it down. Corruption at the top does not stay there; the whole country pays for it.

Verse Explanation

A king who rules with justice makes the land stable, but one who takes bribes tears it down. Corruption at the top does not stay there; the whole country pays for it.

“The king establishes the land by justice,”

To establish is to make something stand firm. A land’s stability rests on fair judgment more than on armies, buildings, or wealth. When courts decide cases by the truth and rulers treat rich and poor by the same standard, people can plan, work, and trust one another. Proverbs says the same of the throne itself: “a throne is established by righteousness” (16:12).

“But he who receives bribes overthrows it.”

The contrast is a ruler who uses his position to take for himself. Bribes bend judgment toward whoever pays, and corruption at the top soon reaches every household below. The same authority that could hold a land up instead tears it down, undermining the very land it was meant to support. The verse speaks of a king, yet its principle applies wherever trust has been given, whether in government, business, church, or home.

Study Notes

The Hebrew behind “he who receives bribes” is literally a man of contributions, which the 1611 KJV margin renders as a man of oblations. The Hebrew word for contributions is often used elsewhere for offerings brought to the LORD. Here it most likely describes a ruler who demands gifts or payments for himself. Translations divide: the NKJV and NASB read “bribes,” while the ESV reads “exacts gifts,” pointing to heavy demands placed on the people.

Proverbs 29:5
Proverbs 29:5
NKJV

Proverbs 29:5 A man who flatters his neighbor Spreads a net for his feet.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:5 To flatter friendsis to lay a trap for their feet. (NLT)

Main Idea

Flattering a neighbor is like spreading a net at his feet. Smooth praise sets a trap for the one who believes it, and the net may well catch the flatterer too.

Verse Explanation

Flattering a neighbor is like spreading a net at his feet. Smooth praise sets a trap for the one who believes it, and the net may well catch the flatterer too.

“A man who flatters his neighbor”

To flatter is to speak smoothly, offering praise that is not true in order to gain something. The target is a “neighbor,” someone near enough to trust the speaker. Flattery feels like kindness, which is exactly what makes it dangerous. Proverbs warns that “a flattering mouth works ruin” (26:28) and that honest rebuke wins more favor in the end than flattering words (28:23).

“Spreads a net for his feet.”

A hunter’s net was laid out of sight on the path where an animal would walk. Flattery works the same way: hidden behind pleasant words, it catches a person by the feet, steering his steps into pride, false confidence, or foolish decisions. The Hebrew, like the English, does not say whose feet. The plain sense is the neighbor’s, yet Proverbs also knows that the one who sets a trap often falls into it himself (26:27), and 29:6 goes on to show the evil man snared by his own transgression.

Study Notes

The NASB reads “his steps” where the NKJV has “his feet,” keeping the picture of a trap laid along the path a person walks.

Proverbs 29:6
Proverbs 29:6
NKJV

Proverbs 29:6 By transgression an evil man is snared, But the righteous sings and rejoices.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:6 Evil people are trapped by sin,but the righteous escape, shouting for joy. (NLT)

Main Idea

An evil man is caught in the snare of his own transgression, while the righteous man sings and rejoices. The trap of verse 5 continues: sin entangles the sinner, but the righteous walk free and glad.

Verse Explanation

An evil man is caught in the snare of his own transgression, while the righteous man sings and rejoices. The trap of verse 5 continues: sin entangles the sinner, but the righteous walk free and glad.

“By transgression an evil man is snared,”

Transgression is rebellion against what is right, a deliberate crossing of God’s lines. The trap is built into the sin itself. No outside hunter is needed, because the wrong that promised gain binds the one who does it with guilt, fear, consequences, and the further lies needed to cover the first. The thought follows directly from the flatterer’s net in 29:5. Proverbs says it plainly elsewhere: “His own iniquities entrap the wicked man” (5:22).

“But the righteous sings and rejoices.”

The righteous man is free where the evil man is caught. Singing is the opposite of being snared: a trapped animal struggles, while the righteous lift their voices in joy. Their life is not free from trouble, but they have nothing hidden to fear and nothing to keep covering. Their gladness comes from walking openly before God.

Study Notes

Word for word, the Hebrew of the first line reads in the transgression of an evil man is a snare, and the KJV keeps that order. The wording places the trap inside the wrongdoing itself.

The Hebrew word for “snare,” a hunter’s noose or trap, returns in 29:25, where “The fear of man brings a snare” (29:25).

Proverbs 29:7
Proverbs 29:7
NKJV

Proverbs 29:7 The righteous considers the cause of the poor, But the wicked does not understand such knowledge.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:7 The godly care about the rights of the poor;the wicked don’t care at all. (NLT)

Main Idea

The righteous man pays attention to the cause of the poor, but the wicked has no understanding of such concern. Knowing and defending the rights of the needy is part of what righteousness knows.

Verse Explanation

The righteous man pays attention to the cause of the poor, but the wicked has no understanding of such concern. Knowing and defending the rights of the needy is part of what righteousness knows.

“The righteous considers the cause of the poor,”

The Hebrew word translated “cause” is a legal term for a case or plea, so the “cause” of the poor is his claim to justice. The Hebrew verb behind “considers” is the ordinary word for knowing. The righteous man takes the trouble to know the poor man’s situation and his rights, especially in the courts at the city gate, where the poor were easily overlooked or brushed aside. Job described this kind of righteousness: “I was a father to the poor, And I searched out the case that I did not know” (Job 29:16). The righteous do not stop at sympathy; they find out what is true and take the poor man’s side in justice.

“But the wicked does not understand such knowledge.”

The wicked man’s failure is moral before it is mental. He does not care to know, so he never understands. The verse treats concern for the poor as a kind of knowledge that belongs to righteousness. The LORD said of King Josiah, “He judged the cause of the poor and needy; Then it was well. Was not this knowing Me?” (Jeremiah 22:16). To be blind to the poor is to be blind to what God cares about.

Study Notes

Where the NKJV reads “considers the cause,” the ESV keeps the verb “knows” and renders the noun “rights,” bringing out its legal sense.

Proverbs 29:8–14 — Wise Men, Truthful Rulers, and the Poor

Proverbs 29:8
Proverbs 29:8
NKJV

Proverbs 29:8 Scoffers set a city aflame, But wise men turn away wrath.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:8 Mockers can get a whole town agitated,but the wise will calm anger. (NLT)

Main Idea

Scoffers set a whole city on fire with their contempt and agitation, but wise men calm anger and turn it away. The scorner, the most dangerous of the fools, endangers a community, while the wise make peace.

Verse Explanation

Scoffers set a whole city on fire with their contempt and agitation, but wise men calm anger and turn it away. The scorner, the most dangerous of the fools, endangers a community, while the wise make peace.

“Scoffers set a city aflame,”

The scoffer is the scorner of earlier chapters, the most dangerous of the fools because his problem lies in his will: he will not be taught (9:7–8). Here his contempt reaches beyond his own life. By mocking authority, truth, and decency, scoffers stir up resentment and division until a whole city is inflamed. They do not always start the fire; they fan the embers until it spreads. Proverbs elsewhere gives the remedy: “Cast out the scoffer, and contention will leave” (22:10).

“But wise men turn away wrath.”

Wise men do the opposite. They calm anger and cool a city down. Their tools are patient, truthful, gentle words, for “A soft answer turns away wrath” (15:1). Scripture gives an example in the wise woman of Abel, who called from the wall to Joab, reasoned with him, and saved her city from destruction (2 Samuel 20:16–22). One wise voice can protect many people.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “set aflame” basically means to breathe or blow. Here it describes scoffers stirring a city up, as breath fans a fire until it spreads. The KJV main text reads “a snare” where modern versions have the fire image, and the 1611 margin gives “on fire” as the alternative.

Proverbs 29:9
Proverbs 29:9
NKJV

Proverbs 29:9 If a wise man contends with a foolish man, Whether the fool rages or laughs, there is no peace.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:9 If a wise person takes a fool to court,there will be ranting and ridicule but no satisfaction. (NLT)

Main Idea

When a wise man takes up a dispute with a foolish man, there is no peace, whether the fool rages or laughs. Argument with a fool goes nowhere, because he will not deal with the matter honestly.

Verse Explanation

When a wise man takes up a dispute with a foolish man, there is no peace, whether the fool rages or laughs. Argument with a fool goes nowhere, because he will not deal with the matter honestly.

“If a wise man contends with a foolish man,”

To contend is to dispute or argue a case, possibly in a formal legal setting. The wise man may be right, patient, and reasonable, yet the question is what happens when he takes up a quarrel with a fool.

“Whether the fool rages or laughs, there is no peace.”

The fool has two responses, and neither deals honestly with the matter. He either flies into a rage or laughs the whole thing off. Anger and ridicule both refuse to listen. So there is “no peace” (29:9), no rest or settlement. Wisdom learns to recognize when a dispute cannot be won and when it is better to stop, as the paired sayings on answering a fool show (26:4–5). Truth met with rage or laughter is not always proof that it was poorly spoken.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “contends” comes from the word for judging, so the dispute may be a case at law.

The Hebrew of the second line does not name its subject. The NKJV and ESV supply “the fool,” and the NASB names the foolish man, while the KJV keeps the open “he rage.” That open wording would allow the sense that whether the wise man answers angrily or lightly, nothing is settled. Either reading ends without rest. The NASB and KJV render the final phrase “no rest.”

Proverbs 29:10
Proverbs 29:10
NKJV

Proverbs 29:10 The bloodthirsty hate the blameless, But the upright seek his well-being.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:10 The bloodthirsty hate blameless people,but the upright seek to help them. (NLT)

Main Idea

Bloodthirsty men hate the person of integrity, but the upright seek his well-being. The blameless man draws opposite responses: the violent want him gone, while the upright want him to flourish.

Verse Explanation

Bloodthirsty men hate the person of integrity, but the upright seek his well-being. The blameless man draws opposite responses: the violent want him gone, while the upright want him to flourish.

“The bloodthirsty hate the blameless,”

The Hebrew behind “bloodthirsty” is literally men of blood, people ready for violence. The “blameless” is a person of integrity, whole and consistent in his ways. Violent men hate him for what he is more than for anything he has done to them. His integrity exposes them. Scripture traces this pattern to the beginning: Cain murdered Abel “Because his works were evil and his brother’s righteous” (1 John 3:12).

“But the upright seek his well-being.”

The same blameless person draws the opposite response from the upright. The Hebrew phrase is literally seek his life. Elsewhere that expression usually describes enemies trying to kill someone (Exodus 4:19; 1 Samuel 20:1), but here the upright seek his life in order to protect and preserve it. Ebed-Melech did this when he drew Jeremiah out of the dungeon (Jeremiah 38:7–13). How people treat a person of integrity reveals what kind of people they are.

Study Notes

The second line is read in two ways. The NKJV, NASB, and NLT take it as protection of the blameless. Because seek his life usually describes hostility in Hebrew, the ESV takes “the upright” as the object of the bloodthirsty, so that both lines describe hostility: the violent hate the blameless and seek the life of the upright. The NKJV reading fits the contrast that usually marks the two lines of these sayings.

Proverbs 29:11
Proverbs 29:11
NKJV

Proverbs 29:11 A fool ventsall his feelings, But a wise man holds them back.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:11 Fools vent their anger,but the wise quietly hold it back. (NLT)

Main Idea

Venting every feeling marks a fool, but a wise man holds his feelings back. The wise still feel deeply, yet they keep their emotions in check and choose when and how to express them.

Verse Explanation

Venting every feeling marks a fool, but a wise man holds his feelings back. The wise still feel deeply, yet they keep their emotions in check and choose when and how to express them.

“A fool vents all his feelings,”

The Hebrew says that the fool brings out all his spirit, everything stirring inside him. The key word is “all.” Having feelings is not the problem. The fool releases every one of them, whenever and however they arise, without filter or thought for the damage. Anger is the most obvious case, but the same is true of resentment, fear, impatience, and contempt. Proverbs has already said, “A fool’s wrath is known at once” (12:16).

“But a wise man holds them back.”

The wise man still feels, sometimes deeply, but he restrains and quiets his feelings so they do not rule him. He chooses when and how to speak. This is strength, for “he who rules his spirit” is better “than he who takes a city” (16:32). The verse warns against venting without restraint; it does not forbid the honest expression of emotion at the right time and in the right way.

Study Notes

The NASB and NLT narrow the first line to anger or temper, while the NKJV and ESV keep the broader sense of all a person feels. The KJV’s “mind” is similarly broad.

The second line contains a Hebrew word that basically means back or behind. Most modern versions join it to the verb, so the wise man holds his feelings back. The KJV and YLT take it as a time word, “till afterwards,” meaning that the wise man waits for a fitting time.

The Hebrew verb translated “holds them back” means to calm or still. It is the same verb used of God in “When its waves rise, You still them” (Psalm 89:9). The wise man quiets his feelings the way the LORD quiets a stormy sea.

Proverbs 29:12
Proverbs 29:12
NKJV

Proverbs 29:12 If a ruler pays attention to lies, All his servants become wicked.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:12 If a ruler pays attention to liars,all his advisers will be wicked. (NLT)

Main Idea

If a ruler listens to lies, all his officials become wicked. Servants quickly learn what pleases the man at the top, and a leader who welcomes falsehood fills his court with liars.

Verse Explanation

If a ruler listens to lies, all his officials become wicked. Servants quickly learn what pleases the man at the top, and a leader who welcomes falsehood fills his court with liars.

“If a ruler pays attention to lies,”

The verse does not say the ruler lies. It says he listens to lies. He welcomes flattery, slander, and false reports, and he rewards those who tell him what he wants to hear. A leader’s ear decides what kind of speech will flourish around him.

“All his servants become wicked.”

His “servants” are his officials and ministers. They quickly learn what gets a hearing and what gets them promoted. Honest people are pushed aside or give up, and the rest learn to deceive. The word “all” shows how complete the corruption becomes. A ruler who does not love the truth fills his court with liars without ever telling a lie himself. The principle reaches beyond kings to anyone who leads others, for those under a leader take on the standard of truth the leader accepts.

Study Notes

The ESV renders “servants” as “officials,” and the NASB reads “ministers,” making clear that the verse has a ruler’s court and government in view.

Proverbs 29:13
Proverbs 29:13
NKJV

Proverbs 29:13 The poor man and the oppressor have this in common: The LORD gives light to the eyes of both.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:13 The poor and the oppressor have this in common —the LORD gives sight to the eyes of both. (NLT)

Main Idea

The poor man and his oppressor share this in common: the LORD gives light to the eyes of both. Both owe their life to the same Creator, so the oppressor has no ground to despise the poor, and both will answer to the God who made them.

Verse Explanation

The poor man and his oppressor share this in common: the LORD gives light to the eyes of both. Both owe their life to the same Creator, so the oppressor has no ground to despise the poor, and both will answer to the God who made them.

“The poor man and the oppressor have this in common:”

The two men stand at opposite ends of society, one crushed and the other doing the crushing. The Hebrew says literally that the two meet together. Whatever separates them, one thing joins them, and the verse names it in the second line. Proverbs used the same pattern for the rich and the poor: “The rich and the poor have this in common, The LORD is the maker of them all” (22:2).

“The LORD gives light to the eyes of both.”

To give light to the eyes is to give sight and life itself. David prayed, “Enlighten my eyes, Lest I sleep the sleep of death” (Psalm 13:3). Every breath the oppressor draws and every day the poor man lives come from the same Creator. The oppressor therefore has no ground to despise the poor man, whose life is as much God’s gift as his own. The poor man is not forgotten, for God keeps his eyes alight. Both will answer to the God who made them.

Study Notes

The ESV and KJV keep the literal wording of the first line: the two “meet together.”

The Hebrew describes the second man literally as a man of oppressions, someone who takes advantage of others. The KJV reads “deceitful,” with “the usurer,” a lender who charges crushing interest, in its 1611 margin. Modern versions agree on “oppressor.”

Proverbs 29:14
Proverbs 29:14
NKJV

Proverbs 29:14 The king who judges the poor with truth, His throne will be established forever.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:14 If a king judges the poor fairly,his throne will last forever. (NLT)

Main Idea

Judging the poor with truth establishes a king’s throne forever. Faithful justice for the weakest is the general pattern that gives a reign stability, but a throne that truly lasts forever is found only in Christ, the King who judges the poor with righteousness (Isaiah 11:4).

Verse Explanation

Judging the poor with truth establishes a king’s throne forever. Faithful justice for the weakest is the general pattern that gives a reign stability, but a throne that truly lasts forever is found only in Christ, the King who judges the poor with righteousness (Isaiah 11:4).

“The king who judges the poor with truth,”

To judge with truth is to decide faithfully, according to what is actually right, without being swayed by bribes, status, or pressure (29:4). The poor have nothing to offer a king in return, so justice for them is the clearest test of a ruler. The verse rests the strength of a reign on its treatment of the weakest people in it.

“His throne will be established forever.”

As a general pattern, faithful justice gives a reign stability: “Mercy and truth preserve the king” (20:28). The verse describes how God’s world usually works and is not a guarantee for any human throne, for even the best human kings die and their kingdoms pass. The phrase recalls God’s promise to David, “Your throne shall be established forever” (2 Samuel 7:16). A throne that truly lasts forever belongs only to Christ, the Son of David, who will judge the poor with perfect truth. Isaiah wrote of Him, “with righteousness He shall judge the poor” (Isaiah 11:4). The saying finds its fullest answer in His reign.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “truth” also means faithfulness or reliability, which is why the ESV and KJV render “with truth” as “faithfully.” The truth in view is faithful, dependable judgment, not only accurate information.

Proverbs 29:15–21 — Correction and Revelation

Proverbs 29:15
Proverbs 29:15
NKJV

Proverbs 29:15 The rod and rebuke give wisdom, But a child left to himself brings shame to his mother.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:15 To discipline a child produces wisdom,but a mother is disgraced by an undisciplined child. (NLT)

Main Idea

The rod and rebuke give a child wisdom, while a child left to himself brings shame to his mother. Correction given in love, never in rage, harshness, or abuse, teaches, but neglect leaves a child to his own folly.

Verse Explanation

The rod and rebuke give a child wisdom, while a child left to himself brings shame to his mother. Correction given in love, never in rage, harshness, or abuse, teaches, but neglect leaves a child to his own folly.

“The rod and rebuke give wisdom,”

Two means of correction stand together: the rod, which corrects by action, and rebuke, which corrects by word. In its historical setting the rod was the familiar tool of a parent’s physical correction, and Proverbs has already spoken of it (13:24; 22:15; 23:13–14). Pairing it with rebuke shows that discipline is more than punishment. It is the whole work of teaching, guiding, correcting, and training a child toward maturity, with consequences serving that larger aim. The goal is named plainly: the rod and rebuke “give wisdom,” forming character and understanding rather than mere compliance. Such correction reflects the Father’s own, for “whom the LORD loves He corrects” (3:12). It is never an outlet for rage, harshness, humiliation, or abuse, which contradict His goodness and provoke children instead of training them (Ephesians 6:4).

“But a child left to himself brings shame to his mother.”

“Left to himself” describes a child let loose to go his own way without guidance. That is neglect, not freedom. The verse names the mother, and 10:1 likewise speaks of a foolish son as “the grief of his mother” (10:1). Scripture records David’s failure with his son Adonijah, for he “had not rebuked him at any time” (1 Kings 1:6), and Adonijah grew up to try to seize the throne. The saying states a pattern of wisdom; it is not a verdict on parents whose children wander despite faithful care.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “left to himself” is the common word for sending. Here its form describes a child sent off or let loose, without restraint. The NASB (1995) interprets this as a child who gets his own way.

Proverbs 29:16
Proverbs 29:16
NKJV

Proverbs 29:16 When the wicked are multiplied, transgression increases; But the righteous will see their fall.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 29:16 When the wicked are in authority, sin flourishes,but the godly will live to see their downfall. (NLT)

Main Idea

When the wicked multiply, transgression increases with them, but the righteous will see their fall. Evil may flourish for a time, yet it does not have the last word.

Verse Explanation

When the wicked multiply, transgression increases with them, but the righteous will see their fall. Evil may flourish for a time, yet it does not have the last word.

“When the wicked are multiplied, transgression increases;”

Sin spreads as the people who love it multiply. When wicked people grow in number and influence, wrongdoing becomes normal, laws bend, and transgression increases throughout a society. The saying looks back to 29:2, where the increase of the righteous made the people rejoice. Here the opposite increase produces more sin.

“But the righteous will see their fall.”

Wickedness may flourish for a time, but it does not last. The verse does not say how the wicked fall; it says that the righteous will “see” it. Proverbs says elsewhere, “When the whirlwind passes by, the wicked is no more” (10:25). The verse does not promise that every believer will witness the downfall of each wicked person in this life, and it gives no license to gloat (24:17). It assures God’s people that evil does not have the last word, so they can keep to God’s way and wait for Him.

Study Notes

The verb behind “are multiplied” and “increases” is the same Hebrew word, so the line reads with a deliberate echo: as the wicked increase, transgression increases. The ESV brings this out with “increase” in both places.

Sayings about the rise and fall of the wicked and the increase of the righteous recur through the end of this collection (28:12, 28; 29:2, 16).

To correct is to discipline in the full sense of training a child, with teaching, clear expectations, patience, consequences when needed, and forgiveness that restores fellowship. “Yes, he will give delight to your soul.”

“Yes” intensifies the promise of the first line. Beyond rest, the corrected son becomes a source of delight to his parent’s soul, a joy felt deep within. The verse describes the usual fruit of loving, patient correction. It is a pattern of wisdom, not a guarantee, and it never condemns parents whose children stray despite their faithful care. Proverbs leaves room for each child’s own choices, as it does throughout its sayings about sons.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “delight” usually refers to rich, choice food. Jacob uses it of the “royal dainties” that Asher’s land would yield (Genesis 49:20). Applied to a son, the word pictures the deep satisfaction a wise child brings a parent, like the enjoyment of a fine meal.

The ESV reads “Discipline” for “Correct,” and the NASB reads “comfort” for the NKJV’s “rest.”

When God’s prophetic word is missing, people throw off restraint, but the one who keeps God’s law is happy. The revelation in view is a word from God, as in the days when the word of the LORD was rare (1 Samuel 3:1), and it goes hand in hand with obeying what He has already said.

“Where there is no revelation,”

Here revelation means a prophetic word from God, the vision given to a prophet. The same Hebrew word introduces the prophecy of Isaiah (Isaiah 1:1). In Samuel’s boyhood Israel lived through such a time: “the word of the LORD was rare in those days; there was no widespread revelation” (1 Samuel 3:1). The verse is about hearing from God, not about planning, goal-setting, or an organization’s vision statement.

“the people cast off restraint;”

Without God’s word, people are not left neutral; they run loose. The same Hebrew verb describes Israel at the golden calf, when “Moses saw that the people were unrestrained” (Exodus 32:25). When the voice of God is absent or ignored, nothing holds a people back from following their own desires. Amos warned of a famine “of hearing the words of the LORD” (Amos 8:11), and such times bring moral collapse.

“But happy is he who keeps the law.”

The second line joins revelation to obedience. “The law” is God’s instruction already given. A people needs God’s living word, and the person who keeps what God has already said is happy, blessed in the fullest sense. Even when prophetic revelation is rare, the one who obeys God’s law walks in His favor. The verse holds hearing and obeying together.

Study Notes

The Hebrew noun translated “revelation” refers to a vision God gives a prophet, a message from Him rather than human foresight. The ESV renders it “prophetic vision,” and the KJV “vision.”

The Hebrew verb behind “cast off restraint” means to let go or let loose; elsewhere it describes hair let down loose. The KJV’s familiar “perish” differs from modern versions, which read “cast off restraint” or “unrestrained.” The 1611 KJV margin offers “made naked,” closer to the sense of being let loose.

The second line goes further. Uncontrolled anger does more than start quarrels; it multiplies sin. Harsh words, rash decisions, injustice, bitterness, and sometimes violence all flow from it. The “furious man” is ruled by his wrath rather than ruling it. Scripture does not condemn every kind of anger, for Paul writes, “Be angry, and do not sin” (Ephesians 4:26), but it warns plainly about anger that has become a master.

Study Notes

The Hebrew behind “a furious man” is literally a master or owner of wrath, an idiom for a person characterized by rage.

The ESV renders the second line with the verb “causes,” suggesting that his anger leads others into sin as well as himself.

A man’s pride will bring him low, but the humble in spirit will retain honor. Pride and humility reverse their outcomes, the one who lifts himself up falling and the one who bows low being honored, as Jesus also taught (Luke 14:11).

“A man’s pride will bring him low,”

Pride lifts a person up in his own eyes, and the verse says where it ends: low. The proud man reaches for a height he cannot hold, and the very thing he grasps brings him down. Proverbs has warned of this before: “Pride goes before destruction, And a haughty spirit before a fall” (16:18). Pride is a matter of the heart that the fear of the LORD exposes, for the LORD says, “Pride and arrogance and the evil way And the perverse mouth I hate” (8:13).

“But the humble in spirit will retain honor.”

The humble in spirit do not grasp at honor, yet honor holds on to them. The outcomes reverse: the one who lifts himself falls, and the one who bows low is honored. “Before honor is humility” (15:33). Jesus taught the same reversal: “whoever exalts himself will be humbled, and he who humbles himself will be exalted” (Luke 14:11). Humility here is a lowly spirit before God and others, not a show of modesty.

Study Notes

In Hebrew the two lines are linked by related words. The verb “bring him low” and the word “humble” come from the same Hebrew root, a word meaning low: pride makes a man low, and the humble are literally the low in spirit. The proud man is made low against his will, while the humble man chooses lowliness and receives honor.

The ESV and NASB read “obtain honor,” and the KJV makes honor the subject: honour shall “uphold” the humble.

Verse Explanation

Partnering with a thief means hating your own life, because when the partner is put under oath he swears to tell the truth yet reveals nothing. Sharing in another man’s crime traps him into silence and guilt before God (compare Leviticus 5:1).

“Whoever is a partner with a thief hates his own life;”

The partner shares in the thief’s gains, perhaps only by receiving a portion or keeping quiet. He may think he has profited, but he has acted against himself. By joining the crime he has bound his own soul to its guilt. The phrase “hates his own life” means he is his own enemy, choosing what will harm him.

“He swears to tell the truth, but reveals nothing.”

The setting is a legal one. When a crime was investigated, a public oath, carrying a curse, called on anyone with knowledge to testify. The Law of Moses said that a witness who heard “the utterance of an oath” (Leviticus 5:1) and did not tell what he knew “bears guilt” (Leviticus 5:1). The thief’s partner hears that call, even swears, yet tells nothing, because speaking would condemn him. His silence adds perjury to theft. He is trapped either way, and the guilt lies on him before God.

Study Notes

The Hebrew behind “He swears to tell the truth” says literally that he hears the oath. The word refers to an oath that calls down a curse on anyone who withholds testimony, the situation addressed in Leviticus 5:1. The NKJV expresses this as swearing to tell the truth, the ESV speaks of hearing “the curse,” and the KJV reads “heareth cursing.”

The fear of man is a snare, but whoever trusts in the LORD will be safe. Fear of what others think or can do traps a person into compromise, while trust in the LORD, which grows from a right view of Him, sets him on secure ground in God even when harm still comes.

“The fear of man brings a snare,”

The fear of man is fear of what other people think, say, or can do. It traps a person into compromise, silence, flattery, and lies, as the thief’s partner of 29:24 is trapped by fear of exposure. King Saul confessed this snare: “I feared the people and obeyed their voice” (1 Samuel 15:24). The book began with “The fear of the LORD” (1:7) as the beginning of knowledge. The fear of the LORD, a right view of God, sees God as great and people as they truly are. When people loom larger than God, their opinions take control.

“But whoever trusts in the LORD shall be safe.”

Trust in the LORD is the way out of the snare. It grows from seeing God rightly, as faithful, strong, and good. “Safe” pictures being lifted to a high place beyond an enemy’s reach. This describes security in God; it is not a promise that the one who trusts will never suffer harm. Those who trusted God have often suffered for it, yet they were held securely by Him. Freed from the fear of man, a person can do what is right and leave the outcome with God.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “snare” here also appears in 29:6, where the evil man is caught in his own transgression.

The Hebrew verb behind “safe” means to be set high, out of an enemy’s reach. Its form here is passive: the one who trusts is set on high rather than climbing there himself. The 1611 KJV margin gives the literal sense, set on high, and the NASB (1995) renders it “exalted.” The same verb describes the righteous in “The name of the LORD is a strong tower; The righteous run to it and are safe” (18:10).

“And he who is upright in the way is an abomination to the wicked.”

The feeling runs both ways. The wicked detest the person who walks “upright in the way,” for his straight path exposes their crooked one. “The way” recalls the book’s picture of two paths and their two ends. The collection of Solomon’s proverbs closes here with those two ways fully opposed, each rejecting what the other loves. The reader is left to choose which way to walk, and the following words of Agur open a new section of the book.

Study Notes

This verse closes the proverbs of Solomon copied by Hezekiah’s men, which began at 25:1, and with them the sayings attributed to Solomon in the book. The words of Agur begin at 30:1.
