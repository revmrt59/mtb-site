Proverbs 30:1
Proverbs 30:1
NKJV

Proverbs 30:1 The words of Agur the son of Jakeh, his utterance. This man declared to Ithiel"”to Ithiel and Ucal:(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:1 The sayings of Agur son of Jakeh contain this message. I am weary, O God;I am weary and worn out, O God. (NLT)

Main Idea

The chapter records the words of Agur the son of Jakeh, a wise man otherwise unknown, given as his utterance to Ithiel and Ucal. A new voice now speaks, distinct from Solomon’s, and he begins with a confession instead of confident instruction.

Verse Explanation

The chapter records the words of Agur the son of Jakeh, a wise man otherwise unknown, given as his utterance to Ithiel and Ucal. A new voice now speaks, distinct from Solomon’s, and he begins with a confession instead of confident instruction.

“The words of Agur the son of Jakeh,”

A new heading opens the chapter and a new voice speaks. Agur the son of Jakeh is otherwise unknown; Scripture names him only here and gives no family history beyond his father’s name. He is presented as his own author, distinct from Solomon, whose proverbs ended at 29:27. God did not need a famous name to give His people wisdom, and Agur’s words stand in the book beside the king’s.

“his utterance.”

The NKJV calls Agur’s words an “utterance,” a solemn and weighty saying. The term marks what follows as serious speech meant to be heard and received, not casual opinion.

“This man declared to Ithiel—to Ithiel and Ucal:”

“This man declared” introduces Agur’s formal address. Read as the NKJV gives it, Ithiel and Ucal are the people to whom he spoke, perhaps his students or sons, and the repetition of Ithiel’s name gives the line a personal, earnest tone. Wisdom in Proverbs is always passed on to someone. What Agur declares will begin, surprisingly, with a confession of his own limits rather than confident instruction.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “utterance” is often translated oracle or burden, a weighty message. The ESV and NASB read “oracle,” and the KJV “prophecy.” Some take the word instead as the name of a people or region, Massa, which appears among the sons of Ishmael in Genesis 25:14. The same word stands in the heading of Lemuel’s words at 31:1.

The names Ithiel and Ucal are also debated. By dividing the Hebrew letters differently, some read the line as a lament, and the ESV and NLT render it, “I am weary, O God,” continuing with words about being worn out. The NKJV, NASB, and KJV keep both names. The current NIV (2011) keeps Ithiel and then reads the rest as a lament about weariness, noting in its footnote that this follows a different division of the Hebrew words.

The medieval Jewish commentator Rashi identified Agur with Solomon, explaining the name as the one who gathered understanding and saying that the Sages read it this way. The text itself gives no such identification, and the heading presents Agur’s words as a separate collection.

Proverbs 30:2
Proverbs 30:2
NKJV

Proverbs 30:2 Surely I am more stupid than any man, And do not have the understanding of a man.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:2 I am too stupid to be human,and I lack common sense. (NLT)

Main Idea

Agur declares himself more stupid than any man, lacking even ordinary human understanding. His words are the honest humility of a man who has measured himself against God, and that humility is where his wisdom begins.

Verse Explanation

Agur declares himself more stupid than any man, lacking even ordinary human understanding. His words are the honest humility of a man who has measured himself against God, and that humility is where his wisdom begins.

“Surely I am more stupid than any man,”

Agur opens with a startling confession. “Stupid” is a strong word, used in Proverbs of the brutish man who “hates correction” (12:1). Agur applies it to himself. He has measured himself against God and found that, by that measure, he has nothing to boast of. This is not false modesty or self-hatred; it is honesty. Wisdom begins when a person stops pretending to know what he does not know.

“And do not have the understanding of a man.”

He adds that he lacks even ordinary human understanding. The point is not that Agur was ignorant in everyday matters, for the rest of the chapter shows a keen observer of life. He is confessing that his own understanding cannot reach the things that matter most. His humility is the doorway to the questions of 30:4 and the trust of 30:5. In Proverbs, the person who knows his limits is teachable, and the teachable person grows wise.

Study Notes

The KJV reads “brutish” for “stupid,” bringing out the sense of being like a beast in understanding.

Proverbs 30:3
Proverbs 30:3
NKJV

Proverbs 30:3 I neither learned wisdom Nor have knowledge of the Holy One.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:3 I have not mastered human wisdom,nor do I know the Holy One. (NLT)

Main Idea

He has not learned wisdom and does not have knowledge of the Holy One. Agur admits that God cannot be reached by human effort alone, which is the right view of God that the fear of the LORD starts with.

Verse Explanation

He has not learned wisdom and does not have knowledge of the Holy One. Agur admits that God cannot be reached by human effort alone, which is the right view of God that the fear of the LORD starts with.

“I neither learned wisdom”

Agur says he has not learned wisdom. Coming from a man whose sayings are part of Proverbs, this is not a denial that he had studied; it is a confession that wisdom in its fullness lies beyond what study alone can master. He speaks as one who knows how much remains unknown.

“Nor have knowledge of the Holy One.”

The deepest lack is knowledge of “the Holy One,” God who is unlike any other. Proverbs has already said, “the knowledge of the Holy One is understanding” (9:10). Agur admits that this knowledge cannot be reached by human effort. God is known only as He makes Himself known. This admission is itself the right view of God with which the fear of the LORD begins: God is high and holy, and people are dependent on Him. Agur’s confession does not express doubt or unbelief. It is the posture of a man who reverences God too much to claim that he has grasped Him.

Study Notes

The phrase “knowledge of the Holy One” repeats the wording of 9:10, where it is paired with the fear of the LORD as the beginning of wisdom.

In Hebrew the verb in the second line is know, as the KJV margin notes: Agur says he does not know the knowledge of the Holy One.

Proverbs 30:4
Proverbs 30:4
NKJV

Proverbs 30:4 Who has ascended into heaven, or descended? Who has gathered the wind in His fists? Who has bound the waters in a garment? Who has established all the ends of the earth? What is His name, and what is His Son's name, If you know?(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:4 Who but God goes up to heaven and comes back down?Who holds the wind in his fists?Who wraps up the oceans in his cloak?Who has created the whole wide world?What is his name — and his son’s name?Tell me if you know! (NLT)

Main Idea

Agur presses his confession with questions only the Creator can answer, about the One who rules heaven, wind, waters, and the ends of the earth. He ends, “What is His name, and what is His Son’s name, If you know?” Agur does not supply the name, but the question finds its answer in Jesus, who told Nicodemus in John 3:13 that the Son of Man had come down from heaven.

Verse Explanation

Agur presses his confession with questions only the Creator can answer, about the One who rules heaven, wind, waters, and the ends of the earth. He ends, “What is His name, and what is His Son’s name, If you know?” Agur does not supply the name, but the question finds its answer in Jesus, who told Nicodemus in John 3:13 that the Son of Man had come down from heaven.

“Who has ascended into heaven, or descended?”

Agur presses his confession with questions only the Creator can answer. The first asks who has gone up to heaven and come back down, able to report what is there. No human being can climb into heaven by his own power and come back with full, independent knowledge of God to report. Whatever people know of heaven, they know because God has chosen to reveal it.

“Who has gathered the wind in His fists? Who has bound the waters in a garment? Who has established all the ends of the earth?”

The next questions point to the Creator’s works. Only God holds the wind as if it were in His closed hands, wraps the waters as in a cloak, holding the clouds and seas in their place, and fixes the boundaries of the earth. The capital “His” in the NKJV shows that the One in view is God. These are questions that humble the questioner, much like the questions God put to Job.

“What is His name, and what is His Son’s name, If you know?”

Agur ends by asking the name of this Creator and the name of “His Son.” He does not supply the answer, and the questions leave every human reader without one. Yet the question about God’s Son finds its answer in Jesus, who told Nicodemus that “No one has ascended to heaven but He who came down from heaven, that is, the Son of Man” (John 3:13). Agur’s question does not show that he knew the Son’s name. It stands as a prophetic witness, answered when the Son came down from heaven and made the Father known.

Study Notes

Similar questions about the Creator’s works appear in God’s questions to Job (Job 38:4–11) and in Isaiah 40:12, which asks who has “measured the waters in the hollow of His hand” (Isaiah 40:12).

The ending is rendered in two ways, because the Hebrew can be read either as a question or as an exclamation. The NKJV and KJV read it as a question, while the ESV and NASB read it as an exclamation beginning “Surely,” and the NLT renders it as a challenge: “Tell me if you know!”

Proverbs 30:5–6 — Every Word of God Is Pure

Proverbs 30:5
Proverbs 30:5
NKJV

Proverbs 30:5 Every word of God is pure; He is a shield to those who put their trust in Him.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:5 Every word of God proves true.He is a shield to all who come to him for protection. (NLT)

Main Idea

Against his own limits Agur sets the reliability of God: every word of God is pure, tested and true, and He is a shield to those who trust Him. The man who cannot climb to heaven can rest on what God has spoken.

Verse Explanation

Against his own limits Agur sets the reliability of God: every word of God is pure, tested and true, and He is a shield to those who trust Him. The man who cannot climb to heaven can rest on what God has spoken.

“Every word of God is pure;”

Against his own limits Agur sets the reliability of God. He cannot climb to heaven, but God has spoken, and “every word” He speaks is pure. The picture is of metal refined in fire until nothing false remains in it. God’s words have been tested and found true. Agur does not say most of God’s words, but every one.

“He is a shield to those who put their trust in Him.”

The line moves from God’s word to God Himself. The shield is not the word as a separate thing but God, who stands between His people and harm. Those who trust Him find Him to be their protection. David used almost the same words in his song of deliverance, “He is a shield to all who trust in Him” (Psalm 18:30). Agur’s honest uncertainty about himself finds its resting place here: he may not have the knowledge of the Holy One by his own effort, but he can rely on the Holy One’s word and take shelter in Him.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “pure” means refined, the way metal is purified in a furnace until no impurity remains. The KJV margin reads “purified,” the NASB “tested,” and the ESV and NLT “proves true.” Psalm 12:6 uses the same picture: “The words of the LORD are pure words, Like silver tried in a furnace of earth” (Psalm 12:6).

The verse closely matches Psalm 18:30 and 2 Samuel 22:31, which also join the tested word of the LORD with the LORD as a shield to all who trust in Him.

Proverbs 30:6
NKJV

Proverbs 30:6 Do not add to His words, Lest He rebuke you, and you be found a liar.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:6 Do not add to his words,or he may rebuke you and expose you as a liar. (NLT)

Main Idea

Because God’s words are pure, no one should add to them, or God will rebuke him and expose him as a liar. Human ideas must never be passed off as God’s word (compare Deuteronomy 4:2).

Verse Explanation

Because God’s words are pure, no one should add to them, or God will rebuke him and expose him as a liar. Human ideas must never be passed off as God’s word (compare Deuteronomy 4:2).

“Do not add to His words,”

Because God’s words are pure, nothing needs to be mixed into them. Adding to them corrupts what was perfect. Moses gave the same warning to Israel: “You shall not add to the word which I command you, nor take from it” (Deuteronomy 4:2). Human ideas, traditions, and claims must never be passed off as what God has said. This warning applies to every teacher and every study of Scripture, including this one: careful interpretation remains interpretation and must not be presented as Scripture itself.

“Lest He rebuke you, and you be found a liar.”

God Himself will rebuke the one who adds to His words. The person who claims God’s authority for his own ideas will be exposed for what he is, a liar. The warning is especially serious for anyone who says, “God says,” about something God has not said. It calls for humility and care whenever God’s word is taught or applied.

Study Notes

The word for “liar” here comes from the same Hebrew root as “lies” in 30:8, where Agur asks God to keep lies far from him.

Proverbs 30:7–9 — Neither Poverty nor Riches

Proverbs 30:7
Proverbs 30:7
NKJV

Proverbs 30:7 Two things I request of You (Deprive me not before I die):(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:7 O God, I beg two favors from you;let me have them before I die. (NLT)

Main Idea

Turning now to prayer, Agur asks God for two things and pleads not to be refused before he dies. His humility leads him to ask, and he wants these requests answered for the whole of his life.

Verse Explanation

Turning now to prayer, Agur asks God for two things and pleads not to be refused before he dies. His humility leads him to ask, and he wants these requests answered for the whole of his life.

“Two things I request of You”

Agur turns from confession and declaration to prayer, speaking directly to God. His humility leads him to ask. He names a number before he names the requests, a habit that marks the rest of his chapter with its threes and fours. Prayer is the natural response of a man who knows his limits and trusts God’s word.

“(Deprive me not before I die):”

He pleads that God will not refuse him these two things for as long as he lives. The requests are not for a single moment of need but for the whole of his life. He wants to live and die with these gifts. The urgency shows how much Agur values what he is about to ask: a truthful heart and a life provided for in a way that keeps him faithful to God.

Study Notes

The KJV reads “deny me,” and the NASB reads “refuse me,” where the NKJV has “Deprive me not.”

Proverbs 30:8
Proverbs 30:8
NKJV

Proverbs 30:8 Remove falsehood and lies far from me; Give me neither poverty nor riches"” Feed me with the food allotted to me;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:8 First, help me never to tell a lie.Second, give me neither poverty nor riches!Give me just enough to satisfy my needs. (NLT)

Main Idea

He asks God to keep falsehood and lies far from him, and to give him neither poverty nor riches but only the food appointed for him each day. Agur wants a truthful life and enough to live on (compare Matthew 6:11).

Verse Explanation

He asks God to keep falsehood and lies far from him, and to give him neither poverty nor riches but only the food appointed for him each day. Agur wants a truthful life and enough to live on (compare Matthew 6:11).

“Remove falsehood and lies far from me;”

The first request is for truth. Falsehood here is emptiness, what is hollow and worthless, and lies are deceptive words. Agur asks God to keep both far from him, whether they come from others or from his own heart. Having just warned against adding lies to God’s words (30:6), he asks to be kept from lies himself. He knows he cannot guard his heart by his own strength, so he asks God to do it.

“Give me neither poverty nor riches—”

The second request is for a life between two extremes. Agur does not idealize poverty, and he does not seek wealth. He asks for what will keep him faithful to God, and 30:9 explains why both extremes are dangerous.

“Feed me with the food allotted to me;”

“Allotted” describes a set portion, the daily share appointed for him. Agur asks for enough: neither so little that he is driven to desperation nor so much that he forgets God. Jesus taught His disciples to pray in the same spirit, “Give us this day our daily bread” (Matthew 6:11). Paul later wrote, “having food and clothing, with these we shall be content” (1 Timothy 6:8). Contentment with God’s provision is a form of trust in Him.

Study Notes

The KJV reads “vanity” for “falsehood,” bringing out the sense of emptiness. For “the food allotted to me,” the NASB reads “my portion,” and the KJV margin gives “allowance.”

Main Idea

He explains the danger at each extreme: fullness could lead him to deny God and say, “Who is the LORD?” while poverty could tempt him to steal and dishonor the name of his God. Both plenty and want can pull a heart away from God, and Agur prays to stay faithful in between.

Verse Explanation

He explains the danger at each extreme: fullness could lead him to deny God and say, “Who is the LORD?” while poverty could tempt him to steal and dishonor the name of his God. Both plenty and want can pull a heart away from God, and Agur prays to stay faithful in between.

“Lest I be full and deny You, And say, ‘Who is the LORD?’”

Agur explains the danger of riches first. A full person can come to feel he needs no one, not even God. Comfort quietly becomes self-sufficiency, and self-sufficiency turns into denial. “Who is the LORD?” is the question of a heart that no longer feels its dependence. Pharaoh spoke the same words in open defiance: “Who is the LORD, that I should obey His voice” (Exodus 5:2). Moses warned Israel that when they had eaten and were full, their hearts could be lifted up and forget the LORD (Deuteronomy 8:12–14). Agur knows his own heart well enough to fear what plenty might do to it.

“Or lest I be poor and steal, And profane the name of my God.”

The opposite danger is poverty. Desperate need can tempt a person to steal, and a thief who belongs to God brings dishonor on God’s name. Agur is concerned for more than his own comfort. He cares how his life reflects on the God he serves. His prayer is that he will stay faithful in between, held where both his dependence on God and his honor of God’s name are kept safe. Both plenty and want can pull a heart away from God.

Study Notes

The KJV renders “profane” as taking the name of God “in vain,” and its margin gives “belie thee” for “deny You.”

Proverbs 30:10–14 — A Generation That Curses and Devours

Proverbs 30:10
Proverbs 30:10
NKJV

Proverbs 30:10 Do not malign a servant to his master, Lest he curse you, and you be found guilty.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:10 Never slander a worker to the employer,or the person will curse you, and you will pay for it. (NLT)

Main Idea

Do not slander a servant to his master, or the servant will curse you and you will be found guilty. Even the lowly have a just cause before God, and running them down brings guilt on the accuser.

Verse Explanation

Do not slander a servant to his master, or the servant will curse you and you will be found guilty. Even the lowly have a just cause before God, and running them down brings guilt on the accuser.

“Do not malign a servant to his master,”

To malign is to slander, to speak against someone with harmful words. A servant was in a weak position, easily accused and seldom able to defend himself before his master. Agur warns against using that weakness to bring him down with an accusation. The lowly are not fair game for careless or malicious words, and God sees how they are treated.

“Lest he curse you, and you be found guilty.”

The wronged servant may call down a curse on the accuser, and the slanderer will be found guilty. His guilt is real because his accusation was unjust, and the One who judges it is God, not the servant. Proverbs also says that “a curse without cause shall not alight” (26:2); a curse has no power of its own, and the outcome rests with the LORD. Even the lowliest person has a just cause before God. The saying stands at the head of the section that follows, where contempt and cruelty toward others, especially the weak, mark a corrupt generation.

Study Notes

The Hebrew verb behind “malign” means to use the tongue against someone, that is, to slander. The KJV margin gives this literal sense: Hurt not with thy tongue. The ESV reads “slander.”

Proverbs 30:11
Proverbs 30:11
NKJV

Proverbs 30:11 There is a generation that curses its father, And does not bless its mother.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:11 Some people curse their fatherand do not thank their mother. (NLT)

Main Idea

Agur describes a generation that curses its father and refuses to bless its mother. The first of four portraits of a corrupt generation begins with contempt for parents.

Verse Explanation

Agur describes a generation that curses its father and refuses to bless its mother. The first of four portraits of a corrupt generation begins with contempt for parents.

“There is a generation that curses its father,”

These words introduce the first of four portraits of a kind of people. Here the word generation means a class or type of person rather than everyone born in a given period. This kind of person curses its father, speaking against him with contempt and wishing him harm. God’s law treated such cursing as a grave offense (Exodus 21:17), and Proverbs warns that the one who curses his parents will have his lamp put out (20:20).

“And does not bless its mother.”

The second line describes neglect rather than open attack. This generation does not bless its mother, withholding the honor, gratitude, and good words she is owed. The command to “Honor your father and your mother” (Exodus 20:12) is broken both by cursing and by silent ingratitude. The portrait of a corrupt generation begins at home, with contempt for parents, and it will end in cruelty toward the poor (30:14).

Study Notes

The NASB renders “generation” as “a kind,” and the ESV as “those,” showing that the word describes a type of people rather than an age group or historical period. The same opening is repeated in 30:11–14.

Main Idea

Another mark of this generation is that it is pure in its own eyes yet has never been washed from its filth. Self-righteousness blinds people to the very uncleanness that most needs washing.

Verse Explanation

Another mark of this generation is that it is pure in its own eyes yet has never been washed from its filth. Self-righteousness blinds people to the very uncleanness that most needs washing.

“There is a generation that is pure in its own eyes,”

The second portrait is self-righteousness. This kind of person judges himself clean by his own standard and sees no need for washing. Proverbs warns, “All the ways of a man are pure in his own eyes, But the LORD weighs the spirits” (16:2). Self-assessment is easily fooled.

“Yet is not washed from its filthiness.”

The truth is the opposite of the self-image. This generation is still filthy, unwashed from its sin. The word filthiness is strong, describing real defilement. The tragedy is that the uncleanness that most needs washing is hidden from those who think themselves pure. Only someone who admits he is dirty seeks to be washed. The contrast with Agur is striking: he confessed his lack before God (30:2–3), while this generation boasts of a purity it does not have.

Study Notes

The ESV reads “clean” for “pure” and “filth” for “filthiness.”

“There is a generation—oh, how lofty are their eyes!”

The third portrait is pride. Agur breaks into an exclamation, “oh” (30:13), as if the sight of such arrogance startles him. Lofty eyes look down on everyone. A proud look is among the things the LORD hates (6:17), and pride shows itself first in how a person looks at others.

“And their eyelids are lifted up.”

The second line paints the same picture in detail: eyelids raised in disdain, the expression of someone who holds others in contempt. The portraits are building toward a climax. Contempt for parents (30:11) and confidence in one’s own purity (30:12) grow into an arrogance that looks down on the whole world, and that arrogance prepares the way for the cruelty of 30:14.

Proverbs 30:14
Proverbs 30:14
NKJV

Proverbs 30:14 There is a generation whose teeth are like swords, And whose fangs are like knives, To devour the poor from off the earth, And the needy from among men.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:14 They have teeth like swordsand fangs like knives.They devour the poor from the earthand the needy from among humanity. (NLT)

Main Idea

Worst of all, this generation has teeth like swords and fangs like knives, devouring the poor from the earth and the needy from among men. Contempt for parents, self-righteousness, and pride end in cruelty toward the weakest.

Verse Explanation

Worst of all, this generation has teeth like swords and fangs like knives, devouring the poor from the earth and the needy from among men. Contempt for parents, self-righteousness, and pride end in cruelty toward the weakest.

“There is a generation whose teeth are like swords, And whose fangs are like knives,”

The fourth portrait is the worst. Agur pictures people as predators with teeth like swords and fangs like knives. Their mouths are weapons. They tear at others with their words and their schemes as a wild animal tears its prey. The picture shows how far the earlier sins can go when they are not checked.

“To devour the poor from off the earth, And the needy from among men.”

Their victims are the poor and needy, those least able to defend themselves. This generation consumes them, taking their land, wages, and livelihood until they are wiped from the earth. Contempt for parents, self-righteousness, and pride end in cruelty toward the weakest. Proverbs repeatedly shows that the LORD defends the poor, for “the LORD is the maker of them all” (22:2). Those who devour the poor set themselves against God.

Study Notes

The NASB, KJV, and AMP read “jaw teeth” for “fangs.” The ESV omits “like,” giving a stronger metaphor in which the teeth are swords.

The four portraits of 30:11–14 each begin with “There is a generation,” and they move from the home (parents) to the self (purity, pride) to society (the poor).

Proverbs 30:15–17 — Things Never Satisfied

Proverbs 30:15
Proverbs 30:15
NKJV

Proverbs 30:15 The leech has two daughters" (H1323)” Give and Give! There are three things that are never satisfied, Four never say, "Enough!":(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 30:15 The leech has two suckersthat cry out, “More, more!” There are three things that are never satisfied —no, four that never say, “Enough!”: (NLT)

Main Idea

The leech has two daughters, “Give and Give!” a picture of appetite that only demands more. Agur then introduces a numerical saying: three things, yes four, are never satisfied and never say, “Enough!”

Verse Explanation

The leech has two daughters, “Give and Give!” a picture of appetite that only demands more. Agur then introduces a numerical saying: three things, yes four, are never satisfied and never say, “Enough!”

“The leech has two daughters— Give and Give!”

Agur begins a new kind of saying with a vivid picture. The leech clings and sucks blood. Its “two daughters” are the offspring that share its nature, and their names are their only words: “Give and Give!” The picture is appetite that only demands more and is never filled. It makes a fitting bridge from the devouring generation of 30:14, whose hunger for what belongs to others is never satisfied.

“There are three things that are never satisfied, Four never say, ‘Enough!’:”

Agur now introduces a numerical saying. The pattern of three things, then four, gathers several examples and lands with emphasis on the last. It is a teaching device, not a riddle about numbers; the same pattern appears in “These six things the LORD hates, Yes, seven” (6:16). Each of the four that follow shares one trait: it never says, “Enough!”

Study Notes

The Hebrew word for “leech” occurs only here in the Old Testament; it comes from a root meaning to suck. The KJV reads “horseleach,” and the NLT interprets the two daughters as the leech’s two “suckers.”

The numerical pattern of one number followed by the next (three, then four) recurs through the chapter (30:15, 18, 21, 29), as it does in 6:16.

The four are the way of an eagle in the air, a serpent on a rock, a ship in the midst of the sea, and a man with a virgin, each passing without leaving a trail, the last being the mystery of love between a man and a young woman. As a layered, spiritual reading, the four ways picture Christ’s ascension, the tempter finding no hold on Christ the Rock, the Church carried through the world by the Spirit, and the great mystery of Christ and His Bride (Ephesians 5:32).

“The way of an eagle in the air,”

The four “ways” share one trait in their plain sense: each moves in a way that is hard to explain and leaves no trail behind it. The eagle rides the wind high in the sky, rising and turning without visible effort, and the air holds no track of its flight.

“The way of a serpent on a rock,”

A snake has no legs, yet it moves smoothly over bare rock. On stone it leaves no mark, and no one can follow its path afterward.

“The way of a ship in the midst of the sea,”

A ship finds its way across the open sea with no road beneath it, and its wake closes behind it so that no trace of its route remains.

“And the way of a man with a virgin.”

The last way is the climax: the mystery of love between a man and a young woman. How attraction begins, how two lives are drawn together into one, is as wonderful and untraceable as the other three. Agur does not explain it; he marvels at it as part of God’s good design.

Beyond this plain meaning, the four ways can also be read at a deeper, spiritual level that Agur does not state. As a layered reading, the eagle pictures Christ’s ascension, when “He was taken up, and a cloud received Him out of their sight” (Acts 1:9), or the unseen movement of the Spirit (John 3:8). The serpent on the rock pictures the tempter finding no foothold on Christ, the Rock (1 Corinthians 10:4), for Jesus said, “the ruler of this world is coming, and he has nothing in Me” (John 14:30). The ship pictures the Church carried through the waters of this world by the wind of the Spirit. The man with a virgin, the summit of the four, pictures Christ and His Bride: “This is a great mystery, but I speak concerning Christ and the church” (Ephesians 5:32).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “virgin” describes a young woman of marriageable age. It is used of Rebekah, called “the virgin” in Genesis 24:43, of Miriam, called “the maiden” in Exodus 2:8, and in Isaiah 7:14. The ESV agrees with “virgin,” and the NASB and KJV read “maid.”

In Hebrew the ship is literally in the heart of the sea, as the KJV margin notes, meaning far out in open water.

Proverbs 30:33 As the beating of cream yields butterand striking the nose causes bleeding,so stirring up anger causes quarrels. (NLT)

Main Idea

As churning milk produces butter and wringing the nose produces blood, so forcing anger produces strife. Pressure applied always yields its result, and the chapter ends with a warning to stop pressing before wrath turns into open conflict.

Verse Explanation

As churning milk produces butter and wringing the nose produces blood, so forcing anger produces strife. Pressure applied always yields its result, and the chapter ends with a warning to stop pressing before wrath turns into open conflict.

“For as the churning of milk produces butter,”

Agur explains his warning with three pictures of pressure. When milk is churned or pressed, butter or curds come out. Pressing always produces its result.

“And wringing the nose produces blood,”

Squeeze or twist a nose hard enough, and blood flows. The same action produces a painful result.

“So the forcing of wrath produces strife.”

The third picture is the point. When anger is pressed and pushed, by stirring it up in oneself or provoking it in others, strife is the inevitable result. Pride and scheming, if they are not stopped (30:32), lead to angry words, and angry words pressed further lead to open conflict. The chapter ends with practical counsel: stop pressing before wrath becomes a quarrel. Agur’s words, which began with humility before God, end with humility before people.

Study Notes

The Hebrew uses the same noun three times, a word meaning squeezing or pressing. The NKJV varies it as “churning,” “wringing,” and “forcing,” while the ESV keeps “pressing” throughout. The repetition makes the point plain: the same kind of pressure that brings butter from milk and blood from a nose brings strife out of anger.

The verse also plays on words. The Hebrew word for “nose” is also the ordinary word for anger, which was pictured in flared nostrils and hard breathing, and “wrath” in the third line is a form of the same word. In Hebrew, pressing the nose and pressing anger sound almost alike.
