Verse-by-Verse
Proverbs 31:1
Proverbs 31:1
NKJV

Proverbs 31:1 The words of King Lemuel, the utterance which his mother taught him:(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:1 The sayings of King Lemuel contain this message, which his mother taught him. (NLT)

Main Idea

The book’s last collection is the teaching of King Lemuel, the utterance his mother taught him. Lemuel is otherwise unknown, and here a queen mother’s wisdom trains a king, just as a mother’s teaching was honored at the book’s beginning (1:8).

Verse Explanation

The book’s last collection is the teaching of King Lemuel, the utterance his mother taught him. Lemuel is otherwise unknown, and here a queen mother’s wisdom trains a king, just as a mother’s teaching was honored at the book’s beginning (1:8).

“The words of King Lemuel,”

The book’s final collection carries the name of King Lemuel. He is otherwise unknown; Scripture names him only in this chapter, and the text does not identify him with Solomon or any other known king of Israel or Judah. What matters is that a king is being taught, and the teaching is meant to govern how he uses his power.

“the utterance which his mother taught him:”

The words are called an “utterance,” a weighty and solemn saying, and they come from his mother. Proverbs opened with a father’s instruction and the charge, “do not forsake the law of your mother” (1:8). Now, at the end of the book, a mother’s voice is heard directly. A queen mother, who held real influence in ancient royal courts, trains her son for the throne. Lemuel passes on what she taught him, honoring her by preserving her words. Wisdom handed from parent to child, which has run through the whole book, reaches its last example here.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “utterance” is the same word used in Agur’s heading (30:1), often translated oracle or burden, a weighty message. The ESV and NASB read “oracle,” and the KJV “prophecy.” Some read it as the name of a people or region, Massa (Genesis 25:14), which would make Lemuel a king of Massa.

Proverbs 31:2

Proverbs 31:2 What, my son? And what, son of my womb? And what, son of my vows?(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:2 O my son, O son of my womb,O son of my vows, (NLT)

Main Idea

She opens with an urgent, tender cry: “What, my son? And what, son of my womb? And what, son of my vows?” The threefold address shows a mother pouring out her heart for the son she carried and prayed for with vows.

Verse Explanation

She opens with an urgent, tender cry: “What, my son? And what, son of my womb? And what, son of my vows?” The threefold address shows a mother pouring out her heart for the son she carried and prayed for with vows.

“What, my son? And what, son of my womb? And what, son of my vows?”

Lemuel’s mother opens with an urgent cry, repeating “what” three times as if she can hardly find the words. Her concern is sharp: what is her son doing, and what will he become? Each address draws closer to her heart. He is “my son,” her own child. He is “son of my womb,” the one she carried and bore. And he is “son of my vows,” a child she prayed for and dedicated to God with promises. Hannah offers a picture of such a mother, who “made a vow” (1 Samuel 1:11) and gave her son to the LORD. The warnings that follow are hard counsel, but they come from a mother’s love. She speaks plainly because she cares too much to stay silent.

Study Notes

The ESV expands each question to ask what the son is doing, while the NLT renders the verse as a series of tender addresses beginning “O my son.”

Proverbs 31:3
Proverbs 31:3
NKJV

Proverbs 31:3 Do not give your strength to women, Nor your ways to that which destroys kings.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:3 do not waste your strength on women,on those who ruin kings. (NLT)

Main Idea

Her first warning is not to spend his strength on women or give his ways to what destroys kings. Indulgence drains a ruler’s strength and turns his heart, a danger Solomon’s own many wives displayed (1 Kings 11:1–4).

Verse Explanation

Her first warning is not to spend his strength on women or give his ways to what destroys kings. Indulgence drains a ruler’s strength and turns his heart, a danger Solomon’s own many wives displayed (1 Kings 11:1–4).

“Do not give your strength to women,”

Her first warning concerns how the king spends himself. His “strength” is his vigor, his time, his resources, and his attention. A king who gives himself to many women, whether a large harem or a life of sexual indulgence, drains the strength that belongs to his people and his calling. Israel’s law warned that a king must not “multiply wives for himself, lest his heart turn away” (Deuteronomy 17:17). The warning concerns indulgence and the misuse of power; it is not a slight against women, and the chapter will end by praising a woman of strength.

“Nor your ways to that which destroys kings.”

Indulgence does not stay private. It turns a ruler’s heart and ruins his reign. Solomon’s own history displayed the danger: “his wives turned away his heart” (1 Kings 11:3). The mother wants her son to keep his ways for the purposes God gave him, not hand them over to what has brought down kings before him.

Study Notes

The ESV takes the danger as people rather than a thing, reading “those” who destroy kings.

Proverbs 31:4
Proverbs 31:4
NKJV

Proverbs 31:4 It is not for kings, O Lemuel, It is not for kings to drink wine, Nor for princes intoxicating drink;(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:4 It is not for kings, O Lemuel, to guzzle wine.Rulers should not crave alcohol. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wine and strong drink are not for kings and princes, she tells Lemuel by name. A ruler’s clear mind belongs to the people he has been given to serve.

Verse Explanation

Wine and strong drink are not for kings and princes, she tells Lemuel by name. A ruler’s clear mind belongs to the people he has been given to serve.

“It is not for kings, O Lemuel,”

The mother calls her son by name, and the repetition “It is not for kings” gives the warning weight. What may be permitted to others is not fitting for a man who holds the power of judgment over a nation.

“It is not for kings to drink wine, Nor for princes intoxicating drink;”

Wine and strong drink cloud the mind and loosen restraint. Proverbs has already warned that “Wine is a mocker, Strong drink is a brawler” (20:1). For a ruler the danger is multiplied, because his decisions fall on others. Isaiah pronounced woe on “men mighty at drinking wine” who “justify the wicked for a bribe” (Isaiah 5:22–23). A king’s clear mind is not his own to spend; it belongs to the people he has been given to serve. The warning focuses on rulers and their duty, and the next verse states the reason.

Study Notes

The ESV, NASB, and KJV read “strong drink” for “intoxicating drink,” and the NASB speaks of rulers who “desire” it.

Proverbs 31:5
Proverbs 31:5
NKJV

Proverbs 31:5 Lest they drink and forget the law, And pervert the justice of all the afflicted.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:5 For if they drink, they may forget the lawand not give justice to the oppressed. (NLT)

Main Idea

Drink could make a king forget the law and twist justice for all the afflicted. The cost of a clouded judgment falls on the weakest people in the land.

Verse Explanation

Drink could make a king forget the law and twist justice for all the afflicted. The cost of a clouded judgment falls on the weakest people in the land.

“Lest they drink and forget the law,”

Drink makes a king forget what he must never forget, the law he is charged to uphold. A ruler’s first duty is to remember what is right and to judge by it. Wine dulls the memory and the conscience at the very point where the people need them most.

“And pervert the justice of all the afflicted.”

The cost of a clouded mind falls on the weakest. The “afflicted” are the poor, the oppressed, and those without power to defend themselves, and their only hope is a judge who will see clearly and rule fairly. A drunken king perverts their justice, twisting their cases or neglecting them altogether. Every one of them is affected, for the verse speaks of “all the afflicted.” Lemuel’s mother is teaching that the purpose of royal sobriety is justice for those who cannot protect themselves.

Study Notes

The Hebrew word behind “the law” means what has been decreed, and the ESV and NASB render it that way; they also read “rights” for “justice.” The KJV margin gives the literal Hebrew phrase behind “all the afflicted”: all the sons of affliction.

Proverbs 31:6
Proverbs 31:6
NKJV

Proverbs 31:6 Give strong drink to him who is perishing, And wine to those who are bitter of heart.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:6 Alcohol is for the dying,and wine for those in bitter distress. (NLT)

Main Idea

Strong drink is to be given to the one who is perishing, and wine to those whose hearts are bitter. Over against the king who must stay clear-headed, she most likely points to the dying and the deeply afflicted, for whom drink could bring a measure of relief; it is no general prescription for drinking away poverty or distress.

Verse Explanation

Strong drink is to be given to the one who is perishing, and wine to those whose hearts are bitter. Over against the king who must stay clear-headed, she most likely points to the dying and the deeply afflicted, for whom drink could bring a measure of relief; it is no general prescription for drinking away poverty or distress.

“Give strong drink to him who is perishing,”

Over against the king who must stay clear-headed, his mother names those for whom drink may serve a different purpose. The one “who is perishing” is most likely someone dying or in the last stages of suffering. For such a person, strong drink could bring a measure of relief from pain. The contrast is with the ruler: the king must keep his mind clear for others, while the dying man has no more duties of judgment to perform.

“And wine to those who are bitter of heart.”

The parallel line widens the circle to those whose hearts are bitter, people crushed by grief or deep affliction. The verse is not a general prescription for drinking away poverty or distress, and it does not overturn the book’s many warnings against drunkenness (23:29–35). It most likely describes compassion toward the dying and the deeply afflicted, set against the ruler’s calling to sobriety. Some have read 31:6–7 as ironic, but the plain contrast with 31:4–5 is the more natural reading.

Study Notes

The KJV describes him as “ready” to perish. Behind “bitter of heart” is a Hebrew phrase meaning literally bitter of soul, which the KJV gives in its margin. The ESV and NLT read “those in bitter distress.”

The Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 43a) cites this verse for a later Jewish custom: a man being led out to execution was given wine mixed with frankincense to dull his senses. That later practice does not settle the verse’s original setting. Mark records that Jesus was offered “wine mingled with myrrh to drink, but He did not take it” (Mark 15:23); the Gospel does not explain that drink by this custom.

Proverbs 31:7

Proverbs 31:7 Let him drink and forget his poverty, And remember his misery no more.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:7 Let them drink to forget their povertyand remember their troubles no more. (NLT)

Main Idea

Let such a one drink and forget his poverty and remember his misery no more. The contrast with verses 4–5 is deliberate: the king must remember the law and do justice, while the dying sufferer may be allowed for a while to forget his pain.

Verse Explanation

Let such a one drink and forget his poverty and remember his misery no more. The contrast with verses 4–5 is deliberate: the king must remember the law and do justice, while the dying sufferer may be allowed for a while to forget his pain.

“Let him drink and forget his poverty,”

The suffering man may drink and forget, for a while, what weighs on him. The word “forget” links this verse to 31:5. The king must not drink, because he would forget the law; the sufferer may drink, so that he can forget his misery. The contrast is deliberate, and it keeps the focus on the king’s responsibility.

“And remember his misery no more.”

For the man at the end of his strength, relief from remembering his misery is a mercy. The verse describes temporary relief, not a cure, and it gives no endorsement to escaping life’s troubles through drink. The deeper answer for the poor and afflicted comes in the next two verses, where the king himself is charged to speak for them and defend their cause. The ruler who keeps his mind clear can do for the poor what wine never can.

Study Notes

The NASB reads “trouble” for “misery.” The ESV and NLT render the verse in the plural, applying it to all the afflicted of 31:6.

Proverbs 31:8–9 — Open Your Mouth for the Speechless

Proverbs 31:8
Proverbs 31:8
NKJV

Proverbs 31:8 Open your mouth for the speechless, In the cause of all who are appointed to die.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:8 Speak up for those who cannot speak for themselves;ensure justice for those being crushed. (NLT)

Main Idea

Lemuel is to open his mouth for those who cannot speak for themselves, taking up the cause of all who are appointed to die. Turning from warnings to a positive charge, his mother shows what royal power is for: speaking up for the defenseless.

Verse Explanation

Lemuel is to open his mouth for those who cannot speak for themselves, taking up the cause of all who are appointed to die. Turning from warnings to a positive charge, his mother shows what royal power is for: speaking up for the defenseless.

“Open your mouth for the speechless,”

The mother turns from warnings to a positive charge. Royal power is for speaking. The speechless are those who cannot speak for themselves, whether because they are powerless, poor, ignored, or silenced. The king is to be their voice in the place of judgment. A ruler who keeps his mind clear (31:4–5) is able to use his mouth for the people who have no voice.

“In the cause of all who are appointed to die.”

The king is to take up the legal cause of those marked for destruction, people facing death through injustice, oppression, or abandonment. They have no one else to plead for them. The charge applies to everyone who holds influence over others, and it should not be assigned to a single modern cause. Wherever people are about to be crushed without anyone to speak for them, those with power and a voice are called to open their mouths.

Study Notes

The ESV and NASB read “mute” for “speechless.” Behind “appointed to die” is a Hebrew phrase meaning literally the sons of destruction, which the KJV gives in its margin, while the ESV reads “destitute,” taking the phrase to mean those who are perishing through want.

Proverbs 31:9 Yes, speak up for the poor and helpless,and see that they get justice. (NLT)

Main Idea

She repeats the charge: open your mouth, judge righteously, and plead the cause of the poor and needy. A king’s voice in judgment exists to defend the weak, and her counsel ends where the book has often pointed, at justice for the poor.

Verse Explanation

She repeats the charge: open your mouth, judge righteously, and plead the cause of the poor and needy. A king’s voice in judgment exists to defend the weak, and her counsel ends where the book has often pointed, at justice for the poor.

“Open your mouth, judge righteously,”

The mother repeats the charge, “Open your mouth,” and adds what kind of speech she means. The king must judge righteously, deciding cases by what is right and not by bribes, favoritism, or pressure. His voice carries the authority of judgment, and it must be used justly.

“And plead the cause of the poor and needy.”

To plead a cause is to argue a case as an advocate. The king is not only a neutral judge; he is to take the side of the poor and needy, making sure their rights are heard and protected. Lemuel’s mother ends where the book has often pointed: the measure of a ruler is justice for the weakest. “The king who judges the poor with truth, His throne will be established forever” (29:14). Her counsel closes the words of Lemuel with a call to use power for others.

Study Notes

The ESV and NASB read “defend” and “rights” for “plead the cause,” bringing out the legal setting of the charge.

Proverbs 31:10–12 — Who Can Find a Virtuous Wife?

Proverbs 31:10
Proverbs 31:10
NKJV

Proverbs 31:10 Who can find a virtuous wife? (H802) For her worth is far above rubies.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:10 Who can find a virtuous and capable wife?She is more precious than rubies. (NLT)

Main Idea

The closing poem, an acrostic whose verses follow the Hebrew alphabet, opens by asking who can find a virtuous wife, a woman of strength and worth whose value is far above rubies. She is first a real woman of wisdom, and at the deeper, layered level she pictures the Church, the Bride of Christ, precious because He purchased her with His own blood (Acts 20:28).

Verse Explanation

The closing poem, an acrostic whose verses follow the Hebrew alphabet, opens by asking who can find a virtuous wife, a woman of strength and worth whose value is far above rubies. She is first a real woman of wisdom, and at the deeper, layered level she pictures the Church, the Bride of Christ, precious because He purchased her with His own blood (Acts 20:28).

“Who can find a virtuous wife?”

The book’s closing poem begins with a question. A “virtuous wife” is a woman of strength, valor, and worth. The Hebrew word behind “virtuous” describes capability and character together, the same word Boaz used when he called Ruth “a virtuous woman” (Ruth 3:11). The question “Who can find” does not mean such women do not exist; it means they are rare and precious, worth searching for. The young man of the book’s opening chapters was urged to find Wisdom, for “whoever finds me finds life” (8:35), and was told that “He who finds a wife finds a good thing” (18:22). Now the book shows him the woman worth finding.

“For her worth is far above rubies.”

Her value exceeds the most costly jewels. Proverbs used the same comparison of wisdom itself: “She is more precious than rubies” (3:15). The woman of this poem is wisdom lived out in a real life. She is first a real woman, strong, trusted, and wise, and the poem praises her as such. It is not a checklist laid on every woman, and its praise, grounded in the fear of the LORD (31:30), belongs equally to single women and widows who fear Him.

At a deeper, layered level, the poem also portrays the Church as the Bride of Christ. Her worth far above rubies pictures the Church as precious to God, “the church of God which He purchased with His own blood” (Acts 20:28). Christ seeks and finds His Bride, and He presents her to Himself as “a glorious church” (Ephesians 5:27).

Study Notes

The poem of 31:10–31 is an acrostic: its twenty-two verses begin with the twenty-two letters of the Hebrew alphabet in order, from the first letter in 31:10 to the last in 31:31.

The Hebrew phrase translated “virtuous wife” also appears in 12:4, where the NKJV renders it “An excellent wife,” and in Ruth 3:11. The Hebrew word for “virtuous” can describe strength, ability, wealth, or valor. It is the same word as “strength” in 31:3, where the king is warned not to give his strength to women, and it returns in 31:29, “Many daughters have done well.”

The ESV and NASB read “An excellent wife,” and they read “jewels” for “rubies.”

Whether 31:10–31 continues the words of Lemuel’s mother or stands as a separate poem is debated. The text gives the poem no heading of its own.

Proverbs 31:11
Proverbs 31:11
NKJV

Proverbs 31:11 The heart of her husband safely trusts her; So he will have no lack of gain.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:11 Her husband can trust her,and she will greatly enrich his life. (NLT)

Main Idea

Her husband trusts her with his whole heart, and through her he lacks nothing of value. Her faithfulness frees him from worry about his household; in the layered reading, Christ entrusts His Bride with the gospel and the care of His household on earth.

Verse Explanation

Her husband trusts her with his whole heart, and through her he lacks nothing of value. Her faithfulness frees him from worry about his household; in the layered reading, Christ entrusts His Bride with the gospel and the care of His household on earth.

“The heart of her husband safely trusts her;”

Her husband’s trust is complete. “Heart” means his inner self, and he rests on her with full confidence. He does not need to check up on her, worry about the household, or fear what she does when he is away. Her faithfulness frees him. Trust like this grows from a long record of reliability, which 31:12 goes on to describe.

“So he will have no lack of gain.”

Because of her, he lacks nothing of value. Her diligence, wisdom, and good management bring real increase to the household, and the following verses show how. The gain is material, but it is also the richness of a life shared with a trustworthy wife.

In the layered reading of the poem, the trusting husband pictures Christ entrusting His Bride with the gospel, the care of souls, and the management of His estate on earth, as this woman manages her husband’s house and fields (31:16).

Study Notes

The Hebrew word translated “gain” usually means spoil or plunder taken in war, as the KJV’s “spoil” shows. The image fits a poem about a woman of strength and valor, though here the word describes the household’s gain rather than literal plunder.

Verse Explanation

She does him good and never harm, every day of her life. Her goodness is steady and lifelong, which is why the trust of verse 11 is well placed.

“She does him good and not evil”

Her treatment of her husband is consistently good. The verse states it both positively and negatively: she brings him good, and she never brings him harm. She is not a source of trouble, shame, or strife in his life, unlike the contentious wife Proverbs warns about elsewhere. Her goodness is active, seeking his welfare in practical ways.

“All the days of her life.”

Her goodness is not seasonal or dependent on mood. It lasts every day of her life. This steady faithfulness, day after day and year after year, is why the trust of 31:11 is well placed. The poem praises a character proven over time, not a single act. In the book’s portrait of wisdom, lasting faithfulness is worth more than any display.

Study Notes

The ESV and NLT read “harm” for “evil,” making clear that the line concerns the effect of her conduct on her husband.

Proverbs 31:13–27 — Her Strength, Diligence, and Kindness

Proverbs 31:13
Proverbs 31:13
NKJV

Proverbs 31:13 She seeks wool and flax, And willingly works with her hands.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:13 She finds wool and flaxand busily spins it. (NLT)

Main Idea

Wool and flax are what she seeks out, and she works them with willing hands. Her industry is glad and freely given, and the layered reading sees in wool and linen the outward deeds of mercy and the inward purity of heart woven together in the Christian life.

Verse Explanation

Wool and flax are what she seeks out, and she works them with willing hands. Her industry is glad and freely given, and the layered reading sees in wool and linen the outward deeds of mercy and the inward purity of heart woven together in the Christian life.

“She seeks wool and flax,”

The poem now turns to her works. Wool from sheep and flax for linen were the raw materials for clothing in ancient Israel. She does not wait for them to arrive; she seeks them out, choosing good materials for the work ahead.

“And willingly works with her hands.”

Her work is glad and freely given. “Willingly” describes hands that work with delight, not under compulsion. Her industry flows from a willing heart. This is the opposite of the lazy man of the book’s earlier portraits, who craves but will not work.

As a layered reading, Augustine saw in the wool and the linen two sides of the Christian life woven together: wool, an outer garment, picturing outward deeds of mercy, and linen picturing inward purity of heart and faith. The Bride’s work joins what is seen and what is hidden into one garment.

Study Notes

In Hebrew she works with the delight of her palms, that is, with hands that are glad to work. The ESV speaks of “willing hands,” and the NASB reads “in delight.”

Proverbs 31:15
Proverbs 31:15
NKJV

Proverbs 31:15 She also rises while it is yet night, And provides food for her household, And a portion for her maidservants.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:15 She gets up before dawn to prepare breakfast for her householdand plan the day’s work for her servant girls. (NLT)

Main Idea

Before daylight she rises to provide food for her household and portions for her maidservants. Her care begins before the day does and reaches everyone under her roof, including those who serve.

Verse Explanation

Before daylight she rises to provide food for her household and portions for her maidservants. Her care begins before the day does and reaches everyone under her roof, including those who serve.

“She also rises while it is yet night,”

She rises before daylight. In a household that baked its own bread and prepared every meal from raw materials, the day’s work began early. Her care for her household begins before the day does. The poem presents this as a picture of diligence, not a rule that every woman must rise before dawn.

“And provides food for her household,”

Her first concern is that those under her roof are fed. She makes sure the household has what it needs for the day.

“And a portion for her maidservants.”

Her care reaches those who serve her. The maidservants receive their portion, either their food or their assigned tasks for the day. No one in her house is overlooked, including those of lower station.

As a layered reading, her rising while it is still night pictures the Bride’s watchfulness through the darkness of this age, ready for the Bridegroom, as in the parable where “at midnight a cry was heard: ‘Behold, the bridegroom is coming’” (Matthew 25:6). The picture is illustrative and does not set out an end-time timetable.

Study Notes

The NLT interprets the last line as planning the day’s work for her servant girls, taking “portion” as assigned tasks; the ESV and NASB read “portions” for her maidens.

Proverbs 31:16
NKJV

Proverbs 31:16 She goes to inspect a field and buys it;with her earnings she plants a vineyard. (NLT)

Main Idea

Considering a field carefully, she buys it, then plants a vineyard from what she has earned. Her wisdom extends to business, as she weighs a decision carefully and invests her profits in something that will keep producing.

Verse Explanation

Considering a field carefully, she buys it, then plants a vineyard from what she has earned. Her wisdom extends to business, as she weighs a decision carefully and invests her profits in something that will keep producing.

“She considers a field and buys it;”

Her wisdom reaches into business. She does not buy on impulse. She considers the field, weighing its value and its usefulness, and then acts decisively. Careful thought followed by action is a mark of wisdom throughout Proverbs. The verse shows a woman trusted to make significant decisions about property and the household’s future.

“From her profits she plants a vineyard.”

She invests what she has earned in something that will keep producing. A vineyard takes years before it bears fully, so planting one is an act of foresight and patience. Her profits do not go to waste or to show; they go into the household’s long-term good.

Study Notes

The Hebrew reads from the fruit of her palms, which the ESV and KJV render “the fruit of her hands,” tying her purchase to her own labor. For “buys,” the KJV margin notes that the Hebrew verb is literally taketh.

Proverbs 31:17
Proverbs 31:17
NKJV

Proverbs 31:17 She girds herself with strength, And strengthens her arms.(NKJV)

NLT

Proverbs 31:17 She is energetic and strong,a hard worker. (NLT)

Main Idea

Girding herself with strength, she makes her arms strong. The picture of a woman tying up her garments for hard work shows her meeting her tasks with vigor and readiness.

Verse Explanation

Girding herself with strength, she makes her arms strong. The picture of a woman tying up her garments for hard work shows her meeting her tasks with vigor and readiness.

“She girds herself with strength,”

To gird oneself was to tie up long garments with a belt so they would not hinder vigorous work or movement. She prepares for her tasks the way a worker or a soldier prepares for his. The word “strength” fits the poem’s description of her as a woman of valor. Her strength is physical and practical, and it is also the readiness of a person who meets her duties without hesitation.
