Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Chapter 31
Main Idea:

Proverbs 31 ends the book with wisdom on a throne and in a household: a mother teaches King Lemuel that his strength and clear mind belong to the defenseless, and an alphabet poem praises a woman of valor who fears the LORD.

Setting:

Lemuel is otherwise unknown, and the text does not identify him with Solomon. The closing poem, which may or may not continue his mother’s words, follows her counsel to the king (31:1–9) and stands in contrast to the adulterous woman of the previous chapter (30:20).

Chapter Outline:

  • Proverbs 31:1–7 — A Mother’s Counsel to a King

  • Proverbs 31:8–9 — Open Your Mouth for the Speechless

  • Proverbs 31:10–12 — Who Can Find a Virtuous Wife?

  • Proverbs 31:13–27 — Her Strength, Diligence, and Kindness

Key Verse:

“Charm is deceitful and beauty is passing, But a woman who fears the LORD, she shall be praised.” (Proverbs 31:30)

Go Deeper

The Heart of the Chapter

Proverbs began with a father and mother teaching a son (1:8), and it ends with a mother’s voice. Lemuel’s mother cries, “What, my son? And what, son of my womb? And what, son of my vows?” (31:2), and her love speaks as hard counsel. A king’s strength and clear mind are not his to waste, because wine can make him “forget the law” (31:5), and his mouth is to be opened “for the speechless” (31:8).

Then, after the mother’s counsel to her son, comes the woman of valor, a contrast with the adulterous woman of the previous chapter who wipes her mouth and denies any wrong (30:20). Her alphabet poem gives a complete picture of wisdom lived: strong, trusted, skilled in trade, generous to the poor, and kind in speech. She is a real woman first, praised by a husband who knows her (31:29). At a deeper, layered level, she is the bride worth finding. “Who can find a virtuous wife?” (31:10) recalls Wisdom’s promise, “whoever finds me finds life” (8:35), and she pictures the Church that Christ presents to Himself as “a glorious church” (Ephesians 5:27).

The poem’s last word explains all the rest: “a woman who fears the LORD, she shall be praised” (31:30). The book that called the fear of the LORD the beginning of knowledge (1:7) closes with a life built on that right view of God.

Chapter Sections

Proverbs 31:1–7 — A Mother’s Counsel to a King

Lemuel’s mother, who calls him “son of my vows” (31:2), warns him not to spend his strength on women or his judgment on wine, because a king who drinks forgets the law and the afflicted pay the price. Strong drink belongs instead to the dying and the bitter of heart, most likely as relief in their suffering, and the verses give no general approval of drinking away trouble.

Proverbs 31:8–9 — Open Your Mouth for the Speechless

Her warnings give way to a charge: the mouth a king keeps clear of wine is to be opened for people who have no voice, even “all who are appointed to die” (31:8). In her teaching, a ruler’s authority is held for the sake of the poor and needy who cannot defend themselves.

Proverbs 31:10–12 — Who Can Find a Virtuous Wife?

The poem opens by asking where such a woman can be found and answers with her worth, far above rubies, and with a husband whose trust in her is never disappointed, because she does him good all her days. Read at a deeper, layered level, she pictures the Church, precious because Christ purchased her “with His own blood” (Acts 20:28).

Proverbs 31:13–27 — Her Strength, Diligence, and Kindness

Fifteen verses show her at work: spinning wool and flax, rising before daylight to feed her household, buying a field and planting a vineyard, selling linen garments, opening her hands to the needy, and speaking with “the law of kindness” on her tongue (31:26). The layered reading sees the watchful Bride ready for the Bridegroom in her lamp that “does not go out by night” (31:18; Matthew 25:6), a household covered by the blood of Christ in the scarlet of 31:21, the Bride’s fine linen (Revelation 19:7–8) in her clothing (31:22), and Christ seated at the Father’s right hand in the husband who “is known in the gates” (31:23).

Proverbs 31:28–31 — A Woman Who Fears the LORD Shall Be Praised

Her children and her husband praise her first, and he tells her that many daughters have done well but she excels them all. The last verses name the ground of that praise, the fear of the LORD rather than charm or beauty, and send her own works to praise her in the gates, the public place where Wisdom first cried out (1:21).

Why This Chapter Matters

Proverbs 31 shows what strength and plenty are for. Lemuel’s mother teaches that authority is held for those who cannot speak for themselves, and the woman of valor turns the profit of her hands into food for her household and open hands for the poor (31:20). In this chapter wisdom always reaches past the self.

The chapter also guards how its poem is read. Her praise rests on the fear of the LORD, not on how much she accomplishes, so her portrait is never a checklist to burden women, and the same honor belongs to every woman who fears Him, whether single, widowed, or married (31:30). Because she also pictures the Bride, her burning lamp and her household clothed in scarlet describe a Church that keeps watch for her Bridegroom, covered by His blood.

Key Themes to Watch For

Strength rightly used. Lemuel must not give his strength to women, while the woman of valor girds herself with strength and wears strength and honor as clothing (31:3, 17, 25).

Opened mouths. The king opens his mouth for the speechless and the poor, and the woman opens hers with wisdom and kindness (31:8–9, 26).

Willing hands. Her hands work wool and flax and hold the spindle, and the same hands reach out to the needy, until the fruit of her hands is given back to her as praise (31:13, 19–20, 31).

Honor in the gates. Her husband is known among the elders in the gates, and in the end her own works praise her there (31:23, 31).

The fear of the LORD. Charm and beauty pass, but the woman who fears the LORD is praised, and the book closes on the truth with which it opened (31:30; 1:7).

Key Words & Concepts

King Lemuel (31:1). A king known only from this chapter and not identified in the text with Solomon. His words are an “utterance” (31:1) his mother taught him.

Son of my vows (31:2). The third of the mother’s tender addresses, describing a son she carried and prayed for with vows. Her warnings come from that love.

Virtuous wife (31:10). The Hebrew word behind “virtuous” (chayil) means strength, valor, and worth, which is why she is often called the woman of valor. The same word returns in “Many daughters have done well” (31:29) and describes Ruth as “a virtuous woman” (Ruth 3:11).

The alphabet poem (31:10–31). In Hebrew the twenty-two verses form an acrostic, each beginning with the next letter of the alphabet. The form presents her portrait as complete, from the first letter to the last.

Scarlet (31:21). Rich, warm cloth that lets her face winter without fear for her household. At the layered level, the scarlet pictures her household covered by the blood of Christ.

The fear of the LORD (31:30). A right view of God: terror for those who defy Him and awe for all at His glory, majesty, and holiness, for He is unlike any other. It is the beginning of knowledge (1:7) and the true source of this woman’s worth.
