SINNERS - ( chatta', Strong's H2400 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chattâ' refers to those who habitually miss the mark of God's standard of righteousness, choosing instead to wander from the path of His law. In Psalm 1:1, "sinners" represents those whose lives are characterized by active, ongoing rebellion and deviation from God’s design. Unlike the godly man who delights in the Torah, these individuals establish a settled lifestyle of disobedience, making their path an unsafe road for the righteous to walk or linger upon.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of chattâ' is chata', which originally carries the concrete, physical meaning of "missing a mark" or "stumbling." An archer who shoots an arrow and misses the target, or a traveler who takes a wrong turn and misses the destination, demonstrates the root concept of this word. In the moral and spiritual realm of the Old Testament, the "target" or "mark" is God's perfect law and holy character. To sin is to miss this divine target. Chattâ' represents a nominal form of this root, emphasizing a person whose character is defined by this habitual missing of the mark—not an occasional slip, but a settled status of being a "miss-marker."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun chattâ' refers to a habitual sinner, one who is intensively or characteristically devoted to sin. It denotes a person characterized by ongoing, active transgression against God, rather than someone who merely commits an isolated error. The term describes those who are openly rebellious, actively choosing a lifestyle of disobedience and moral failure. In the semantic range of the Old Testament, it contrasts sharply with the righteous (tsaddiq) and is often paired with the wicked (rasha').

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Archer's Miss: The underlying root is used in Judges 20:16 to describe Benjamite slingers who could sling a stone at a hair and "not miss" (chata'). In a spiritual sense, the chattâ' is one whose entire life direction misses the moral target set by God.

• The Wayward Traveler: The word pictures a traveler who deliberately leaves the established highway to wander in dangerous, unauthorized terrain.

• The Settled Stance: In Hebrew poetry, the progression from "walking" to "standing" to "sitting" shows a deepening involvement. To "stand" in the path of sinners is to halt one's progress and linger in their way of life, adopting their habits and worldview.

4. Theological Implications

Sin is fundamentally relational and theological, not merely ethical. A chattâ' is not just someone who violates an abstract code of conduct; they violate their relationship with the Covenant God. The existence of "sinners" highlights the absolute holiness of God, who establishes the standard, and the moral agency of humanity, which is accountable to that standard. Because the chattâ' lives in habitual opposition to God’s design, they stand under His righteous judgment and are ultimately excluded from the assembly of the righteous.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 1:1, chatta'im (the plural form) represents the second stage of spiritual degeneration: walking in the counsel of the ungodly (rasha'), then standing in the path of sinners (chatta'im), and finally sitting in the seat of the scornful (letsim). Here, "sinners" represents a more defined, active, and public lifestyle of sin than the "ungodly." While the "ungodly" refers to a general godlessness of mind and attitude, the "sinners" actively act out their rebellion in a distinct "path" or lifestyle. To "stand" in their path means to conform to their pattern of behavior, making oneself comfortable in their company and adopting their moral compromises.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 13:13: "But the men of Sodom were exceedingly wicked and sinners against the LORD."

• Psalm 25:8: "Good and upright is the LORD; Therefore He teaches sinners in the way."

• Psalm 104:35: "May sinners be consumed from the earth, And the wicked be no more. Bless the LORD, O my soul! Praise the LORD!"

• Proverbs 1:10: "My son, if sinners entice you, Do not consent."

• Isaiah 33:14: "The sinners in Zion are afraid; Fearfulness has seized the hypocrites: 'Who among us shall dwell with the devouring fire? Who among us shall dwell with everlasting burnings?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) translates chatta'im in Psalm 1:1 using the Greek word hamartolon (from hamartolos), which carries the same primary meaning of "one who misses the mark" or "sinner." This connects the Old Testament concept directly to the New Testament's frequent use of hamartolos to describe those who are estranged from God, whom Jesus famously came to call to repentance (Luke 5:32).
