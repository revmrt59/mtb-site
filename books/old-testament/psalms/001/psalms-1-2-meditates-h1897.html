MEDITATES - ( hagah , Strong's H1897 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word hagah describes a low, continuous vocalization—such as murmuring, sighing, whispering, or even the low growl of a lion over its prey. Unlike the modern Western concept of meditation, which often implies emptying the mind in silent contemplation, biblical meditation involves filling the mind with God's Truth and actively speaking, muttering, or rehearsing it aloud. In Psalm 1:2, it depicts a believer who is deeply satisfied by God's Word, constantly and audibly rehearsing Scripture to internalize its life-giving truth day and night.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israel, reading was almost exclusively done aloud. When a person read the Scriptures, they did not merely scan the words with their eyes; they pronounced them with their mouth. The word hagah captures this physical, vocal dimension of study.

• The word evokes the sound of a lion growling over its prey. The growl of a lion is a sound of fierce possession, deep satisfaction, and undivided focus. The lion is not merely eating; it is thoroughly enjoying, digesting, and protecting its food.

• It is also used to describe the cooing or moaning of a dove, representing a persistent, low, repetitive sound that reflects the deep, internal state of the heart.

• Therefore, the word picture behind hagah is that of a person so consumed by God's Word that they constantly chew on it, mutter it to themselves, and find their ultimate satisfaction in its taste.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew verb hagah has a vibrant semantic range that bridges physical sound and mental activity.

• The primary meanings include to mutter, murmur, growl, utter, speak, sigh, or moan.

• The secondary meanings include to meditate, muse, ponder, devise, plan, or plot.

• The transition from physical vocalization to mental processing is natural: to muse or ponder in the ancient world was to speak the words quietly to oneself. It represents the active process of talking oneself through the truth of God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs hagah in several vivid contexts that clarify its spiritual application:

• The Lion’s Focus: A young lion growls (hagah) over its prey, refusing to be distracted or driven away by shepherds. This illustrates a believer who fiercely grips the Word of God, refusing to let the distractions of the world pull them away from their spiritual nourishment.

• The Dove’s Groan: A dove moans (hagah) in distress or longing. This reflects a deep, earnest expression of the soul, where one pours out their heart in prayerful meditation upon God during times of trial.

• The Plotter's Scheme: The wicked plot (hagah) a vain thing against the Lord. This shows the misdirection of meditation; it is the focused, deliberate planning of the mind toward a specific goal. For the believer, this intense focus is redirected toward God's instruction.

4. Theological Implications

The biblical concept of meditation stands in stark contrast to eastern forms of meditation.

• Eastern meditation seeks to empty the mind, detach from reality, and look inward.

• Biblical meditation seeks to fill the mind, attach to objective divine revelation, and look outward to God.

• Biblical meditation is the bridge between hearing the Word of God and obeying it. It is the process of spiritual digestion. Without hagah, Scripture remains external information; through hagah, it becomes internal transformation. It ensures that the Word moves from the head, through the mouth, and into the heart, shaping the believer's character and actions.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 1:2, hagah is positioned as the defining habit of the blessed man.

• Contrast with the Wicked: The psalm begins by warning against listening to the counsel of the wicked, standing in the path of sinners, and sitting in the seat of the scornful. The blessed man avoids these negative influences by filling his mind with a superior voice. He tunes out the noise of the world by murmuring the truth of God.

• The Source of Meditation: The object of this meditation is the "law of the LORD" (the Torah or instruction of Yahweh). The blessed man does not meditate on abstract concepts but on the concrete, revealed will of God.

• The Frequency of Meditation: He meditates "day and night." This is not a formal quiet time restricted to a few minutes in the morning. It is an ongoing, habitual state of mind. Throughout the busy hours of the day and during the quiet watches of the night, he is quietly speaking God's promises and commands to himself, applying them to every situation he encounters.

• The Motive of Meditation: This meditation is fueled by "delight." It is not a burdensome, legalistic duty, but the joyful response of a soul that has found life, sweetness, and guidance in the words of its Creator.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Joshua 1:8 - "This Book of the Law shall not depart from your mouth, but you shall meditate in it day and night, that you may observe to do according to all that is written in it."

• Psalm 63:6 - "When I remember You on my bed, I meditate on You in the night watches."

• Psalm 77:12 - "I will also meditate on all Your work, And talk of Your deeds."

• Psalm 143:5 - "I remember the days of old; I meditate on all Your works; I muse on the work of Your hands."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The connection between the mouth and meditation is explicitly reinforced in Joshua 1:8, where God commands that the Book of the Law "shall not depart from your mouth," immediately followed by "but you shall meditate in it." This confirms that meditation is a spoken discipline.

• For the pastor and teacher, hagah highlights the importance of Scripture memorization and oral reading. To help believers meditate, they must be encouraged to slow down, read Scripture aloud, memorize key verses, and actively talk to themselves about the character and promises of God throughout the day. It turns Bible reading from a passive academic exercise into an active, life-sustaining dialogue with the Living God.
