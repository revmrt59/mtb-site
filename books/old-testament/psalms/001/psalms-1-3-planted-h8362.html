PLANTED - ( shathal , Strong's H8362 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shathal refers to the deliberate act of transplanting a tree from one location to another, more favorable environment. In Psalm 1:3, it describes the righteous person who is actively established by God beside continuous, life-giving water sources. Rather than growing wild, wild-sown, or subject to the whims of nature, this tree is intentionally cared for, securing a permanent and thriving source of nourishment that ensures fruitfulness and resilience even in times of drought.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete imagery of shathal centers on the agricultural practice of transplanting. In the arid climate of the ancient Near East, a tree left to grow wild in the dry scrubland was entirely dependent on unpredictable rainfall. To ensure its survival and fruitfulness, an experienced gardener would carefully dig up a choice young sapling from its native, dry soil and transplant it into a cultivated garden, directly alongside irrigation canals. This action implies deliberate choice, ownership, and ongoing cultivation by the gardener. The tree does not plant itself; it is placed by an external hand into an environment designed for its thriving.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Shathal means to transplant, plant, or establish securely. While the common Hebrew word for planting (nata) refers to the general act of putting seeds or plants in the ground, shathal specifically denotes the relocation of a plant to a new, irrigated setting. In the passive participle form used in Psalm 1:3 (shathul), the word describes a permanent state of being firmly rooted, well-watered, and established by an intentional planter.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Literal Agriculture: Used to describe the physical transplanting of vines, shoots, or saplings into fertile, well-watered soil.

• Spiritual Relocation: Metaphorically depicts the transition of a person or nation from a place of spiritual barrenness, vulnerability, and judgment to a place of covenant security, blessing, and divine favor.

• Rooted Stability: Contrasts sharply with the windblown, rootless chaff in Psalm 1:4. The transplanted tree has deep, hidden roots reaching toward a reliable, unseen water source, allowing it to withstand external pressures and harsh climates.

4. Theological Implications

• Divine Sovereign Initiative: A tree cannot transplant itself. The passive form of shathal demonstrates that spiritual life and positioning are initiated entirely by God. The believer is passive in the planting but active in the drawing of nourishment.

• Covenant Security: Being transplanted by God means the believer is under His ownership and constant care. The tree is no longer subject to the wilderness but is situated in the Lord's private, protected garden.

• Grace over Circumstance: Spiritual vitality does not depend on outward circumstances (such as weather, trials, or cultural droughts) but on the source of life to which the believer has been connected—the Word and Spirit of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 1:3, the righteous person is "like a tree planted by the rivers of water." The Hebrew word for "rivers" (peleg) refers to engineered irrigation canals or channels dug by a gardener to distribute water systematically. This emphasizes the highly intentional placement of the tree. The righteous person is not growing by a random, seasonal stream, but is strategically situated by God alongside multiple, engineered channels of divine grace and instruction (the Law of the Lord). Consequently, the tree yields fruit in its season and its leaf does not wither, illustrating a life of sustained, supernatural productivity.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Jeremiah 17:8: "For he shall be like a tree planted by the waters, Which spreads out its roots by the river, And will not fear when heat comes; But its leaf will be green, And will not be anxious in the year of drought, Nor will cease from yielding fruit."

• Psalm 92:13: "Those who are planted in the house of the LORD Shall flourish in the courts of our God."

• Ezekiel 17:10: "Behold, it is planted, will it prosper? Will it not utterly wither when the east wind touches it? It will wither in the garden-terrace where it grew."

• Ezekiel 19:10: "Your mother was like a vine in your vineyard, Planted by the waters, Fruitful and full of branches Because of many waters."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), shathal is translated using the Greek word phyteuo (to plant). This links the imagery directly to New Testament passages, such as Jesus' declaration in Matthew 15:13: "Every plant which My heavenly Father has not planted will be uprooted."

• The contrast in Psalm 1 is absolute and binary: the righteous are deeply rooted, stable, and living (shathul), while the ungodly are weightless, rootless, and dead (mots—chaff).
