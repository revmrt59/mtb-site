RIVERS - ( peleg , Strong's H6388 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word peleg refers to artificial irrigation channels or small divided streams channeled specifically to bring life-giving water to agricultural fields or gardens. Unlike wild, unpredictable torrents, these "rivers" are deliberately designed, dug, and directed by a cultivator to provide a constant, reliable source of nourishment. In Psalm 1:3, this word paints a vivid portrait of the believer who is intentionally positioned by God next to the inexhaustible, well-managed supply of His Word, ensuring continual spiritual growth, fruitfulness, and resilience even in times of drought.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the arid, sun-scorched landscape of the ancient Near East, survival depended entirely on access to water. While wild rivers could flood destructively or run completely dry during summer droughts, ancient agriculturalists developed sophisticated networks of irrigation channels. The word peleg comes from a root meaning "to split" or "divide." These channels split off from a central, permanent water source to guide water directly to specific crops, orchards, or trees. A tree planted by these channels did not rely on unpredictable seasonal rains; it had a direct, cultivated conduit of life-giving water carved out specifically for its benefit.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun peleg refers to a channel, trench, canal, or split stream of water. It designates a small, artificial watercourse used for agricultural irrigation rather than a large, naturally occurring river. The semantic range encompasses:

• An irrigation ditch or canal dug to water dry fields.

• A stream, runnel, or division of water.

• Metaphorically, an abundant, flowing source of refreshment or tears.

The root verb palag means "to divide" or "to split." Thus, a peleg is literally a "divider" of water—a channel that splits off from a central reservoir to distribute water to thirsty soil.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In Scripture, peleg is frequently paired with "water" (peleg mayim) to depict abundant, directed irrigation. It stands in contrast to nachal (a seasonal torrent or wadi that dries up in summer) and nahar (a major river). The peleg is controlled, constant, and purposeful. The word is used in Hebrew literature to describe:

• Irrigation networks that make agricultural land highly productive.

• The sovereign direction of God, who guides human hearts and events as easily as a farmer channels irrigation water (Proverbs 21:1).

• Overwhelming, flowing tears of grief or repentance (Psalm 119:136).

4. Theological Implications

The use of peleg highlights the active, personal sovereignty of God in the believer's life. The tree in Psalm 1:3 does not happen to grow by a river by chance; it is "planted" (transplanted) by a husbandman who has specifically carved out irrigation channels to feed it. This reveals that spiritual vitality is not self-generated. It is the result of divine placement and constant connection to the "streams" of God’s grace and truth. It emphasizes the contrast between the self-sufficient, unstable wicked (who are like chaff blown by the wind) and the dependent, secure righteous (who are rooted by an unchanging water supply).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 1:3, the righteous man is described as "a tree planted by the rivers (palgey) of water."

• The plural "rivers" (palgey) suggests that the tree is placed at the intersection of multiple irrigation channels, receiving a surplus of nourishment from every side.

• The passive participle "planted" (shathul) implies a conscious act of transplanting. The believer is taken out of the wild, dry soil of the world and deliberately placed in a cultivated garden where the channels of God's Word flow directly.

• Because the water source is a managed irrigation channel, it does not dry up during summer heat. The tree's leaf does not wither, and it yields fruit in its proper season. The context links this constant nourishment directly to meditating on the Law of the Lord day and night (Psalm 1:2).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 46:4 – "There is a river whose streams shall make glad the city of God, The holy place of the tabernacle of the Most High."

• Proverbs 21:1 – "The king’s heart is in the hand of the Lord, Like the rivers of water; He turns it wherever He wishes."

• Psalm 119:136 – "Rivers of water run down from my eyes, Because men do not keep Your law."

• Proverbs 5:16 – "Let your fountains be dispersed abroad, Streams of water in the streets."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Greek equivalent in the Septuagint (LXX) for peleg in Psalm 1:3 is diexodous (from diexodos), which refers to an outlet, passage, or channel where waters find their way out. This reinforces the idea of a planned, directed outflow rather than a stagnant pool or an untamed, chaotic flood.

• In Middle Eastern agriculture, a tree planted directly along these irrigation trenches grows exceptionally deep, thick roots because the water is localized. This structural stability mirrors the emotional and spiritual stability of the believer who builds their life upon Biblical truth.
