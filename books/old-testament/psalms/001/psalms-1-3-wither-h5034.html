WITHER - ( nabel , Strong's H5034 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nabel refers to the wilting, drying up, or decaying of vegetation due to a lack of moisture or the natural progression of seasons. In Psalm 1:3, this term forms a powerful agricultural metaphor contrasting the permanent spiritual vitality of the righteous person with the temporary, fragile nature of those cut off from God. The promise that the believer's leaf "shall not wither" guarantees that continuous meditation on God's Word secures enduring spiritual life, strength, and fruitfulness through every trial.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the arid and sun-scorched landscape of the ancient Near East, water was the ultimate determinant of life and death. Vegetation away from a direct water source quickly dried up under the blistering heat of the summer sun. The word nabel evokes the image of a plant losing its internal moisture, drooping, shriveling, turning brown, and eventually dropping its leaves. It represents a state of exhaustion, depletion, and the loss of the vital life force.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of nabel is to wilt, wither, fall, or fade away. It denotes the physical decay of leaves, grass, and flowers. Figuratively, it expresses the concept of wearing out, losing strength, fading away in disgrace, or sinking down exhausted. When applied to human beings, nations, or strength, it signifies the inevitable collapse and loss of vitality that comes from being disconnected from the source of life, or from being subjected to divine judgment.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs nabel in several descriptive ways:

• The Wilting of Nature: It describes leaves falling from a vine or fig tree, and grass and flowers drying up under the hot desert wind.

• The Fading of Human Glory: It represents the temporary nature of human achievements, beauty, and wealth, which quickly fade away when confronted with God's holiness or the passage of time.

• The Exhaustion of Strength: It is used metaphorically for a person or nation whose physical or political strength has completely evaporated, leaving them helpless and fragile like a dry leaf.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nabel highlights the fundamental difference between the creature and the Creator. Human existence and power are inherently fragile and subject to decay. Apart from God, even the most prominent human endeavors eventually wither and turn to dust. True stability and endurance are not self-generated; they are derivative. They depend entirely upon a covenantal connection to God. Preservation from withering is a direct result of being rooted in God's eternal truth, which never fades.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 1:3, nabel is used with the negative particle (lo yibbol) to declare that the leaf of the righteous "shall not wither." The righteous person is compared to a tree intentionally "planted" by streams of living water. Unlike wild brush that depends on erratic desert rainfall, this tree has a deep, constant, and secure water supply.

• The "leaf" represents the outward testimony, daily health, and visible vitality of the believer.

• The promise that the leaf "shall not wither" means that even during times of external heat—such as suffering, trials, persecution, or spiritual drought—the believer's faith, joy, and spiritual vitality will not dry up or fail.

• This endurance is not due to the tree's inherent strength, but because its roots are constantly drawing life from the law of the Lord (Psalm 1:2).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 37:2 - "For they shall soon be cut down like the grass, And wither as the green herb." (Here, nabel highlights the swift end and loss of vitality of evildoers).

• Isaiah 1:30 - "For you shall be as an oak whose leaf fades, And as a garden that has no water." (Here, nabel describes the spiritual decay of those who forsake the Lord).

• Isaiah 40:8 - "The grass withers, the flower fades, But the word of our God stands forever." (Here, nabel is translated as "fades," contrasting the fleeting nature of humanity with the eternal endurance of God's Word).

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Contrast with Chaff: The preservation of the tree in verse 3 contrasts sharply with the ungodly in verse 4, who are described as "chaff which the wind drives away." Chaff has no root, no moisture, and no leaf; it has completely withered and died, leaving it weightless and useless.

• New Testament Parallel: The imagery of nabel corresponds to the Greek word xeraino (to dry up or wither) used by Jesus in John 15:6, where the branch that does not abide in the vine is cast out and "withers." Both passages stress that abiding in the source of life is the sole prerequisite for perpetual greenness and fruitfulness.
