CHAFF - ( mōṣ , Strong's H4671 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word mōṣ refers to the dry, weightless husks of grain separated during the threshing and winnowing process. In the Old Testament, particularly in Psalm 1:4, chaff serves as a vivid spiritual metaphor for the ungodly. It represents a life devoid of spiritual substance, stability, and eternal value. While the righteous are like deeply rooted, fruit-bearing trees, the wicked are like chaff—rootless, dry, and easily swept away by the wind of divine judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, the harvest culminated at the threshing floor, which was typically situated on an elevated hill or ridge to catch the afternoon breeze. Farmers brought the cut sheaves of wheat or barley to be crushed by the hooves of oxen or heavy wooden sleds. This process broke the hard outer husks (the chaff) away from the heavy, valuable grain. The mixture was then tossed high into the air with a winnowing fork. The heavy grain fell straight back down to the floor to be gathered and stored, while the light, worthless chaff was instantly caught by the wind and scattered uselessly across the surrounding hills. This agricultural reality provides a stark, visual picture of division, worthlessness, and sudden disappearance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun mōṣ refers strictly to chaff, the dry outer casing of seeds that is separated during winnowing. It denotes that which is physically light, structurally hollow, and agriculturally useless. Semantically, it stands in direct opposition to grain, which represents life, nourishment, and substance. To be like mōṣ is to be devoid of spiritual weight, lacking any lasting quality or ultimate purpose.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Old Testament poetry and prophecy, mōṣ is utilized to depict:

• Extreme instability: It cannot withstand even a gentle breeze, illustrating how easily the wicked are unsettled by circumstances and trial.

• Absolute worthlessness: It has no nutritional value, cannot be used for planting, and is fit only to be scattered by the wind or consumed by fire.

• Swift judgment: It illustrates how quickly and effortlessly God can remove and disperse those who oppose Him.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the metaphor of chaff highlights the sovereign distinction God makes between the righteous and the ungodly.

• Lack of Spiritual Weight: In Hebrew thought, glory (kabod) is associated with "weight" or "heaviness." The righteous possess spiritual weight because they are anchored in God. The ungodly, regardless of their earthly wealth, power, or influence, are spiritually weightless.

• The Certainty of Separation: Just as the winnowing process inevitably separates the wheat from the chaff, God's holiness and judgment will ultimately and cleanly separate the righteous from the wicked.

• Temporal vs. Eternal: Chaff represents the temporary and fleeting nature of a life lived apart from God. It has no permanent dwelling place and no future legacy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 1, the contrast is absolute. The righteous person is introduced in verses 1–3 as a tree planted by rivers of water, characterized by deep roots, constant nourishment, evergreen leaves, and seasonal fruit. Verse 4 begins with the abrupt and definitive contrast: "The ungodly are not so."

Instead of being anchored, they are rootless. Instead of being watered, they are dry. Instead of being fruitful, they are useless. The phrase "which the wind drives away" emphasizes their complete lack of control, stability, and defense. The "wind" (ruach) represents the breath of God's judgment. When God blows upon the wicked, they have no capacity to stand, highlighting the utter futility of a life built on rebellion against God's law.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Job 21:18: "They are like straw before the wind, And like chaff that a storm carries away."

• Psalm 35:5: "Let them be like chaff before the wind, And let the angel of the LORD chase them."

• Isaiah 17:13: "The nations will rush like the rushing of many waters; But God will rebuke them and they will flee far off, And be chased like the chaff of the mountains before the wind, Like a rolling thing before the whirlwind."

• Hosea 13:3: "Therefore they shall be like the morning cloud and like the early dew that passes away, Like chaff blown off from a threshing floor And like smoke from a chimney."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The imagery of chaff carries directly into the New Testament through the Greek word achyron. John the Baptist uses this exact Old Testament imagery to describe the coming Messiah’s ministry in Matthew 3:12 and Luke 3:17: "His winnowing fan is in His hand, and He will thoroughly clean His threshing floor, and gather His wheat into the barn; but He will burn up the chaff with unquenchable fire." This New Testament parallel reinforces the reality that Jesus is the ultimate Winnower who separates the genuine believer from the empty professor.
