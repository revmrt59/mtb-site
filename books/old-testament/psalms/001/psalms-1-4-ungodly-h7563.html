UNGODLY - ( rāšā‘ , Strong's H7563 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word rāšā‘ represents those who are actively or passively out of relationship with God, living in self-governed independence rather than under His sovereign law. Translated primarily as "ungodly" or "wicked" in the NKJV, it describes a fundamental posture of hostility, lawlessness, and rebellion against God's covenant. In Psalm 1:4, the ungodly are contrasted sharply with the righteous; while the righteous are stable and fruitful like well-watered trees, the ungodly are depicted as weightless, unstable chaff, devoid of spiritual reality and destined for swift judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The background of rāšā‘ is rooted in the ancient Near Eastern legal and relational spheres. In a legal context, it denotes a person who is "in the wrong" or "guilty" of breaching a covenant or social contract. It stands in direct antithesis to the righteous (tsaddiq), who is declared innocent and lives in alignment with the community and God. Metaphorically, the word conjures the image of restlessness and turbulence. Rather than reflecting the calm order of God’s creation, the ungodly are like the troubled sea that cannot rest. In the agricultural landscape of ancient Israel, they are represented as chaff—the dry, worthless husks separated from the wheat during winnowing, possessing neither root, substance, nor lasting value.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term rāšā‘ defines a state of being legally guilty and morally corrupt. It refers to those who are loose, disjointed, or actively chaotic in their moral behavior. Key aspects of its lexical range include:

• Covenant Guilt: Being legally convicted of violating God’s standards and laws.

• Active Rebellion: Living in a state of hostility toward God's authority, characterized by a refusal to submit to His divine order.

• Moral Turbulence: A lifestyle of ethical confusion and wickedness that disrupts peace (shalom) within the community.

The word does not merely describe an occasional lapse in judgment, but an ongoing, settled condition of heart and life that ignores or defies the Creator.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture employs vivid imagery to illustrate the character and fate of the rāšā‘:

• The Restless Sea: They are compared to the tossing sea that casts up mire and dirt, illustrating their inner turmoil and inability to experience true peace.

• The Weightless Chaff: In the winnowing process, the heavier grain falls to the threshing floor while the wind carries away the light, useless chaff. The ungodly are characterized by this same lack of spiritual weight and permanence.

• The Fading Grass: They may temporarily flourish and appear green, but they are quickly cut down and wither away.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, rāšā‘ underscores the reality of human accountability before a holy God. It reveals that ungodliness is not a neutral state but a condition of active opposition to God's righteousness.

• The Standard of Judgment: God’s holiness is the objective standard. To be rāšā‘ is to be found legally guilty in the cosmic courtroom of the Sovereign Judge.

• The Source of Instability: Apart from God, there is no ontological security. True stability only exists in connection to the Creator. Therefore, the ungodly inherently lack the foundation needed to withstand life's storms and final judgment.

• The Separation of Destiny: Scripture maintains a binary view of humanity—the righteous and the ungodly. Their paths, characters, and ultimate destinies are diametrically opposed, culminating either in eternal life under God’s favor or absolute ruin under His wrath.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 1:4, the phrase "The ungodly are not so" serves as a dramatic hinge point. The previous verses described the righteous man as a thriving, deeply rooted tree planted by rivers of water, yielding fruit in season, and remaining green even in drought.

• Absolute Contrast: By stating they "are not so," the psalmist strips the ungodly of every positive quality attributed to the righteous. They have no roots, no lifegiving water, no fruit, and no spiritual longevity.

• The Chaff Metaphor: Rather than a sturdy tree, they are like "chaff which the wind drives away." Chaff represents the worthless, dry casing of the grain. In the ancient winnowing process on a hilltop, the farmer threw the threshed grain into the air; the wind blew the useless chaff away, leaving only the valuable grain. This signifies that the ungodly lack substance, have no permanent place in God's kingdom, and will be easily swept away by the wind of divine judgment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 1:1 - "Blessed is the man / Who walks not in the counsel of the ungodly, / Nor stands in the path of sinners, / Nor sits in the seat of the scornful;"

• Psalm 1:5 - "Therefore the ungodly shall not stand in the judgment, / Nor sinners in the congregation of the righteous."

• Psalm 1:6 - "For the Lord knows the way of the righteous, / But the way of the ungodly shall perish."

• Psalm 73:12 - "Behold, these are the ungodly, / Who are always at ease; / They increase in riches."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Hebrew term rāšā‘ is structurally opposed to tsaddiq (righteous) throughout Wisdom Literature, especially in the Book of Proverbs. While the modern ear often associates "ungodly" with extreme, outward acts of depravity, the biblical definition is far more pervasive. It includes anyone who lives functionally independent of God, regardless of their societal respectability. The tragedy of the rāšā‘ is their false sense of security. Because they may prosper materially in this life (as noted in Psalm 73), they assume all is well. However, the spiritual reality of Psalm 1:4 exposes their ultimate vulnerability: without Christ, they are weightless chaff awaiting the inevitable wind of God's holy evaluation.
