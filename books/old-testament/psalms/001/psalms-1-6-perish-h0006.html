PERISH - ( abad , Strong's H0006 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb abad speaks of ruin, loss, wandering astray, and ultimately perishing. In Psalm 1:6, it describes the tragic, final destination of the ungodly. While the way of the righteous is intimately known and sustained by Yahweh, the path of the wicked leads to complete ruin, vanishing like chaff in the wind. This word emphasizes not just sudden annihilation, but a path that loses its direction, disintegrates, and ends in eternal separation from God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The background of abad is rooted in the imagery of a lost, wandering sheep or a traveler who has lost his way in the desert. It conveys the idea of straying so far from safety and sustenance that destruction becomes inevitable. It portrays a gradual but absolute unraveling of life, security, and purpose when one is separated from the source of life.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb abad carries the primary meaning of perishing, dying, or being destroyed. In its basic Qal stem, it denotes vanishing, going astray, being lost, or meeting ruin. When applied to things, it refers to being lost or ruined; when applied to people, it means to die or be physically destroyed; when applied to plans, ways, or memory, it means to utterly fail, fade away, or come to nothing.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Lost property or livestock: A sheep that has wandered off and is lost, helpless to find its own way back.

• Physical destruction: People dying in battle or due to divine judgment, vanishing from the earth.

• Fading memories and hopes: The name of the wicked rotting away and their hope failing.

4. Theological Implications

The term highlights the binary nature of human existence in Scripture. Life is lived either in communion with God (leading to preservation) or in rebellion against Him (leading to abad—ruin). In biblical theology, perishing is the organic consequence of choosing a path independent of the Creator. God does not actively force the wicked into a state of aimless wandering; rather, their chosen path naturally detours away from the Source of life, resulting in inevitable self-destruction and final judgment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 1:6, the contrast is stark. The Lord "knows" (watches over, intimately cares for) the way of the righteous, but the way of the ungodly "shall perish" (tobed). Notice that it is the way (path/course of life) of the ungodly that perishes. The path itself disintegrates. It leads to a dead end. Like the chaff of verse 4, which is blown away by the wind, the life and legacy of the wicked lack substance, weight, and endurance. They vanish into eternal ruin, leaving nothing of lasting value behind.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Numbers 16:33: "They and all those with them went down alive into the pit; the earth closed over them, and they perished from among the assembly."

• Deuteronomy 30:18: "I announce to you today that you shall surely perish; you shall not prolong your days in the land which you cross over the Jordan to go in and possess."

• Psalm 119:176: "I have gone astray like a lost sheep; seek Your servant, for I do not forget Your commandments."

• Proverbs 10:28: "The hope of the righteous will be gladness, but the expectation of the wicked will perish."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint translates abad in Psalm 1:6 with the Greek verb apollymi, which means to destroy, ruin, or lose. This same Greek word is used in John 3:16 ("should not perish") and Luke 19:10 ("to seek and to save that which was lost"). This links the Old Testament concept of the perishing path directly to the New Testament's call to salvation in Jesus Christ, who came to rescue humanity from the perishing way of sin.
