DESIRED - ( chamad , Strong's H2530 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb chamad refers to a deep, passionate desire, attraction, or delight in something precious or beautiful. While it is frequently used in a negative sense to mean "coveting" what is forbidden, its use in Psalm 19:10 highlights a holy, positive longing. Here, God's laws, testimonies, and precepts are described as supremely desirable—far exceeding the value of the finest refined gold. True wisdom and righteousness are not burdensome obligations; they are the ultimate treasure, capturing the heart's deepest affection and satisfying the soul's deepest hunger.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root chamad points to an intense inward attraction that creates a strong urge to possess, enjoy, or take pleasure in an object. It represents a beauty or value so compelling that it commands the heart's attention.

• In the ancient Near East, gold, especially highly refined "fine gold," was the ultimate standard of earthly security, status, and wealth. By declaring God's Word more desirable than gold, the writer uses chamad to show that divine truth possesses an intrinsic beauty and utility that dwarfs the most precious physical commodities. The scriptures are presented not as a cold textbook, but as a breathtaking treasure.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb chamad means to desire earnestly, to delight in, to take pleasure in, or to covet. Depending on the context, this desire can be either highly virtuous or deeply sinful.

• In Psalm 19:10, the word appears in the Niphal participle form (nechmadim), which functions as an adjective meaning "desirable," "precious," or "lovely." It describes an objective quality of being worthy of intense desire due to inherent excellence and beauty.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The dual nature of chamad is central to its usage across the Old Testament. In the Tenth Commandment, it is the word for "covet" (Exodus 20:17), describing a visual attraction that degenerates into a sinful, possessive craving for what belongs to another.

• Conversely, when directed toward God, His sanctuary, or His truth, chamad depicts a sanctified passion. It is the same word used to describe the trees in the Garden of Eden that were "pleasant to the sight" (Genesis 2:9) and the "desirable treasure" in the dwelling of the wise (Proverbs 21:20). It captures the transition from outward sight to inward delight.

4. Theological Implications

• Biblical faith is not merely an intellectual assent to truth or a mechanical performance of duties; it is a matter of reformed affections. God desires His people to find their ultimate pleasure and delight in Him and His revealed will.

• The use of chamad in a positive spiritual context implies that the regeneration of the human heart changes its appetite. A heart aligned with God no longer views His commands as restrictive or dry, but views them as a source of supreme joy, beauty, and satisfaction.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Psalm 19, David moves from the silent praise of God's general revelation in the heavens (verses 1-6) to the perfect, restorative, and soul-enlightening power of His written Word (verses 7-9).

• In verse 10, the climax of this praise, David asserts that these divine ordinances are "more to be desired than gold." The passive nuance of the Niphal participle indicates that the Word of God is inherently desirable, regardless of human perception. However, for the believer, this objective worth becomes a subjective reality. The Word is sweet to the spiritual taste and invaluable to the spiritual life, offering a wealth that gold cannot buy and a sweetness that honey cannot match.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:9: "And out of the ground the LORD God made every tree grow that is pleasant to the sight and good for food."

• Exodus 20:17: "You shall not covet your neighbor’s house; you shall not covet your neighbor’s wife, nor his male servant, nor his female servant, nor his ox, nor his donkey, nor anything that is your neighbor’s."

• Psalm 68:16: "Why do you fume with envy, you mountains of many peaks? This is the mountain which God desires to dwell in; Yes, the LORD will dwell in it forever."

• Proverbs 21:20: "There is desirable treasure and oil In the dwelling of the wise, But a foolish man squanders it."

• Song of Solomon 2:3: "Like an apple tree among the trees of the woods, So is my beloved among the sons. I sat down in his shade with great delight, And his fruit was sweet to my taste."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Human beings are inherently desiring creatures, designed to pursue what they perceive as valuable. Sin distorts this design, leading to the destructive covetousness warned against in the Decalogue.

• The gospel restores this design by offering the ultimate Object of desire. The spiritual wealth of God's Word satisfies the soul so deeply that it breaks the power of sinful covetousness. True freedom is not the absence of desire, but the possession of a supreme desire for the truth of God.
