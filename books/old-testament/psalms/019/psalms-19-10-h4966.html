SWEETER - ( mathoq , Strong's H4966 )

Summary:

The Hebrew adjective mathoq denotes literal sweetness, most commonly associated with honey, but is frequently employed in Scripture as a metaphor for spiritual, intellectual, and relational delight. In Psalm 19:10, it describes the judgments and law of Yahweh as surpassing the sweetest natural substance known to the ancient world—honey dripping directly from the comb. This word conveys that God’s Word is not merely a legal duty or abstract truth, but a source of profound satisfaction, joy, and nourishment for the soul.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, refined sugar did not exist. Honey was the primary and most highly prized sweetener available. It was a luxury, a symbol of agricultural abundance, and a vital source of physical energy. The word mathoq evokes the physical sensation of tasting something intensely rich, pleasant, and revitalizing. When applied metaphorically to speech, wisdom, or divine law, it implies that these realities bring immediate pleasure and long-term refreshment to the inner being, much like tasting honey revives a physically exhausted person.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The adjective mathoq means "sweet" or "pleasant." It derives from the verbal root mathaq, which means "to be sweet" or "to taste sweet." The semantic range of mathoq spans:

• Literal taste: describing sweet foods, water, or agricultural produce.

• Metaphorical quality: describing pleasant words, wise counsel, sleep, and the overall experience of life or divine truth.

In comparative constructions, the addition of the preposition min ("than") establishes the comparative degree: "sweeter than."

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• mathoq is used to contrast sweetness with bitterness (mar), illustrating the fundamental moral or existential choices humans face.

• It is paired with honey to define the standard of ultimate sweetness in the physical world.

• It describes the sweetness of deep, intimate friendship and shared counsel.

• It illustrates the deceptive, short-lived "sweetness" of sin or stolen waters, which ultimately turns bitter in the stomach.

### 4. Theological Implications

The use of mathoq to describe God’s law shifts the perception of God's commands from burdensome demands to life-giving gifts. It reveals that the righteous person finds intellectual, emotional, and spiritual fulfillment in God's revealed will. It highlights the sensory, experiential nature of faith. God does not just want intellectual assent; He desires that His people taste His goodness. The Word of God satisfies the deepest spiritual appetites of human nature, nourishing the soul in a way that physical food nourishes the body.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 19:7-11, David uses a series of synonyms for Scripture (law, testimony, statutes, commandment, fear, judgments) and describes their perfect attributes and effects. In verse 10, David presents a double comparison of value and desirability: the judgments of the Lord are "more to be desired than gold... sweeter also than honey and the honeycomb."

The "honeycomb" (nopheth tsûphîm) refers to the purest, clearest liquid honey that naturally drips from the comb, which was considered the highest grade of honey. By declaring God's Word sweeter (mathoq) than this, David emphasizes that the divine instructions are the peak of experiential delight. Obedience is not a dry, legalistic duty but a sweet, satisfying privilege that protects, warns, and rewards the servant of God.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Judges 14:18: "So the men of the city said to him on the seventh day before the sun went down: 'What is sweeter than honey? And what is stronger than a lion?'"

• Proverbs 24:13: "My son, eat honey because it is good, And the honeycomb which is sweet to your taste;"

• Ecclesiastes 5:12: "The sleep of a laboring man is sweet, Whether he eats little or much; but the abundance of the rich will not suffer him to sleep."

• Isaiah 5:20: "Woe to those who call evil good, and good evil; Who put darkness for light, and light for darkness; Who put bitter for sweet, and sweet for bitter!"

• Proverbs 27:7: "A satisfied soul loathes the honeycomb, But to a hungry soul every bitter thing is sweet."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between mathoq (sweet) and mar (bitter) in prophetic literature often highlights moral inversion. When a society begins to call bitter sweet and sweet bitter (Isaiah 5:20), it marks the collapse of divine order. The reclamation of true spiritual health begins when a believer's palate is restored to find God's holy law sweet rather than restrictive or bitter.

In Ezekiel 3:3 and Revelation 10:9, the eating of the scroll of God's Word is described as "sweet as honey" in the mouth, but later causing bitterness in the stomach. This shows that while the reception of God's truth is intensely sweet, the living out of its prophetic warnings in a hostile world can bring bitter trials.
