CLEANSE - ( naqah , Strong's H5352 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb naqah carries the primary legal and moral meaning of being empty, clean, free from guilt, acquitted, or declared innocent. In the context of Psalm 19:12, it is a passionate plea from King David for God to absolve and clear him of the "secret faults" he cannot even detect in his own heart. Rather than representing a mere physical or ceremonial washing, this term points to a judicial acquittal and a relational restoration, where God declares the repentant sinner free from the guilt, liability, and punishment of sin.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root idea of naqah relates to being empty or clean, like a vessel that has been completely emptied of its contents and wiped clean. It evokes the image of a clean slate, a cup scraped clean of any residue, or a citizen who is completely exempt from a tax, military duty, or legal obligation. In judicial and moral settings, this physical picture of emptiness translates into being free from guilt, blameless, or entirely exempted from punishment. It represents a state where no charge remains on the record to be held against the individual.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb naqah operates in several biblical stems to describe a state of freedom from moral or legal liability. In the active Qal stem, it means to be clean, free, or exempt from punishment or an oath. In the causative Piel stem (which is used in Psalm 19:12 as naqqeni), it means to acquit, hold guiltless, leave unpunished, or declare innocent. The core lexical definition is not merely physical cleansing but the legal and spiritual act of absolution—rendering a person free from the moral liability of their offenses.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Exemption from Oaths: The word is used when a person is released or "free" from a solemn oath, covenant, or promise once the conditions are met (Genesis 24:8).

• Freedom from Punishment: It frequently expresses the idea of escaping punishment or being held guiltless. When scripture states that God "will by no means clear the guilty," it uses a doubled form of naqah to show that sin cannot simply be overlooked without a righteous basis.

• Acquittal in Court: It functions as a judicial term where a judge pronounces a defendant not guilty, removing the threat of condemnation and restoring their standing before the community.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of naqah in the Old Testament is profound:

• The Limit of Self-Examination: Human beings are incapable of recognizing every single deviation from God's perfect law. Self-examination, while healthy, is limited by our spiritual blindness.

• God as Judge and Justifier: Only God has the authority to declare a sinner naqah (acquitted). True justification is an act of divine grace, not human effort.

• Foretaste of New Testament Justification: This concept directly prefigures the New Testament doctrine of justification by faith. To be cleansed in this sense means that God looks at the sinner and declares, "There is no charge remaining against you." This legal acquittal is made possible ultimately through the substitutionary sacrifice of Jesus Christ, who bore our guilt so we could be declared legally innocent.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 19, David transitions from praising the perfection, reliability, and purity of God's written law (verses 7–11) to examining his own heart (verses 12–14). Confronted by the blinding, perfect light of God's law, David realizes his own spiritual blindness, crying out: "Who can understand his errors?"

Recognizing that he has hidden, unperceived sins ("secret faults"), David does not try to excuse or minimize them. Instead, he cries out, "Cleanse [naqah] me..."

The nuance here in the Piel imperative form is "acquit me." David is asking God to judicially declare him innocent of those sins which he committed unknowingly. Since he cannot confess what he does not know, he throws himself on God's mercy to clear his account entirely. It is a prayer for absolute, comprehensive pardon.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 20:7 - "You shall not take the name of the Lord your God in vain, for the Lord will not hold him guiltless who takes His name in vain."

• Exodus 34:7 - "...keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, by no means clearing the guilty..."

• Jeremiah 30:11 - "For I am with you,' says the Lord, 'to save you; though I make a full end of all nations where I have scattered you, yet I will not make a complete end of you. But I will correct you in justice, and I will not let you go altogether unpunished."

• Job 9:28 - "I am afraid of all my sorrows; I know that You will not hold me innocent."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Contrast with Other Cleansing Words: In Hebrew, tahor refers to ceremonial purity (being clean to enter the tabernacle), and kabas refers to physical washing or laundering (like washing dirty garments in Psalm 51:2). By contrast, naqah is primarily judicial. David does not just want to feel clean or look clean; he needs the cosmic Judge to strike the charges from his record.

• Pastoral Comfort: This word provides immense comfort to believers who suffer from a sensitive conscience or scrupulosity. We do not need to remember and confess every single microscopic sin we have ever committed to be saved; God's covenant acquittal covers even our "secret faults" when we walk in fellowship with Him. His clearance is total.
