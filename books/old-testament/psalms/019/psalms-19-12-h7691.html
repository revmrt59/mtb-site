ERRORS - ( shegiah , Strong's H7691 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun shegiah refers to inadvertent errors, strayings, or mistakes committed out of ignorance, weakness, or lack of vigilance. Occurring only once in the Old Testament in Psalm 19:12, it points to the blind spots in human self-examination—sins we commit without realizing they violate God's perfect standard. In the context of the law's perfection, the word highlights the impossibility of complete self-knowledge and underscores the believer's deep, constant need for divine cleansing and grace.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The noun shegiah is derived from the root verb shagah (Strong's H7686), which literally means to wander, stray, or reel.

• It evokes the picture of a sheep that gets distracted, loses its footing, and drifts away from the flock without any deliberate, rebellious intent to run away.

• Historically, this word family describes errors born of human frailty, ignorance, or lack of attention rather than high-handed, willful rebellion against God. It represents the tragedy of drifting off course because one is not paying sufficiently close attention to the path of righteousness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• shegiah refers to an inadvertent sin, a mistake, a straying, or an error. It denotes an offense committed without conscious awareness of its wrongness at the moment of commission.

• Because it derives from a root associated with mental or physical wandering, it emphasizes the cognitive blind spots where human understanding fails to recognize divine boundary lines. It refers to the deep recesses of the human heart where we violate God's law without even knowing we have done so.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• While shegiah itself is a hapax legomenon (occurring only once in the Hebrew Bible), its verbal root shagah is used to describe a blind man wandering off the road (Deuteronomy 27:18) or a priest reeling and staggering due to strong drink (Isaiah 28:7).

• These pictures highlight a lack of control, a lack of clear vision, or a state of spiritual confusion.

• The "errors" described are not calculated acts of treason against God's sovereign authority, but the inevitable missteps of a fallen creature walking through a broken world with compromised spiritual vision.

4. Theological Implications

• Universal Blindness: No human being possesses perfect self-awareness. Our self-examination is always limited by our fallen nature; we are inherently blind to many of our own failures.

• The Standard of Holiness: God's law is so pure, perfect, and all-encompassing that even our unintentional deviations are still classified as sins requiring cleansing. God's holiness, not our subjective awareness, is the objective standard of righteousness.

• The Insufficiency of Conscience: A clear conscience is not a guarantee of sinlessness. We can be completely unaware of our sins and yet still stand in need of atonement.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Psalm 19, David transitions from praising the majesty of creation (verses 1-6) and the perfection of God's law (verses 7-11) to a raw, personal introspection (verses 12-14).

• Having just declared that God's law is "perfect," "sure," "right," "pure," and "clean," David is struck by the staggering contrast between the perfect light of God's Word and the darkness of his own heart. He cries out, "Who can understand his errors?"

• The rhetorical question expects the answer: "No one." The light of the Law exposes not only our known sins but the reality that there are depths of sin within us that we cannot even detect. This leads directly to his next plea: "Cleanse me from secret faults"—sins hidden even from his own eyes.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because shegiah is used only once in the Hebrew Bible, its theological range is illuminated by looking at its direct verbal root shagah (H7686) and its closely related noun cognate shegaggah (H7684), which refers to unintentional sins:

• Psalm 19:12 – "Who can understand his errors [shegiah, H7691]? Cleanse me from secret faults."

• Leviticus 4:2 – "Speak to the children of Israel, saying: 'If a person sins unintentionally [shegaggah, H7684] against any of the commandments of the Lord...'"

• Leviticus 5:18 – "...So the priest shall make atonement for him regarding his ignorance [shegaggah, H7684] in which he erred and did not know it, and it shall be forgiven him."

• Proverbs 5:23 – "He shall die for lack of instruction, and in the greatness of his folly he shall go astray [shagah, H7686]."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Septuagint (LXX) translates shegiah using the Greek word paraptoma, which means a slip-up, deviation, trespass, or false step. This reinforces the idea of losing one's footing rather than a deliberate defiance.

• This distinction between "errors" (unintentional sins) and "presumptuous sins" (willful, defiant sins) is critical in Israel's sacrificial system. While there were sacrifices for shegaggah (unintentional errors), there was no sacrifice under the Mosaic system for high-handed, defiant rebellion (Numbers 15:30). This makes David’s plea for cleansing from both errors (v. 12) and presumptuous sins (v. 13) a deeply prophetic yearning for a greater grace.
