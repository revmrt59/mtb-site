DOMINION - ( mashal , Strong's H4910 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb mashal means to rule, reign, or exercise dominion over someone or something. In Psalm 19:13, it depicts the enslaving power of "presumptuous sins" (willful, defiant acts of rebellion) acting as a cruel master over the believer. The psalmist pleads for divine preservation so that these sins do not seize sovereign control over his life, allowing him to remain upright and innocent of great transgression.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word mashal carries the basic idea of exercising governance, domain, or authority. In the ancient Near East, dominion was associated with kingship, lordship, and master-servant relationships. The word picture in Psalm 19:13 is one of subjugation. It portrays sin not merely as a moral mistake, but as an aggressive, hostile tyrant seeking to conquer, bind, and rule over the human heart. To be under the mashal of sin is to be a helpless vassal under a ruthless king.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb mashal refers to the exercise of power and authority over others. It denotes the act of ruling, governing, or reigning. Unlike other words for ruling that might imply gentle guidance, mashal emphasizes the concrete reality of dominion and control, whether rightful (such as God's sovereign rule or an appointed king's governance) or usurped (such as the rule of sin over a person).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term occurs over eighty times in the Old Testament. It is used in several distinct contexts to paint a picture of authority:

• Creation: The sun and moon were created to rule (mashal) over the day and night (Genesis 1:18).

• Political Authority: Joseph was appointed to rule (mashal) over all the land of Egypt (Genesis 45:26).

• Divine Sovereignty: God rules (mashal) over the nations and the physical universe by His power forever (Psalm 22:28; 66:7).

• Spiritual Conflict: In Genesis 4:7, God warns Cain that sin's desire is to conquer him, but Cain must rule (mashal) over it. In Psalm 19:13, the dynamic is reversed: the psalmist fears that sin will rule (mashal) over him.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, mashal reveals that sin has a personified, conquering power. Fallen humanity is not entirely autonomous; we are always ruled by something. Either we are ruled by God in life-giving submission, or we are ruled by the tyrannical dominion of sin. Willful, presumptuous sins have a unique spiritual gravity. While errors and secret faults (Psalm 19:12) plague every believer, willful rebellion seeks to dethrone God in the human heart and establish its own sovereign dominion. True spiritual freedom is found only when God intervenes to break the ruling power of sin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 19, David contemplates the absolute perfection, purity, and power of God's written Law (verses 7–11). This exposure to God's holiness causes him to realize his own inner corruption, leading him to pray for cleansing from hidden faults (verse 12). He then moves to a deeper level of danger: "presumptuous sins" (zedim—arrogant, high-handed rebellions). David recognizes that while secret faults are dangerous, willful sins are addictive and enslaving. Once a person willfully steps into known rebellion, that sin seeks to establish dominion (mashal) over them. David’s defense is not his own willpower, but a desperate plea for God's restraining grace: "Keep back Your servant... Let them not have dominion over me."

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:16: "To the woman He said: 'I will greatly multiply your sorrow and your conception; In pain you shall bring forth children; Your desire shall be for your husband, And he shall rule over you.'"

• Genesis 4:7: "If you do well, will you not be accepted? And if you do not do well, sin lies at the door. And its desire is for you, but you must rule over it."

• Genesis 37:8: "And his brothers said to him, 'Shall you indeed reign over us? Or shall you indeed have dominion over us?' So they hated him even more for his dreams and for his words."

• Psalm 22:28: "For the kingdom is the LORD’S, And He rules over the nations."

• Proverbs 16:32: "He who is slow to anger is better than the mighty, And he who rules his spirit than he who takes a city."

7. Other Relevant Insights

This Old Testament concept of sin's dominion directly anticipates New Testament sanctification theology. In Romans 6:12, Paul exhorts believers, "Therefore do not let sin reign in your mortal body," and in Romans 6:14, he declares, "For sin shall not have dominion over you." The Greek equivalent used in the Septuagint and the New Testament (kyrieuo) mirrors mashal, showing that the work of Christ delivers the believer from the ruling authority of sin and restores them to the life-giving dominion of God.
