INNOCENT - ( naqah , Strong's H5352 )

Summary:

The Hebrew root naqah fundamentally means to be empty, clean, or free from guilt, punishment, or obligation. In a legal and moral sense, it denotes being acquitted, declared innocent, or exempted from punishment. In the context of Psalm 19:13, the psalmist desires to be cleansed, absolved, and kept free from the devastating hold of willful rebellion ("great transgression") against God. It conveys a state of moral purity and relational wholeness that is only possible through God's restraining grace and cleansing power.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primary concrete idea of naqah lies in being empty or clean, such as a vessel that has been completely emptied of its contents, or teeth that are clean of food due to famine. From this physical picture of emptiness and cleanliness, the word transitions into the legal and moral sphere. To be naqah is to be empty of charges, free from moral stain, or exempted from an oath or covenant obligation. It pictures a person standing before a judge or an injured party, where a thorough investigation reveals no remaining liability, guilt, or grounds for condemnation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verbal root naqah occurs in several stems. In the Qal stem, it means to be empty, clean, or free from punishment. In the Niphal stem, which is used in Psalm 19:13, it means to be cleared, declared innocent, acquitted, or free from guilt and punishment. In the Piel stem, it functions causatively, meaning to leave unpunished, to acquit, or to cleanse. The lexical core is not merely subjective feelings of innocence, but an objective legal status. It represents a divine declaration of acquittal or a state of being morally undefiled and free from the consequences of transgression.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Legal Absolution: The term is frequently used in contexts involving oaths and legal liabilities. For example, if a party fulfills their conditions, they are "clear" or "free" from the oath.

• Exemption from Punishment: It describes being spared from divine judgment or consequences. When God declares He will by no means "clear" the guilty, the verbal root emphasizes that sin cannot simply be overlooked without satisfaction.

• Physical Cleanness: The word-picture of being poured out or scrubbed clean underlies its moral application. Just as a cup is washed clean of residue, the sinner who is cleared has been thoroughly cleansed of the residue and defilement of rebellion.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, naqah highlights the absolute moral purity of God and the standard of holiness He requires. Human beings cannot declare themselves innocent, nor can they escape the legal consequences of their actions by their own power. The process of becoming acquitted or cleansed requires divine intervention. It points forward to the New Testament doctrine of justification, where God legally declares the believer righteous and free from condemnation. It also demonstrates that true innocence is not merely the absence of outward sins but a deep, inward cleansing of the heart and motives by God's grace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 19, David reflects on the perfection of God's written law and immediately transitions to his own human frailty. He asks, "Who can understand his errors? Cleanse me from secret faults" (verse 12). He then prays to be kept back from "presumptuous sins" (deliberate, high-handed rebellions) so that they do not rule over him. In verse 13, David states, "Then I shall be blameless, and I shall be innocent of great transgression." The contextual nuance of naqah here is a protective acquittal. David recognizes that if God restrains his prone-to-wander heart from willful, defiant rebellion, he will remain free from the crushing guilt and devastating consequences of apostasy. It is a plea for preserving grace that keeps the believer's record clean and his relationship with God unmarred.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The relationship between the verb naqah and the adjective naqi (innocent, guiltless) is significant. The adjective is often used to describe "innocent blood," which must not be shed. This linguistic connection reinforces that the verbal root carries a heavy moral weight. To be naqah is to be legally and morally untouchable by guilt. It is the state of a person whose hands are clean and whose heart is pure, standing before a holy God who searches the minds and hearts.
