SERVANT - ( ‘ebed , Strong's H5650 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ‘ebed denotes a servant, slave, or worshiper who is completely subordinate to a master, yet finds safety, identity, and purpose within that relationship of ownership. In the context of the Old Testament, being a servant of Yahweh is not a position of degrading bondage, but an honorable status of covenant loyalty, protection, and total dependence. In Psalm 19:13, David identifies himself as God's ‘ebed, appealing to his Master for preservation, cleansing, and protection from the dominion of willful, presumptuous sins.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The term ‘ebed is derived from the verb ‘abad, which means "to work," "to serve," or "to till." In the ancient Near East, a servant's entire existence was defined by the master's will, provision, and protection. Far from being a mere tool, a trusted servant in a royal household held significant authority and acted as an extension of the master's own person. When applied to Israel or devout individuals in relation to Yahweh, ‘ebed reflects a voluntary, loving subordination within a covenant relationship. It emphasizes that the believer is not their own master; they belong to God, who in turn acts as their protector, redeemer, and provider.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word ‘ebed refers to a slave, servant, or subject. Its semantic range includes:

• A literal slave or bondservant owned by a master.

• A subject of a king or political ruler.

• A tributary nation serving a more powerful nation.

• A worshiper or pious follower of the true God.

• An official or high-ranking minister in a royal court.

In a religious and covenantal sense, it designates someone who belongs wholly to God, performing His service and obeying His voice.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The relationship of an ‘ebed to their master is pictured throughout Scripture in several rich ways:

• The Eager Watcher: The eyes of the servant are fixed on the hand of the master, waiting for direction, provision, and mercy (Psalm 123:2).

• The Protected Dependent: A master is legally and morally responsible for the well-being and defense of his servant. If anyone harms a servant, they answer to the master.

• The Trusted Steward: Abraham’s servant managed his master’s entire household and held the power of attorney, showing that a servant's status could be incredibly high and trusted (Genesis 24).

• The Servant of the Lord: Moses, Joshua, and David are frequently called "the servant of Yahweh," a title of ultimate honor and divine commission.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, calling oneself an ‘ebed of Yahweh represents the intersection of submission and privilege.

• Ownership and Redemption: The servant belongs to God. In Israel, redemptive history began with God rescuing them from being servants to Pharaoh so they could become servants to Him (Leviticus 25:55). True freedom is not autonomy, but changing masters from sin and tyranny to the living God.

• Covenant Security: Because the servant is God's property, God is committed to protecting them. An attack on the servant is an affront to the Master.

• Humility and Obedience: It excludes any room for self-will, pride, or independent action. The servant’s primary duty is absolute obedience to the Master’s commands.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 19, David has just extolled the perfection, trustworthiness, and life-giving power of God's written law (verses 7–11). Recognizing the holiness of God’s standards, David is struck by his own frailty and proneness to sin (verse 12). In verse 13, he cries out: "Keep back Your servant also from presumptuous sins."

By using ‘ebed here, David bases his plea on his relationship to God as Master. He is essentially praying: "I am Your servant; I belong to You. Therefore, protect Your property. Do not allow pride or willful rebellion to seize mastery over me." David recognizes that if sin rules over him, he is no longer serving his true Master. He appeals to God's ownership and protective responsibility to keep him clean, blameless, and fit for the Master's service.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 24:34 - "So he said, 'I am Abraham’s servant.'"

• Joshua 1:1 - "After the death of Moses the servant of the LORD, it came to pass that the LORD spoke to Joshua the son of Nun, Moses’ assistant..."

• Psalm 116:16 - "O LORD, truly I am Your servant; I am Your servant, the son of Your maidservant; You have loosed my bonds."

• Isaiah 53:11 - "He shall see the labor of His soul, and be satisfied. By His knowledge My righteous Servant shall justify many, For He shall bear their iniquities."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek Old Testament (LXX), ‘ebed is most frequently translated by doulos (bondservant/slave) or pais (child/servant). This directly connects the Old Testament theology of the servant to the New Testament, where Jesus takes the form of a bondservant (doulos in Philippians 2:7) and where Paul, Peter, James, Jude, and John proudly introduce themselves as "servants of Jesus Christ."
