TRANSGRESSION - ( pesha , Strong's H6588 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun pesha refers to rebellion, revolt, or a willful violation of authority, law, or covenant. Rather than an accidental slip, a structural weakness, or a mistake of ignorance, it represents an intentional, defiant breaking of relationship—a conscious decision to cross a boundary set by a rightful sovereign. In Psalm 19:13, it highlights the climax of a downward moral progression, warning against the "great transgression" of deliberate, unrepentant rebellion against God's loving rule.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root meaning of pesha is grounded in the social, political, and covenantal spheres of the ancient Near East.

• In a political context, it describes a treaty violation or a political rebellion where a vassal state deliberately breaks its covenant with a sovereign king. It is a declaration of independence, an act of treason, or a willful revolt against legitimate authority.

• In a relational context, it indicates a breach of trust, an act of unfaithfulness, or a betrayal of friendship. It is not merely falling short of a mark, but a deliberate turning of one's back on a relationship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Pesha signifies rebellion, revolt, transgression, or a willful offense.

• It denotes a conscious, intentional violation of a law, covenant, or moral boundary.

• It contrasts sharply with other Hebrew words for sin, such as chata' (missing the mark or falling short) and 'avon (iniquity, crookedness, or guilt). Pesha is the most severe of these terms because it contains the element of willful, stubborn defiance and active rebellion against God's law.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The word often appears in legal and covenantal contexts where Israel is accused of breaking their covenant with Yahweh.

• It is pictured as a heavy burden that weighs down the sinner or as a defiling stain that requires ritual cleansing and atonement.

• In the prophetic books, it describes the systemic, stubborn rebellion of the nation against God's instruction, treating His laws with contempt rather than reverence.

4. Theological Implications

• Pesha exposes the true nature of sin as a relational betrayal rather than just a broken rule. It demonstrates that sin is directed personally against God Himself.

• It highlights the gravity of human depravity. Mankind does not merely wander off the path out of ignorance; we actively rebel against our Creator's authority.

• The necessity of substitutionary atonement is underscored by pesha. Defiant rebellion cannot be simply overlooked; it must be paid for. This makes the Isaianic promise of the Messiah being "wounded for our transgressions" profoundly beautiful, showing that Christ bore even our active rebellion.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Psalm 19:11-13, David lays out a progression of sin: "secret faults" (unintentional, hidden errors, v. 12), "presumptuous sins" (willful, arrogant choices, v. 13a), and finally, "great transgression" (pesha, v. 13b).

• David's plea is for total preservation. If left unchecked, "presumptuous sins" will gain "dominion" over a person, leading inevitably to "great transgression"—a state of hardened, apostate rebellion against God.

• By praying to be kept from "great transgression," David seeks the preserving grace of God to prevent his heart from becoming calloused, completely alienated, and settled in defiance against his Lord.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 50:17: "'I beg you, please forgive the trespass of your brothers and their sin; for they did evil to you.' Now please, forgive the trespass of the servants of the God of your father."

• Psalm 32:1: "Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, Whose sin is covered."

• Proverbs 17:9: "He who covers a transgression seeks love, But he who repeats a matter separates friends."

• Isaiah 53:5: "But He was wounded for our transgressions, He was bruised for our iniquities; The chastisement for our peace was upon Him, And by His stripes we are healed."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In the Day of Atonement ritual (Leviticus 16:16, 21), the high priest had to make atonement for the Holy Place because of the uncleanness of the children of Israel, and because of their "transgressions" (pesha). This demonstrates that God's grace and sacrificial system were designed to cover even willful, rebellious sins, provided there was repentance and faith.

• The ultimate resolution of pesha is found in Jesus Christ, who took our active rebellion upon Himself, satisfying the righteous wrath of God and offering reconciliation to those who were once hostile rebels.
