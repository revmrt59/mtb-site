LORD - ( Yahweh , Strong's H3068 )

Summary:

The name Yahweh is the personal, covenant-keeping name of the God of Israel, revealed most deeply to Moses at the burning bush. Derived from the Hebrew verb meaning "to be," it denotes God's absolute self-existence, eternity, and unchangeable nature. While other divine names highlight His power as Creator, Yahweh stresses His intimate presence and relational commitment to His people. In Psalm 19:14, David appeals to this covenant name, expressing confidence that the God who commands the cosmos is also the personal redeemer who hears the private whispers and meditations of the human heart.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Yahweh is represented in the Hebrew Bible by four consonants—Y-H-W-H—commonly referred to as the Tetragrammaton. Out of extreme reverence for the holiness of God’s personal name, ancient Jewish readers avoided pronouncing it aloud, substituting the word Adonai ("Lord") when reading Scripture. English translations preserve this distinction by rendering Yahweh as "LORD" in all capital letters. The name is not a generic title for a deity, but a unique, personal name. It was historically revealed in power to Moses in Exodus 3, where God associated the name with the phrase "I AM WHO I AM." This background establishes the name as a guarantee of God's active, saving presence in the history of His people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name Yahweh is etymologically rooted in the verb hayah, which means "to be" or "to become." It is the third-person singular form, meaning "He is," "He will be," or "He exists." The name defines God as the self-existent One who has life in Himself, possessing no beginning and no end. Unlike creation, which relies on external causes to exist, Yahweh is entirely self-sufficient. Furthermore, the name denotes active presence; it is not a declaration of static existence, but a promise that He will actively manifest His presence on behalf of those with whom He has entered into covenant.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, Yahweh is used over 6,800 times, making it the most frequent name for God in Scripture. It is frequently paired with descriptive terms that highlight His relation to humanity, such as Yahweh-Yireh ("The LORD will provide"), Yahweh-Nissi ("The LORD is my banner"), and Yahweh-Shalom ("The LORD is peace"). The scriptures use vivid anthropomorphic language to describe Yahweh: He is a shepherd guiding His flock, a husband loving an unfaithful bride, a warrior fighting for the oppressed, and a strong tower where the righteous find safety. These word pictures demonstrate that the self-existent Creator is deeply involved in the emotional and relational realities of human life.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of Yahweh centers on God's immutability, sovereignty, and faithfulness. Because He is the "I AM," He does not change; His character, purposes, and promises remain constant across generations. The name is intrinsically tied to His covenant. Whenever God establishes a covenant, He binds Himself to His people by His name Yahweh. It guarantees that His grace is not dependent on human performance, but on His own eternal character. Consequently, the name stands as the ultimate foundation for biblical faith, assurance, and hope.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 19 is divided into two distinct halves, which is mirrored in the names used for God. In verses 1–6, David describes God's revelation in the physical creation, using the name El—the title for God that emphasizes His supreme power, majesty, and strength as Creator. However, in verses 7–14, as David shifts his focus to the perfection of God's written law and its restoring effect on the human soul, he switches exclusively to the name Yahweh, using it seven times.

In the final verse, Psalm 19:14, David cries out: "Let the words of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be acceptable in Your sight, O LORD [Yahweh], my strength and my Redeemer." By invoking Yahweh, David moves from observing a distant, powerful God in the heavens to pleading with a personal, covenant God who cares about the inner thoughts of a servant. The transcendent Creator (El) of the cosmos is revealed to be the intimate Savior (Yahweh) who redeems the soul.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), Yahweh was translated as Kurios ("Lord"). This connection is crucial for understanding the New Testament, where the writers frequently apply the title Kurios to Jesus Christ. When the apostle Paul declares that "confessing with your mouth that Jesus is Lord" is necessary for salvation (Romans 10:9), or when he writes that "at the name of Jesus every knee should bow... and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord" (Philippians 2:10-11), he is directly identifying Jesus as Yahweh of the Old Testament. The eternal, self-existent, covenant-keeping God of Israel has made Himself fully known in human flesh.
