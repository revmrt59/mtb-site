WORDS - ( 'emer , Strong's H0561 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun 'emer refers to an utterance, saying, speech, or word that is externalized and communicated. Derived from the root verb 'amar (to speak), it represents the tangible, verbal expression of one's thoughts and inner character. In Psalm 19:14, the psalmist petitions God that his spoken words, paired with his innermost meditations, might be pleasing and acceptable to his Lord, establishing a profound connection between external speech and internal spiritual alignment.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun 'emer is rooted in the common Semitic verb 'amar, which means "to say, speak, or command." While the verb focuses on the act of speaking, the noun 'emer emphasizes the product of that speech—the verbalized utterance that enters the auditory space of others. In ancient Near Eastern culture, spoken words were not viewed as empty breath but as dynamic entities carrying weight, power, and the very essence of the speaker's character. Once spoken, a word was considered an active force that could bless, build, tear down, or bind. In biblical poetry, 'emer often stands in parallel with other communication terms like dabar (word/matter) or meditation (internal thought), reflecting the ancient Israelite understanding that external speech is the direct harvest of internal character.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, 'emer refers to a saying, speech, utterance, command, or promise. It designates the verbal output of a person, ranging from everyday speech to divine declarations. In its plural form (as used in Psalm 19:14, 'imre-pi—"the words of my mouth"), it encompasses the entirety of a person’s verbal expressions. The term does not merely point to grammatical units or vocabulary, but to the semantic content and moral character of what is spoken. It denotes the articulation of thoughts, whether they manifest as wisdom, foolishness, righteousness, or deceit.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The noun 'emer occurs approximately 49 times in the Old Testament, with the vast majority of its occurrences found in poetic and wisdom literature, particularly Job, Proverbs, and the Psalms.

• The Harvest of the Heart: In the wisdom literature, a person's 'emer is pictured as a crop or fruit produced by the seed of the heart. For example, "pleasant words" are compared to a honeycomb, bringing sweetness to the soul and health to the bones.

• The Vehicle of Wisdom: Proverbs frequently uses the term to describe "words of understanding" or "words of knowledge." The wise person is recognized by the restraint, truth, and grace of their 'emer.

• The Power of Speech: In Job, the word is used to describe the sheer impact of human arguments and counsel, highlighting how verbalized thoughts can either bring comfort or cause deep wounds.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'emer underscores the biblically established connection between human speech and spiritual reality. Because human beings are made in the image of a God who speaks, human speech possesses immense moral responsibility.

• The Diagnostic of the Soul: Speech serves as the ultimate diagnostic tool of the spiritual life. What comes out of the mouth ('emer) reveals the condition of the heart.

• Sanctified Communication: The request for one's 'emer to be acceptable to God indicates that speech is not a neutral, secular activity. Every word spoken is an act of worship or an act of defilement.

• Speech as a Sacrifice: In a worship context, verbal expressions—whether prayers, songs, or everyday speech—are treated as spiritual offerings. To desire that these "words" be acceptable to God is to present one's mouth as an altar of righteousness.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 19, the context moves from cosmic revelation to personal consecration. The psalm begins by declaring how the heavens "pour forth speech" ('omer, a closely related noun) without audible voice, revealing God's glory through general revelation (verses 1–6). It then transitions to special revelation, praising the perfect, soul-restoring Law of the Lord (verses 7–11).

After contemplating the purity of God's Word, David is brought to a place of deep self-examination regarding his own hidden faults and presumptuous sins (verses 12–13). In verse 14, he offers his climactic petition: "Let the words ['emer] of my mouth and the meditation of my heart Be acceptable..."

Here, David juxtaposes the public, audible "words of my mouth" with the private, silent "meditation of my heart." By pairing these two concepts, David recognizes that true holiness requires seamless integrity between the inner life and outer expression. He desires that his spoken 'emer be just as pure as his private thoughts, offering both to Yahweh as a pleasing sacrifice.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), 'emer in Psalm 19:14 is translated using the plural of logion (logia), which means "sayings" or "oracles." In the New Testament, logia is typically reserved for divine utterances or the "oracles of God" (as in Romans 3:2 and 1 Peter 4:11). This linguistic connection highlights the lofty standard to which David held his own speech: he desired his personal, human utterances to be so aligned with God's truth that they would mirror the holiness of divine oracles.
