LANGUAGE - ( dabar , Strong's H1697 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word dabar is one of the most foundational and dynamic terms in the Old Testament, occurring over 2,500 times. While translated as "word," "speech," "matter," or "language," it carries a dual nuance of both the spoken word and the concrete reality behind it. In Psalm 19:3, dabar (occurring in the plural debarim) is translated as "language" in the NKJV, describing the silent but universally understood testimony of God's creation. Unlike human speech, which is localized and audible, the "words" of creation are a non-verbal proclamation of God's glory that transcends human speech barriers, reaching to the ends of the earth.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In Semitic thought, a word is not merely an abstract sound or a passive symbol of mental activity; it is an active, dynamic force. A spoken word possesses concrete existence and carries the power to accomplish something. The root meaning of dabar is associated with "backing" or "the background of a thing," pointing to the substance, order, or arrangement of reality. Thus, dabar refers to both the outward expression (the word spoken) and the inward substance (the matter, event, or thing itself). When God speaks, His dabar is simultaneously a message and a creative act; the word and the event are unified.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Dabar carries a wide semantic range depending on its context. Its primary definitions include:

• Speech, discourse, utterance, or spoken words.

• A matter, affair, business, or case (a concrete thing or situation being discussed).

• A command, decree, or message (especially when originating from a king or from God).

• A book, chronicle, or written record (frequently in the plural as "the acts/events of").

In the plural form (debarim), it often denotes continuous speech, collections of words, or languages. In Psalm 19:3, it specifically represents the "words" or "articulations" of the created order, translated as "language" to contrast with localized human speech.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently uses dabar to illustrate the unstoppable power of communication. God's dabar does not return void but actively shapes history, creates the universe, and directs His people.

• The Path of the Word: A prominent word picture associated with dabar is that of a physical path. To "walk in the word" is to align one's life with God's ordered reality.

• Word as Event: Because dabar also means "thing" or "event," history itself is viewed as a series of God's "words" unfolding in time.

• Personified Messenger: In poetic literature, dabar is personified as a messenger sent from heaven to heal, deliver, or execute judgment (e.g., Psalm 107:20).

### 4. Theological Implications

The concept of dabar reveals a God who is inherently communicative. God does not remain hidden or silent; He initiates relationship by speaking.

• Creation and Revelation: There is an inseparable link between revelation and creation. The same dabar that brought the heavens into existence is the dabar that constitutes the Law, the Prophets, and the wisdom of Israel.

• The Foundation of the Logos: Dabar lays the theological groundwork for the New Testament concept of the Logos (the Word) in John 1. Jesus Christ is the ultimate embodiment of God's dabar—not just a speaker of truth, but the Truth itself, the Word made flesh.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 19, David compares two books of divine revelation: the book of nature (verses 1–6) and the book of Scripture (verses 7–11).

• In Psalm 19:3, "There is no speech nor language Where their voice is not heard," the psalmist employs a beautiful paradox. Creation has no literal tongue, no vocal cords, and no audible debarim (words/language) that can be picked up by human ears.

• Yet, this "silent speech" is a universal "language" (dabar). It requires no translation, is bound by no national borders, and is accessible to all of humanity. The "words" of the sun, moon, and stars are visual, constant, and universally understood. Their silence is itself a powerful proclamation of the Creator's glory.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 15:1 - "After these things the word of the LORD came to Abram in a vision, saying, 'Do not be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward.'"

• Psalm 33:6 - "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made, And all the host of them by the breath of His mouth."

• Psalm 119:105 - "Your word is a lamp to my feet And a light to my path."

• Isaiah 40:8 - "The grass withers, the flower fades, But the word of our God stands forever."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Near Eastern cultures, gods were often silent or spoke only through obscure, terrifying omens interpreted by elite priests. The God of Israel, however, reveals Himself through dabar—clear, structured, and accessible communication. The dual nature of dabar as both "word" and "deed" explains why Hebrew history books are called Dibre hayyamim (literally, "the words/events of the days," translated as Chronicles). In the biblical worldview, words are events, and events are the spoken words of God written on the canvas of time.
