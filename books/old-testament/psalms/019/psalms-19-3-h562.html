SPEECH - ( omer , Strong's H562 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun omer denotes an utterance, speech, saying, or command. In Psalm 19:3, it describes the quiet yet powerful declaration of creation. The heavens continuously "speak" of God's glory, delivering a non-verbal sermon that is universally understood. This word highlights that true communication is not limited to human vocalization; the silent testimony of nature speaks profoundly to all humanity, revealing the reality and majesty of the Creator without uttering a single audible sound.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word omer derives from the root verb amar, which means "to say, speak, or command." While the verb is used thousands of times to denote ordinary human and divine speech, the noun omer carries a poetic flavor. It evokes the image of a thought being shaped, projected, and delivered to an audience.

• In ancient Hebrew thought, the physical world was not viewed as a silent mechanism but as an active proclamation of its Maker. The heavens serve as an eloquent witness, constantly sending out their testimony. Omer represents this ongoing stream of expressive revelation flowing from the cosmos to humanity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Omer refers to a saying, speech, word, promise, or utterance. It signifies the verbal expression of an idea, though in poetic literature it is frequently used to describe non-verbal but clear communication.

• Unlike dabar, which often focuses on the substance, matter, or historical event behind a word, omer centers on the active expression and delivery of the message. It is the "saying" itself, the manifestation of communication.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Eloquent Heavens: In poetic contexts, omer is used to personify day and night as preachers who take turns speaking. Each day "pours forth speech," passing the baton of testimony to the night.

• Divine Decrees: The word can refer to the powerful commands of God. When God speaks, His omer has the authority to direct history and creation.

• Wise Counsel: In wisdom literature, omer can describe the valuable sayings or instructions of a teacher or elder, representing words that carry weight and moral guidance.

4. Theological Implications

• General Revelation: God has made His existence, power, and divine nature known to all people. The omer of creation leaves humanity without excuse because it is a message broadcasted to the entire world.

• The Universality of God's Voice: Because the heavens speak without physical vocal cords, they bypass human linguistic barriers. This silent omer is a global dialect, understood by every tribe, tongue, and nation.

• Continuous Witness: The testimony of creation is unbroken. Unlike human sermons that end, the omer of the skies is a perpetual declaration that never ceases to display God’s majesty.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Psalm 19:3, the writer uses omer alongside dabar ("language" or "speech") to show the paradoxical nature of creation's testimony. The verse states, "There is no speech (omer) nor language Where their voice is not heard."

• The nuance here is a beautiful paradox: the heavens speak, yet they are silent. They do not possess human vocalization (omer in the literal sense), nor do they speak a localized language. Yet, their message is so clear that it is as though their voice is heard everywhere. The "speech" of creation is a silent eloquence that communicates the reality of God more powerfully than human words ever could.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 19:2 - "Day unto day utters speech, And night unto night reveals knowledge."

• Psalm 19:3 - "There is no speech nor language Where their voice is not heard."

• Psalm 68:11 - "The Lord gave the word; Great was the company of those who proclaimed it."

• Job 22:28 - "You will also declare a thing, and it will be established for you; So light will shine on your ways."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Greek Septuagint translates omer in Psalm 19:3 using the term lalia, which refers to talk, dialect, or speech. This highlights that the testimony of the cosmos is not a chaotic jumble of sounds, but a structured, coherent dialect that communicates specific truths about God's character.

• In Romans 10:18, the Apostle Paul quotes Psalm 19 to argue that Israel has heard the gospel message, applying the universal reach of the heavens' "voice" to the worldwide proclamation of the Gospel. Just as the omer of creation goes to the ends of the earth, so the message of Christ is meant to reach all nations.
