VOICE - ( qol , Strong's H6963 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun qol refers to a sound, voice, noise, or call, ranging from the audible speech of humans to the thunderous proclamation of God or the silent declaration of His creation. In the context of Psalm 19:3, it describes the silent yet globally understood "voice" of the cosmos, declaring the glory of God without spoken words. This word underscores the theological reality that God's general revelation is universally accessible, speaking continuously to all humanity through the very fabric of creation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of qol is associated with producing sound or calling out. It is a highly versatile noun used over 500 times in the Old Testament. Historically, the word captures the physical reality of sound waves, whether the gentle whisper of a human voice, the blast of a trumpet, the roar of thunder, or the rustle of leaves. In ancient Near Eastern culture, a person's "voice" was not merely acoustic but carried their authority, presence, and character. To hear someone's qol was to encounter the person themselves.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• qol denotes several categories of sound:

• In relation to humans, it represents spoken speech, crying out, weeping, or singing.

• In relation to God, it frequently signifies His active command, His audible revelation (such as His voice from Mount Sinai), or thunder (often called the "voice of Yahweh" in the Psalms).

• In relation to inanimate objects or nature, it refers to the sound or rumble they produce, such as the sound of chariot wheels, rushing waters, or, as in Psalm 19, the silent communication of the heavens.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Voice of Thunder: Scripture frequently equates thunder with the voice of God (qol YHWH), as seen in Psalm 29, where His voice shakes the wilderness and strips the forests bare. This depicts God’s absolute power and majestic presence.

• The Voice of Blood: In Genesis 4:10, Abel’s blood cries out (qol) from the ground. This poetic usage highlights how even silent, inanimate things can cry out for justice in the ears of God.

• The Voice of the Shofar: The trumpet blast (qol of the shofar) represents warning, assembly, and the terrifying presence of God at Sinai (Exodus 19:16).

4. Theological Implications

• God is a speaking God. Unlike the silent, lifeless idols of the nations, the living God makes His qol known. He communicates with His creation.

• Creation acts as a cosmic mirror and megaphone of God's character. By using qol to describe the silent testimony of the heavens, the text reveals that creation communicates truth about God's eternal power and divine nature.

• This "voice" establishes human accountability. Because creation's voice reaches the ends of the earth, no human being is without excuse regarding the knowledge of the Creator (a truth Paul expands upon in Romans 1:20 and Romans 10:18).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Psalm 19:1-4, David contrasts the silent revelation of the heavens with the spoken and written revelation of God's Law (verses 7-11).

• Psalm 19:3 states, "There is no speech nor language Where their voice is not heard." The paradox is striking: the heavens have "no speech nor language" in a human sense, yet "their voice (qol)" is heard.

• The nuance here is a silent yet powerful proclamation. Creation does not use Hebrew, Greek, or English, yet its "voice"—its design, order, beauty, and vastness—is universally translated and understood. It is a visual voice, an audible silence that speaks louder than words to the human heart.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:8 - "And they heard the sound [qol] of the Lord God walking in the garden in the cool of the day..."

• Genesis 4:10 - "And He said, 'What have you done? The voice [qol] of your brother’s blood cries out to Me from the ground.'"

• Exodus 19:19 - "And when the blast [qol] of the trumpet sounded long and became louder and louder, Moses spoke, and God answered him by voice [qol]."

• Psalm 29:3 - "The voice [qol] of the Lord is over the waters; The God of glory thunders; The Lord is over many waters."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) translates qol in Psalm 19:3 (Psalm 18:4 in the LXX/Vulgate numbering) using the Greek noun phonē (meaning voice, sound, or tone). In Romans 10:18, the Apostle Paul quotes the Greek equivalent of Psalm 19:4 ("Their voice has gone out to all the earth") to prove that the message of God's truth has indeed been proclaimed globally, showing how the silent "voice" of creation lays the foundation for the spoken "voice" of the Gospel.
