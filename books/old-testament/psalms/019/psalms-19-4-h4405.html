WORDS - ( millah , Strong's H4405 )

Summary:

In Psalm 19:4, the Hebrew noun millah (plural here, translated as "words") refers to the articulate, non-verbal communication of God’s creation. While the heavens do not speak with audible human language, their silent witness is so powerful and universal that it constitutes a clear, divine message sent to the very ends of the earth. This term highlights the expressive nature of God’s general revelation, demonstrating that creation speaks a language everyone can understand.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun millah comes from an ancient Semitic root associated with speaking, uttering, or conversing. While the standard Hebrew word for "word" or "thing" (dabar) carries heavy theological and historical weight, millah is a more poetic and expressive term. It focuses on the actual utterance, the speech, or the specific words spoken in a discourse. The term is highly concentrated in the poetic books of the Old Testament, particularly in Job and the Psalms. The word picture behind millah is one of articulate expression—shaping thoughts and realities into concrete, understandable communication.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun millah denotes a word, speech, utterance, concept, or matter. It can refer to an individual word or collectively to a whole speech, discourse, or message. It functions as a poetic synonym for dabar, but carries a distinct emphasis on the intelligibility, delivery, and impact of the message. In the plural, it represents the complete body of utterances that convey a coherent thought.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The vast majority of occurrences of millah are found in the Book of Job, where the characters use it to describe their arguments, defense, and debates.

• In these poetic contexts, words are described as things that can be "weighed," "restrained," "multiplied," or "poured out." This creates a word picture of speech as something tangible and measurable, representing the outward overflow of a person's inner thoughts and character.

• When used of God or His inspired messengers, millah represents the reliable, perfect expression of truth.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of millah in the context of creation is profound. It demonstrates that God's general revelation is not passive. Creation does not merely exist as a silent monument; it actively communicates. The cosmos acts as a divine preacher, uttering "words" that declare the glory, power, and wisdom of the Creator. This establishes the biblical truth that all human beings are without excuse, because the silent "words" of creation have reached every corner of the globe, bypassing language barriers and cultural divides.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 19:1–4, David presents a beautiful literary paradox. In verse 3, he writes, "There is no speech nor language where their voice is not heard." Yet, in verse 4, he immediately declares, "Their line has gone out through all the earth, and their words (millah) to the end of the world."

While the heavens lack physical vocal cords and human vocabulary, their silent testimony is so clear, orderly, and constant that it functions as actual "words." The day-to-day and night-to-night rhythm of the cosmos is an eloquent sermon. The nuance of millah here is that creation's silent testimony is just as informative and authoritative as spoken language.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• 2 Samuel 23:2: "The Spirit of the LORD spoke by me, and His word was on my tongue."

• Job 4:4: "Your words have upheld him who was falling, and you have strengthened the feeble knees."

• Job 38:2: "Who is this who darkens counsel by words without knowledge?"

• Psalm 139:4: "For there is not a word on my tongue, but behold, O LORD, You know it altogether."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the New Testament, the Apostle Paul quotes Psalm 19:4 in Romans 10:18 to describe the spread of the gospel message: "Their voice has gone out to all the earth, and their words to the ends of the world." In doing so, Paul links the universal, non-verbal "words" (millah) of creation with the verbal proclamation of the gospel (rhema in Greek). Both revelations are global in scope, designed by God to ensure that His truth is made known to all mankind.
