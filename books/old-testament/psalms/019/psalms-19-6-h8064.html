HEAVEN - ( shamayim , Strong's H8064 )

Summary:

In Psalm 19:6, the word "heaven" (shamayim) refers to the visible sky or expanse above the earth through which the sun travels. While the term can denote the spiritual dwelling place of God, here it serves a physical and observational function, highlighting the vast, all-encompassing reach of the sun's journey from one horizon to the other. This physical reality serves as a powerful metaphor for the pervasive, inescapable reach of God's revelation, transitioning from the silent, global testimony of creation to the perfect, soul-restoring power of His written Law.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The Hebrew word shamayim is structurally a dual noun (ending in the suffix -ayim), suggesting a pair or a vast, matching expanse, often conceptualized as the overarching dome or vault of the sky.

• It conveys the physical space above the earth—the atmosphere where birds fly and clouds gather, as well as the higher cosmic realm where the sun, moon, and stars reside.

• Historically, it represents heights, loftiness, and the visible limits of human observation, pointing to a reality far grander than earthly existence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The word shamayim denotes the sky, the physical heavens, or the atmospheric expanse above the earth.

• It signifies the visible heavens where meteorological phenomena occur, such as rain, dew, and wind.

• It refers to the astronomical heavens, the realm of the sun, moon, and stars.

• It designates the unique, transcendent dwelling place of God, the spiritual heaven from which He rules and observes humanity.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The dual form implies an immense, dual-horizoned canopy stretching from east to west, wrapping the earth.

• It is frequently paired with "earth" (erets) to form a merism ("heavens and earth"), representing the entirety of creation.

• The physical heavens are often personified as witnesses to God's covenant, or as active participants in praise, declaring the glory of their Creator.

• The term also depicts a canopy or tent, emphasizing God's effortless sovereignty in stretching out the cosmos.

4. Theological Implications

• The physical heaven serves as a primary canvas for general revelation. By housing the sun and stars, it continuously communicates God's power, order, and majesty to all humanity without spoken words.

• It establishes the vast distinction between the Creator and the creation. Heaven is high above the earth, symbolizing God's holiness, transcendence, and sovereign authority over human affairs.

• It connects physical light with spiritual truth. Just as the physical sky is completely dominated and warmed by the sun, the spiritual life of humanity is intended to be illuminated and ordered by God's perfect law.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Psalm 19:6, shamayim is used in its physical, astronomical sense. The psalmist describes the sun rising from one "end of heaven" and making its "circuit to the other end of it."

• This geographical vastness emphasizes that no part of the earth is hidden from the sun's heat.

• Contextually, this physical omnipresence of the sun's heat prepares the reader for the second half of the psalm (verses 7-11), which highlights the spiritual omnipresence and penetrating power of the Law of the Lord. Just as nothing is hidden from the sun's heat under the heaven, nothing in the human heart is hidden from the searching, purifying light of God’s Word.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:1: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth."

• Psalm 19:1: "The heavens declare the glory of God; And the firmament shows His handiwork."

• Psalm 115:16: "The heaven, even the heavens, are the LORD’s; But the earth He has given to the children of men."

• Deuteronomy 10:14: "Indeed heaven and the highest heavens belong to the LORD your God, also the earth with all that is in it."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Hebrew concept of shamayim avoids the mythological traps of surrounding ancient Near Eastern cultures, which often deified the sky or the sun. In Hebrew thought, the heaven is merely a creature, a "handiwork" of God, and a servant carrying His light.

• The movement of the sun across the heaven in Psalm 19:6 uses poetic, phenomenological language (describing how things appear from an earthly perspective) rather than scientific mechanics, making the theological truth universally accessible to all generations.
