WISE - ( chakam , Strong's H2449 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb chakam refers to the acquisition, possession, or application of practical, moral, and spiritual wisdom. In Psalm 19:7, it describes the transformative power of God's testimony, which takes the "simple"—the open-minded, naive, or easily misled individual—and imparts to them a divine skill for living. This wisdom is not merely intellectual or academic; it is a practical, life-orienting discernment that enables a person to navigate the moral complexities of life in alignment with God’s character and will. The Word of God actively infuses the receptive soul with this vital skill.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, wisdom was not conceptualized as abstract philosophical speculation, but as a practical craftsmanship. The root noun chokmah was used to describe the technical skills of artisans, the tactical intelligence of military commanders, and the navigational capabilities of sailors. To be chakam (the verb form) is to develop the necessary competence to build a life that stands firm against storms and trials. The word picture is that of a master builder or a skilled navigator. When applied to the moral and spiritual life, chakam is the learned capability to steer one’s soul safely through the treacherous waters of temptation, foolishness, and judgment.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb chakam means to become wise, to act wisely, or to show oneself wise. It belongs to a semantic domain representing cognitive, ethical, and practical competence.

• In the active Qal stem, it denotes the state of being wise or the process of gaining wisdom through experience, discipline, and teaching.

• In the causative Hiphil stem (which is the exact form used in Psalm 19:7, makimat), it means to make someone wise, to impart wisdom, or to instruct.

• In the intensive Piel stem, it means to teach wisdom or to make wise.

The word does not signify mere intellectual brightness or raw IQ. Instead, it refers to a moral-cognitive capacity that translates directly into righteous living, sound decision-making, and reverence for God.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout wisdom literature, chakam is consistently contrasted with the posture of the fool (kesil) or the simpleton (peti).

• The path of chakam is depicted as a straight, well-lit highway that avoids the hidden traps, moral pitfalls, and sudden ruins that swallow up the foolish.

• It is also intimately connected to the ear. In biblical thought, to be made wise is to be teachable. One cannot be chakam without a willingness to listen, receive correction, and submit to instruction.

• The word is frequently associated with the "fear of the Lord," highlighting that true wisdom begins when a human being yields their autonomy to the Creator.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, chakam reminds the believer that true wisdom is a divine commodity, not a human achievement. Human beings do not possess the inherent resources to navigate life righteously on their own. Left to human devices, our "wisdom" degenerates into self-deception and moral folly.

By anchoring the action of "making wise" in the "testimony of the Lord," the text reveals that God has externalized His wisdom in written revelation. The Scriptures are the repository of divine skill. When we submit our minds to God’s Word, we are not just learning facts about God; we are being actively re-engineered to think His thoughts after Him and to live skillfully in His world.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 19:7, the psalmist pairs two parallel declarations: "The law of the Lord is perfect, converting the soul; The testimony of the Lord is sure, making wise the simple."

The target of this divine wisdom is the "simple" (peti). The Hebrew root of peti suggests an open door. The simple person is open-minded in the worst sense—lacking boundaries, easily swayed by every passing wind of doctrine, gullible, and highly vulnerable to spiritual danger.

The instrument of transformation is the "testimony of the Lord" (edut), which is described as "sure" (ne'eman—stable, trustworthy, verified). When the unstable, open-minded simpleton encounters the rock-solid, unchanging truth of God's Word, a transformation occurs. The causative verb makimat ("making wise") acts upon the simple person, closing the open door of vulnerability and establishing a firm boundary of discernment. The naive person is transformed into a skilled moral navigator.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Proverbs 6:6: "Go to the ant, you sluggard! Consider her ways and be wise,"

• Proverbs 19:20: "Listen to counsel and receive instruction, That you may be wise in your latter end."

• Psalm 119:98: "You, through Your commandments, make me wiser than my enemies; For they are ever with me."

• Proverbs 20:1: "Wine is a mocker, Strong drink is a brawler, And whoever is led astray by it is not wise."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a direct theological bridge between chakam in Psalm 19:7 and the Greek verb sophizo in the New Testament. In 2 Timothy 3:15, Paul tells Timothy "that from childhood you have known the Holy Scriptures, which are able to make you wise (sophisai) for salvation through faith which is in Christ Jesus."

The Septuagint (LXX) uses this same Greek verb sophizo to translate chakam in Psalm 19:7. This demonstrates a seamless biblical continuity: the Old Testament scriptures "make wise" the simple, and those same scriptures "make wise" the sinner to see their need for Christ and receive His salvation.
