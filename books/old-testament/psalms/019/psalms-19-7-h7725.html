CONVERTING - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

In Psalm 19:7, the English word "converting" translates the rich Hebrew verb shub, which means to turn back, return, restore, or refresh. Within this poetic context, it describes the supernatural capacity of God's perfect Word to revive a weary spirit, restore a broken life, and turn a wandering soul back to its Creator. This word represents a holistic transformation—bringing vitality, spiritual renewal, and a complete realignment of one's life toward God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical background of the Hebrew verb shub lies in the action of turning around to go back to a starting point, returning to a home country, or retreating along a path. It portrays a traveler who has taken a wrong turn, realized the error, and turned back to find the right path. Spiritually, this physical image translates into the biblical concept of repentance and restoration. It is the movement of a person returning to covenant faithfulness with God after wandering astray.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shub is one of the most frequent verbs in the Old Testament, occurring over 1,000 times. Its basic active meaning is "to turn back" or "return." In the specific grammatical form used in Psalm 19:7 (the Hiphil causative participle), the verb means "to cause to return," "to bring back," or "to restore." It refers to the act of restoring something to its original, healthy state or refreshing someone who is exhausted. The lexical range spans physical restoration, spiritual conversion, and the emotional refreshing of a weary person.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Returning: Used of returning to the dust of the earth at death, or returning to one's home after a journey.

• Spiritual Repentance: Used extensively by the Hebrew prophets to call Israel to "turn back" to Yahweh from their idols and wicked deeds.

• Vital Restoration: Used to describe the reviving of physical strength, the restoration of life to a dead child, or the refreshing of a person's inner vitality. The word picture is that of a dying embers being fanned back into a roaring flame.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shub connects the power of God's written revelation with the work of spiritual regeneration and sanctification. The Scripture is not merely an intellectual textbook; it is a living, active tool that the Holy Spirit uses to regenerate the spiritually dead and revive the spiritually exhausted. The use of this word in relation to the "soul" (nephesh, meaning the entire inner life, appetite, and seat of vitality) implies that the Word of God is totally sufficient to completely realign, rebuild, and re-energize the total person.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 19:7, the phrase "converting the soul" sits in close parallel with "making wise the simple." The Hebrew word for "perfect" (tamim) means complete, sound, and without blemish. Because God's Word is perfect and complete, it has the unique ability to fully restore (shub) the soul. It takes a soul that is bent, broken, or exhausted by the trials of life and the weight of sin, and it turns it back to a state of spiritual health, joy, and vigor. It does not merely refer to a one-time conversion experience, but also to the daily, ongoing restoration and revival of the believer's inner life.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 23:3 - "He restores [shub] my soul; He leads me in the paths of righteousness For His name’s sake."

• Psalm 51:13 - "Then I will teach transgressors Your ways, And sinners shall be converted [shub] to You."

• Lamentations 5:21 - "Turn [shub] us back to You, O Lord, and we will be restored; Renew our days as of old."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In classical Hebrew thought, there is no sharp division between the physical and the spiritual. When the NKJV translates shub as "converting," it captures the spiritual reality of a life turned back to God. However, when compared with the pastoral reality of Psalm 23:3 ("He restores my soul"), we see that "converting" also carries the deep sense of comforting and refreshing the saint who is crushed by affliction. The Word of God meets both needs: it brings the sinner to repentance (conversion) and brings the suffering saint back to spiritual strength (restoration).
