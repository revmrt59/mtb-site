ENLIGHTENING - ( or , Strong's H215 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb or (specifically used in the causative Hiphil stem in Psalm 19:8 as me'irat) means to shine, give light, or illuminate. In the context of Scripture, it represents the life-giving, direction-imparting, and joy-producing power of God's Word. When the Psalmist declares that the commandment of the Lord is "enlightening the eyes," he refers to how divine truth cuts through moral and spiritual darkness, reviving the weary soul and granting clear, spiritual perception for daily living.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, light was the ultimate symbol of life, order, safety, and divine favor, while darkness represented chaos, danger, ignorance, and death. The Hebrew root or is first introduced in Genesis 1:3 at the very dawn of creation when God commanded, "Let there be light."

The word picture behind "enlightening the eyes" is deeply experiential. It is the image of a traveler lost in a pitch-black, treacherous wilderness who is suddenly handed a bright, blazing torch. The path is instantly made clear, and the panic of uncertainty is replaced by the joy of safe passage. Additionally, in Hebrew thought, the "brightening" or "enlightening" of the eyes speaks of physical and emotional rejuvenation. When a person is completely exhausted, starving, or near death, their eyes grow dim. When they receive nourishment and life-giving hope, their eyes literally light up with restored vitality.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root verb or means to become light, to shine, or to be illuminated. In the Hiphil (causative) stem used in Psalm 19:8, it means to cause to shine, to kindle, to illuminate, or to direct light upon an object.

When the object of this illumination is the "eyes," the term goes beyond mere intellectual instruction. It denotes:

• The restoration of spiritual vitality and strength to a weary soul.

• The bestowal of spiritual discernment to see reality from God's perspective.

• The removal of moral blindness, allowing a person to walk in uprightness and avoid the hidden traps of sin.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, or is used in three primary ways that help illuminate its theological depth in Psalm 19:

• The Dawn of a New Day: It describes the rising sun breaking over the horizon, systematically dispelling the shadows of the night. God’s Word does this for a mind clouded by confusion or sorrow.

• The Nourishment of the Weary: It is used to describe the immediate physical revival that occurs when a starving person tastes sweet food. Their eyes, once glazed and failing, instantly "light up" with life.

• The Favor of the King: It describes the shining of a ruler’s face, particularly the priestly blessing where God "makes His face shine upon you" (Numbers 6:25). To have illuminated eyes is to live under the joyful, smiling favor of God.

### 4. Theological Implications

The declaration that God's Word is "enlightening the eyes" carries major theological weight:

• The Necessity of Revelation: Human reason is not a sufficient light in a fallen world. Because human nature is corrupted by sin, our spiritual eyes are naturally blind. We cannot generate our own light; true illumination must come from an objective source outside of ourselves—the revealed Word of God.

• The Purity of Scripture: The Psalm links "enlightening" with "pure" (barah—clean, clear, bright). The reason God's Word can illuminate our lives without distorting our vision is that it is completely untainted by error, bias, or deceit. It is pure light.

• The Spiritual-Physical Connection: True spiritual enlightenment does not lead to abstract, mystical disengagement. Instead, it affects the whole person, bringing joy to the heart, health to the body, and practical wisdom to daily behavior.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 19, David constructs a beautifully balanced hymn comparing the general revelation of God in nature (verses 1–6) with the special revelation of God in Scripture (verses 7–11).

In verse 8, David writes: "The commandment of the Lord is pure, enlightening the eyes." The term "commandment" (mitswah) refers to God's authoritative, direct orders. While the word "commandment" might sound restrictive or heavy to the human ear, David shows that God's laws are actually a supreme gift. Because they are "pure" (radiant and free from the darkness of self-interest), they do not blind or oppress us. Instead, they act as a spiritual salve, curing our moral blindness, clearing away the fog of worldly philosophy, and showing us exactly how to walk in a way that pleases the Creator.

• Psalm 13:3 - "Consider and hear me, O Lord my God; Enlighten my eyes, lest I sleep the sleep of death;"

• Psalm 119:130 - "The entrance of Your words gives light; It gives understanding to the simple."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

This Old Testament concept finds its ultimate fulfillment in the New Testament person of Jesus Christ. The Greek equivalent of this illumination is photizo (to give light, render evident, or enlighten). John’s Gospel introduces Jesus as "the true Light which gives light to every man coming into the world" (John 1:9).

Paul picks up this exact imagery in Ephesians 1:18, praying that "the eyes of your understanding being enlightened" you may know the hope of His calling. Thus, when we read the pure Word of God, the Holy Spirit actively performs a work of "enlightening"—taking the objective light of Scripture and subjective-ly applying it to our hearts, enabling us to see and savor the beauty of Jesus Christ.
