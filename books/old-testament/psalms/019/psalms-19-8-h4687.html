COMMANDMENT - ( mitsvah , Strong's H4687 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word mitsvah refers to an authoritative instruction, charge, or commandment given by a sovereign. In the context of Psalm 19:8, "the commandment of the Lord" represents God's specific, binding directions for life that are "pure," clean, and unadulterated. Rather than being a heavy burden, this divine command acts as a guide that clears away moral darkness, bringing spiritual discernment and "enlightening the eyes" to see reality as God sees it.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word mitsvah is deeply rooted in the concept of covenantal authority and relationship. It derives from the verb tsavah, which carries the sense of laying a charge upon someone, commissioning them, or establishing a boundary. In the ancient Near East, a monarch would issue a mitsvah to set the order of the kingdom and protect its citizens. In the biblical context, God's commandments are not arbitrary rules designed to restrict freedom; rather, they are protective boundary markers set up by a loving Sovereign. They are designed to keep His people on the path of life, preserving them from the destructive cliffs of sin.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term mitsvah denotes a mandate, precept, or obligation imposed by an authority. It can refer to a single commandment, a collection of laws, or the entire body of divine instruction given to Israel. In its broadest sense, it describes the concrete expression of God's will for human behavior. Unlike general wisdom, a mitsvah carries the weight of moral obligation; it is a direct order that requires a response of obedience or disobedience.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The word occurs over 180 times in the Old Testament, heavily concentrated in Deuteronomy and Psalm 119.

• It is frequently paired with other terms for God's revelation, such as "law" (torah), "statutes" (chuqqim), and "testimonies" (edot), highlighting different facets of God's spoken will.

• A prominent word picture associated with mitsvah is that of a path or a lamp. It functions as a guiding light in a dark wilderness, showing the traveler exactly where to step to avoid stumbling.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereign Authority: The existence of mitsvah asserts that Yahweh is the ultimate King who has the right to dictate human conduct.

• Moral Clarity: Because God is holy, His commandments provide an absolute, unchanging standard of righteousness. They remove subjective moral ambiguity.

• The Graciousness of Law: God does not leave humanity to guess how to please Him or how to live optimally. The commandment is a gift of grace, revealing the Creator's design for human flourishing.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Psalm 19:8, the singular "commandment of the LORD" (mitsvat YHWH) is described as "pure" (barah), meaning clear, bright, clean, or unmixed. It is free from the impurities of human self-interest, compromise, or error.

• The effect of this pure commandment is "enlightening the eyes." In Hebrew thought, dim eyes represent physical exhaustion, despair, or spiritual blindness. When God's pure commandment enters a person's life, it acts like a clarifying light that restores vitality, grants deep moral discernment, and allows them to see spiritual realities clearly. It clears away the fog of cultural deception and personal confusion.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 26:5 - "because Abraham obeyed My voice and kept My charge, My commandments, My statutes, and My laws."

• Deuteronomy 6:1 - "Now this is the commandment, and these are the statutes and judgments which the LORD your God has commanded you to teach you, that you may observe them in the land which you are crossing over to possess,"

• Psalm 119:98 - "You, through Your commandments, make me wiser than my enemies; For they are ever with me."

• Proverbs 6:23 - "For the commandment is a lamp, And the law a light; Reproofs of instruction are the way of life,"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Unlike human laws, which can be burdensome, inconsistent, or unjust, God's mitsvah is perfectly aligned with His good character.

• In the New Testament, Jesus summarizes all the mitsvot (commandments) into two great commands: to love God and to love one's neighbor. This demonstrates that the ultimate goal of every commandment is love.

• Keeping the commandments is never presented in Scripture as a way to earn salvation, but rather as the joyful response of a redeemed person living in covenant security.
