ANSWER - ( 'anah , Strong's H6030 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament scriptures, the Hebrew verb 'anah represents far more than a verbal reply; it signifies a decisive, active response that meets a specific need. In times of distress, when a believer cries out, God's "answering" is His powerful intervention, deliverance, and personal presence breaking into human helplessness. It is the assurance that the Sovereign Lord is not silent, distant, or indifferent, but actively engaged in rescuing His people.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew root 'anah has several homonyms in the Old Testament, which include meanings such as "to be afflicted, bowed down" or "to sing." The primary root used in the context of communication refers to responding, declaring, or reacting to a stimulus. In the ancient Near East, when a deity or a king "answered" a petitioner, it was a formal, legal, and relational act. It did not merely mean speaking words in return; it meant granting an audience, rendering a favorable verdict, and extending sovereign protection. The word picture is one of a superior bending down to hear, pay attention to, and actively resolve the crisis of a subordinate who has cried out for help.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb 'anah primarily means to answer, respond, testify, or speak in return. In a relational or prayer context, it denotes responding to a cry for help. The definition extends beyond cognitive or verbal communication; it is existential and operational. To answer is to grant a request, deliver from trouble, or manifest presence. It carries the nuance of paying attention, responding with authority, and providing a favorable resolution to a crisis.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb occurs frequently alongside the Hebrew word for calling (qara'). This established pairing ("call... and I will answer") highlights the dialogical and covenantal relationship between God and humanity.

• The Courtroom Response: 'anah is used in legal contexts to describe giving testimony or responding to charges. When God answers, He acts as the supreme Advocate and Judge who vindicates His people.

• The Sovereign's Favor: It represents a king listening to a subject's petition and issuing a decree of release or assistance.

• The Responsive Father: It mirrors a parent responding to a child's cry of distress, where the response is immediate, physical, and protective.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of 'anah rests on the character of God as a prayer-hearing and prayer-answering deity.

• Contrast with Idols: The living God of Israel is defined by His ability to answer, directly contrasting with the silent, impotent idols of the nations who cannot hear or respond.

• Covenant Loyalty: God's answers are not arbitrary; they are rooted in His covenant loyalty (chesed). His response is guaranteed by His own character and promises, not by the merit of the petitioner.

• Immanence and Sovereignty: The act of answering demonstrates that God is both transcendent (reigning in heaven) and immanent (actively involved in history on behalf of His people).

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 20:1, the setting is a corporate liturgical prayer for King David before he leads the nation's army into battle. The phrase "May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble" carries specific weight:

• The Day of Trouble: The "day of trouble" (yom tsarah) refers to a time of tight distress, confinement, or military crisis.

• Action Over Words: The king does not need a verbal explanation from God; he needs military deliverance, protection, and victory on the battlefield. Therefore, the plea for God to "answer" is a cry for tangible, saving intervention.

• Poetic Parallelism: The petition "answer you" is structurally parallel to "defend you" (literally, "set you on high" out of the reach of enemies). God’s answer is functionally equivalent to His physical protection and vindication of the king before his adversaries.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 35:3: "Then let us arise and go up to Bethel; and I will make an altar there to God, who answered me in the day of my distress and has been with me in the way which I have gone."

• Psalm 22:21: "Save me from the lion’s mouth And from the horns of the wild oxen! You have answered me."

• Psalm 91:15: "He shall call upon Me, and I will answer him; I will be with him in trouble; I will deliver him and honor him."

• Isaiah 58:9: "Then you shall call, and the LORD will answer; You shall cry, and He will say, ‘Here I am.’"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The greatest fear of the Old Testament believer was not suffering itself, but the silence of God. When God does not answer, it indicates judgment, hidden favor, or broken fellowship. Consequently, when God answers, it signals the restoration of relationship, the breaking of silence, and the active presence of grace.
