DEFEND - ( sagab , Strong's H7682 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sagab literally means to be high, inaccessibly high, or exalted. In the context of divine protection, it paints a vivid picture of God lifting a believer far above the reach of danger, placing them in a secure, high fortress where the enemy cannot touch them. In Psalm 20:1, the psalmist prays that the "name of the God of Jacob" would defend—or set on high—the king in the day of trouble, reminding us that true security is found not in earthly armaments, but in the exalted and unassailable position of being kept by God Himself.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, warfare was heavily defined by topography. Cities were built on hills, and fortresses were constructed on high, rocky cliffs. To be "on high" (sagab) was to be in a position of safety, far above the reach of arrows, spears, and battering rams. The word evokes the image of a steep, rugged mountain peak or a high stone tower (misgab). When applied to God's defense of His people, it signifies that God does not merely stand as a shield on the level ground of the battlefield; He snatches the believer up and places them on a high, unassailable cliff where the enemy's weapons are rendered utterly useless.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The root meaning of sagab is to be high, elevated, or inaccessible.

• In its basic active sense (Qal), it refers to being too high to capture, reach, or comprehend.

• In the intensive and causative senses (Piel and Hiphil), it means to set on high, to make securely high, to exalt, or to defend by placing out of reach.

• In the passive sense (Niphal), it describes that which is exalted, lofty, or safely out of reach.

• Ultimately, the word captures the dual reality of exaltation (praising God's lofty status) and protection (God placing His people in a lofty, safe position).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Inaccessible Fortress: The word is frequently used to describe a high tower or refuge (misgab, derived from the same root). To be defended by God is to be placed in a spiritual high tower.

• The Safe Heights: It contrasts with the lowlands of vulnerability. In the lowlands, the believer is exposed to the arrows of adversity. By sagab, God lifts the believer into the heavenly heights of His sovereign care.

• The Exalted Savior: The word also applies to God Himself being exalted. His name alone is excellent, and His power is high above all nations.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereignty and Transcendence: Because God is transcendent (highly exalted), He has the authority and power to lift His people above their earthly crises.

• The Sufficiency of the Name: In Psalm 20:1, it is the name of the God of Jacob that defends. In Hebrew thought, the name represents the very character, presence, and covenant faithfulness of God. The covenant name of Yahweh is itself an impenetrable fortress.

• Grace to the Lowly: The God of Jacob—the God of a flawed, struggling patriarch—is the one who lifts the weak and vulnerable to safety. This demonstrates that divine defense is not based on human merit but on covenant grace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• Psalm 20 is a liturgical prayer offered by the congregation for the king (specifically David) as he prepares to go out to battle.

• The "day of trouble" refers to the imminent danger of military conflict.

• While the enemy trusts in chariots and horses (Psalm 20:7), the congregation prays that the King will be defended by the "name of the God of Jacob."

• The use of sagab here is a deliberate play on defense. Instead of trusting in the height of physical city walls or the strength of a human shield, the king's ultimate defense is being set on high by God's personal covenant name. It promises that though the battle may rage on the plains below, the king is spiritually positioned in an unassailable fortress.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Proverbs 18:10: "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; The righteous run to it and are safe [H7682, sagab]."

• Psalm 59:1: "Deliver me from my enemies, O my God; Defend [H7682, sagab] me from those who rise up against me."

• Psalm 91:14: "Because he has set his love upon Me, therefore I will deliver him; I will set him on high [H7682, sagab], because he has known My name."

• Psalm 107:41: "Yet He sets the poor on high [H7682, sagab], far from affliction, And makes their families like a flock."

• Isaiah 26:5: "For He brings down those who dwell on high, The lofty [H7682, sagab] city; He lays it low, He lays it low to the ground, He brings it down to the dust."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) translates sagab in Psalm 20:1 using the verb hyperaspizo, which means "to shield" or "to defend." This highlights the protective aspect of the word, assuring the believer that being "set on high" is the ultimate form of divine shielding.

• The reference to "the God of Jacob" is highly intentional. Jacob was a deceiver, a man of troubles, constantly fleeing or facing crisis. Yet God was his defense. Calling Him the "God of Jacob" reminds the believer that God protects and exalts those who are weak, stumbling, and in deep need of mercy.
