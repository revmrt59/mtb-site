ACCEPT - ( dasen , Strong's H1878 )

Summary:

In Psalm 20:3, the Hebrew word translated "accept" is dasen, a rich term deeply connected to the concepts of abundance, oiliness, and fatness. Rather than a sterile, formal transaction, this word carries the vivid imagery of turning a sacrifice to ashes or finding it "fat"—meaning robust, highly valuable, and deeply pleasing to God. In the context of ancient worship, the "fat" was the most precious portion reserved exclusively for the Lord. When the psalmist prays for God to "accept" the king's offerings, he is asking the Lord to look upon the sacrifices with pleasure, finding them sweet, fully satisfying, and worthy of a favorable response.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the agricultural world of the ancient Near East, fatness was the ultimate symbol of health, fertility, wealth, and prosperity. A lean animal or a dry harvest indicated scarcity and struggle, whereas a "fat" animal or a field rich with oil represented the very best of God's blessing. This cultural reality directly influenced Israel's worship. Under the Mosaic Law, the fat of the peace offering belonged entirely to God and was burned on the altar as a sweet aroma.

The word dasen draws on this background. It paints a picture of a sacrifice that is not meager, half-hearted, or diseased, but rather one that is rich, complete, and brimming with the worshiper's best. Additionally, dasen relates to the process of consuming the sacrifice on the altar, turning it to ashes as a sign that the Lord has fully consumed and received the offering.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb dasen fundamentally means to grow fat, to become oily, to satisfy, or to make prosperous. In its basic form, it describes the physical state of being well-fed or anointed.

In the specific grammatical form used in Psalm 20:3 (the Piel stem), the verb takes on a causative and declarative meaning. It means "to regard as fat," "to accept as fat," or "to turn to ashes." In sacrificial contexts, this refers to God looking upon an offering, declaring it to be "fat" (highly acceptable), and consuming it with the fire of His presence. It denotes a warm, favorable, and highly pleased reception.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses dasen in several descriptive ways to illustrate abundance and divine satisfaction:

• Anointing and Blessing: It describes the pouring of rich oil over a guest's head, signifying honor, joy, and the overflowing presence of the Holy Spirit.

• Inward Strength and Vitality: It describes physical and spiritual health, where a person’s bones are made "fat" or healthy by receiving good news or walking in wisdom.

• Spiritual Prosperity: It characterizes the life of the generous and diligent soul who trusts in God and is subsequently "made rich" or "prospered" in every way.

• Sacrificial Acceptance: It depicts the holy fire of God consuming the choice portions on the altar, leaving behind the ashes as physical proof of divine acceptance.

4. Theological Implications

The use of dasen in Psalm 20:3 reveals deep theological truths about worship and prayer:

• God Desires Our Best: God does not merely tolerate our worship; He delights in it when it is offered in faith and sincerity. He looks for "fat" sacrifices—offerings of our time, energy, and hearts that cost us something and reflect His worth.

• God Initiates the Acceptance: The transition of an offering from an earthly gift to a heavenly acceptance requires divine action. When God "makes it fat" or "turns it to ashes," He is actively receiving the worship.

• The Role of the Ultimate Sacrifice: This word points forward to the sacrifice of Jesus Christ on the cross. His offering was the ultimate "fat" sacrifice—completely spotless, perfectly rich in love and obedience, and fully consumed by God's justice. Because God accepted His sacrifice, we are forever accepted in Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 20 is a community prayer offered on behalf of the king (David) as he prepares to lead Israel into a dangerous battle. The congregation gathers at the sanctuary to pray for his protection and victory.

In verse 3, the people cry out, "May He remember all your offerings, and accept [dasen] your burnt sacrifice." The contextual nuance is clear: the success of the military campaign depends entirely on the king’s standing with God. If the Lord "finds his sacrifices fat"—meaning He accepts the king's worship and recognizes his humble dependence on Yahweh—then the battle is already won. The acceptance of worship is the indispensable foundation for answered prayer and victory over the enemy.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The word dasen serves as a powerful diagnostic tool for our personal worship. It contrasts sharply with the begrudging, routine offerings condemned by the prophets in later centuries. For instance, in Malachi, the priests offered blind, lame, and sick animals—sacrifices that were "lean" and worthless.

In contrast, dasen reminds us that true worship is costly, vibrant, and satisfying to the heart of God. When we pray, serve, or give, we must ask ourselves: Is this a "fat" offering? Is it rich with faith, love, and devotion, or is it merely the leftovers of our lives?
