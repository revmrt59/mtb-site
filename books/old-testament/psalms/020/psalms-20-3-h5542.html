SELAH - ( selah , Strong's H5542 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word selah is a liturgical and musical notation found predominantly in the Psalms. While its precise technical definition remains a subject of discussion, its root meaning points to "lifting up"—either a lifting up of the voice in praise, a swelling of the musical accompaniment, or a deliberate pause for the listener to lift up their heart in quiet reflection. In the context of worship, it serves as a holy punctuation mark, inviting believers to stop, breathe, and deeply ponder the weight of the truths just sung or spoken.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The background of selah is deeply rooted in the ancient temple worship of Israel. The word is primarily a musical and liturgical signpost used by the choir director and musicians. It occurs 74 times in the Old Testament—71 times in the book of Psalms and 3 times in the prayer of Habakkuk (chapter 3).

A helpful word picture comes from its likely etymological connection to the Hebrew verb salal, which means "to lift up" or "to cast up a highway." In ancient times, when a king was coming, workers would build up a road, clearing away rocks and obstacles so the king’s journey would be smooth and prominent. In a worship setting, selah functions as a spiritual highway-builder. It is a call to clear away the mental debris of daily life, raise the music or the voice, and elevate the thoughts of the congregation toward the majesty of God.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Selah is a technical term used in the Hebrew Bible to direct the singer or reader. Its meaning comprises several complementary nuances:

• A Pause or Interlude: It represents a suspension of the vocal song to allow the stringed instruments to play a solo, or a complete silence for quiet contemplation.

• An Exaltation or Lifting Up: Derived from salal, it denotes a lifting up of the hands, voices, or musical volume (a crescendo) to emphasize the greatness of the statement just made.

• An Eternal Affirmation: The ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) translates selah as diapsalma, indicating a musical interlude or a division in the song. Other ancient translations, such as the Targums, render it as "forever" or "always," suggesting that the truth being paused over has eternal significance.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The usage of selah in Hebrew poetry acts like a modern musical rest or a dramatic pause in a theatrical performance.

• The Musical Bridge: In the temple orchestra, led by the sons of Asaph, the singers would pause their voices, and the harps, lyres, and cymbals would swell in volume. This instrumental transition allowed the words of the previous stanza to echo in the minds of the listeners.

• The Sacred Stop Sign: It functions as a visual and auditory cue that says, "Do not rush past this. Stop, breathe, and think about what you just confessed."

### 4. Theological Implications

The presence of selah in Scripture teaches several profound theological truths:

• The Value of Meditation: God does not desire mindless repetition or hurried worship. Selah reveals that spiritual digestion requires time. Truth must not only be heard; it must be chewed and internalized.

• Worship as a Dialogue: When the congregation sings and then pauses at a selah, they are invited to listen to how God is speaking to their hearts through the lyrics. It balances our speaking to God with our listening to God.

• The Gravity of God's Character and Deeds: Selah is most frequently placed after heavy theological statements—such as declarations of God’s judgment, His deliverance, or human frailty. It signals that some truths are too heavy to be glossed over; they demand a pause out of sheer reverence.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 20:3, the congregation is praying a blessing over the king before he goes out to battle. The text reads: "Remember all your offerings, And accept your burnt sacrifice. Selah"

The contextual placement of selah here is highly significant:

• The Gravity of Sacrifice: The prayer asks God to "remember" (zakar) the king's grain offerings (minchah) and "accept" (literally, "turn to ashes," signifying divine acceptance by fire) his burnt sacrifice (olah). The selah immediately follows this plea.

• An Appeal for Divine Favor: It creates a dramatic pause during which the worshipers would watch the smoke of the sacrifice ascend to heaven. The congregation stops singing to contemplate the necessity of blood atonement and the burning sacrifice. It is a moment of quiet, fearful anticipation: Will God accept the offering? Will He grant victory in the upcoming battle?

• Elevating the Prayer: It serves as a musical crescendo, perhaps accompanied by a blast of priestly trumpets, lifting this crucial petition directly to the throne of God.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 3:2: "Many are they who say of my soul, 'There is no help for him in God.' Selah" (A pause to feel the weight of the enemy's mocking before David immediately counters with, "But You, O LORD, are a shield for me.")

• Psalm 46:3: "Though its waters roar and be troubled, Though the mountains shake with its swelling. Selah" (An invitation to pause and find quiet rest in God’s sovereignty amidst cosmic and global chaos.)

• Habakkuk 3:3: "God came from Teman, The Holy One from Mount Paran. Selah His glory covered the heavens, And the earth was full of His praise." (A pause to contemplate the terrifying and magnificent physical manifestation of God's glory.)

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

For the modern believer and pastor, selah is an antidote to the hurry of contemporary life. In a culture driven by constant noise, information overload, and rapid transitions, the biblical concept of selah calls the church back to quietness. It reminds the preacher that sermon delivery needs pauses, and it reminds the worship leader that space must be left for the Holy Spirit to work in the silence.
