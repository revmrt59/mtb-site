BURNT SACRIFICE - ( 'olah , Strong's H5930 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun 'olah designates a "burnt offering" or "burnt sacrifice," deriving from a root verb that means "to ascend." Unlike other sacrificial offerings where part of the meat was consumed by the priests or the worshipper, the 'olah was entirely burned upon the altar, symbolizing total surrender, absolute consecration, and undivided devotion to God. In the context of Psalm 20:3, the congregation petitions God to favorably accept the king's burnt sacrifice prior to battle, acknowledging that ultimate victory relies not on human strength, but on God's grace and the leader's complete alignment with His covenant will.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The term 'olah comes from the active participle of the primitive Hebrew root verb 'alah, which means "to ascend," "to climb," or "to go up."

• This etymological root paints a vivid word picture of the offering: as the animal is consumed by the fire on the altar, its essence is converted into smoke and a sweet aroma that physically climbs or ascends upward toward heaven, where God dwells.

• The physical ascent of the smoke represents a bridge between the earthly worshipper and the heavenly Creator, carrying the devotion, prayers, and surrender of the offerer up to God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The word signifies a sacrifice that is wholly burned. In classical Greek translations (such as the Septuagint), this word is rendered as holokautoma, the source of the English word "holocaust," meaning "that which is completely burned."

• It represents an offering of an unblemished animal (a bull, ram, male goat, turtle-dove, or young pigeon) where the entire animal—excluding the hide—is placed on the wood of the altar and reduced to ashes by fire.

• It is distinct from peace offerings (shelem) or sin offerings (hatt'at), where portions of the meat were preserved for the priests or eaten in a communal meal. With the 'olah, nothing is kept; everything belongs to God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The 'olah is the oldest and most prominent sacrifice mentioned in the Hebrew Scriptures, predating the Levitical law. It was offered by Noah after surviving the flood (Genesis 8:20) and by Abraham when tested on Mount Moriah (Genesis 22:2).

• In the tabernacle and temple worship, it served as the daily continual offering (the tamid), sacrificed every morning and evening to maintain the nation's constant standing of devotion and atonement before God.

• Word pictures associated with 'olah include:

• The Ascending Smoke: A representation of a life completely consumed for God’s pleasure, rising as a "sweet aroma" (reah nihah).

• The Unblemished Standard: The requirement for an animal without defect represents offering God our absolute best, not our leftovers.

• The Consuming Fire: The active presence of God's holiness consuming the substitute, signifying both His holy judgment and His merciful acceptance of the worshipper.

4. Theological Implications

• Absolute Surrender: The complete consumption of the sacrifice teaches that God desires undivided loyalty. It is the physical manifestation of loving God with all of one's heart, soul, mind, and strength.

• Substitutionary Atonement: Leviticus 1:4 establishes that the worshipper laid hands on the head of the 'olah so that "it will be accepted on his behalf to make atonement for him." It shows that sinful humanity can only approach a holy God through an innocent, spotless life given in their place.

• Christological Fulfillment: Jesus Christ is the ultimate realization of the 'olah. He offered Himself up without spot to God as a voluntary sacrifice, a "sweet-smelling aroma" (Ephesians 5:2). His devotion to the Father was absolute, holding nothing back, even unto death.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• Psalm 20 is a royal psalm, functioning as a liturgical prayer spoken by the congregation for King David as he prepares to lead Israel's army into battle.

• In Psalm 20:3, the prayer asks: "May He remember all your offerings, And accept your burnt sacrifice."

• The Hebrew word translated "accept" is dashen, which literally means "to turn to ashes" or "make fat."

• In this historical and military context, the congregation prays that God would physically demonstrate His acceptance of the king's 'olah by turning it to ashes—often accomplished in Israel's history by divine fire descending upon the altar. This acceptance would serve as a spiritual green light, assuring the king and his army of God's favor, presence, and guaranteed victory in the upcoming conflict.

• It reminds the king that victory is not achieved by reliance on horses or chariots (v. 7), but through a heart of total surrender represented by the fully consumed sacrifice.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 8:20 - "Then Noah built an altar to the LORD, and took of every clean animal and of every clean bird, and offered burnt offerings on the altar."

• Genesis 22:2 - "Then He said, 'Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah, and offer him there as a burnt offering on one of the mountains of which I shall tell you.'"

• Leviticus 1:3 - "If his offering is a burnt sacrifice of the herd, let him offer a male without blemish; he shall offer it of his own free will at the door of the tabernacle of meeting before the LORD."

• 1 Samuel 15:22 - "So Samuel said: 'Has the LORD as great delight in burnt offerings and sacrifices, As in obeying the voice of the LORD? Behold, to obey is better than sacrifice, And to heed than the fat of rams.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• While other sacrifices deal specifically with restoration after committing a particular sin, the 'olah was often offered as an act of voluntary adoration and general atonement, focusing heavily on the character of the worshipper's ongoing relationship with God.

• Prophetically, God frequently corrected Israel when they performed the outward ritual of the 'olah without the inward reality of obedience (as seen in Isaiah 1:11 and Micah 6:6-8). The physical smoke of the ascending sacrifice is offensive to God if the heart of the worshipper does not likewise ascend to Him in holy living.
