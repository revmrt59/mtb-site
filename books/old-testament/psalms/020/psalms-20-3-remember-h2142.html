REMEMBER - ( zakar , Strong's H2142 )

Summary:

In the Hebrew Scriptures, remembering is far more than a cognitive or mental recall of past events; it is an active, relational term that implies moving on behalf of the one remembered. When God "remembers," He acts in faithfulness to His covenant, demonstrating His favor, mercy, and deliverance. In Psalm 20:3, the psalmist petitions God to actively acknowledge and favorably respond to the king’s offerings, translating His memory into tangible protection and victory on the day of battle.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, memory is not a passive filing cabinet of the mind. To remember (zakar) is to bring a past commitment, relationship, or event into the active present so that it influences current action. The opposite of remembering is not merely forgetting due to a lapse in memory, but actively ignoring, neglecting, or failing to act. When a person or God remembers, they are stirred to do something tangible. For example, when God "remembered" Noah in the ark, He did not suddenly recall Noah's existence after a period of forgetfulness; rather, He initiated the wind to dry up the floodwaters and rescue him. The word picture is one of covenantal engagement—attention directed toward a subject that culminates in saving action.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb zakar primarily means to remember, recall, call to mind, or bring to remembrance. In specific stems, it can mean to make mention of, declare, praise, or confess. Lexically, the word has three primary dimensions:

• Mental reflection: Directing the mind toward a past event or person.

• Verbal proclamation: Speaking or declaring something aloud as a memorial.

• Existential action: Behaving in a way that honors the memory, fulfilling an obligation, or delivering someone from distress.

When applied to God, zakar denotes His attention turning toward His people to grant them grace, deliver them from enemies, or honor His covenant.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Rainbow Covenant: In Genesis, God establishes the rainbow so that He may "remember" His everlasting covenant with all living creatures. The physical sign triggers the divine action of preservation.

• The Memorial Portion: In the Levitical sacrificial system, a portion of the grain offering is called the azkarah (a derivative of zakar), which is burned as a "memorial portion" before the Lord. This act is designed to bring the worshiper to God's favorable attention.

• The Sound of the Trumpet: Israel was commanded to blow trumpets in times of war and during feasts so that they would be "remembered before the Lord your God" and saved from their enemies. The physical sound of the trumpet acts as an audible prayer for God to act.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, zakar is rooted in the character of God as a covenant-keeper. God does not suffer from cognitive limitations; He never truly forgets. Therefore, when Scripture says God remembers, it is an anthropomorphic expression highlighting His faithfulness.

• Covenant Faithfulness: God’s remembering is always tied to His promises. When Israel cried out in Egypt, God "remembered" His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, which immediately set the Exodus in motion.

• The Basis of Salvation: When God chooses "not to remember" our sins, He is not experiencing amnesia; He is choosing not to take legal or punitive action against us based on those sins.

• Human Responsibility: Conversely, humans are called to zakar—to remember the Sabbath, the Law, and God's past deliverances. Remembering God’s deeds guards the heart against rebellion and fosters lifelong worship.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 20:3, the setting is a liturgical prayer offered by the congregation on behalf of the Davidic king before he goes out to battle. The people pray: "May He remember all your offerings, and accept your burnt sacrifice."

• Here, zakar is used in a sacrificial context. The king has offered grain offerings and burnt offerings to secure God's favor.

• The petition "May He remember" is a request for God to look upon these expressions of devotion, acknowledge the king's loyalty, and actively intervene on the battlefield.

• The parallel phrase, "and accept your burnt sacrifice," shows that remembering is synonymous with acceptance. If God remembers the sacrifices, He will respond by granting the king victory over his adversaries.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 8:1 - "Then God remembered Noah, and every living thing, and all the animals that were with him in the ark. And God made a wind to pass over the earth, and the waters subsided."

• Exodus 2:24 - "So God heard their groaning, and God remembered His covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob."

• Psalm 25:7 - "Do not remember the sins of my youth, nor my transgressions; According to Your mercy remember me, For Your goodness’ sake, O Lord."

• Psalm 136:23 - "Who remembered us in our lowly state, For His mercy endures forever;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Hebrew noun zeker refers to a memorial, remembrance, or renown. It represents the lasting impact or reputation of a person's name.

• The name Zechariah (Zekaryah) literally means "Yahweh has remembered," serving as a perpetual testimony to God's active faithfulness.

• In the New Testament, this concept carries over into the Greek verb mnaomai and the noun anamnesis (used by Jesus at the Last Supper: "Do this in remembrance of Me"). To remember Christ's sacrifice in the Lord's Supper is to actively participate in the present benefits of His completed work.
