SALVATION - ( yeshuah , Strong's H3444 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun yeshuah refers to deliverance, victory, safety, and salvation. Rooted in a verb meaning "to be spacious" or "to make wide," it conveys the physical and spiritual reality of being brought out of a tight, oppressive place of danger into a broad place of freedom, security, and triumph. In Psalm 20:5, it expresses the congregation's joyous anticipation of God's saving victory for His anointed king, highlighting that true deliverance comes not from human military might but from the sovereign intervention of Yahweh.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete word picture behind yeshuah is rooted in the verbal root yasha, which means to be wide, spacious, or roomy. In the ancient Near Eastern mindset, to be in trouble or under oppression was described as being in a "narrow, tight place" (tsar or tsarah, often translated as trouble, distress, or anguish). Therefore, salvation is the act of being rescued from a confining, suffocating space of danger and brought into a wide-open, safe space where one can breathe, move, and live without fear. It is the transition from confinement to freedom, from vulnerability to safety, and from defeat to victory.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun yeshuah denotes:

• Deliverance or rescue from temporal dangers, enemies, or life-threatening situations.

• Victory, particularly in a military or national context, where God intervenes to defeat the foes of His people.

• Safety, welfare, health, and prosperity, representing the state of peace (shalom) that follows deliverance.

• Spiritual and eternal salvation, where the physical deliverance from earthly enemies serves as a type and shadow of ultimate deliverance from sin, death, and divine judgment.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, yeshuah is used to describe both immediate, physical rescues and long-term, spiritual preservation.

• In historical narratives, it often describes physical rescue from military enemies. It is a tangible, visible event that demonstrates God's power over pagan nations.

• In poetic and wisdom literature, the word expands to describe God's personal care for the brokenhearted, the sick, and the afflicted. To experience God's yeshuah is to experience His personal presence breaking through the dark circumstances of life.

• In prophetic literature, particularly in Isaiah, yeshuah takes on an eschatological and Messianic character. It becomes the ultimate, everlasting comfort and redemption that God will bring to His covenant people and the nations.

4. Theological Implications

The theology of yeshuah centers on the character of Yahweh as the sole Author and Source of deliverance.

• Salvation is Monergistic: Human effort, wealth, and military strength (chariots and horses) are utterly useless for ultimate deliverance. Salvation belongs entirely to the Lord; He alone possesses the power and the will to rescue.

• Covenantal Faithfulness: God's yeshuah is not arbitrary. It flows from His covenant loyalty (chesed). He saves His people because He has bound Himself to them by oath.

• The Bridge to the New Testament: The Greek Septuagint translation of the Old Testament regularly renders yeshuah as soteria (salvation) or soterion (saving act). Furthermore, the Hebrew name of Jesus (Yeshua) is derived from this exact root. When New Testament believers speak of Jesus, they are speaking of the personal embodiment of God's yeshuah.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 20, the setting is a corporate prayer for the king (David) as he prepares to go out to battle against formidable enemies who trust in "chariots and horses." Psalm 20:5 states, "We will rejoice in your salvation, And in the name of our God we will set up our banners!"

• The phrase "your salvation" refers directly to the victory and deliverance that Yahweh will grant to His anointed king. The people are so confident in God's faithfulness that they speak of this future victory as an accomplished reality.

• The connection between "salvation" and "the name of our God" shows that the king's deliverance is not for his personal glory, but for the vindication of Yahweh's covenant name.

• The setting up of "banners" is a military act of claiming victory. The congregation does not wait for the battle to end to rejoice; they rejoice beforehand because the source of their victory is the unchanging power of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 14:13: "And Moses said to the people, 'Do not be afraid. Stand still, and see the salvation of the LORD, which He will accomplish for you today.'"

• Psalm 3:8: "Salvation belongs to the LORD. Your blessing is upon Your people."

• Isaiah 12:2: "Behold, God is my salvation, I will trust and not be afraid; 'For YAH, the LORD, is my strength and song; He also has become my salvation.'"

• Jonah 2:9: "But I will sacrifice to You With the voice of thanksgiving; I will pay what I have vowed. Salvation is of the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural layout of Psalm 20 places this declaration of confidence right before the turning point of the psalm. In verses 1–4, the people petition God to answer the king. In verse 5, the mention of yeshuah shifts the tone from petition to absolute assurance. This demonstrates that meditating on God's character as Deliverer transforms our prayers from anxious begging into confident, joy-filled anticipation.
