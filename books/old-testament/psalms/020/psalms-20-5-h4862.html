PETITIONS - ( mish'alah , Strong's H4862 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun mish'alah refers to an earnest request, petition, or deep desire presented to a higher authority, most notably to God Himself. Occurring only twice in the Old Testament, the word carries a rich pastoral significance, distinguishing itself from casual or fleeting wishes. It represents the concrete, prayerful longings of the heart that are brought before the Lord with expectancy and faith. In Psalm 20:5, these petitions represent the specific, strategic prayers offered by the king and the congregation prior to battle, demonstrating a profound reliance on Yahweh's sovereign grace to grant deliverance and victory.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun mish'alah is derived from the common root verb sha'al, which means to ask, inquire, request, or demand. In the ancient Near Eastern context, presenting a petition was a formal act of submission and trust. A subject would enter the presence of a king or deity, bow, and present a prepared list of requests. The petition was not a demand made by an equal, but a humble plea presented to the sovereign who possessed the sole power to grant or deny it.

The word picture behind mish'alah is that of a vessel waiting to be filled, or a written scroll of requests placed into the hands of a benevolent ruler. It implies that the petitioner is completely dependent upon the favor of the one being addressed. In Israel's worship, this act of petitioning was elevated from mere political protocol to an intimate, covenantal dialogue between God and His people.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun mish'alah means a petition, request, or desire. It denotes the specific object of what is asked. It is a precise request formulated in the heart and spoken aloud in prayer.

Because of its derivation from sha'al, it carries the weight of a deliberate, sought-after objective rather than a passing thought. It refers to the deep, settled aspirations of the soul that have been refined and brought before the Lord.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word mish'alah is exceptionally rare in the Hebrew Bible, appearing only two times. This scarcity concentrates its meaning, linking the granting of these requests directly to a right relationship with God.

• In the first occurrence, the word represents the outward, public prayers of God’s people on behalf of their leader in a time of crisis.

• In the second occurrence, the word represents the inward, personal desires of an individual believer whose heart is aligned with the character of God.

The word picture is one of complete alignment: when the human will aligns with the divine will, the petitions of the servant become the purposes of the Sovereign, resulting in answered prayer.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, mish'alah reveals that God is a personal, listening Father who invites His people to voice their specific needs. Prayer in the Old Testament is never a generic, meditative exercise; it is an active presentation of concrete petitions.

Furthermore, this word highlights the relationship between divine sovereignty and human responsibility. God is the one who "fulfills" (male', meaning to fill up or complete) the petitions. Human plans and desires are empty outlines until God pours His power and favor into them. Ultimately, the fulfillment of a believer's petitions is not meant to fuel human pride, but to display God's faithfulness and covenant loyalty, leading to praise and the lifting of banners in His name.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 20 is a royal psalm, structured as a prayer for the king before he leads Israel's army into battle. The congregation gathers at the sanctuary to pray for their leader. In verse 5, the climax of the people's prayer is reached: "May the LORD fulfill all your petitions."

The contextual nuance of mish'alah here is highly strategic. The "petitions" of the king are not selfish requests for personal wealth or glory. Rather, they are his strategic, prayerful plans for the defense of God's covenant nation and the vindication of God's name against pagan enemies. The congregation recognizes that military strength (horses and chariots) is useless without the divine favor that answers these petitions. By praying for the fulfillment of the king's petitions, the people are declaring their shared trust in Yahweh's salvation.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because mish'alah occurs only twice in the Old Testament, both occurrences are vital for understanding its full semantic range:

• Psalm 20:5 – "We will rejoice in your salvation, And in the name of our God we will set up our banners! May the LORD fulfill all your petitions."

• Psalm 37:4 – "Delight yourself also in the LORD, And He shall give you the desires of your heart."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A beautiful connection exists between Psalm 20:5 and Psalm 37:4. In Psalm 37:4, the "desires" (mish'alot, the plural form) of the heart are promised to those who "delight" in the Lord. When a believer's chief joy is God Himself, their inner desires are reshaped to match His holy will.

Applying this to Psalm 20:5, the king's "petitions" are granted precisely because his heart is delighted in the Lord and committed to the covenant. This serves as a vital pastoral lesson: the secret to answered prayer is not the intensity of our pleading, but the alignment of our hearts with the character and purposes of God. When we desire what God desires, our petitions will never be denied.
