STRENGTH - ( geburah , Strong's H1369 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun geburah refers to mighty power, strength, and valor, particularly as demonstrated in heroic deeds or royal, divine might. In Psalm 20:6, it represents the victorious, saving power of God exerted on behalf of His anointed king. Unlike mere physical force, this strength is characterized by triumph, covenant faithfulness, and the active, saving intervention of Yahweh. It assures the believer that God's power is not passive but dynamically applied to rescue, defend, and vindicate those who trust in Him.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun geburah is derived from the root verb gabar, which means to be strong, mighty, or to prevail. It is closely related to the noun gibbor, which refers to a mighty warrior, hero, or champion. The word picture behind geburah is that of a victorious warrior-king entering the battlefield equipped with overwhelming strength to conquer enemies and rescue his people. It evokes images of military triumph, royal authority, and the undeniable execution of sovereign will. When applied to God, it frames Yahweh not merely as a source of abstract energy, but as the ultimate Warrior-King whose right hand performs valiant, saving deeds on behalf of His covenant partners.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of geburah is strength, might, power, or courage. In administrative or military contexts, it denotes valor, bravery, mighty deeds, or victory. When used in reference to God, it highlights His omnipotence, His sovereign control over history, and His majestic acts of deliverance. Rather than indicating a latent or static ability, geburah signifies the active, visible, and dynamic demonstration of power.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, geburah is used in several distinct ways:

• Divine Might vs. Human Weakness: It is frequently contrasted with fragile human efforts, emphasizing that ultimate strength belongs to God alone.

• Creation and Cosmos: It describes the awesome power of God in establishing the world and ruling over the tumultuous elements.

• Military Exploits: It refers to the fierce physical strength of warhorses or the brave deeds of military champions, illustrating a force that overcomes all opposition.

• Anthropomorphic Right Hand: Metaphorically paired with God's "right hand," it represents the ultimate expression of active power, authority, favor, and direct divine intervention in human affairs.

4. Theological Implications

• Divine Omnipotence in Action: God's geburah is never static; it is always revealed through historical actions, particularly in the redemption and preservation of His people.

• The Messianic Connection: Because geburah is associated with the "anointed" king in the Psalms, it points forward to the ultimate displays of divine power in Jesus Christ, who is described as the power and wisdom of God, and the ultimate conquering King.

• The Insufficiency of Human Might: The Old Testament consistently contrasts the geburah of Yahweh with human armies, chariots, and horses. True victory belongs to the Lord, and human security is a delusion apart from divine strength.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 20, a liturgical prayer offered before going into battle, the community expresses trust in God's name rather than in chariots and horses. The sudden transition in verse 6 ("Now I know...") marks a moment of absolute assurance. The Psalmist is fully confident of victory because of the "saving strength of His right hand." The strength mentioned here is specifically "saving" (derived from the root for salvation or victory), meaning it is power deployed for the preservation and triumph of God's anointed leader. This ensures that the covenant line is secured and God's people are vindicated. It contrasts the false, fragile strength of military machinery with the true, supernatural, and active geburah of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 106:2: "Who can utter the mighty acts of the LORD? Who can declare all His praise?"

• Isaiah 11:2: "The Spirit of the LORD shall rest upon Him, The Spirit of wisdom and understanding, The Spirit of counsel and might, The Spirit of knowledge and of the fear of the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) often translates geburah with terms like dynasteia (mighty power, lordship) or dynamis (inherent power, strength, ability). This directly links the Old Testament concept of God's saving strength to the New Testament power of the Gospel and the resurrection of Christ. Additionally, in Psalm 20:6, the pairing of geburah with the word for "saving" (salvation) demonstrates that God's power is never destructive or chaotic toward His people; it is always intentionally shaped by His covenant desire to deliver and restore.
