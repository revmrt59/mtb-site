SAVES - ( yasha , Strong's H3467 )

Summary:

The Hebrew root yasha communicates the profound transition from confinement, distress, and imminent danger into a state of freedom, safety, and broad, spacious liberty. In Psalm 20:6, this word marks a decisive turning point from petition to absolute assurance, declaring that Yahweh rescues, delivers, and preserves His anointed king from military and existential threats. Ultimately, this temporal deliverance points to the greater spiritual rescue accomplished by the ultimate Anointed One, Jesus, whose very name derives from this same root.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• yasha is deeply rooted in the spatial realities of ancient Near Eastern life. The concrete concept behind the word is that of width, spaciousness, and freedom from restriction.

• It stands in direct contrast to words denoting narrowness, confinement, or being pressed from all sides (such as tsar, the root for trouble, distress, or enemy).

• To be in distress is to be trapped in a cramped, dangerous defile with no way out. To be saved is to be led out of that narrow gorge into a wide-open, safe valley where one can breathe and move freely.

• In historical and military contexts, it depicts a weak or besieged city being rescued by a powerful deliverer who breaks the siege and secures their freedom.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• In its verbal forms, particularly the causative Hiphil stem used in Psalm 20:6 (hoshia), the word means to deliver, rescue, save, or bring to safety. It carries the active sense of intervening to liberate someone from a situation where they are completely helpless.

• The semantic range encompasses:

• Liberation from physical danger, such as enemies, war, or life-threatening situations.

• The granting of victory or deliverance in battle, where the victory is recognized as a divine gift rather than a human achievement.

• Preservation from harm, ensuring safety, security, and welfare.

• Spiritual deliverance from sin, judgment, and ultimate destruction, a meaning that develops progressively throughout the Old Testament and culminates in the New Testament.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Scripture frequently pairs yasha with powerful anthropomorphic expressions of God's physical intervention. One of the primary word pictures associated with this rescue is the "right hand" (yamin) and "arm" (zeroa) of Yahweh. The arm represents God's unleashed strength, while the right hand symbolizes His personal, authoritative, and victorious action.

• Another major word picture is the "shield" of salvation. God does not merely provide a tool for defense; He Himself acts as the shield that absorbs the blows of the enemy, guaranteeing safety.

• The noun derivatives of this root include yeshua (salvation, deliverance, welfare), which is the Hebrew name for Joshua and the etymological origin of the name Jesus (Yeshua), illustrating that salvation is not merely an abstract concept but is embodied in a personal Savior.

4. Theological Implications

• Salvation is fundamentally an act of God. The Hebrew Bible consistently emphasizes that human beings cannot save themselves, nor can they rely on earthly powers—such as horses, chariots, or foreign alliances—to bring deliverance. Salvation belongs exclusively to Yahweh.

• God's act of saving is an expression of His covenant faithfulness (chesed). He rescues His people not because they are strong or deserving, but because He has bound Himself to them by covenant promise.

• The concept of salvation moves from physical, temporal deliverance (such as the Exodus or deliverance from military enemies) to a holistic, spiritual salvation. It encompasses peace (shalom), righteousness, and ultimate reconciliation with God, demonstrating that physical deliverance is a shadow of the comprehensive redemption of the cosmos.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Psalm 20, a liturgical prayer of preparation before battle, the first five verses consist of communal petitions on behalf of the king ("May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble...").

• Verse 6 marks a sudden, dramatic shift in tone from petition to absolute certainty: "Now I know that the LORD saves His anointed." The phrase "Now I know" indicates a flash of prophetic assurance or a deep realization of faith.

• The verb hoshia ("saves" or "has saved") functions here as a prophetic perfect. Even though the battle lies ahead and the physical deliverance has not yet occurred, the speaker views the king's victory as an accomplished fact because Yahweh's character and promise are unwavering.

• The text specifies that God saves "His anointed" (mashiach). The rescue is not random; it is directed toward the one chosen and consecrated by God. God answers him "from His holy heaven," showing that while the battle is fought on earth, the source of victory is transcendent and secure.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 14:30: "So the LORD saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians, and Israel saw the Egyptians dead on the seashore."

• Psalm 28:9: "Save Your people, and bless Your inheritance; shepherd them also, and bear them up forever."

• Isaiah 45:22: "Look to Me, and be saved, all you ends of the earth! For I am God, and there is no other."

• Zechariah 9:9: "Rejoice greatly, O daughter of Zion! Shout, O daughter of Jerusalem! Behold, your King is coming to you; He is just and having salvation, lowly and riding on a donkey, a colt, the foal of a donkey."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In the Septuagint (LXX), the Greek translation of the Old Testament, yasha is regularly translated by the Greek verb sozo. In Psalm 20:6, the translators used esoosen ("He saved"). This establishes a direct linguistic and theological bridge to the New Testament concept of salvation.

• The historical-typological link in Psalm 20:6 is profound. The immediate historical referent is the Davidic king, but the ultimate "Anointed One" (mashiach/Christ) is Jesus. God saved Jesus from the ultimate enemy—death—by raising Him from the dead with the "saving strength of His right hand." Through Him, this salvation is extended to all who trust in His name.
