HEAVEN - ( shamayim , Strong's H8064 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shamayim refers to the heights, encompassing the physical atmosphere, the starry expanse, and the transcendent, spiritual dwelling place of God. In Psalm 20:6, the term points specifically to "His holy heaven"—the cosmic throne room of Yahweh. It highlights that the ultimate source of help and victory for God's anointed does not come from earthly alliances, human strategies, or physical fortifications, but from the sovereign, supreme realm of God Himself. It is a word of absolute assurance, shifting the believer's gaze from earthly vulnerability to heavenly invincibility.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word shamayim is morphologically dual or plural, suggesting a vast, layered expanse or a region of great extension. While the exact etymology is debated, the root is historically associated with loftiness, height, or that which is stretched out over the earth. In the ancient Near Eastern context, where nations worshiped local deities bound to specific geographic territories or physical idols housed in earthly temples, the Hebrew concept of shamayim set Yahweh apart. The heavens represent a realm that is utterly beyond human control, manipulation, or limitation. To say God dwells in the heavens is to declare His absolute sovereignty, His unlimited perspective, and His freedom from the constraints of the material world.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun shamayim carries a threefold semantic range in Hebrew literature, determined strictly by context:

• The Atmospheric Heaven: The visible sky where clouds gather, wind blows, and birds fly (e.g., the "birds of the air" or "rain from heaven").

• The Celestial Expanse: The cosmic realm containing the sun, moon, and stars (e.g., the "host of heaven").

• The Abode of God: The unseen, transcendent realm where God sits enthroned, surrounded by His heavenly host.

In Psalm 20:6, the term is coupled with "holiness" (qodesh) to form sheme qodsho ("His holy heaven"), denoting the third category—the transcendent sanctuary and sovereign command center of the universe.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, shamayim is frequently used to contrast the human and the divine. While the earth is the realm of weakness, decay, and transient kingdoms, the heavens are characterized by stability, purity, and eternal dominion.

• The Throne Room: Heaven is described as God's throne, while the earth is merely His footstool (Isaiah 66:1). This graphic Anthropomorphism illustrates that the highest seat of cosmic authority belongs to Yahweh.

• The Source of Abundance and Judgment: Heaven is the treasury from which God pours out blessings (like rain and dew) or executes judgment (like fire or hail). Thus, looking up to shamayim is a physical posture of absolute dependence.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of shamayim in the Psalms rests on the tension between God's transcendence (He is high above) and His immanence (He hears and acts on earth).

• Sovereign Intervention: Because God's dwelling is shamayim, His resources are limitless. He is not restricted by earthly battles, military deficits, or geopolitical crises.

• The True Sanctuary: While Israel worshiped at the earthly tabernacle or temple, they understood that the earthly structure was merely a shadow. The true, ultimate sanctuary is the "holy heaven."

• The Divine Answer: When God answers from heaven, it is not a weak or tentative response. An answer from the shamayim carries the full authority and executive power of the Creator of the universe.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 20 is a liturgical prayer offered by the congregation of Israel as the king ("His anointed") prepares to lead the army into battle. The people pray for the king's protection, remembering his sacrifices and asking for victory.

In verse 6, the tone shifts dramatically from petition to supreme confidence: "Now I know that the LORD saves His anointed..." The basis of this sudden assurance is the source of the rescue. The king does not find safety in his chariots or horses (v. 7), but in the "saving strength of His right hand" coming directly from "His holy heaven."

This contextual contrast is vital. Earthly enemies trust in visible, tangible military assets on the battlefield. Israel's king trusts in the invisible Ruler in the shamayim. The nuance here is that help from heaven is superior to any earthly force. The physical battle on earth is already decided because the decree of victory has been issued from the heavenly throne room.

• Psalm 115:3 - "But our God is in heaven; He does whatever He pleases."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew thought, the plural nature of shamayim also prevents any localization of God. It acts as a linguistic barrier against idolatry. One cannot trap or contain a God whose dwelling place is the limitless shamayim. Solomon beautifully captures this during the dedication of the temple, noting that even the "heaven and heaven of heavens cannot contain" God (1 Kings 8:27). Therefore, when Psalm 20:6 promises an answer from His holy heaven, it assures the believer that the response comes from an uncontainable, unhindered, and omnipotent God.
