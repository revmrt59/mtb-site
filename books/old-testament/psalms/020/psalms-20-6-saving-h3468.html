SAVING - ( yesha , Strong's H3468 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word yesha, translated as "saving" in Psalm 20:6, refers to deliverance, safety, rescue, victory, and salvation. In its covenant context, it represents the active, liberating power of God breaking into human circumstances to rescue His people and His anointed king. Rather than denoting a passive state of safety, this word conveys a dynamic, mighty rescue that brings the believer out of distress, restriction, and confinement into a spacious place of security, triumph, and freedom.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun yesha is derived from the primary verbal root yasha, which literally means to be wide, spacious, or free. In the ancient Near Eastern world, danger and distress were conceptualized as being hemmed in, trapped, or squeezed into a narrow, suffocating space by enemies or hostile circumstances. Consequently, to experience rescue or salvation meant to be brought out of that tight grip into a wide-open, spacious place where one could breathe, move, and live without fear. The word picture behind yesha is one of liberation from confinement—moving from the suffocating pressure of adversity into the expansive freedom of God's sovereign protection.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The masculine noun yesha designates deliverance, salvation, rescue, safety, and welfare. It does not merely describe an abstract theological concept or a future state of existence; it denotes a tangible, historical, and experiential intervention by God. In Psalm 20:6, it is joined in a construct state with the plural noun geburot ("mighty deeds" or "strength"), resulting in the phrase "the saving strength" (literally, "the mighty deeds of the salvation of"). It defines the specific character of God’s power: it is a power that is inherently redemptive, protective, and victorious.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Old Testament, yesha is repeatedly paired with military, natural, and royal imagery to depict the absolute dependability of God:

• The Shield of Salvation: It is paired with defensive armor, showing that God’s salvation acts as a barrier that deflects the lethal blows of the enemy.

• The Horn of Salvation: The "horn" represents the strength, dignity, and conquering power of a mighty animal. Yesha as a horn represents triumphant, conquering power that vanquishes foes.

• The Right Hand: In Hebrew thought, the right hand is the hand of ultimate authority, skill, and active strength. The "saving strength of His right hand" paints a picture of God stretching out His arm to actively tilt the scales of history and battle in favor of His covenant people.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yesha establishes that salvation belongs exclusively to Yahweh. It is initiated, executed, and sustained by Him alone. This word emphasizes that human resources, military hardware, and strategic alliances are ultimately useless without divine intervention. Furthermore, yesha has deep covenantal significance. God provides this deliverance because of His covenant faithfulness to His anointed king (the mashiach). Ultimately, this OT pattern of temporal deliverance lays the theological foundation for the spiritual, eternal salvation (yeshua) brought by Jesus Christ, the ultimate Anointed One, who saves His people from their sins and brings them into the infinite spaciousness of God's grace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 20, the nation of Israel is preparing for a military conflict. The congregation gathers to pray for King David as he prepares to lead the army into battle. In verses 1–5, the people petition God to answer the king in the day of trouble. In verse 6, a dramatic shift occurs from petition to absolute certainty: "Now I know that the LORD saves His anointed." The basis for this sudden assurance is "the saving strength of His right hand." While the pagan nations rely on the visible, material strength of "chariots and horses" (v. 7), the psalmist relies on the invisible, invincible saving power of Yahweh. The nuance here is a defiant trust in God’s saving action over and against the most formidable earthly resources.

• Habakkuk 3:18: "Yet I will rejoice in the LORD, I will joy in the God of my salvation."

7. Other Relevant Insights

For pastors and teachers, this word provides a powerful antidote to modern anxiety and self-reliance. Often, when believers encounter trials, they look for "chariots and horses"—tangible, worldly mechanisms of escape, finance, or human intellect. Yesha reminds the church that true safety and deliverance are relational, found only in the active power of God. When life "hems us in" on every side, the covenant-keeping God promises to stretch out His right hand to rescue us, bringing us into a spiritual spaciousness that the world can neither give nor take away.
