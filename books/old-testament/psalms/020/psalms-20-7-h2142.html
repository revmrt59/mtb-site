REMEMBER - ( zakar , Strong's H2142 )

Summary:

In Hebrew thought, to remember is not merely a passive mental recall of past information, but an active, decisive engagement of the mind and will that leads to covenant loyalty, trust, and action. In Psalm 20:7, the verb appears in a form that means to invoke, declare, or publicly praise. Facing the intimidating military might of worldly powers, the people of God make a deliberate choice: while others place their ultimate trust in physical weapons, God's people will actively invoke and rely upon the character, covenant, and presence of Yahweh their God.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew concept of memory differs significantly from the modern Western understanding. In the Western tradition, influenced by Greek philosophy, remembering is primarily cognitive—an internal retrieval of stored data or a nostalgic reflection on the past. In the ancient Hebrew mindset, remembering (zakar) is dynamic and action-oriented. It is an event that bridges the past and the present, bringing a historical reality to bear on immediate behavior.

When God "remembers" His people or His covenant, He is not recovering from a lapse of memory; rather, He is acting decisively on their behalf in accordance with His promises. Similarly, when humans "remember" God, it is never a passive thought. It is a conscious, visible reorientation of the heart, resulting in obedience, worship, and absolute trust. The word picture behind zakar is that of marking something clearly in the mind so that it dictates one's current direction and choices.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root verb zakar primary means to mark, mention, call to mind, or record.

• In the Qal (basic) stem, the verb means to recall, bring to mind, think about, or keep in mind. It describes the deep mental and spiritual focus of a person on a specific truth.

• In the Hiphil (causative) stem—which is the specific form used in Psalm 20:7 (nazkir)—the verb means to cause to remember, make mention of, declare, invoke, or praise.

Therefore, in the context of worship and warfare, to "remember" the name of the Lord is to vocalize His attributes, to publicly confess His sovereign power, and to invoke His covenantal protection as the ultimate defense.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, zakar is used to show the link between memory and action:

• The Divine Action: When God "remembered" Noah (Genesis 8:1) or "remembered" Abraham (Genesis 19:29), the text immediately describes a saving action that followed. God's memory is His covenant faithfulness in motion.

• The Memorial Sacrifice: Certain offerings were called a azkarah (memorial), designed to symbolically bring the worshiper before God’s attention, prompting His favor.

• The Vocal Confession: In historical narratives and the Psalms, zakar frequently describes speaking aloud. To remember God is to sing of His deeds, tell of His wonders to the next generation, and shout His praise in the midst of the congregation. It is the opposite of silent contemplation; it is a vocal declaration of allegiance.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theology of zakar is rooted in the covenant. Because God has revealed Himself in history through specific, redemptive acts, His people have a tangible foundation for their faith.

• Faith is Historical: Biblical faith does not look inward for subjective feelings; it looks backward to the objective, historical acts of God (such as the Exodus) and looks forward with confidence that He will do it again.

• Memory as a Weapon: In spiritual and physical conflict, memory is a powerful weapon. Forgetting God leads to fear, anxiety, and ultimate apostasy. Remembering God destroys fear by realigning the soul with the reality of God’s supreme authority.

• The Sovereignty of the Name: In the Ancient Near East, a name represented the very essence, character, and authority of the person. To invoke "the name of the LORD" is to summon the full weight of His divine character—His holiness, His omnipotence, and His unchanging loyalty to His chosen people.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 20 is a royal psalm, structured as a prayer for the king before he leads Israel's army into battle. The surrounding context highlights a stark contrast in where people place their ultimate security:

• The enemy nations boast in visible, tangible, and highly advanced military hardware: "some trust in chariots, and some in horses."

• Israel, often militarily disadvantaged, chooses a different source of strength: "But we will remember the name of the LORD our God."

In the Hebrew structure of Psalm 20:7, the verb is nazkir (we will make mention/invoke). While the pagan armies shout their battle cries and boast in their weaponry, the armies of Israel raise a shout of praise, invoking the covenant name Yahweh. This is not a passive mental recollection while marching; it is a defiant, public declaration of faith. It asserts that the ultimate outcome of the battle does not belong to military technology, but to the sovereign God who governs all history.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 9:16 – "The rainbow shall be in the cloud, and I will look at it to remember the everlasting covenant between God and every living creature of all flesh that is on the earth."

• Exodus 20:8 – "Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy."

• Psalm 77:11 – "I will remember the works of the LORD; Surely I will remember Your wonders of old."

• Psalm 103:18 – "To such as keep His covenant, And to those who remember His commandments to do them."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Contrast with Forgetting: The direct opposite of zakar is shakach (to forget). In scripture, forgetting God is not a cognitive slip, but a moral failure. To forget God is to live as if He does not exist or has not spoken. Conversely, to remember Him is to live in active, daily submission to His presence.

• The New Testament Connection: This Hebrew concept carries directly into the New Testament. When Jesus instituted the Lord's Supper, He said, "Do this in remembrance (anamnesis) of Me" (Luke 22:19). This is the Greek equivalent of zakar. The Lord’s Supper is not just a quiet mental recollection of Christ’s death, but an active, public proclamation of His redemptive sacrifice and an invocation of His presence until He returns.
