CHARIOTS - ( rekeb, Strong's H7393 )

Summary:

In Psalm 20:7, "chariots" (rekeb) represents the pinnacle of ancient military power, technological superiority, and human self-reliance. While worldly kingdoms place their confidence in visible, material instruments of warfare, the people of God are called to place their absolute trust in the invisible, omnipotent character of Yahweh. This term serves as a sharp contrast between temporary, human-made security and the eternal, sovereign protection of the covenant God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• In the ancient Near East, the chariot was the ultimate weapon of decisive warfare, comparable to the modern battle tank or fighter jet.

• It was a highly sophisticated machine of war, constructed of wood and leather, sometimes reinforced with iron, and pulled by specially trained, high-speed warhorses.

• Possessing a fleet of chariots required immense wealth, centralized government organization, and a professional class of warriors. Consequently, empires like Egypt, Assyria, and the Canaanite city-states used their chariotry to terrorize and dominate smaller nations.

• For Israel, a nation of infantrymen living largely in rugged, mountainous terrain, the sight of a chariot force charging across the plains was an incredibly intimidating and terrifying spectacle.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The masculine noun rekeb refers collectively to a team of chariots, a fleet of war-chariots, or a single chariot vehicle. It also carries the passive sense of "that which is ridden upon."

• The noun is derived from the primary root verb rakab, which means "to mount and ride," "to ride upon an animal," or "to drive a chariot."

• Rather than referring to a single carriage, rekeb often functions as a collective singular noun representing an entire army's mobile armored division (the chariotry).

• The word carries the semantic range of military apparatus, mobile strength, horse-drawn vehicles of state, and figuratively, the sovereign spiritual forces of heaven.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• rekeb is used to describe the overwhelming military threat of Pharaoh's army pursuing Israel at the Red Sea, demonstrating that human technology is completely helpless when God fights for His people.

• The term is used to depict the massive armies of Israel’s northern Canaanite enemies under Sisera, who possessed nine hundred chariots of iron, representing an insurmountable physical obstacle to Israel's survival.

• rekeb is utilized metaphorically to describe the spiritual armies of God. The unseen angelic forces surrounding Elisha are described as "chariots of fire," illustrating that God's invisible spiritual protection is far greater than visible physical threats.

• The word serves as a prophetic warning tool. Prophets used the accumulation of chariots as a primary diagnostic sign of a nation turning away from faith in God and falling into pagan self-reliance.

4. Theological Implications

• The contrast between trusting in rekeb (chariots) and trusting in the "name of the LORD" highlights the perpetual struggle between sight and faith. Chariots are tangible, visible, and physically impressive; the Lord is invisible, requiring faith, yet infinite in power.

• Relying on chariots represents the sin of self-determinism—the belief that human ingenuity, wealth, and military technology can secure our future apart from God's sovereign will.

• True spiritual victory is never achieved through human resources or political alliances. When God's people rely on their own "chariots," they strip themselves of divine power. When they acknowledge their weakness and look to the Name (the character, covenant faithfulness, and presence) of God, they participate in His victory.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• Psalm 20 is a liturgical prayer offered by the congregation of Israel before the king goes out to battle. It acknowledges the physical reality of the threat: the surrounding pagan nations have superior military hardware ("some trust in chariots, and some in horses").

• David, the king and author, was well aware of this disparity. However, he deliberately chose not to build Israel's defense strategy around the acquisition of chariotry. Instead of matching the enemy's hardware, David's strategy was spiritual memory: "But we will remember the name of the LORD our God."

• The word "remember" (zakar) in this context means to invoke, boast in, or actively rely upon. The passage teaches that when the battle lines are drawn, the believer’s ultimate weapon is a conscious, active reliance on the character and track record of the covenant-keeping God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 14:9: "So the Egyptians pursued them, all the horses and chariots of Pharaoh, his horsemen and his army, and overtook them camping by the sea beside Pi Hahiroth, before Baal Zephon."

• Joshua 11:4: "So they went out, they and all their armies with them, as many people as the sand that is on the seashore in multitude, with very many horses and chariots."

• 2 Kings 6:17: "And Elisha prayed, and said, 'LORD, I pray, open his eyes that he may see.' Then the LORD opened the eyes of the young man, and he saw. And behold, the mountain was full of horses and chariots of fire all around Elisha."

• Psalm 68:17: "The chariots of God are twenty thousand, Even thousands of thousands; The Lord is among them as in Sinai, in the Holy Place."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• God specifically commanded Israelite kings in Deuteronomy 17:16 not to "multiply horses" to prevent them from building up massive chariot forces and relying on military might or returning to Egypt for alliances.

• King Solomon directly violated this command later in Israel's history by establishing chariot cities and importing thousands of horses and chariots from Egypt (1 Kings 10:26-29). This physical security program directly preceded his spiritual compromise and the eventual dividing of the kingdom.

• For the modern believer, "chariots" represent any earthly security system—financial savings, professional credentials, political structures, or personal talent. While these tools have practical value, they make dangerous masters. When the storms of life arrive, the things we "ride upon" will fail, but the name of the Lord remains an unshakable tower.
