CALL - ( qara , Strong's H7121 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qara fundamentally means to cry out, call aloud, summon, or invite. In the context of prayer, it refers to an earnest, audible cry of distress to God, representing a declaration of absolute dependence. In Psalm 20:9, it is the desperate yet confident cry of God's covenant people petitioning their divine King for deliverance on the eve of battle, assured that He hears and will answer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Near Eastern culture, calling out was not merely a mental exercise but an audible, vocalized action. To qara is to lift one's voice, to break the silence in a moment of urgent need or formal invitation. It carries the imagery of a subject standing before a monarch, a child crying out to a father, or a soldier shouting for reinforcements. The word picture is one of vocalized dependency, where the caller recognizes that help must come from outside of themselves.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb qara occurs over 700 times in the Old Testament and encompasses several semantic nuances depending on context. Its primary definitions include:

• To call out, cry aloud, or proclaim.

• To summon or invite someone to a specific place or task.

• To name or designate a person, place, or thing.

• To pray, supplicate, or invoke the name of a deity.

In the context of worship and prayer, qara specifically denotes the act of invoking Yahweh's name, appealing to His covenant loyalty, and pleading for His intervention.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Cry of Distress: Often used when a person or nation is at the end of their own strength. It is the cry that rises from the depths of trouble.

• The Formal Summon: Used when a king calls his subjects or when God summons His prophets.

• The Naming of Creation: God called (qara) the light "day" and the darkness "night" in Genesis 1, demonstrating sovereign authority.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, qara reveals a God who is relational, attentive, and interactive. Prayer is not a useless ritual; it is a direct appeal to a personal God who has bound Himself to His people by covenant. When God's people qara, they are not merely expressing emotion; they are claiming the promises of God's covenant name, Yahweh. It emphasizes that while God is transcendent, He is immanently available to those who call upon Him in truth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 20 is a liturgical prayer offered before the king goes out to battle. The people pray for the king's victory, trusting not in chariots and horses, but in the name of the Lord (v. 7). The psalm culminates in verse 9: "Save, LORD! May the King answer us when we call."

Here, qara operates as the trigger for divine action. The "King" (referring to Yahweh, the ultimate King of Israel) is petitioned to "answer" (anah) when the people "call" (qara). The nuance is one of desperate, collective petition on the brink of crisis. It highlights the striking contrast between the silence of false gods and the responsive ear of Israel's King. The call is vocal, urgent, and filled with expectant faith.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 4:26 - "And as for Seth, to him also a son was born; and he named him Enosh. Then men began to call on the name of the LORD."

• Psalm 18:3 - "I will call upon the LORD, who is worthy to be praised; So shall I be saved from my enemies."

• Psalm 50:15 - "Call upon Me in the day of trouble; I will deliver you, and you shall glorify Me."

• Isaiah 55:6 - "Seek the LORD while He may be found, Call upon Him while He is near."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The relationship between "calling" (qara) and "answering" (anah) is a recurring motif in the Psalms. It establishes a reciprocal dynamic: God speaks, and man hears; man calls, and God answers. This demonstrates that biblical faith is conversational and relational, not mechanical.
