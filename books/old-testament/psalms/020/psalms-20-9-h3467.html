SAVE - ( yasha , Strong's H3467 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word yasha means to deliver, rescue, save, or bring into a spacious, safe place. It conveys the transition from confinement, distress, and impending defeat to freedom, safety, and victory. In the Old Testament, and specifically in Psalm 20:9, it is a desperate yet confident cry for divine rescue, recognizing that ultimate victory and safety belong solely to the Lord, not to human armies, chariots, or horses.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical concept underlying yasha is space. To be in distress or trouble in the ancient Hebrew mind was to be in a narrow, cramped, or suffocating place (tsarah). Salvation, therefore, is the act of bringing someone out of a tight, restricted, and dangerous place into a wide-open, spacious, and safe territory where they can breathe and live without fear. It is the movement from confinement to freedom, from limitation to abundance, and from vulnerability to safety.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Yasha is a primitive root verb occurring over 200 times in the Old Testament. In its active, causative form (the Hiphil stem, as found in Psalm 20:9), it means to cause deliverance, to rescue, to defend, to preserve, or to give victory. It does not merely mean to wish for safety, but to actively step in and secure victory for someone who is completely helpless. The name Joshua (Yehoshua), Elisha (Elisha), and the name of Jesus (Yeshua) are all derived from this same verbal root, meaning "Yahweh is salvation."

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The primary Old Testament usage of yasha is physical and national deliverance. It describes Israel's rescue from slavery in Egypt, their preservation through the Red Sea, and their subsequent military victories over oppressive nations under the leadership of the Judges.

• The word picture of yasha is that of a powerful champion or savior (moshia) who hears the cry of the defenseless and rushes to their aid, overpowering their oppressors.

• It is also used in legal contexts, depicting an advocate who steps in to save a vulnerable person (such as an orphan or widow) from unjust exploitation.

### 4. Theological Implications

• Salvation belongs exclusively to God: The Scriptures consistently emphasize that human efforts, military might, and political alliances cannot bring ultimate rescue. Only Yahweh is the true Savior.

• Covenant Preservation: God's acts of saving are not random; they flow from His covenant faithfulness (chesed). He rescues His people because they belong to Him.

• The Foundation of Christ's Name: When the angel told Joseph to name the child Jesus (Yeshua), it was because "He will save His people from their sins" (Matthew 1:21). The physical rescues of the Old Testament foreshadowed the ultimate, spiritual deliverance from sin and death accomplished on the cross.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 20, King David is preparing to lead the nation into battle. The congregation gathers to pray for his protection and success. The psalm builds a contrast between worldly security and spiritual security: "Some trust in chariots, and some in horses; but we will remember the name of the LORD our God" (Psalm 20:7).

In verse 9, the climax of the psalm is reached with the corporate plea: "Save, LORD!" (Hebrew: Hoshiah Yahweh). By crying out for God to save, the people are acknowledging that even with the best military preparations, victory lies entirely in God's hands. It is a petition of total surrender and absolute trust. They do not look to their own strength; they look to the King of Kings to preserve their earthly king.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 14:30: "So the LORD saved Israel that day out of the hand of the Egyptians, and Israel saw the Egyptians dead on the seashore."

• Psalm 3:7: "Arise, O LORD; Save me, O my God! For You have struck all my enemies on the cheekbone; You have broken the teeth of the ungodly."

• Psalm 18:3: "I will call upon the LORD, who is worthy to be praised; So shall I be saved from my enemies."

• Jeremiah 17:14: "Heal me, O LORD, and I shall be healed; Save me, and I shall be saved, For You are my praise."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The specific Hebrew form used in Psalm 20:9 is hoshiah. When combined with the particle of entreaty na ("please" or "now"), it becomes hoshia-na, which is the source of the Greek word Hosanna. When the crowds lined the streets of Jerusalem during Jesus’ triumphal entry, shouting, "Hosanna to the Son of David!" (Matthew 21:9), they were directly quoting the messianic hope embedded in this Hebrew root. They were crying out, "Save us, we pray, O King!"
