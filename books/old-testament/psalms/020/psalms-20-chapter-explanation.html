Chapter Study

Psalms 20

Verse-by-Verse
Psalms 20:1

NKJV

Psalms 20:1 To the Chief Musician. A Psalm of David. May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble; May the name of the God of Jacob defend you;(NKJV)

NLT

Psalms 20:1 In times of trouble, may the LORD answer your cry.May the name of the God of Jacob keep you safe from all harm. (NLT)

Main Idea

Psalm 20 opens with a prayer that God would answer and protect His anointed king when trouble comes. The people place their confidence not merely in the king’s strength, but in the God of Jacob who is able to defend him.

Verse Explanation

“May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble”

Psalm 20 is a prayer for the king, probably offered as he prepared to face battle or another national crisis. The people pray that when “the day of trouble” comes, the LORD will hear the king’s cry and respond.

The emphasis is not simply that trouble might come, but that God can be called upon when it does. Israel’s security ultimately depends upon the LORD answering His people in their distress.

“May the name of the God of Jacob defend you”

In Scripture, God’s “name” represents more than a title. It points to God Himself as He has revealed His character, authority, power, and faithfulness. To trust in His name is to trust in who He is.

“The God of Jacob” reaches back into Israel’s history. Jacob repeatedly experienced danger, weakness, uncertainty, and circumstances beyond his ability to control, yet God preserved him and remained faithful to His purposes. The God who had been faithful to Jacob was still the God upon whom Israel and its king could depend.

“Defend you” carries the idea of placing someone securely out of reach, as though setting him in a high and protected place. The prayer is that God Himself would be the king’s security when enemies threaten.

Study Notes

Psalm 20 and Psalm 21 appear to belong together. Psalm 20 is largely a prayer for the king before conflict, while Psalm 21 celebrates the LORD’s strength and the victory He has given the king.

The repeated “you” in the opening verses is singular, supporting the understanding that the congregation is addressing Israel’s king. Later the psalm shifts to “we” as the people express their shared confidence in the LORD.

Spirit & Life

“The day of trouble” is not presented as evidence that God has abandoned His people. It becomes an occasion to call upon Him. The first instinct of this psalm is not panic, calculation, or self-reliance, but prayer.

Before Israel's king went into battle, the people prayed for him. They understood that victory would not ultimately depend on strategy, soldiers, or weapons, but on the Lord's help. Every significant work of God should likewise begin with prayer, acknowledging our dependence on Him before we take our first step.

Psalms 20:2

NKJV

Psalms 20:2 May He send you help from the sanctuary, And strengthen you out of Zion;(NKJV)

NLT

Psalms 20:2 May he send you help from his sanctuaryand strengthen you from Jerusalem. (NLT)

Main Idea

The people pray that God Himself would send the king the help and strength he needs. His true support would come from the LORD who dwelt among His people in His sanctuary at Zion.

Verse Explanation

“May He send you help from the sanctuary”

The prayer of verse 1 continues. As the king faces “the day of trouble,” the people ask God to send him “help.” The king may have armies, weapons, advisers, and resources, but the help that matters most must come from the LORD.

“The sanctuary” points to the place of God’s dwelling among His people. At this period, before Solomon’s temple was built, this would refer to the sacred dwelling associated with the ark and the worship of the LORD. The point is not that God was confined to a building or location, but that help comes from the God who had chosen to dwell in the midst of Israel.

“And strengthen you out of Zion”

“Zion” refers to Jerusalem, particularly as the place God had chosen for His dwelling and worship. The people pray that from Zion God would “strengthen” or support the king as he goes out to face the enemy.

The picture is striking: the king may be leaving Jerusalem for battle, but he does not leave behind the help of Israel’s God. The LORD can extend His sustaining power from His sanctuary to wherever His servant must go.

Study Notes

“Sanctuary” and “Zion” are parallel expressions here, both directing attention to God’s presence among His people. David had brought the ark of the covenant to Jerusalem and placed it in a tent there (2 Samuel 6:17), while the tabernacle and its altar were at Gibeon during part of this period (1 Chronicles 16:39; 21:29). The permanent temple on Mount Zion would come later under Solomon.

The word translated “strengthen” can also mean to support or sustain. The prayer is therefore not merely for an inward feeling of courage but for God Himself to uphold the king in the conflict ahead.

Spirit & Life

The verse distinguishes between having resources and having God’s help. Israel’s king could prepare for battle, but preparation was never intended to replace dependence upon the LORD.

There is also a larger biblical trajectory in the idea of help coming from God’s dwelling place. For Israel, Zion represented the LORD dwelling among His people. In Christ, God has come to dwell among us in an even greater way, and through Him we can “come boldly to the throne of grace” to receive mercy and “find grace to help in time of need” (Hebrews 4:16).

Psalms 20:3

NKJV

Psalms 20:3 May He remember all your offerings, And accept your burnt sacrifice. Selah(NKJV)

NLT

Psalms 20:3 May he remember all your giftsand look favorably on your burnt offerings. Interlude (NLT)

Main Idea

The people pray that God would remember and favorably receive the king’s worship. Before the battle is fought, attention is directed to the king’s relationship with God and the offerings he has brought before Him.

Verse Explanation

“May He remember all your offerings”

The prayer continues for Israel’s king as he prepares for the day of trouble. “Remember” does not suggest that God might literally forget what the king has offered. In biblical language, when God “remembers,” the emphasis is often on His acting favorably toward someone or something He has taken into account.

“Offerings” refers to the gifts the king had presented to the LORD in worship. The people pray that God would look favorably upon the king’s devotion and receive what he has brought before Him.

“And accept your burnt sacrifice.”

The burnt offering was completely consumed on the altar and belonged wholly to God. Here the people ask that the king’s sacrifice would be “accepted” by the LORD-that He would regard it with favor.

The underlying expression is vivid and literally has the sense of finding the sacrifice “fat,” meaning rich or satisfactory. The point is not the physical size of the offering but God’s favorable reception of it.

“Selah”

“Selah” appears frequently in the Psalms, though its exact meaning is uncertain. It was probably a musical or liturgical notation, perhaps indicating an interlude or pause. At minimum, it marks a natural break and gives weight to what has just been prayed.

Study Notes

The offerings in this verse fit naturally with a setting in which the king and people sought the LORD before battle. Sacrifice was not a way to purchase military success from God. Israel’s worship expressed dependence upon Him and acknowledged that victory ultimately rested in His hands.

The wording behind “accept” is unusual and memorable. It comes from a word associated with fatness, which in the ancient world could signify richness or abundance. In context, it expresses the prayer that God would regard the king’s burnt offering favorably.

Spirit & Life

Before Psalm 20 speaks about banners, chariots, horses, or victory, it speaks about prayer, God’s presence, and worship. The battle matters, but the king’s posture before God matters first.

There is also a searching principle here: outward offerings have value only when God receives the worship behind them. The deepest question is never merely what we have brought to God, but whether what we bring comes from a heart genuinely surrendered to Him.

Psalms 20:4

NKJV

Psalms 20:4 May He grant you according to your heart's desire, And fulfill all your purpose.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalms 20:4 May he grant your heart’s desiresand make all your plans succeed. (NLT)

Main Idea

The people pray that God would grant the king the desires and plans he has formed for the coming conflict. The request assumes that those purposes have been brought before God and are aligned with the responsibilities God has given him.

Verse Explanation

“May He grant you according to your heart’s desire”

The people continue praying for the king as he faces “the day of trouble.” His “heart’s desire” in this setting is not a general promise that God will give someone whatever he wants. It refers particularly to what the king longs to see accomplished as he leads God’s people-deliverance, victory, and the success of the mission before him.

The prayer is that God would bring those desires to fulfillment. The king has worshiped and sought the LORD; now the people ask God to favor what is in his heart.

“And fulfill all your purpose.”

“Purpose” refers to the king’s plan or counsel-what he has determined to do in response to the crisis. A king preparing for battle would make real plans, arrange his forces, and decide how to proceed. Psalm 20 does not treat planning as contrary to trusting God.

Yet the people recognize that even the best plans require God’s help for their fulfillment. The king can form the purpose, but only God can ultimately cause it to succeed.

Study Notes

The parallel expressions “your heart’s desire” and “all your purpose” belong to the immediate setting of the royal prayer. They should not be detached from that context and treated as an unrestricted promise that God will fulfill every personal desire or plan.

The word behind “purpose” can carry the sense of counsel or plan. This fits the military setting particularly well: the king has a strategy, but the congregation asks God to bring that strategy to successful completion.

Spirit & Life

Psalm 20 holds planning and dependence together. The king has a “purpose,” yet the people still pray, “May He…fulfill” it. Trusting God does not eliminate thoughtful preparation; it keeps us from treating our preparation as though it were sufficient without Him.

There is also a healthy order here. The king has sought God, worshiped before Him, and then formed desires and plans for what lies ahead. The safest desires are not simply those we strongly want, but those that have been brought into God’s presence and flow from there.

Psalms 20:5

NKJV

Psalms 20:5 We will rejoice in your salvation, And in the name of our God we will set up our banners! May the LORD fulfill all your petitions.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalms 20:5 May we shout for joy when we hear of your victoryand raise a victory banner in the name of our God.May the LORD answer all your prayers. (NLT)

Main Idea

The people confidently anticipate celebrating the victory God will give their king. Their joy will center on God’s salvation, and their banners will openly identify their triumph with His name.

Verse Explanation

“We will rejoice in your salvation”

The prayer shifts from “you” to “we.” The king’s deliverance will become the people’s celebration because his victory affects the whole nation.

“Salvation” here includes very practical deliverance from the danger before them. The people are anticipating God rescuing the king and giving victory over his enemies. Their rejoicing will therefore be directed toward what God has done, not merely toward military success.

There is also confidence in “We will rejoice.” The battle has not yet been fought, but prayer has already begun to give way to expectation.

“And in the name of our God we will set up our banners!”

Banners were rallying standards associated with armies and victory. To raise them “in the name of our God” means that Israel will publicly identify its victory with the LORD. His name-not merely the king’s ability or Israel’s military strength-will stand over their celebration.

This continues the emphasis introduced in verse 1: “the name of the God of Jacob” is their security. The same God they called upon in trouble will receive the honor when deliverance comes.

“May the LORD fulfill all your petitions.”

The verse closes by gathering up the prayers offered for the king. His “petitions” are the requests he has brought before God concerning the crisis and the coming battle.

The people ask the LORD to answer them fully. Their confidence about future celebration does not make continued prayer unnecessary; confidence and petition remain side by side.

Study Notes

The shift from “you” to “we” is significant. The preceding verses have largely addressed the king, but his deliverance is not merely personal. As Israel’s representative ruler, what happens to him affects the people he leads.

The banners probably refer to military standards raised as visible signs of identity and triumph. The wording emphasizes that Israel’s banner is raised “in the name of our God.” This prepares for the sharp contrast in verse 7 between trusting military resources and remembering “the name of the LORD our God.”

Spirit & Life

There is a beautiful movement here from prayer to anticipated praise. The people are still asking God to act, yet they are already imagining the rejoicing that will follow His answer. Faith can pray earnestly about a battle that has not yet been won while remaining confident in the God to whom it is praying.

And when the victory comes, they know where the banner belongs: “in the name of our God.” God’s answers are not merely occasions for relief; they are opportunities to make much of the One who answered.

Psalms 20:6

NKJV

Psalms 20:6 Now I know that the LORD saves His anointed; He will answer him from His holy heaven With the saving strength of His right hand.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalms 20:6 Now I know that the LORD rescues his anointed king.He will answer him from his holy heavenand rescue him by his great power. (NLT)

Main Idea

Confidence now replaces petition: the LORD will save His anointed king and answer him with mighty power. Israel’s hope rests in God’s commitment to the king He has appointed and His ability to deliver.

Verse Explanation

“Now I know that the LORD saves His anointed”

The psalm shifts from the congregation’s prayers to a strong confession of confidence: “Now I know.” What has been requested in verses 1-5 is now expressed as something the speaker trusts God to do.

“His anointed” refers immediately to Israel’s king, who had been set apart for his royal calling through anointing with oil. The Hebrew term is mashiach, from which we get “Messiah.” In this verse, it first describes the reigning Davidic king, though the biblical theme of God’s Anointed ultimately reaches its fullest expression in Jesus Christ.

“He will answer him from His holy heaven”

Earlier the people prayed that help would come “from the sanctuary” and “out of Zion” (verse 2). Here God answers “from His holy heaven.” God’s presence among His people at Zion did not mean He was confined there. He reigns from heaven over everything.

The king can therefore call upon a God whose authority and power extend far beyond the battlefield.

“With the saving strength of His right hand.”

God’s “right hand” is a biblical picture of strength, authority, and victorious action. David is not describing God physically but using familiar imagery for His ability to intervene powerfully.

“Saving strength” joins deliverance and power together. The God who intends to save His anointed possesses all the strength necessary to accomplish that salvation.

Study Notes

The word “anointed” deserves careful attention. Mashiach simply means “anointed one” and can refer to an Israelite king. We should therefore first understand Psalm 20:6 in its historical royal setting rather than immediately treating every occurrence as a direct prediction of Jesus. At the same time, the developing biblical expectation of the ultimate Davidic King gives this royal language genuine messianic significance.

The contrast between “sanctuary” in verse 2 and “holy heaven” here is useful. God had chosen Zion as the earthly center of Israel’s worship, yet His throne and sovereign rule are heavenly. His presence at Zion never limited His greatness.

Spirit & Life

The movement from “May the LORD…” in the earlier verses to “Now I know…” is striking. Prayer has brought the speaker to settled confidence in God. The circumstances may not yet have changed, but something has become firm within him: the LORD is able to save.

For Christians, the language of “His anointed” ultimately opens onto Jesus, the true Messiah and Son of David. The victories of Israel’s earthly kings were temporary, but God’s final Anointed King has conquered sin and death. Our deepest confidence rests not in our ability to win our battles, but in the victorious King God has given us.

Psalms 20:7

NKJV

Psalms 20:7 Some trust in chariots, and some in horses; But we will remember the name of the LORD our God.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalms 20:7 Some nations boast of their chariots and horses,but we boast in the name of the LORD our God. (NLT)

Main Idea

Some people place their confidence in visible strength and human resources, but God’s people place their confidence in the LORD Himself. The decisive issue is not what resources we possess, but whom we ultimately trust.

Verse Explanation

“Some trust in chariots, and some in horses”

Chariots and horses represented some of the most formidable military resources of the ancient world. They provided speed, mobility, and battlefield power. Nations possessing large numbers of them could appear almost unbeatable.

David is not necessarily condemning the use of military resources. The issue is “trust.” Chariots and horses become dangerous when confidence shifts from God to the strength we can see, count, organize, and control.

The verse therefore exposes the temptation created by visible power. What appears strong can easily begin to feel more dependable than the God who cannot be seen.

“But we will remember the name of the LORD our God.”

Israel’s alternative is not passivity but God-centered confidence. To “remember” the LORD’s name means more than mentally recalling it. It means calling Him to mind in such a way that His character, power, faithfulness, and past acts shape how they face the present crisis.

“The name of the LORD” represents the LORD as He has made Himself known. Israel can face armies with chariots and horses because they know who their God is.

The words “our God” make the confession personal. He is not merely a God whose power they have heard about; He is the God to whom they belong and upon whom they have learned to depend.

Study Notes

Israel’s law specifically warned its kings not to “multiply horses for himself” (Deuteronomy 17:16). The concern was larger than the animals themselves. Israel’s king was not to build his security around the same forms of military power upon which surrounding nations depended.

The contrast in Psalm 20 is carefully developed. Verse 1 speaks of “the name of the God of Jacob,” verse 5 raises banners “in the name of our God,” and verse 7 reaches the central confession: “we will remember the name of the LORD our God.” God’s name stands over the entire battle.

Spirit & Life

Our chariots and horses look different, but the temptation remains. Money, ability, connections, planning, technology, position, and experience are all useful resources, yet any of them can quietly become the place where our confidence rests.

David does not tell us to pretend those resources do not exist. He tells us where ultimate confidence belongs. Faith is not having nothing else available; it is having other things available and still knowing that none of them can take the place of “the LORD our God.”

Psalms 20:8

NKJV

Psalms 20:8 They have bowed down and fallen; But we have risen and stand upright.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalms 20:8 Those nations will fall down and collapse,but we will rise up and stand firm. (NLT)

Main Idea

Those who trust in human strength ultimately collapse, but those who trust in the LORD are upheld. The verse shows the final contrast between confidence in visible power and confidence in God.

Verse Explanation

“They have bowed down and fallen”

“They” refers to those in verse 7 who trust in “chariots” and “horses.” The military strength that appeared so impressive has not been able to preserve them. They are pictured first bending or sinking down and then falling completely.

The language is especially fitting in a battle setting. Chariots and horses suggested strength, mobility, and superiority, yet the people relying upon them end up on the ground. David’s point is not that resources have no value, but that they cannot bear the weight of ultimate trust.

“But we have risen and stand upright.”

The contrast is complete. Those who appeared strongest have fallen, while those who remembered “the name of the LORD our God” have been enabled to rise and remain standing.

“Stand upright” carries the sense of being established or restored to a firm position. Their security does not come from possessing superior resources but from the God in whom they have placed their confidence.

Study Notes

There is a striking reversal between verses 7 and 8. Chariots and horses appear to give one side the advantage, yet the outcome is exactly opposite to what outward appearances might suggest: “They…fallen,” but “we…stand upright.”

The Hebrew verbs can carry the sense of the enemy collapsing while God’s people rise and are firmly established. The verse is therefore not merely about surviving the battle but about God overturning the apparent advantage of those who trusted in military strength.

Spirit & Life

Psalm 20 does not deny that some things look more secure than others. Chariots really were powerful. Horses really did provide military advantage. Faith does not require pretending visible strength is unreal; it refuses to treat visible strength as ultimate.

There are moments when what we can see seems to favor everything except trust in God. Verse 8 reminds us that appearances at the beginning of the battle do not determine who will be standing at the end.

Psalms 20:9

NKJV

Psalms 20:9 Save, LORD! May the King answer us when we call.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalms 20:9 Give victory to our king, O LORD!Answer our cry for help. (NLT)

Main Idea

Psalm 20 ends where it began-with prayer. After expressing strong confidence in God’s power to give victory, the people still call upon the LORD to save and to answer them when they cry.

Verse Explanation

“Save, LORD!”

The closing prayer is brief and urgent. “Save” gathers up the central request of the entire psalm: the people need the LORD to intervene and give deliverance.

This is significant after the confidence expressed in verses 6-8. They have declared that the LORD saves His anointed, rejected trust in chariots and horses, and anticipated standing victorious. Yet confidence does not eliminate prayer. Because they trust God, they continue calling upon Him.

“May the King answer us when we call.”

The most natural reading takes “the King” as the LORD Himself. The psalm has prayed for Israel’s earthly king, but it closes by looking beyond him to the supreme King upon whom both ruler and people depend.

“Answer us” also brings the psalm full circle. Verse 1 began, “May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble,” speaking to the king. Now the entire people pray that God would answer “us.” King and people alike depend upon the LORD’s response.

“When we call” expresses their continuing posture of dependence. Their confidence is not self-confidence after prayer; it remains active reliance upon God.

Study Notes

The Hebrew of the final line can be punctuated or understood differently. The NKJV reads “May the King answer us when we call,” making God the King who answers. Some translations render the line along the lines of “O LORD, save the king! May he answer us when we call,” taking “king” as Israel’s human ruler. The Hebrew permits discussion, but the NKJV reading fits the psalm’s larger emphasis on the LORD as the ultimate source of salvation.

There is a deliberate echo between verses 1 and 9: “May the LORD answer you” becomes “answer us when we call.” The whole psalm is enclosed by dependence upon God’s answering prayer.

Spirit & Life

One of the strongest features of Psalm 20 is that confidence never outgrows dependence. “Now I know that the LORD saves” in verse 6 does not make “Save, LORD!” unnecessary in verse 9. Biblical confidence keeps praying because its confidence is in the One being called upon.

And the final title matters: “the King.” Earthly kings need saving; armies can fall; chariots and horses can fail. Above them all stands the true King. Our security rests finally not in the strength of what we possess, but in the God who hears when His people call.
