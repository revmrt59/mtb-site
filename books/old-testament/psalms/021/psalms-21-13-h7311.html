EXALTED - ( rum , Strong's H7311 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb rum fundamentally means to be high, lofty, or physically elevated. When applied to God, it transcends physical height to describe His absolute supremacy, majesty, and sovereign authority. In Psalm 21:13, the call for Yahweh to be exalted is both a declaration of His supreme rule and a prayer for Him to display His unmatched power in the sight of humanity. It invites God to take His rightful place of supreme honor, prompting His people to respond with worship and praise.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, height was synonymous with power, security, and divinity. High walls protected cities, lofty mountains were viewed as the abodes of the gods, and a king’s throne was physically elevated above his subjects to project authority. The word rum captures this physical concept of rising up or being set on high. It is used to describe Noah’s ark rising above the earth, mountains towering over valleys, and a ruler lifting his scepter. When biblical writers applied this term to Yahweh, they adapted a familiar spatial metaphor to communicate His ontological and moral supremacy. God is not merely physically high; He is infinitely above all creation in holiness, power, and glory.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root rum occurs over 190 times in the Old Testament. It functions primarily as a verb with several distinct nuances depending on the grammatical stem:

• To be high or lofty: Expressing physical stature or metaphorical supremacy.

• To rise or rise up: Describing movement upward.

• To lift up or exalt (Active/Causative): Raising someone to a position of honor, power, or success.

• To be exalted (Passive/Intransitive): Being highly praised, extolled, or recognized as supreme.

In Psalm 21:13, the verb appears in the Qal imperfect (functioning as a jussive), which translates as a petition or a passionate acclamation: "Be exalted!" It is a plea for God to actively manifest His inherent greatness so that His creation cannot ignore it.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Hebrew Scriptures use rum in several descriptive ways:

• The Upraised Hand of Power: God's "exalted hand" (Numbers 33:3) represents His active, visible deliverance of Israel from Egypt. It is a picture of triumph and sovereign victory.

• The Horn of Strength: To "exalt the horn" (Psalm 89:17) is a metaphor derived from horned animals, symbolizing the restoration of strength, dignity, and victory to the righteous.

• The Snare of Pride: Negatively, rum describes the "lifting up" of a person's heart or eyes in arrogance (Deuteronomy 8:14; Psalm 131:1). Human self-exaltation is always presented as an offensive counterfeit to God’s true loftiness.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of rum rests on the doctrine of God’s transcendence. God does not need human permission or assistance to be exalted; He is inherently supreme. However, when scripture commands or requests God to "be exalted," it is a prayer for His hidden majesty to be made visible to a rebellious or blind world.

Furthermore, God’s self-exaltation is never selfish or tyrannical. When God exalts Himself by demonstrating His strength, He simultaneously delivers His covenant people. His glory and His people's good are perfectly joined. The proper human response to His self-exaltation is not terror, but glad adoration.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 21 is a royal psalm celebrating a military victory granted by Yahweh to the king (David). The psalm opens with praise for God's strength (v. 1) and details the blessings, crown, and victories God has bestowed on the monarch.

The final verse (v. 13) serves as a grand doxological climax to the entire psalm. It shifts from speaking about God to addressing Him directly: "Be exalted, O LORD, in Your own strength!"

• "In Your own strength": This phrase emphasizes that God’s triumph does not depend on human armies, strategic alliances, or the king's prowess. His victory is self-sustained and self-wrought.

• "We will sing and praise Your power": The king and the congregation recognize that when God lifts Himself up in victory, the only appropriate response is corporate, joyful worship. God acts, and His people sing.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 46:10 - "Be still, and know that I am God; I will be exalted among the nations, I will be exalted in the earth!"

• Psalm 57:5 - "Be exalted, O God, above the heavens; Let Your glory be above all the earth."

• Psalm 18:46 - "The LORD lives! Blessed be my Rock! Let the God of my salvation be exalted."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The root of rum is preserved in several Hebrew proper names, most notably Abram (meaning "exalted father"), which was later changed by God to Abraham ("father of a multitude"). This linguistic link highlights how the concept of "exaltation" was deeply embedded in the patriarchal covenant and the identity of God's chosen family.
