MEET - ( qadam , Strong's H6923 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qadam refers to going before, anticipating, or meeting someone with active intent. In Psalm 21:3, it describes God's proactive grace, where He sovereignly anticipates the king's needs and meets him with rich blessings of goodness before he even asks.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of qadam is associated with the noun qedem, which means "front," "east," or "ancient times." Spatially, the east was the direction ancient Israelites faced to determine orientation; thus, "front" and "before" are closely linked. The word picture is one of movement—stepping out in front of someone, arriving ahead of time to prepare a place, or running to encounter a person on their journey. It evokes the image of a host who does not wait for a guest to reach the door, but runs down the road to greet them with gifts and provisions, taking the absolute initiative.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb qadam occurs in the Piel stem in Psalm 21:3. It signifies:

• To go before or precede in time.

• To meet or encounter, either in a friendly or hostile manner.

• To anticipate, forestall, or do something beforehand.

• To bring something quickly to meet someone's immediate need.

In the context of divine action, it denotes God's preventive and prevenient actions, where He acts in advance of human prayer or effort to secure a favorable outcome.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs qadam in several distinct ways:

• Hostile Confrontation: It describes enemies who surprise and attack, standing in the way of a person during a crisis (2 Samuel 22:19).

• Hospitable Greeting: It denotes meeting travelers with life-sustaining provisions, a duty of hospitality that Moab and Ammon failed to perform for Israel (Deuteronomy 23:4).

• Prayer and Worship: It describes a person rising early to meet God in prayer before the sun comes up, or coming before His presence with thanksgiving (Psalm 119:147; Psalm 95:2).

• Divine Intervention: It depicts God's mercy rushing forward to intercept a believer in distress, ensuring that grace arrives before despair takes hold (Psalm 79:8).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, qadam highlights the doctrine of prevenient grace. God is the first actor in salvation and blessing. Before humanity seeks God, God has already reached out to humanity. His love is initiative-taking, not merely responsive. It also underscores God's meticulous sovereignty. He knows what His servants need before they ask, preparing blessings in eternity past to be delivered at the precise moment of need in the present. In a Messianic sense, it points to the Father's anticipation of the Son's obedience, rewarding the Messiah with glory, honor, and an eternal crown.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 21:3, David celebrates the victories and blessings God has bestowed upon him. The king had prayed for life and deliverance (verse 4), but God’s response exceeded his requests. God did not wait for David to earn these blessings or struggle through a long period of deprivation. Instead, God met him with "the blessings of goodness." The use of qadam here emphasizes that God's favor was proactive. Before David could even articulate his deep need for stability and validation as king, God was already there, placing a crown of pure gold upon his head. God's grace outran David’s petitions.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 23:4 - "...because they did not meet you with bread and water on the way when you came out of Egypt..."

• Psalm 59:10 - "My God of mercy shall meet me; God shall let me see my desire on my enemies."

• Psalm 79:8 - "Oh, do not remember former iniquities against us! Let Your tender mercies speedily meet us, For we have been brought very low."

• Psalm 119:147 - "I rise before the dawning of the morning, And cry for help; I hope in Your word."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The King James Version famously translates qadam in this passage and others as "prevent" (e.g., "For thou preventest him with the blessings of goodness"). In seventeenth-century English, "prevent" retained its Latin root meaning (praevenire), which means "to go before." While modern readers associate "prevent" with stopping or hindering, the original English translation perfectly captured the Hebrew concept of God going before His people to prepare their path. This linguistic shift highlights the importance of matching ancient Hebrew concepts with accurate modern equivalents like "meet" or "anticipate."
