MERCY - ( chesed , Strong's H2617 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chesed represents the unfailing, loyal, covenant-keeping love of God. Far more than a fleeting emotion or mere pity, it is an active, persistent commitment to show favor, grace, and redemption to those in covenant relationship with Him. In Psalm 21:7, this "mercy" is the bedrock of the king's stability, ensuring that he will never be shaken because he rests in the secure, unchanging devotion of the Most High.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concept of chesed is deeply rooted in the ancient Near Eastern understanding of covenant (berit). When two parties entered into a covenant, they bound themselves to act with loyalty, kindness, and reliability toward one another. In the biblical context, chesed is almost exclusively used to describe the relational devotion that exists within a covenant framework.

The word picture is not one of detached legal obligation, but of a passionate, enduring commitment. It is the love of a spouse who remains faithful through every trial, or a parent who refuses to abandon a wayward child. It combines the ideas of love, loyalty, and strength. When applied to God, it highlights His voluntary decision to bind Himself to His people, promising to act on their behalf with redeeming grace, even when they fail to keep their end of the covenant.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of chesed is exceptionally broad, making it one of the most difficult Hebrew words to translate with a single English term. It encompasses:

• Covenant Loyalty: Faithful adherence to a promised relationship.

• Lovingkindness: Active, affectionate goodness directed toward another.

• Mercy and Grace: Unmerited favor and compassion shown to those in need or distress.

• Unfailing Love: Persistent devotion that does not give up or lapse over time.

While classical translations like the King James Version frequently render it as "mercy" or "lovingkindness," modern translations often use "steadfast love" or "unfailing love" to capture its dual emphasis on affection and permanence. It is goodness in action, motivated by a deep relational bond.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, chesed is used to describe both human-to-human relationships (such as the loyalty between Jonathan and David) and God’s relationship with humanity.

• The Unshakable Cord: God’s chesed is frequently paired with His emet (truth, faithfulness, or reliability). Together, these terms form a hendiadys representing God's completely dependable, loving character.

• An Enduring Canopy: In the Psalms, chesed is described as reaching to the heavens (Psalm 36:5) and enduring forever (Psalm 136). It functions as a protective shield and an atmosphere of safety for the believer.

• A Active Pursuer: In Psalm 23:6, the writer declares that goodness and chesed will "follow" (literally, hunt or pursue) him all the days of his life. God’s loyal love is active, tracking down His children to bless them.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, chesed defines the very nature of God’s relational character.

• The Foundation of Revelation: When God revealed His glory to Moses on Mount Sinai, He self-identified as "abounding in goodness [chesed] and truth" (Exodus 34:6). This is the definitive Old Testament confession of God's character.

• The Ground of Salvation: Israel's hope of forgiveness and restoration was never based on their own merit, but solely on God’s abundant chesed. He forgives because He is loyal to His own covenant promises.

• The Basis of the Davidic Covenant: God promised David that while He might discipline his descendants, His chesed would never depart from him as it did from Saul (2 Samuel 7:15). This guarantees the ultimate arrival of the Messiah, the ultimate expression of God's covenant loyalty.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 21:7, the setting is a royal psalm of thanksgiving and triumph. The king (traditionally David) has experienced victory, answering prayers, and blessings of goodness.

The verse states: "For the king trusts in the LORD, and through the mercy [chesed] of the Most High he shall not be moved."

Here, chesed acts as the ultimate stabilizer for human leadership:

• A Shared Dependency: The verse sets up a parallel between human trust and divine loyalty. The king places his trust (batach) in Yahweh, and in response, the chesed of the Most High (Elyon) preserves him.

• Immovable Security: The phrase "he shall not be moved" (bal-yimmot) refers to being shaken, overthrown, or slipping. A king's throne in the ancient world was highly unstable, constantly threatened by rebellion and war. Yet, this king is secure. His stability does not rest on his military strategy, financial wealth, or political alliances, but on the loyal love of God.

• The Title "Most High": By pairing chesed with Elyon (the Most High, the supreme Sovereign over all nations), the psalmist emphasizes that the highest authority in the universe is personally, loyally committed to upholding the king.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 20:6 - "...but showing mercy to thousands, to those who love Me and keep My commandments."

• 2 Samuel 7:15 - "But My mercy shall not depart from him, as I took it from Saul, whom I removed from before you."

• Psalm 23:6 - "Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me All the days of my life; And I will dwell in the house of the LORD Forever."

• Psalm 136:1 - "Oh, give thanks to the LORD, for He is good! For His mercy endures forever."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), chesed is most frequently translated using the Greek word eleos (mercy, compassion, active pity). In the New Testament, this concept expands into the full revelation of charis (grace) and eleos in the person of Jesus Christ. Jesus is the ultimate manifestation of God's chesed—the Word made flesh, full of grace and truth, sent to fulfill the covenant promises of God to His people.
