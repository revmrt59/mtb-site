WRATH - ( 'aph , Strong's H639 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'aph literally refers to the "nose" or "nostril," but is most frequently used figuratively in the Old Testament to denote "anger," "wrath," or "burning displeasure." The imagery is derived from the rapid, flared breathing of an angry person or animal, suggesting a passionate, visible manifestation of burning indignation. In the context of Psalm 21:9, 'aph represents the intense, consuming judgment of Yahweh against His enemies, functioning not as an arbitrary, out-of-control temper, but as the righteous, holy reaction of the Divine King against wickedness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The primary concrete meaning of 'aph is "nose," "nostril," or "face" (as in Genesis 2:7, where God breathed the breath of life into man's nostrils).

• In the ancient Near East, deep, heavy, or hot breathing through the nose was understood as the primary physical indicator of intense rage. Thus, when a person's anger "burns," the nostrils flare, and hot breath is expelled.

• This anthropomorphic language is applied to God to communicate the reality, intensity, and personal nature of His righteous opposition to sin. It portrays His wrath not as a cold, detached judicial decree, but as a passionate, active, and consuming force.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The noun 'aph possesses a dual range of meaning. Physically, it refers to the nose, face, or nostrils. Figuratively, and far more frequently, it refers to anger, wrath, or heat of passion.

• When applied to God, it denotes His holy, burning anger directed against covenant violation, injustice, and rebellion. The term emphasizes the sudden, powerful expression of divine judgment that sweeps away opposition.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• 'Aph is often paired with the verb charah ("to burn"), forming the phrase "his anger burned" (literally, "his nose grew hot").

• In the Psalms, God's 'aph is frequently associated with fire, smoke, and devouring heat, indicating that His wrath has a tangible, destructive effect on those who oppose Him.

• This word picture contrasts God's patience (expressed as 'arek 'appayim, literally "long of nose" or "slow to anger") with the sudden, fierce ignition of His wrath when the day of mercy has ended.

4. Theological Implications

• Divine 'aph is never an irrational, petulant tantrum. It is the necessary reaction of absolute holiness against moral evil.

• Because God is holy and just, His love for righteousness requires an active, burning opposition to wickedness.

• The wrath of God in the Old Testament is intrinsically tied to His covenant. To violate the covenant or harm His anointed king (as in Psalm 21) is to provoke the divine 'aph.

• It serves as a sobering reminder of the gravity of sin and the absolute certainty of divine judgment for those who reject His grace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• Psalm 21 is a royal psalm celebrating the victory and coronation of the Davidic king.

• In verse 9, the psalmist details the ultimate fate of the king's enemies, who are also God's enemies.

• The parallel structures emphasize total destruction: "fiery oven" (v. 9a), "swallow them up in His wrath ['aph]" (v. 9b), and "fire shall devour them" (v. 9c).

• Here, 'aph acts as the driving force behind the consuming fire. God's wrath is not passive disapproval; it is an active, swallowing tide of judgment. The Lord's personal presence (the "presence" or "face" mentioned in the first half of the verse) brings the consuming heat of His 'aph upon those who rebel against His anointed.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 2:12 - "Kiss the Son, lest He be angry, And you perish in the way, When His wrath is kindled but a little. Blessed are all those who put their trust in Him."

• Psalm 78:38 - "But He, being full of compassion, forgave their iniquity, And did not destroy them. Yes, many a time He turned His anger away, And did not stir up all His wrath."

• Psalm 90:7 - "For we have been consumed by Your anger, And by Your wrath we are terrified."

• Micah 7:18 - "Who is a God like You, Pardoning iniquity And passing over the transgression of the remnant of His heritage? He does not retain His anger forever, Because He delights in mercy."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The opposite of God's quick 'aph (anger) is His character trait of being 'arek 'appayim—literally "long of nostrils"—which the NKJV translates as "slow to anger" or "longsuffering." This idiomatic contrast shows that while His wrath is a real, consuming fire, His default disposition toward His covenant people is one of immense patience, holding back the fire of His judgment to allow space for repentance.

• In the Septuagint (LXX), 'aph is most frequently translated by the Greek words orge (settled, controlled anger) or thymos (passionate, boiling anger). In Psalm 21:9 (LXX 20:10), the Greek translators used orge ("The Lord shall swallow them up in His wrath [orge]").
