FORSAKEN - ( 'azab , Strong's H5800 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'azab means to leave, abandon, neglect, or loose. In the context of Psalm 22:1, it expresses the agonizing sense of utter desertion, where a person feels completely cut off from God's presence, active help, and covenantal protection. Prophetically, this term points to the ultimate abandonment experienced by Jesus Christ on the cross when He bore the sins of the world, making it one of the most doctrinally significant words in the Old Testament.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical background of 'azab involves letting go of something, leaving something behind, or loosening a knot or burden. In its earliest usage, it described unloosing a beast of burden or releasing a load. Over time, the word developed a relational and emotional color. It came to describe physically walking away from a place, leaving a family member, or deserting a partner. The word picture is not merely of distance, but of active relinquishment—deliberately choosing not to hold onto, protect, or maintain an association with someone or something.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb 'azab occurs over 200 times in the Hebrew Bible. Its semantic range includes:

• To leave behind or depart from a place or person.

• To abandon or desert someone in distress, leaving them vulnerable to enemies.

• To neglect or fail to care for (such as neglecting the house of God or the poor).

• To loose, release, or let go of a physical object or burden.

• To forsake a covenant, law, or relationship (frequently used of Israel forsaking God).

In the context of personal lament, 'azab denotes the terrifying reality or perception of being abandoned by one's protector, leaving the sufferer entirely exposed to ruin.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Covenantal Departure: A man leaves ('azab) his father and mother to cleave to his wife (Genesis 2:24). This shows the positive aspect of leaving a primary relationship to establish a new, deeper covenant bond.

• The Physical Unloading: A person is commanded to help a neighbor's donkey that is struggling under a heavy load by helping to release ('azab) it (Exodus 23:5).

• The Tragic Abandonment: A wife of youth who is cast off and rejected is described as "forsaken and grieved in spirit" (Isaiah 54:6). Here, the word paints a picture of relational betrayal and deep emotional pain.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'azab stands at the center of covenant faithfulness. God repeatedly promises His people that He will never leave them nor forsake them (Deuteronomy 31:6; Joshua 1:5). Conversely, Israel's history is characterized by their repeatedly forsaking God to serve idols.

When a righteous sufferer cries out that God has forsaken him, it creates a profound covenantal tension. If God is faithful, why has He abandoned His servant? This tension reaches its peak on the cross. Jesus quoted the Aramaic equivalent of Psalm 22:1 ("My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?"). At that moment, the Father did not merely hide His face; He judicially withdrew His fellowship and hand of protection from the Son, who became sin for us. Jesus experienced the literal, horrific reality of 'azab so that believers would never have to experience it.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:1, David is in extreme distress, surrounded by enemies who mock his faith. The nuance of 'azab here is not a permanent loss of salvation, but the acute, painful perception that God is completely silent and inactive while His servant suffers.

David pairs "forsaken" with the phrase, "Why are You so far from helping Me?" To be 'azab by God in this context means to be left to the mercy of cruel adversaries without any divine intervention. Yet, the opening words, "My God, My God," demonstrate that even in the midst of felt abandonment, the sufferer clings to God in covenant faith. The cry of being forsaken is not a denial of God's existence or covenant, but a desperate appeal for God to act in accordance with His character.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:24: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and they shall become one flesh."

• Deuteronomy 31:6: "Be strong and of good courage, do not fear nor be afraid of them; for the LORD your God, He is the One who goes with you. He will not leave you nor forsake you."

• Joshua 1:5: "No man shall be able to stand before you all the days of your life; as I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will not leave you nor forsake you."

• Isaiah 49:14: "But Zion said, 'The LORD has forsaken me, And my Lord has forgotten me.'"

• Isaiah 54:6: "For the LORD has called you Like a woman forsaken and grieved in spirit, Like a youthful wife when you were refused,' Says your God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) renders 'azab in Psalm 22:1 using the verb egkataleipo. This is the exact Greek verb used in Matthew 27:46 and Mark 15:34 when Jesus cries out from the cross. It is also the word used in Hebrews 13:5, which quotes the Old Testament promise: "I will never leave you nor forsake you." The linguistic link connects the agonizing cry of the cross directly to the comforting promise of eternal presence for the believer. Because Christ was forsaken ('azab / egkataleipo), the believer is guaranteed never to be forsaken.
