ROARING - ( sheagah , Strong's H7581 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sheagah refers to a loud, deep, resonant cry, most literally the fierce roar of a lion or, figuratively, the intense, agonizing groan of a human being in deep physical or spiritual distress. In Psalm 22:1, though translated as "groaning" in the NKJV, the word carries this primary animalistic, gut-wrenching quality of a "roaring" cry. It denotes an expressive, uninhibited outlet of severe pain, sorrow, and perceived abandonment, capturing the raw intensity of the speaker’s suffering. It is not a quiet whimper or a silent prayer, but a loud, desperate cry that pours out of a crushed soul, finding its ultimate prophetic fulfillment in the agonizing cry of Jesus Christ on the cross.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word sheagah is derived from the verbal root shaag, which means "to roar" or "to cry out." The primary word picture is that of a lion roaring in the wilderness. In the ancient Near East, the roar of a lion was a sound of absolute power, terror, and raw, unrestrained energy, signaling danger, hunger, or distress. When applied to human experience, this word shifts from a sound of predatory power to a sound of desperate, uncontrollable suffering. It represents the guttural, unarticulated cry of a person whose agony has bypassed intellectual speech and burst forth as a raw, physical sound. It paints a picture of a sufferer who is completely overwhelmed, whose pain is so heavy that it can only express itself in animal-like roars or deep groanings of distress.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• sheagah means a roaring, a groaning, or an agonizing cry. The noun occurs only a few times in the Old Testament, split between literal animal roars and figurative human cries of anguish:

• In its literal sense, it refers to the loud, terrifying roar of a lion.

• In its figurative sense, it refers to the deep, rumbling groan of a human in extreme distress, physical suffering, or spiritual agony. It indicates a sound that is loud, continuous, and expressive of profound grief or a crushed spirit.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses sheagah to describe the overwhelming nature of grief and spiritual conviction:

• The Torrent of Grief: In Job 3:24, Job describes his "groanings" (sheagah) pouring out like water, suggesting a ceaseless, fluid torrent of agonizing cries that cannot be contained.

• The Weight of Conviction: In Psalm 32:3, David describes the physical toll of unconfessed sin, where his bones grew old through his "groaning" (sheagah) all day long. Here, the roar is the sound of a conscience under the heavy, pressing hand of God.

• The word picture highlights that some suffering is too deep for articulate words. It is a crying out from the very depths of one's being, mimicking the loud, unstoppable vibration of a lion's roar, but filled with sorrow instead of strength.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of sheagah in the Scriptures represents the extremity of human suffering in a fallen world, showing that God-inspired Scripture validates and records the rawest forms of human grief. God does not demand polite or muted prayers; He hears the desperate "roaring" of the brokenhearted.

Crucially, this word carries profound Messianic implications. Psalm 22 is the ultimate Crucifixion psalm. When Christ cried out, "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" He was embodying the very essence of sheagah. His cry on the cross was not a quiet theological reflection but a loud, agonizing cry of absolute abandonment as He bore the wrath of God for sin. The word underscores the reality of the substitutionary atonement: the sinless Savior experienced the ultimate spiritual "roaring" of separation from the Father so that His people might never be eternally forsaken.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:1, David is in a state of severe crisis, feeling completely abandoned by God. The structure of the verse contrasts the holiness and near-presence of God with David's own sense of distance: "Why are You so far from helping Me, and from the words of my groaning [roaring]?"

The phrase "the words of my groaning" contains a striking paradox. "Words" (dabar) implies articulate speech, while "groaning/roaring" (sheagah) implies inarticulate, animal-like cries. David's prayers have degenerated from structured petitions into raw, repetitive, roaring cries of pain.

While the NKJV translates this as "groaning," which softens the raw Hebrew imagery, understanding it as "roaring" exposes the animal-like desperation of the Psalmist (and ultimately Christ) under the weight of divine abandonment. It is the cry of a soul being devoured by circumstances, yet still directed "to" God ("My God, My God").

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Job 3:24 – "For my sighing comes before I eat, And my groanings pour out like water."

• Psalm 32:3 – "When I kept silent, my bones grew old Through my groaning all the day long."

• Isaiah 5:29 – "Their roaring will be like a lion, They will roar like young lions; Yes, they will roar and lay hold of the prey; They will carry it away safely, And no one will deliver."

• Ezekiel 19:7 – "He knew their desolate places, And laid waste their cities; The land with its fullness was desolated By the noise of his roaring."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A fascinating textual variant exists in the ancient translations of Psalm 22:1. The Hebrew Masoretic Text clearly reads sheagathi ("my roaring/groaning"). However, the Greek Septuagint (LXX) translates this phrase as ton paraptomaton mou, which means "of my transgressions" (rendering the phrase "far from my salvation are the words of my transgressions"). The LXX translators likely misread the Hebrew root or chose to interpret the suffering as a result of sin.

This distinction is highly significant for Christology. When Jesus quoted Psalm 22:1 on the cross, He quoted the Hebrew/Aramaic text, not the Septuagint. Because He was entirely sinless, He could not speak of "the words of my transgressions." However, He fully experienced "the words of my roaring"—the agonizing, raw cry of a perfect soul enduring the terrifying weight of divine judgment on behalf of others.
