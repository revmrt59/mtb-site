CAST - ( shalak , Strong's H7993 )

Summary:

In Psalm 22:10, the Hebrew verb shalak (in the passive Hophal form) translates to being "cast" or thrown upon God from birth. It expresses a state of total, involuntary dependency and absolute trust. Rather than implying rejection, being "cast upon" God signifies a deliberate positioning of care—like an infant placed immediately into the secure arms of a guardian. It highlights God's lifelong, sovereign ownership and the believer's enduring reliance on Him from the very first breath of life.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primitive root shalak fundamentally refers to the action of throwing, flinging, or hurling something. In everyday ancient Near Eastern life, this verb was used for physical actions such as casting stones, throwing a staff to the ground, or casting a net. It was also used in darker contexts, such as throwing a corpse into a pit or casting someone out of a city.

However, in Psalm 22:10, the verb is used in the Hophal (passive) form, hoshlakti, meaning "I was cast." When paired with the preposition al ("upon"), the physical word picture of throwing transforms into a beautiful metaphor of protective custody. It depicts a newborn infant who, immediately upon leaving the womb, is not abandoned or left to fend for itself, but is carefully placed ("cast") directly into the lap or arms of its father or guardian to be claimed, protected, and sustained.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shalak carries several primary meanings depending on its grammatical stem and context:

• To throw or hurl: The basic action of sending an object through the air with force.

• To cast away or reject: In the Hiphil stem, it frequently denotes casting off a yoke, throwing away idols, or God casting people out of His sight due to rebellion.

• To be thrown or deposited: In the passive Hophal stem, it denotes being placed, thrust, or dropped into a specific state, location, or relationship.

• To commit to another's care: When used idiomatically with the preposition "upon," it means to shift one's weight, burdens, or very existence onto someone else, transferring the responsibility of preservation to the receiver.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament utilizes shalak to paint vivid pictures of spiritual realities:

• The Picture of Divine Forgiveness: God casts all our sins into the depths of the sea, rendering them gone and forgotten.

• The Picture of Judgment and Rejection: The wicked are cast out like a useless branch or thrown into a pit of destruction.

• The Picture of Active Trust: Believers are commanded to cast their burdens upon the Lord, shifting the heavy load of anxiety off their own shoulders and onto God's infinite strength.

• The Picture of Sovereign Entrustment: In Psalm 22:10, the passive "I was cast" demonstrates that the speaker's relationship with God was established before the speaker had the conscious ability to choose. It is a picture of absolute, God-initiated security.

4. Theological Implications

This term carries profound theological weight regarding God's sovereignty and the believer's security:

• Prevenient Grace and Divine Election: Being "cast upon" God from the womb illustrates that God's care and ownership precede our conscious faith. Before the psalmist could speak, pray, or choose, God was already designated as his caregiver and God.

• Messianic Fulfillment: Psalm 22 is a deeply Messianic psalm, quoted by Jesus on the cross. While Christ suffered the ultimate human rejection and felt the agony of being forsaken, He comforted Himself with the reality of shalak—that from His incarnation in the womb, His entire human existence was cast upon the Father in perfect, uninterrupted dependency.

• The Fatherhood of God: In ancient Hebrew culture, a father officially acknowledged a child as his own by placing the newborn on his knees. To be "cast upon" God from birth implies that God immediately claimed paternal responsibility for the psalmist's life.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22, the psalmist is surrounded by mockers, facing intense physical and emotional suffering. In verses 7 and 8, his enemies mock his trust in God, saying, "He trusted in the LORD, let Him rescue Him."

In response, the psalmist appeals to his history with God in verses 9 and 10. He argues that his dependency on Yahweh is not a recent, fragile phase of life, but an foundational reality. He was "taken... out of the womb" (v. 9) and immediately "cast upon" God (v. 10). The nuance is one of bold defense against mockery: My trust in God is not a groundless boast; I have been His responsibility from the moment I drew my first breath. He cannot abandon me now, for I have no other guardian.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 51:11: "Do not cast me away from Your presence, and do not take Your Holy Spirit from me."

• Psalm 55:22: "Cast your burden on the LORD, and He shall sustain you; He shall never permit the righteous to be moved."

• Micah 7:19: "He will again have compassion on us, and will subdue our iniquities. You will cast all our sins into the depths of the sea."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) translates hoshlakti in Psalm 22:10 with the Greek verb aperriphen (from aporripto), which means "to be thrown upon" or "cast upon." This reinforces the passive nature of the action. The infant did not throw itself; it was placed there by the sovereign hand of God through the circumstances of birth.

Furthermore, this passage provides deep pastoral comfort. It reminds those who feel abandoned by their earthly parents, or who feel entirely helpless in the face of suffering, that their ultimate standing is not based on their own strength, but on the fact that they have been deposited into the hands of a faithful Creator who has sustained them from day one.
