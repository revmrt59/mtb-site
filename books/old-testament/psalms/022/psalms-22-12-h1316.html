BASHAN - ( Bashan , Strong's H1316 )

Summary:

Bashan is a fertile, elevated region east of the Jordan River, renowned in the Old Testament for its rich volcanic soil, lush pastures, towering oak trees, and massive, well-fed livestock. In the messianic lament of Psalm 22:12, the "strong bulls of Bashan" serve as a vivid and terrifying metaphor for the powerful, arrogant, and aggressive enemies who surrounded the suffering Savior during His crucifixion. These adversaries were not merely strong; they were pampered by the prosperity of the world, prideful in their station, and completely unrestrained in their hostility, perfectly prefiguring the spiritual principalities and human authorities who conspired to mock and crucify Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

Geographically, Bashan was the northernmost region of the Transjordan, stretching from the border of Gilead in the south to the slopes of Mount Hermon in the north. It was a region blessed with rich volcanic soil and heavy rainfall, making it the premier agricultural and pastoral land in the ancient Near East. Because of these ideal conditions, the livestock raised in Bashan grew exceptionally large, healthy, and strong. In Israelite thought, Bashan became synonymous with abundance, physical superiority, and luxury. However, this material abundance often led to pride and fierce, untamed behavior. Historically, the region was also associated with giants, such as Og, king of Bashan, adding an undercurrent of formidable, menacing power to the name.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper noun Bashan is derived from a root meaning "smooth, fertile soil" or "soft earth." Lexically, the term always designates this specific geographic territory. In Hebrew poetry and prophecy, however, the word functions metonymically. It represents the height of worldly prosperity, pride, and stubborn self-reliance. When applied to animals or people, it denotes those who have become fat, powerful, and arrogant because of their abundant resources.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture employs several distinct word pictures associated with Bashan to convey spiritual realities:

• The Oaks of Bashan: Used alongside the cedars of Lebanon to represent human pride, lofty stature, and high positions that God promises to bring low in the day of His judgment.

• The Cows of Bashan: Used by the prophets to describe the wealthy, self-indulgent elite who pamper themselves at the expense of the poor and helpless.

• The Bulls of Bashan: Used to represent fierce, wild, and territorial beasts. Unlike domesticated oxen, these bulls were unrestrained, massive, and highly aggressive, charging at anything that entered their territory. In Psalm 22, they represent savage, ungodly men who use their strength to terrorize the helpless.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Psalm 22 is one of the most profoundly messianic passages in the entire Old Testament, accurately detailing the crucifixion of Jesus Christ centuries before the Roman Empire invented this form of execution. The appearance of the "strong bulls of Bashan" in this context highlights the spiritual warfare taking place at Calvary.

• The Confrontation of Power: The messianic Speaker is described as a "worm" (Psalm 22:6)—helpless, crushed, and stripped of dignity. In stark contrast, His tormentors are pictured as "strong bulls," representing the crushing weight of institutional, political, and demonic power.

• The Pride of the Wicked: The strength of these bulls is fueled by their self-sufficiency. They represent the high priests, scribes, Pharisees, and Roman soldiers who looked down upon the crucified Christ with utter contempt, confident in their own righteousness and temporal authority.

• Unrestrained Rebellion: Just as wild bulls of the pasture recognize no master, these enemies cast off all fear of Yahweh to attack His Anointed One.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:12, the writer utilizes synonymous parallelism: "Many bulls have surrounded Me; Strong bulls of Bashan have encircled Me."

The Hebrew word translated "strong" in this verse is abbir, which refers to mighty, stubborn, or noble ones. By pairing this with "Bashan," the psalmist emphasizes that these are not ordinary adversaries. They are the chief of enemies—the most powerful, well-fed, and formidable opponents. The verbs "surrounded" and "encircled" paint a picture of a victim trapped in the center of a stampeding herd with no human way of escape. At the cross, Christ was completely surrounded by hostile forces, both human and demonic, who gloated over His apparent defeat.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 32:14: "Curds from the cattle, and milk of the flock, With fat of lambs; And rams of the breed of Bashan, and goats, With the choicest wheat; And you drank wine, the blood of the grapes."

• Isaiah 2:13: "Upon all the cedars of Lebanon that are high and lifted up, And upon all the oaks of Bashan;"

• Ezekiel 39:18: "You shall eat the flesh of the mighty, Drink the blood of the princes of the earth, Of rams and lambs, Of goats and bulls, All of them fatlings of Bashan."

• Amos 4:1: "Hear this word, you cows of Bashan, who are on the mountain of Samaria, Who oppress the poor, Who crush the needy, Who say to your husbands, 'Bring a drink, let us drink!'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Psalm 68:15, Bashan is described as a "mountain of God" with many high peaks. Yet, the psalmist notes that God did not choose the lofty, impressive peaks of Bashan for His dwelling place; instead, He chose the humble, low hill of Mount Zion. This geographical reality reinforces a consistent biblical theme: God rejects the proud, towering, and self-sufficient (represented by Bashan) and dwells with the humble, the lowly, and the contrite (represented by Zion and the suffering Savior of Psalm 22).
