BULLS - ( abiyr , Strong's H6499 )

Summary:

The Hebrew term abiyr fundamentally denotes strength, might, or one who is mighty, and when applied to animals in Scripture, it specifically designates strong, vigorous, and untamed bulls. In Psalm 22:12, this word paints a vivid picture of the fierce, hostile opposition the Messiah would face from ruthless enemies who surrounded Him like aggressive, horned beasts. Spiritually, it underscores the terrifying reality of human and demonic fury unleashed against the suffering Savior, highlighting both His profound vulnerability in the passion and the absolute triumph of God's ultimate deliverance.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

- The root verb from which abiyr derives relates to being strong, mighty, or valiant.

- In the agrarian and pastoral world of the ancient Near East, the bull was universally recognized as a supreme symbol of raw physical power, untamed aggression, and unyielding stubbornness.

- When applied to literal animals, the term highlights their formidable horns, massive size, and dangerous unpredictability, especially when infuriated or gathered in a menacing herd.

- In poetic literature, the imagery of powerful livestock is frequently borrowed to describe ruthless, proud oppressors who use their strength to intimidate and crush the weak.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

- Lexical definitions identify abiyr as an adjective or substantive meaning "strong," "mighty," "bull," or "steed."

- When used substantively in poetic texts, it refers specifically to strong or fierce bulls.

- Beyond animal imagery, the exact same Hebrew word is used as an epithet for God Himself—the "Mighty One" of Jacob—showing a striking linguistic contrast between the powerful bulls of Bashan and the true Mighty One who ultimately defends the afflicted.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

- The word evokes the physical posture of an aggressive beast lowering its head, pawing the dust, and preparing to gore its prey.

- It portrays enemies not merely as vocal critics, but as a physical, crushing force capable of trampling a defenseless victim.

- The plural form emphasizes a collective, mob-like hostility, illustrating how the righteous sufferer is swarmed on every side by relentless adversaries.

4. Theological Implications

- Psalm 22 is an intensely prophetic messianic psalm, famously echoed in the crucifixion of Jesus Christ.

- The description of the "strong bulls" gives theological depth to the psychological and physical isolation Jesus endured on the cross, surrounded by mocking religious leaders, brutal Roman soldiers, and unseen demonic powers.

- It demonstrates that human wickedness, at its most violent and animalistic, was fully unleashed against the Son of God, yet operating entirely within the sovereign framework of redemptive history.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

- In Psalm 22:12, the psalmist cries, "Many bulls have surrounded Me; Strong bulls of Bashan have encircled Me."

- Bashan was a region east of the Jordan River renowned for its rich pastures, which produced exceptionally large, well-fed, and fierce livestock.

- The specific reference to Bashan elevates the metaphor: these are not ordinary, domesticated animals, but prime, arrogant beasts fattened on luxury, pride, and cruelty, representing oppressive rulers and enemies who feel untouchable in their strength.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

- Psalm 50:13: "Shall I eat the flesh of bulls, Or drink the blood of goats?"

- Isaiah 34:7: "The wild oxen shall come down with them, And the young bulls with the mighty bulls; Their land shall be soaked with blood, And their dust saturated with fatness."

- Jeremiah 46:15: "Why are your valiant men swept away? They did not stand because the Lord drove them away." (Note: Here abiyr is translated as "valiant men" or mighty ones, highlighting the root concept of strength).

7. Other Relevant Insights

- The transition in Psalm 22 from the terrifying imagery of raging bulls to the eventual praise of God in the great congregation reminds the believer that earthly tyranny and mob mentality never have the final word.

- Recognizing the linguistic link between the "strong bulls" that attack and the "Mighty One" (Abiyr) who saves reminds us that human strength pales entirely before the sovereignty of God.
