RAGING - ( sha'ag , Strong's H2963 )

Summary:

The Hebrew term sha'ag primarily describes the terrifying, aggressive roar of a lion or the violent turmoil of natural elements, capturing a visceral sense of predatory menace and destructive power. In Psalm 22:13, it paints a vivid portrait of malicious adversaries closing in for the kill, expressing both physical danger and deep emotional distress.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Rooted in the natural world, sha'ag mimics the booming, resonant vocalization of a lion over its prey.

• In the ancient Near East, the lion was the ultimate symbol of untamed, lethal threat, striking fear into the hearts of solitary travelers and defenseless flocks.

• The imagery invokes the visceral terror of hearing a predator in the darkness, realizing that the beast has targeted you and is preparing to strike.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• To roar, growl, or cry out loudly in fierce anger, pain, or hunger.

• Used predominantly of lions roaring over their prey (Amos 3:4).

• Can also describe the emotional or physical groaning of human beings enduring intense suffering (Psalm 38:8).

• Conveys a sound that is loud, menacing, and unrestrained, reflecting an aggressive posture.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The word is frequently paired with animal imagery to depict the predatory nature of wicked enemies who hunt the righteous.

• It bridges the gap between sound and action; the roar is not merely noise, but the terrifying prelude to an attack.

• It communicates an overwhelming sensory experience where the victim is surrounded and defenseless against a superior, hostile force.

4. Theological Implications

• In the context of Lament Psalms like Psalm 22, the roaring adversary represents the relentless pressure of evil that seeks to devour the soul of the faithful.

• It highlights the profound isolation of the sufferer, who hears the deafening noise of the enemy while feeling seemingly abandoned by God.

• Yet, even amidst the roar of the lion, the Psalm ultimately pivots toward deliverance, showing that God's sovereignty surpasses the fury of any earthly or spiritual foe.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Psalm 22:13 ("They gape at me with their mouths, Like a raging and roaring lion"), the term describes the taunting, vicious hostility of the psalmist's human enemies.

• The adversaries are depicted as a pack of beasts, confident in their strength, opening wide their mouths to consume the helpless sufferer.

• The context emphasizes vulnerability; the speaker is completely surrounded, stripped of strength, and subjected to the psychological and verbal assault of merciless opponents.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Amos 3:4 - "Does a lion roar (sha'ag) in the forest, when he has no prey? Does a young lion cry out of his den, if he has caught nothing?"

• Psalm 38:8 - "I am feeble and severely broken; I groan (sha'ag) because of the turmoil of my heart."

• Jeremiah 25:30 - "Therefore prophesy against them all these words, and say to them: 'The Lord will roar (sha'ag) from on high, and utter His voice from His holy habitation; He will roar mightily against His fold.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• While often applied to wicked men or wild beasts, the same root is occasionally used to describe the righteous judgment of God roaring against sin.

• The progression in Psalm 22 moves from the terrifying roar of the lion in verse 13 to the ultimate rescue of the sufferer in the later verses, demonstrating that God hears the cry of the afflicted over the roar of the enemy.
