POTSHERD - ( cherep, Strong's H2789 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word cherep, translated as "potsherd" in Psalm 22:15, refers to a broken piece of earthenware or a fragment of a clay pot. In the context of biblical suffering, it vividly portrays extreme physical dryness, dehydration, and utter worthlessness in the eyes of mockers. This vivid metaphor underscores both the intense physical agony of the Messiah and the profound humiliation He endured on the cross.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

- Cherep derives from a root meaning to be scraped, peeled, or baked hard.

- In the ancient Near East, clay pots were essential household items for carrying water, storing grain, and cooking.

- Once broken, a clay vessel could not be mended; the shards were typically discarded in rubbish heaps, becoming symbols of uselessness and destitution.

- In dry climates, broken pottery fragments baked under the relentless sun would become completely devoid of moisture, hot, and brittle.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

- Refers specifically to a fragment, shard, or broken piece of an earthen vessel.

- Used metaphorically to describe human fragility, weakness, and vulnerability before God.

- Carries the secondary nuance of being insignificant, cast away, or treated with contempt.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

- Extreme Dehydration: In Psalm 22:15, "My strength is dried up like a potsherd," the image describes the agonizing physical state of severe thirst. Just as a baked clay shard loses every trace of moisture, the sufferer experiences a mouth parched and devoid of saliva.

- Human Fragility: Scripture frequently uses earthenware imagery to remind humanity of its dust origin and ultimate frailty, which can easily be shattered by divine judgment or life's trials.

- Worthlessness to Enemies: Broken shards lying in the dirt represent things of no value, mirroring how the righteous sufferer is viewed and trampled upon by hostile observers.

4. Theological Implications

- Messianic Prophecy: Psalm 22 is a profound messianic psalm detailing the crucifixion of Jesus Christ. The mention of strength drying up like a potsherd prophetically aligns with Jesus' actual physical suffering on the cross, including His cry, "I thirst" (John 19:28).

- The Humiliation of the Savior: The Son of God willingly entered into human frailty, allowing Himself to be broken like a fragile vessel for the redemption of sinners.

- Divine Sovereignty Over Human Vessels: God uses broken, fragile vessels to display His power, reminding believers that the surpassing excellence belongs to Him and not to us (2 Corinthians 4:7).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

- Psalm 22:15 reads: "My strength is dried up like a potsherd, and My tongue cleaves to My jaws; You have brought Me to the dust of death."

- Here, the psalmist expresses the total collapse of physical vitality. The metaphor of the potsherd explains the internal burning and dehydration that accompany intense suffering, abandonment, and the approach of death.

- The verse transitions from physiological distress ("dried up like a potsherd") to the finality of physical death ("brought Me to the dust of death"), painting a stark picture of complete human helplessness answered only by divine deliverance in the latter half of the psalm.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

- Job 2:8 - "And he took for himself a potsherd with which to scrape himself while he sat in the ashes."

- Proverbs 26:23 - "Fervent lips with an evil heart are like earthenware [cherep] covered with silver dross."

- Isaiah 45:9 - "Woe to him who strives with his Maker! Let the potsherd strive with the potsherds of the earth!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

- Archeologically, potshards were frequently used as cheap writing material (ostraca) for quick notes, receipts, or temporary messages in the ancient world, yet the biblical use focuses almost exclusively on their breakage, dryness, and worthlessness.

- The Septuagint translates the Hebrew term with words implying baked earthenware, maintaining the emphasis on intense heat and dryness.
