PIERCED - ( karah , Strong's H3738 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word karah (Strong's H3738) primarily means "to dig," "to bore," or "to carve out." In the prophetic context of Psalm 22:16, the NKJV translates this root as "pierced," describing the horrific physical trauma inflicted upon the Messiah’s hands and feet. This term conveys a deep, boring excavation rather than a superficial puncture, serving as an astonishingly precise, divinely inspired prediction of crucifixion written centuries before the Roman Empire invented this method of execution.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the agrarian and pastoral life of ancient Israel, karah was a common, hands-on verb. It described the hard labor of digging a well into dry earth, excavating a cistern, or carving out a burial tomb from solid rock. The word picture is one of forceful, repeated penetration of a solid surface to hollow it out or make a deep passage through it. When this word is applied metaphorically or physically to human flesh, it shifts from the labor of excavation to the agony of mutilation. The hands and feet of the sufferer are not merely scratched or cut; they are deeply bored through, as if a tool were digging directly into the bone and sinew.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root karah refers to the action of digging, boring, or excavating. The semantic range includes:

• To dig out a pit, grave, or well in the earth.

• To bore through or hollow out a solid object.

• To pierce or dig through flesh (specifically in the context of Psalm 22:16).

In Psalm 22:16, the inflected form ka'aru functions as a verb meaning "they dug through" or "they pierced." Rather than a superficial wounding, it denotes a deep, structural piercing that penetrates completely through the limbs.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs karah in several vivid contexts:

• The Excavation of Life and Death: It is used for digging wells to sustain life (Genesis 26:25) and carving out graves for the dead (Genesis 50:5).

• The Digging of Traps: It frequently describes the wicked digging a pit (karah) to trap the righteous, only to fall into it themselves (Psalm 7:15).

• The Opened Ear: In Psalm 40:6, the writer declares, "My ears You have opened [literally: dug or bored]." This refers to hollowing out an ear to make it receptive, or possibly boring the ear of a willing servant as a sign of lifelong devotion.

• The Pierced Messiah: In Psalm 22:16, the word transitions from digging the earth to boring through human flesh, illustrating the physical reality of nails driven through hands and feet.

4. Theological Implications

The use of karah in Psalm 22:16 provides one of the most compelling messianic defenses of Scripture. King David wrote this psalm around 1000 B.C., an era when the primary Jewish method of capital punishment was stoning. Crucifixion was completely unknown. Yet, under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, David used a term meaning "to dig through" to describe the execution of the coming righteous King.

This ties directly into the theology of the Suffering Servant. To purchase redemption, the Messiah’s body had to be "dug through." The iron nails driven through Jesus’ hands and feet on the cross at Calvary literally excavated His flesh, fulfilling this prophecy with absolute literalness.

Furthermore, there is a beautiful theological link between Psalm 40:6 and Psalm 22:16. In Psalm 40, the Messiah's ears are "dug" (karah) in willing obedience to do the Father's will. In Psalm 22, that same obedience culminates in His hands and feet being "dug" (karah) on the cross. His voluntary submission led directly to His ultimate sacrifice.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 22 is a lament of extreme physical and psychological torment. In verse 16, the speaker is surrounded by "dogs" (a common Jewish term for Gentiles) and an "assembly of the wicked" (representing the hostile religious leaders). In this state of helpless encirclement, He says, "They pierced my hands and my feet."

There is a well-known textual variant in the Masoretic Hebrew text, which reads ka'ari ("like a lion my hands and my feet"). This reading creates a grammatical fragment lacking a verb. However, the Septuagint (the Greek translation of the Old Testament dating to the 3rd century B.C.) translates the Hebrew as oryxan ("they dug" or "they pierced"). This pre-Christian translation confirms that the ancient translators understood the original Hebrew root to be karah. This reading is powerfully vindicated by the Dead Sea Scrolls (specifically the Psalms scroll from Nahal Hever), which explicitly uses the spelling ka'aru ("they dug/pierced"), confirming "pierced" as the original, authentic reading. The contextual imagery of disjointed bones, extreme thirst, and the dividing of garments (verses 14–18) perfectly aligns with the physical reality of being pierced and hung on a cross.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 50:5: "‘Indeed I am dying; in my grave which I dug for myself in the land of Canaan, there you shall bury me.’"

• Exodus 21:33: "And if a man opens a pit, or if a man digs a pit, and does not cover it, and an ox or a donkey falls in it,"

• Psalm 7:15: "He made a pit and dug it out, And fallen into the ditch which he made."

• Psalm 40:6: "Sacrifice and offering You did not desire; My ears You have opened [digged]. Burnt offering and sin offering You did not require."

• Psalm 57:6: "They have prepared a net for my steps; My soul is bowed down; They have dug a pit before me; Into the midst of it they themselves have fallen."

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is valuable to compare karah with another Old Testament word translated as "pierced." In Zechariah 12:10 ("then they will look on Me whom they have pierced"), the Hebrew word is daqar (Strong's H1856), which means to thrust through with a sword or spear.

Together, these two Hebrew words paint a complete, multi-dimensional prophetic canvas of the crucifixion of Jesus Christ:

• Psalm 22:16 (karah) predicts the boring through of His hands and feet by the nails.

• Zechariah 12:10 (daqar) predicts the thrusting through of His side by the Roman soldier's spear.
