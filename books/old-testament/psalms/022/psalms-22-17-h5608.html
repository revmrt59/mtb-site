COUNT - ( saphar , Strong's H5608 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb saphar primarily means to count, number, tally, recount, or declare. In Psalm 22:17, it vividly describes the agonizing physical toll of crucifixion centuries before the practice was invented, as the suffering Servant declares that He can count all His bones while His enemies stare and gloat over Him.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

- The root verb saphar originally carried the physical idea of scratching, marking, or scoring a tally upon a surface to keep an accurate record.

- Over time, this evolved into the broader mental and verbal acts of numbering items, recounting events, and publishing a formal report or declaration.

- In the ancient Near East, before modern record-keeping, counting was often done by making notches or marks, connecting the physical act of marking to the mental act of accounting.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

- Saphar functions primarily as a verb with a dual semantic range: quantitative calculation (to count, number, compute) and verbal communication (to recount, relate, declare, rehearse).

- In its quantitative sense, it means to tally discrete objects or individuals to ascertain a total sum.

- In its communicative sense, it means to articulate a narrative or make known a detailed account of past events.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

- The word is used throughout the Old Testament for taking censuses, counting wealth, or numbering the stars (Genesis 15:5).

- In poetic and narrative literature, it frequently describes telling the wondrous deeds or judgments of God to subsequent generations (Psalm 78:4).

- In Psalm 22:17, the word creates a stark, skeletal visual: the intense emaciation and stretching of the body under extreme suffering make every rib and bone individually visible and countable.

4. Theological Implications

- God's ability to "count" the stars (Psalm 147:4) and "count" human tears (Psalm 56:8) highlights His exhaustive omniscience and intimate pastoral care.

- Conversely, when man is reduced to counting his own bones, it portrays the absolute depth of humiliation, physical collapse, and alienation experienced by the sufferer.

- In messianic context, the detailed physical suffering of Psalm 22 underscores the substitutionary and historical reality of the atonement, fulfilling the scriptural design down to the very bones of the Savior.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

- Psalm 22 is a profound Messianic lament detailing the psychological and physical agony of crucifixion centuries before Roman execution methods were established.

- Verse 17 states: "I can count all My bones. They look and stare at Me." Here, saphar captures the horrific anatomical visibility of a body stretched taut upon a cross, where weight loss, dehydration, and muscle displacement cause the skeletal structure to protrude sharply against the skin.

- The enemies do not merely injure Him; they observe Him with cold, detached scrutiny, watching the slow, agonizing destruction of His body.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

- Genesis 15:5 - "Then He brought him outside and said, 'Look now toward heaven, and count the stars if you are able to number them.' And He said to him, 'So shall your descendants be.'"

- Psalm 78:4 - "We will not hide them from their children, telling to the generation to come the praises of the Lord, and His strength and His wonderful works that He has done." (Note: translated as telling, utilizing the verbal narrative aspect of saphar).

- Psalm 90:12 - "So teach us to number our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom."

- Psalm 147:4 - "He counts the number of the stars; He calls them all by name."

7. Other Relevant Insights

- A related noun derived from this root is sepher, which means a book, scroll, document, or written letter, showing the strong conceptual link between counting/recording and written documentation in ancient Hebrew culture.

- The Septuagint (LXX) translates saphar in Psalm 22:17 using the Greek verb exarithmeo, meaning to count out thoroughly or enumerate completely, reinforcing the precise visibility of the bones.
