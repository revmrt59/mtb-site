STARE - ( ra'ah, Strong's H7200 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ra'ah carries a broad semantic range primarily meaning "to see," "to look," or "to behold." However, when used in Psalm 22:17 in the context of hostile onlookers gazing upon the suffering Messiah, it takes on the nuance of malicious staring, gloating, and scrutinizing. Rather than a mere casual glance, it depicts enemies visually feasting upon the helplessness of the righteous sufferer, fulfilling prophetically the intense public mockery Christ endured at the cross.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

- The root verb ra'ah fundamentally relates to physical sight, but in ancient Hebrew thought, looking is rarely passive. To look upon someone in distress often carries moral and relational weight—either of compassionate intervention or cruel detachment and gloating.

- In Psalm 22, the psalmist is surrounded by adversaries who take visual pleasure in his humiliation. The act of staring becomes an instrument of psychological torture, magnifying the isolation of the sufferer.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

- Ra'ah functions as a primary verb for seeing, perceiving, inspecting, or looking.

- Lexical sources classify its nuances into literal physical vision, mental perception or understanding, and relational focus (such as looking with favor or, conversely, looking with malice).

- In the specific stem and context of Psalm 22:17, the action points to a fixed, intent gaze—watching an event unfold with vested interest, specifically the painful degradation of another human being.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

- The verb appears over a thousand times in the Hebrew Bible, spanning every shade of visual experience from divine revelation to human observation.

- When applied to enemies, ra'ah often paints a picture of triumph over a fallen foe. The adversaries stop, stare, and gloat, taking in the full measure of the victim's defeat.

- This creates a striking visual contrast: the sufferer is exposed and laid bare before the eyes of those who neither pity nor understand him.

4. Theological Implications

- The hostile stare in Psalm 22 underscores the profound loneliness of the suffering servant. Physical pain is compounded by the psychological weight of being publicly mocked and scrutinized.

- In the New Testament fulfillment (such as Matthew 27:39-43), this malicious staring is realized as the crowds, religious leaders, and soldiers pass by, shaking their heads and looking upon the crucified Christ.

- The open exposure of the righteous sufferer under the gaze of wicked men highlights the humiliation Christ willingly endured on behalf of sinners.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

- Psalm 22:17 states: "I can count all my bones. They look and stare at me."

- Here, the bones—protruding through emaciated, tortured flesh—are personified as objects of the enemies' gaze, or the enemies themselves are depicted as staring intently at the physical wreckage of the sufferer's body.

- The context is absolute vulnerability. The gazing crowd sees a man reduced to skeletal remains, yet instead of offering mercy, they feast their eyes on his misery.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

- Genesis 22:14 - "And Abraham called the name of the place, The-LORD-Will-Provide; as it is said to this day, 'In the mount of the LORD it shall be provided.'" (Using ra'ah in the sense of seeing/providing).

- Exodus 3:3 - "Then Moses said, 'I will now turn aside and see this great sight, why the bush does not burn.'" (Using ra'ah for physical observation).

- Psalm 118:7 - "The LORD is for me among those who help me; therefore I shall see my desire on those who hate me." (Using ra'ah to look upon enemies in triumph).

- Isaiah 53:11 - "He shall see the labor of His soul, and be satisfied." (Using ra'ah for deep spiritual perception and satisfaction).

7. Other Relevant Insights

- The transition from a simple glance to a hard stare reveals the hardening of the human heart in the presence of suffering. Where God sees and has compassion, fallen humanity often stares with cold indifference or malicious delight.

- In prophetic literature, God Himself is the ultimate witness who "sees" affliction, contrasting sharply with the abusive gaze of human oppressors.
