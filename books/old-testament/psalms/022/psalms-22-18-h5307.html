LOTS - ( naphal , Strong's H5307 )

Summary:

In Psalm 22:18, the English phrase "they cast lots" translates the Hebrew verb naphal (Strong's H5307) in its causative (Hiphil) form, paired with the Hebrew noun goral (Strong's H1486), which refers to the physical lot itself. While naphal literally means "to fall" or "to cause to fall," its idiomatic combination with goral depicts the physical throwing of stones, pebbles, or tablets to let them fall and decide an outcome. Historically, casting lots was a practice used to determine divine will or to ensure an unbiased distribution of property. In the immediate context of Psalm 22:18, it vividly illustrates the cold-blooded indifference of the sufferer's enemies, who treat him as already dead by dividing his garments and gambling for his clothing. This detail stands as one of the most remarkably precise messianic prophecies in the Old Testament, fulfilled literally by the Roman soldiers at the crucifixion of Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The core meaning of the Hebrew verb naphal is "to fall," "to lie down," or "to be cast down."

• In the ancient world, casting lots (yappilu goral) was not considered a game of random chance, but a method of submitting a decision to a higher, sovereign power.

• The physical process involved placing small marked stones, pieces of wood, or pottery shards into a vessel or the fold of a garment. The vessel was shaken, and the stone that fell out (naphal) first determined the selection.

• In Psalm 22:18, the word picture is one of hostile triumph. The enemies do not even wait for the victim to expire before they partition his belongings like plunder.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Naphal (verb): To fall, fall down, light, or bring down. In the Hiphil (causative) stem used here, it means "to cause to fall" or "to cast."

• Goral (noun): A pebble, stone, or lot used for casting; by extension, it refers to a portion, share, allotment, or destiny.

• In Psalm 22:18, the Hebrew text reads yappilu goral (literally, "they cause to fall the lot"). The grammar denotes a deliberate, active throwing down of the lot to distribute the sufferer's prized outer garment.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Allotment of Land: The Hiphil of naphal is frequently used to describe the sovereign distribution of the Promised Land to the tribes of Israel, where God "caused the land to fall" to them as an inheritance (cf. Joshua 13:6; Psalm 78:55).

• The Fall of Decisions: The verb is used to show how a decision "falls" or settles upon a person, such as when Haman "cast" (naphal) the lot to determine the day for the destruction of the Jews (Esther 3:7).

• Prayer as "Falling": Metaphorically, the verb is used when presenting or "casting" a petition down before the Lord, letting the request fall before His throne in humility (Daniel 9:18).

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereign Control: Scripture teaches that nothing falls outside of God's control. The physical act of throwing a stone (naphal) is subject to divine execution: "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the Lord" (Proverbs 16:33).

• The Messianic Pattern: The transition of naphal from dividing inheritance land to gambling for a sufferer's clothes illustrates a dark reversal. The righteous sufferer is stripped of his earthly inheritance.

• Preservation of Scripture: The detailed division of the clothes (which was done by tearing) versus the casting of lots (which preserved the seamless tunic whole) demonstrates that even the arbitrary actions of pagan soldiers were governed by the sovereign decree of God to fulfill His written Word.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• Psalm 22 is a lament of David that transcends his personal history, serving as a direct prophetic template of the Messiah's passion.

• In verse 18, David uses poetic parallelism: "They divide My garments among them" (the general dividing of various items) and "for My clothing they cast lots" (the determination of ownership for a single, undivided garment).

• The enemies view the sufferer as a helpless corpse. By casting lots (yappilu) for his clothes while he is still alive and watching, they mock his dignity, strip him of his identity, and treat his impending death as a foregone conclusion.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Jonah 1:7 – "And they said to one another, 'Come, let us cast lots, that we may know on whose account this trouble has come upon us.' So they cast lots, and the lot fell on Jonah."

• Proverbs 1:14 – "Cast in your lot among us, Let us all have one purse"

• Nehemiah 10:34 – "We cast lots among the priests, the Levites, and the people, for bringing the wood offering into the house of our God, according to our fathers’ houses, at the appointed times year by year, to burn on the altar of the Lord our God as it is written in the Law."

• 1 Chronicles 25:8 – "And they cast lots for their duty, the small as well as the great, the teacher with the student."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The New Testament directly records the literal fulfillment of this verse in all four Gospels (Matthew 27:35; Mark 15:24; Luke 23:34; John 19:23–24).

• John's Gospel highlights the precise distinction between the soldiers dividing the garments (himatia) into four parts and casting lots for the seamless tunic (chiton). This historical detail shows that the Roman soldiers—unaware of Jewish prophecy—acted in perfect alignment with the words written by David a thousand years earlier.
