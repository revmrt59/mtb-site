STRENGTH - ( 'eyaluth , Strong's H360 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'eyaluth refers to vital, protective strength or immediate succor. Occurring uniquely in the Messianic cry of Psalm 22:19, it describes God not merely as an abstract source of power, but as the personal, active rescuer who arrives to sustain those whose own vitality has completely vanished. It represents the ultimate lifeline for the helpless, pointing to Yahweh as the sole preservation of the suffering Savior in His hour of deepest agony.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun 'eyaluth derives from the root 'eyal or 'ayil, which carries the primary concept of political, physical, or structural power. In the ancient Near East, cognates of this root refer to a pillar, a sturdy oak, a ram, or a strong leader. The word picture is one of structural stability and defensive force—an immovable support beam or an unyielding pillar that can be grasped or leaned upon when everything else is collapsing. It evokes the image of a sovereign defender who steps into a chaotic, hostile environment to establish order and safety for the weak.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• 'Eyaluth is a feminine noun meaning "strength," "help," or "succor." It is a hapax legomenon, meaning it occurs only once in the entire Hebrew Bible. Lexically, the term denotes active, intervening assistance rather than inherent physical muscle or static power. It is power mobilized for rescue. While other Hebrew terms for strength emphasize human physical capability (koach) or defensive fortresses ('oz), 'eyaluth emphasizes the dynamic reinforcement brought to bear on behalf of someone who is utterly depleted and besieged.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Because 'eyaluth occurs only in Psalm 22:19, its usage must be understood alongside its closely related masculine cognate, 'eyal (Strong's H353). In Psalm 88:4, the writer laments being like a dying man who has "no strength" ('eyal). This linguistic relationship shows that 'eyaluth is the divine answer to human exhaustion. Where human 'eyal fails and drains away to nothing, the divine 'eyaluth steps in as an external, saving force. It functions as a titles for God Himself: He does not merely give strength; He is the personified Strength and Succor of His people.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of 'eyaluth shines brightest in its Messianic context. Psalm 22 is the premier crucifixion psalm, quoted directly by Jesus on the cross. The sufferer in the psalm is surrounded by predatory enemies (bulls, lions, and wild dogs) and is physically poured out like water. In this state of ultimate spiritual and physical weakness, the sufferer appeals to Yahweh. The use of 'eyaluth reveals that:

• God is the ultimate refuge when all human agency has failed.

• The Messiah, in His state of self-emptying and death, relied entirely upon the Father as His personal strength.

• True strength in the biblical paradigm is not self-generated but is received through covenant relationship with Yahweh.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:19, the psalmist reaches the climax of His petition. After describing His bones being out of joint, His heart melting like wax, and His tongue clinging to His jaws (verses 14–15), He turns from His suffering to His Sovereign. He cries, "But You, O LORD, do not be far from Me; O My Strength, hasten to help Me!"

The phrase "O My Strength" ('eyaluti) acts as a direct address to God. The immediate literary context contrasts the distant, silent God of verse 1 with the near, active Helper of verse 19. By addressing God as 'eyaluth, the sufferer declares that even though His physical body is completely broken, God remains His structural support, the one who will prevent Him from being utterly destroyed by the grave.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because 'eyaluth is used only once in the Hebrew Bible, its sole occurrence is listed below, followed by its masculine cognate 'eyal to demonstrate the biblical contrast:

• Psalm 22:19 — "But You, O LORD, do not be far from Me; O My Strength, hasten to help Me!"

• Psalm 88:4 [cognate 'eyal] — "I am counted with those who go down to the pit; I am like a man who has no strength."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), 'eyaluth is translated using the noun antilepsis, which means "help," "succor," or "the act of coming to the aid of someone." This same Greek root appears in the New Testament in 1 Corinthians 12:28, where the Apostle Paul lists the spiritual gift of "helps" (antilepseis). This linguistic connection reinforces that the "strength" of Psalm 22:19 is not a cold, mechanical power, but the loving, relational act of bearing another person's heavy burden.
