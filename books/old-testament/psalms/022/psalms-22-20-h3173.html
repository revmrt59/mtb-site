PRECIOUS - ( yachid , Strong's H3173 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word yachid translates to "precious life" in Psalm 22:20, denoting that which is unique, solitary, and utterly irreplaceable. While its primary meaning is "only" or "unique"—often used of an only child—its poetic use in parallel with "soul" captures the immense value and extreme vulnerability of human life. In this messianic psalm, it points to the singular, priceless life of the suffering Savior laid down for humanity.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb is yachad, which means to unite or be joined together. From this root, the adjective yachid develops the sense of being completely alone, solitary, or unique. It represents a single entity that has no duplicate, sibling, or equivalent. The primary word picture is that of an only child, the sole focus of a parent’s love, devotion, and future hope. When applied to human life, it depicts the soul as a singular treasure. Just as a parent has only one beloved child, an individual has only one life. There is no backup, no replacement, and no second chance; therefore, it is of supreme, irreplaceable value.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term yachid means "only," "only begotten," "unique," or "solitary." Lexically, it describes:

• An only child, highlighting a unique relationship of love and inheritance.

• A solitary or lonely person who stands apart from others.

• The human life or soul, viewed as a singular, irreplaceable possession.

When used in poetic parallelism with nephesh ("soul" or "life"), the term translates contextually as "precious life" or "darling," expressing the ultimate worth of one's existence.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament uses yachid in several distinct contexts to evoke deep emotion:

• The Only Child: It describes Isaac (Genesis 22:2) and Jephthah's daughter (Judges 11:34). It conveys the agonizing grief of losing one's only hope, as seen in Jeremiah 6:26 and Amos 8:10.

• The Solitary Soul: It describes the lonely, isolated person who feels abandoned and cut off from community (Psalm 25:16; Psalm 68:6).

• The Priceless Life: In the Psalms, it is a poetic synonym for the soul under deadly assault (Psalm 22:20; Psalm 35:17). The word picture shifts from a beloved child to the beloved life itself, portraying a person pleading with God to protect the one thing they cannot afford to lose.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of yachid shines brightest in its Christological connections:

• The Value of Life: God does not view human lives as generic or expendable. Each soul is a yachid—unique, solitary, and precious in His sight.

• The Messianic Sacrifice: Psalm 22 is the preeminent crucifixion psalm. When Christ, suffering on the cross, prays for the deliverance of His yachid, it emphasizes the infinite value of the life He willingly offered. He did not lay down a generic human life, but the unique, peerless life of the sinless Son of God.

• The Only Begotten: The concept of the yachid lays the Old Testament groundwork for the New Testament doctrine of Christ as the "only begotten" (Greek monogenes) Son, who is uniquely loved by the Father.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:20, the speaker is surrounded by deadly threats: "the sword" and "the power of the dog." The verse employs Hebrew poetic parallelism:

• Line A: "Deliver my soul from the sword..."

• Line B: "...My precious life from the power of the dog."

The term yachid (rendered "precious life") stands in parallel with "soul" (nephesh). This parallelism sharpens the emotional urgency of the prayer. The psalmist is not merely asking to be saved from physical pain; he is pleading for his very existence, his "only one." The contrast between the savage, unclean "dog" and the "precious life" underscores the horror of the threat. It is a cry of absolute vulnerability—a solitary, irreplaceable treasure facing imminent destruction.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 22:2 - "Then He said, 'Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love, and go to the land of Moriah...'"

• Psalm 25:16 - "Turn Yourself to me, and have mercy on me, For I am desolate and afflicted."

• Psalm 35:17 - "Lord, how long will You look on? Rescue my soul from their destructions, My precious life from the lions."

• Zechariah 12:10 - "...then they will look on Me whom they pierced. Yes, they will mourn for Him as one mourns for his only son..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, translates yachid in Psalm 22:20 (numbered as 21:21 in the Greek text) using the word monogenes. This is the exact Greek term used by the apostle John to describe Jesus as the "only begotten" Son of God (John 1:14; 3:16; 1 John 4:9). Thus, the Greek translation of this Messianic psalm directly links the "precious, only life" of the sufferer to the unique, beloved status of the Savior.
