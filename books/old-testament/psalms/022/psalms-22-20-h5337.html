DELIVER - ( natsal , Strong's H5337 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb natsal primarily means to snatch away, pluck out, or forcefully rescue someone from imminent danger, captivity, or death. It depicts a hands-on, energetic extraction where the deliverer actively pulls the helpless victim out of a hostile grip. In Psalm 22:20, this word functions as a desperate, urgent cry for God to snatch the psalmist's life out of the reach of deadly weapons and violent, beast-like adversaries.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical background of natsal involves tearing away, stripping off, or plucking something out of a tight spot. It is a highly active word that implies a struggle or an obstacle to overcome. A classic Old Testament picture of this word is a shepherd rushing to snatch a lamb out of the mouth of a lion or a bear, or pulling a half-burnt stick out of a blazing fire. It is not a gentle, passive release; it is a rescue that requires decisive, forceful intervention to break the power of an oppressor or predator.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In the Hiphil stem (the causative active form used in Psalm 22:20), natsal carries the specific meaning of rescuing, saving, or delivering from danger.

• To snatch away or pluck out of a perilous situation.

• To rescue captives or plunder from the hand of an enemy.

• To preserve or spare a life that is on the brink of destruction.

The word focuses heavily on the source of the danger and the immediate transition of the victim from a state of confinement or threat to a state of safety.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Shepherd’s Salvage: A shepherd rescues a portion of a sheep—two legs or a piece of an ear—from the mouth of a predator (Amos 3:12).

• The Rescue from Egypt: God’s mighty deliverance of Israel from Pharaoh’s grasp is repeatedly characterized by this word, demonstrating that Israel was forcefully snatched from the hand of a crushing superpower (Exodus 3:8).

• Plucking from Fire: It describes grabbing a burning branch out of the coals before it is completely destroyed (Amos 4:11; Zechariah 3:2).

• In the poetic and wisdom literature, it is the standard term used when a person is utterly trapped by death, sickness, or enemies, and has no power to free themselves.

4. Theological Implications

• Total Helplessness of the Victim: The use of natsal implies that the one needing deliverance is completely unable to save themselves. Deliverance must come entirely from an outside agent.

• God’s Sovereign Power: Yahweh is the ultimate Deliverer. When human rescue fails, God’s arm is strong enough to rip His people out of the tightest traps and the most fearsome grips.

• Messianic Fulfillment: Psalm 22 is a deeply prophetic, Messianic psalm detailing the sufferings of Christ. On the cross, Jesus was surrounded by hostile mockers who scoffed, "He trusted in the LORD, let Him rescue Him; Let Him deliver Him" (Psalm 22:8). While Jesus was not spared from physical death, God ultimately delivered His soul from the permanent grip of the grave through the resurrection.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:20, the psalmist cries, "Deliver my soul from the sword, My precious life from the power of the dog."

• The grammatical form is a Hiphil imperative (hatsilah), indicating an urgent, pleading request for immediate action.

• The context of Psalm 22 is one of extreme, life-threatening persecution. The adversaries are described as wild beasts—bulls, lions, and dogs.

• The "sword" represents violent execution, while "the power [literally, the hand] of the dog" represents the snapping jaws of vicious, pack-like mockers.

• To "deliver" here means to reach directly into the snapping jaws of the enemy and yank the psalmist’s "precious life" (literally, "my only one") out of danger just before the fatal blow lands.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 32:11 - "Deliver me, I pray, from the hand of my brother, from the hand of Esau; for I fear him, lest he come and attack me and the mother with the children."

• Exodus 3:8 - "So I have come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians, and to bring them up from that land to a good and large land..."

• Psalm 91:3 - "Surely He shall deliver you from the snare of the fowler And from the perilous pestilence."

• Psalm 142:6 - "Attend to my cry, For I am brought very low; Deliver me from my persecutors, For they are stronger than I."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Contrast with Yasha: While yasha (the root of Yeshua/Jesus) emphasizes safety, broad space, and victory, natsal focuses on the critical moment of extraction from danger. Yasha is the state of being saved; natsal is the emergency rescue pull.

• Septuagint Translation: The ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament (LXX) translates natsal in Psalm 22:20 using the Greek verb rhuomai, which also means to rescue or drag out of danger. This is the same verb used in the Lord’s Prayer: "Deliver (rhuomai) us from the evil one" (Matthew 6:13), preserving the urgent Old Testament word picture of being snatched out of a predator's reach.
