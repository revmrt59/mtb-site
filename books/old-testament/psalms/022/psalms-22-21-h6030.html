ANSWERED - ( anah , Strong's H6030 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'anah refers to a response that is active, relational, and powerful. In Psalm 22:21, this word serves as the pivotal turning point of the entire Psalm. After twenty verses of agonizing lament and feeling utterly forsaken, the sufferer suddenly declares, "You have answered me." This single word marks the transition from desperate pleading to triumphant praise, demonstrating that God is not silent to the cries of His afflicted people but actively intervenes to deliver them.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root 'anah carries the primary concrete concept of paying attention, responding to a stimulus, or providing a matching counterpart. In the ancient Near Eastern world, an "answer" from a deity or a king was not merely a verbal reply; it was a decisive action. When a petitioner cried out to a sovereign, to receive an "answer" meant that the king had heard the plea, accepted the petition, and set his power in motion to resolve the crisis. The word picture is one of relational engagement, where a cry of distress from below is met by a corresponding movement of rescue from above.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb means to answer, respond, testify, or speak in turn. In the context of prayer, it refers to God responding to human cries with divine intervention and deliverance. It denotes a relational responsiveness, indicating that God is neither deaf nor indifferent to the plight of His people. The word goes beyond mere communication; it signifies that God has taken notice, sided with the sufferer, and acted to change their circumstances.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb occurs over 300 times in the Hebrew Bible and is used in several distinct ways:

• Conversational Response: The most common usage, where one person speaks and another responds to keep the dialogue moving.

• Legal Testimony: To "answer" in a legal assembly means to testify, bear witness, or give evidence in a court of law.

• Liturgical Response: Singing or chanting responsively in worship settings, where one group responds to another.

• Divine Intervention: God answering human prayer, which is almost always accompanied by a physical act of salvation, provision, or revelation.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'anah highlights that the God of Israel is a personal, communicative, and covenant-keeping God. Unlike the silent, unresponsive idols of the pagan nations, Yahweh hears and responds to His people. This responsiveness is the very foundation of biblical hope. Furthermore, God's answer is what bridges the gap between petition and praise. When God answers, the believer's reality is transformed, requiring a public testimony of thanksgiving to the community of faith.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 22 is the ultimate Psalm of lament, famously quoted by Jesus on the cross. For the first twenty-one verses, the Psalmist describes an agonizing reality of being forsaken by God, mocked by enemies, and surrounded by deadly forces described metaphorively as bulls, lions, dogs, and wild oxen.

At the very end of verse 21, the tone of the Psalm shifts instantly and radically. The Hebrew text concludes with the single word 'anitani ("You have answered me"). This is the great "hinge" of Psalm 22. The moment God answers, the darkness vanishes. Immediately following this word, the Psalmist promises to declare God's name to his brethren and lead the congregation in ecstatic praise (v. 22). The grammatical perfect tense ("You have answered") indicates the certainty of faith; the deliverance is so sure in the mind of the speaker that it is spoken of as a completed reality.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 20:1 - "May the LORD answer you in the day of trouble; May the name of the God of Jacob defend you;"

• Psalm 118:5 - "I called on the LORD in distress; The LORD answered me and set me in a broad place."

• Isaiah 58:9 - "Then you shall call, and the LORD will answer; You shall cry, and He will say, ‘Here I am.’"

• Jonah 2:2 - "I cried out to the LORD because of my affliction, And He answered me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A grammatical and textual tension exists in Psalm 22:21. While the Masoretic Text reads 'anitani ("You have answered me"), some ancient translations, such as the Septuagint, read the word as 'aniyati ("my affliction" or "my humility"), translating the phrase as "Save me... from the horns of the wild oxen, [look upon] my affliction." However, the Hebrew manuscript tradition strongly supports "You have answered me." This reading provides the most dramatic, powerful, and theological transition from the depths of despair to the heights of praise, perfectly mirroring the death and resurrection of the Messiah.
