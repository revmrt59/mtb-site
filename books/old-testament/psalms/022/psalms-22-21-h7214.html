WILD OXEN - ( re'em , Strong's H7214 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word re'em refers to the wild ox (specifically the aurochs, Bos primigenius), a massive, extinct beast known in the ancient Near East for its untamable nature, immense strength, and towering horns. In the Old Testament, particularly in poetic and prophetic literature, it symbolizes fierce, uncontrollable power and lethal danger. In Psalm 22:21, the "horns of the wild oxen" represent the terrifying, overwhelming forces of demonic and human enemies surrounding the suffering Messiah, highlighting both the extremity of His peril and the dramatic suddenness of God's deliverance.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The re'em is not a mythical unicorn, despite older translation traditions influenced by the Greek Septuagint. It is the aurochs, the wild ancestor of domestic cattle. Standing up to six feet tall at the shoulder with massive, forward-pointing horns, the wild ox was one of the most feared beasts of the biblical world. Ancient Near Eastern reliefs depict kings hunting the wild bull as the ultimate display of royal power and courage. This animal was characterized by its sheer, untamed physical strength and its complete refusal to be domesticated, yoked, or controlled by man.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary lexical definition of re'em is a wild ox or aurochs. The term denotes a large, wild, horned animal noted for its great strength and destructive power. Semitic cognates, such as the Akkadian rimu (wild bull) and Arabic ri'm (white antelope), confirm that the term identifies a powerful, horned, wild bovid. The word functions in biblical Hebrew as a prime metaphor for unstoppable physical force and prideful elevation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Untamable Strength: In the book of Job, the wild ox represents the limits of human authority and the vastness of God's sovereignty. No human can force it to furrow the ground or trust it to bring in the harvest.

• Military Might: The horns of the wild ox are used to picture military dominance and the crushing power of a kingdom, as seen in the blessings of Joseph.

• Sovereign Judgment: In prophetic judgment scenes, the wild oxen represent the proud and mighty leaders of nations who will be brought down and slaughtered in God's day of vengeance.

• Exaltation: To have one’s horn "exalted like a wild ox" is a metaphor for restored dignity, strength, and victory granted by God.

4. Theological Implications

• God’s Sovereignty over Chaos: The wild ox represents the wild, untamable elements of creation that defy human control but remain completely subject to the command of Yahweh.

• The Messianic Agony: In Psalm 22, the suffering servant is surrounded by a terrifying menagerie of predators: demonic "bulls of Bashan," "roaring lions," "dogs," and finally, "wild oxen." These animal metaphors represent the raw, animalistic, and spiritual brutality of the forces arrayed against Jesus on the cross.

• Deliverance from the Utmost Peril: The horns of the wild ox represent the point of no return—being cornered and gored. God's salvation is most glorious because it rescues the believer from the very brink of destruction.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:21, the psalmist cries: "Save Me from the lion’s mouth And from the horns of the wild oxen! You have answered Me." The mention of "horns" evokes the imagery of being trapped, tossed, and gored to death. The wild ox does not merely bite; it impales and destroys.

The placement of this word is the literary and theological climax of the first half of the Psalm. The plea is uttered at the moment of highest tension. Crucially, the verse ends with the dramatic Hebrew word anitanu ("You have answered Me"). The transition from the terrifying horns of the wild oxen to the sudden declaration of answered prayer marks the transition from Christ's suffering to His resurrection triumph. The horns of death are broken by the voice of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 33:17 - "His glory is like a firstborn bull, And his horns are like the horns of the wild ox; Together with them He shall push the peoples To the ends of the earth."

• Job 39:9 - "Will the wild ox be willing to serve you? Will he bed by your manger?"

• Psalm 92:10 - "But my horn You have exalted like a wild ox; I have been anointed with fresh oil."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The translation history of re'em is historically significant. The translators of the Septuagint (LXX) rendered the word as monokeros (one-horned), which the Latin Vulgate carried over as unicornis. This ultimately led to the King James Version translating the word as "unicorn." This translation was not based on modern fantasy lore, but likely on ancient artistic depictions of the aurochs in profile (where only one horn was visible) or confusion with the one-horned rhinoceros. Modern discoveries of Assyrian and Babylonian monuments depicting the rimu (two-horned wild bull) have fully resolved this translation anomaly, confirming the accuracy of modern translations like the NKJV.
