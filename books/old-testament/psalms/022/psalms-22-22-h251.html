BRETHREN - ( 'ach , Strong's H251 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ach refers to a biological brother, a kinsman, or a member of the same covenant community. In Psalm 22:22, it marks a pivotal transition from isolation to restoration, as the suffering speaker—prophetically pointing to Jesus Christ—vows to declare God's saving name to his "brethren." Within this worship context, the term represents the assembly of faith, illustrating how God's deliverance of the individual benefits, encourages, and unites the entire spiritual family.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israelite culture, brotherhood was the foundational unit of social structure, security, and identity. A brother ('ach) was not merely a sibling but a protector, a co-heir of the family estate, and a guardian of the family name. The concept carried strong duties of loyalty, mutual defense, and redemption. If an Israelite fell into poverty or bondage, it was the duty of his "brother" to step in as a redeemer (goel). Thus, to call someone a "brother" in the ancient Near East was to extend a pledge of covenant loyalty, shared destiny, and ultimate solidarity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of 'ach denotes a male sibling sharing one or both parents. However, its semantic range expands significantly across the Old Testament scriptures:

• Kinship and Relatives: Extended family members, such as uncles, nephews, or cousins (e.g., Abraham and Lot).

• National and Covenantal Kinship: Fellow members of the tribes of Israel, bound together by the Mosaic Covenant.

• Allies and Friends: Individuals bound by a treaty, mutual affection, or shared office (e.g., David and Jonathan).

• Spiritual Companions: Fellow worshippers who share a common devotion to Yahweh.

In Psalm 22:22, the word transitions from biological kinship to a deep, covenantal, and spiritual relationship. The "brethren" are those who stand in the same faith community, looking to the same God for deliverance.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently uses 'ach to paint vivid pictures of relationship and responsibility:

• The Companion in Adversity: Proverbs 17:17 declares that "a brother is born for adversity," presenting the brother as the one who stands firm when all other support fails.

• The Harmonious Community: Psalm 133:1 depicts the beauty of "brethren to dwell together in unity," comparing this fellowship to sacred anointing oil and life-giving dew.

• The Covenant Neighbor: Deuteronomy 15 instructs Israelites never to harden their hearts or shut their hands against a poor "brother," showing that brotherhood demands practical compassion.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of 'ach in Psalm 22:22 is immense, particularly in its Messianic fulfillment. Psalm 22 begins with the agonizing cry of abandonment: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (v. 1). The sufferer is surrounded by enemies, mocked, and stripped of dignity. Yet, in verse 22, the tone shifts dramatically from petition to praise.

The sufferer is delivered, and his first instinct is to share the testimony of God’s faithfulness with his "brethren." This demonstrates that God's redemptive work is never private; it is designed to build and encourage the faith community.

Furthermore, Hebrews 2:11–12 quotes Psalm 22:22 to explain the incarnation and work of Jesus Christ. By taking on human flesh, suffering, and rising again, Jesus is "not ashamed to call them brethren." He brings His followers into the very family of God, transforming servants into brothers and co-heirs.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the immediate context of Psalm 22:22, "brethren" stands in parallel with "the assembly" (qahal—the congregation of Israel). The psalmist’s private deliverance becomes public proclamation. He does not retreat into silent gratitude; he stands in the midst of the temple courts to praise Yahweh. The "brethren" are those who have also known affliction, and by hearing the sufferer's testimony of deliverance, their own hearts "shall live forever" (v. 26). The sufferer’s victory becomes the victory of the entire family of faith.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 13:8 - "So Abram said to Lot, 'Please let there be no strife between you and me, and between my herdsmen and your herdsmen; for we are brethren.'"

• Deuteronomy 15:7 - "If there is among you a poor man of your brethren, within any of the gates in your land which the Lord your God is giving you, you shall not harden your heart nor shut your hand from your poor brother,"

• Psalm 133:1 - "Behold, how good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), 'ach in Psalm 22:22 is translated as adelphois (the dative plural of adelphos). This is the exact word used by the New Testament writers to describe the church family.

It is highly significant that before His resurrection, Jesus referred to His disciples as "disciples," "servants," or "friends." But on the morning of His resurrection, having accomplished the suffering of Psalm 22, He tells Mary Magdalene, "Go to My brethren and say to them, 'I am ascending to My Father and your Father...'" (John 20:17). The suffering of the Cross forever established the biological and spiritual enemies of God as the beloved "brethren" of the Risen King.
