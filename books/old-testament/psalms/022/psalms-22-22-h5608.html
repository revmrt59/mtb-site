DECLARE - ( saphar , Strong's H5608 )

Summary:

In Psalm 22:22, the Hebrew word saphar (translated "declare") carries the rich meaning of recounting, enumerating, or systematically telling a story in detail. Rather than a simple, brief announcement, it refers to a thorough, public testimony of God's righteous character and saving deeds. In this messianic psalm, the sufferer—having been delivered from the jaws of death—vows to proclaim God's name and faithfulness to his brethren, shifting the tone of the psalm from agonizing lamentation to triumphant congregational praise.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root saphar is closely associated with counting, listing, or recording data. It is the linguistic basis for the Hebrew words for "scribe" (sopher) and "book" (sepher). The foundational word picture is that of a scribe carefully counting out units, keeping a tally, or recording historical events line by line to ensure absolute accuracy and completeness. When applied to speech—specifically in the intensive Pi'el stem used in Psalm 22:22—saphar means to recount or enumerate the glorious deeds of Yahweh with the same systematic precision as a scribe counting items. It represents an orderly, thorough rehearsal of God's actions, leaving out no detail of His deliverance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb saphar occurs over 100 times in the Old Testament.

• In the Qal stem, it means to count, number, register, or reckon (such as counting stars, days, or money).

• In the Pi'el stem, the meaning intensifies from mathematical counting to narrative recounting. In this stem, it means to tell, declare, rehearse, report, or proclaim.

• The lexical definition emphasizes an orderly, sequential narration of events. To "declare" God's name is to recount the specific attributes, actions, and saving interventions that define who He is. It is not a generic shout of praise, but an exposition of His covenant faithfulness based on historical reality.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The connection between writing and declaring is highly significant. Just as a book (sepher) preserves a permanent, ordered record, the spoken declaration (saphar) provides an orderly, memorable oral testimony.

• This word is frequently used in contexts of covenant renewal and generational discipleship, where parents are commanded to "tell" or "recount" the mighty acts of God to their children so that the next generation will not forget.

• Creation itself performs this action: "The heavens declare (saphar) the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1). The cosmos acts as a silent scribe, continuously and systematically laying out the evidence of the Creator's power and majesty.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, saphar highlights that biblical faith is rooted in historical events. God acts in space and time, and those actions must be remembered, articulated, and passed down. Recounting prevents spiritual amnesia.

Furthermore, "the name" of God represents His character, authority, and saving nature. Declaring His name is an act of worship that reveals Him to others.

In its messianic context, Psalm 22 is the ultimate crucifixion psalm. Verse 22 marks the sudden, dramatic pivot from suffering to vindication. The writer of the Epistle to the Hebrews quotes Psalm 22:22 in Hebrews 2:12, placing these words directly into the mouth of the risen Christ. Christ is the one who, having conquered death, declares the Father's saving name to His "brethren" (the Church), leading the congregation in triumphant praise.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:1-21, the psalmist is surrounded by enemies, mocked, poured out like water, and facing imminent death. However, at the end of verse 21, a breakthrough occurs ("You have answered Me").

Immediately in verse 22, the tone shifts from agonizing petition to joyful proclamation. The sufferer vows to "declare Your name to My brethren." The contextual nuance of saphar here is a vow of public testimony. The deliverance of the individual is not meant to be kept private; it must be shared in the "midst of the assembly" (qahal) so that the entire community of faith is encouraged, strengthened, and prompted to worship. The individual's salvation becomes a corporate sermon on God’s reliability.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 15:5 — "Then He brought him outside and said, 'Look now toward heaven, and tell the stars if you are able to number them.' And He said to him, 'So shall your descendants be.'" (Here, saphar is used in its foundational Qal sense of counting).

• Psalm 19:1 — "The heavens declare the glory of God; And the firmament shows His handiwork."

• Psalm 96:3 — "Declare His glory among the nations, His wonders among all peoples."

• Psalm 102:21 — "To declare the name of the LORD in Zion, And His praise in Jerusalem."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Israel, a testifier of God's deliverance would often stand before the temple congregation to offer a thanksgiving sacrifice and verbally recount their story. The use of saphar indicates that this testimony was expected to be highly specific. The testifier would recount the depth of their distress, the urgency of their prayer, and the exact manner of God's rescue. This detailed narrative format is what transformed personal experiences into enduring theology for the congregation.
