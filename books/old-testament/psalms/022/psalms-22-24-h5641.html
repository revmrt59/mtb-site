HIDDEN - ( satar , Strong's H5641 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb satar means to hide, conceal, or shield, often carrying the sense of withholding one's presence, favor, or protection. In the context of Psalm 22:24, it describes God's refusal to turn His back on the suffering of the afflicted one. While the sufferer initially felt abandoned, the reality is that Yahweh did not shroud His presence or ignore his desperate cry, demonstrating God's ultimate attentiveness and compassion toward those who suffer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root satar has the concrete, primary meaning of putting something in a secret place, covering it up, or shielding it from sight. In the Ancient Near East, a king hiding his face meant a withdrawal of favor, a refusal to grant an audience, or an act of judgment. Conversely, a king showing his face meant favor, mercy, and life. The physical act of covering or veiling one's face translates into the relational act of breaking fellowship or withholding help.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb satar occurs over 80 times in the Old Testament, primarily in the Hiphil (causative) and Niphal (reflexive/passive) stems. In the Hiphil form used in Psalm 22:24 (hastîr), it means "to hide" or "to conceal," and is most frequently paired with the noun panîm (face). In the Niphal stem, it means "to hide oneself" or "to be hidden." The lexical range covers:

• To actively conceal someone or something from view.

• To withdraw favor or attention (specifically of God hiding His face).

• To protect or shield from danger by keeping out of sight.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Hiding the Face (hastîr panîm): This is a highly significant anthropomorphic idiom in Hebrew scripture. It symbolizes the withdrawal of divine favor, blessing, and presence, leaving the individual vulnerable to enemies, distress, and death.

• Hiding for Protection: In other contexts, satar can be positive, such as God hiding His saints in the secret place of His presence to protect them from evil (Psalm 31:20).

• Hiding Sin: It is also used of human attempts to cover up their transgressions from God’s sight, an effort that is ultimately futile.

4. Theological Implications

The hiding of God's face is one of the most painful experiences of the righteous sufferer. It raises deep existential and theological questions: Has God abandoned His covenant? Is He angry? In salvation history, God's face being hidden represents judgment for sin. However, for the suffering servant, the temporary feeling of abandonment is answered by the reality of God's active hearing and deliverance. Redemption is secure because God does not permanently hide His face from His anointed.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 22 begins with the agonizing cry, "My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?" (v. 1). The sufferer feels completely abandoned, exposed to mockers, and left to die. Verse 24 marks a triumphant pivot. After the cry for rescue, the sufferer declares to the congregation that God did not hide His face. Though it felt like abandonment during the darkest hours of suffering (which this messianic psalm foreshadows on the cross), God did not turn away in disgust from the afflicted one's suffering. The Father heard the Son's cry, leading to vindication and resurrection.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 31:17 - "Then My anger shall be aroused against them in that day, and I will forsake them, and I will hide My face from them, and they shall be devoured."

• Psalm 13:1 - "How long, O LORD? Will You forget me forever? How long will You hide Your face from me?"

• Psalm 27:9 - "Do not hide Your face from me; Do not turn Your servant away in anger; You have been my help; Do not leave me nor forsake me, O God of my salvation."

• Isaiah 54:8 - "With a little wrath I hid My face from you for a moment; But with everlasting kindness I will have mercy on you,” Says the LORD, your Redeemer."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) translates satar in Psalm 22:24 (21:25 in the LXX) using the Greek verb apostrepho ("to turn away"), specifically apestrepsen to prosopon autou ("did not turn away his face"). This emphasizes the relational turn—God did not avert His gaze or reject the sufferer.
