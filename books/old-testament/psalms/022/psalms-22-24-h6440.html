FACE - ( panim , Strong's H6440 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word panim literally means "face" or "presence." In the Old Testament, the face is the primary index of a person's inner disposition, emotions, and attitudes. When applied anthropomorphically to God, His "face" represents His personal, intimate presence, His active attention, and His divine favor. To "hide the face" is a powerful biblical idiom signifying the withdrawal of favor, grace, and communication, leaving the individual in a state of distress and perceived abandonment. In Psalm 22:24, the declaration that God has not hidden His face from the afflicted sufferer serves as the glorious turning point of the psalm. It assures the believer that even in the deepest trials, God remains intimately present, attentive, and favorably disposed to answer the cries of His people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun panim is plural in form but singular in construction. This plural nature reflects the multi-faceted, dynamic character of the face, which is constantly shifting to display a variety of emotions, expressions, and attitudes. It comes from the root verb panah, which means "to turn" or "to face." Thus, the face is fundamentally that which is turned toward another to establish relationship or communication. In ancient Near Eastern culture, the face of a king was the seat of life-giving favor; to be granted access to the king’s face meant acceptance, safety, and blessing, whereas a turned-away face signaled rejection, wrath, or condemnation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of panim is the physical face of a human or animal. By extension, it denotes the personal presence of an individual. In theological contexts, it refers to the presence of God Himself. Panim is also frequently translated as "countenance," "surface," or "front." When joined with prepositions, it functions idiomatically to mean "before," "in the presence of," or "against." Lexically, the term emphasizes personal proximity and relational availability. It is not a static depiction of God's form, but a dynamic description of His relational posture toward His creation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs panim in several vivid relational word pictures:

• Hiding the Face (hastir panim): This idiom represents a deliberate withdrawal of divine favor, protection, and fellowship. It is the ultimate form of judgment or relational distance, leaving the human soul in deep anguish (Deuteronomy 31:17).

• The Shining Face: To have God’s face "shine" upon someone is a metaphor for the manifestation of His grace, joy, and peace (Numbers 6:25). It implies a warm, smiling, and approving countenance.

• Seeking the Face: To seek God’s face means to pursue His presence, favor, and intimate fellowship, rather than merely seeking His hand for material benefits (Psalm 27:8).

• Face to Face (panim el-panim): This describes unparalleled, direct, and intimate communication, such as God shared with Moses (Exodus 33:11) or Jacob experienced at Peniel (Genesis 32:30).

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of panim centers on the relational nature of Yahweh. God is not an impersonal force or an aloof deity; He has a "face." He is a personal God who desires to look upon His people and be looked upon by them. In the context of Psalm 22—the ultimate messianic psalm of crucifixion and vindication—the speaker shifts from the agonizing cry of abandonment in verse 1 ("My God, My God, why have You forsaken me?") to the triumphant realization in verse 24 that God has not hidden His face. This reveals a profound theological truth: though the righteous sufferer may experience the subjective feeling of abandonment during intense trials, the objective reality is that God’s favorable presence remains steadfast. Ultimately, this points to Jesus Christ, who bore the temporary hiding of the Father's face on the cross so that believers might eternally enjoy the light of God’s countenance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:24, the psalmist exults, "Nor has He hidden His face from him; But when he cried to Him, He heard." The contextual placement of this verse is critical. It marks the transition from the agonizing laments of the first half of the psalm to the corporate praise and global worship of the second half. The "afflicted" one has been delivered from the horns of the wild oxen and the mouth of the lion. The nuance of panim here is one of attentive, compassionate rescue. God did not turn His back on the sufferer, nor did He look away in disgust from his misery. Instead, God kept His face turned toward the sufferer, actively listening, deeply caring, and ready to save.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 32:30 - "So Jacob called the name of the place Peniel: 'For I have seen God face to face, and my life is preserved.'"

• Numbers 6:25 - "The LORD make His face shine upon you, And be gracious to you;"

• Psalm 27:9 - "Do not hide Your face from me; Do not turn Your servant away in anger; You have been my help; Do not leave me nor forsake me, O God of my salvation."

• Psalm 102:2 - "Do not hide Your face from me in the day of my trouble; Incline Your ear to me; In the day that I call, answer me speedily."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), panim in Psalm 22:24 (numbered as 21:25 in the LXX) is translated using the word prosopon, which likewise means "face," "countenance," or "person." This same Greek word is used in the New Testament to describe the glory of God shining "in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6). Thus, the divine face that was not hidden from the suffering Psalmist is the very face of love and grace revealed to believers in the person of Jesus.
