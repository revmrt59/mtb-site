HEARD - ( shama , Strong's H8085 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, the Hebrew verb shama goes far beyond the passive physical perception of sound; it represents an active, responsive hearing that leads to decisive action. In Psalm 22:24, this word serves as the glorious turning point of the entire psalm. While the suffering psalmist initially cried out in the agony of perceived abandonment, the ultimate reality is that God did not turn away. When the afflicted one cried out, Yahweh heard—meaning He listened with deep compassion and acted sovereignly to deliver him from the jaws of death. This responsive hearing forms the foundation of biblical hope, proving that God is intimately attentive to the cries of His suffering people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew root shama is deeply relational and dynamic. In ancient Near Eastern thought, hearing was not separate from doing. To hear a command was to obey it; to hear a cry for help was to answer it. The physical mechanics of the ear taking in sound waves are only the starting point. The true word picture behind shama is that of bowing the ear, turning one's full attention toward the speaker, absorbing the weight of their words, and moving into action. When applied to God, His "hearing" is the necessary precursor to His saving intervention. When God hears, the circumstances of the sufferer are about to change.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shama carries a broad semantic range that includes:

• To hear, perceive, or listen to a sound.

• To pay attention, give heed, or consent to a request.

• To obey, keep, or carry out a command (often translated as "obey" in English).

• To grant a petition, answer a prayer, or show favor to a suppliant.

In Psalm 22:24, the verb is used in the Qal stem (simple active voice), indicating a direct, complete, and literal act of hearing by God. It denotes that God did not merely register the auditory frequency of the sufferer's scream, but that He favorably received the petition and intervened.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb shama appears over 1,100 times in the Old Testament, making it one of the most common and significant verbs in the Hebrew scriptures. It is used in several key theological contexts:

• The Covenant Call: The most famous use is the Shema in Deuteronomy 6:4 ("Hear, O Israel..."), where hearing demands an exclusive, loving allegiance to Yahweh.

• The Cry of the Oppressed: Throughout Israel's history, God is characterized as the One who hears the cry of the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the slave.

• The Refusal to Hear: Scripture often warns of a spiritual deafness, where Israel has ears but does not hear God's prophets, leading God to temporarily refuse to "hear" their prayers in times of judgment.

4. Theological Implications

The theology of shama reveals that Yahweh is not like the silent, unresponsive idols of the surrounding nations. Pagan deities had ears but could not hear (Psalm 115:6). Yahweh, however, is a personal, living God who invites His people to pour out their hearts to Him because He is capable of being moved by their suffering.

Furthermore, God's hearing is rooted in His covenant faithfulness. When God hears, He remembers His promises. In the context of redemption, God's hearing is the divine pivot between human misery and divine deliverance.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 22 is a masterpiece of messianic suffering and ultimate triumph.

• The Contrast with Silence: The psalm begins with the agonizing cry of abandonment: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken me?... O My God, I cry in the daytime, but You do not hear" (vv. 1-2).

• The Turning Point: In verse 21, the tone abruptly shifts from desperate lament to triumphant praise ("You have answered me").

• The Vindication: Verse 24 explains why the psalmist is now praising God in the great congregation: "For He has not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted; nor has He hidden His face from him; but when he cried to Him, He heard."

The nuance of shama here is divine vindication. Though it appeared to onlookers—and felt to the sufferer—that God had abandoned Him to His enemies, God was actually listening the entire time. The resurrection of Jesus Christ is the ultimate historical fulfillment of this verse; God heard the cries of His righteous Servant on the cross and answered by raising Him from the dead.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 16:11: "And the Angel of the LORD said to her: 'Behold, you are with child, and you shall bear a son. You shall call his name Ishmael, because the LORD has heard your affliction.'"

• Exodus 2:24: "So God heard their groaning, and God remembered His covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob."

• Deuteronomy 6:4: "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one!"

• Psalm 34:17: "The righteous cry, and the LORD hears, and delivers them out of all their troubles."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint/LXX), shama in Psalm 22:24 is translated using the verb eisakouo, which means "to listen to, to look upon with favor, or to answer a prayer." This same Greek word is used in Hebrews 5:7 to describe Jesus’ prayers in Gethsemane and on the cross: "who, in the days of His flesh, when He had offered up prayers and supplications, with vehement cries and tears to Him who was able to save Him from death, and was heard because of His godly fear." The New Testament explicitly links the "hearing" of Psalm 22:24 to the Father's response to the Son's passion.
