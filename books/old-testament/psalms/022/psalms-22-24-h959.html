DESPISED - ( bazah , Strong's H959 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bazah conveys a deep sense of contempt, disdain, or regarding something as worthless and insignificant. In the context of Psalm 22:24, it highlights the marvelous truth that Yahweh does not look down upon, disregard, or treat with contempt the suffering of the afflicted King, nor the cries of His hurting people. This stands in sharp contrast to the way the world treats the vulnerable, offering profound comfort to those who feel abandoned in their trials.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root concept of bazah carries the physical and mental imagery of looking down upon someone or something, deeming it of little or no value, and turning away in disgust or indifference. It is the functional opposite of esteeming, honoring, or valuing. In the ancient Near Eastern culture, where honor and shame were the primary social currencies, to "despise" someone was to actively strip them of their honor, relegate them to the lowest social tier, and treat them as if they did not exist or have any right to life and dignity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb bazah means to despise, hold in contempt, disdain, or view as worthless. It denotes a deliberate attitude of mind that undervalues a person, covenant, or object, which inevitably manifests in actions of neglect, rejection, or active hostility. The semantic range spans from passive undervaluation (treating something of high value as insignificant) to active, scornful rejection.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, bazah is used to describe a variety of attitudes where sacred or valuable things are treated with cheap contempt:

• It describes Esau's attitude toward his birthright, which he exchanged for a single meal, showing he viewed his spiritual heritage as worthless.

• It describes the giant Goliath looking down upon young David with arrogant disdain because of his youth and appearance.

• It represents the attitude of those who despise God's word, His name, or His covenantal instructions, treating the holy creator as if He were insignificant.

• The underlying word picture is that of weighing something on a scale and declaring it completely weightless, empty, and fit for the trash heap.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, bazah reveals a stark contrast between human judgment and divine grace. Humans naturally despise what is weak, broken, or suffering. This is why the suffering Messiah was "despised and rejected by men" (Isaiah 53:3). However, God's character is the absolute reverse of human pride. While human systems and rulers despise the poor and afflicted, Yahweh does not. God's holiness does not make Him aloof or dismissive of human pain; rather, His eyes are drawn to the brokenhearted. The fact that God does not "despise" the afflicted means He validates their pain, hears their cries, and moves to rescue them.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22, the speaker transitions from agonizing suffering, intense mockery, and a sense of abandonment (verses 1–21) to a glorious declaration of praise and deliverance (verses 22–31). In verse 24, bazah is paired synonymously with shaqats ("abhor" or "detest").

While humanity "despised" the suffering King (as seen in verse 6, "a reproach of men, and despised by the people"), God the Father did not despise or abhor His affliction. When the Messiah hung on the cross, despised by the passing crowds and Jewish leaders, the Father did not look upon His suffering with contempt or turn away in permanent abandonment. He heard the cry for help. This is the turning point of the Psalm: the suffering of the righteous sufferer was not a sign of God's rejection, but the path to ultimate vindication.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 25:34 - "And Jacob gave Esau bread and stew of lentils; then he ate and drank, arose, and went his way. Thus Esau despised his birthright."

• 2 Samuel 12:9 - "Why have you despised the commandment of the LORD, to do evil in His sight?"

• Psalm 51:17 - "The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit, A broken and a contrite heart—These, O God, You will not despise."

• Isaiah 53:3 - "He is despised and rejected by men, A Man of sorrows and acquainted with grief. And we hid, as it were, our faces from Him; He was despised, and we did not esteem Him."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the New Testament, the Greek equivalent often used in the Septuagint (LXX) for bazah is exoutheneo or kataphroneo, meaning to look down upon or treat as nothing. This connects Psalm 22 directly to the crucifixion narrative in the Gospels. The soldiers, chief priests, and thieves mocked and despised Jesus, yet God highly exalted Him. For the believer today, this word provides immense comfort: when the world treats your pain, your faith, or your very life as worthless, your Heavenly Father does not despise you.
