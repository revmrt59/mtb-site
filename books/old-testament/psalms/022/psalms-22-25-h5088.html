VOWS - ( neder , Strong's H5088 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word neder refers to a solemn, voluntary promise or pledge made to God, typically initiated during times of distress, sickness, or battle. In these moments, an individual would promise to offer a sacrifice, perform a specific service, or give a gift to the Lord upon receiving His deliverance. In Psalm 22:25, "vows" represents the public celebration and sacrificial feast offered by the rescued sufferer. Having been delivered from the jaws of death, the speaker publicly fulfills his promised offerings in the presence of the congregation, transforming a private trial into a communal celebration of God's faithfulness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, a vow was a sacred transaction. Unlike pagan vows, which often sought to manipulate or bribe capricious deities, the Israelite neder was rooted in a relationship of trust with the covenant-keeping God, Yahweh. It was a voluntary act, but once uttered, it became a binding obligation.

Vows were typically accompanied by the shelem (peace or fellowship offering), specifically the todah (thanksgiving offering). When God answered the prayer and delivered the petitioner, the individual would travel to the sanctuary to "pay" the vow. This involved sacrificing an animal, a portion of which was offered to God, a portion given to the priests, and the remainder cooked and eaten in a joyous communal feast. This feast included family, friends, the levites, and the poor, ensuring that the deliverance of one brought joy and sustenance to many.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun neder means a vow, a promise made to God, or the votive offering itself. It originates from the verbal root nadar, which means to make a vow or to dedicate.

The lexical range of neder encompasses:

• A verbal pledge of devotion or abstinence made to God.

• The physical sacrificial animal or gift brought to the altar to fulfill that pledge (a votive offering).

• A formal declaration of praise and gratitude performed in a public, liturgical setting.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word neder is almost always paired with the Hebrew verb shalam, translated as "to pay," "to perform," or "to make complete." This pairing creates a powerful word picture:

• To "pay" a vow is not a commercial transaction where one buys God’s favor. Rather, it means to bring a situation to completion, wholeness, or peace (shalom).

• When a believer is in distress, the relationship feels disrupted by trial. Making a vow looks forward to deliverance, and "paying" the vow restores the visible harmony between the believer, God, and the community.

• Fulfilling a neder is depicted as a public testimony. It was never done in secret, but always in the "assembly" so that others could witness the goodness of God.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the neder links human prayer with divine sovereignty and human stewardship. It demonstrates that true worship is responsive; we promise and give because God has first heard and delivered.

In the wider scope of biblical theology, Psalm 22 is deeply messianic. Jesus quoted its opening line on the cross ("My God, my God, why have You forsaken Me?"). The shift in the psalm from agonizing abandonment (verses 1–21) to triumphant praise (verses 22–31) is marked by the paying of vows. Jesus, as the ultimate Sufferer, is delivered from the grave. His "vows" are fulfilled in the resurrection, leading to a great, worldwide feast of salvation where "the poor shall eat and be satisfied" (Psalm 22:26). The ultimate theological fulfillment of the neder is found in the Lord’s Supper and the eternal marriage supper of the Lamb, where Christ’s victory is celebrated by the global assembly of believers.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:25, the speaker declares: "My praise shall be of You in the great assembly; I will pay my vows before those who fear Him."

• This occurs immediately after the Lord has answered the sufferer's cry (verse 21).

• The "vows" here are the thanksgiving offerings promised while the speaker was surrounded by "bulls," "lions," and "dogs."

• The "great assembly" (qahal rab) refers to the congregation of Israel gathered at the temple.

• By paying his vows publicly, the Psalmist is not merely performing a legal duty; he is hosting a feast. The poor, the afflicted, and those who seek the Lord are invited to eat the sacrificial meat of his peace offerings. His deliverance becomes the source of life and encouragement for the entire community of faith.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 50:14 - "Offer to God thanksgiving, and pay your vows to the Most High."

• Psalm 61:8 - "So I will sing praise to Your name forever, that I may daily perform my vows."

• Psalm 116:14 - "I will pay my vows to the LORD now in the presence of all His people."

• Proverbs 20:25 - "It is a snare for a man to devote rashly something as holy, and afterward to reconsider his vows."

• Ecclesiastes 5:4 - "When you make a vow to God, do not delay to pay it; for He has no pleasure in fools. Pay what you have vowed."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Scripture warns strictly against making rash vows. Deuteronomy 23:21–23 states that while making a vow is voluntary, failing to pay it once spoken is a sin.

• The New Testament does not forbid vows but cautions against the abuse of oaths (Matthew 5:33–37; James 5:12).

• The concept of neder underlies the practice of Christian stewardship and testimony. When God delivers a believer from a crisis, it is fitting to share that testimony publicly and to dedicate resources, time, and praise to Him in the presence of the local church.
