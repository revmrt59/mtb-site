POOR - ( ‘ānāw , Strong's H6035 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ‘ānāw, translated as "poor" in Psalm 22:26, refers to those who are humble, meek, gentle, and afflicted. Rather than denoting mere material destitution, this term describes individuals who have been brought low by life's circumstances and have consequently abandoned self-reliance to depend entirely on God's grace and deliverance. In this context, the "poor" are the spiritually dependent, pious remnant who find their ultimate satisfaction and sustenance not in earthly wealth, but in the faithful presence and provision of Yahweh.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root of ‘ānāw is the verb ‘ānāh (Strong's H6031), which means to be bowed down, afflicted, humbled, or brought low. It evokes a vivid physical image of a person bent double under a heavy burden, crouched in submission, or forced to stoop by oppressors. Over time, this physical posture of being bowed down developed a moral and spiritual dimension. The ‘ānāw is one who has transitioned from being physically oppressed to being spiritually yielded. Instead of reacting to affliction with anger, pride, or rebellion, they respond with quiet trust, meekness, and voluntary submission to God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word means humble, meek, gentle, or afflicted. While frequently translated as "poor" or "meek" in the Old Testament, it does not primarily signify financial poverty (which is typically represented by the Hebrew words 'ebyôn or dal). Instead, ‘ānāw denotes a subjective state of mind and spirit. It describes a person who does not assert their own rights, lacks self-assertion, and quietly waits upon the Lord for vindication. The term designates those who recognize their complete spiritual bankruptcy before God and look to Him alone as their redeemer and defender.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• ‘ānāw is heavily concentrated in the poetic and prophetic literature of the Old Testament, particularly in the Psalms and Isaiah.

• It is often used in contrast with the proud, the mockers, and the ruthless oppressors who rely on their own strength and wealth.

• The word picture painted throughout Scripture is that of "the quiet of the land"—those who do not make a loud, aggressive display of self-sufficiency but instead seek righteousness and humility in the shadows of society.

• In Israel's worship, the ‘ānāw represents the ideal worshiper: one who approaches God without pretense, fully aware of their need for mercy.

4. Theological Implications

The ‘ānāw (often referred to in theological discussion by the plural anawim) hold a highly favored place in God's economy.

• God maintains a special covenantal concern for them; He hears their desire, prepares their heart, and does not forget their cry.

• This concept bridges the Old and New Testaments. It serves as the direct theological foundation for the Beatitudes, where Jesus pronounces blessings upon the "poor in spirit" and the "meek" (Matthew 5:3, 5).

• The ‘ānāw demonstrate that true spiritual wealth and inheritance are unlocked only through the pathway of humility and self-emptying.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:26, "The poor shall eat and be satisfied," the context shifts dramatically from agonizing lamentation (vv. 1–21) to a triumphant declaration of praise and victory (vv. 22–31). The righteous sufferer—who has been delivered from the jaws of death—celebrates a communal feast of thanksgiving (the shelamim, or peace offerings).

• In ancient Israel, a portion of the thanksgiving sacrifice was shared in a festive meal. The sufferer invites the ‘ānāw (the humble, afflicted companions) to partake in this banquet.

• The promise that they "shall eat and be satisfied" carries deep spiritual significance. While their physical hunger is met, their souls are deeply satisfied by witnessing God's vindication of the righteous sufferer.

• Their participation in this feast prompts the blessing, "Let your heart live forever!"—pointing to an eternal life and joy that transcends physical circumstance.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Numbers 12:3: "(Now the man Moses was very humble, more than all men who were on the face of the earth.)"

• Psalm 25:9: "The humble He guides in justice, And the humble He teaches His way."

• Psalm 37:11: "But the meek shall inherit the earth, And shall delight themselves in the abundance of peace."

• Isaiah 61:1: "The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon Me, Because the LORD has anointed Me To preach good tidings to the poor..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In the Septuagint (LXX), ‘ānāw is translated using terms like praus (meek, gentle) and ptōchos (poor, destitute). This dual translation demonstrates how Jewish translators recognized that physical lowliness and spiritual meekness are beautifully intertwined.

• The ultimate expression of the ‘ānāw is found in Jesus Christ, who described Himself as "gentle [meek] and lowly in heart" (Matthew 11:29), perfectly embodying the trusting, dependent posture of the righteous sufferer in Psalm 22.
