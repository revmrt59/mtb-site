NATIONS - ( goy , Strong's H1471 )

Summary:

In Psalm 22:27, the term translated "nations" refers to the non-Israelite peoples of the world (the Gentiles) viewed corporately as distinct ethnic and political groups. While the term historically distinguished the pagan world from the covenant nation of Israel, its use in this prophetic context signals a massive theological shift. The suffering of the righteous sufferer—ultimately fulfilled in the Messiah—does not end in despair but in a global triumph that brings all the ethnic families of the earth into voluntary, covenantal worship of Yahweh.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun goy (plural goyim) originally carried the basic meaning of a body or a defined group of people. Over time, it developed into a political and sociological designation for a nation, defined by a shared territory, government, language, and lineage. Unlike am (which typically refers to a people bound by close familial or covenantal ties, specifically Israel), goy focuses on the corporate and political structure of a people group. In the Old Testament, the plural form goyim frequently designates the surrounding non-Hebrew nations, highlighting their distinct identity apart from the covenant community of Israel.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term denotes a nation, people group, or Gentile collective. The primary semantic categories include:

• A political body or nation-state, which can occasionally include Israel when viewed in its political structure (e.g., Genesis 12:2).

• The non-Israelite nations of the world, contrasting the heathen or Gentile world with the unique covenant nation of God.

• The heathen or pagan peoples who do not know Yahweh and are characterized by idolatry.

In the prophetic and poetic literature, the word often expands to encompass all of humanity outside the immediate borders of Israel, representing the global stage of God's redemptive actions.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture uses goyim to depict the vast, diverse, and often hostile world surrounding the small nation of Israel. The word pictures associated with the nations range from roaring, chaotic waters threating to overwhelm God's people to a massive assembly summoned to the courtroom of God to witness His sovereign judgments. In Psalm 22:27, the term is paired with "all the ends of the world" and "all the families," creating a vivid geographical and sociological word picture. The entire globe, organized into its distinct linguistic, cultural, and political groupings, is pictured bowing down in unified worship before the true King.

4. Theological Implications

The theological trajectory of the goyim is central to the biblical narrative. In the Abrahamic Covenant, God promised that in Abraham "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3), indicating that the distinct nations were always the ultimate target of God’s redemptive plan. Psalm 22:27 serves as a key link in this theology. The suffering of the Messiah is the exact mechanism that unlocks the reconciliation of the nations. The Gentiles, who were once alienated from the covenants of promise and characterized by rebellion, are brought into active, humble submission to Yahweh. The text guarantees that global mission is not a human invention but a divinely decreed consequence of the Messiah's finished work.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 22 is the premier messianic psalm of suffering and glory. The first half of the psalm (verses 1–21) details the agonizing physical and spiritual suffering of the individual. The second half (verses 22–31) dramatically shifts to a song of triumph and praise. The scope of this praise expands in concentric circles: first to the sufferer’s immediate brethren (v. 22), then to the congregation of Israel (v. 23), and finally to the global community of the goyim (v. 27). The nuance in verse 27 is highly eschatological and conversionary. The nations do not merely submit under military coercion; they "remember and turn to the LORD" and "worship before You" out of genuine, heartfelt devotion because they have witnessed the deliverance of the sufferer.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:2 – "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you And make your name great; And you shall be a blessing."

• Psalm 2:1 – "Why do the nations rage, And the people plot a vain thing?"

• Psalm 2:8 – "Ask of Me, and I will give You The nations for Your inheritance, And the ends of the earth for Your possession."

• Psalm 9:17 – "The wicked shall be turned into hell, And all the nations that forget God."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), goyim is consistently translated as ethne, from which we derive the English word "ethnic." This linguistic link directly connects the Old Testament hope of Psalm 22 with the New Testament commission. When Jesus commands His followers to "make disciples of all the nations (ethne)" in Matthew 28:19, He is directing them to fulfill the prophetic reality declared in Psalm 22:27. The ultimate fulfillment of this verse is depicted in Revelation 7:9, where a great multitude from "all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues" stands before the throne in worship.
