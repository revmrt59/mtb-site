REMEMBER - ( zakar , Strong's H2142 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, to "remember" (zakar) is not merely a cognitive recall of information but a covenantal, action-oriented response. When humanity "remembers" God, it involves a turning away from self or idols and a turning toward Him in worship, obedience, and trust. In Psalm 22:27, this remembering represents a massive, global paradigm shift where the nations, awakened by the suffering and vindication of the righteous Sufferer, recall their true Creator and return to Him in worship.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, memory is intrinsically linked to action. Unlike the modern Western concept of remembering—which is often passive, quiet, and purely intellectual—the Hebrew concept of zakar requires the whole person to engage. To remember is to act on behalf of the object or person remembered, or to let that memory dictate one's present behavior.

When God "remembers" His people, He is not suddenly recalling someone He forgot; rather, He is moving to act graciously on their behalf in accordance with His covenant. Conversely, when human beings "remember" God, they are shaking off spiritual apathy, recognizing His rightful lordship, and aligning their lives with His truth. It is a re-establishing of a vital, active relationship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb zakar means to remember, recall, call to mind, or bring to remembrance. Its broader semantic range includes to mention, recite, proclaim, declare, or commemorate. It is a functional verb that bridges the gap between past knowledge and present action.

In the Qal stem, as seen in Psalm 22:27, the word denotes the active mental process of recalling God's character, His deeds, or His covenant, which immediately triggers a change in posture. It is a restorative memory that leads to repentance and worship.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs zakar in several distinct ways that enrich our understanding of the word:

• Covenantal Activation: God "remembers" His covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, prompting Him to deliver Israel from Egypt. The memory is the catalyst for redemption.

• Liturgical Commemoration: Worshipers "remember" the name of Yahweh or His past miracles during temple worship, reciting them aloud to anchor the faith of the community.

• Ethical Obligation: Israel is commanded to "remember" that they were once slaves in Egypt, a memory that must actively shape their compassion toward the poor, the widow, and the stranger.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the greatest tragedy of human sin is "forgetting" God. Hosea, Jeremiah, and the Psalmists frequently charge Israel and the nations with forgetting their Maker, which leads directly to idolatry and moral ruin. Forgetting is not an intellectual slip; it is a willful, covenantal rebellion.

Therefore, when the nations "remember" in Psalm 22:27, it signifies a profound spiritual resurrection. It represents the reversal of the Fall and the healing of the dispersion at the Tower of Babel. By remembering Yahweh, the global community recalls its original purpose: to reflect His image and offer Him praise. This eschatological remembering marks the end of spiritual amnesia for the Gentile world.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 22 begins in the darkest depths of suffering ("My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?") but transitions dramatically in verse 22 into a song of triumph and global evangelism. The Sufferer has been delivered from the horns of the wild oxen, and His response is to declare God's name to His brethren.

In verse 27, the impact of this deliverance expands from the immediate congregation of Israel to "all the ends of the world." The catalyst for this global conversion is that they "shall remember." What do they remember? They remember the Sufferer’s agony and vindication, and through that event, they remember the true and living God whom they had forgotten through centuries of idolatry. This cognitive awakening immediately produces two behavioral fruits: they "turn to the LORD" (repentance) and "worship before You" (devotion).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 8:1 - "Then God remembered Noah, and every living thing, and all the animals that were with him in the ark."

• Exodus 20:8 - "Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy."

• Psalm 103:14 - "For He knows our frame; He remembers that we are dust."

• Psalm 105:8 - "He remembers His covenant forever, The word which He commanded, for a thousand generations,"

• Jonah 2:7 - "When my soul fainted within me, I remembered the LORD; And my prayer went up to You, Into Your holy temple."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek Septuagint (LXX), the Hebrew zakar in Psalm 22:27 is translated using the verb mnaomai (specifically mnesthesontai), which likewise means to remember, recollect, or be mindful of. This connection underscores the New Testament reality of the gospel's spread to the Gentiles. Through the apostolic proclamation of Christ’s death and resurrection (the fulfillment of Psalm 22), the nations of the earth are shaken out of their spiritual forgetfulness and brought back to the true worship of Yahweh.
