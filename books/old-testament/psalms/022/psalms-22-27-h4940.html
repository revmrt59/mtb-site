FAMILIES - ( mishpachah , Strong's H4940 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun mishpachah denotes a clan, subdivision of a tribe, or an extended family group sharing a common lineage and geographical inheritance. In the ancient world, it represented the vital social unit linking the individual household to the larger tribe. In the theological landscape of Psalm 22:27, the term expands far beyond the borders of Israel to encompass all the ethnic clans and subgroups of the Gentile world. This usage signals the ultimate fulfillment of the Abrahamic Covenant, demonstrating that the direct result of the Messiah’s suffering and deliverance is the global, multi-ethnic worship of the one true God by all the families of the earth.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Israelite society, social structure was organized in a nested hierarchy: the tribe (shebet or matteh), the clan or family group (mishpachah), and the ancestral house or immediate household (bet ab). The mishpachah was the intermediate, yet highly functional, level of this structure. It was composed of several households bound by blood ties, shared history, and geographical proximity.

The mishpachah served as the primary sphere for legal protection, economic survival, and the preservation of ancestral land. Key covenantal duties—such as the redemption of property or the protection of vulnerable relatives—occurred at the clan level. Thus, the word evokes deep connotations of belonging, mutual responsibility, relational closeness, and inherited identity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun mishpachah refers to a clan, family, sub-tribe, or class of people. It denotes a group of individuals unified by common descent or shared characteristics. In some contexts, it can even refer to subdivisions of animals or distinct species.

In its primary socio-political usage, it designates a subdivision of an Israelite tribe. In broader global and prophetic contexts, it refers to the various ethnic, linguistic, and national subgroups of humanity. It represents the distinct organic units that make up the global human family, emphasizing that God views humanity not merely as a massive, undifferentiated population, but as structured, distinct communities.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term is used extensively throughout the Old Testament, appearing over 300 times.

• The Census and Land Allotment: In the books of Numbers and Joshua, the term occurs repeatedly to designate the structural divisions of Israel. Lands were distributed "according to their families" (mishpachot), ensuring that every clan received its designated portion of the promised inheritance.

• The Covenantal Promise: The term first takes on global significance in the call of Abraham. God promises that "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3). Here, the word acts as a comprehensive term for all human groupings, indicating that God’s redemptive plan would penetrate every cultural and kinship line.

• Classes or Guilds: Occasionally, the word describes professional or social groups, such as the "families of the scribes" (1 Chronicles 2:55), illustrating a shared identity and purpose.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of mishpachah in the Old Testament rests on the tension between God’s election of one specific family (Israel) and His ultimate design for all the families of the earth. Amos 3:2 highlights Israel's unique status: "You only have I known of all the families of the earth." Yet, this election was never intended to be exclusive; it was a mechanism of mediation.

The prophetic vision repeatedly looks forward to a day when the boundary of the covenant expands. The global mishpachot, once alienated from the covenants of promise, will be brought into covenant relationship with Yahweh. This represents the realization of the Abrahamic promise—not by dissolving cultural and kinship distinctions, but by bringing those diverse families into unified worship.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 22 is the premier Messianic psalm of suffering and vindication. The first half of the psalm (verses 1–21) details the agonizing cry of the righteous sufferer, surrounded by mockers and facing death. The second half (verses 22–31) transitions dramatically into a song of triumph and global declaration.

In Psalm 22:27, the suffering of the individual gives way to a global awakening: "All the ends of the world shall remember and turn to the LORD, / And all the families of the nations shall worship before You."

• The Scope of Worship: The parallel structure pairs "all the ends of the world" with "all the families of the nations." The worship of Yahweh will not be confined to Israel's clans, but will extend to every foreign mishpachah (translated here as "families of the nations").

• The Cause of Worship: This universal conversion is presented as the direct consequence of the sufferer’s deliverance. The suffering of the Messiah purchases the reclamation of the Gentile clans.

• The Nature of Worship: By specifying "families" (mishpachot) rather than just generic "nations" (goyim), the psalmist underscores that this global worship will be intimate, communal, and representative of the world's diverse cultural households.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Joshua 13:15 - "And Moses had given to the tribe of the children of Reuben an inheritance according to their families."

• Amos 3:2 - "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities."

• Zechariah 14:17 - "And it shall be that whichever of the families of the earth do not come up to Jerusalem to worship the King, the LORD of hosts, on them there will be no rain."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), mishpachah in Psalm 22:27 (21:28 in the LXX) is translated using the Greek word patria, which refers to lineage, family, or ancestry. This same Greek word is utilized by the Apostle Peter in Acts 3:25 when he quotes the Abrahamic covenant to his Jewish audience: "And in your seed all the families (patriai) of the earth shall be blessed."

This linguistic link directly connects the suffering and resurrection of Jesus Christ to the in-gathering of the Gentile clans. The New Testament picture of heaven in Revelation 5:9 and 7:9—where a multitude from every tribe (phyle), tongue, people, and nation stands before the Lamb—is the ultimate realization of the prophetic promise first sung in Psalm 22:27.
