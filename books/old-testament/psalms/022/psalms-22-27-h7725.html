TURN - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub is a foundational Old Testament word meaning to return, turn back, or go back. In the context of Psalm 22:27, it signifies a decisive spiritual U-turn—a radical conversion and return of the nations to the one true God in repentance, worship, and allegiance. This turning is not merely physical movement but a reorientation of the heart, responding to the global declaration of Yahweh's ultimate victory and reign.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The physical root meaning of shub is to turn back, return, or retrace one's steps. It is a verb of motion, occurring over a thousand times in the Old Testament, making it one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Bible.

• The basic word picture is that of a traveler who realizes they are going the wrong way, stops, turns 180 degrees, and walks back to their point of origin or home.

• In a spiritual context, shub portrays humanity as having wandered far from God, traveling down a destructive path. Repentance is thus visualized not as a mere feeling of regret, but as the physical act of turning around and walking back to God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb means to return, turn back, turn away from, or restore. It is the primary Old Testament term used to express the concept of repentance.

• The semantic range spans from simple physical movement (such as returning to a tent or country) to profound spiritual and relational transformation.

• In the active Qal stem, when paired with the preposition el ("to" or "toward") and directed at Yahweh, it denotes a complete change of direction, a loyal return to the covenant Creator, and the abandonment of false gods or sinful paths.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Path Imagery: Biblical poetry frequently conceptualizes human behavior as a path (derek). Wandering off the path is sin; turning back (shub) is restoration.

• Relational Homecomings: It is used of a wife returning to her husband or a runaway child returning to a father, highlighting the relational warmth and reconciliation inherent in the word.

• Global Reclamation: In Psalm 22:27, the word picture expands from individual or national Israelite repentance to a global homecoming. The "ends of the world" are pictured as exiled children who finally remember their origin and make the long trek back home to their Creator.

4. Theological Implications

• Repentance (teshuvah): The theological heart of shub is that true repentance requires both a turning from sin and a turning to God. One cannot exist without the other.

• Messianic Triumph: Psalm 22 begins with the agonizing cry of the suffering Messiah ("My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?") but ends with global triumph. The turning of the nations is the direct fruit of the Messiah's suffering and vindication. His victory secures the salvation of the world.

• Sovereign Grace: The call to turn always implies God's willingness to receive the turning sinner. Yahweh is not a distant deity but a welcoming Father waiting for the nations to return.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Psalm 22:27, the act of turning (shub) is paired with "remembering." The cognitive realization of God's sovereign power and the Messiah's victory triggers the volitional act of turning to Him.

• The destination of this turning is explicitly "to the LORD" (el-YHWH). It is not a generic moral improvement but a personal, covenantal alignment with Israel's God.

• The scope is breathtakingly universal: "All the ends of the world" and "all the families of the nations." This marks a prophetic pivot where the covenant blessings of Abraham finally burst past Israel's borders to encompass the entire globe.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ezekiel 33:11: "Say to them: ‘As I live,’ says the Lord GOD, ‘I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked, but that the wicked turn from his way and live. Turn, turn from your evil ways! For why should you die, O house of Israel?’"

• Joel 2:12: "Now, therefore," says the LORD, "Turn to Me with all your heart, With fasting, with weeping, and with mourning;"

• Zechariah 1:3: "Therefore say to them, ‘Thus says the LORD of hosts: "Return to Me," says the LORD of hosts, "and I will return to you," says the LORD of hosts.’"

• Isaiah 55:7: "Let the wicked forsake his way, And the unrighteous man his thoughts; Let him return to the LORD, And He will have mercy on him; And to our God, For He will abundantly pardon."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Septuagint (LXX) translates shub in Psalm 22:27 with the Greek verb epistrepho, which means to turn back, return, or convert.

• This connects directly to the New Testament usage of epistrepho in the book of Acts and the Pauline Epistles to describe the conversion of the Gentiles (e.g., Acts 15:19; 1 Thessalonians 1:9, "how you turned to God from idols"). The apostolic mission to the Gentiles is the direct historical fulfillment of the prophetic promise made in Psalm 22:27.
