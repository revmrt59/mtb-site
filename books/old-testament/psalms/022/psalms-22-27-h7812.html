WORSHIP - ( shachah , Strong's H7812 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shachah fundamentally means to bow down, prostrate oneself, or pay homage. It depicts a physical posture of complete submission, reverence, and adoration before a superior, most notably before the sovereign God. In Psalm 22:27, this word describes the global response of the nations to Yahweh's reign, pointing forward to a day when all peoples will abandon their idols and prostrate themselves in willing submission and worship before the true King.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The physical picture behind shachah is that of a person bowing low to the ground, falling prostrate, or lying flat with the face touching the earth. In the ancient Near East, this was the standard posture of a subject before a monarch, or a servant before a master. It was a visible, undeniable demonstration of one's vulnerability, loyalty, and recognition of the other's absolute authority. When applied to God, this gesture of prostration represents the ultimate externalization of an internal attitude of absolute surrender, awe, and total dependence upon the Creator.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shachah means to bow down, prostrate oneself, do obeisance, or worship. Occurring over 170 times in the Old Testament, it is the primary Hebrew word used to denote worship. While it can describe civil respect or honor shown to human superiors, its most critical and frequent theological usage is to designate the exclusive worship due to Yahweh. The term emphasizes the relational dependency of the worshiper, showing that biblical worship is not merely a cognitive acknowledgment of God, but an active, physical, and volitional submission to His lordship.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently pairs shachah with other verbs of physical posture to emphasize the depth of devotion:

• Falling down and worshiping: Worshipers do not merely stand and sing; they fall to their knees and bow their heads to the earth, illustrating their smallness in the presence of the infinite God.

• The prohibition of false worship: The word is repeatedly used in warnings against idolatry, where God's people are commanded not to "bow down" (shachah) to graven images. Worship is a zero-sum reality; to bow to an idol is to rob God of His exclusive right to our submission.

• A sign of total surrender: It represents the complete subjugation of the worshiper's will, rights, and life to the majesty of the one being worshiped.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shachah demonstrates that true worship requires the lowering of self so that God may be exalted. It undercuts any form of casual or self-centered worship.

• The Righteousness of God's Reign: Worship is the logical response to God's holiness and sovereignty. When we see Him as He is, our natural reaction is to lower ourselves in His presence.

• Eschatological Horizon: The Old Testament prophets and psalmists look forward to a day when the entire cosmos will bow before Yahweh. Shachah is the ultimate posture of the restored creation. Every knee will bow, and every tongue will confess His lordship.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:27, "worship" (shachah) serves as the climax of a remarkable three-fold response of the global community: "remember," "turn," and "worship."

• The Transition from Suffering to Praise: Psalm 22 begins with the agonizing cry of the abandoned sufferer ("My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?"). Yet, by verse 21, the tone shifts dramatically to vindication and triumph.

• The Global Impact of the Sufferer's Vindication: Because of the deliverance of this righteous sufferer (ultimately fulfilled in the resurrection of Jesus Christ), the saving message of Yahweh's grace will go forth to the ends of the earth.

• From Nations to Servants: The families of the nations will no longer bow down to their local, useless idols; instead, they will fall prostrate (shachah) before the living God. It highlights a voluntary, joyful submission of the Gentile world to the God of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 22:5: "And Abraham said to his young men, 'Stay here with the donkey; the lad and I will go yonder and worship, and we will come back to you.'"

• Exodus 20:5: "you shall not bow down to them nor serve them. For I, the LORD your God, am a jealous God..."

• Psalm 95:6: "Oh come, let us worship and bow down; Let us kneel before the LORD our Maker."

• Psalm 99:5: "Exalt the LORD our God, And worship at His footstool— He is holy."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), shachah is almost exclusively translated by the Greek verb proskyneo. This Greek word carries the same primary meaning of bowing down, falling prostrate, or kissing the hand toward someone in deep reverence. This direct conceptual link explains why, in the New Testament, individuals fell at the feet of Jesus to "worship" (proskyneo) Him. In doing so, they were recognizing Him as the sovereign Lord of Psalm 22:27, the one before whom all the families of the earth must ultimately bow.
