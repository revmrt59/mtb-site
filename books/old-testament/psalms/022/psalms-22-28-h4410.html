KINGDOM - ( melukah , Strong's H4410 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word melukah refers to kingship, royalty, or sovereign rule rather than merely a geographical territory. In Psalm 22:28, it declares that ultimate, absolute dominion belongs inherently to Yahweh. This term shifts our focus from a physical realm to the active, reigning authority of God over all nations, serving as a foundational truth that anchors the believer's trust during times of deep suffering and global upheaval.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun melukah is derived from the root verb malak, which means to reign, rule, or become king. In the ancient Near East, kingship was the primary form of government, representing order, justice, and protection. Melukah does not focus on the physical boundaries of a country or its citizens, but rather on the status, dignity, power, and active authority of the monarch. It represents the very essence of royal dignity and the right to exercise sovereign rule. When applied to God, it paints a vivid picture of Yahweh seated on His cosmic throne, actively directing history and governing the affairs of humankind with absolute authority.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Melukah refers to kingship, royal power, dominion, or sovereign reign. It denotes the office, status, and authority of a king. While standard translations often render it as "kingdom," the semantic focus is on the exercise of kingly authority rather than a geopolitical realm. It is the dynamic expression of royal rule.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

While other Hebrew words for kingdom focus on different aspects—such as malkut (the realm or political empire) and mamlakah (the kingdom as a political entity)—melukah specifically highlights the personal, active kingship and royal dignity of the ruler.

• In human contexts, it describes the royal status of kings like Saul and Solomon, emphasizing their legitimacy and the establishment of their royal authority.

• In divine contexts, it depicts God's personal and undisputed sovereignty over the universe. God's melukah is not delegated or inherited; it belongs to Him inherently and eternally.

4. Theological Implications

The statement that "the kingdom is the LORD's" carries massive theological weight:

• Universal Sovereignty: God's rule is not localized or restricted to Israel. His melukah extends over all Gentile nations, demanding universal submission and worship.

• Messianic Triumph: Psalm 22 is a profoundly messianic psalm, beginning with the agonizing cry of the cross ("My God, my God, why have You forsaken Me?") and ending with a triumphant vision of worldwide worship. The transition from suffering to global dominion is secured because Yahweh holds absolute royal authority.

• The Foundation of Worship: The nations will return to Yahweh and worship Him because He alone possesses the legitimate right to rule. Divine kingship is the theological engine that drives global missions and eschatological hope.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the immediate context of Psalm 22, the declaration of God's melukah in verse 28 serves as the turning point of the psalm's final movement. After enduring horrific suffering and apparent abandonment, the speaker is delivered. This individual deliverance has cosmic implications. The psalmist declares that all the families of the nations will worship before Yahweh because "the kingdom is the LORD's." The nuance here is pastoral and comforting: no matter how chaotic, painful, or unjust the immediate circumstances appear, God's royal rule has not been compromised. His sovereign authority guarantees that righteousness will ultimately prevail and that the afflicted will find satisfaction.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• 1 Kings 2:12: "Then Solomon sat on the throne of his father David; and his kingdom was firmly established."

• Isaiah 34:12: "They shall call its nobles to the kingdom, But none shall be there, and all its princes shall be nothing."

• Obadiah 1:21: "Then saviors shall come to Mount Zion To judge the Mountains of Esau, And the kingdom shall be the LORD’s."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), melukah in Psalm 22:28 is translated as basileia. This directly links the Old Testament concept of Yahweh's royal rule with the New Testament proclamation of the "Kingdom of God" (basileia tou theou) preached by Jesus Christ. The suffering of the Messiah in Psalm 22 is the very means by which this divine melukah is established and manifested to the ends of the earth.
