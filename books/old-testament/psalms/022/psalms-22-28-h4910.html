RULES - ( māšal , Strong's H4910 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb māšal conveys the exercise of dominion, governance, and sovereign rule. In Psalm 22:28, it affirms Yahweh's active, absolute, and undisputed reign over all geopolitical and ethnic boundaries. While earthly rulers govern by delegated or temporary authority, Yahweh’s rule is inherent, eternal, and universal, transitioning the psalm from a cry of individual suffering to a triumphant declaration of cosmic divine sovereignty.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb māšal fundamentally relates to the exercise of authority, dominion, or power over others. Unlike the Hebrew verb malak (which means to reign specifically as an enthroned king), māšal emphasizes the active exertion of governance, the management of affairs, or the position of master over a domain.

The underlying word picture is one of standing in a position of superior strength and recognized authority where one’s will, decree, and order direct the actions of those under dominion. It implies both the legitimate right to rule and the power necessary to enforce that rule. It is used to describe the dominion of the sun and moon over the day and night, the authority of a governor over a province, and the absolute sovereignty of God over His creation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of māšal is to rule, have dominion, reign, govern, or take charge. Its semantic range includes several distinct contexts:

• To exercise political or royal dominion: Governing nations, kingdoms, or peoples with sovereign power.

• To manage or administer: Serving as a steward or governor over another's household or property.

• To exert mastership or control: Having mastery over physical forces, circumstances, or internal human passions and desires.

When applied to God, māšal denotes an active, continuous, and absolute exercise of His sovereign will over the created order, history, and the affairs of mankind.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs māšal in various contexts to illustrate different dimensions of authority:

• Stewardship and Administration: Abraham’s chief servant, Eliezer, is described as the one who "ruled" over all that Abraham possessed (Genesis 24:2). This demonstrates māšal as trusted management and executive authority.

• The Created Order: In the creation account, God appointed the sun and the moon to "rule" over the day and the night (Genesis 1:18). This designates functional supremacy and the ordering of time.

• Moral and Internal Control: The term is applied to self-mastery in Proverbs 16:32, which declares that he who "rules" his spirit is better than he who takes a city. Here, māšal represents ethical discipline and emotional restraint.

• Divine Empire: God’s rule is contrasted with human failure. While human rulers are often oppressive or temporary, Yahweh's māšal is characterized by perfect justice, ultimate power, and eternal duration.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of māšal in the Old Testament rests on the absolute supremacy of God:

• Universal Sovereignty: God’s rule is not localized or restricted to Israel. The use of māšal in connection with the "nations" (the goyim) asserts His jurisdiction over the entire Gentile world, defying the ancient Near Eastern concept of localized, territorial deities.

• The Messianic Arc of Redemption: Psalm 22 begins with the agonizing cry of the abandoned sufferer ("My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?") but concludes with a glorious vision of global worship. The suffering of the righteous servant ultimately results in the nations recognizing that Yahweh "rules over the nations." In New Testament theology, this find its ultimate fulfillment in the resurrection and ascension of Jesus Christ, who has been given all authority in heaven and on earth.

• Providential Control: History is not a series of random events. God actively governs and directs the rise and fall of empires according to His divine decrees.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:28, the phrase "And He rules over the nations" occurs in the climax of the psalm. The suffering has ended, the deliverance has occurred, and the congregation is called to praise. The scope of this praise immediately widens from the local congregation of Israel to "all the ends of the world" (v. 27).

The declaration that God "rules" over the nations serves as the theological foundation for this universal conversion. The nations will turn to Yahweh because the kingdom belongs to Him. The verb māšal is a present active participle (mōšēl), indicating an ongoing, active, and present reality. Even when the nations rage or ignore God, His sovereign rule is active and uninterrupted. The suffering Messiah’s vindication serves as the catalyst for the world to recognize this pre-existing reality.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:18 – "and to rule over the day and over the night, and to divide the light from the darkness. And God saw that it was good."

• Genesis 3:16 – "To the woman He said: 'I will greatly multiply your sorrow and your conception; In pain you shall bring forth children; Your desire shall be for your husband, And he shall rule over you.'"

• Psalm 103:19 – "The LORD has established His throne in heaven, And His kingdom rules over all."

• Proverbs 16:32 – "He who is slow to anger is better than the mighty, And he who rules his spirit than he who takes a city."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament) translates māšal in Psalm 22:28 (numbered as 21:29 in the Greek text) using the Greek verb despoto (despotzei), from which the English word "despot" is derived. While "despot" has a negative, tyrannical connotation in modern English, in ancient Greek it denoted absolute, unchallenged mastership and ownership. The translators chose this term to emphasize that Yahweh's authority over the nations is not a negotiated, democratic leadership, but the absolute lordship of the Creator-Owner of the universe.
