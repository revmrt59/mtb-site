ALIVE - ( chayah , Strong's H2421 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb chayah in Psalm 22:29 carries the fundamental meaning of living, breathing, or being preserved in life. In this specific verse, it is used in the Piel stem, which carries an active, causative nuance ("to keep alive" or "to preserve life"). Within the immediate context of this Messianic psalm, the word highlights humanity's absolute inability to sustain its own existence or escape the inevitability of death. It underscores the ultimate sovereignty of Yahweh over life and death, setting the stage for a posture of total dependence and universal worship before the sovereign Lord.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root chayah is deeply tied to the ancient Near Eastern concept of life as dynamic activity, vitality, and relationship rather than mere biological existence. To "live" or to "be kept alive" is to be filled with the breath of God, to possess physical strength, and to be preserved from the threatening forces of chaos, sickness, and Sheol. In the Old Testament, the contrast between life (chayim) and death (muth) is not just a clinical distinction but a spiritual and relational reality. Being "alive" means participating in the covenant community and enjoying the light of God’s presence, whereas going down to the dust represents the cessation of that earthly worship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb chayah occurs over 280 times in the Hebrew Bible. Its basic Qal stem meaning is to live, exist, remain alive, or recover from sickness.

• In the Piel stem (the form used in Psalm 22:29, chiyyah), the verb transitions from the state of being alive to the active preservation or restoration of life. It means to preserve alive, let live, quicken, or restore to life.

• In Psalm 22:29, the phrase "keep his own soul alive" (napsho lo chiyyah) utilizes the Piel form to denote the preservation of one’s own soul (nephesh) or life-breath. The lexical emphasis is on the sheer impossibility of any human being sustaining their own vital force or preventing their own descent into the grave.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, chayah is used in several vivid word pictures:

• The Breath of Life: It is connected to God breathing into man the breath of life to make him a living soul (Genesis 2:7).

• Resurrection and Revival: It describes dry bones coming back to life (Ezekiel 37:9) or a dead body reviving upon touching the bones of Elisha (2 Kings 13:21).

• Preservation in Crisis: It is used when God preserves life during famines, wars, or plagues (Genesis 45:7).

• Spiritual Quickenings: The psalmists frequently cry out for God to "revive" them (chayah) in times of deep affliction, linking physical survival with spiritual renewal (Psalm 119:25).

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of chayah in Psalm 22:29 centers on the doctrine of divine sovereignty and human limitation.

• God as the Sole Sustainer: Life is a gift that cannot be self-generated or self-sustained. Humanity is fundamentally dependent; no man possesses the inherent power to keep his own soul alive.

• The Levelling of Humanity: Both the wealthy ("the prosperous") and the dying ("those who go down to the dust") are subject to mortal decay. This levels all human distinctions, showing that wealth, power, and prestige cannot purchase immunity from death.

• The Messianic Hope: In the context of Psalm 22—a psalm that moves from the agony of crucifixion to the glory of global dominion—this limitation of human self-preservation prepares the reader for the necessity of a resurrection. Because man cannot keep himself alive, God must step in to conquer death and offer eternal life.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 22:29 stands near the climax of this great Messianic psalm. Having endured the suffering of the cross (verses 1-21), the Speaker transitions to a declaration of worldwide praise (verses 22-31).

• The setting of verse 29 is a great, universal feast where "all the prosperous of the earth" eat and worship, alongside "all those who go down to the dust" (the dying or those of low estate).

• The phrase "And no one can keep his own soul alive" acts as a vital parenthetical truth. It explains why all these diverse groups must bow before Yahweh: because every participant in this great assembly, regardless of earthly status, is mortal. They cannot preserve their own lives; they are entirely dependent on the King of Kings for both physical breath and eternal salvation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 45:7 - "And God sent me before you to preserve a posterity for you in the earth, and to save your lives [chayah] by a great deliverance."

• Nehemiah 9:6 - "You, even You, are LORD alone; You have made heaven, the heaven of heavens, with all their host, the earth and everything on it, the seas and all that is in them, and You preserve [chayah] them all."

• Psalm 30:3 - "O LORD, You brought my soul up from the grave; You have kept me alive [chayah], that I should not go down to the pit."

• Psalm 119:25 - "My soul clings to the dust; Revive [chayah] me according to Your word."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek Septuagint (LXX), the Hebrew text of Psalm 22:29 (numbered as Psalm 21:30 in the LXX) presents a slightly different textual tradition, translating the phrase as "and my soul lives to Him." However, the Masoretic Text’s reading ("and his own soul he could not keep alive") remains the more challenging and grammatically consistent reading. This reading emphasizes the absolute contrast between human frailty and the eternal, self-existent life of God, a theme that Jesus later fulfills by declaring that He has life in Himself and has the power to raise up whom He wills (John 5:21-26).
