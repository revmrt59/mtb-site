SERVE - ( abad , Strong's H5647 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb abad represents a rich biblical concept that blends physical labor, royal allegiance, and sacred worship. In Psalm 22:30, it describes the response of future generations to the deliverance wrought by the suffering Messiah. Rather than a burdensome obligation, this service is a voluntary, joyful devotion of one's entire life to the reigning King. It denotes a shift in ownership, where those redeemed by the Lord's suffering now dedicate their strength, obedience, and adoration to Him forever.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root abad is deeply embedded in the daily life of ancient Israel, originating in agricultural labor and domestic service. It portrays a servant working the ground, cultivating a vineyard, or performing duties under the authority of a master. To abad is to expend energy and strength to carry out the will of another. In the ancient Near East, a servant’s identity was entirely wrapped up in their master. Therefore, when used in relation to Yahweh, the word pictures a life completely surrendered to His will, where one’s labor is not empty toil but a meaningful act of devotion.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb abad primary means to work, labor, serve, or till. Depending on the context, its meaning ranges from physical work to spiritual worship:

• Labor and Cultivation: To till the soil, dress a vineyard, or work a field.

• Subjugation and Service: To serve a human master, king, or political ruler as a subject or tributary.

• Sacred Worship: To serve Yahweh through obedience, sacrificial offerings, and temple duties.

In religious contexts, service and worship are inseparable. To serve God is to worship Him, and to worship Him is to serve Him. It represents the total alignment of one's actions, allegiance, and affection toward the Creator.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs abad in several distinct ways to illustrate the relationship between a servant and a master:

• The Edenic Ideal: In the Garden of Eden, humanity was commissioned to "tend" (abad) the garden. This establishes that serving is a noble, pre-fall calling, reflecting human dignity as God's stewards.

• The Exodus Transition: Israel was enslaved to Pharaoh, serving (abad) him under harsh bondage. God delivered them with a specific purpose: to leave Egypt so they could serve (abad) Yahweh in the wilderness. True freedom is not the absence of a master, but serving the rightful Master.

• The Levitical Ministry: Priests and Levites performed the physical and spiritual service of the Tabernacle. This service was sacred, holy, and highly regulated, emphasizing that serving God requires reverence and purity.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, abad demonstrates that worship is not merely an inward feeling or a temporary ritual, but an active, life-encompassing lifestyle of obedience.

• Exclusive Allegiance: God demands exclusive service. Serving Yahweh stands in direct opposition to serving idols or foreign gods.

• The Servant of the Lord: This term culminates in the prophetic "Servant of the LORD" (Ebed Yahweh) in Isaiah. The Messiah is the ultimate Servant who perfectly submits to the Father's will, even unto death, to redeem others.

• Redemption and Ownership: Because God redeems His people, they become His servants. Service is the natural, grateful response to redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 22 begins with the agonizing cry of the abandoned sufferer ("My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?") but ends in a triumphant declaration of God's global kingdom. In verse 30, the suffering has achieved its purpose: "A posterity shall serve Him."

The contextual nuance here is highly significant:

• A Future Legacy: The Hebrew zera (posterity, seed) refers to future generations. The suffering of the Messiah produces a spiritual offspring.

• Eternally Bound Service: This posterity does not merely study Him or admire Him from afar; they serve Him. Their lives are characterized by active, continuous submission and worship to the resurrected and vindicated King.

• The Catalyst for Service: This generational service is motivated entirely by the proclamation of the Lord's righteousness and His finished work ("He has done this," v. 31).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:15: "Then the LORD God took the man and put him in the garden of Eden to tend and keep it."

• Exodus 3:12: "And He said, 'I will certainly be with you. And this shall be a sign to you that I have sent you: When you have brought the people out of Egypt, you shall serve God on this mountain.'"

• Joshua 24:15: "And if it seems evil to you to serve the LORD, choose for yourselves this day whom you will serve... But as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD."

• Psalm 100:2: "Serve the LORD with gladness; Come before His presence with singing."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (LXX), the Greek translation of the Old Testament, the Hebrew abad in Psalm 22:30 is translated using the Greek verb latreuo. This Greek term refers specifically to sacred service, priestly ministry, and religious worship. This linguistic link highlights that the service of the future generation is not menial slavery, but a dignified, priestly devotion. Every believer, as part of this promised "posterity," participates in a royal priesthood, offering spiritual sacrifices of praise and obedience to the Lord.
