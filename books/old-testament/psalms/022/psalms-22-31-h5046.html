DECLARE - ( nagad , Strong's H5046 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nagad means to make conspicuous, announce, or openly report. In Psalm 22:31, it describes a future generation publicly proclaiming the saving righteousness of Yahweh to those yet unborn. This is not private contemplation, but an energetic, clear, and loud declaration of God's finished work of salvation, signaling the global and multi-generational impact of the Messiah's suffering and triumph.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primitive root nagad carries the core concept of placing something out in the front, making it conspicuous, or bringing it to light. It is a visual and dynamic word. The underlying imagery is that of a messenger standing directly in front of someone to deliver a report that cannot be ignored. Rather than keeping information concealed or whispered in secret, nagad involves pulling back the veil and placing the facts squarely in the sight of the listener.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb nagad means to tell, declare, announce, report, or make known. In the Hiphil stem (the form used in Psalm 22:31), it specifically carries the active sense of declaring, proclaiming, or expounding. It signifies an authoritative, clear, and public communication of a message. It is the opposite of hiding or keeping silent; it is the deliberate act of making a truth manifest and fully understood by the recipient.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Across the Old Testament, nagad is used in several key contexts:

• The Royal Messenger: It describes a messenger arriving from the battlefield to "tell" or "report" the outcome of a battle to the king (1 Sam 4:17).

• Divine Revelation: It is used of God "declaring" His decrees, His covenant, or His future plans to His people, leaving no doubt about His intentions (Deut 4:13).

• Public Testimony: It is used of individuals publicly "declaring" God's wondrous works and praises in the congregation (Ps 9:11).

The word picture is that of a royal herald standing in the public square, reading a decree from the king. It leaves no room for ambiguity, hesitation, or private interpretation.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nagad highlights the objective and historical nature of biblical faith. God's righteousness and saving acts are not subjective feelings or secret philosophies; they are historical events that must be reported and declared. Furthermore, the word underscores the necessity of intergenerational discipleship. God's faithfulness is preserved on earth through the active, vocal proclamation of one generation to the next. Worship is not complete until the deeds of God are declared to those who did not witness them firsthand.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 22 begins in the darkest depths of suffering and desertion ("My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?") but shifts dramatically to a triumphant shout of praise in verse 22. By the end of the Psalm (vv. 27–31), the scope of this praise expands globally and temporally. In verse 31, "They will come and declare His righteousness to a people who will be born," the acts of the suffering yet vindicated Savior become the content of an ongoing, historical declaration. The future generation will act as heralds, placing the finished work of God directly in front of the next generation, ensuring the message of salvation is never lost.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 71:18 - "Now also when I am old and grayheaded, O God, do not forsake me, Until I declare Your strength to this generation, Your power to everyone who is to come."

• Psalm 97:6 - "The heavens declare His righteousness, And all the peoples see His glory."

• Psalm 145:4 - "One generation shall praise Your works to another, And shall declare Your mighty acts."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), nagad in Psalm 22:31 is translated using anangello, which means to announce back, report, or rehearse. This same Greek word is used in the New Testament to describe the apostles "declaring" the gospel of Jesus Christ (1 John 1:3).

Furthermore, the final phrase of Psalm 22:31, "That He has done this" (Hebrew: asah), carries the exact same theological weight as Jesus’ final cry on the cross: "It is finished" (John 19:30). Therefore, to "declare" His righteousness is to preach the completed, substitutionary work of Jesus Christ to every generation.
