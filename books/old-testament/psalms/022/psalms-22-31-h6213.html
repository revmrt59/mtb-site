DONE - ( asah , Strong's H6213 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb asah in Psalm 22:31 serves as the triumphant climax of a psalm that begins in deep agony and ends in absolute victory. Meaning "to do," "to make," or "to accomplish," the word in this context declares that God has decisively acted to achieve salvation. Just as Jesus's final words on the cross ("It is finished") announced the completion of His redemptive work, this Old Testament climax assures God's people that the work of deliverance is not left incomplete or uncertain, but has been sovereignly executed and finished by the Lord Himself.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew verb asah is a word of active completion. Unlike bara (which means to create out of nothing and is reserved exclusively for divine activity), asah is a broader term that emphasizes fashioning, shaping, producing, or bringing a plan to fruition. It is the language of a craftsman completing a project, a king executing a decree, or a savior performing an act of deliverance. In the ancient Near East, the greatness of a god was measured by his deeds—what he had actually "done" in history. By concluding Psalm 22 with asah, the psalmist points away from human effort and anchors the hope of future generations in the concrete, completed historical actions of Yahweh.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of asah is to do, work, make, produce, or accomplish. The verb has a massive semantic range across its more than 2,600 occurrences in the Old Testament, but when used of God, it specifically denotes His active, sovereign intervention in the world. It carries the following lexical nuances:

• To accomplish a divine decree: Bringing God's spoken promises into physical reality.

• To execute judgment or deliverance: Actively vindicating the righteous and saving those who trust in Him.

• To finish or complete a work: Bringing an undertaking to its intended, perfect end.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, asah is used to describe the tangible reality of God’s actions:

• The Creator at Work: In the Genesis creation account, God "made" (asah) the firmament, the sun, the moon, and the beasts of the earth, showcasing His orderly execution of cosmic design.

• The Promise Keeper: God does not merely speak covenants; He performs (asah) them. What His mouth promises, His hand accomplishes.

• The Sovereign Ruler: When God acts, no one can hinder Him. The word picture is one of ultimate efficacy; when God says He has done something, it stands as an unalterable reality that history must reckon with.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of asah in Psalm 22:31 is immense, especially when viewed through a Christological lens. Psalm 22 is the premier messianic psalm of crucifixion, famously quoted by Jesus on the cross ("My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?").

• The Finished Work of Redemption: The psalm transitions from the agonizing suffering of the righteous sufferer (vv. 1–21) to a global song of praise and triumph (vv. 22–31). The very last phrase of the psalm is ki asah—literally, "for He has done [it]." This serves as the Old Testament equivalent of Christ’s victory cry, Tetelestai ("It is finished") in John 19:30.

• Sovereign Grace: Salvation is entirely monergistic in this declaration. It does not say "what the people have done," but "what He has done." The basis of eternal hope for "a people who will be born" is not human performance, but the completed work of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:31, the English translation adds the word "this" ("He has done this") to smooth out the transition, but the Hebrew text ends abruptly and powerfully with the single verb asah: "that He has done." This absolute use of the verb heightens its impact. It leaves the reader with a sense of awe. What has He done? He has heard the cry of the sufferer, He has conquered the powers of death, He has extended His salvation to the ends of the earth, and He has established His righteousness forever. The context demands that we see this "doing" as a comprehensive, completed victory that secures the worship of all future generations.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:2: "And on the seventh day God ended His work which He had done, and He rested on the seventh day from all His work which He had done."

• Psalm 52:9: "I will praise You forever, Because You have done it; And in the presence of Your saints I will wait on Your name, for it is good."

• Isaiah 44:23: "Sing, O heavens, for the Lord has done it! Shout, you lower parts of the earth; Break forth into singing, you mountains, O forest, and every tree in it! For the Lord has redeemed Jacob, And glorified Himself in Israel."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek Septuagint (LXX), the Hebrew asah in Psalm 22:31 is translated using the Greek verb poieo ("He made" or "He did"). This same Greek root is used throughout the New Testament to describe the works of God. For the preacher, this word provides a powerful sermon transition: the agonizing "why" of Psalm 22:1 finds its ultimate answer in the victorious "done" of Psalm 22:31. Christian faith is not a system of "do," but a message of "done."
