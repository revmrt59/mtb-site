DELIVERED - ( palat , Strong's H6403 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb palat in Psalm 22:4 refers to a decisive, active rescue where God brings those in imminent danger into a place of safety and escape. Set within a messianic lament of deep distress, this word highlights God's historical faithfulness to those who trust in Him, emphasizing that He does not merely comfort the afflicted but actively snatches them out of the hand of the enemy.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root idea of palat is to slip out, escape, or be smooth. It carries the physical imagery of slipping through an enemy's fingers, escaping a tight trap, or being safely brought out of a confined, dangerous space. In the ancient world, this word was used to describe physical escape from battle, wild beasts, or sudden calamity. It conveys the picture of a person who is cornered with no human way out, only to be suddenly and securely swept away to safety.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In its basic Qal form, palat means to escape or slip away. In Psalm 22:4, the verb is used in the Piel (causative/intensive) stem, which shifts the meaning from escaping to actively rescuing or delivering. In this form, it means to cause to escape, to preserve, or to bring safely through danger. It denotes a swift, powerful act of rescue. While other Hebrew words for salvation focus on broad space (yasha) or pulling out of a pit (natsal), palat emphasizes the act of providing a secure path of escape from immediate peril.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb occurs approximately 25 times in the Hebrew Bible, primarily within the Psalms. It frequently appears in contexts where David is running for his life or surrounded by enemies.

• It is used of physical deliverance from violent men, where God acts as the ultimate escape route.

• The participle form of the verb, mepalet, is translated as "deliverer." When the psalmists call God their deliverer, they are picturing Him as the one who consistently opens a door of escape when they are completely trapped.

• The term is often paired with military and defensive vocabulary, such as rocks, fortresses, and shields, showing that God's deliverance leads to a place of absolute security.

4. Theological Implications

Deliverance in the Old Testament is never an abstract or purely philosophical concept; it is a concrete, historical reality.

• Covenant Faithfulness: God's deliverance is the direct result of His covenant commitment to His people. When the vulnerable cry out to Him, He is obligated by His own character to act on their behalf.

• The Response to Faith: There is an inseparable link between trust (batach) and deliverance (palat). Deliverance is not earned by human strength or wisdom, but is granted to those who utterly rely on Yahweh.

• Messianic Significance: Psalm 22 is a profoundly messianic psalm, quoted by Jesus on the cross. The historical reality of God delivering the "fathers" serves as the painful backdrop to the Messiah's apparent abandonment, which ultimately resolved in the ultimate deliverance of the resurrection.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:4, the psalmist is crying out in a state of extreme agony, feeling utterly forsaken by God. To process this crisis, the psalmist looks back at Israel's history: "Our fathers trusted in You; They trusted, and You delivered them."

• The double emphasis on trust ("trusted... trusted") highlights the absolute dependency of the ancestors.

• God's response was swift and definitive: He delivered them.

• By using palat, the psalmist is remembering how God repeatedly made a way of escape for Israel—whether from the chariots of Egypt at the Red Sea or from surrounding hostile nations.

• The contrast is agonizing: the fathers trusted and were delivered, yet the speaker is currently trusting but feels abandoned. This tension drives the emotional and theological weight of the lament, serving as a plea for God to act according to His established character.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 17:13 - "Arise, O LORD, Confront him, cast him down; Deliver my life from the wicked with Your sword,"

• Psalm 43:1 - "Vindicate me, O God, And plead my cause against an ungodly nation; Oh, deliver me from the deceitful and unjust man!"

• Psalm 71:4 - "Deliver me, O my God, out of the hand of the wicked, Out of the hand of the unrighteous and cruel man."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), the translators used the Greek verb rhyomai to translate palat in Psalm 22:4. This Greek word means to rescue, draw to oneself, or deliver from danger. It is the same word used in the New Testament in the Lord's Prayer ("deliver us from the evil one" in Matthew 6:13) and by the Apostle Paul when he refers to Jesus as "The Deliverer" who comes from Zion (Romans 11:26). This connects the Old Testament cry for physical escape with the New Testament reality of spiritual deliverance through Jesus Christ.
