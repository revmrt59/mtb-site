TRUSTED - ( batah , Strong's H982 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bataḥ conveys a deep sense of security, unreserved reliance, and bold confidence. It describes a state of mind that is secure and free from anxiety, not because of one's own self-sufficiency, but because one's safety is anchored in a completely reliable object or person. In Psalm 22:4, the word is repeated twice to emphasize the absolute, unwavering dependence of Israel’s ancestors upon Yahweh. Amid deep suffering and perceived abandonment, the psalmist recalls this historical, covenant-keeping trust as the ground for his own hope, reminding us that true biblical trust is an active, relational resting in the character of God that inevitably leads to His deliverance.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The physical root of bataḥ conveys the action of throwing oneself down face forward, or sliding down to lie flat and secure upon the ground. It pictures a person who is completely helpless on their own, yet fully relaxed and safe because they are resting on something unshakeable. Another closely related physical concept is that of clinging or leaning heavily on someone else for support. It is the posture of a child sleeping securely in a mother's arms, or a traveler resting without fear inside a well-fortified city. Over time, this physical image of safe resting transitioned into a mental and spiritual concept of absolute confidence and reliance upon God.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb bataḥ means to trust, feel secure, be confident, or have hope. In the Qal stem, it expresses the subjective feeling of safety and security that arises from objective, reliable support. It is not a shallow optimism or a blind leap of faith, but a calculated, deliberate reliance upon a trustworthy object. The lexical range includes:

• To put one's confidence in someone or something.

• To feel safe and secure, free from fear or care.

• To rely upon or depend on another for safety, provision, or victory.

In the Old Testament, when the object of this trust is Yahweh, the word denotes a secure, unshakeable state of peace. When the object of this trust is anything else—such as wealth, physical strength, idols, or foreign nations—it represents a foolish, deceptive, and ultimately destructive security.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Hebrew Scriptures, bataḥ is used to contrast false security with true covenant security.

• The Secure Dwelling: The word is frequently used in the adverbial sense of "dwelling securely" or "in safety" (belaḥ). For example, under Solomon's reign, Judah and Israel dwelt safely, each man under his vine and fig tree.

• The Shield and Refuge: Trusting in Yahweh is regularly paired with metaphors of God as a shield, a fortress, and a high tower. To trust in Him is to run into the fortress and be safe.

• The Deceptive Trust: The prophets frequently warn against trusting in Egypt, chariots, or the fortresses of Israel. This is pictured as leaning on a broken reed that pierces the hand of the one who leans on it.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, bataḥ represents the Old Testament equivalent of the New Testament concept of faith (pisteuo). It is the proper, relational response of the covenant people to Yahweh's chesed (covenant love and loyalty).

• A Relational Choice: Trust is not merely an emotion; it is a relational decision. It requires a deliberate transfer of the burden of one's security from oneself, or from worldly assets, to the living God.

• The Antidote to Fear: Bataḥ and fear are mutually exclusive. Where true trust in Yahweh exists, terror and anxiety are driven out because the believer knows that the sovereign Lord is in control.

• The Basis for Deliverance: In the Old Testament narrative, trust is always the precursor to divine deliverance. God does not deliver because of human merit, but because His people have placed their ultimate reliance upon His name and character.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage (Psalm 22:4)

Psalm 22 is a individual lament of intense suffering and messianic prophetic significance, beginning with the agonizing cry, "My God, my God, why have You forsaken me?" In verses 4 and 5, the psalmist transitions from his immediate feelings of isolation to the historical reality of Israel’s relationship with God.

In verse 4, the psalmist uses bataḥ twice: "Our fathers trusted (bāṭeḥû) in You; they trusted (bāṭāḥû), and You delivered them."

• The Power of Repetition: The repetition of the verb emphasizes the enduring, habitual, and intense nature of the ancestors' trust. It was not a sporadic reliance, but a continuous lifestyle of dependence through generations of crises (such as the Exodus and the era of the Judges).

• The Argument of Faith: The psalmist uses this historical trust as an appeal to God's character. Because God is immutable, and because He faithfully delivered the fathers who trusted in Him, the suffering psalmist (and ultimately Christ on the cross) has a legitimate claim on God's deliverance.

• The Contrast of Circumstance: While the psalmist feels poured out like water and surrounded by enemies, he anchors his hope not in his present feelings, but in the historic, proven reliability of God as the object of His people's trust.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 56:3: "Whenever I am afraid, I will trust in You."

• Proverbs 3:5: "Trust in the LORD with all your heart, And lean not on your own understanding;"

• Isaiah 26:3: "You will keep him in perfect peace, Whose mind is stayed on You, Because he trusts in You."

• Psalm 115:9: "O Israel, trust in the LORD; He is their help and their shield."

• Psalm 37:3: "Trust in the LORD, and do good; Dwell in the land, and feed on His faithfulness."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The noun form beṭaḥ is often used as an adverb meaning "securely" or "in safety." This highlights that the lifestyle of trust produces a tangible environment of peace and safety.

• In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), bataḥ is most frequently translated by elpizo (to hope) or peitho (to persuade/have confidence). This demonstrates that Old Testament trust is forward-looking and expectant; it is a confidence that God will act on behalf of those who rely on Him.
