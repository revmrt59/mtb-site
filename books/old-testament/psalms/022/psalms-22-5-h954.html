ASHAMED - ( bowsh , Strong's H954 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bowsh captures the profound psychological and spiritual experience of being put to shame, confounded, or disappointed. In Psalm 22:5, it describes the ultimate vindication of those who trust in God, contrasting the bitter humiliation of unanswered hope with the steadfast reliability of the Lord. To be bowsh is to experience the sinking realization that one's confidence was misplaced—an outcome God's faithful people ultimately escape.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

- The root idea behind bowsh is often associated with turning pale or feeling a sudden flush of humiliation, reflecting a physical reaction to dashed expectations or exposed failure.

- In the ancient Near Eastern honor-shame culture, to be "ashamed" was not merely an internal feeling of embarrassment; it was a public standing of defeat, abandonment, and ruined trust.

- When applied to faith, it pictures the posture of a petitioner waiting anxiously for a benefactor who either cannot or will not arrive, leaving the requester exposed to mocking enemies.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

- Bowsh primarily means to be ashamed, confounded, disappointed, or put to shame.

- It denotes both the emotional state of humiliation and the objective reality of being let down by someone or something in which trust was placed.

- The term frequently carries a relational dimension: it is the bitter disappointment experienced when a person's loyal, dependent cry to God or an ally goes unmet—though Scripture consistently affirms that true trust in Yahweh never results in this outcome.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

- The word is often paired with concepts of waiting, hoping, and seeking, creating a contrast between confident expectation and sudden ruin.

- In prophetic literature, it describes the quiet humiliation of idols that cannot save their worshippers, contrasted with the enduring salvation of God.

- In lament psalms, the plea "let me not be ashamed" is an appeal to God's character, asking Him to act so that the petitioner's faith is publicly vindicated rather than mocked.

4. Theological Implications

- Faith is inherently vulnerable; it exposes the believer to the gaze of a skeptical world that watches to see if God will act.

- To be unashamed in Scripture is proof of God's unwavering faithfulness. God guards His reputation by rescuing those who lean their full weight upon Him.

- The ultimate theological resolution of bowsh is found in the New Testament concept of hope that "does not disappoint" (Romans 5:5) because the object of our trust—God in Christ—never fails.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

- In Psalm 22:5, David looks back at the historical pattern of God's people: "They cried to You, and were delivered; they trusted in You, and were not ashamed."

- Even amidst the agonizing physical and emotional suffering described earlier in the psalm, this verse anchors the sufferer in the historical reliability of God.

- The context emphasizes that generations of ancestors placed their lives in God's hands during crises, and God never allowed them to experience the devastating public humiliation of unanswered faith.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

- Psalm 25:2 - "O my God, I trust in You; Let me not be ashamed; Let not my enemies triumph over me."

- Isaiah 49:23 - "Kings shall be your foster fathers, and their queens your nursing mothers; they shall bow down to you with their faces to the earth, and lick up the dust of your feet. Then you will know that I am the Lord, for they shall not be ashamed who wait for Me."

- Joel 2:26 - "You shall eat in plenty and be satisfied, and praise the name of the Lord your God, who has dealt wondrously with you; and My people shall never be ashamed."

- Romans 9:33 - As it is written: "Behold, I lay in Zion a stumbling stone and rock of offense, and whoever believes on Him will not be put to shame."

7. Other Relevant Insights

- The Septuagint (LXX) generally translates bowsh using words related to shame (kataischunō) or confusion, which the New Testament authors later adopt when applying Old Testament prophecies of vindication to faith in Jesus Christ.

- The word forms a crucial bridge in biblical anthropology, showing that human trust is never neutral; it either terminates on fragile things that inevitably lead to bowsh, or on the Creator, who secures ultimate honor and salvation.
