REPROACH - ( cherpah , Strong's H2781 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun cherpah denotes a state of disgrace, shame, insult, or social mockery that strips a person of their honor and standing in the community. In Psalm 22:6, it captures the intense social alienation and public humiliation experienced by the suffering Righteous One, who is treated as an object of scorn rather than a human being worthy of dignity. This term underscores both the depth of Christ's substitutionary suffering on the cross and the painful isolation of those who bear reproach for God's sake.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, honor and shame were the primary cultural currencies. Honor was equivalent to life and social existence, while shame or reproach was a social death. Cherpah comes from the root verb charaph, which means to taunt, defy, or pluck. Linguistically, it carries the imagery of winter or the stripping of a harvest. This word picture represents someone who has been stripped bare of their protective standing, reputation, and dignity, leaving them exposed to the biting cold of public ridicule and hostile exposure.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Cherpah refers to the status of being disgraced, the object of taunting, or the verbal insults themselves. The lexical range includes:

• An object of scorn, mockery, or derision.

• A state of shame, disgrace, or ignominy.

• A condition of vulnerability and loss of social status (such as barrenness, widowhood, or defeat in battle).

• Taunts and blasphemies directed against a person or against God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, cherpah is used to describe various conditions of profound vulnerability:

• The Shame of Barrenness: Rachel refers to her childlessness as a reproach that God finally removed (Genesis 30:23).

• Defeat and Subjugation: Nations defeated in battle or sent into exile became a "reproach" to their neighbors, symbolizing God's judgment and abandonment (Joel 2:19).

• Physical Vulnerability: Uncircumcision was viewed as a physical and spiritual reproach among the covenant people (Genesis 34:14).

• The Target of Insults: The word often visualizes a target at which enemies throw mud, spit, or wag their heads, turning the victim into a public warning sign.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, bearing reproach is frequently the cost of faithfulness to Yahweh. The righteous suffer cherpah not because of their own wrongdoing, but because of their allegiance to God. The insults aimed at God's servants are ultimately insults directed at God Himself.

In a Messianic context, cherpah shines a spotlight on the substitutionary nature of the Messiah's suffering. True humanity includes dignity and communal honor; by entering into the absolute depth of cherpah, the Messiah allowed Himself to be stripped of His human dignity to redeem humanity from the ultimate, eternal shame of sin.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:6, the speaker cries out, "But I am a worm, and no man; A reproach of men, and despised by the people."

The context is a stark contrast between the covenant fathers who cried out to God and were delivered (vv. 4–5) and the current state of the sufferer. The sufferer is so reduced by the mocking of his enemies that he feels subhuman ("a worm, and no man").

The phrase "a reproach of men" (cherpat adam) means he has become the very embodiment of disgrace in the eyes of humanity. He is not merely suffering physically; he is suffering socially and relationally. The community has rejected him, treating him as an outcast. This found its literal fulfillment at the cross, where Jesus was mocked by the Roman soldiers, the Jewish leaders, and passersby, bearing the full weight of human scorn.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 30:23 - "And she conceived and bore a son, and said, 'God has taken away my reproach.'"

• Psalm 69:7 - "Because for Your sake I have borne reproach; Shame has covered my face."

• Psalm 69:20 - "Reproach has broken my heart, And I am full of heaviness; I looked for some to take pity, but there was none; And for comforters, but I found none."

• Proverbs 14:34 - "Righteousness exalts a nation, But sin is a reproach to any people."

• Joel 2:19 - "The Lord will answer and say to His people... 'I will no longer make you a reproach among the nations.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (LXX), cherpah is translated by the Greek word oneidismos. This connects Psalm 22:6 directly to the New Testament theology of suffering. In Romans 15:3, Paul quotes Psalm 69:9 to describe Christ: "The reproaches of those who reproached You fell on Me." Furthermore, Hebrews 13:13 calls believers to "go forth to Him outside the camp, bearing His reproach (oneidismos)." To bear Christ's reproach is to share in His rejection by a world that refuses to honor God.
