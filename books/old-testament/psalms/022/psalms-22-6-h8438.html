WORM - ( tola'at , Strong's H8438 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word tola'at refers to a specific type of worm, the female crimson grub (Coccus ilicis), which was crushed in antiquity to extract a brilliant red dye used for royal garments and tabernacle furnishings. In Psalm 22:6, the Messiah uses this term to describe His utter humiliation, lowliness, and crushing on the cross, representing both His absolute self-emptying and the shedding of His crimson blood to cover human sin.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The tola'at is the Coccus ilicis (or kermes), a scale insect common to the Middle East. The life cycle of this insect provides a vivid picture of sacrifice. When the female is ready to lay her eggs, she climbs the trunk of a tree—typically the holm oak—and attaches her body permanently to the wood. She forms a hard, protective red shell over her eggs. In the process of birthing her offspring, her body is crushed, releasing a brilliant crimson fluid that stains both the wood of the tree and her emerging young. The mother dies in this act of preservation, leaving her offspring marked by her crimson life-fluid. The dried bodies of these female grubs were gathered and ground into a powder to produce the highly prized "scarlet" or "crimson" dye of the ancient world.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term tola'at (also appearing as tola' or tolah) refers to a worm, grub, or maggot. By extension, the word denotes the color scarlet, crimson, or purple dye derived from this insect, as well as fabrics dyed with this pigment. The term represents extreme lowliness, insignificance, and physical vulnerability when used metaphorically of human beings, but it carries royal, priestly, and sacrificial associations when used in the context of textiles and offerings.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament utilizes tola'at in two primary ways:

• As an Object of Decay and Lowliness: It describes the worms that consumed the spoiled manna in the wilderness, the maggots that feed on the dead in Sheol, and the state of human insignificance. A worm is a creature of the dust, easily trampled, completely defenseless, and associated with mortality and corruption.

• As a Source of Royal Color: It describes the "scarlet" thread and fabrics used extensively in the Tabernacle curtains, the garments of the High Priest, and the cleansing rituals for leprosy. In this sense, the tola'at is the source of the color of majesty and atonement.

4. Theological Implications

The dual nature of the tola'at—representing both the lowest state of a trampled creature and the source of royal crimson dye—finds its ultimate fulfillment in the person and work of Jesus Christ:

• The Humiliation of Christ: In His incarnation and crucifixion, Christ emptied Himself of His glory. On the cross, He was treated not as a man with rights and dignity, but as a worm—despised, mocked, and crushed under the weight of human sin and divine judgment.

• The Crimson Blood of Atonement: Just as the female crimson grub attaches herself to the wood of a tree and dies to give life to her offspring, staining them with her red fluid, Jesus willingly gave His life on the wooden cross. His shed blood covers His people, cleansing them and granting them spiritual life.

• The Royal Covering: The very dye that clothed kings and adorned the Tabernacle was birthed from the crushing of this humble worm. Christ's crushing on the cross provides the royal robe of righteousness that covers the believer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:6, the speaker cries out, "But I am a worm, and no man; A reproach of men, and despised by the people." Psalm 22 is a deeply messianic lament, directly quoted by Jesus on the cross. The context is one of intense physical agony, social ostracization, and spiritual abandonment. By choosing the word tola'at instead of the standard Hebrew word for a common earthworm (rimmah), the text highlights not only the Savior's extreme abasement—being stepped on, ignored, and deemed worthless—but also points prophetically to the crimson sacrifice He was accomplishing. He was being crushed like the scale insect so that His crimson blood could purchase redemption.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 26:1 - "Moreover you shall make the tabernacle with ten curtains of fine woven linen, and blue, purple, and scarlet thread; with artistic designs of cherubim you shall weave them." (Here, tola'at is translated as "scarlet," denoting the sacred color used in the Tabernacle).

• Isaiah 1:18 - "“Come now, and let us reason together,” Says the LORD, “Though your sins are like scarlet, They shall be as white as snow; Though they are red like crimson, They shall be as wool." (The word "crimson" is tola'at, representing the deep, indelible stain of sin that only divine grace can cleanse).

• Isaiah 14:11 - "Your pomp is brought down to Sheol, And the noise of your stringed instruments; The maggot is spread under you, And worms cover you." (Here, tola'at is translated as "worms," illustrating the ultimate decay and humiliation of earthly pride).

• Isaiah 41:14 - "“Fear not, you worm Jacob, You men of Israel! I will help you,” says the LORD and your Redeemer, the Holy One of Israel." (The word "worm" is tola'at, depicting Israel's weakness, helplessness, and total dependence on God for deliverance).

7. Other Relevant Insights

After the female Coccus ilicis dies and releases her crimson fluid, her remaining body undergoes a physical transformation over the subsequent three days. It dries out, loses its red color, and turns into a white, waxy substance resembling a flake of snow that falls to the ground. This biological cycle mirrors the red-to-white transformation promised in Isaiah 1:18, where the crimson stain of sin is washed white as snow through the sacrificial death and subsequent resurrection of Christ on the third day.
