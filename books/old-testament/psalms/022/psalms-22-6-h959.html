DESPISED - ( bazah , Strong's H959 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bazah conveys the action of holding someone or something in low esteem, viewing them with utter contempt, or treating them as completely worthless. In Psalm 22:6, this term describes the Messiah's profound experience of social rejection and humiliation. Rather than being treated with the dignity due to a human being made in the image of God, He is looked down upon by humanity, deemed insignificant, and treated as though He has zero value.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The literal root of bazah relates to the act of looking down on something or someone and deciding it has no weight, value, or importance. In Hebrew thought, honor (kabad) is heavy and weighty, whereas to despise (bazah) is to treat someone as light, trivial, or worthless. It is the mental and emotional act of stripping a person of their worth and treating them as refuse or an object of mockery.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb bazah means to despise, disdain, hold in contempt, or regard with insolence.

• In the active stems, it denotes the conscious decision to value something far below its true worth, or to treat holy things with irreverence.

• In the passive participle form (as seen in Psalm 22:6, bazuy), it describes the state of being despised, deemed contemptible, or rendered highly unpopular and humiliated in the eyes of others.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs bazah in several vivid contexts to show how contempt manifests:

• Despising Sacred Things: Esau despised his birthright (Genesis 25:34), treating a covenantal, spiritual blessing as if it were worth no more than a single bowl of red stew.

• Despising Authority: Michal despised King David in her heart (2 Samuel 6:16), looking out the window and feeling deep disdain for his joyful, uninhibited worship of God.

• Despising God's Name: The corrupt priests in Malachi's day despised the altar of God by offering blind, lame, and sick animal sacrifices, treating the holy table of Yahweh as a cheap, meaningless ritual (Malachi 1:6).

4. Theological Implications

The presence of bazah in Psalm 22:6 points directly to the suffering Messiah. It reveals that the crucifixion was not merely a physical execution, but a systematic campaign of public humiliation and psychological degradation.

• Humanity looked at the Creator of the universe hanging on a tree and assigned Him a value of zero.

• This directly connects to the suffering servant prophecy in Isaiah 53:3, which uses the same root word twice (nibhzeh) to state that "He is despised and rejected by men... He was despised, and we did not esteem Him."

• The theological tension is profound: the one whom God highly values and will ultimately exalt is the very one whom humanity deems utterly worthless.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:6, the speaker laments, "But I am a worm, and no man; A reproach of men, and despised by the people."

• The structure of the verse shows a downward spiral of humiliation. The speaker is reduced to a "worm" (insignificant, helpless, and easily crushed underfoot), becomes a "reproach" (an object of public mockery and shame), and is ultimately "despised by the people" (am).

• The phrase "by the people" indicates that this was not the isolated opinion of a few enemies, but a widespread, collective agreement among the crowd. They looked upon Him, judged His state of suffering as proof of divine abandonment, and dismissed Him with contemptuous disdain.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 25:34: "And Jacob gave Esau bread and stew of lentils; then he ate and drank, arose, and went his way. Thus Esau despised his birthright."

• 2 Samuel 6:16: "Now as the ark of the LORD came into the City of David, Michal, Saul’s daughter, looked through a window and saw King David leaping and whirling before the LORD; and she despised him in her heart."

• Isaiah 53:3: "He is despised and rejected by men, A Man of sorrows and acquainted with grief. And we hid, as it were, our faces from Him; He was despised, and we did not esteem Him."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), bazah in Psalm 22:6 is translated using a form of exoutheneo, which means to count as absolutely nothing, to treat with contempt, or to mock. This is the exact word used in the New Testament when Herod and his soldiers mock Jesus, treating Him as a nobody (Luke 23:11). The ultimate King of Kings submitted Himself to the worst human assessment so that those who believe in Him would never have to face the ultimate, eternal rejection of God.
