SHAKE - ( nua' , Strong's H5128 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nua' means to quiver, totter, stagger, or shake. In Psalm 22:7, used in the causative Hiphil stem, it refers to the physical shaking or wagging of the head. Far from a harmless gesture of disagreement, this shaking of the head is a culturally potent expression of deep contempt, mocking derision, and triumphant self-righteousness. It visually communicates that the observer regards the sufferer as utterly ruined, defeated, and abandoned by God. This precise physical gesture serves as a vivid prophetic detail directly fulfilled during the crucifixion of Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root nua' describes unsteady, waving, tottering, or unstable movement. In the physical world, it is used to depict a reed waving back and forth in the wind, a drunkard staggering down a street, or the trees of a forest shaking during a violent storm. It conveys a picture of instability, weakness, and a loss of control or footing.

When this verb is applied to human gestures—specifically the shaking or wagging of the head (rosh)—it represents a deliberate, mocking imitation of this instability. The mockers shake their heads at the victim to signal that the victim's life has become completely unstable, collapsed, and ruined. It is a somatic (bodily) display of rejection, acting out the belief that the victim has fallen past the point of recovery.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In its basic Qal form, nua' means to shake, tremble, stagger, wander, or reel. It describes:

• the physical trembling of doorposts or foundations.

• the staggering of a drunken person who cannot keep their balance.

• the aimless wandering of fugitives or landless people.

In the Hiphil (causative) form, which is used in Psalm 22:7, the verb means to cause to stagger, to disturb, to wag, or to shake. When combined with "the head" (rosh), it specifically means to wag or shake the head in a gesture of scorn, insult, and malicious satisfaction. The semantic range of the word emphasizes the transition from physical instability to a physical gesture representing the perceived moral and spiritual collapse of another person.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs nua' in several distinct ways to build its theological and descriptive imagery:

• Physical Agitation and Trembling: The word describes the trees of the forest shaking in the wind (Isaiah 7:2) or the earth reeling to and fro like a drunkard under the weight of divine judgment (Isaiah 24:20).

• Aimless Wandering: It describes the punishment of wandering without a home, such as the Israelites wandering in the wilderness (Numbers 32:13) or the wicked wandering in search of food (Psalm 59:15).

• Gestures of Mockery: Most significantly, shaking the head is paired with other physical gestures of disdain, such as hissing, clapping hands, and gaping mouths. It is the ultimate non-verbal expression of gloating over someone else's misfortune, indicating that the victim is getting what they deserve.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of nua' in Psalm 22:7 lies in its messianic fulfillment and its revelation of human depravity.

• Prophetic Precision: Psalm 22 is the preeminent crucifixion psalm. The shaking of the head predicted in verse 7 was fulfilled down to the exact physical gesture at Golgotha. The religious leaders and passersby did not merely reject Jesus intellectually; they mocked Him bodily.

• The Anatomy of Contempt: The passage reveals how sin enlists the entire body in rebellion against the righteous. The mockers use their eyes to gaze, their lips to shoot out insults, and their heads to shake in self-satisfied judgment.

• The Suffering of the Innocent: The shaking of the head implies that the observers have already pronounced a final verdict of guilt and divine abandonment upon the sufferer. They assume his physical suffering is proof of his spiritual rejection by God, which is the very heart of Christ's substitutionary suffering—bearing the curse of abandonment though completely innocent.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:7, the sufferer laments, "All those who see Me ridicule Me; They shoot out the lip, they shake the head..." The immediate context is one of extreme social isolation and public humiliation. The sufferer is treated not as a man, but as a worm (v. 6).

The gesture of "shaking the head" works in tandem with "shooting out the lip" (making a grimace or sneer). Together, these actions form a silent, devastating chorus of condemnation. By shaking their heads, the mockers are saying, "Look at him. He claimed to trust in Yahweh; let Yahweh deliver him" (v. 8). The head-shake is the physical exclamation point on their theological mockery, expressing a smug assurance that the sufferer's faith was a delusion and that his ruin is absolute.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) translates the Hebrew nua' in Psalm 22:7 (21:8 in the LXX numbering) using the Greek verb kineo ("they moved/shook their heads").

This linguistic connection is critical because the Gospel writers use the exact same Greek root in their eyewitness accounts of the crucifixion. Matthew writes that those who passed by blasphemed Him, "wagging (kinountes) their heads" (Matthew 27:39), and Mark records the exact same detail (Mark 15:29). The Holy Spirit preserved this specific physical link across languages to ensure that readers of the Gospels would immediately connect the mockery at the cross to the prophetic portrait painted by David in Psalm 22.
