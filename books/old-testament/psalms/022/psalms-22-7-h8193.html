LIP - ( śāpâ , Strong's H8193 )

Summary:

In Psalm 22:7, the Hebrew word śāpâ refers to the physical lip, but functions here as a vivid physical idiom for mockery, contempt, and bitter derision. Used in this messianic context, it depicts the visceral, outward manifestation of human hostility directed against the suffering Savior on the cross. It captures the reality that human rebellion against God is not merely an internal attitude, but a visible, vocalized rejection that distorts even the human face in scorn.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun śāpâ is derived from an unused root meaning to scrape, shave, or sweep away, which relates to the idea of a natural boundary, edge, or lip. In the ancient world, the "lip" represented the physical organ of speech and the border of the mouth, but it was also used to describe geographic boundaries, such as the "lip" (shore) of a sea or the "lip" (bank) of a river.

In Hebrew thought, the physical parts of the body are closely tied to the inner life of the soul. The lips are not just biological features; they are the gatekeepers of the heart. What fills the heart overflows through the lips. Consequently, physical gestures involving the lips—such as compressing them, biting them, or "shooting them out"—were deeply understood expressions of a person's moral and spiritual state.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term śāpâ has three primary categories of meaning in classical Hebrew:

• The physical lip: The anatomical lips of a human being, which can be kissed, compressed, or distorted in facial expressions.

• Speech, language, or talk: By metonymy, the lip represents the words spoken or the specific language/dialect of a people group (e.g., the "one lip" of Babel).

• An edge, border, or margin: The physical boundary of a body of water, a vessel, or a garment.

In the specific context of Psalm 22:7, śāpâ refers to the physical lip used in a specific cultural gesture of extreme contempt, translating to "opening wide the mouth" or "pouting/shooting out the lip" to mock someone.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, śāpâ is used to paint vivid pictures of human communication and heart conditions:

• The Lip of Deceit: "Lying lips" (śǝpat-šeqer) are frequently contrasted with "truthful lips" in the Wisdom literature. The lip becomes the weapon of the flatterer and the deceitful.

• The Lip of Boundary: The "lip of the sea" describes the seashore, depicting God's sovereignty in setting boundaries that the waters cannot cross.

• The Lip of Mockery: In Psalm 22:7, the enemies "shoot out the lip." This Hebrew idiom (yapṭîrû bəśāpâ) refers to parting the lips, grimacing, spitting, or thrusting the lip forward to express utter disdain, mimicking a gesture still common in many cultures today to signify disgust and rejection.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of śāpâ in Psalm 22:7 is deeply tied to the suffering of the Messiah. Psalm 22 is the premier Messianic psalm of the crucifixion. When Jesus hung on the cross, His physical agony was compounded by intense psychological and spiritual humiliation.

The mockers used their lips—organs created by God to offer Him praise—to physically sneer at and reject the incarnate Son of God. This highlights the tragedy of the Fall: human beings take the very instruments of communication given by the Creator and weaponize them against Him. Furthermore, this gesture shows the physical manifestation of sin; the inward hatred of the scribes, soldiers, and passersby could not be contained, bursting forth in distorted facial expressions and verbal abuse.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 22:7, the psalmist cries out: "All those who see Me ridicule Me; They shoot out the lip, they shake the head..."

• The parallelism of "shoot out the lip" and "shake the head" portrays a synchronized, theatrical display of public shaming.

• The verb accompanying śāpâ is pāṭar, which means to let loose, open wide, or set free. Literally, they "open wide with the lip." It is an unbridled, unashamed release of venomous mockery.

• The nuance is one of absolute dismissal. They do not merely disagree with the Sufferer; they treat Him as something worthless, subhuman, and completely forsaken by God. This was perfectly fulfilled when the onlookers at Golgotha wagged their heads and mocked Christ's trust in His Father (Matthew 27:39-43).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Proverbs 10:19 - "In the multitude of words sin is not lacking, But he who restrains his lips is wise."

• Proverbs 12:19 - "The truthful lip shall be established forever, But a lying tongue is but for a moment."

• Isaiah 6:5 - "So I said: 'Woe is me, for I am undone! Because I am a man of unclean lips, And I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips; For my eyes have seen the King, The LORD of hosts.'"

• Isaiah 29:13 - "Therefore the Lord said: 'Inasmuch as these people draw near with their mouths And honor Me with their lips, But have removed their hearts far from Me...'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), śāpâ in Psalm 22:7 is translated using the Greek word cheilos (lip). This Greek term is the same one used in the New Testament to describe the crowds mocking Jesus, as well as the term used in Hebrews 13:15, which exhorts believers to offer "the sacrifice of praise to God, that is, the fruit of our lips, giving thanks to His name." The very lips that once mocked Him at the cross are, through redemption, transformed to offer Him eternal praise.
