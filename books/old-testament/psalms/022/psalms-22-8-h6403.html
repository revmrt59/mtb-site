RESCUE - ( malat , Strong's H6403 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb malat fundamentally means to slip away, escape, save, or deliver. In Psalm 22:8, it captures the scornful mockery hurled at the suffering Messiah as His enemies demand that the Lord—if He truly delights in Him—step in and rescue Him. Rather than merely implying physical removal from danger, malat in this context highlights the urgent cry for divine intervention and deliverance from overwhelming destruction, a motif ultimately fulfilled at the cross.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

- The root verb malat carries the physical imagery of slipping away from a tight spot, escaping to safety, or being set free from a trap.

- In ancient usage, it often described a fugitive or prey successfully evading a hunter or a pursuing enemy by slipping through their grasp.

- In theocratic Israel, this physical imagery was elevated to describe the ultimate refuge found in God, where the helpless are snatched away from the jaws of death or the hands of the wicked.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

- Malat functions primarily as a verb denoting deliverance, rescue, salvation, and escaping.

- Lexical definitions highlight two major active stems used in the Hebrew Bible: the Niphal (to escape, slip away) and the Piel (to cause to escape, deliver, save, rescue).

- In Psalm 22:8, the form is a Piel imperative, functioning as a direct, urgent demand or sarcastic taunt: "Let Him deliver [rescue] Him!"

3. Usage and Word Pictures

- The word is frequently used in military and hunting contexts, describing soldiers escaping battle or animals escaping a hunter's snare.

- When applied to God as the rescuer, the word paints a vivid picture of the Almighty reaching down to snatch His people out of imminent, inescapable peril.

- It emphasizes the suddenness and completeness of the deliverance—moving instantly from a place of deadly constraint into broad, open safety.

4. Theological Implications

- The use of malat in Psalm 22:8 highlights the painful paradox of the cross: the righteous sufferer trusts completely in God, yet the mockers use that very trust as proof of His abandonment.

- It underscores the biblical tension between human helplessness and divine rescue; true deliverance cannot be manufactured by human effort (malat), but must be wrought by God alone.

- Prophetically, the taunt serves to validate Jesus' identity, as the religious leaders unknowingly echoed the exact scriptural blueprint of Messiah's rejection (Matthew 27:43).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

- In Psalm 22:8, the enemies mock the psalmist's absolute dependence on Yahweh: "He trusted in the Lord, let Him rescue Him; let Him deliver Him, since He delights in Him!"

- The nuance here is one of bitter irony and cruel skepticism; the tormentors weaponize the covenant language of divine pleasure and rescue against the suffering righteous man.

- The verse reveals the psychological agony of the sufferer, whose faith in God's historical pattern of rescuing His people is thrown back in his face while he undergoes intense suffering.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

- Genesis 19:19 - "Indeed now, your servant has found grace in your sight, and you have shown your mercy which you have shown me by saving my life; but I cannot escape to the mountains, lest some evil overtake me and I die."

- 1 Samuel 19:11 - "The Saul also sent messengers to David's house to watch him and to kill him in the morning. And Michal, David's wife, told him, saying, 'If you do not save your life tonight, tomorrow you will be killed.'"

- Job 22:30 - "He will even deliver one who is not innocent; yes, he will be delivered by the purity of your hands."

- Psalm 32:7 - "You are my hiding place; You shall preserve me from trouble; You shall surround me with songs of deliverance."

7. Other Relevant Insights

- The Septuagint (LXX) often translates malat with terms related to saving (sozo) or pulling out, which the New Testament authors pick up when describing salvation through Christ.

- While the mockers in Psalm 22:8 demanded a physical rescue that would bypass the cross, God's ultimate answer to the Messiah's cry was not rescue from death, but resurrection through death.
