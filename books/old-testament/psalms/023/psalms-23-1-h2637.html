WANT - ( chasar , Strong's H2637 )

Summary:

In the context of the beloved Shepherd Psalm, the Hebrew verb chasar does not refer to a lack of emotional desire or unmet worldly wishes, but rather to a state of deficiency, deprivation, or running dry. To say "I shall not want" is a bold declaration of absolute covenant security: under the care of Yahweh, the believer will never lack the essential provisions, protection, and guidance necessary for their physical and spiritual life. It is the quiet confidence that the Shepherd’s resources are inexhaustible and His care is unfailing.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete imagery behind chasar is that of a vessel slowly draining of its contents, a well running dry, or a supply of food steadily diminishing until nothing is left. It represents a state of emptying out, falling short, or being left destitute. In the ancient Near East, an arid and rugged environment, "wanting" was a constant, life-threatening danger. For a flock of sheep, a lack of pasture or water meant certain death. A sheep left to itself would inevitably find itself in a state of chasar—diminishing in strength, lacking food, and exposed to predators. Therefore, the word picture behind "I shall not want" is that of a sheep resting in a pasture where the supply of food, water, and safety is continuously replenished by a vigilant, wealthy owner.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, the verb chasar means to decrease, to diminish, to be lacking, or to fail. In the Qal stem, which is used in Psalm 23:1, it expresses the state of lacking something or being in need. Combined with the absolute negative particle lo ("not"), the phrase lo echsar translates literally as "I will not lack" or "I will not suffer deficiency." It is an ongoing, active state of having all essential needs fully met. The word does not imply that the believer will have every self-indulgent desire satisfied, but rather that they will never fall short of the Shepherd's perfect, sovereign provision.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Decreasing of Waters: The word is used in Genesis 8:3 to describe the floodwaters "decreasing" or receding, showing a physical reduction in volume.

• The Miracle of Sustenance: During the wilderness wanderings, God promised that Israel's clothing would not wear out and they would "lack nothing" (Deuteronomy 2:7).

• The Depleted Jar: It describes the widow of Zarephath's bin of flour and jar of oil that did not "run dry" or "fail" during the famine (1 Kings 17:14).

• Spiritual and Moral Deficiencies: In a metaphorical sense, the word is also used to describe someone who "lacks" wisdom, understanding, or moral sense (Proverbs 10:21).

4. Theological Implications

• The Sufficiency of Yahweh: The statement "I shall not want" is less about the sheep's circumstances and more about the Shepherd's character. Because Yahweh is the self-existent, all-sufficient Creator, those who belong to Him are tethered to His infinite resources.

• The Antidote to Discontentment: Human nature is plagued by a chronic sense of lack, always craving more. Chasar reminds the believer that true contentment is not found in the accumulation of things, but in the presence of the Shepherd.

• Covenant Trust: By declaring "I shall not want," David exercises covenant trust. God has bound Himself to His flock by oath; therefore, failing to provide for His sheep would be a compromise of His own holy name.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 23:1, "I shall not want" is the immediate, logical consequence of the clause "The LORD is my shepherd." The sheep does not need to worry about the future, the weather, the predators, or the path, because the Shepherd has already assumed full responsibility for those details.

• Because He is the Shepherd, I shall not lack rest (He makes me to lie down in green pastures).

• I shall not lack peace (He leads me beside the still waters).

• I shall not lack restoration (He restores my soul).

• I shall not lack guidance (He leads me in the paths of righteousness).

• I shall not lack courage (I will fear no evil).

• I shall not lack comfort (Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me).

• The placement of this verb at the very beginning of the Psalm establishes the thematic foundation for every verse that follows.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 2:7 - "These forty years the LORD your God has been with you; you have lacked nothing."

• Psalm 34:10 - "The young lions lack and suffer hunger; but those who seek the LORD shall not lack any good thing."

• Proverbs 13:25 - "The righteous eats to the satisfying of his soul, but the stomach of the wicked shall be in want."

• Ecclesiastes 9:8 - "Let your garments be always white, and let your head lack no oil."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), chasar in Psalm 23:1 is translated using the Greek verb hystereo, which means to fall short, to be in need, or to be left behind. This is the same verb used in the New Testament when the prodigal son spent all he had and "began to be in want" (Luke 15:14), and in the famous theological declaration that all have sinned and "fall short" of the glory of God (Romans 3:23). While the world outside of God's fold is in a constant state of spiritual insolvency and "falling short," the believer in Christ is brought into a state of spiritual abundance where nothing essential is missing.
