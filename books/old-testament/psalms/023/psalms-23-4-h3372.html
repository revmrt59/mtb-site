FEAR - ( yare , Strong's H3372 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, the Hebrew verb yare represents a broad spectrum of emotional and spiritual responses, ranging from raw, paralyzing terror to profound, worshipful reverence. In the context of Psalm 23:4, where the psalmist walks through "the valley of the shadow of death," the word expresses the dread of harm, calamity, or destruction. By declaring "I will fear no evil," David is not denying the presence of genuine danger, but is declaring a conscious, faith-filled refusal to let terror master his soul. This freedom from fear is not rooted in self-confidence, but in the comforting, protective presence of the Shepherd.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root yare fundamentally relates to the physical and emotional sensation of fear.

• In ancient nomadic and pastoral life, fear was a constant, protective mechanism against wild beasts, sudden flash floods, enemy raiders, and treacherous mountain passes.

• In a sheep’s life, a dark, narrow ravine (a "valley of deep shadow") is a place of vulnerability where predators hide and footing is treacherous. The natural, instinctual response of a sheep in this environment is panic and terror (yare).

• By shifting the imagery from a vulnerable sheep to a trusting believer, the word picture shows that the presence of the Shepherd transforms a place of natural dread into a place of secure passage.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• In the Qal stem (the basic verbal form used in Psalm 23:4), yare means to be afraid, to fear, to dread, or to stand in awe.

• When the object of the verb is a threat, enemy, or calamity, it denotes the emotional state of fear or anxiety.

• When the object of the verb is God, it denotes a holy, reverent awe that leads to obedience and worship.

• In Psalm 23:4, the grammatical form is ’iyra (first-person singular imperfect), which functions as a statement of resolve: "I will not continually fear" or "I will not succumb to dread." The object of this fear is "evil" (ra'), referring to harm, disaster, or affliction.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physiological Panic: The word describes the physical sensation of terror that causes shaking, weakness, or fleeing (such as the Israelites fearing the Egyptians at the Red Sea).

• Relational Distance: It describes the dread of punishment or exposure, such as Adam hiding from God in the garden because he was "afraid."

• Covenantal Reverence: It is frequently used to describe the "fear of the LORD," which is not a terror of destruction, but a healthy, life-giving reverence that serves as the beginning of wisdom and the foundation of covenant faithfulness.

4. Theological Implications

• The command or resolve "do not fear" is one of the most frequent exhortations in Scripture. It implies that fear is a natural human reaction to a broken world, but one that can be overcome through faith.

• Scripture does not teach the absence of dangerous circumstances, but the presence of a sovereign God within those circumstances.

• The antidote to the debilitating fear (yare) of earthly circumstances is a deeper, covenantal fear (reverence and trust) of Yahweh. When God is the supreme object of a believer's awe, all earthly threats shrink to their proper perspective.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• Psalm 23 undergoes a critical grammatical shift in verse 4. In verses 1–3, David speaks about God in the third person ("He makes me," "He leads me," "He restores"). In verse 4, as the scene shifts into the dark, terrifying valley, David speaks to God in the second person ("for You are with me").

• The presence of the Shepherd changes the subjective experience of the objective danger. The "valley of the shadow of death" and the "evil" are real, but the emotional grip of terror (yare) is broken because the Shepherd is physically near with His rod and staff to defend and guide.

• The resolve "I will fear no evil" is a theological choice. David chooses to look at the Shepherd rather than the shadows.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 15:1: "After these things the word of the Lord came to Abram in a vision, saying, 'Do not be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward.'"

• Psalm 3:6: "I will not be afraid of ten thousands of people who have set themselves against me all around."

• Psalm 27:1: "The Lord is my light and my salvation; Whom shall I fear? The Lord is the strength of my life; Of whom shall I be afraid?"

• Psalm 112:7: "He will not be afraid of evil tidings; His heart is steadfast, trusting in the Lord."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The imperfect tense of yare in Psalm 23:4 suggests an ongoing state of mind. David is not merely describing a one-time heroic feeling, but a habitual attitude of trust. Even when walking through repeated or prolonged seasons of darkness, his ongoing decision is to reject panic.

• The Greek equivalent of this word in the Septuagint (LXX) translation of Psalm 23:4 is phobeo, which carries the same dual meaning of terror and reverence, and is the root of the English word "phobia."
