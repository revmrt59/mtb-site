EVIL - ( ra', Strong's H7451 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ra' in Psalm 23:4 describes not merely abstract wrongdoing, but profound adversity, distress, calamity, and deep trouble. When the psalmist declares, "I will fear no evil," he acknowledges the grim reality of terrifying, life-threatening circumstances while affirming that God's immediate presence completely disarms their power to destroy.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

- The root verb ra' carries the fundamental idea of breaking, bursting, or shattering into pieces, similar to shattering a potter's vessel.

- Culturally and linguistically, the word moves away from a purely moral definition (wickedness) to an experiential one (ruin, disaster, or severe affliction).

- In the pastoral setting of Psalm 23, the imagery is that of a sheep facing perilous danger in the wilderness—such as dark ravines, predatory beasts, or sudden storms—where disaster threatens immediate destruction.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

- Ra' is the primary Old Testament term for adversity, distress, misery, injury, calamity, and general harm.

- While it frequently translates as "wickedness" or "sin" in a moral sense (deserving of God's judgment), its predominant function in descriptive and narrative texts is that of trouble, pain, and overwhelming misfortune.

- It represents everything that opposes human well-being, peace, and life.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

- The word is remarkably broad, used for physical injuries, famine, plagues, military defeat, and personal grief.

- It often appears in tandem with safety or peace to highlight the contrast between a secure state and threatening disaster.

- It paints a vivid picture of a crushing weight or a shattering experience that threatens to break a person's spirit or life.

4. Theological Implications

- The biblical worldview never minimizes the reality of ra' (adversity/evil); it assumes believers will walk directly into valleys marked by it.

- However, the presence of evil does not mean the absence of God. The theological pivot of Psalm 23:4 rests entirely on the prepositional phrase: "For You are with me."

- God’s sovereignty encompasses the dark valley; He does not always prevent the believer from entering the shadow of ra', but He sovereignly redeems, comforts, and protects through it.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

- In Psalm 23:4, the phrase "shadow of death" (tsalmaveth) modifies and deepens the meaning of ra', pointing to the darkest, most terrifying ravines where death feels imminent.

- The sheep does not fear the ra' precisely because the Shepherd’s rod and staff are present to defend and guide.

- The focus is relational trust overcoming circumstantial terror; the fear is cast out not by a change in circumstances, but by the personal companionship of the Shepherd.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

- Genesis 50:20: "But as for you, you meant evil against me; but God meant it for good, in order to bring it about as it is this day, to save many people alive."

- Job 2:10: "But he said to her, 'You speak as one of the foolish women speaks. Shall we indeed accept good from God, and shall we not accept evil?'"

- Amos 3:6: "If a trumpet is blown in a city, will not the people be afraid? If there is calamity [ra'] in a city, will not the Lord have done it?"

- Psalm 91:10: "No evil shall befall you, nor shall any plague come near your dwelling."

7. Other Relevant Insights

- The ancient mind did not separate natural disaster from the broader spectrum of trouble; ra' unifies moral failure, natural calamity, and personal suffering under a single umbrella of human vulnerability.

- Recognizing that ra' means "calamity" or "distress" in Psalm 23 prevents the verse from being reduced to moral temptation; the context is danger and death, not sin.
