MERCY - ( chesed , Strong's H2617 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word chesed is one of the most rich and foundational terms in the Old Testament, representing God's loyal, covenant-keeping love, steadfastness, and mercy. It is not a sentiment or a fleeting feeling, but an active, enduring commitment to show undeserved kindness and faithfulness to those in covenant relationship with Him. In Psalm 23:6, this mercy is depicted not as a passive sentiment, but as an active, relentless pursuit of the believer by the Good Shepherd.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The cultural background of chesed lies in the ancient Near Eastern concept of covenant. A covenant was a binding agreement that established a familial bond between two parties, complete with mutual obligations. While covenants between equals involved mutual chesed, the chesed of God toward humanity is entirely asymmetrical. God, the sovereign King, binds Himself to His weak and failing people, promising to show them unwavering favor, protection, and forgiveness. The word picture behind chesed is that of a strong, reliable, and deeply affectionate loyalty that refuses to let go, even when the recipient of that love is unfaithful.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term chesed escapes simple translation because it encompasses several English concepts simultaneously. It means:

• Lovingkindness / Steadfast Love: Active, affectionate concern and benevolent grace.

• Loyalty / Covenant Faithfulness: Absolute reliability based on a prior commitment or promise.

• Mercy / Pity: Compassionate action taken on behalf of those in distress, misery, or guilt.

It is never abstract; it is always demonstrated through concrete deeds of rescue, preservation, and forgiveness. It is the intersection of love, commitment, and action.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Hebrew Scriptures, chesed is frequently paired with other attributes of God to paint a complete picture of His character:

• Paired with Truth ('emet): This pair (often translated "mercy and truth" or "lovingkindness and faithfulness") shows that God's love is grounded in His absolute integrity. He is both warm-hearted and completely trustworthy.

• The Shepherd's Pursuit: In Psalm 23:6, the verb translated "follow" (radaph) is highly intense. Elsewhere in Scripture, radaph refers to enemies pursuing, hunting down, or persecuting someone. David uses this word in a stunning reversal: instead of enemies hunting him down to destroy him, God's goodness and chesed are hunting him down to bless him. They are the twin "guard dogs" of the Good Shepherd, chasing the believer with relentless grace.

4. Theological Implications

The theology of chesed is central to the Old Testament understanding of salvation and grace.

• Rooted in God's Character: It is not prompted by the worthiness of the recipient. Israel repeatedly broke the covenant, yet God's chesed remained undiminished because His own name and character were at stake.

• The Foundation of Prayer: Because of chesed, believers can appeal to God for forgiveness and deliverance even when they have sinned. David did this in Psalm 51:1, crying out, "Have mercy upon me, O God, according to Your lovingkindness (chesed)."

• The Bridge to New Testament Grace: The Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint) frequently translates chesed using eleos (mercy). This directly connects the covenant faithfulness of Yahweh to the grace and mercy revealed in Jesus Christ, who is the ultimate embodiment of God's chesed.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 23:6, David reflects on his life under the care of the Good Shepherd. Having walked through the "valley of the shadow of death" (v. 4) and sat at a table in the presence of his enemies (v. 5), David concludes with absolute certainty. The Hebrew word 'ak (translated "surely") expresses firm conviction.

David's life was marked by being pursued—first by King Saul, then by his own son Absalom. He knew the terror of being hunted. Yet, in this final verse, David declares that a different kind of pursuit is taking place. The sovereign grace (tov, "goodness") and covenant loyalty (chesed, "mercy") of Yahweh will pursue him every single day of his life, ultimately escorting him into the eternal presence of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 136:1 – "Oh, give thanks to the LORD, for He is good! For His mercy endures forever."

• Psalm 103:8 – "The LORD is merciful and gracious, Slow to anger, and abounding in mercy."

• Micah 7:18 – "Who is a God like You, Pardoning iniquity And passing over the transgression of the remnant of His heritage? He does not retain His anger forever, Because He delights in mercy."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A helpful way to understand the interplay between the different translations of chesed (mercy, lovingkindness, loyal love) is to recognize that:

• Grace is God giving us what we do not deserve.

• Mercy is God not giving us what we do deserve.

• Chesed is the deep, covenantal commitment of love that guarantees both grace and mercy will be poured out on His people consistently, without fail, for eternity.
