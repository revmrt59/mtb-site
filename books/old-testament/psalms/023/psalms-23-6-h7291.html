FOLLOW - ( radaph , Strong's H7291 )

Summary:

In Psalm 23:6, the Hebrew word radaph, translated as "follow," does not mean to merely trail behind at a distance or drift along passively. Rather, it means to actively pursue, chase, or hunt down. While overwhelmingly used in the Old Testament to describe enemies pursuing their prey with hostile and deadly intent, David brilliantly reverses this imagery. In the covenant care of Yahweh, it is God's active, aggressive, and relentless "goodness and mercy" that chase the believer, ensuring they are never abandoned.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word radaph conjures up the intense imagery of a chase. In the Ancient Near East, it was primarily a military, hunting, or judicial term. It represents a predator tracking its prey, a hunter chasing down game, or a victorious army pursuing a fleeing enemy to destroy them. It carries the energy of swift, determined, and goal-oriented movement. By using this intense word, David paints a vivid and surprising picture: God's covenant blessings of goodness and mercy are not static or passive; they are actively hunting the believer down to overtake them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb radaph means to pursue, chase, press on, run after, or persecute. It denotes directed, energetic movement toward a target with the intent to catch or overtake it. In a hostile sense, it means to harass or persecute. In a positive or moral sense, it means to eagerly run after righteousness, peace, or justice.

In Psalm 23:6, the verb is used in the Qal imperfect form, which denotes continuous, ongoing, and unceasing action. This indicates that the pursuit of God's goodness and mercy is not a temporary or sporadic occurrence, but an uninterrupted, lifelong chase that persists "all the days of my life."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Hostile Pursuit: The dominant usage of radaph in the Old Testament describes enemies pursuing the vulnerable. It is used of Pharaoh's army pursuing Israel at the Red Sea, and of Saul relentlessly hunting David in the wilderness like a wild animal.

• Moral and Spiritual Pursuit: The word is used metaphorically for the zealous pursuit of a righteous lifestyle. Believers are commanded to run after peace, justice, and godliness with the same intensity that a soldier pursues a fleeing enemy.

• The Divine Reversal: In Psalm 23:6, David reverses the standard hostile word picture. Throughout his life, David was intimately familiar with being pursued (radaph) by Saul, Absalom, and foreign armies. He rewrites his experience of fear: it is no longer deadly enemies who are gaining on him, but God’s personified attributes of goodness and lovingkindness chasing him from behind.

4. Theological Implications

• The Aggressive Grace of God: Grace is not passive. God does not merely wait for His children to stumble back to Him; His goodness and covenant loyalty actively run after them. We do not have to chase down God's blessings; His blessings hunt us down.

• Covenant Security: The pursuit is driven by chesed (loyal covenant love) and tov (goodness). Because God has bound Himself to His people by a covenant, He guarantees that His protective care will aggressively follow them into every dark valley and difficult season.

• A Shield Against Fear: For a sheep, the danger always comes from behind, out of the shepherd's direct line of sight. David reassures the believer that even their "blind spots" are covered. What is lurking behind them is not destruction, but the relentless, loving pursuit of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Throughout Psalm 23, the Shepherd leads, guides, and restores. The sheep follow the Shepherd's lead. However, in verse 6, the direction of movement shifts. Now, something is coming up from behind the psalmist. As David looks back over his shoulder at the path he has traveled—including the valley of the shadow of death—he does not see the shadows of wolves or enemies. Instead, he sees God’s goodness (tov) and covenant love (chesed) trailing his every step like two divine escorts, ensuring he safely reaches his final destination: the house of the Lord forever.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 15:9 - "The enemy said, 'I will pursue [radaph], I will overtake, I will divide the spoil; My desire shall be satisfied on them. I will draw my sword, My hand shall destroy them.'"

• Psalm 34:14 - "Depart from evil and do good; Seek peace and pursue [radaph] it."

• Proverbs 15:9 - "The way of the wicked is an abomination to the LORD, But He loves him who follows [radaph] righteousness."

• Isaiah 51:1 - "Listen to Me, you who follow [radaph] after righteousness, You who seek the LORD: Look to the rock from which you were hewn, And to the hole of the pit from which you were dug."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament), radaph in Psalm 23:6 is translated using the Greek verb dioko. This is the exact same word used in the New Testament for "persecute" or to "press on" toward a mark (as in Philippians 3:14). It reinforces the absolute intensity of the pursuit. God's mercy is "persecuting" us in the most loving way possible—He refuses to let us go. This concept is the biblical foundation for what theologians often call "irresistible grace" or the relentless pursuit of the "Hound of Heaven."
