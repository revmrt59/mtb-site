Psalms 23

Verse-by-Verse
Psalm 23:1
Psalm 23:1
NKJV

Psalms 23:1 A Psalm of David. The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 23:1 The LORD is my shepherd;I have all that I need. (NLT)

Main Idea

In this psalm of David, the shepherd who became Israel’s king confesses that the LORD is his own Shepherd, a title kings bore for their people and Scripture gives to God Himself, so that under His care David lacks nothing he truly needs. Every believer may make the same personal confession, because the LORD who shepherded David shepherds His people through Jesus, the Good Shepherd (John 10:11).

Verse Explanation

In this psalm of David, the shepherd who became Israel’s king confesses that the LORD is his own Shepherd, a title kings bore for their people and Scripture gives to God Himself, so that under His care David lacks nothing he truly needs. Every believer may make the same personal confession, because the LORD who shepherded David shepherds His people through Jesus, the Good Shepherd (John 10:11).

“A Psalm of David.”

The title names David as the author of the psalm but gives no occasion for it. That matters, because the man who wrote these lines had spent his youth with sheep. He kept his father’s flock, and when a lion or a bear took a lamb, he went after it and rescued the lamb from its mouth (1 Samuel 17:34–35). Later the LORD took him “from the sheepfolds” to shepherd His people Israel (Psalm 78:70–71). The psalm reads as the settled trust of a man looking back over a life spent under the LORD’s care and looking ahead to His house. Episodes from David’s story help us hear these words on his lips, but none of them is named as the setting, and the psalm was given for every worshiper who prays it.

“The LORD is my shepherd;”

The psalm opens with the divine name, “The LORD,” the God who made Himself known to Israel and bound Himself to His people. Joined to that name is a title with royal weight. Rulers in the ancient world were called shepherds of their people, and the LORD Himself told David, “You shall shepherd My people Israel” (2 Samuel 5:2). Scripture gives the same title to God: Jacob spoke of “the Mighty God of Jacob (From there is the Shepherd, the Stone of Israel)” (Genesis 49:24), and Asaph prayed, “Give ear, O Shepherd of Israel” (Psalm 80:1). So the shepherd who became king confesses that he is himself a sheep, and that the LORD is his Shepherd.

The little word “my” makes the confession personal. A shepherd owned his flock, fed it, led it, and defended it, and the sheep depended on him for everything. David is not describing God from a distance. He is saying to whom he belongs and who takes responsibility for him.

Read through the rest of Scripture, this confession belongs to every believer. The LORD promised His scattered flock, “Indeed I Myself will search for My sheep and seek them out” (Ezekiel 34:11), and Jesus declared, “I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd gives His life for the sheep” (John 10:11). Psalm 23 does not name Jesus, but the LORD who shepherded David is the One who shepherds His people through Him.

“I shall not want.”

To “want” here means to lack or to be without. Because the LORD is his Shepherd, David lacks nothing he truly needs. Moses used the same word of Israel’s forty years in the wilderness: “These forty years the LORD your God has been with you; you have lacked nothing” (Deuteronomy 2:7). A barren wilderness was no place to find plenty, yet God’s people lacked nothing there because He was with them. David’s confidence likewise rests on the Shepherd, not on easy circumstances.

The line confesses sufficiency under the Shepherd’s care. It does not promise every desire, wealth, or a life without hardship; within a few lines the same psalm speaks of a dark valley and of enemies. What it does promise is that the sheep of this Shepherd will have what the Shepherd knows they need. Everything that follows in the psalm unfolds this opening line.

Study Notes

In Hebrew the line reads YHWH ro‘i lo’ ’echsar, literally “The LORD my shepherd, I shall not lack.” Ro‘i is a participle of the verb ra‘ah, “to pasture” or “to tend,” with the suffix “my.” The familiar title YHWH-Rohi, “the LORD my Shepherd,” comes from this line. It is a confession drawn from Psalm 23 rather than a compound name that appears elsewhere in Scripture.

The Hebrew verb translated “want” means “to lack” and is the same word used in Deuteronomy 2:7 and Nehemiah 9:21 to describe how Israel lacked nothing during the forty years in the wilderness.

Kings were commonly called shepherds in the ancient Near East. In L. W. King’s English translation of the Code of Hammurabi, the Babylonian king calls himself “the shepherd of the oppressed and of the slaves” in the prologue and “the salvation-bearing shepherd, whose staff is straight” in the epilogue.

The superscription “A Psalm of David” stands at the head of verse 1 in the NKJV, and the Hebrew Bible also counts the title as part of verse 1 here. Psalm 23 therefore has the same verse numbers in Hebrew and English.

The NLT renders the second line “I have all that I need,” expressing positively what the Hebrew states as a negative.

Psalm 23:2

Psalms 23:2 He makes me to lie down in green pastures; (H5116) He leads me beside the still waters. (H4325)(NKJV)

Psalm 23:2 He lets me rest in green meadows;he leads me beside peaceful streams. (NLT)

Main Idea

The Shepherd makes David lie down in green pastures and leads him beside still waters, literally waters of rest, so that rest and refreshment come from the Shepherd’s leading rather than the sheep’s own searching. Read through the rest of Scripture, this is the rest Christ gives to those who come to Him (Matthew 11:29).

Verse Explanation

The Shepherd makes David lie down in green pastures and leads him beside still waters, literally waters of rest, so that rest and refreshment come from the Shepherd’s leading rather than the sheep’s own searching. Read through the rest of Scripture, this is the rest Christ gives to those who come to Him (Matthew 11:29).

“He makes me to lie down in green pastures;”

The verb is causative: the shepherd brings the sheep to a place where it can lie down. A sheep settles when it has eaten and feels safe, so the picture is of a flock fed and at rest. “Green pastures” are meadows of fresh, tender grass, valuable in a dry land where good grazing could not be taken for granted. David had led flocks through the hills around Bethlehem and knew how much depended on a shepherd who knew where to find it. The sheep does not discover the pasture on its own; the Shepherd leads it there and settles it down. Rest is His gift before it is the sheep’s experience.

“He leads me beside the still waters.”

The Hebrew reads literally “waters of rest.” These are waters where the flock can drink and lie down, a quiet place of refreshment rather than a rushing torrent. The verb “leads” describes guiding a flock to a watering place and letting it rest there. Again the Shepherd is the one who acts. Together the two lines picture the daily needs of the flock, food, water, and rest, supplied by the Shepherd’s leading rather than by the sheep’s own searching.

Read through the rest of Scripture, this rest points to the rest Christ gives. He called, “Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest,” and promised, “you will find rest for your souls” (Matthew 11:28–29). The weary believer is brought to rest the same way David was: by following the Shepherd who leads.

Study Notes

“Green pastures” combines a word for a pasture or meadow with deshe’, fresh or tender grass. The KJV margin renders the phrase “pastures of tender grass.”

The Hebrew behind “still waters” is me menuchot. The noun menuchah means rest or a resting place. It is the word in Psalm 95:11, “They shall not enter My rest,” and in Psalm 116:7, “Return to your rest, O my soul.”

The verb translated “leads” in this line is nahal. Isaiah 40:11 uses it of the LORD who will “gently lead those who are with young,” and Isaiah 49:10 of the One who will guide His people “by the springs of water.”

“Makes me to lie down” is the Hiphil of rabats, a verb often used of animals lying at rest. The LORD uses the same form in Ezekiel 34:15: “I will feed My flock, and I will make them lie down.”

The NLT reads “He lets me rest in green meadows; he leads me beside peaceful streams.”

Psalms 23:3 He restores my soul; He leads me in the paths of righteousness For His name's sake.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 23:3 He renews my strength.He guides me along right paths,bringing honor to his name. (NLT)

Main Idea

The LORD revives David’s whole weary life and leads him on right paths, tracks that go where the Shepherd intends and keep him in what is right before God, and He does so “for His name’s sake,” for the honor of His own character. Spiritually, the Shepherd restores believers who have fallen or wandered, as He restored David after his sin, and His guidance rests on His faithfulness rather than on the sheep’s performance.

Verse Explanation

The LORD revives David’s whole weary life and leads him on right paths, tracks that go where the Shepherd intends and keep him in what is right before God, and He does so “for His name’s sake,” for the honor of His own character. Spiritually, the Shepherd restores believers who have fallen or wandered, as He restored David after his sin, and His guidance rests on His faithfulness rather than on the sheep’s performance.

“He restores my soul;”

In the Old Testament the “soul” is the whole living person, not a separate compartment inside a person. To “restore” it is to bring it back, to renew and revive it. David is speaking of the Shepherd reviving a weary or failing life, the way a worn-out sheep is brought back to strength. The same words can suggest bringing back a sheep that has wandered, though in David’s picture the main thought is renewal.

Read through the rest of Scripture, the Shepherd also restores believers who have fallen or wandered, as He restored David himself after his sin. When Nathan confronted him, David confessed, “I have sinned against the LORD,” and Nathan answered, “The LORD also has put away your sin” (2 Samuel 12:13). David’s prayer of repentance asks, “Restore to me the joy of Your salvation” (Psalm 51:12).

“He leads me in the paths of righteousness”

The word “paths” describes tracks or well-worn trails. A shepherd leads the flock along tracks that go somewhere, the right way through rough and unfamiliar country. “Righteousness” first describes paths that are right, true and reliable, leading where the Shepherd intends. In Israel’s vocabulary the word also carries its moral sense of what is right before God. The Shepherd keeps David on the road that reaches its goal, and on that road David walks in what pleases God.

“For His name’s sake.”

God’s “name” is His revealed character and reputation. The Shepherd leads this way because of who He is and for the honor of His own name. That gives David’s security a firm foundation. The LORD’s guidance does not rest on how well the sheep performs; it rests on His faithfulness to Himself. David prays on the same ground elsewhere: “For You are my rock and my fortress; Therefore, for Your name’s sake, Lead me and guide me” (Psalm 31:3).

Study Notes

“Restores” translates the Polel form of shuv, the common verb for turning or returning. Lamentations 1:16 uses another form of the same verb with the same noun, nephesh, for the comforter “who should restore my life.”

“Paths” translates ma‘gal. Proverbs 4:11 uses the same word: “I have led you in right paths.” The Hebrew phrase here is literally “tracks of righteousness.” The ESV, NIV, and NASB keep “paths of righteousness,” and the NLT reads “right paths.”

The NLT renders the first line “He renews my strength,” taking “soul” in its sense of the whole person’s life and vigor.

Psalm 23:4 — Through the Valley, You Are with Me

Psalm 23:4
Psalm 23:4
NKJV

Psalms 23:4 Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; For You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 23:4 Even when I walkthrough the darkest valley,I will not be afraid,for you are close beside me.Your rod and your staffprotect and comfort me. (NLT)

Main Idea

Even in the valley of the shadow of death, a place of real and deadly danger, David will fear no evil, and here he turns from speaking about the LORD to speaking to Him: “For You are with me.” The rod that defended the flock and the staff that supported and guided it comfort him, and read through the rest of Scripture they speak of the Shepherd’s loving discipline and guidance of His people, as Hebrews 12:6 shows.

Verse Explanation

Even in the valley of the shadow of death, a place of real and deadly danger, David will fear no evil, and here he turns from speaking about the LORD to speaking to Him: “For You are with me.” The rod that defended the flock and the staff that supported and guided it comfort him, and read through the rest of Scripture they speak of the Shepherd’s loving discipline and guidance of His people, as Hebrews 12:6 shows.

“Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death,”

The road now leads down into a “valley,” a deep ravine where light is poor and danger can hide. The NKJV keeps the traditional rendering “the shadow of death.” The Hebrew word describes deep darkness, and Scripture often links that darkness with death and mortal danger (Job 10:21–22; 38:17). On either understanding, this is a place of real and potentially deadly threat. Death is certainly within the valley, but it is not limited to the hour of dying; it covers every dark stretch of life where danger presses close.

The words “walk through” assume passage. David expects to keep moving and to come out on the other side. The Shepherd leads into the valley, and He leads through it.

“I will fear no evil;”

“Evil” here means harm or calamity of any kind. David does not pretend the danger is unreal; he refuses to be ruled by fear of it. The next words give the reason.

“For You are with me;”

Here David turns from speaking about the LORD to speaking to Him. Until now he has said “He”; now he says “You.” In the darkest part of the road David addresses his Shepherd directly, and this line stands at the heart of the psalm. His courage rests on the Shepherd’s presence, not on escape. The LORD does not promise to keep David out of the valley; He is with him in it.

“Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me.”

The “rod” was a short, heavy club the shepherd carried to drive off wild animals; the same word can name a ruler’s scepter. The “staff” was a longer stick for walking, for guiding the flock, and for supporting a sheep in difficult ground. Both belong to the Shepherd, “Your rod and Your staff,” and both “comfort” David, because they show that his Shepherd is equipped and ready to protect and guide him.

Read through the rest of Scripture, the rod speaks of the Shepherd’s authority and loving discipline, and the staff of His guidance and support. The prophets picture God claiming His flock under the rod: “I will make you pass under the rod” (Ezekiel 20:37). Hebrews says, “For whom the LORD loves He chastens” (Hebrews 12:6). Because David says these things comfort him, the discipline in view is the reassuring care of a Shepherd who will not let His sheep be lost, never harshness or threat.

Study Notes

“The shadow of death” translates tsalmawet. The traditional understanding divides the word into tsel, “shadow,” and mawet, “death,” and the Septuagint, the Targum, and the Syriac all render it that way. Many Hebrew scholars instead read a single noun meaning “deep darkness,” formed from a root meaning to be dark. Amos 5:8, where the LORD “turns the shadow of death into morning,” uses the word for the darkness of night. Both readings describe a place of mortal danger.

“Valley” translates gai’. Isaiah 9:2 uses tsalmawet for “the land of the shadow of death.” The ESV, NIV, and NASB keep “the valley of the shadow of death”; the NLT reads “the darkest valley.”

“Rod” translates shebet, which can also mean a tribe. Micah 7:14, “Shepherd Your people with Your staff,” uses the same word for the shepherd’s implement, and Leviticus 27:32 speaks of the animals of the flock that pass “under the rod” to be counted for the tithe.

“Staff” translates mish‘enet, from a verb meaning to lean on. Elsewhere it is a walking stick, as in Exodus 21:19, “walks about outside with his staff,” and Zechariah 8:4, “Each one with his staff in his hand.”

In Hebrew, verse 4 uses the independent pronoun ’attah, “You,” followed by “Your rod and Your staff,” and the direct address continues through verse 5. The verb “comfort” is from nacham in its form meaning to console.

Psalm 23:5–6 — The LORD’s Table and the LORD’s House

Psalm 23:5
NKJV

Psalms 23:5 You prepare a table beforeme in the presence of my enemies; You anoint my head with oil; My cup runs over.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 23:5 You prepare a feast for mein the presence of my enemies.You honor me by anointing my head with oil.My cup overflows with blessings. (NLT)

The picture changes from shepherd to host: the LORD prepares a table for David in full view of his enemies, honors him as a welcomed guest by anointing his head with oil, and fills his cup to overflowing. Verse Explanation

The picture changes from shepherd to host: the LORD prepares a table for David in full view of his enemies, honors him as a welcomed guest by anointing his head with oil, and fills his cup to overflowing. Spiritually, the table points to communion with God amid the enemies that surround the soul, seen most fully at the Lord’s table (1 Corinthians 10:16), and the oil points to the Holy Spirit poured out on the believer.

“You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies;”

The picture changes from shepherd to host. The LORD is the same, and David is still speaking to Him directly. To “prepare a table” is to set out a meal, arranging the food for a guest. In David’s world a host who welcomed a guest to his table took responsibility for that guest’s protection and honor. David knew both sides of such a table. In the wilderness during Absalom’s revolt, friends brought food “for David and the people who were with him to eat” (2 Samuel 17:29), and as king he told Jonathan’s son, “you shall eat bread at my table continually” (2 Samuel 9:7).

The words “in the presence of my enemies” keep the danger in view. The enemies have not vanished. Yet the LORD’s welcome stands in full view of them, and the meal publicly honors and vindicates His guest. Long before, Israel had challenged God in the wilderness, “Can God prepare a table in the wilderness?” (Psalm 78:19). David answers with confident trust: the LORD does prepare a table, even with enemies looking on.

Read through the rest of Scripture, the table points to communion with God in the presence of the enemies that surround the soul: sin, the flesh, and the spiritual powers of darkness (Ephesians 6:12). The New Testament’s fullest picture of that communion is the Lord’s table: “The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not the communion of the blood of Christ?” (1 Corinthians 10:16). Psalm 23 does not predict the Lord’s Supper, and the table does not make believers immune to temptation or opposition. But the enemies of the soul cannot cancel the welcome the Shepherd gives His own.

“You anoint my head with oil;”

Oil on the head was the honor and refreshment a host gave a welcomed guest. Jesus once said to a host who had neglected that courtesy, “You did not anoint My head with oil” (Luke 7:46). The verb here is not the word used for anointing kings and priests. It describes enriching and refreshing, so in David’s picture this is the honor of a guest, not a coronation.

Read through the rest of Scripture, oil points to the Holy Spirit. When Samuel anointed David, “the Spirit of the LORD came upon David from that day forward” (1 Samuel 16:13), and John tells believers that “the anointing which you have received from Him abides in you” (1 John 2:27). The Spirit refreshes and honors those who sit at the Shepherd’s table.

“My cup runs over.”

The host keeps his guest’s cup filled, and David’s cup overflows. The provision is more than enough. The psalm that began with “I shall not want” now pictures abundance beyond need, poured out by the same LORD.

Study Notes

“Prepare” translates ‘arak, to arrange or set in order, the same verb used for drawing up troops in battle order (1 Samuel 4:2). Psalm 78:19, quoted in the Verse Explanation, uses the same verb with the same noun for “table.”

“My enemies” translates a participle meaning those who show hostility, “my adversaries,” as Young’s Literal Translation renders it.

“Anoint” translates dashen, literally to make fat; the KJV margin reads “makest fat.” The verb for royal anointing is mashach, used when Samuel anointed David (1 Samuel 16:13).

“Runs over” translates a noun meaning saturation or abundance, revayah, so the Hebrew reads literally “my cup is abundance.” The only other occurrence of the noun is Psalm 66:12, “You brought us out to rich fulfillment.”

Some interpreters hold that the shepherd picture continues through verse 5. The table, the oil on the head, and the filled cup all belong to a meal given for a guest, which favors reading verse 5 as a change to the image of a host.

The NLT reads “You prepare a feast for me in the presence of my enemies. You honor me by anointing my head with oil. My cup overflows with blessings.”

Psalm 23:6
Psalm 23:6
NKJV

Psalms 23:6 Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me All the days of my life; And I will dwell in the house of the LORD Forever.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 23:6 Surely your goodness and unfailing love will pursue meall the days of my life,and I will live in the house of the LORDforever. (NLT)

Main Idea

Where enemies once pursued David, he is sure that the LORD’s goodness and His mercy, His steadfast love, will pursue him all the days of his life, and that he will dwell in the house of the LORD, living in God’s presence for the length of his days. Scripture carries this hope on to unending dwelling with God (Revelation 21:3), the home to which the Shepherd leads His flock.

Verse Explanation

Where enemies once pursued David, he is sure that the LORD’s goodness and His mercy, His steadfast love, will pursue him all the days of his life, and that he will dwell in the house of the LORD, living in God’s presence for the length of his days. Scripture carries this hope on to unending dwelling with God (Revelation 21:3), the home to which the Shepherd leads His flock.

“Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me”

“Surely” expresses certainty. “Goodness” is God’s kindness and generous favor. “Mercy” is His steadfast, faithful love, the loyal love He has promised to His people. These are two distinct gifts, and David names both.

The verb “follow” usually means to pursue or chase, often in hostile pursuit. It is the verb used when Saul “pursued David in the Wilderness of Maon” (1 Samuel 23:25), and when David called after Saul, “Whom do you pursue?” (1 Samuel 24:14). Looking back, David can say that while enemies were hunting him, the LORD’s goodness and steadfast love were pursuing him. Read through the rest of Scripture, this is the Shepherd who goes “after the one which is lost until he finds it” (Luke 15:4).

“All the days of my life;”

The pursuit covers every day, the days in green pastures and the days in the dark valley alike. No stretch of David’s life lies outside the reach of God’s goodness and mercy.

“And I will dwell in the house of the LORD”

“The house of the LORD” named the sanctuary even before the temple was built; it was the place where Hannah went up to worship at Shiloh (1 Samuel 1:7). For David it meant the place of God’s presence, and his great desire was “That I may dwell in the house of the LORD All the days of my life” (Psalm 27:4). To dwell there is to live in fellowship with God, not to take up literal residence in the tabernacle or temple. The guest welcomed at the table becomes one who belongs in the household.

Read through the rest of Scripture, this hope reaches its fulfillment in unending life with God. Jesus told His disciples, “In My Father’s house are many mansions” (John 14:2), and John heard the final announcement, “Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them” (Revelation 21:3). The Shepherd who leads His flock through this life leads it home.

“Forever.”

The Hebrew expression behind “Forever” means “for length of days,” and it parallels “All the days of my life.” In David’s words its first meaning is the whole span of his life lived in God’s presence. Because Scripture goes on to reveal unending dwelling with God, believers rightly hear the eternal hope in it, without claiming that the Hebrew phrase alone means eternity.

Study Notes

“Surely” translates ’ak, which can mean “surely” or “only.” Young’s Literal Translation renders it “Only.”

“Goodness” and “mercy” translate two different Hebrew words, tov and hesed. In Psalm 52:1 the NKJV’s “goodness” translates hesed, but here “goodness” is tov and “mercy” is hesed. Other translations render hesed in this verse as “love” (NIV) or “lovingkindness” (NASB).

Some translators take radaph here in the sense of simple accompaniment rather than pursuit. The NLT makes the pursuit explicit: “Surely your goodness and unfailing love will pursue me.”

“I will dwell” involves a textual question. The Hebrew consonants can be read two ways. The Masoretic vowels read “and I will return,” from shuv. The Septuagint reads “and my dwelling” (kai to katoikein me), and Symmachus has the same sense; the Vulgate reads “I will dwell.” The preposition “in” fits “dwell” more naturally than “return,” and Psalm 27:4 uses the dwelling verb in the same phrase, “dwell in the house of the LORD All the days of my life.” The NKJV, ESV, NIV, and NASB all read “dwell.”

The KJV margin gives the literal sense, “to length of days.” The NKJV translates the same expression “forever” in Psalm 93:5: “Holiness adorns Your house, O LORD, forever.”
