Psalms 23:1
NKJV

Psalms 23:1 A Psalm of David. The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 23:1 “A Psalm of David. The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

In this psalm of David, the shepherd who became Israel’s king confesses that the LORD is his own Shepherd, a title kings bore for their people and Scripture gives to God Himself, so that under His care David lacks nothing he truly needs. Every believer may make the same personal confession, because the LORD who shepherded David shepherds His people through Jesus, the Good Shepherd (John 10:11).

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


The heading, “A Psalm of David,” changes how we hear the first line. David knew shepherding from the inside. He was out keeping his father’s sheep when Samuel came to anoint him (1 Samuel 16:11). He fought a lion and a bear to rescue a lamb from their mouths (1 Samuel 17:34–36). God took him “from the sheepfolds” to shepherd His people Israel (Psalm 78:70–71). Yet when David writes this psalm, he does not speak as the shepherd. He speaks as the sheep. The man who had protected flocks and then ruled a nation knew he still needed the same care he had given to others.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


If David, the shepherd-king, called himself a sheep, then I will never become too strong, too experienced, or too important to need my Shepherd. Whatever responsibility I carry for others, I am still one of His flock. I don’t have to pretend I have outgrown my need for Him. I can lay down the weight of being in charge and let Him lead me.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


David could have said “a shepherd” or “our shepherd,” and both would be true. Israel sang, “We are the people of His pasture, and the sheep of His hand” (Psalm 95:7). But here David says “my.” The God who led the whole nation through the wilderness is personally the Shepherd of one man. Spurgeon called this “the sweetest word of the whole.” Without it, the line is a true statement about God. With it, the line becomes a confession of belonging. Jesus speaks the same way about His flock: “I know My sheep, and am known by My own” (John 10:14).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The LORD is not only a shepherd in general. He is my Shepherd. It is possible to believe that God cares for His people and never rest in His care for me. This verse invites me to say “my.” He knows me by name, even on the days I am a mystery to myself, and He has taken responsibility for me. What a sweet thing to hear: I belong to Him, and He belongs to me.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


“I shall not want” uses the same Hebrew word Moses used at the end of Israel’s wilderness years: “These forty years the LORD your God has been with you; you have lacked nothing” (Deuteronomy 2:7). Nehemiah later says it again: “Forty years You sustained them in the wilderness; they lacked nothing” (Nehemiah 9:21). The wilderness was dry, hot, and dangerous, yet Israel could testify that nothing essential had been missing. David takes the nation’s testimony and speaks it over his own life. This is not a promise that every desire will be met, since the same psalm includes a dark valley and enemies. It means the sheep will not lack what the Shepherd knows it needs.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Under the Lord’s care, I will not lack what I truly need, even in a dry and difficult season. When something I want is missing, I can trust that my Shepherd knows whether I need it today. “My God shall supply all your need” (Philippians 4:19). I can stop measuring His care by my wish list and start trusting it because of who He is.

Psalms 23:2
NKJV

Psalms 23:2 He makes me to lie down in green pastures; (H5116) He leads me beside the still waters. (H4325)(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 23:2 “He makes me to lie down in green pastures; He leads me beside the still waters.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

The Shepherd makes David lie down in green pastures and leads him beside still waters, literally waters of rest, so that rest and refreshment come from the Shepherd’s leading rather than the sheep’s own searching. Read through the rest of Scripture, this is the rest Christ gives to those who come to Him (Matthew 11:29).

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


Both verbs in this verse belong to the Shepherd: “He makes me to lie down,” and “He leads me.” The sheep does not find its own rest. “Makes me to lie down” describes the Shepherd bringing the sheep to the place of rest and settling it there. Spurgeon observed that many people know about God’s blessings but cannot lie down in them. In this verse rest is not something the sheep produces. It is the Shepherd’s work. And sometimes “makes” is exactly the right word, because a restless sheep may have to be stopped before it will rest.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I cannot make myself rest. Fear, conflict, and hunger for more keep me on my feet. This verse invites me to let the Shepherd lead me to rest instead of trying to produce it myself. Sometimes He “makes” me lie down through an illness, a closed door, or a pause I did not choose. In those moments I want to remember that His stopping me can be His care. He knows what I need before I do.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


The rest this verse describes is the rest Jesus offers. He said, “Come to Me, all you who labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest,” and promised that those who come to Him “will find rest for your souls” (Matthew 11:28–29). As in Psalm 23, the rest is given by the Shepherd, not earned by the sheep. At the end of the Bible the picture is still the same: “the Lamb who is in the midst of the throne will shepherd them and lead them to living fountains of waters” (Revelation 7:17).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Jesus is still saying, “Come to Me.” My weariness is not a reason to stay away from Him. It is the reason to come. I can bring Him the heavy load I am carrying today and receive the rest He promised. And I can look forward to the day He leads me to living fountains of water, where my soul will never be weary again.

Psalms 23:3
NKJV

Psalms 23:3 He restores my soul; He leads me in the paths of righteousness For His name's sake.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 23:3 “He restores my soul; He leads me in the paths of righteousness For His name’s sake.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

The LORD revives David’s whole weary life and leads him on right paths, tracks that go where the Shepherd intends and keep him in what is right before God, and He does so “for His name’s sake,” for the honor of His own character. Spiritually, the Shepherd restores believers who have fallen or wandered, as He restored David after his sin, and His guidance rests on His faithfulness rather than on the sheep’s performance.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


The order of this verse matters. First, “He restores my soul.” Then, “He leads me in the paths of righteousness.” Restoration comes before direction. A worn-out or fallen sheep cannot follow a path. It must first be brought back and set on its feet; only then can it walk the right way. God does not demand righteous living from a soul He has not first restored. David prayed in this order after his great sin: “Restore to me the joy of Your salvation… Then I will teach transgressors Your ways” (Psalm 51:12–13). Grace comes first, and obedience follows.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The Lord does not wait for me to fix myself before He helps me. On the days I feel worn out or far away, I don’t have to clean myself up before I come to Him. He restores first, then He leads. That makes me want to follow His paths gladly, not anxiously, because I am walking in strength He has given back to me.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


“He restores my soul” reaches deeper than renewed energy. The word for “soul” (nephesh) means the whole living person, and the verb means to bring back. The Shepherd is not touching up the surface of a life. He reaches its deepest weariness and wandering and brings the whole person back. His restoring is also personal. David says “my soul,” not “the flock.” The Shepherd knows exactly what is broken in each sheep and where. And He restores for a purpose: the restored sheep can walk again, follow Him, and live the life He intends.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I often want God to fix something on the surface: a situation, a mood, a problem that is in my way. He wants to restore me deeply. I can let Him reach the places I would rather keep covered, because He means to heal what is broken, not just help me feel better for a day. What He restores, He restores so I can follow Him.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


“Paths of righteousness” are literally right tracks, well-worn ways that lead where they should. God called His people to “ask for the old paths, where the good way is, and walk in it” (Jeremiah 6:16). Righteousness here means what is right in God’s eyes, and the Shepherd is the one who chooses the path. A sheep left to itself will wander after whatever looks greener. The path is right because it is His path, and He leads on it “for His name’s sake.” It is not always the easy way. The very next verse leads through a dark valley.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The Lord leads me toward what is right in His eyes, not toward where I think I should be led. I often have my own idea of what the right path looks like: easier, quicker, more comfortable. I can stop insisting on my own map and follow the One who knows the way. When He leads, the path is right, even when it is hard.

Psalm 23:4
“Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; For You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says
Even in “the valley of the shadow of death,” a place of real and deadly darkness, David will not fear, because the LORD is with him. Here he stops speaking about the LORD and speaks directly to Him: “For You are with me.” The shepherd’s rod, a club for driving off attackers, and his staff, used to guide and support the sheep, comfort him.

Observation 4

Observe: Something to notice


In verses 1–3 David speaks about the LORD: “He makes… He leads… He restores.” In verse 4 the psalm turns: “For You are with me.” In the darkest place, David stops talking about God and starts talking to Him. Green pastures produced a description; the valley produced a conversation. Jesus did the same in His darkest hour. On the cross, even as He felt forsaken, He still cried, “My God, My God” (Matthew 27:46).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The place where I am most afraid can become the place where I am closest to God. When I walk through a dark season, I don’t have to stand at a distance and talk about Him. I can talk to Him. My valley can turn what I know about God into real prayer.

Observation 5

Observe: Something to notice


David does not say the valley is safe. He says, “I will fear no evil.” The danger is real, and he walks through it, not around it. His courage does not come from denying the darkness or from sheer willpower. It comes from a Person: “for You are with me.” The Shepherd does not always lift His sheep out of the valley. More often He walks with them through it. He may not explain the darkness, but He gives His presence in it. “God has not given us a spirit of fear, but of power and of love and of a sound mind” (2 Timothy 1:7).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I don’t have to pretend the danger isn’t real. My courage can rest on God being with me in it. I may never get an explanation for this valley, but I have His presence, and His presence is enough for me to keep walking without being ruled by fear.

Observation 6

Observe: Something to notice


“You are with me” is fulfilled for believers today by the Holy Spirit. Before He went to the cross, Jesus promised “another Helper, that He may abide with you forever,” and then said, “I will not leave you orphans; I will come to you” (John 14:16, 18). Scripture shows the Spirit doing the Shepherd’s work. He leads God’s children (Romans 8:14), and Isaiah says of Israel in the wilderness, “the Spirit of the LORD causes him to rest” (Isaiah 63:14). Psalm 23 does not mention the Spirit, but the rest of Scripture shows how the Shepherd’s presence stays with His sheep now.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I am not alone in my valley. Jesus has not left me an orphan. By His Spirit He is with me now, as near in the dark as He was in the green pastures. When I feel alone, I can ask the Holy Spirit to make His presence real to me and remind me that I belong to Him.

Observation 7

Observe: Something to notice


“The shadow of death” translates one Hebrew word (tsalmavet) that can also be read “deep darkness.” It describes the darkest places a person can walk, and those places are not only physical death. Many valleys feel like death: a marriage that is failing, health that is slipping away, a dream or a ministry that seems to be dying, a season of grief that will not lift. The One who says “I am with you” at the end of life is with His people in every dying thing along the way. Jesus Himself went down into the low places. He was “a Man of sorrows and acquainted with grief” (Isaiah 53:3), and He is not ashamed to walk with His sheep there.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Christ is with me in the low places of my life and in the seasons when things around me are dying. I don’t have to wait until my last day to need this verse. When something I love is ending, I can say, “You are with me,” and know that the Man of sorrows understands, and that He will lead me through.

Observation 8

Observe: Something to notice


“Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me.” The rod was a short club for driving off predators and for correcting and counting the sheep. The staff was a longer stick for guiding, supporting, and lifting. It may seem strange that a tool of correction brings comfort, but notice the word “Your.” These are the Shepherd’s own tools in the Shepherd’s own hand. In Israel the flock was counted as it passed “under the rod” (Leviticus 27:32), and God told His people, “I will make you pass under the rod, and I will bring you into the bond of the covenant” (Ezekiel 20:37). The rod meant each sheep was known and claimed. Hebrews applies the same truth to discipline: “For whom the LORD loves He chastens” (Hebrews 12:6).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


When the Lord corrects me, I don’t have to hear rejection. His discipline is a sign that I am His. A shepherd does not bother to correct a sheep that is not his own. Passing under His rod humbles me, and it also tells me I am counted, known, and claimed. I can receive His correction as comfort, because it means He will not let me be lost.

Psalms 23:5
NKJV

Psalms 23:5 You prepare a table beforeme in the presence of my enemies; You anoint my head with oil; My cup runs over.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 23:5 “You prepare a table before me in the presence of my enemies; You anoint my head with oil; My cup runs over.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

The picture changes from shepherd to host: the LORD prepares a table for David in full view of his enemies, honors him as a welcomed guest by anointing his head with oil, and fills his cup to overflowing. Spiritually, the table points to communion with God amid the enemies that surround the soul, seen most fully at the Lord’s table (1 Corinthians 10:16), and the oil points to the Holy Spirit poured out on the believer.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


“You prepare a table” echoes one of Israel’s complaints in the desert. Psalm 78 remembers it: “Yes, they spoke against God: They said, ‘Can God prepare a table in the wilderness?’” (Psalm 78:19). David uses the same words and gives the answer: yes, He can, and He has. Israel and David both faced hard places with real needs. Unbelief stares at the desert and asks, “Can He?” Faith sits at the table and says, “You prepare a table before me.” And the table is strength for the road. The guest rises from it ready to go on.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


The Lord can provide for me in the hardest place I am in right now. The question is not whether He is able, but whether I will trust Him or doubt Him here. I want to stop asking “Can God?” while I stare at the wilderness, and start thanking Him for the table He has already set.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


The enemies have not gone away. They are still there, watching, and they cannot stop the meal. God does not wait for every enemy to leave before He feeds and honors His own. The point is not gloating over the enemy. The point is that the host’s welcome cannot be taken away. On the night of the Last Supper, Jesus told His disciples, “In the world you will have tribulation; but be of good cheer, I have overcome the world” (John 16:33).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


I don’t have to wait for every battle to end or every problem to disappear before I can enjoy God’s provision and peace. He welcomes me now, in the presence of whatever presses against me, and no enemy can cancel His invitation. I can sit down and eat in peace, even while the struggle goes on.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


“My cup runs over.” The cup is not just full; it spills over. A host who kept filling a guest’s cup was showing a generous welcome, not handing out the bare minimum. Jesus said, “I have come that they may have life, and that they may have it more abundantly” (John 10:10). He also promised that whoever believes in Him, “out of his heart will flow rivers of living water” (John 7:38), and John explains that He was speaking of the Spirit (7:39). The overflow Scripture celebrates is first of all the life God gives.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


My overflowing cup is not mainly about money or possessions. It is the life God pours into me: forgiveness I could never earn, mercy that meets me every morning, living water that does not run dry. When I count what He has poured into my life, the right response is thanksgiving. And what overflows from my cup is meant to spill onto others.

Psalms 23:6
NKJV

Psalms 23:6 Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me All the days of my life; And I will dwell in the house of the LORD Forever.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 23:6 “Surely goodness and mercy shall follow me All the days of my life; And I will dwell in the house of the LORD Forever.” (NKJV)

Understand: What the verse says

Where enemies once pursued David, he is sure that the LORD’s goodness and His mercy, His steadfast love, will pursue him all the days of his life, and that he will dwell in the house of the LORD, living in God’s presence for the length of his days. Scripture carries this hope on to unending dwelling with God (Revelation 21:3), the home to which the Shepherd leads His flock.

Observation 1

Observe: Something to notice


“Follow” means more than tagging along. The Hebrew word usually means “pursue,” the same word used when Saul hunted David: “he pursued David in the Wilderness of Maon” (1 Samuel 23:25). David’s life was full of pursuers: Saul, and later the men of his own son Absalom. Here he uses the hunter’s word for God’s goodness and mercy. Some read the word more simply as “accompany,” but in David’s life it carries weight. Behind every human pursuer, a faster Pursuer was coming.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


What is chasing me is not my past failures or the enemy. God’s goodness and mercy are chasing me, and they are faster. When I feel hunted by fear or by what I have done, I can remember that love is pursuing me. If God chased me in judgment, I would run. Because He chases me in mercy, I can stop running and let myself be caught.

Observation 2

Observe: Something to notice


“All the days of my life” comes from a man who has just walked through the valley of the shadow of death and eaten in the presence of enemies. David does not say goodness and mercy will follow him on the good days. He says all of them. Lamentations, written from the ruins of Jerusalem, says the same: “Through the LORD’s mercies we are not consumed, because His compassions fail not. They are new every morning” (Lamentations 3:22–23). The promise covers the hospital days, the days of grief and failure, and the ordinary Tuesday.

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


There is no day in my life when God’s goodness and mercy stop following me. Not the hardest day behind me, and not the hardest one ahead. Today counts too. I can look back on my worst seasons and see that His mercy was there, and I can face tomorrow knowing it will be there again.

Observation 3

Observe: Something to notice


The psalm’s journey ends at a home. Pasture, valley, and table all lead to “the house of the LORD.” This was David’s deepest desire: “One thing I have desired of the LORD, that will I seek: that I may dwell in the house of the LORD all the days of my life, to behold the beauty of the LORD” (Psalm 27:4). In David’s day the house of the LORD was the place of God’s presence among His people. Jesus carries the hope further: “In My Father’s house are many mansions” (John 14:2). At the end of the Bible, “the tabernacle of God is with men, and He will dwell with them” (Revelation 21:3).

Hear: What God may be speaking to me


Every day of my life is heading home to God’s presence, and that is the best thing I could want. The valley I am in now is not my destination. I want what David wanted: to live near my Shepherd, to see His beauty, all my days and forever.
