Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Psalm 23
Main Idea:

David confesses the LORD as his Shepherd, who provides for him, stays with him through the darkest valley, and welcomes him as an honored guest to a lifetime in His house.

Setting:

“A Psalm of David” (23:1) stands between Psalm 22, where a cry of abandonment turns to praise, and Psalm 24, where the King of glory enters His holy hill. No occasion is named; the words come from the shepherd who became Israel’s king.

Chapter Outline:

  • Psalm 23:1–3 — The LORD Is My Shepherd

  • Psalm 23:4 — Through the Valley, You Are with Me

  • Psalm 23:5–6 — The LORD’s Table and the LORD’s House

Key Verse:

“The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want.” (Psalm 23:1, NKJV)

Go Deeper

Author

The title attributes the psalm to David (23:1). He had kept his father’s sheep and defended them from the lion and the bear (1 Samuel 17:34–37) before God took him “from the sheepfolds” to shepherd Israel (Psalm 78:70–72). The psalm most likely voices the settled trust of a man looking back over a long life under the LORD’s care.

Occasion

The psalm names no occasion. Events in David’s life give its lines color, such as Saul’s pursuit and the provisions brought to him in the wilderness during Absalom’s revolt (2 Samuel 17:29), but none of them is its stated setting. David wrote it in the first person, and it became the prayer of every worshiper who makes the same confession.

Psalm Type

A psalm of trust. It uses two pictures of the same LORD: the shepherd who leads and protects (23:1–4) and the host who welcomes and honors (23:5–6). At verse 4, in the darkest part of the journey, David stops speaking about the LORD and begins speaking to Him.

The Heart of the Chapter

Psalm 23 begins with its whole message in one line: “The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want” (23:1). Kings in the ancient world were called shepherds of their people, and Scripture gives the title to God Himself. Here the shepherd-king David confesses that he is a sheep, and that under this Shepherd he lacks nothing he truly needs. The rest and provision that follow come from His leading: green pastures, still waters, a soul restored, and right paths “for His name’s sake” (23:3).

Then the road goes down into “the valley of the shadow of death” (23:4). The psalm does not pretend the danger is unreal. What changes is the way David speaks. He turns from “He” to “You”: “I will fear no evil; For You are with me.” That line stands at the heart of the psalm. David’s courage rests on the Shepherd’s presence, not on escape, and the rod that defends and the staff that guides are his comfort.

In verse 5 the Shepherd becomes a host. He prepares a table “in the presence of my enemies,” anoints His guest’s head with oil and fills his cup to overflowing. The enemies are still there, but the LORD’s welcome stands in full view of them. And where enemies once hunted David, he now says that goodness and mercy will “follow” him, a word that usually means to pursue (23:6). The psalm ends with the hope of dwelling in the house of the LORD, a whole life lived in God’s presence.

Read through the rest of Scripture, these same pictures open onto the believer’s life with God. The table points to communion with God in the presence of the enemies of the soul, seen most fully at the Lord’s table, “the communion of the blood of Christ” (1 Corinthians 10:16). The rod and staff speak of the Shepherd’s loving discipline and guidance (Hebrews 12:6). The oil points to the Holy Spirit, who came upon David at his anointing (1 Samuel 16:13) and abides in believers (1 John 2:27). Psalm 23 does not name Jesus, but the LORD who shepherded David is the One who says, “I am the good shepherd” (John 10:11).

Chapter Sections

Psalm 23:1–3 — The LORD Is My Shepherd

David confesses the LORD as his Shepherd and finds in Him everything he needs: rest, refreshment, a revived soul and guidance on right paths. The LORD leads this way for the honor of His own name.

Psalm 23:4 — Through the Valley, You Are with Me

In the darkest valley, where death itself is near, David speaks directly to the LORD and fears no evil because the Shepherd is with him. The rod and staff that protect and guide become his comfort.

Psalm 23:5–6 — The LORD’s Table and the LORD’s House

The LORD welcomes David as His honored guest, setting a table before his enemies, anointing his head and filling his cup. Goodness and mercy pursue him all his days, and his hope is to dwell in the LORD’s house.

Why This Chapter Matters

Psalm 23 shows a trust that looks danger in the face. It speaks of want, a dark valley and enemies, and it confesses the LORD in the middle of them all. In this psalm, faith rests on the Shepherd who is present inside the threat.

The psalm also belongs to the whole of life, not only to funerals. Its pastures, paths, valley and table cover ordinary days as well as the last one, and its closing hope reaches from “all the days of my life” to dwelling with God. Because the LORD shepherds His people in Christ, every believer can make David’s confession personally: the Shepherd provides, stays near in the dark and welcomes His own home.

Key Themes to Watch For

The LORD as Shepherd-King. The divine name joined to a royal title makes the LORD David’s own Shepherd (23:1).

Rest and restoration. The Shepherd leads to rest, revives the soul and guides on right paths for His name’s sake (23:2–3).

Presence in the valley. “For You are with me” stands at the center of the psalm (23:4).

Welcome before enemies. The host’s table, oil and full cup honor the guest in full view of his enemies (23:5).

A lifetime of goodness and mercy. God’s goodness and steadfast love pursue David to the end of his days and into the LORD’s house (23:6).

Key Words & Concepts

YHWH-Rohi. From the Hebrew of 23:1, meaning the LORD my Shepherd. It is a confession drawn from this line rather than a separate name used elsewhere in Scripture.

“Restores my soul.” The Hebrew word for soul means the whole living person. The phrase describes the reviving of a weary or failing life (23:3).

“The shadow of death.” The NKJV’s rendering in 23:4. Many scholars read the Hebrew as a single word for deep darkness; either way, the valley is a place of mortal danger.

Rod and staff. The rod was a club for defense and could stand for authority; the staff was a support for walking and guiding (23:4). Scripture links the rod with God’s oversight of His flock (Ezekiel 20:37).

“You anoint my head.” The verb here describes the refreshing oil a host gave an honored guest, not the word used for anointing kings and priests (23:5).

Goodness and mercy. Goodness is tov; mercy is hesed, God’s steadfast, faithful love. The verb “follow” usually means to pursue, so these blessings chase David down for life (23:6).
