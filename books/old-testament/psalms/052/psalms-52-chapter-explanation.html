Main Idea

The superscription ties Psalm 52 to Doeg the Edomite’s report to Saul, which led to the slaughter of the LORD’s priests at Nob. David’s psalm is his instructive response to that betrayal, and it was given for Israel’s worship.

Verse Explanation

The superscription ties Psalm 52 to Doeg the Edomite’s report to Saul, which led to the slaughter of the LORD’s priests at Nob. David’s psalm is his instructive response to that betrayal, and it was given for Israel’s worship.

“To the Chief Musician.”

This note appears in many psalm titles. It indicates that the psalm was given to the leader of Israel’s worship for use in the congregation. A psalm that grew out of one painful episode in David’s life became a song for all of God’s people.

“A Contemplation of David”

“Contemplation” translates maskil, a word related to wisdom and insight. Its exact meaning as a title is uncertain, but it probably marks the psalm as one that gives understanding or instruction. Psalm 52 teaches by setting the end of the man who trusts in himself beside the security of the one who trusts in God’s mercy.

“when Doeg the Edomite went and told Saul, and said to him, ‘David has gone to the house of Ahimelech.’”

While fleeing from Saul, David went to Nob, where Ahimelech the priest gave him the holy bread and the sword of Goliath (1 Samuel 21:1–9). Doeg was there that day. He was an Edomite who served as chief of Saul’s herdsmen, and he was “detained before the LORD” at the sanctuary (21:7). Later, Saul accused his servants of conspiring with David, and Doeg reported what he had seen (22:9–10). Saul summoned Ahimelech and the priests, rejected Ahimelech’s defense, and ordered them killed. When Saul’s guards refused to strike the priests of the LORD, Doeg killed eighty-five of them himself. Then he put the town of Nob to the sword, including women, children, and nursing infants (22:17–19). Only Abiathar escaped and fled to David (22:20).

Doeg’s report was substantially accurate. Its treachery lay in its use. It gave Saul the evidence he wanted for his suspicion of conspiracy, although Ahimelech had acted in good faith toward a man he knew as the king’s loyal son-in-law (22:14–15). This matters for verses 2–4. The psalm’s language of deceit and lying does not require that Doeg invented his report, because a man can destroy with true words when he uses them treacherously. The psalm also moves beyond Doeg’s single act to portray the character of the wicked, self-confident man, so not every detail needs to be matched to a recorded event in Doeg’s life.

Study Notes

Hebrew verse numbering. In the Hebrew Bible the superscription counts as verses 1–2, so the Hebrew text of Psalm 52 has eleven verses. English Bibles leave the title unnumbered, which makes each NKJV verse number two lower than the Hebrew.

Maskil. Thirteen psalm titles use this word (Psalms 32, 42, 44, 45, 52–55, 74, 78, 88, 89, 142), and Psalms 52–55 form a group of Davidic maskil psalms. The related verb appears in Psalm 32:8: “I will instruct you and teach you in the way you should go.”

Psalms from David’s flight from Saul. Several psalm titles connect their psalms to the years David fled from Saul, including Psalms 34, 54, 56, 57, 59, and 142. Psalms 34 and 56 belong to David’s time in Gath, which immediately followed his visit to Nob (1 Samuel 21:10–15). Read alongside 1 Samuel 21–22, these titles show David bringing a series of real dangers and betrayals to God in worship.

Psalm 52:1
Psalm 52:1
NKJV

Psalms 52:1 To the Chief Musician. A Contemplation of David when Doeg the Edomite went and told Saul, and said to him, "David has gone to the house of Ahimelech." Why do you boast in evil, O mighty man? The goodness of God endures continually.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 52:1 Why do you boast about your crimes, great warrior?Don’t you realize God’s justice continues forever? (NLT)

Main Idea

David challenges the powerful man who boasts in evil. He answers that boasting with a settled truth: the goodness of God endures continually, and no apparent success of wickedness can outlast it.

Verse Explanation

David challenges the powerful man who boasts in evil. He answers that boasting with a settled truth: the goodness of God endures continually, and no apparent success of wickedness can outlast it.

David begins by confronting the wicked man directly, as though speaking to him face to face.

“Why do you boast in evil, O mighty man?”

The question is a rebuke. The man takes pride in the evil he has done, as though it were an accomplishment. “Mighty man” was a title of honor for a warrior, but here it is almost certainly ironic. Doeg displayed his might against priests who carried no weapons and against a town of women, children, and infants (1 Samuel 22:18–19). His strength was spent on people who could not defend themselves.

“The goodness of God endures continually.”

David answers the boasting with a fact about God. “Goodness” translates hesed, God’s steadfast, faithful love: His loyal kindness toward those who belong to Him. “Continually” is literally “all the day.” The man boasts as though evil has carried the day, but God’s faithful love remains through every day. The wicked man’s success is real but temporary. God’s faithfulness does not change with events.

The same Hebrew word returns in verse 8, where the NKJV translates it “mercy.” David opens the psalm by declaring that God’s faithful love endures, and he closes by trusting in it forever. Everything in between is measured against it.

Study Notes

“The goodness of God.” The Hebrew is hesed El. Hesed combines love, loyalty, and kindness. In the Old Testament it often describes God’s faithfulness to His covenant commitments. El is a short form of the word for God; verse 8 uses the fuller Elohim. The NKJV translates hesed in several ways (“goodness,” “mercy,” “lovingkindness”), which can hide the link between verses 1 and 8. The ESV uses “steadfast love” in both places.

A textual alternative. The Septuagint (the ancient Greek translation) reads the second line as a further description of the man’s wrongdoing. Some modern translations revise the Hebrew so that the line refers to evil done “against the godly” (NRSV). The Hebrew text behind the NKJV is well supported and makes good sense: David answers the boaster with the enduring love of God.

Psalm 52:2
Psalm 52:2
NKJV

Psalms 52:2 Your tongue devises destruction, Like a sharp razor, working deceitfully.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 52:2 All day long you plot destruction.Your tongue cuts like a sharp razor;you’re an expert at telling lies. (NLT)

Main Idea

The wicked man’s tongue plots destruction and cuts like a sharpened razor. It does its harm through deceit, turning words into weapons against others.

Verse Explanation

The wicked man’s tongue plots destruction and cuts like a sharpened razor. It does its harm through deceit, turning words into weapons against others.

David moves from the man’s boasting to his tongue, the instrument through which he does his harm.

“Your tongue devises destruction”

“Devises” means to plan or plot. David pictures the tongue itself as scheming, because the man’s plans are carried out through his speech. “Destruction” refers to ruin or disaster. His words are aimed at bringing others down.

“Like a sharp razor, working deceitfully.”

A sharpened razor cuts quickly and cleanly, often before the wound is felt. The image fits speech that does precise, sudden harm. Doeg’s report to Saul was only a few sentences long, yet it led to the deaths of eighty-five priests and the destruction of their town.

“Working deceitfully” describes how the tongue does its work: through treachery. As the background shows, the deceit lay in the purpose and use of the words more than in invented facts. Doeg’s report served Saul’s false accusation, and probably his own standing with the king, since Saul had just appealed to his servants’ self-interest (1 Samuel 22:7–8).

Study Notes

Speech as a weapon in David’s psalms. Several psalms from the period of Saul’s persecution describe an enemy’s words as weapons: “their tongue a sharp sword” (Psalm 57:4) and “swords are in their lips” (Psalm 59:7). Compare Psalm 55:21, where words smoother than butter are “drawn swords,” and Psalm 64:3.

Psalm 52:3
Psalm 52:3
NKJV

Psalms 52:3 You love evil more than good, Lying rather than speaking righteousness. Selah(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 52:3 You love evil more than goodand lies more than truth. Interlude (NLT)

Main Idea

David traces the destructive speech back to what the man loves. He prefers evil to good and lying to speaking what is right, so his words reveal the settled direction of his heart.

Verse Explanation

David traces the destructive speech back to what the man loves. He prefers evil to good and lying to speaking what is right, so his words reveal the settled direction of his heart.

“You love evil more than good”

David moves beneath the man’s words to his desires. What a person loves determines what he chooses. This man has set good and evil side by side and preferred evil, and his destructive speech flows from that preference.

“Lying rather than speaking righteousness.”

The same preference appears in his speech. He would rather speak falsehood than speak what is right. “Righteousness” here describes speech that is just and true, the kind God approves. As the superscription’s background shows, “lying” does not require that Doeg fabricated his report. Falsehood includes words put to an untrue purpose, and the psalm describes a man whose whole inclination is toward deception, not a single misstatement.

“Selah”

“Selah” is a term of uncertain meaning that probably marks a pause in the music or reading. Here it lets the charge settle before David continues.

Study Notes

“More than good.” The Hebrew comparison can mean “more than” or “rather than.” Some interpreters take it as excluding good altogether (“evil and not good”). Either way, the line describes a settled choice.

Selah. The word appears 71 times in Psalms and three times in Habakkuk 3. Proposed meanings include a musical interlude, a pause for reflection, or a signal to raise the voice. In Psalm 52 it follows the charge against the wicked man (v. 3) and the announcement of his judgment (v. 5).

Psalm 52:4
Psalm 52:4
NKJV

Psalms 52:4 You love all devouring words, You deceitful tongue.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 52:4 You love to destroy others with your words,you liar! (NLT)

Main Idea

David completes the charge by addressing the man as a “deceitful tongue.” The man takes pleasure in words that swallow up and ruin others.

Verse Explanation

David completes the charge by addressing the man as a “deceitful tongue.” The man takes pleasure in words that swallow up and ruin others.

“You love all devouring words”

For the second time David says, “You love.” Verse 3 named the man’s love of evil. Verse 4 names the kind of speech he enjoys. “Devouring” translates a verb meaning “to swallow up.” The same verb describes the earth swallowing Korah and his followers (Numbers 16:30–32) and the great fish swallowing Jonah (Jonah 1:17). Doeg’s words swallowed up an entire community of priests. “All” indicates that he is drawn to every kind of word that consumes others.

“You deceitful tongue.”

David ends his charge by addressing the man as a deceitful tongue. The whole person is identified with his speech, as if nothing about him remains except the treachery his mouth produces. With this, the exposure of verses 1–4 is complete. His boasting, plotting, preferences, and speech all reveal the same character.

Study Notes

Deceit throughout the section. Deceit runs through verses 2–4: “working deceitfully” (v. 2), “lying” (v. 3), and “deceitful tongue” (v. 4). Psalm 120:2–4 offers a close parallel. There the psalmist also addresses a “deceitful tongue” directly and announces God’s response to it. See also Proverbs 6:16–19 and James 3:5–8.

Psalm 52:5–7 — God’s Judgment on the Wicked

Psalm 52:5
Psalm 52:5
NKJV

Psalms 52:5 God shall likewise destroy you forever; He shall take you away, and pluck you out of your dwelling place, And uproot you from the land of the living. Selah(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 52:5 But God will strike you down once and for all.He will pull you from your homeand uproot you from the land of the living. Interlude (NLT)

Main Idea

God will answer the wicked man with decisive and lasting judgment. The man who brought destruction on others will himself be destroyed, removed from his dwelling, and uprooted from the land of the living.

Verse Explanation

God will answer the wicked man with decisive and lasting judgment. The man who brought destruction on others will himself be destroyed, removed from his dwelling, and uprooted from the land of the living.

Verses 1–4 exposed what the wicked man has done. Now David announces what God will do.

“God shall likewise destroy you forever”

“Likewise” ties the judgment to the man’s own conduct. His tongue devised destruction for others (v. 2), and now God will bring destruction on him. The verb pictures something torn down, the way a building or an altar is broken down. “Forever” marks the judgment as final. The man’s harm to others was swift, but God’s answer is permanent.

The psalm does not say when or how this judgment will come, and it should not be tied to a particular event. Its certainty rests on God Himself.

“He shall take you away, and pluck you out of your dwelling place”

“Take you away” translates a verb used for snatching up hot coals from a fire. “Pluck you out” means to tear away, and “dwelling place” is literally “tent,” the home where a person lives in security. The man who seemed settled and safe will be seized and torn out of the place he thought was his.

“And uproot you from the land of the living.”

“Uproot” pictures a tree pulled up by its roots. “The land of the living” refers to life in this world among living people. The man will be removed from life itself, with nothing of him left standing. This image of an uprooted tree prepares for verse 8, where David describes himself as a green olive tree flourishing in the house of God.

“Selah”

The second “Selah” follows the sentence of judgment, giving it time to be weighed.

Study Notes

Four images of judgment. The verse uses four Hebrew verbs, each with its own picture: natats, “break down,” used of tearing down altars and buildings (Deuteronomy 12:3; Judges 6:30); hatah, “snatch up,” used of taking coals from a fire (Proverbs 6:27; Isaiah 30:14); nasah, “tear away” (Proverbs 2:22); and sharash, “uproot” (Job 31:8). Together they describe complete removal: the man is demolished, seized, torn from his home, and pulled up by the roots.

Doeg’s end. Scripture records nothing about Doeg after 1 Samuel 22. Later Jewish tradition offered accounts of his end, but they cannot be confirmed. The psalm’s announcement stands on God’s justice, not on a recorded outcome.

Psalm 52:6
Psalm 52:6
NKJV

Psalms 52:6 The righteous also shall see and fear, And shall laugh at him, saying,(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 52:6 The righteous will see it and be amazed.They will laugh and say, (NLT)

Main Idea

The righteous will see God’s judgment and respond with reverent fear. They will laugh as the wicked man’s arrogant confidence collapses, exposing the folly of boasting in human power while refusing to trust in God.

Verse Explanation

The righteous will see God’s judgment and respond with reverent fear. They will laugh as the wicked man’s arrogant confidence collapses, exposing the folly of boasting in human power while refusing to trust in God.

“The righteous also shall see and fear”

Those who belong to God will witness His judgment on the wicked man. “Fear” here is reverent awe toward God. When the righteous see God bring down the man who boasted in evil, they recognize that God sees what is done, judges it, and acts. The same judgment that removes the wicked man deepens the reverence of those who trust God.

“And shall laugh at him, saying,”

Their laughter responds to the collapse of the “mighty man” who boasted in evil (v. 1). He seemed untouchable, and now his confidence has been shown to be foolish. The righteous share God’s own view of such arrogance. Elsewhere the psalms describe God laughing at those who set themselves against Him, because He sees that their day is coming (Psalm 2:4; 37:13). “Saying” introduces the words of the righteous in verse 7, and those words focus on the man’s misplaced trust.

Study Notes

“See” and “fear.” In Hebrew the two verbs sound alike (yir’u, “they will see,” and yira’u, “they will fear”). The same pairing appears in Psalm 40:3: “Many will see it and fear, and will trust in the LORD.”

Laughter and Proverbs 24:17. Proverbs warns, “Do not rejoice when your enemy falls.” Psalm 52:6 describes a different response. The righteous are not settling a personal score; they are responding to God’s judgment, and their words in verse 7 address the folly of the man’s trust rather than his pain.

Psalm 52:7
Psalm 52:7
NKJV

Psalms 52:7 "Here is the man who did not make God his strength, But trusted in the abundance of his riches, And strengthened himself in his wickedness."(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 52:7 “Look what happens to mighty warriorswho do not trust in God.They trust their wealth insteadand grow more and more bold in their wickedness.” (NLT)

Main Idea

The righteous name the wicked man’s real failure: he did not make God his refuge. He trusted his wealth and drew strength from his wickedness, and his fall shows how empty every security apart from God is.

Verse Explanation

The righteous name the wicked man’s real failure: he did not make God his refuge. He trusted his wealth and drew strength from his wickedness, and his fall shows how empty every security apart from God is.

“Here is the man who did not make God his strength”

The righteous point to the fallen man as a lesson for all to see. The one called “mighty man” in verse 1 is now simply “the man.” “Strength” refers to a stronghold or place of refuge. His most basic failure was not his tongue, his violence, or his love of evil. It was that he never made God his refuge, and everything else in the psalm’s description follows from that.

“But trusted in the abundance of his riches”

Having refused God as his stronghold, the man relied on what he possessed. The psalm does not connect this line to a recorded detail of Doeg’s life, and it should not be forced into one. It describes the character of the self-confident man: he leaned on his resources as though they could hold him up.

The word “trusted” is significant. In verse 8 David uses the same verb: “I trust in the mercy of God.” Both men trust. What separates them is the object of their trust.

“And strengthened himself in his wickedness.”

Instead of drawing strength from God, the man made himself strong through wickedness. The word translated “wickedness” is the same word translated “destruction” in verse 2. The ruin he devised for others became the thing he relied on, and it could not hold him when God acted.

Study Notes

“The man.” The Hebrew word geber comes from the same root as gibbor, “mighty man,” in verse 1. The title of strength has been reduced to a plain word for a man.

“Wickedness” and “destruction.” The Hebrew hawwah appears in verse 2 (“destruction”) and here (“wickedness”). The ESV reads “sought refuge in his own destruction,” which can mean the ruin he caused others or the ruin he brought on himself. Both fit the psalm.

Trust in riches. Compare Psalm 49:6–7; 62:10; Proverbs 11:28; and 1 Timothy 6:17, where believers are told not to “trust in uncertain riches but in the living God.”

Psalm 52:8–9 — Flourishing Through God’s Mercy

Psalm 52:8
Psalm 52:8
NKJV

Psalms 52:8 But I am like a green olive tree in the house of God; I trust in the mercy of God forever and ever.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 52:8 But I am like an olive tree, thriving in the house of God.I will always trust in God’s unfailing love. (NLT)

Main Idea

David contrasts the uprooted wicked man with himself, a green olive tree flourishing in the house of God. His life is secure because he trusts forever in the mercy of God, the same enduring goodness he declared in verse 1.

Verse Explanation

David contrasts the uprooted wicked man with himself, a green olive tree flourishing in the house of God. His life is secure because he trusts forever in the mercy of God, the same enduring goodness he declared in verse 1.

“But I am like a green olive tree in the house of God”

“But I” marks the turn of the psalm. David sets himself against the man who will be uprooted (v. 5). Olive trees were among the most enduring trees in Israel. They stayed green through the year, bore fruit for generations, and supplied oil for food, light, and anointing. “Green” means flourishing and full of life. The wicked man will be pulled up by the roots, but David is rooted and thriving.

“In the house of God” refers to the place of God’s presence, the sanctuary where He met with His people. David wrote as a fugitive, so the phrase does not require that he was physically there, and it does not describe a literal tree growing in the sanctuary courts. The image speaks of his relationship with God. His life is rooted in God’s presence and sustained by His care.

“I trust in the mercy of God forever and ever.”

David names the source of his flourishing. “Trust” is the same verb used of the wicked man in verse 7. That man trusted his riches; David trusts the mercy of God. “Mercy” translates hesed, the same word rendered “goodness” in verse 1. The faithful love David declared at the beginning of the psalm is now the ground of his own security. “Forever and ever” answers the “forever” of verse 5. The wicked man’s removal is permanent, and so is David’s confidence in God.

Study Notes

The olive tree in Scripture. Olive trees can live for centuries and send up new shoots from old roots. Their oil fueled the lamp in the tabernacle (Exodus 27:20) and was used for anointing. Jeremiah 11:16 describes Israel as a “Green Olive Tree,” and Psalm 128:3 pictures children as “olive plants” around the family table.

Trees planted in God’s house. Psalm 92:12–14 uses similar language: “Those who are planted in the house of the LORD shall flourish in the courts of our God.” Compare Psalm 1:3. Whether trees actually grew in the sanctuary courts is uncertain, and the image does not depend on it.

Psalm 52:9
Psalm 52:9
NKJV

Psalms 52:9 I will praise You forever, Because You have done it; And in the presence of Your saints I will wait on Your name, for it is good.(NKJV)

NLT

Psalm 52:9 I will praise you forever, O God,for what you have done.I will trust in your good namein the presence of your faithful people. (NLT)

Main Idea

David vows to praise God forever because of what He has done. While waiting for God to act, he will continue trusting in His good name and openly expressing that confidence among God’s faithful people.

Verse Explanation

David vows to praise God forever because of what He has done. While waiting for God to act, he will continue trusting in His good name and openly expressing that confidence among God’s faithful people.

“I will praise You forever”

For the first time in the psalm, David speaks directly to God. He began by addressing the wicked man (v. 1), and he ends by addressing the LORD. “Praise” here means to give thanks and acknowledge God openly. The boasting man’s words will be cut off, but David’s praise will continue forever.

“Because You have done it”

David does not say exactly what God has done. The phrase may refer to what God has already accomplished, or it may express such certainty about what God will do that David speaks of it as done. Either way, the reason for praise is God’s action. David credits the outcome to Him.

“And in the presence of Your saints I will wait on Your name”

“Saints” refers to God’s faithful people, those who have received His faithful love and respond with loyalty to Him. David will not wait alone or in private. He will wait openly, in the company of the godly.

To “wait on” the LORD means to wait with expectant hope, trusting Him to act at the right time. God’s “name” stands for His revealed character, everything He has made known about Himself. David’s hope rests on who God has shown Himself to be.

“for it is good.”

God’s name is good because God Himself is good. He is exactly what He has revealed Himself to be. The wicked man loved evil more than good (v. 3), but David fixes his hope on the God whose name is good.

Study Notes

“Saints.” The Hebrew word hasidim is related to hesed, the word for God’s faithful love in verses 1 and 8. God’s saints are those marked by His faithful love and loyal to Him in return.

“Wait on Your name.” The verb qavah means to wait with expectation (Psalm 27:14; Isaiah 40:31). Some modern translations revise the Hebrew to read “proclaim your name” (NRSV), but the Hebrew text behind the NKJV is well supported and fits David’s settled trust.

“Good” in verses 3 and 9. The same Hebrew word (tov) appears in both verses. The man who loved evil more than good stands opposite the believer who waits on the name of the God who is good.
