Chapter Overview

At a Glance

Psalm 58
Main Idea:

Psalm 58 is David’s prayer for justice: it exposes those who judge corruptly, asks God to break the power that sustains their oppression, and ends with the confession that He judges in the earth.

Setting:

The title calls the psalm “A Michtam of David” set to “Do Not Destroy” (58:1), the setting shared by Psalms 57 and 59, which are tied to David’s flight from Saul. Psalm 58 names no event, but that persecution is a plausible background.

Chapter Outline:

  • Psalm 58:1–5 — Unjust Judges Exposed

  • Psalm 58:6–9 — A Prayer for God to Break the Power of the Wicked

  • Psalm 58:10–11 — Surely He Is God Who Judges in the Earth

Key Verse:

“So that men will say, ‘Surely there is a reward for the righteous; Surely He is God who judges in the earth.’” (Psalm 58:11, NKJV)

Go Deeper

Author

The title attributes Psalm 58 to David (58:1), and its heading “To the Chief Musician” made his prayer part of Israel’s public worship. The psalm gives no further personal detail.

Occasion

Psalm 58 names no event. Its neighbors share the setting “Do Not Destroy” and are tied in their titles to David’s flight from Saul (Psalms 57, 59), so the injustice he suffered under Saul and those who served him is a plausible background, though not a certain one. The psalm’s targets are ungodly enemies, corrupt rulers and those who enable their oppression, people who held the power to judge and used it to do harm.

Psalm Type

A prayer for justice, often grouped with the imprecatory psalms because it asks God to destroy the power of the wicked. It moves from injustice exposed (58:1–5) to divine intervention sought (58:6–9) to justice celebrated and confessed (58:10–11). “Michtam” is a title term of uncertain meaning, and “Do Not Destroy” is most likely the name of a tune or setting; neither fixes a date.

The Heart of the Chapter

Psalm 58 is governed by one concern: justice. It opens with a question aimed at people who hold the power to judge: “Do you indeed speak righteousness, you silent ones? Do you judge uprightly, you sons of men?” (58:1). The answer comes at once: “No, in heart you work wickedness; You weigh out the violence of your hands in the earth” (58:2). The scales that should measure out justice are being used to measure out violence. David then traces the evil to its root in poetic terms. The wicked go astray “as soon as they are born, speaking lies” (58:3), and like “the deaf cobra that stops its ear” (58:4) they refuse every voice that might correct them.

So David turns from the corrupt judges to the true Judge. “Break their teeth in their mouth, O God!” (58:6). He asks God to dismantle the power that makes injustice effective, and he leaves the doing to God. The images that follow show how quickly that power will fail when God acts: fangs broken, water running off into the ground, arrows that shatter, a snail melting away, and a whirlwind that sweeps the wicked off before a cooking pot can feel the heat (58:6–9). Their violence looked permanent, but before God it is fragile. When the righteous see “the vengeance” (58:10), what they see is God’s judicial action against evil, and the conclusion everyone draws is about Him: “Surely He is God who judges in the earth” (58:11). The closing verdict answers the opening failure.

The psalm also reaches beyond the human courtroom. Many translations read the opening address as “gods” or “mighty ones,” and read that way alongside Psalm 82, where God stands “in the congregation of the mighty” and judges among the gods (Psalm 82:1), Psalm 58 points to a second layer: corrupt spiritual rulers who stand behind human injustice. The two psalms share the same charge of unjust judgment, the same expectation that God will bring corrupt rulers down, and the same climax, God judging the earth (Psalm 82:8). Psalm 82 announces a sentence while Psalm 58 prays for one, but both say that no authority, earthly or spiritual, is beyond the reach of the God who judges.

Chapter Sections

Psalm 58:1–5 — Unjust Judges Exposed

David confronts those entrusted with judgment and exposes their hearts: they weigh out violence instead of justice. Their evil runs deep, and like a deaf cobra that will not heed the charmer, they shut their ears to correction.

Psalm 58:6–9 — A Prayer for God to Break the Power of the Wicked

David asks God to break the power that keeps oppression in place, as a lion’s fangs are broken. A string of vivid images shows that the strength of the wicked will drain away and vanish suddenly, swept off as by a whirlwind.

Psalm 58:10–11 — Surely He Is God Who Judges in the Earth

The righteous rejoice when God’s vengeance falls, a battlefield picture of His complete victory over evil, much like the praise for His true judgments in Revelation 19:1–2. The psalm closes with the public confession that God rewards the righteous and truly judges in the earth.

Why This Chapter Matters

Psalm 58 shows that God cares how power is used, and above all by those who judge. Injustice in high places is an offense against the God who judges in the earth (58:1–2, 11), and He will answer it, sometimes within history and fully at the last.

The psalm also gives the oppressed words to bring injustice before God and leave vengeance with Him (Romans 12:19–21). David’s severe language is a prayer, not a plan of action, and the joy it describes is joy in God’s justice, never pleasure in suffering (Proverbs 24:17–18). Read with Psalm 82, it reminds believers that injustice has spiritual roots as well as human ones, and that Christ has already disarmed principalities and powers (Colossians 2:15). So Christians can pray boldly for God to restrain evil and establish justice while still loving their enemies (Matthew 5:44).

Key Themes to Watch For

Justice as the aim. The failure of judgment at the start and the confession of God’s judgment at the end frame the whole psalm (58:1–2, 11).

Hardened refusal to listen. The wicked are like a deaf cobra that will not heed the charmer (58:3–5).

Judgment handed to God. David asks God to break the power of the wicked instead of acting himself (58:6–9).

Corrupt rule, human and spiritual. Read with Psalm 82, the psalm exposes both earthly rulers and the spiritual powers behind injustice (58:1; Psalm 82:1–8).

The certainty of God’s justice. The righteous are vindicated and God is confessed as Judge (58:10–11).

Key Words & Concepts

Michtam. A title term found in Psalm 16 and Psalms 56–60. Its meaning is uncertain; suggestions include an inscription or a carefully crafted poem (58:1).

“You silent ones.” The NKJV’s reading in 58:1. The Hebrew is disputed, and many translations read gods or mighty ones. On that reading the psalm, like Psalm 82, addresses spiritual rulers as well as the “sons of men” who serve injustice.

The deaf cobra. The psalm pictures a cobra that “stops its ear” and “will not heed the voice of charmers, charming ever so skillfully” (58:4–5). It is an image of people whose words do harm and who stubbornly refuse every voice of correction.

Vengeance. Hebrew naqam (58:10). Here it means God’s execution of justice: confronting evil, vindicating the righteous and punishing the wicked, not petty retaliation.

Verse 9. The Hebrew of 58:9 is difficult and translations differ. The NKJV’s image is of cooking pots not yet heated by burning thorns when God sweeps the wicked away, a picture of sudden judgment.

“Surely.” The word opens both lines of 58:11, stressing the certainty of the conclusion: the righteous will be rewarded, and God judges in the earth.
