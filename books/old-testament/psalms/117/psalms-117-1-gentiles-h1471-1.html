GENTILES - (gôwy, Strong's H1471)

Summary:

The Hebrew noun goy (plural goyim) fundamentally designates a socio-political entity, nation, ethnic body, or foreign people group ("Gentiles"). While the term can describe any national body, including Israel itself when viewed as a nation, in OT usage it very frequently refers to the collective non-Israelite nations outside the Mosaic covenant. In Psalm 117:1, goyim identifies the non-Israelite nations of the world who are directly commanded to join in praising Yahweh. By explicitly addressing the goyim, the psalmist expands the scope of divine worship beyond Israel's ethnic borders, establishing that God's covenant kindness and enduring truth are intended to evoke global praise from all foreign nations.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew noun goy refers to a distinct collective group of people defined by shared political structure, territorial boundaries, language, or lineage—most accurately rendered in English as "nation" or "people." In ancient Near Eastern contexts, human society was organized around distinct national groupings, each associated with specific lands, language dialects, and national cults.

Within the Hebrew Scriptures, goy operates alongside other collective terms such as 'am ("people"). While 'am frequently stresses relational or familial connection—often applied to Israel as God's chosen covenant folk—goy emphasizes political or ethnic nationhood. In the post-exilic period and canonical collection of the Psalms, the plural form goyim took on a distinct nuance, routinely denoting the foreign, surrounding non-Israelite peoples ("the Gentiles").

Psalm 117 forms part of the "Egyptian Hallel" (Psalms 113–118), a collection of praise psalms sung during major Jewish pilgrim festivals, particularly Passover. Set within this festal environment, the call for surrounding foreign nations (goyim) to praise Israel's God highlights the expansive, international horizon of divine redemption.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, goy denotes a cohesive national body or ethnic entity. Standard lexical resources divide its semantic range into three main contextual usages:

  • A Socio-Political Nation: A distinct political entity or geographical nation-state.

  • The Foreign Nations / Gentiles: Non-Israelite ethnic groups collectively considered outside the Mosaic covenant community.

  • Israel as a National Entity: Israel viewed specifically as a formal nation established by God among the nations of the earth.

Lexical vs. Contextual Layers: Lexically, goy is a neutral socio-political term for a national body. It does not inherently carry moral disapproval or hostileness. Theological distinctions—such as pagan idolaters versus the holy nation—are supplied by the surrounding biblical context, explicit modifiers, or broader canonical narrative, rather than by the bare lexical definition of the word itself.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, goy exhibits important contextual nuances depending on the genre and theological focus of the passage:

  • Genealogical and Historical Usage: In historical narrative, the plural goyim identifies the various geographic and linguistic divisions of post-Flood humanity (e.g., Genesis 10:5).

  • Wisdom and Psalmic Literature: In the Psalms, goyim is frequently positioned in contrast to Israel. In some settings, foreign nations mock Israel's invisible God or oppose His reign (e.g., Psalms 115:2), while in prophetic liturgy, these same nations are envisioned submitting to the righteous King and acknowledging Yahweh's global supremacy (e.g., Psalms 72:11).

  • Prophetic Usage: Prophetic texts utilize goyim both to describe foreign powers under God's sovereign historical judgment and as the recipients of future restoration and spiritual light (e.g., Ezekiel 20:14; Ezekiel 37:21).

4. Theological Implications

The biblical deployment of goy carries profound theological implications regarding the character of God and His redemptive plan:

  • Universal Sovereignty: Yahweh is not merely a localized or regional deity bound to one land; He exercises supreme authority over every earthly goy.

  • The Abrahamic Horizon: God's election of Abraham was never intended to terminate on Israel alone, but to bring blessing to all the goyim of the earth.

  • Redemptive Inclusion: The command for Gentiles to praise God demonstrates that divine mercy is designed to break across ethnic boundaries.

  • Canonical Fulfillment: In Romans 15:11, the Apostle Paul directly cites Psalm 117:1 to prove from the Hebrew Scriptures that God's long-declared purpose was for Gentiles (goyim) to glorify God for His mercy alongside Israel through Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 117:1 states: "Praise the LORD, all you Gentiles! Laud Him, all you peoples!"

The opening verse establishes a classic poetic parallelism:

  • Line A: "Praise the LORD, all you Gentiles [goyim]!"

  • Line B: "Laud Him, all you peoples ['ummim]!"

The target word goyim functions as the primary vocative subject of the imperative command to praise Yahweh (halelu 'et-YHWH). The grammatically plural, masculine form explicit in the text emphasizes that this command is directed toward a multiplicity of foreign nations simultaneously.

The immediate structural context connects verse 1 directly to verse 2, which supplies the explicit causal basis for the command: "For His merciful kindness [chesed] is great toward us, and the truth ['emet] of the LORD endures forever."

Herein lies the striking contextual nuance of the word goyim in this passage: the non-covenant nations are commanded to praise Yahweh because of the extraordinary covenant faithfulness (chesed and 'emet) He displays toward His covenant people ("toward us"). Yahweh's covenant fidelity to Israel is presented not as a private national secret, but as a public, globally transformative reality that demands the worship and praise of every Gentile nation on earth.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 10:5 — "From these the coastland peoples of the Gentiles were separated into their lands, everyone according to his language, according to their families, into their nations."

  • Insight: Shows the foundational post-Flood division of humanity into distinct geographic, linguistic, and ethnic groups.

  • Psalms 72:11 — "Yes, all kings shall fall down before Him; All nations shall serve Him."

  • Insight: Highlights the royal messianic expectation where every foreign nation eventually submits to and serves the promised Davidic King.

  • Psalms 115:2 — "Why should the Gentiles say, 'So where is their God?'"

  • Insight: Illustrates the ongoing tension where foreign nations question Israel's invisible God, a tension resolved when those same nations are called to praise Him in Psalm 117.

  • Ezekiel 37:21 — "Then say to them, 'Thus says the Lord GOD: "Surely I will take the children of Israel from among the nations, wherever they have gone, and will gather them from every side and bring them into their own land..."'"

  • Insight: Uses the word to denote the foreign lands of exile from which Yahweh promises to gather and restore His scattered people.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Structural Significance: Psalm 117 is famous as the shortest psalm in the Psalter (consisting of only two verses), yet it possesses one of the broadest theological horizons in all of Scripture, encompassing the entire Gentile world in a universal call to praise.

  • Festal Liturgy: As part of the Hallel chanted during Passover, this reference to the goyim reminded Israel during her most nationalist celebration that the ultimate goal of her deliverance from Egypt was the cosmic expansion of Yahweh's glory to all nations.
