LAUD - (shâbach, Strong's H7623)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shabach means to praise, laud, commend, or extol. In Psalm 117:1, shabach is rendered "Laud" and functions as an imperative command issued to all non-Israelite nations and peoples to publicly praise YHWH. Within this short psalm, the word serves to expand the scope of worship beyond Israel's borders, demonstrating that all humanity is called to extol the LORD on the grounds of His lovingkindness and enduring truth.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root shabach is a Hebrew verb used in Old Testament poetry and wisdom literature to express vocal commendation, praise, and honor. Psalm 117, though the shortest chapter in the Psalter, possesses vast canonical significance. Placed toward the conclusion of the Egyptian Hallel (Psalms 113–118)—a collection sung during major biblical feasts—Psalm 117 issues a global summons to praise.

In the ancient Near Eastern context, nations typically praised only their own local deities. Psalm 117 dramatically overturns this localized view of worship by commanding all Gentile nations (goyim) and all peoples (ummin) to laud Israel's God, YHWH. The inclusion of shabach alongside halal ("praise") in verse 1 underscores the universality of God's sovereign worthiness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, shabach is used in two main senses in the Old Testament text:

  • To praise, laud, commend, or boast in (e.g., Ps 63:3; Ps 117:1; Ps 145:4; Eccl 8:15).

  • To still, calm, or pacify (e.g., Ps 65:7; Ps 89:9; Prov 29:11).

In contexts concerning worship and social commendation, shabach signifies speaking highly of a person or God, publicly proclaiming someone's noble deeds, character, or excellence.

It is essential to distinguish between these semantic layers. The secondary sense of "stilling" or "stilling waves/anger" belongs to a distinct context and must not be imported into praise contexts. In Psalm 117:1, the lexical meaning belongs entirely to the first category: to offer vocal praise and commendation to God.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

In Hebrew poetic structures, shabach frequently works in parallel with other praise verbs, such as halal ("praise") or yadah ("give thanks"). While halal often carries a connotation of exuberant celebration, shabach frequently highlights the act of commending worth and speaking words of praise.

Regarding its grammatical form in Psalm 117:1, the authoritative morphology establishes the word as:

  • Form: imperative

  • Gender: masculine

  • Number: plural

The imperative form turns the statement into a direct command, while the plural number addresses the multitude of Gentile nations. Rather than merely describing potential worship, the text issues an authoritative summons to the broad array of non-Jewish nations to direct their commendation to YHWH.

4. Theological Implications

The command for Gentiles to shabach YHWH carries major canonical and redemptive implications:

  • Universal Sovereignty: God is not merely a regional deity of Israel; He is the sovereign Creator worthy of praise from every nation and language group on Earth.

  • Gentile Inclusion: Long before the New Testament era, the Old Testament Psalter established that the end goal of God's redemptive work includes non-Israelite nations joining in God's praise.

  • New Testament Fulfillment: Apostle Paul explicitly cites Psalm 117:1 in Romans 15:11 ("And again: 'Praise the LORD, all you Gentiles! Laud Him, all you peoples!'") to prove that the reception of Gentiles into the church through Jesus Christ fulfills the Old Testament scriptural promises.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 117 consists of two brief verses structured as a summons followed by its grounding cause:

  • Verse 1 (The Summons): "Praise the LORD, all you Gentiles! Laud [shabach] Him, all you peoples!"

  • Verse 2 (The Cause): "For His merciful kindness is great toward us, and the truth of the LORD endures forever. Praise the LORD!"

The word shabach in verse 1 forms the second half of a poetic parallelism paired with halal ("praise"). The immediate sentence sets up a direct call-and-response dynamic: the nations are commanded to laud God (shabach), and verse 2 supplies the explicit reason why they must do so.

The basis for Gentile praise is God's "merciful kindness" (chesed, His steadfast covenant love) and His "truth" ('emet, His enduring faithfulness). The passage establishes that God's faithful love to His people overflows in such a way that foreign nations are affected and compelled to laud Him. The word shabach here represents the vocal, verbal honoring that all peoples owe to the faithful Creator.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Psalms 63:3 — "Because Your lovingkindness is better than life, my lips shall praise You." Here, shabach (rendered "praise") expresses the psalm writer's verbal response to God's lovingkindness.

  • Psalms 145:4 — "One generation shall praise Your works to another, and shall declare Your mighty acts." In this passage, shabach (rendered "praise") refers to passing down public commendation of God's deeds across generations.

  • Psalms 147:12 — "Praise the LORD, O Jerusalem! Praise your God, O Zion!" Here, shabach (rendered "Praise") serves as a parallel command calling God's holy city to glorify Him.

  • Ecclesiastes 8:15 — "So I commended enjoyment, because a man has nothing better under the sun than to eat, drink, and be merry..." In this wisdom context, shabach (rendered "commended") describes recommending or praising a particular course of life under God's providence.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Care must be taken not to blend distinct lexical senses of shabach. While the root can mean "to still" or "to calm" in contexts involving raging seas (Psalm 89:9) or anger (Proverbs 29:11), its use in Psalm 117:1 strictly denotes praise and commendation. The text of Psalm 117:1 does not carry the imagery of quieting or pacifying, but focuses entirely on active, vocal extolling of God's name.

The brevity of Psalm 117 reinforces its focused message. By stripping away lengthy narrative context, the psalm presents a pure, concentrated call to global worship, highlighting that the ultimate destination of God's redemptive truth is universal praise.
