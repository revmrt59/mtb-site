LORD - (YHWH, Strong's H3068)

Summary:

The Hebrew word Yahweh (rendered "Lord" in the NKJV) is the proper personal name of the God of Israel. Lexically, it functions as a proper noun distinguishing Israel's God from all false deities, historically connected with the Hebrew root for being or existence. In Psalm 117:1, Yahweh serves as the explicit object of praise directed to all Gentile nations and peoples. By invoking this specific covenant name in a universal summons to worship, the psalmist declares that the God who revealed Himself to Israel is the supreme God over all earth's nations, calling every people group to acknowledge His sovereign rule and redemptive grace.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The target word is the personal divine name of the God of Israel, written in the Hebrew Bible with the four consonants Y-H-W-H (the Tetragrammaton). In authoritative grammatical analysis, the term functions as a proper noun. As a proper name, it identifies a unique Person rather than expressing an abstract quality or general divine title.

The historical background of the name is grounded in God's revelation to Moses at the burning bush in Exodus, where God connects His personal name with the verb of existence ("I AM WHO I AM"). While rooted in the Hebrew concept of being, the name functions in Old Testament narrative and worship not as a philosophical category, but as the distinct personal name by which God made Himself known to His covenant people.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, Yahweh is the proper personal name of God. It is crucial to distinguish between the bare lexical definition of the proper name and the expansive theological truths revealed about God across the biblical canon:

  • Layer 1 (Lexical Meaning): A proper noun designating the personal identity of the God of Israel.

  • Layer 2 (Immediate Context): In praise literature, the name identifies the specific deity who commands the allegiance and worship of the worshipers.

  • Layer 3 (Canonical Significance): Across redemptive history, God reveals His self-existence, eternal presence, faithfulness, sovereignty, and righteousness in connection with this name.

While standard lexicons connect the name etymologically to the root hayah ("to be" or "to exist"), the lexical entry itself is simply the proper name of God. The rich theological doctrines of immutability, covenant love, and absolute sovereignty are taught by the context of God's actions and speeches throughout Scripture, rather than contained entirely within the dictionary definition of the word itself.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Throughout the Hebrew Old Testament, Yahweh is used when emphasizing God's personal, covenantal relationship with His creation and His people.

In ancient Jewish tradition, out of deep reverence for the sacred name, readers substituted the title Adonai ("Lord") when reading the text aloud. English translation tradition reflects this practice by rendering Yahweh as "LORD" in capital or small capital letters.

The term appears across every genre of Old Testament literature:

  • In narrative, it marks God's personal intervention in human history.

  • In legal texts, it establishes the divine authority behind the covenant commandments.

  • In prophetic literature, it introduces divine declarations with solemn authority.

  • In poetic and liturgical texts, such as the Psalms, it designates the recipient of Israel's worship, prayer, and Thanksgiving.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of Yahweh rests on God's self-revelation. By giving His personal name to Israel, God demonstrated His willingness to enter into a real, binding covenant relationship with human beings.

In the context of Old Testament theology, Yahweh is not merely one localized deity among many national gods. Though He chose Israel as His covenant people, the Scriptures consistently reveal that Yahweh is the Creator of heaven and earth and the Lord of all nations. The theological movement of the Psalter frequently expands the scope of praise from Israel to all the earth, anticipating a day when all nations acknowledge the true God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 117 is the shortest psalm in the Psalter, consisting of only two verses, yet it carries immense theological significance. Verse 1 presents a universal command: "Praise the Lord, all you Gentiles! Laud Him, all you peoples!"

In this passage, the proper noun Yahweh ("Lord") is the direct object of the imperative to praise. The surrounding context highlights two critical nuances:

  • Universal Scope: The psalmist addresses "all you Gentiles" (goyim) and "all you peoples" (ummim). Rather than limiting the worship of Yahweh to the ethnic boundaries of Israel, the passage commands foreign nations to offer praise to Israel's personal God.

  • Covenant Grounding: Psalm 117:2 immediately provides the reason for this universal praise: "For His merciful kindness is great toward us, and the truth of the Lord endures forever." The Gentile nations are called to praise Yahweh because His covenant mercy (chesed) and enduring truth ('emet) demonstrated toward His people reveal His true character to the entire world.

By using the specific proper name Yahweh rather than a generic term for deity, the psalmist insists that true worship among all nations must be directed to the one true God who revealed Himself in covenant relationship with Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 2:4 — "This is the history of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the Lord God made the earth and the heavens." In this foundational narrative, "Lord" is paired with "God" (Elohim) to introduce the personal God who creates humanity and enters into relationship with creation.

  • Psalms 24:10 — "Who is this King of glory? The Lord of hosts, He is the King of glory." In this liturgical psalm of entrance, "Lord" identifies the supreme ruler and commander of the heavenly armies.

  • Psalms 103:20 — "Bless the Lord, you His angels, who excel in strength, who do His word, heeding the voice of His word." In this hymn of praise, the psalmist invokes "Lord" as he summons heavenly beings to join in praising the covenant God.

  • Isaiah 43:16 — "Thus says the Lord, who makes a way in the sea and a path through the mighty waters." In prophetic discourse, "Lord" introduces God's authoritative declaration reminding His people of His power to deliver them as He did at the Exodus.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The canonical trajectory of Psalm 117:1 reaches its climax in the New Testament. In Romans 15:11, the Apostle Paul explicitly quotes Psalm 117:1 ("Praise the Lord, all you Gentiles! Laud Him, all you peoples!") as scriptural proof that God's eternal plan always included the redemption and inclusion of the Gentiles. Paul demonstrates that through Jesus Christ, the Gentiles glorify God for His mercy, fulfilling the psalmist's ancient summons to praise Yahweh.

Grammatically, the target word in Psalm 117:1 functions simply as a proper noun. The text does not build its argument on grammatical complexity, but on the startling directness of its command: all nations are summoned to praise the specific, named God of Israel.
