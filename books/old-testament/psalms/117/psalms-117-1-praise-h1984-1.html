PRAISE - (hâlal, Strong's H1984)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb halal fundamentally denotes boasting, extolling, commending, or making a clear, festive demonstration of honor. In its primary religious expression, it refers to vocal exultation and worship offered to God. In Psalm 117:1, the word appears as a direct command requiring all Gentile nations to exult in and publicly celebrate Yahweh. By employing this word, the psalmist summons non-Israelite nations into joyful, vocal praise, grounded in the universal reach of God’s covenantal mercy and enduring truth.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew root halal carries a broad semantic range in classical biblical Hebrew. At its basic level, the root expresses the idea of commending, exulting, or boasting. Depending on context, its usage ranges from everyday social commendation to the highest corporate acts of worshipping Yahweh. In the cultic life of ancient Israel, halal became one of the central verbs used to call the congregation to praise and glorify God.

In the poetic and liturgical literature of the Old Testament, particularly within the Book of Psalms, halal serves as a primary term for corporate adoration. It forms the verbal root of the liturgical formula Hallelu-Yah ("Praise the LORD"). Within the Psalter, the term functions repeatedly as an imperative call to worship, summoning God's people—and ultimately all creation—to respond to God's character and mighty works with audible exaltation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, halal encompasses several distinct but related senses across Hebrew literature:

  • To praise, extol, or magnify (especially in liturgical contexts where Yahweh is the object).

  • To boast, glory, or pride oneself in someone or something (whether positively in Yahweh or negatively in self, wealth, or false gods).

  • To commend or speak favorably of a person or quality.

  • In specific poetic contexts, to shine or emit light, though in the vast majority of biblical occurrences it refers to verbal commendation or praise.

When God is the object of halal, the word signifies an outward, vocal declaration of worship. It denotes more than a quiet internal attitude; it describes an articulate, publicly expressed honor rendered to God for His supreme attributes and righteous deeds.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament genres, halal functions in several distinct settings:

  • Liturgy and Corporate Worship: It frequently serves as a direct command inviting the congregation to exalt Yahweh (e.g., Psalms 106:48; 113:9; 135:1). In these settings, halal demands visible, audible exultation during public worship gatherings.

  • Proper Boasting versus Unrighteous Pride: The verb can describe human vanity or self-exaltation, as well as the derision of enemies (e.g., Psalms 102:8). Conversely, when properly oriented, halal describes a believer boasting in the LORD rather than in human wisdom, power, or wealth (e.g., Psalms 34:2; Jeremiah 9:23).

  • Social Commendation: The root is also used in non-religious contexts to describe commending human virtue or standing, such as Pharaoh's court officials commending Sarah's beauty (Genesis 12:15) or a family commending a virtuous woman (Proverbs 31:28).

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of halal rests on the obligation and privilege of creation to acknowledge God's absolute majesty. Praise in the Old Testament is the fitting, expected response of the creature to the Creator.

When directed toward God, halal highlights several key truths:

  • God's Absolute Worthiness: Praise is anchored in who God is and what He has accomplished in history.

  • Public Witness: Halal requires external declaration, making God's glory known among the worshipping community and before the surrounding nations.

  • Reorientation of Human Boasting: Human beings naturally tend to boast. The biblical imperative to halal Yahweh reorients this impulse away from human achievements and grounds it exclusively in knowing and magnifying God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 117 is the shortest psalm in the Psalter, yet its theological horizon is vast. Verse 1 opens with an invitation: "Praise the LORD, all you Gentiles! Laud Him, all you peoples!" The target word halal is expressed as a masculine plural imperative, functioning as a direct command issued to all non-Israelite nations ("Gentiles").

In this immediate passage, the choice of halal provides vital contextual nuances:

  • Universal Scope: The psalm does not restrict the call to worship Yahweh to Israel. By addressing "all you Gentiles" with an imperative to halal, the psalmist reaches beyond national boundaries, summoning all the peoples of the earth to join in vocal exultation of Israel's God.

  • Rationale for Gentile Praise: Verse 2 supplies the explicit reason for this universal command: "For His merciful kindness is great toward us, and the truth of the LORD endures forever." The Gentile nations are commanded to praise Yahweh because His covenant mercy (chesed) and truth ('emet) extend to encompass all humanity.

  • Redemptive-Historical Significance: The call for Gentiles to halal Yahweh anticipates the inclusion of non-Jewish nations into the worship of the one true God—a reality cited by the Apostle Paul in Romans 15:11 to demonstrate that the global mission to the Gentiles fulfills Old Testament scripture.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 12:15 — "The princes of Pharaoh also saw her and commended her to Pharaoh. And the woman was taken to Pharaoh’s house."

  • Psalms 34:2 — "My soul shall make its boast in the LORD; The humble shall hear of it and be glad."

  • Psalms 56:10 — "In God (I will praise His word), In the LORD (I will praise His word),"

  • Jeremiah 9:23 — "Thus says the LORD: 'Let not the wise man glory in his wisdom, Let not the mighty man glory in his might, Nor let the rich man glory in his riches;'"

  • Psalms 150:6 — "Let everything that has breath praise the LORD. Praise the LORD!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew poetry, halal frequently appears alongside other worship verbs, such as shabach ("laud, praise"), which occurs in the parallel line of Psalm 117:1 ("Laud Him, all you peoples!"). This poetic pairing emphasizes the thoroughness and vigor with which honor is to be rendered to God.

Additionally, the well-known worship expression Hallelujah is a direct compound formed from the plural imperative of halal joined to the abbreviated divine name Yah. Whenever this term is spoken or sung, it echoes the imperative command of Psalm 117:1, calling all hearers to direct their exultation exclusively to Yahweh.
