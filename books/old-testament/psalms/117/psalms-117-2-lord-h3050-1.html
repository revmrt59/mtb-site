LORD - (Yâhh, Strong's H3050)

Summary:

The Hebrew proper noun yah is a shortened, poetic form of the covenant name YHWH (Yahweh), identifying the personal, divine Lord of Israel. Lexically, it functions as a proper divine name rather than a generic title for God, appearing most frequently in exultant poetry, songs of deliverance, and liturgical invocations. In Psalm 117:2, yah appears within the closing liturgical call to worship ("Praise the LORD" / halelu-yah), anchoring the universal praise of all Gentile nations in the distinct covenant identity of Israel's God, whose lovingkindness and truth endure forever.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The noun yah serves in Old Testament Hebrew as a poetic abbreviation of the tetragrammaton (YHWH). Grammatically, the term is a masculine proper noun. It is prominently featured in Israel's hymnody, particularly within the Psalter, as well as in ancient songs of victory celebrating God's redemptive interventions.

Rather than replacing the full covenant name, yah operates as an exultatory title that frequently condenses the divine identity into a memorable liturgical refrain. It forms the second element in the compound praise formula halelu-yah ("Praise Yah"), an imperative phrase directed to the worshiping congregation. Canonically, the term is especially concentrated in post-exilic worship texts and the Hallel psalms (Psalms 113–118), which were recited during Israel's major pilgrimage festivals.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, yah denotes the personal divine name of the God of Israel. Standard Hebrew lexicons (such as Brown-Driver-Briggs and the Theological Wordbook of the Old Testament) classify yah as an independent noun that functions synonymously with YHWH in poetic contexts.

  • Layer 1 (Lexical Meaning): A proper noun identifying the unique, self-existent God of Israel. It does not mean "god" in a general or generic sense (like 'el or 'elohim), but specifically designates the covenant God who revealed Himself to Moses and redeemed Israel from bondage.

  • Layer 2 (Poetic/Liturgical Usage): Within Hebrew poetry, yah frequently stands in parallelism with YHWH or 'elohim, emphasizing God's personal presence, saving strength, and worthiness of concentrated praise.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament hymnody and narrative poetry, yah carries several distinct contextual nuances:

  • Divine Strength and Deliverance: Yah often appears when God's people celebrate physical or spiritual salvation, portraying God as an active Deliverer who intervenes on behalf of the afflicted.

  • Liturgical Exultation: In the Psalms, yah is repeatedly embedded in corporate calls to worship, serving as the focal point of congregational gratitude and praise.

  • Prayer in Distress: In individual and communal laments, calling upon yah appeals directly to God's established covenant commitment when human help fails.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of yah rests upon its identity as a covenant name. While ancient Near Eastern nations worshiped regional deities, Israel praised yah as the sovereign Lord over all the earth.

Theologically, the use of yah links redemption directly to doxology. God does not merely deliver His people for their comfort; He delivers them so that His name may be exalted among the nations. Canonically, the invitation to praise yah expands beyond Israel's borders. The Psalms systematically present yah as the rightful recipient of global worship, anticipating the canonical fulfillment wherein Gentiles join Israel in magnifying the one true God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 117 is a concise hymn composed of two verses. Verse 1 issues a direct command to all Gentiles and nations to praise the LORD (YHWH). Verse 2 provides the objective basis for this universal command: "For His merciful kindness [chesed] is great toward us, And the truth ['emet] of the LORD endures forever." The psalm then culminates in the exultant summary phrase containing yah: "Praise the LORD!" (halelu-yah).

In this immediate setting, the term yah contributes a crucial covenantal foundation to the psalmist's argument:

  • Rooting Global Praise in Covenant Mercy: The psalmist demands that all Gentile nations praise yah because of what God has demonstrated toward His covenant people ("toward us"). God's covenant loyalty (chesed) and enduring truth ('emet) revealed to Israel are the ultimate proof of His character, making Him worthy of global adoration.

  • Structural Bookends: The psalm opens with a call for international praise and closes with the direct invocation of yah. This structure establishes that the praise of the nations is not directed toward an unknown deity, but specifically toward yah, the God of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Exodus 15:2 — "The LORD is my strength and song, And He has become my salvation..." Here the NKJV Span "Lord" translates yah in the Song of the Sea, where Israel exults in God's historical triumph over Pharaoh at the Red Sea.

  • Psalms 68:4 — "Sing to God, sing praises to His name; Extol Him who rides on the clouds, By His name YAH, And rejoice before Him." In this verse, the NKJV Span "Yah" explicitly highlights the divine name as the focal point of joyful liturgical exultation.

  • Psalms 118:5 — "I called on the LORD in distress; The LORD answered me and set me in a broad place." The NKJV Span "Lord" here represents yah in a personal testimony of deliverance, demonstrating that the name was invoked in moments of intense personal crisis.

  • Psalms 130:3 — "If You, LORD, should mark iniquities, O Lord, who could stand?" In this penitential psalm, the NKJV Span "Lord" translates yah to emphasize that if the covenant God rendered strict judgment without mercy, no human being could endure.

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • New Testament Quotation: Apostle Paul explicitly quotes Psalm 117:1 in Romans 15:11 ("And again: 'Praise the LORD, all you Gentiles! Laud Him, all you peoples!'") to prove that the inclusion of Gentiles into the body of Christ was the deliberate, promised climax of God's redemptive plan.

  • Historical Liturgical Setting: As the shortest psalm within the Egyptian Hallel collection (Psalms 113–118), Psalm 117 and its concluding invocation of yah formed part of the traditional hymn sung at the Passover meal. Consequently, this passage and its call to praise yah were on the lips of Jesus and His disciples in the Upper Room prior to His crucifixion (Matthew 26:30).
