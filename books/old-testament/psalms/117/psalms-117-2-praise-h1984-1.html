PRAISE - (hâlal, Strong's H1984)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb halal fundamentally refers to boasting, commending, shining, or offering loud, enthusiastic praise. In secular and general contexts, it describes boasting of human achievements or praising human beauty and status, while in worship contexts, it represents the vocal, celebratory extolling of God's character and deeds. In Psalm 117:2, halal appears as a direct command to praise YHWH. It functions as the emphatic conclusion to the psalm's global summons, calling all nations and peoples to respond with exuberant praise because God's covenant mercy and enduring truth extend to the whole world.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The root halal is one of the primary Hebrew terms used to express praise and celebration in the Old Testament. Etymologically, the root is connected to the physical idea of shining or radiating light, a nuance preserved in passages dealing with heavenly bodies or flashes of light. From this physical sense of shining, the verb developed the metaphorical sense of making something visible or prominent through speech—hence boasting, commending, or offering public praise.

In ancient Near Eastern culture and Hebrew social life, public commendation was a central means of honoring individuals, kings, or deities. The term halal reflects an energetic, outward expression of honor rather than quiet, internal contemplation. When directed toward human subjects, it can denote secular commendation or vanity; when directed toward God, it serves as the foundational liturgical imperative in Israel's worship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, halal encompasses several distinct shades of meaning depending on the context:

  • To shine or flash light (in physical descriptions).

  • To boast, glory, or make a show (which can carry either a negative sense of foolish arrogance or a positive sense of holy boasting in the LORD).

  • To commend or speak highly of a person's attributes or actions.

  • To praise, extol, or celebrate God with vocal jubilation.

It is important to distinguish between the core lexical meaning of halal and the theological weight it carries in worship contexts. Lexically, the word itself simply means to commend, boast, or extol. The ultimate value, holiness, and depth of the praise expressed by halal derive from its Object—YHWH—and the covenantal context in which the praise is offered, rather than from an intrinsic theological definition within the word itself.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The biblical usage of halal demonstrates a wide semantic range across different contexts:

  • Secular Commendation: The word is used when human beings are praised for their physical beauty or courtly status, as when Pharaoh's princes commended Sarai.

  • Negative Boasting or Foolishness: The term can describe arrogant boasting or mad conduct, as well as the taunts of enemies who deride the afflicted.

  • Sacred Boasting: In contrast to self-exaltation, the faithful are instructed to let their soul boast exclusively in God, renouncing pride in human wisdom, strength, or wealth.

  • Liturgical Extolling: The primary theological usage occurs in worship, where the verb commands or describes vocal praise to God for His attributes, creation, and saving works. It forms the verbal root of the liturgical exclamation Hallelu-Yah ("Praise YHWH").

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of halal in the Old Testament rests on the nature of God as the only legitimate object of ultimate glory. Because human boasting in self, strength, or wisdom is fundamentally foolish, halal shifts the human impulse to boast away from creaturely accomplishments and redirects it entirely toward YHWH.

Furthermore, biblical praise is never presented as an optional emotional outburst, but as the appropriate creaturely response to God's revelation. God's mighty acts of deliverance and His enduring covenant character demand a public, audible response. When the psalms command halal, they proclaim that God's glory is not to be kept hidden, but declared openly before the congregation and the surrounding nations.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 117:2, halal occurs in the grammatical form of a masculine plural imperative. Syntactically, this imperative directly addresses the plural audience introduced in verse 1—all nations (goyim) and all peoples (ummim). The masculine plural imperative functions as a universal command, instructing the Gentile world to join Israel in active praise.

Psalm 117 is the shortest psalm in the Psalter, yet it carries immense canonical weight. The psalm consists of a structural call to praise in verse 1, followed by the grounds for praise in verse 2a ("For His merciful kindness is great toward us, and the truth of the LORD endures forever"), and concludes with the final imperative in verse 2b ("Praise the LORD!").

The placement of halal at the end of verse 2 creates an emphatic doxological bookend for the psalm. The author uses this word to demand that the Gentile nations actively acknowledge YHWH. The rationale provided is God's chesed (merciful kindness) and emet (truth/faithfulness). The author demonstrates that God's covenant loyalty to His people has global implications, making it fitting for all earth's inhabitants to boast in YHWH. In New Testament theology, Paul explicitly quotes Psalm 117:1 in Romans 15:11 to demonstrate that the inclusion of the Gentiles into the praise of God was the intended goal of redemptive history.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Genesis 12:15 — "The princes of Pharaoh also saw her and commended her to Pharaoh. And the woman was taken to Pharaoh’s house."

  • Psalms 34:2 — "My soul shall make its boast in the LORD; The humble shall hear of it and be glad."

  • Psalms 150:6 — "Let everything that has breath praise the LORD. Praise the LORD!"

  • Jeremiah 9:23 — "Thus says the LORD: 'Let not the wise man glory in his wisdom, Let not the mighty man glory in his might, Nor let the rich man glory in his riches...'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

  • Liturgical Setting: Psalm 117 forms part of the Hallel collection (Psalms 113–118), which was traditionally recited during the major Jewish feasts, particularly the Passover meal. Consequently, this call to halal would have been sung by Jesus and His disciples in the Upper Room prior to His crucifixion.

  • The Compound Hallelu-Yah: The common transliteration "Hallelujah" is a direct rendering of the Hebrew imperative hallelu (masculine plural imperative of halal) combined with Yah (a shortened form of the divine name YHWH). It literally translates as "Praise Yahweh, all of you."
