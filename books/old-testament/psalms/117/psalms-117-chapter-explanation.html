Verse Explanation

The shortest psalm in the Bible opens with a call for every nation and people on earth, not just Israel, to praise the LORD. Paul later quotes this verse to show that God always planned for the Gentiles to join in worshiping Him through Christ.

"Praise the LORD, all you Gentiles!"

The Hebrew verb translated "Praise" is the same one found in "Hallelujah," and it means to praise joyfully and openly. "The LORD" stands for God's personal name, the name He revealed to Moses (Exodus 3:14–15) and tied to the promises He made to Israel. So this is not a call to honor some general idea of a god. It is a call to worship Israel's own God by name.

What makes the call remarkable is who receives it. "Gentiles" translates a Hebrew word for nations, used for the peoples of the world outside Israel. These were the nations that bowed to idols and often fought against God's people. Yet this psalm, sung in Israel's worship, invites every one of them to praise the LORD. That fits what God told Abraham at the very beginning: "And in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3). God set Israel apart, but He never meant His glory to stay within Israel's borders. This shortest psalm in the Bible makes that point in its very first words.

"Laud Him, all you peoples!"

"Laud" is a less common word for praise that carries the idea of speaking well of someone, commending and honoring Him out loud. "Peoples" is another word for the nations. Hebrew poetry often states a thought and then repeats it in different words, and here the repetition stresses that no nation is left out. Every people on earth is called to join in.

Centuries later, the apostle Paul quoted this verse word for word in Romans 15:11. He was writing to a church made up of both Jewish and Gentile believers, urging them to "receive one another, just as Christ also received us, to the glory of God" (Romans 15:7). To show that Gentiles belonged in God's family, Paul gathered several Old Testament passages, including this one, to prove that God had always intended for "the Gentiles [to] glorify God for His mercy" (Romans 15:9). Gentiles worshiping the God of Israel through Christ was not a new idea or an afterthought. Israel had been singing about it all along.

Study Notes

Psalm 117 belongs to a group of six psalms, Psalms 113–118, that Jewish tradition calls the Hallel, from the Hebrew word for "praise." It is often called the "Egyptian Hallel" because these psalms celebrate God's rescue of Israel from Egypt (Psalm 114 retells the Exodus directly). By New Testament times these psalms were sung at Israel's great festivals, including Passover. Early Jewish sources describe the Passover meal with the Hallel divided in two: the first part sung before the meal and the rest, which includes Psalm 117, sung after it.

That custom gives added depth to Matthew 26:30, which says of Jesus and His disciples after the Last Supper, "And when they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives." The Gospels do not name the hymn. Still, many interpreters reasonably conclude that it was the closing portion of the Hallel. If so, on the night before His death Jesus sang this call for all nations to praise the LORD, along with Psalm 118's words about the rejected stone becoming the chief cornerstone. This cannot be proved, but it fits what is known of Passover practice in that period.

Spirit & Life

[Discovery] Singing to People Who Weren’t in the Room. Israel sang this psalm in Jerusalem, in Hebrew, at the temple. The Gentiles it addresses were not there. They did not know the LORD, could not understand the words, and were bowing to other gods. Yet Israel called out to them anyway: “Praise the LORD, all you Gentiles!” It was like Ezekiel preaching to bones that could not yet hear (Ezekiel 37:4), or God calling “those things which do not exist as though they did” (Romans 4:17). Real worship does more than look back at what God has done. It leans forward into what He has promised. Every time God’s people praise Him, they are announcing to a world that isn’t listening yet what that world was made for.

[Application] Jonah Knew the Same Mercy and Wanted It Fenced. Israel had every human reason to keep its praise private. The nations had enslaved them in Egypt, raided them in the days of the judges, and threatened them in every generation. Yet the song Israel sang at Passover throws the doors open to those same peoples. Jonah knew the very mercy this psalm celebrates: “I know that You are a gracious and merciful God… abundant in lovingkindness” (Jonah 4:2). That is exactly why he was angry. He did not want it reaching Nineveh. It is possible to love God’s mercy for ourselves and resent it for others. The test of whether we understand grace is whether we want our enemies to have it too.

[Application] A Prejudiced Heart Is Not God’s Heart. The psalm says “all” twice, “all you Gentiles” and “all you peoples,” and the two words together leave no nation, tribe, language, or clan outside the call. That matches the promise to Abraham, “in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed” (Genesis 12:3), and the redeemed in heaven, who come “out of every tribe and tongue and people and nation” (Revelation 5:9). We can rejoice that God has welcomed us while quietly keeping others at a distance. A prejudiced heart is not God’s heart. A church that sings “all you peoples” on Sunday should look like it means it on Monday, in who it welcomes, honors, and makes room for.

[Insight] The Psalm Paul Used to Heal a Divided Church. Paul quotes Psalm 117:1 in Romans 15:11, and the surprising thing is where he quotes it. Romans 14–15 is not a missions sermon. It is counsel to a church quarreling over food, holy days, and matters of conscience. Paul calls them to “receive one another, just as Christ also received us,” so that “with one mind and one mouth” they may glorify God together (Romans 15:6–7). At the cross, Christ put to death the hostility between Jew and Gentile and created “one new man” from the two (Ephesians 2:14–16). If Christ has put the hostility to death, His people must not revive it. The fruit of the cross is a church that bears with one another in unity and love (Ephesians 4:1–3).

[Insight] Two Kinds of Praise: Shining and Recommending. The verse uses two different verbs. “Praise” is halal, the root of “hallelujah,” exuberant praise that gives God glory. “Laud” is shabach, which also carries the sense of commending something to others: “One generation shall praise [shabach] Your works to another” (Psalm 145:4). To halal God is to praise Him. To shabach Him is to speak well of Him to someone else, to recommend Him, to tell others what He is worth. Worship and witness are two sides of one response here. If your praise of God never spills over into speaking well of Him to anyone else, it has stopped halfway.

[Christ & Life] Giving Jesus His Inheritance. The first time the Psalter mentions the nations, they are raging “against the LORD and against His Anointed” (Psalm 2:1–2). In that same psalm the Father promises His Son, “Ask of Me, and I will give You the nations for Your inheritance” (Psalm 2:8). Psalm 117 is that inheritance being called home. When we pray for the lost and bring people to Jesus, we are partnering with the Father’s desire to give His Son what He promised. John the Baptist called himself “the friend of the bridegroom” and rejoiced when people came to Jesus (John 3:29). Sharing our faith is an expression of love for the Bridegroom and joy in seeing Him receive His Bride (Revelation 19:7). Every person you lead to Jesus is part of His inheritance coming home. Every person you write off is someone the Father promised to His Son.

[Christ & Life] The Spirit Turns a Command Into a Gift. Psalm 117 gives the nations a command they could not obey on their own. People cannot praise a God they do not know. The New Testament shows how the command came true: by the Holy Spirit. Right after quoting this psalm, Paul describes his ministry as presenting “the offering of the Gentiles… sanctified by the Holy Spirit” (Romans 15:16). When the Spirit fell on Cornelius’s household, the first Gentile believers in Acts were heard to “speak with tongues and magnify God” (Acts 10:46). Psalm 117:1 came true the moment the Spirit put praise into Gentile mouths. If your heart feels too dull to worship, the answer is not to try harder. Ask the Spirit to do in you what this psalm commands.

[Application] A Call to Worship Is Also a Call to Witness. No one can praise a God they have never heard of. If all nations are to praise the LORD, someone has to tell them who He is. Paul presses that exact logic: “How shall they believe in Him of whom they have not heard?” (Romans 10:14). So this verse carries a hidden assignment. A church that sings Psalm 117 without caring whether the nations ever hear the gospel has not understood what it is singing. Somewhere there are people with your neighbor’s name and your coworker’s face who have never heard the song. Who in your life have you assumed God isn’t calling?

[Insight] Two Heralds Calling the Nations. The rare word for “peoples” here is common in the Aramaic of Daniel, where it appears in one of Scripture’s most chilling scenes. Nebuchadnezzar’s herald cries, “To you it is commanded, O peoples, nations, and languages,” and everyone who will not bow to the golden image goes into the furnace (Daniel 3:4–6). Every empire eventually summons the peoples to bow to its image, and it uses fear to get it. The LORD summons the peoples too, but the reason He gives is “merciful kindness.” Then the story turns. After God humbled him, Nebuchadnezzar used the very verb of this psalm: “I, Nebuchadnezzar, praise and extol and honor the King of heaven, all of whose works are truth” (Daniel 4:37). The man who built the furnace ended up answering Israel’s song. There is hope for all who are far from God.

Verse Explanation

The reason all nations should praise God is that His faithful love toward His people is great and His truth, meaning His reliability in keeping His promises, never ends. The psalm closes as it began, with "Praise the LORD," showing that God's steady love and faithfulness are the heart of all true worship.

"For His merciful kindness is great toward us,"

"For" gives the reason behind the call for all the nations to praise the LORD. The Hebrew word translated "merciful kindness" is hesed, which describes loyal, steady love: the kind that keeps showing kindness because of a commitment already made. God had bound Himself to Israel by covenant, a solemn promise that He would be their God and they would be His people, and hesed is the love that stays true to that promise even when His people fail. The verb translated "is great" means to be strong or to prevail. It is used of the floodwaters that "prevailed" over the earth (Genesis 7:18). God's kindness toward His people is not small or fragile; it overpowers everything that might stand against it, including their own sin.

"Us" refers to God's people, those who have experienced His kindness firsthand. Yet the psalm calls every nation to praise God for what He has done for them. That makes sense in light of God's promise to Abraham: "in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3). God's love for Israel was never meant to stop at Israel's borders. What He did for His people became a reason for the whole world to worship Him, because through them He was working to bless all the nations. Just before quoting the verse Paul uses about Gentile worship, Paul explains that Christ came "to confirm the promises made to the fathers, and that the Gentiles might glorify God for His mercy" (Romans 15:8–9).

"And the truth of the LORD endures forever."

The Hebrew word for "truth," emet, means more than accurate information. It describes what is firm and reliable, and when used of God it means His faithfulness: He does what He says. Kindness and truth often appear together in Scripture. When the LORD revealed His character to Moses, He called Himself "abounding in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6), using the same two Hebrew words found here. God's love moves Him to make His promises, and His faithfulness guarantees He will keep them.

"Endures forever" means His faithfulness never runs out or wears down over time. People change, and nations rise and fall, but the LORD's reliability does not fade. The promises He made to Abraham centuries before this psalm were still standing, and they remain trustworthy for every generation after.

"Praise the LORD!"

In Hebrew this is Hallelujah. The psalm ends with the same call to praise the LORD that opened it. Between those two calls stand only two truths about God: His great kindness and His lasting faithfulness. Everything in this short psalm is built around them. The nations are not asked to praise God for vague reasons, but for His steady love and dependable promises, shown to His people and offered through them to the world.

Study Notes

In the Hebrew text, which the NKJV follows, the closing "Praise the LORD" (Hallelujah) stands at the end of Psalm 117. The Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, places these hallelujahs differently. It moves the Hallelujah that ends one psalm to the beginning of the next. So in the Greek, Psalm 117 opens with "Alleluia," and the Hallelujah after verse 2 becomes the first word of Psalm 118.

This difference is about where a heading-like word of praise belongs, not about the content of the psalm. It does matter, however, for seeing how the psalm is built. The pattern noted in the Verse Explanation, where the psalm begins and ends with a call to praise around its two truths about God, rests on the Hebrew arrangement. In that arrangement the opening command, "Praise the LORD," and the closing Hallelujah frame verse 2's statement of God's kindness and faithfulness. Since the Hebrew text is the older and more direct witness to the psalm as Israel sang it, the NKJV's arrangement is well grounded.

Spirit & Life

[Insight] Every Believer Lost a Battle Against Mercy. “His merciful kindness is great toward us.” The verb is gabar, which means to be strong, to prevail, to overpower. It is the root of gibbor, a mighty warrior. Literally, God’s chesed “has prevailed over us.” The same verb describes the Flood: “the waters prevailed exceedingly on the earth” until the high hills were covered (Genesis 7:19). There the waters of judgment prevailed; here the LORD’s mercy prevails over His people. His love is not weak affection. It is mighty, tender kindness that rises over our shame, fear, and resistance until we surrender. “Where sin abounded, grace abounded much more” (Romans 5:20). We were not merely forgiven. We were overpowered by love.

[Foundational Truth] Kindness With Mercy Inside It. The NKJV calls God’s chesed “merciful kindness,” a phrase that holds together mercy for our sin and tenderness toward our weakness. God’s love is never kindness to the deserving; it is always kindness to the undeserving, which is what makes it mercy. Many believers know they are forgiven yet still mistakenly view the Father as harsh, as if He forgave them through clenched teeth. But His love is no reluctant pardon. It is steadfast kindness that stoops to our weakness and quiets our fear. You can stop flinching or hesitating in fear. The Father you approach is full of mercy.

[Christ & Life] From Outsiders to Insiders. Psalm 117 first speaks to the Gentiles as “you,” then declares that God’s mercy is great toward “us,” His people Israel. In Christ, those who were once “far off” have been brought near by His blood (Ephesians 2:13). Now Gentiles are “no longer strangers and foreigners, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God” (Ephesians 2:19). If you are in Christ, you are not on the outside looking in. You may still feel like an outsider, in church or before God, but you are not one. You belong. The “us” of this psalm includes you, and you can say with full confidence, “His merciful kindness is great toward us.”

[Application] Can You Praise God for Someone Else’s Blessing? The nations are told to praise God for mercy shown to someone else. The “us” in this verse was Israel, not them. The rabbis noticed this and asked what business the nations had praising God here. Rahab did it (Joshua 2:9–11). Ruth did it (Ruth 1:16). The Queen of Sheba did it (1 Kings 10:9). Can you? Can you thank God sincerely when He answers someone else’s prayer, heals someone else’s child, or blesses someone else’s ministry, before He has done the same for you? That is one of the clearest signs that worship has become about God and not about you. It is also the cure for envy.

[Christ & Life] Truth for Israel, Mercy for the Nations. Psalm 117 gives two reasons to praise: God’s chesed (merciful kindness) and His emet (truth). These are the two words at the center of God’s name, revealed to Moses: “abounding in goodness [chesed] and truth [emet]” (Exodus 34:6). Just before Paul quotes this psalm, he shows how Christ fulfilled both: Jesus became “a servant to the circumcision for the truth of God, to confirm the promises made to the fathers, and that the Gentiles might glorify God for His mercy” (Romans 15:8–9). Truth kept for Israel; mercy opened to the nations. The Word made flesh came “full of grace and truth” (John 1:14). The psalmist praised mercy and truth as attributes. In Jesus they have a face and hands.

[Christ & Life] From the Courtroom to Communion. When we keep rehearsing our failures, prayer starts to feel like standing trial before our own accusations. We come in as the defendant and leave as the judge, and the verdict is always guilty. Psalm 117 turns our eyes away from ourselves to the LORD, whose “merciful kindness is great toward us.” Mercy has already prevailed over that courtroom. In Christ, His forgiveness invites us to come out of hiding and come home. The prayer closet is meant to be a place of fellowship with our Father, not a courtroom of self-condemnation. Jesus already stood trial for you. Stop holding a second one.

[Foundational Truth] Faithfulness With No Expiration Date. “The truth of the LORD endures forever.” The psalm before this one says, “I said in my haste, ‘All men are liars’” (Psalm 116:11). The psalmist had trusted people and been let down. Psalm 117 answers him: people fail, but God’s truth endures. The answer to betrayal is not cynicism; it is finding out where trust really belongs. “Let God be true but every man a liar” (Romans 3:4). His Word stays steady when people disappoint us, promises are broken, or our emotions rage. When people prove unreliable, don’t stop trusting altogether. Move your trust to the One whose word has never failed.

[Christ & Life] The Truth That Says “Amen.” The Hebrew emet, translated “truth,” shares its root with amen: what is firm, faithful, and able to bear weight. Isaiah calls the LORD “the God of truth,” literally “the God of amen” (Isaiah 65:16). Jesus calls Himself “the Amen, the Faithful and True Witness” (Revelation 3:14), and in Him all God’s promises are “Yes” and “Amen” (2 Corinthians 1:20). Faith is not a feeling of certainty you have to manufacture. It is leaning your full weight on the One who cannot give way.

[Discovery] Mercy That Prevails Under Lifted Hands. The same verb, gabar, appears at Rephidim: “when Moses held up his hand… Israel prevailed; and when he let down his hand, Amalek prevailed” (Exodus 17:11). Victory depended on hands lifted on a hill until the sun went down. Christians have long seen in that scene a picture of intercession, and of hands stretched out on a cross. Moses’ arms got tired; Aaron and Hur had to hold them up. Christ’s never come down. He “always lives to make intercession” for those who come to God through Him (Hebrews 7:25). The mercy that prevails over you is held in place by hands that will never drop.
