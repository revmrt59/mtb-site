Verse Explanation

The shortest psalm in the Bible opens with a call for every nation and people on earth to praise the LORD. Paul later quotes this verse to show that God always planned for the Gentiles to join in worshiping Him through Christ.

"Praise the LORD, all you Gentiles!"

"Praise" translates halal, which means to praise, celebrate, or boast in someone. It is the root of Hallelujah. "The LORD" stands for God's personal name, the name He revealed to Moses (Exodus 3:14–15). The nations are called to worship Israel's own God by name.

In this setting, "Gentiles" translates the Hebrew word for nations and points to the peoples of the world beyond Israel. These were nations that bowed to idols and often fought against God's people, yet a psalm sung in Israel's worship invites every one of them to praise the LORD. God told Abraham at the very beginning, "And in you all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3). He chose Israel as the people through whom His blessing would reach every nation.

"Laud Him, all you peoples!"

"Laud" translates shabach, a less common word meaning to praise, commend, or declare someone's greatness. "Peoples" is another word for the nations. As Hebrew poetry often does, the second line repeats the first in different words. The two verbs overlap in meaning, and the repetition makes the call emphatic and extends it to every people on earth.

Paul quotes this verse in Romans 15:11. He was writing to a church of Jewish and Gentile believers, urging them to "receive one another, just as Christ also received us, to the glory of God" (Romans 15:7). He gathers several Old Testament passages, including this one, to show that Gentiles worshiping the God of Israel through Christ was God's plan from the start. Israel had been singing about it all along.

Study Notes

Psalm 117 belongs to Psalms 113–118, which Jewish tradition calls the Hallel, from the Hebrew word for "praise." This group is often called the "Egyptian Hallel" because it celebrates God's rescue of Israel from Egypt (Psalm 114 retells the Exodus directly).

By New Testament times these psalms were sung at Israel's great festivals, including Passover. Early Jewish sources describe the Hallel divided in two at the Passover meal, with the first part sung before the meal and the rest, including Psalm 117, sung after it.

Verse Explanation

The reason all nations should praise God is that His faithful love toward His people is great and His truth, meaning His reliability in keeping His promises, lasts forever. The psalm closes as it began, with "Praise the LORD," showing that God's steady love and faithfulness are the heart of all true worship.

"For His merciful kindness is great toward us,"

"For" gives the reason the nations should praise the LORD. "Merciful kindness" translates hesed, God's loyal, steadfast love: love that keeps showing kindness because of a commitment already made. God had bound Himself to Israel with the promise that He would be their God and they would be His people, and hesed is the love that stays true to that promise even when His people fail.

"Is great" translates gabar, which speaks of strength and prevailing power. The same verb describes the floodwaters that "prevailed" over the earth (Genesis 7:18). Here it emphasizes the greatness of God's merciful kindness toward His people.

"Us" most naturally refers to Israel, the people who had experienced His kindness firsthand. Yet every nation is called to praise God for what He has done for them, because His promise to Abraham meant that His blessing on Israel would reach all the families of the earth. Paul makes the same connection just before quoting this psalm, explaining that Christ came "to confirm the promises made to the fathers, and that the Gentiles might glorify God for His mercy" (Romans 15:8–9).

"And the truth of the LORD endures forever."

"Truth" translates emet, which describes what is firm and reliable. Used of God, it means His faithfulness: He does what He says. When the LORD revealed His character to Moses, He called Himself "abounding in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6), using the same two Hebrew words found here. His love moves Him to make His promises, and His faithfulness guarantees He will keep them.

"Endures forever" means His faithfulness lasts through every generation. The promises God made to Abraham centuries before this psalm still stood, and they remain trustworthy today.

"Praise the LORD!"

In Hebrew this is Hallelujah. The psalm ends with the same call to praise that opened it, and between the two calls stand two truths about God: His great kindness and His lasting faithfulness. The nations are called to praise Him for His steady love and dependable promises, shown to His people and offered through them to the world.

Study Notes

The NKJV follows the Hebrew arrangement, where the closing "Praise the LORD" (Hallelujah) ends Psalm 117. In the Greek arrangement represented by the Rahlfs edition and the New English Translation of the Septuagint, this psalm is numbered 116 and the following psalm, Hebrew Psalm 118, is numbered 117. That following Greek psalm begins with Hallelujah. The opening and closing praise of Psalm 117 in the NKJV follow the Hebrew arrangement.
