Chapter Reflections

Psalms 117

Reflect and Respond
Psalm 117:1–2 — Praise the LORD, All You Nations

Reflections

Psalm 117:1

[Application] Jonah Knew the Same Mercy and Wanted to Keep It for Himself. The nations had enslaved Israel in Egypt and attacked them for generations. Yet the psalm Israel sang at Passover calls those same nations to praise the LORD. Jonah knew that mercy well. He prayed, “I know that You are a gracious and merciful God, slow to anger and abundant in lovingkindness” (Jonah 4:2). “Lovingkindness” is hesed, the same word as “merciful kindness” in Psalm 117:2. That is exactly why Jonah was angry. He did not want God’s mercy reaching Nineveh, the great city of Israel’s enemy. It is possible to love God’s mercy for ourselves and resent it for others. Most of us have a Nineveh: someone who hurt us, a group we have written off, or a nation we see in the news as the enemy. Do we want God’s mercy to reach them too?

[Application] “All You Peoples.” The psalm says “all” twice: “all you Gentiles” and “all you peoples.” In heaven that call is answered by people “out of every tribe and tongue and people and nation” (Revelation 5:9). Paul quoted this psalm to a church divided into groups (Romans 15:7–11). It is possible to rejoice that God welcomed us while quietly keeping certain people at a distance. Sometimes it is prejudice, in how we think and talk about people of another race, country, or language. Sometimes it is a clique, the same circle at every church gathering while others stand at the edges. A prejudiced heart is not God’s heart. God’s heart reaches all peoples, and ours should too. Is there anyone I am keeping at a distance whom God has welcomed?

[Insight] The Psalm Paul Used to Heal a Divided Church. Paul quotes Psalm 117:1 in Romans 15:11 while writing to a church quarreling over food, holy days, and matters of conscience (Romans 14–15). The groups were largely Jewish and Gentile believers. Paul wanted them to glorify God together “with one mind and one mouth” (Romans 15:6). He showed them from the Scriptures, including this psalm, that God had always intended Jews and Gentiles to praise Him together. At the cross Christ broke down the wall between them and put their hostility to death, creating “one new man from the two” (Ephesians 2:15–16). If Christ has put the hostility to death, His people must not revive it.

[Christ & Life] Giving Jesus His Inheritance. The first time the Psalms mention the nations, they are raging “against the LORD and against His Anointed” (Psalm 2:1–2). In that same psalm God says to His Son, “Ask of Me, and I will give You the nations for Your inheritance” (Psalm 2:8). Psalm 117 does not mention Psalm 2, but it calls those same nations to praise the LORD. Read together, the two psalms show the nations moving from rage to praise as the Son receives what was promised Him. When we pray for the lost and bring people to Jesus, we are joining the Father in giving His Son His inheritance. Who can I pray for by name this week?

[Christ & Life] The Spirit Puts Praise in Gentile Mouths. Psalm 117 calls the nations to praise a God most of them did not know. The New Testament shows how that call was answered: by the Holy Spirit. Just after quoting this psalm, Paul describes his ministry as presenting “the offering of the Gentiles… sanctified by the Holy Spirit” (Romans 15:16). When the Spirit fell on Cornelius’s household, those Gentile believers were heard to “speak with tongues and magnify God” (Acts 10:46). The same Spirit gives praise to God’s people today. When my heart feels dry in worship, I can ask the Holy Spirit to fill me and put praise in my mouth again.

[Insight] Two Heralds Calling the Nations. The word for “peoples” in this verse is rare in Hebrew, but it appears often in the Aramaic chapters of Daniel. Nebuchadnezzar’s herald uses it to summon the world: “To you it is commanded, O peoples, nations, and languages.” Whoever would not bow to the golden image was thrown into the furnace (Daniel 3:4–6). Psalm 117 also calls the nations to worship, and the reason it gives is God’s merciful kindness. Later, after God humbled him, Nebuchadnezzar used the same verb as this psalm’s “laud”: “I, Nebuchadnezzar, praise and extol and honor the King of heaven, all of whose works are truth” (Daniel 4:37). The king who built the furnace ended up giving God the praise this psalm calls for. Earthly rulers have often demanded worship through fear, while the LORD calls the nations to Himself with His mercy.

Psalm 117:2

[Application] How Great Is His Mercy? Psalm 117:2 says His merciful kindness “is great toward us.” Psalm 103 uses the same two Hebrew words together and gives them a measure: “For as the heavens are high above the earth, So great is His mercy toward those who fear Him” (Psalm 103:11). David then says what that mercy does: “As far as the east is from the west, So far has He removed our transgressions from us” (Psalm 103:12). Many believers know God has forgiven them but still carry the weight of what they have done. God’s mercy toward His people is greater than their sin. Is there something I keep carrying that His mercy has already removed?

[Foundational Truth] Merciful Is the First Word. When God revealed His character to Moses, the first thing He said about Himself was “The LORD, the LORD God, merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abounding in goodness and truth” (Exodus 34:6). It is possible to believe we are forgiven and still picture the Father as harsh, as if He forgave us reluctantly. God describes Himself first as merciful and gracious, and Psalm 117 says His merciful kindness toward His people is great. I can come to Him without fear. In Christ, the Father I approach is full of mercy.

[Christ & Life] From Outsiders to Insiders. Psalm 117 speaks to the Gentiles as “you” in verse 1, then says God’s mercy is great toward “us,” His people Israel, in verse 2. The Gentiles begin the psalm on the outside. In Christ, those who were once “far off” have been brought near by His blood (Ephesians 2:13). They are now “fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God” (Ephesians 2:19). Some believers still feel like outsiders, at church or before God. If you are in Christ, you belong in God’s household. You can join Israel in saying, “His merciful kindness is great toward us.”

[Application] Can You Praise God for Someone Else’s Blessing? The nations are told to praise God for mercy He showed to Israel. The Queen of Sheba did exactly that when she visited Solomon: “Blessed be the LORD your God… Because the LORD has loved Israel forever” (1 Kings 10:9). She praised God for His love toward another people. That means thanking God sincerely when He answers someone else’s prayer, heals someone else’s child, or blesses someone else’s ministry, before He has done the same for us. Praise like that keeps envy from taking root. Can we thank God for what He is doing in someone else’s life?

[Christ & Life] Mercy and Truth with a Face. Psalm 117:2 praises God for His merciful kindness (hesed) and His truth (emet). Moses heard those same two words when he asked to see God’s glory and the LORD passed by, proclaiming Himself “abounding in goodness and truth” (Exodus 33:18; 34:6). Moses heard God described, but he was not allowed to see His face. John describes Jesus in words that echo that moment: “We beheld His glory… full of grace and truth” (John 1:14). The mercy and truth this psalm praises are seen in Jesus. Jesus is the face of mercy and truth.

[Foundational Truth] Faithfulness With No Expiration Date. “The truth of the LORD endures forever.” The psalm just before this one says, “I said in my haste, ‘All men are liars’” (Psalm 116:11). In his distress the psalmist felt that no one could be trusted. At Passover these two psalms were sung one after the other, so the next thing Israel sang was that the truth of the LORD endures forever. Paul draws on that same verse: “Let God be true but every man a liar” (Romans 3:4). People will disappoint us, and promises will be broken. God’s word still stands. Have I let disappointment with people make me slow to trust God?

[Christ & Life] The Truth That Says “Amen.” The Hebrew word emet, translated “truth,” comes from the same root as amen. Both describe what is firm and faithful, something that can bear weight. Isaiah calls the LORD “the God of truth,” literally “the God of amen” (Isaiah 65:16). Jesus calls Himself “the Amen, the Faithful and True Witness” (Revelation 3:14), and in Him all God’s promises are “Yes” and “Amen” (2 Corinthians 1:20). Faith means leaning my full weight on the One who will not give way. Every time I end a prayer with “Amen,” I am saying that God and His Word can be trusted.

Section Reflections

[Foundational Truth] Call, Cause, Call. The psalm moves in three steps: a call (“Praise the LORD”), a cause (“For His merciful kindness… and the truth of the LORD”), and the call again (“Praise the LORD!”). The small word “for” holds it together. Biblical praise is a response to what is true about God. Take away verse 2, and verse 1 is a shout with nothing behind it. If your worship rises and falls with the music or with your emotions, go back to the “for.”

[Application] A Psalm That Asks for Nothing. Psalm 117 has no petition, complaint, or plea for rescue. In a Psalter full of cries for help, this psalm asks God for nothing. It tells the nations to praise Him and gives the reasons: His merciful kindness and His truth. Much of our prayer is rightly asking. We can also come into His presence simply to thank Him, enjoy Him, and declare His goodness. Try spending part of your prayer time this week with no requests at all, only praise for who He is and what He has already done. If God never gave you another thing, would what He has already shown you be enough to praise Him?

[Foundational Truth] Nations Rise and Fall; His Truth Remains. Psalm 117 calls every nation to praise the LORD because His truth endures forever. Israel watched Assyria, Babylon, Persia, Greece, and Rome rise and fall, and each of them claimed authority over many peoples. Every one of them is gone. “The grass withers, the flower fades, But the word of our God stands forever” (Isaiah 40:8). Rulers make promises they cannot keep, and whole cultures change, but God does not change with them. Whoever is in power and whatever the headlines say, the truth of the LORD will outlast all of it.

[Application] What the Nations See in God’s People. The nations are told to praise God because of His merciful kindness “toward us.” The evidence they are given is what God has done among His people. Israel’s life was meant to show the world what God is like. The same is true of the church. Jesus said, “By this all will know that you are My disciples, if you have love for one another” (John 13:35). When people look at us, they should see the mercy and truth this psalm praises. When God’s people are harsh, divided or dishonest, they make His mercy and truth harder to believe. What does the way we treat one another tell people about God?

[Insight] From “My Voice” to “All You Peoples.” Psalm 117 sits right after a very personal psalm. Psalm 116 begins, “I love the LORD, because He has heard my voice” (116:1). By its end, the psalmist is praising God “in the presence of all His people” (116:18). Then Psalm 117 calls all the nations to praise. Read together, the praise moves outward: from one rescued person, to God’s people, to the whole world. Personal experience of God’s mercy should widen our hearts toward others. The person who can honestly say “He heard my voice” is the one most ready to say, “Praise Him, all you peoples.” Has what God has done for me made me more concerned for people who do not know Him yet?

[Discovery] Many Tongues, One Song. Psalm 117 calls the nations and peoples to praise the LORD. At Babel, God scattered the peoples by confusing their languages (Genesis 11:7–9). At Pentecost, people from many nations heard the wonderful works of God, each “in our own language” (Acts 2:8, 11). In Revelation, the redeemed are still “of all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues” (Revelation 7:9). God brings the nations together in praise and keeps their languages. A church where people worship in more than one language is a small picture of what heaven will sound like. Can I enjoy worship that sounds different from mine?

[Discovery] Many Tongues, One Song. Psalm 117 calls the nations and peoples to praise the LORD. At Babel, God scattered the peoples by confusing their languages (Genesis 11:7–9). At Pentecost, people from many nations heard the wonderful works of God, each “in our own language” (Acts 2:8, 11). In Revelation, the redeemed are still “of all nations, tribes, peoples, and tongues” (Revelation 7:9). God brings the nations together in praise and keeps their languages. A church where people worship in more than one language is a small picture of what heaven will sound like. Heaven will not sound like your church’s preferred style of worship. It will sound like every people on earth singing to one Lamb.

[Christ & Life] A House of Prayer for All Nations. The same week Jesus likely sang the Hallel, He drove the buyers and sellers out of the temple, quoting Isaiah: “My house shall be called a house of prayer for all nations” (Mark 11:17). The trading was most likely in the outer court, the one place Gentiles could come to pray. The place where the nations should have been praising God had been crowded out by business. Jesus cleared it, likely sang the psalm that summons the nations, and went to the cross where the dividing wall came down (Ephesians 2:14). Churches can crowd out the nations the same way without noticing, filling the room with programs, preferences, and insider language until there’s no space left for the outsider. Jesus is still clearing that court. What in our church might make it harder for an outsider to come near to God?

[Christ & Life] Hallelujah: The Exodus Song Handed to the Nations. The psalm ends with Hallelujah, “Praise Yah,” using the short form of God’s name. That short form first appears in Israel’s song at the Red Sea: “The LORD [Yah] is my strength and song, And He has become my salvation” (Exodus 15:2). The name the nations are called to praise is the name Israel first sang after God brought them through the sea. In the New Testament, “Alleluia” appears only in Revelation 19, rising again and again from “a great multitude in heaven” (19:1, 3, 4, 6). Earlier in Revelation, the victorious sing “the song of Moses… and the song of the Lamb,” declaring, “All nations shall come and worship before You” (Revelation 15:3–4). For centuries Israel sang this psalm to the nations. One day the nations will sing it back.

Psalm 117. The whole psalm rests on two truths about God: His merciful kindness toward His people is great, and His truth endures forever. God wants me to know His heart and character. I can trust Him completely because He is always true to Himself and to His promises.

What God may be saying: “See how great My love is toward you. You can trust My heart, rest in My faithfulness, and draw near to Me without fear. Let what you have seen of My love and faithfulness fill your heart with praise.”
