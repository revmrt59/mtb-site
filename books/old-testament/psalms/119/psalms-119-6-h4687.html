Summary:

COMMANDMENTS - ( mitsvah , Strong’s [4687])

Summary:

A divine charge, order, or injunction. It refers to the authoritative instructions given by a superior to a subordinate, representing the specific expressions of God’s will that demand total and undivided obedience.

Full Study:

1. Hebrew Roots / Derivation

Derived from the verb tsavah (Strong’s [6680]), which means "to command," "to charge," "to order," or "to enjoin."

The root suggests the act of a king or a father laying a specific obligation upon someone, establishing a clear line of authority and expectation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

A commandment, precept, or religious duty.

It signifies a specific "order" that has been issued by God, often as part of a larger code of conduct or covenant.

In the Old Testament, mitsvah is the standard term for the individual requirements within the Law of Moses, highlighting that God’s will is expressed through clear, actionable instructions.

3. Usage in Hebrew Culture & Word Pictures

- The King’s Edict: In Hebrew culture, mitsvah was used for a royal decree that required the immediate attention and submission of every subject in the kingdom.

- The Father’s Charge: It pictured a father on his deathbed giving his "final charge" or "commands" to his sons, representing a solemn and binding legacy of instruction.

- The Boundary Marker: It was used as a "picture" of the lines of duty that defined the relationship between a master and a servant, where the master’s word was the servant’s absolute rule of life.

4. Theological Implications

Commandments reflect the "lordship" of God; He has the right to command, and His creatures have the duty to obey.

They are the specific means by which "righteousness" is practiced in daily life, moving the believer from theory to actual "good works" (Titus 3:8).

Theological focus is on "totality"—the commandments are not a menu to choose from but a complete "charge" that requires "respect" (verse 6).

5. Contextual Nuance in Psalm 119:6

In the NKJV of Psalm 119:6, it states, "Then I would not be ashamed, when I look into all Your commandments (mitsvotic—plural)."

The psalmist links "shame" to a lack of "respect" for "all" the commandments.

This shows that partial obedience is a source of spiritual disgrace; true "blessedness" (verse 1) is only found when the believer’s heart is aligned with the "whole" counsel of God’s orders, leaving no room for "unprofitable" selective obedience.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

- Genesis 26:5: “...Abraham obeyed My voice and kept My charge, My commandments (mitsvotic), My statutes, and My laws.”

- Exodus 20:6: “...but showing mercy to thousands, to those who love Me and keep My commandments (mitsvotic).”

- Deuteronomy 6:1: “Now this is the commandment (hamitsvah), and these are the statutes and judgments...”

- Psalm 19:8: “...The commandment (mitsvat) of the LORD is pure, enlightening the eyes;”

- Psalm 119:127: “Therefore I love Your commandments (mitsvotic) more than gold, yes, than fine gold!”

7. Other Relevant Insights

This word highlights that "fruitfulness" (Titus 3:14) is the result of a "disciplined" response to God’s charges.

It suggests that a "watchful" (Mark 13:37) servant is one who has "taken heed" (Mark 13:33) to every specific order the Master has left behind.

By "looking into all" the commandments, the believer avoids the "shame" of hypocrisy and ensures their "way" (verse 1) is truly "undefiled."
