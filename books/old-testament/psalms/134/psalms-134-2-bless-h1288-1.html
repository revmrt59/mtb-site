BLESS - (bârak, Strong's H1288)

Summary:

The Hebrew verb barak fundamentally means to bestow favor, invoke a blessing, or express praise and adoration. When God is the subject blessing humans, the verb denotes the bestowal of life, protection, prosperity, or holy favor. Conversely, when humans are the subject "blessing" God, the word signifies rendering spoken praise, declaring His worth, and offering heartfelt adoration. In Psalm 134:2, the word serves as a direct imperative command to the Levites and priests ministering in the temple during the night hours, instructing them to lift their hands in the sanctuary and actively ascribe praise and exaltation to Yahweh.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The verb barak is one of the foundational verbs of covenant worship and divine interaction in the Hebrew Scriptures. Etymologically, the root is associated in early Semitic contexts with kneeling or taking a posture of respect and adoration. In biblical Hebrew, however, the term functions primarily within the realm of speech acts and relational declarations.

The historical and canonical setting of Psalm 134 places this word at the climax of the Songs of Ascents (Psalms 120–134). Pilgrims traveling up to Jerusalem for the annual feasts concluded their journey at the temple. Psalm 134 addresses the temple personnel—the priests and Levites who maintained the sanctuary night watch—calling them to sustain the continuous worship of Yahweh even when the crowds of pilgrims had departed.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, barak conveys the bestowal or declaration of benefit, honor, and praise. The specific meaning of the verb depends upon the directional relationship between the subject and the object:

  • Divine to Human (God blessing man): Bestowing life, spiritual favor, physical abundance, protection, or divine endorsement.

  • Human to Human (Man blessing man): Pronouncing a benediction, greeting with goodwill, or invoking God's favor upon another person.

  • Human to Divine (Man blessing God): Exalting, praising, extolling, and acknowledging God's inherent greatness and worthiness.

When human beings are commanded to "bless" God, the lexical meaning does not imply imparting a benefit or gift to God, as God lacks nothing. Rather, human blessing directed toward Yahweh is an act of verbal adoration, declaring His glory and magnifying His holy name.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

The directionality of barak creates distinct semantic nuances throughout the Old Testament text:

  • Salutation and Greeting: Between human parties, the verb can refer to a standard greeting or formal expression of respect upon arrival or departure, as seen when Saul went out to meet Samuel (1 Samuel 13:10, NKJV: "greet").

  • Generational and Covenantal Endowment: In patriarchal narratives, divine and human blessings communicate God's covenant promises, fruitfulness, and generational heritage (Genesis 1:22, NKJV: "blessed"; Deuteronomy 15:18, NKJV: "bless").

  • Liturgical Adoration: In poetic and liturgical literature, the verb becomes a primary term for congregational and priestly worship, where worshippers assemble to exalt the name of the LORD (Psalms 96:2, NKJV: "bless"; Psalms 135:20, NKJV: "Bless").

4. Theological Implications

The theological dynamics of barak highlight the reciprocal yet asymmetric nature of the biblical covenant. God initiates blessing toward His creation out of sovereign grace, bestowing life and goodness. Creation and redeemed humanity respond by returning blessing to God in the form of worship and praise.

Human praise does not add to God's intrinsic essence or divine attributes; rather, it acknowledges reality. To "bless the LORD" is to align speech and posture with the truth of who God is. It forms the human response to God's prior grace, forming an unbroken cycle of divine favor received and reverent praise rendered.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Psalm 134:2, the verb appears in the imperative form, plural in number and masculine in gender, directly addressing the night ministers in the Jerusalem temple. The psalmist charges these servants: "Lift up your hands in the sanctuary, and bless the LORD."

The immediate context pairs the verbal act of blessing Yahweh with the bodily action of lifting hands. Lifting hands in the sanctuary was an established bodily posture of prayer, dependence, and adoration. By pairing this physical gesture with the command to "bless," the text emphasizes that temple service must not degenerate into mere routine or passive watchkeeping. The night servants are called to engage in conscious, active, and vocal adoration of Yahweh.

Furthermore, verse 2 forms part of an intentional literary framework within Psalm 134. Verses 1–2 contain human commands addressed to the temple ministers to "bless the LORD," while verse 3 closes the psalm with a pronounced benediction from Yahweh back upon the worshipers: "The LORD who made heaven and earth bless you out of Zion!"

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

  • Numbers 6:23 — "Speak to Aaron and his sons, saying, 'This is the way you shall bless the children of Israel. Say to them...'"

  • 1 Samuel 13:10 — "Now it happened, as soon as he had finished presenting the burnt offering, that Samuel came; and Saul went out to meet him, that he might greet him."

  • Psalms 96:2 — "Sing to the LORD, bless His name; proclaim the good news of His salvation from day to day."

  • Psalms 135:20 — "Bless the LORD, O house of Levi! You who fear the LORD, bless the LORD!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The occurrence of barak in Psalm 134 underscores the continuity of worship across time and space. Worship in the sanctuary did not cease when darkness fell or when the general congregation departed. The imperative directed to the night guards reveals that Yahweh is worthy of continuous, unceasing adoration. The priestly duty of blessing the LORD during the quiet hours of the night served as a testament to Israel's conviction that the God who guards Israel neither slumbers nor sleeps.
