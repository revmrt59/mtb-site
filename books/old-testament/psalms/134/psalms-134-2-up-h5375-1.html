UP - (nâsâʼ, Strong's H5375)

Summary:

The primary lexical meaning of the Hebrew verb nasa' is to lift up, bear, or carry. Depending on its context, it can refer to physically raising objects or body parts, bearing burdens, carrying away sin or guilt, or exalting someone in honor.

In Psalm 134:2, nasa' functions as a plural command ("Lift up") directed to the levitical ministers serving during the night watch in the Jerusalem temple. It summons them to raise their hands toward the sanctuary in active, visible adoration, transforming their silent night service into express worship directed toward God's earthly dwelling place.

Full Study:

1. Word Background & Setting

The Hebrew verb nasa' is a fundamental and widely used verb throughout the Hebrew Bible. Its core concrete image is physical elevation or bearing weight—moving an object upward or carrying a load from one location to another.

In the ancient Near Eastern world, physical gestures carried significant symbolic weight in liturgical and royal contexts. Raising one's hands was a universal bodily expression of petition, submission, homage, and adoration. When brought into Israel's worship tradition, nasa' frequently describes the intentional upward movement of hands, eyes, head, or voice toward God, signaling a heart oriented in faith and reverence toward the Sovereign Lord.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, nasa' encompasses several distinct senses across its various stems:

  • Physical Lifting or Raising: To elevate something spatially, such as lifting hands in prayer, raising eyes to look, raising a head in confidence, or raising a signal flag.

  • Carrying or Bearing: To hold, carry, or support a physical load or burden, as standard-bearers or carriers of sanctuary vessels do.

  • Bearing Off / Removing: To lift away, take away, or carry off a burden, which extends metaphorically to taking away guilt, forgiving transgression, or pardoning sin.

  • Exalting / Honoring: To raise someone to a higher status or position of dignity, power, or self-exaltation.

In the imperative form found in Psalm 134:2, the verb carries the direct sense of bodily elevation—specifically ordering the lifting of hands in sacred service.

3. Biblical Usage & Nuance

Across Old Testament literature, nasa' appears in several key contextual domains:

  • Worship and Supplication: The verb often describes bodily postures during prayer and worship. Worshipers lift (nasa') their eyes to God in dependent expectation, or raise their hands toward the Holy of Holies as an act of prayer, blessing, and self-offering.

  • Cultic and Priestly Responsibility: Priests were tasked to carry (nasa') the holy vessels and furniture of the tabernacle. On a spiritual level, Aaron and the priesthood were appointed to bear (nasa') the guilt or legal responsibilities of the community before God, serving a mediator role in God's covenantal assembly.

  • Forgiveness and Atonement: In legal and prophetic passages, God is described as One who lifts or carries away (nasa') transgression, iniquity, and sin, conceptualizing forgiveness as the active removal of a heavy spiritual burden from the sinner.

4. Theological Implications

The use of nasa' in worshipping context highlights the theological connection between bodily posture and inner devotion. In Scripture, bodily actions are not mere empty rituals; raising the hands represents the lifting of the soul itself to God.

Furthermore, the verb bridges the physical and the spiritual. Just as priests physically lifted holy objects and sacrifices, Israel's worshipers lifted their hands and voices as spiritual sacrifices. In redemptive history, the ultimate thematic trajectory of nasa' points to the divine bearing of human sin, where the Lord bears away the guilt of His people to restore them to fellowship.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

Psalm 134 is the final psalm in the collection known as the Songs of Ascents (Psalms 120–134). These psalms were sung by Israelite pilgrims journeying up to Jerusalem for the sacred feasts. Psalm 134 serves as a liturgical exchange at the close of the pilgrimage cycle.

In Psalm 134:1, the pilgrims address the temple personnel: "Behold, bless the LORD, all you servants of the LORD, who by night stand in the house of the LORD!" In verse 2, the call continues with the imperative form of nasa': "Lift up your hands in the sanctuary, and bless the LORD."

Several contextual details deepen the force of nasa' in this verse:

  • Grammatical Form: The verb is a masculine plural imperative, directly addressing the collective group of levitical night-servants.

  • Transition from Standing to Action: While verse 1 notes that these servants merely "stand" ('amad) in the temple during the night hours, verse 2 commands active engagement. They are not simply to maintain a passive vigil; they are to lift up (nasa') their hands in dynamic worship.

  • Directional Orientation: The gesture of lifting hands is directed "in the sanctuary" (or "toward the holy place"). The physical elevation of hands focuses attention upon the dwelling place of God's earthly presence, symbolizing dependence, reverence, and consecrated praise.

  • Pairing with Blessing: The act of lifting up hands works alongside the command to "bless" (barak) the LORD. The physical action gives external expression to the verbal praise offered to God in the sanctuary.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

The following selected passages illustrate the range of nasa' across different Biblical contexts:

  • Psalms 24:7 — "Lift up your heads, O you gates! And be lifted up, you everlasting doors! And the King of glory shall come in."

  • Psalms 123:1 — "Unto You I lift up my eyes, O You who dwell in the heavens."

  • Isaiah 51:6 — "Lift up your eyes to the heavens, and look on the earth beneath."

  • Exodus 28:38 — "So it shall be on Aaron’s forehead, that Aaron may bear the iniquity of the holy things which the children of Israel hallow in all their holy gifts..."

  • Job 7:21 — "Why then do You not pardon my transgression, and take away my iniquity?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the structural context of the Songs of Ascents, Psalm 134 creates a striking liturgical dialogue. The pilgrims command the temple guards and priests to lift up their hands and bless the Lord throughout the night (verses 1–2). In response, verse 3 pronounces a blessing back upon the departing pilgrims: "The LORD who made heaven and earth bless you from Zion!"

The elevation of hands (nasa') by the sanctuary ministers forms the crucial hinge of this psalm. By lifting holy hands in the sanctuary, the priesthood faithfully represents the worshipping community before God, receiving and mediating the divine blessing that flows outward from Zion to the ends of the earth.
