Book Overview

At a Glance

Author

Unknown; Jewish tradition names Samuel, but the book does not identify its writer.

Audience

Israel, looking back on the judges’ era and the ancestry of David.

Date

Set during the judges; the date of composition is uncertain, with the closing genealogy reaching David.

Historical Setting

During the days of the judges, famine led Naomi’s family from Bethlehem to Moab. After losing her husband and sons, Naomi returned with Ruth, a woman from a people who had been enemies of Israel.

Purpose

To reveal God’s redeeming grace through the romance of redemption, as a Moabite widow finds refuge among His people and becomes part of the family line leading to David and Jesus Christ.

Central Message

In the days when the judges ruled, God carries Naomi’s emptied household from Moab back to Bethlehem and brings Ruth the Moabitess, through her costly allegiance and the faithful love of Boaz, into redemption, marriage, and belonging among His people. This romance of redemption restores Naomi’s family, leads to David, and pictures the grace of Jesus Christ, the greater Redeemer who brings His people into relationship with Himself.

Key Themes

God’s redeeming grace; redemption into relationship; tested trust; faithful love; providence through initiative; refuge for a Moabite; God’s good and eternal purpose.

Book Flow

  • The Appeal at the Threshing Floor (Ruth 3:1–18)

  • Redemption at the Gate (Ruth 4:1–12)

  • A Son for Naomi and the Line of David (Ruth 4:13–22)

Go Deeper

Background

Author

The book of Ruth does not name its author. Jewish tradition recorded in the Talmud, a collection of Jewish teachings, credits Samuel, but the book itself makes no such claim and leaves its writer unnamed. What the book does reveal is the writer’s interest. He tells a single family’s story from the days of the judges and then ends with the words “Jesse begot David” (4:22). Whoever wrote Ruth wanted readers to see how God’s quiet care for one widowed household led to Israel’s king.

Audience

Ruth was written for Israel, for readers who knew Bethlehem, the tribe of Judah, and the name of David. They would recognize Rachel and Leah, “the two who built the house of Israel,” and Perez, “whom Tamar bore to Judah” (4:11–12). They would also know Moab’s history of hostility toward Israel. Learning that their king’s great-grandmother was a Moabite would give this story of God’s grace particular significance.

Date

The story takes place “in the days when the judges ruled” (1:1), before Israel had a king. The book was written later and ends by tracing Ruth’s family to David (4:17–22). It does not tell us exactly when it was written.

Historical Setting

The period of the judges was one of the darkest in Israel’s history. The book of Judges ends with the summary, “In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes” (Judges 21:25). Ruth is set in that same world, and the contrast is striking. In an age of idolatry, violence, and broken loyalties, this book shows ordinary people in a small town living with kindness, integrity, and faith in the LORD.

The story begins with famine in Bethlehem, the house of bread. Elimelech takes his family to Moab, east of the Dead Sea. Moab was an enemy of Israel. Its king had hired Balaam to curse God’s people, and Moabite women had led Israelites into sin (Deuteronomy 23:4; Numbers 25:1–3). During the judges’ era, Israel also suffered eighteen years under Eglon, king of Moab (Judges 3:12–14). The Law excluded Moabites from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6). Ruth comes from this people. Her welcome and redemption reveal the grace of God against that background of hostility and exclusion.

Family land and family names mattered deeply. Each household’s land was its inheritance from the LORD and its means of living, and the Law provided ways to keep land and family lines from being lost (Leviticus 25:25; Deuteronomy 25:5–6). Naomi’s losses struck at both. With her husband and both sons dead, her household had lost the men through whom its land and Elimelech’s name would ordinarily pass to the next generation, and its future would depend on the help of relatives and the Law’s provision for the poor. That loss is what Naomi means when she says she came home “empty” (1:21).

Occasion

The book does not name a particular occasion for its writing. Its closing genealogy shows why the household story matters for Israel: Ruth’s faithful love and Boaz’s redemption stand in the history of David’s family (4:18–22). The story also reveals God’s providential care and the welcome of a foreign woman who takes refuge in Him. A proposed defense of David’s ancestry may help explain the book’s setting, but the narrator does not announce that purpose.

Understanding the Book

What This Book Is About

Ruth is the story of a family emptied by loss and filled again by the faithful love of God. In four short chapters a famine drives Elimelech’s household to Moab, where Naomi buries her husband and both sons. She returns to Bethlehem with her Moabite daughter-in-law Ruth, who has refused to leave her. Ruth gleans in the field of Boaz, a relative of Elimelech, and receives his protection and generosity. At Naomi’s direction she goes to him at the threshing floor and asks him to act as their close relative. Boaz settles the matter publicly at the city gate, redeems Elimelech’s land, and takes Ruth as his wife. The LORD gives them a son, Obed, the grandfather of David.

The opening places trust under pressure. Elimelech’s name means My God is King, yet famine leads him to seek relief in Moab. His family’s stay becomes an extended residence. The story warns how a practical answer to hardship can become a settled life of compromise. The deaths are not divine punishment; their causes are not disclosed. Naomi’s return begins when she hears that the LORD has given His people bread (1:1–7).

Naomi’s bitterness is lament within continuing faith. She prays for her daughters-in-law and seeks their good even while grieving. Ruth’s attachment joins compassion for Naomi with allegiance to the God whose reality she has encountered through her. Naomi calls herself empty, but Ruth accompanies her and the harvest is beginning. The closing blessing in Bethlehem answers that lament with renewed family hope (1:8–22; 4:14–17).

God’s providence works through ordinary events and faithful action. Ruth goes to work and reaches Boaz’s field; Boaz uses his authority and resources to protect and provide. His greeting to his workers shows faith already shaping his life. The narrator explicitly attributes bread and conception to the LORD, while the events between reveal His purposeful care (1:6; 2:1–18; 4:13).

At the heart of the book is a love story that reveals God’s redeeming grace. Ruth is a Moabite widow who has come to trust the God of Israel. Her loyalty is known throughout Bethlehem (2:11; 3:11), yet she cannot recover the family inheritance or secure its future herself. Boaz welcomes and protects her, pays the cost of redemption, and takes her as his wife. A woman from an enemy people receives a place in his household and in the family line leading to David. This romance of redemption points beyond Bethlehem to our Redeemer, Jesus Christ.

Why This Book Matters

Matthew includes Ruth in the genealogy of Jesus Christ: “Boaz begot Obed by Ruth” (Matthew 1:5). She came from Moab, an enemy nation whose people were excluded from the assembly of the LORD. Yet she found refuge in Israel’s God and received an honored place in the family leading to David and Jesus. Her story reveals God’s redeeming grace toward someone whose background would have marked her as an outsider. That grace is seen in Boaz’s welcome, his willingness to redeem, and the future God gives her.

Ruth shows that grief does not erase faith or end a person’s witness. Naomi returns and prays before her emotions are healed, and Ruth binds herself to Naomi and her God. God is already providing before Naomi can recognize the extent of His care. The book makes room for honest lament while directing attention to the larger purpose unfolding around the sufferer.

Most of all, Ruth gives the Old Testament’s fullest narrative picture of a redeemer. The Law describes the duty of a close relative to buy back family land (Leviticus 25:25), but Ruth shows that duty carried out by a man who is related, able, and willing, and who does it at his own cost. It also shows that redemption is personal. Boaz does not only recover a field. He brings a vulnerable outsider into his house as his wife, restores a family’s name, and gives an emptied widow hope. That joining of rescue and relationship prepares readers to understand what Christ the Redeemer has done.

The story also gives concrete expression to the assurance of Romans 8:28–29. God works all things toward His good purpose for those who love Him, and that purpose reaches beyond present relief to life in Christ. What the characters experience as hunger, work, loss, and a need for security becomes part of the history leading to David and the Messiah. God will bring His eternal purpose to pass.

Book Flow

  • Loss in Moab and the Return to Bethlehem (Ruth 1:1–22)

Famine takes the family to Moab, where Naomi loses her husband and sons. Her return begins with news of God’s provision. She releases her daughters-in-law for their good; Orpah returns, while Ruth clings and joins herself to Naomi, her people, and her God. Read Naomi’s bitterness as lament within faith. Ruth’s presence and the beginning harvest signal provision before Naomi recognizes its fullness (1:6–22).

  • Favor in the Field of Boaz (Ruth 2:1–23)

Ruth takes initiative to glean, and her arrival in Boaz’s field reveals providence at work through ordinary action. Boaz’s faith is visible in his greeting to the reapers and in his protection, meal, and deliberate generosity. His initial kindness expresses godly character rather than romantic pursuit. Ruth eats sufficiently, saves food, and brings it home to Naomi with her gleanings (2:4, 14–18). His identity as a close relative opens the next stage of hope.

  • The Appeal at the Threshing Floor (Ruth 3:1–18)

Naomi seeks Ruth’s rest and guides her toward an honorable appeal for marriage and redeeming protection. Ruth asks, “Take your maidservant under your wing, for you are a close relative” (3:9), echoing the refuge Boaz invoked in 2:12. He understands, commends her virtue, and promises to act while honoring the nearer relative’s prior claim. He sends her back with barley, and she waits for the matter to be settled. The encounter is governed by integrity, without sexual misconduct or manipulation.

  • Redemption at the Gate (Ruth 4:1–12)

The nearer relative first accepts the prospect of redeeming land, then withdraws when Ruth the Moabitess and the continuation of the deceased man’s name enter the transaction. His stated concern is his inheritance (4:3–6). His response is also best read as revealing prejudice against Ruth; that is an inference from the sequence and her named identity, alongside the inheritance explanation. Boaz accepts both Ruth and the responsibility, and the witnesses publicly confirm the redemption and bless their household.

  • A Son for Naomi and the Line of David (Ruth 4:13–22)

The LORD gives Ruth conception. The women bless God for renewed family hope and honor Ruth’s love for Naomi. Obed’s birth answers the earlier scene of emptiness, and the genealogy carries the family to David. Matthew 1:5–6 locates Ruth and Boaz in the ancestry of Christ, revealing how this household’s redemption participates in God’s enduring purpose.

Key Themes to Watch For

The romance of redemption. Boaz’s redemption restores inheritance and family continuity while bringing Ruth into marriage and belonging. Through this personal rescue, the book pictures the grace of our greater Redeemer, Jesus Christ.

God’s redeeming grace. Ruth belongs to a people excluded from the assembly of the LORD, yet she comes to trust Him and finds favor, protection, and a home among His people. Her Moabite background makes the grace of her redemption especially clear (Deuteronomy 23:3–6; Ruth 2:10–12; 4:10–17).

Trust tested by famine. Elimelech’s profession meets the pressure of lack. His family’s extended stay in Moab warns how temporary relief can become a settled dwelling place (1:1–5).

Faithful love. Naomi seeks the women’s good, Ruth clings and provides, and Boaz protects and redeems. Their kindness makes loyalty visible in costly action (1:8; 2:20; 3:10).

Providence through initiative. God’s direction and human responsibility belong together. Ruth works and shares; Boaz provides and completes the public transaction (2:1–18; 4:1–10).

Lament within faith. Naomi’s grief speaks to God and about Him. Her pain does not erase her prayers, witness, or response to His provision (1:6–9, 20–22).

Refuge and belonging. Ruth comes from a people who had opposed Israel, yet she seeks refuge under the LORD’s wings (2:12). She is still named as a Moabite when Boaz receives her as his wife (4:10). God’s grace gives her a place among His people.

God’s good and eternal purpose. The movement from loss to restored family hope reaches beyond one household to David and Christ. Read that purpose alongside Romans 8:28–29.

Key Concepts to Understand Before Reading

Close relative (kinsman-redeemer). The NKJV’s “close relative” translates the Hebrew go’el, a family member with the right and responsibility to buy back land a poor relative had sold (Leviticus 25:25) or to redeem a relative sold into service (Leviticus 25:47–48). Jeremiah later buys a cousin’s field under the same “right of redemption” (Jeremiah 32:7). Boaz is a close relative, but another man is nearer (2:20; 3:12), which is why the matter must be settled at the gate.

Marriage and the deceased man’s name. Deuteronomy 25:5–10 concerns brothers dwelling together when one dies without a son; the surviving brother is to marry the widow to continue the deceased brother’s name. Boaz is a relative rather than Mahlon’s brother. Ruth joins land redemption, marriage, and family continuity without explaining every detail of the custom (4:5, 9–10).

Gleaning. The Law told landowners not to harvest the edges of their fields or go back for forgotten sheaves, but to leave them “for the poor and the stranger” and for the widow (Leviticus 19:9–10; Deuteronomy 24:19). Ruth qualifies on every count. Boaz goes further than the Law requires when he lets her glean among the sheaves and has grain dropped for her on purpose (2:15–16).

Kindness (faithful love). The Hebrew word behind “kindness” in 1:8, 2:20, and 3:10 describes loyal, steadfast love that acts for another’s good beyond what duty requires. In 2:20 Naomi blesses the LORD, “who has not forsaken His kindness to the living and the dead.” Interpreters differ on whether “His” refers to the LORD or to Boaz; either way, Naomi sees God’s faithfulness at work in Boaz’s generosity.

Naomi and Mara. Naomi’s request for the name Mara expresses bitterness in grief (1:20). Read her speech as lament within faith, allowing her prayers, concern for others, and return to Bethlehem their full weight.

The threshing floor and covering. The threshing floor was the place where grain was beaten out and separated from its husks. There Ruth makes an honorable marriage appeal that Boaz understands. The exact customary form is incompletely described. Her request for his wing in 3:9 connects with God’s protective wings in 2:12; Ezekiel 16:8 also uses covering language for marriage and covenant protection. Boaz commends Ruth and follows the rightful process.

The city gate. Town business and legal matters were settled publicly at the gate before the elders (4:1–2, 9–11). Boaz’s redemption of the land and his marriage to Ruth were witnessed, binding, and known to all.

Christ and Life

Ruth’s story ends with David, and through David it reaches Jesus. Matthew includes “Boaz begot Obed by Ruth” in the genealogy of Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:5), and the town where Boaz redeemed Ruth is the same Bethlehem where the Son of David was born (Luke 2:4; Micah 5:2). The faithful love that brought a Moabite widow into Israel became part of the history through which God sent His Son.

Ruth does not name Christ, but read alongside the rest of Scripture, Boaz is a living picture of Jesus Christ, the greater Redeemer. Boaz could redeem because he was a relative; Christ took on our humanity so that He could redeem us, and “He is not ashamed to call them brethren” (Hebrews 2:11, 14). Boaz was “a man of great wealth” (2:1), able to pay what Naomi could not; Christ alone was able to pay the price of our redemption, not “with corruptible things, like silver or gold,” but “with the precious blood of Christ” (1 Peter 1:18–19). The nearer relative declined, “lest I ruin my own inheritance” (4:6); Christ willingly gave Himself, saying of His life, “No one takes it from Me, but I lay it down of Myself” (John 10:18). Boaz’s redemption restored a field and a family name. Christ’s redemption brings “the forgiveness of sins, according to the riches of His grace” (Ephesians 1:7).

The romance of redemption brings the redeemed into relationship. Boaz takes Ruth as his wife and gives her a settled place in his household. Christ’s self-giving love brings His people into union with Himself (Ephesians 5:25–32; Romans 7:4). Believers belong to the Redeemer and receive the riches of His grace. This belonging gives confidence in dependence on Him and strength for faithful responsibility.

Ruth asks Boaz, “Why have I found favor in your eyes, that you should take notice of me, since I am a foreigner?” (2:10). Her question expresses the wonder of grace. She comes from Moab, yet she is welcomed, protected, and redeemed. In the fuller revelation of the gospel, Gentiles who were once “strangers from the covenants of promise, having no hope and without God in the world” are brought near through Christ (Ephesians 2:12–13). They become “fellow citizens with the saints and members of the household of God” (Ephesians 2:19). Ruth’s refuge and belonging help us see the grace by which God receives us through our Redeemer, Jesus Christ.

Matthew also names Rahab as Boaz’s mother (Matthew 1:5). Her story enriches the background of his welcome of Ruth, another woman from outside Israel. It is reasonable to reflect on how her experience may have influenced him, while recognizing that Ruth does not describe that influence. His own words honor Ruth’s loyalty and refuge in the LORD (2:11–12).

Reading Guide

Read This Book Remembering…

Read Ruth remembering the grace of God toward this Moabite woman. Her people’s hostility and exclusion give weight to the welcome, redemption, and belonging she receives. Let the historical love story help you see the redeeming grace of Jesus Christ, while giving the people’s grief, choices, work, and kindness their full place.

Let the opening chapters prompt you to examine where you turn when you are in need and whether a temporary answer to hardship is becoming a settled way of life. Honor Naomi’s lament as the speech of a grieving believer, and notice the reality of God that Ruth recognizes in her. Orpah’s return can prompt reflection on choosing familiar, visible security; that application does not require making her the story’s villain.

Read the threshing-floor appeal through Ruth’s virtue, Naomi’s care, and Boaz’s integrity. Watch how blessings become practical responsibilities and are answered as the story unfolds. Trust the God whose purpose reaches beyond what these people can see. Romans 8:28–29 gives assurance of His good purpose without promising each reader the same earthly outcome.

Helpful Notes

Helpful Notes

Ruth’s place in the Bible. English Bibles place Ruth after Judges because of its setting, and it bridges the judges and the beginning of the monarchy in 1 Samuel. In the Hebrew Bible, Ruth belongs to the Writings, and it is traditionally read at the Feast of Weeks, the festival of the wheat harvest. In many printed Hebrew Bibles it follows Proverbs, so that the “virtuous wife” of Proverbs 31:10 is followed by Ruth, whom Boaz calls “a virtuous woman” (3:11).

Ruth and Deuteronomy 23:3. The Law said, “An Ammonite or Moabite shall not enter the assembly of the LORD.” Keep that background in view when reading of Ruth’s welcome and redemption. The book does not explain every question about how this law applied to her; it shows her trust in the LORD and her honored place in the family of David. The contrast between Moab’s exclusion and Ruth’s belonging is essential to seeing the grace of God in her story.

The sandal (4:7–8). The narrator explains that removing a sandal confirmed a transaction in former times. Deuteronomy 25:9 describes a different use of a sandal, when a brother refused his duty to his brother’s widow and she removed his sandal as a public disgrace. In Ruth the sandal simply confirms an exchange, and no one is shamed.

Translation differences. Where the NKJV reads “close relative” (2:20; 3:9), the NLT reads “family redeemer.” In 3:9 the NLT has “Spread the corner of your covering over me,” reflecting the double sense of the Hebrew word for “wing.” In 4:5 the NLT renders Boaz’s words as “your purchase of the land from Naomi also requires that you marry Ruth, the Moabite widow.”

Who is the “close relative” in 4:14. The women bless the LORD for not leaving Naomi “without a close relative,” and then speak of the child. Many understand the close relative here to be the newborn Obed, the one who will carry on the family and care for Naomi in her old age, rather than Boaz.

The genealogy (4:18–22). The line runs ten generations from Perez to David and parallels Matthew 1:3–6 and 1 Chronicles 2:4–15. Matthew adds that Boaz’s mother was Rahab (Matthew 1:5), so the family line of David includes Tamar, Rahab, and Ruth, women whose stories show God’s grace reaching beyond what anyone would expect.

Perez and Tamar (4:12). The elders’ blessing recalls Tamar, a widow whose story in Genesis 38 also concerns the continuation of a dead man’s line. Perez was the son she bore to Judah, and the genealogy at the end of Ruth begins with him.
