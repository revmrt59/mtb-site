Introduction to the Book of Ruth

Unifying Theme

The book of Ruth reveals the romance of redemption as God, through hidden providence and faithful love, brings an outsider into refuge, redemption, belonging, and ultimately into the lineage of the coming Redeemer, Jesus Christ.

Ultimately, Ruth is more than a story about survival after tragedy. It is a story about salvation by grace, redemption, refuge, covenant love, and belonging. Through Ruth and Boaz, the book points forward to Jesus Christ, the greater Redeemer, who brings hopeless outsiders near, covers them under His wings, restores what was lost, and brings His people into restored relationship with Himself.

I. Overview

Author

The human author of Ruth is not explicitly identified. Jewish tradition often associates the book with Samuel, though this cannot be proven.

Date Written

The exact date of composition is also uncertain. While the events of Ruth take place during the time of the Judges, many scholars believe the book itself was written much later, possibly during or after the exile. This conclusion is based on several internal features, including the author’s explanation of older customs, certain linguistic features, and forms of Biblical Hebrew that may reflect a later period.

Historical Setting

The events occur “in the days when the judges ruled” (Ruth 1:1), a spiritually dark and unstable period described in the book of Judges. During this time:

  • Israel repeatedly turned away from God

  • Moral compromise and idolatry were widespread

  • Society was unstable and often violent

  • “Everyone did what was right in his own eyes” (Judges 21:25)

Against this backdrop of spiritual unfaithfulness, Ruth presents a quiet yet powerful story of faithfulness, redemption, integrity, and loyal love.

Main Characters

  • Ruth (friend / companion): A Moabite widow who leaves her homeland to follow Naomi back to Bethlehem and embraces the God of Israel. Though a Gentile outsider, she becomes part of God’s people and ultimately an ancestor of Jesus Christ.

  • Naomi (pleasant / sweet): An Israelite wife and mother who experiences deep grief and bitterness through the loss of her husband and two sons in a foreign land before being fully restored by God's grace in Bethlehem.

  • Elimelech (My God is King): Naomi’s husband who, during a famine, took his family and left Bethlehem to settle in the idolatrous land of Moab in order to provide food for his family.

  • Mahlon (sickly / infirmity): Naomi’s elder son who marries Ruth but dies at a young age in Moab, showing the fragile and tragic end of the family's choice to leave home.

  • Chilion (wasting away / pining): Naomi’s younger son who marries Orpah and likewise dies in Moab, leaving the family completely broken, empty, and without any men to protect them.

  • Orpah (back of the neck / turning away): Naomi’s other daughter-in-law and Ruth’s sister-in-law. She initially began returning to Bethlehem after the death of her husband with Naomi but eventually returned to Moab.

  • Boaz (in him is strength): A wealthy, honorable Israelite landowner who acts with supreme integrity to legally redeem Naomi's land, marry Ruth, and serve as a one of the clearest Old Testament pictures of Christ as Redeemer.

  • Obed (servant / worshiper): The son born to Ruth and Boaz who brings joy and restoration to Naomi's empty arms and became the grandfather of King David.

II. Purpose of the Book

The book of Ruth has several purposes:

A. To Reveal God’s Providence

One of the major themes of Ruth is the hidden providence of God. Providence refers to God’s sovereign guidance, oversight, and active involvement in the events of life as He works out His purposes.

In Ruth, we see no dramatic miracles, visions, or direct speeches from God. Instead, God works quietly through ordinary events, human decisions, timing, relationships, and seemingly “chance” encounters to accomplish His redemptive plan.

The book teaches that God is actively governing events even when He seems silent.

B. To Show the Beauty of Faithful Love

A key Hebrew concept throughout Ruth is hesed - God’s steadfast covenant love expressed through loyal love, faithful kindness, mercy, grace, and faithful love.

This is displayed through:

  • Ruth’s loyalty to Naomi

  • Boaz’s generosity toward Ruth

  • God’s faithful love behind the scenes

C. To Explain David’s Lineage

The book provides the historical background for the family line of David and ultimately Jesus Christ. This is deeply significant because David was the covenant king through whose lineage God promised to bring the future Messiah and eternal King (2 Samuel 7:12–16). Ruth shows how God sovereignly preserved and guided that royal line through ordinary people and seemingly everyday circumstances.

The book also highlights the surprising grace of God’s redemptive plan. Ruth, a Moabite woman and an outsider to the people of God, is brought into:

  • the God’s people

  • the royal and messianic line

  • God’s redemptive purposes

This not only demonstrates God’s grace toward outsiders, but also anticipates the inclusion of the Gentiles in Christ. Ultimately, the book points beyond David to Jesus Christ, the greater Son of David, through whom God’s redemptive promises would be fulfilled.

D. To Display Redemption

The concept of the “kinsman-redeemer” is central to the book. In Israel, a near relative could act as a redeemer by preserving the family inheritance, restoring what had been lost, and raising up a name for a deceased relative. Boaz fulfills this role by redeeming Naomi’s inheritance, marrying Ruth, and preserving the family line. Boaz redeems Ruth, Naomi’s inheritance, and the family line. This becomes a powerful picture of Christ: our Redeemer, our Restorer, our Bridegroom, and the One who brings outsiders near

E. The Romance of Redemption

Ruth is one of Scripture’s clearest pictures of redemption expressed through faithful love and both judicial and relational restoration. Through the relationship between Ruth and Boaz, the book presents a beautiful portrait of the Redeemer bringing an outsider into God’s sheltering care, provision, belonging, inheritance, and ultimately belonging and union with the redeemer.

The story points beyond itself to Jesus Christ, the greater Redeemer, who brings His redeemed people into restored relationship with Himself. Ruth therefore reveals redemption not only as a legal act of rescue, but as a relationship that brings the redeemed into refuge, belonging, and nearness to the Redeemer.

III. Major Themes in Ruth

A. Redemption

The entire book is about redemption-the restoration of what has been lost through the work of a redeemer. Redemption in Ruth includes personal redemption, family redemption, and inheritance redemption. Through Boaz, the book provides a powerful type of Christ, the greater Redeemer who would ultimately bring full redemption to His people.

B. God’s Providence

God’s name is frequently mentioned, yet He never speaks directly in the book. This teaches that God is often working invisibly even when we do not see Him, hear Him, or sense His presence. Our ordinary lives are under God’s sovereign oversight and guidance

God’s name is frequently mentioned, yet He never speaks directly in the book.

C. Grace Toward Outsiders

Ruth was a Moabite, a people historically hostile toward Israel and restricted from the assembly of the LORD (Deuteronomy 23:3–6). Yet through faith in the God of Israel and loyal love, Ruth was graciously brought into God’s people and ultimately into the royal and messianic line. Her story powerfully demonstrates God’s grace toward outsiders and anticipates the inclusion of the Gentiles in Christ, foreshadowing the gospel going to the nations.

D. The Realities of Ordinary Life

Ruth is deeply practical and relatable to everyday life. The book deals with ordinary realities such as farming, widowhood, family responsibility, marriage, work, grief, provision, and daily obedience. Ruth teaches that walking with God is often expressed through quiet faithfulness in ordinary responsibilities, not only through dramatic spiritual moments.

E. Hope After Loss

Naomi begins empty and bitter, experiencing famine, death, loss, and disappointment. Yet God restores her beyond what she imagined. Ruth does not teach that loss is good, but it does show that God can work through loss for His good and redemptive purposes, beautifully illustrating the truth of Romans 8:28. The book repeatedly shows that God restores, redeems broken seasons, is not finished in suffering, and can bring good out of what grief could never have imagined.

F. The Covering of the Redeemer

An important theme in Ruth is refuge under the Redeemer’s covering. Ruth first comes under the wings of the LORD for refuge (Ruth 2:12), and later asks Boaz to spread his covering over her as redeemer (Ruth 3:9). Together these images beautifully point to the safety, covenant protection, and relational nearness ultimately found in Christ

G. Hesed

The Hebrew concept of fierce, sacrificial, covenant-keeping kindness. Ruth demonstrates it by abandoning her homeland to protect Naomi; Boaz models it by providing lavishly for an impoverished outsider; and God reflects it by answering their prayers and bringing restoration through Ruth.

IV. Structure of the Book

Chapter 1 - Loss and Loyalty

  • Famine drives Elimelech’s family to Moab

  • Naomi loses her husband and sons

  • Naomi returns to Bethlehem

  • Ruth chooses to remain with Naomi and commits herself to Israel’s God.

Chapter 2 - Providence and Provision

  • Ruth gleans in Boaz’s field

  • Boaz shows kindness and provision

  • God’s providence begins to emerge visibly

Chapter 3 - Redemption Sought

  • Naomi instructs Ruth regarding Boaz

  • Ruth approaches Boaz as redeemer

  • Boaz promises to act honorably

Chapter 4 - Redemption Accomplished

  • Boaz redeems Ruth and the inheritance

  • Ruth becomes Boaz’s wife and Obed is born

  • The genealogy leads to David

V. Ruth and the Gospel

Ruth contains many pictures that point forward to Christ.

A. Boaz as a Type of Christ

Boaz willingly redeems, pays the price, acts with compassion toward the needy, restores inheritance, takes a Gentile bride, and spreads his covering over Ruth to bring her into covenant union, protecting and providing for her. Jesus fulfills these realities perfectly.

B. Ruth as a Picture of the Redeemed

Ruth was an outsider, poor, helpless, and without inheritance. Yet through redemption she is brought near, accepted, restored, and given a new future. This mirrors salvation in Christ, where outsiders and enemies receive provision, protection, rest, and union with the redeemer.

C. The Kinsman-Redeemer

The redeemer had to be qualified, be willing, and pay the price. Christ fulfills all three perfectly.

D. Inclusion of the Gentiles

Ruth’s inclusion anticipates the gospel to the nations, one people of God in Christ, and grace expanding beyond national Israel.

VII. Why Ruth Matters Today

Ruth speaks powerfully to modern believers because it addresses grief, uncertainty, ordinary life, hidden seasons, waiting, faithfulness, redemption, God’s providence, belonging, and restoration. The book reminds believers that:

  • God is working even when unseen

  • no life is beyond redemption

  • ordinary obedience matters

  • bitterness is not the end of the story

  • God weaves personal stories into His larger redemptive plan

Ruth is a spiritual homecoming that points believers toward salvation by grace, belonging, refuge, identity, and the family of God.

VIII. Theological Contributions

  • God’s providence in ordinary life

  • Faithful love (hesed)

  • Redemption through a kinsman-redeemer

  • Grace toward Gentiles

  • Restoration after suffering (Rom 8:28)

  • Faithfulness amid spiritual darkness

  • The messianic line leading to David and Christ

  • The inclusion of outsiders into God’s people

IX. Key Words and Concepts

  • Redemption - the restoration or recovery of what has been lost through the action of a redeemer.

  • Redeemer - one who rescues, restores, or buys back another, often at personal cost.

  • Hesed - steadfast covenant love expressed through faithful kindness, mercy, loyalty, and grace.

  • Providence - God’s sovereign guidance and active involvement in human events to accomplish His purposes.

  • Faithfulness - steadfast obedience, reliability, loyalty, and devotion toward God and others.

  • Refuge - a place of safety, protection, security, and rest found ultimately in God.

  • Restoration - the renewing, rebuilding, or returning of what has been broken, empty, or lost.

  • Inheritance - the family possession, blessing, identity, or covenant portion passed down through generations.

  • Loyal love - faithful commitment and devotion within a restored relationship.

  • Grace - God’s undeserved favor, kindness, and generous blessing toward others.

  • Harvest - the gathering of provision and fruit, often symbolizing God’s blessing and provision.

  • Refuge under God’s wings - a picture of trusting in God’s protection, care, nearness, and covenant shelter.
