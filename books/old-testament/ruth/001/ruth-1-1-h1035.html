BETHLEHEM - ( bet-lechem , Strong's H1035 )

Summary:

Bethlehem, literally meaning "House of Bread," is a historically and theologically significant town in the tribal territory of Judah. In Ruth 1:1, it serves as the setting for a profound irony: a severe famine strikes the very place known as the "House of Bread," driving Elimelech and his family to seek sustenance in pagan Moab. This geographical and etymological detail highlights the themes of spiritual and physical drought, divine discipline, and the sovereign orchestration of redemption, as Bethlehem ultimately becomes the birthplace of King David and, centuries later, Jesus Christ, the Bread of Life.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Bethlehem is situated in the fertile hill country of Judah, approximately five miles south of Jerusalem.

• Originally known as Ephrath or Ephrathah, the region was famous for its agricultural productivity, characterized by olive groves, vineyards, and barley fields.

• The name is a compound of two Hebrew words: bayit (house) and lechem (bread, food, or grain). This designates the town as a place of agricultural abundance, nourishment, and reliable sustenance.

• As a geographic and cultural center, it represents the heart of the covenant land where Yahweh promised to bless His people with physical and spiritual fruitfulness in response to their obedience.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The proper noun Bet Lechem designates the specific town of Bethlehem in Judah, distinguished from a northern town of the same name in the territory of Zebulun.

• The literal meaning of the compound root is "House of Bread" or "Place of Food."

• While cognate languages such as Arabic suggest a root meaning of "House of Meat" or "House of Flesh," the Old Testament use of lechem consistently denotes grain, bread, and the staple foods produced from the earth.

• The name points to a physical reality of fertility and a spiritual reality of divine provision.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The name Bethlehem is used as a geographic marker of transition and covenant history throughout the Old Testament. It is the burial site of Rachel and the home of Boaz, Jesse, and David.

• The "House of Bread" serves as a physical word picture for God's covenant blessings. When God's people walk in obedience, the House of Bread is full.

• Conversely, a famine in Bethlehem serves as a stark word picture of covenant curse and spiritual bankruptcy. It visually demonstrates the reality of Deuteronomy 28, where disobedience causes the heavens to become brass and the earth to withhold its harvest.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Consequences: A famine in Bethlehem (Ruth 1:1) is not a mere natural disaster; it is a theological alarm. It reveals that Israel's rebellion during the era of the Judges has resulted in the withdrawal of God's physical blessings from His own "House of Bread."

• Divine Providence: Despite the famine, Bethlehem remains the anchor of God's redemptive plan. The book of Ruth traces the journey from empty famine in Bethlehem, to tragic loss in Moab, and back to a restored, overflowing harvest in Bethlehem.

• Messianic Typology: Bethlehem is the geographic link connecting the narrative of Ruth to King David, and ultimately to Jesus Christ. The "House of Bread" is chosen by God to produce the Savior of the world. Jesus, born in Bethlehem, declares Himself to be the "Bread of Life" (John 6:35), satisfying the ultimate spiritual hunger of humanity.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:1, the mention of Bethlehem in the same breath as a famine creates an immediate literary tension. The author highlights the irony: there is no bread in the House of Bread.

• This tension explains Elimelech's decision to leave. Rather than repenting and waiting on the Lord in the place of covenant promise, Elimelech abandons the "House of Bread" to seek food in Moab, a nation historically hostile to God's people.

• The spiritual climate of the Judges is thus mirrored in Elimelech's geographic departure. He leaves the place of promise because the physical signs of God's presence have departed due to the nation's sin.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 35:19: "So Rachel died and was buried on the way to Ephrath (that is, Bethlehem)."

• Ruth 1:19: "Now the two of them went until they came to Bethlehem. And it came to pass, when they had come to Bethlehem, that all the city was excited because of them; and they said, 'Is this Naomi?'"

• 1 Samuel 16:4: "So Samuel did what the LORD said, and came to Bethlehem. And the elders of the town trembled at his coming, and said, 'Do you come peaceably?'"

• Micah 5:2: "But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah, Though you are little among the thousands of Judah, Yet out of you shall come forth to Me The One to be Ruler in Israel, Whose goings forth are from of old, From everlasting."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The geographical identifier "Bethlehem, Judah" (or Bethlehem-Judah) is used specifically in Ruth 1:1 to prevent confusion with the Bethlehem located in the territory of Zebulun (Joshua 19:15).

• The resolution of the famine is described in Ruth 1:6 when Naomi hears that "the LORD had visited His people in giving them bread." This marks the literal restoration of Bethlehem to its namesake identity: the House of Bread once again has bread.

• The harvesting of barley and wheat in the fields of Bethlehem (Ruth 1:22, 2:23) serves as the backdrop for the courtship of Ruth and Boaz, demonstrating that God restores both the land and the lineage of His people in the same sacred space.
