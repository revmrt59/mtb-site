SONS - ( ben , Strong's H1121 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ben refers primarily to a male offspring, son, or descendant. In the context of Ruth 1:1, it denotes the two physical sons of Elimelech and Naomi, Mahlon and Chilion. Symbolically and legally in ancient Israel, sons represented the continuation of the family name, the preservation of the ancestral inheritance, and the future security of the household. In the opening movement of the book of Ruth, these two sons represent both the hope of family survival and, through their subsequent deaths, the tragic loss of legacy that sets the stage for God's sovereign redemption.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun ben is closely related to the verbal root banah, which means "to build." This etymological connection provides a beautiful word picture: a son is one who builds up the family house, preserving its name, lineage, and physical estate for future generations. In the ancient Near East, a household was not merely a private nuclear family but a building block of the covenant community. Sons were the literal "builders" of the ancestral house. Without sons, a family line would face extinction, and their allotted land in Israel—a tangible sign of God's covenant promise—would pass to another clan.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of ben is a male child or son. However, the semantic range of this word is extensive across the Old Testament:

• Direct Male Offspring: A literal son born to a father and mother.

• Descendant / Grandson: Plural forms (banim) often refer to grandchildren or descendants throughout generations (e.g., "sons of Israel").

• Member of a Class or Guild: It can designate belonging to a group, such as "sons of the prophets" (prophetic guild) or "sons of the exile" (captives).

• Characterization: It is used idiomatically to describe a person's character, such as "son of worthlessness" (a wicked person) or "son of death" (one deserving to die).

In Ruth 1:1, the word is used in its primary, literal sense to denote the two direct male offspring born to Elimelech and Naomi.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, the term ben is employed to illustrate profound spiritual and covenantal realities:

• The Heritage of the Lord: Children, specifically sons (banim), are described as a heritage and a reward from God. They are arrows in the hand of a warrior, representing strength, defense, and future impact.

• The Household Builder: Because of the connection to banah ("to build"), the presence of sons is equated with a established, secured home. To be without sons was to have one's "house" broken down or left unfinished.

• The Covenant Carrier: The covenant promises made to Abraham were passed down from father to son (Abraham to Isaac, Isaac to Jacob). Thus, the word ben carries heavy theological weight regarding the transmission of divine promises.

### 4. Theological Implications

The presence of "sons" in the opening verse of Ruth introduces a central theological theme of the book: the preservation of the seed (zera) and the redemption of the family line. God's covenant with Israel tied land ownership to specific families in perpetuity. When a man died without leaving a son, his name was threatened with being wiped out from Israel.

In the broader scope of biblical theology, the preservation of the line of Judah—specifically the family of Elimelech—was vital for the messianic promise. The line of the ultimate "Son"—the Messiah—was woven through the survival of this specific family. The tragic loss of both sons in Moab highlights the vulnerability of the messianic line and underscores the necessity of divine providence to rescue and rebuild Naomi's empty house.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:1, the mention of "his two sons" heightens the tragedy of the family's departure from Bethlehem ("House of Bread") to Moab. The narrator introduces a family of four: a husband, a wife, and their two sons. The names of the sons, Mahlon ("sickly") and Chilion ("pining" or "frail"), contrast sharply with the hope of strength and building that the word ben normally evokes.

By noting that Elimelech had "two sons," the text initially assures the reader of the family's survival and future security. They have double security for their name and inheritance. However, when both young men die in Moab (Ruth 1:5) without leaving offspring, the tragedy is complete. Naomi is left without husband or "sons." The emptiness of Naomi's home is a physical picture of Israel's spiritual desolation during the period of the Judges, where everyone did what was right in their own eyes, leading to spiritual barrenness and death.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The cultural answer to the loss of a son was the law of Levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25:5-6). If a married man died without a son, his brother was obligated to marry the widow, and the firstborn son of that union would legally succeed the deceased brother's name. This legal background explains the urgency of Naomi's lament in Ruth 1:11-13, where she asks her daughters-in-law if she still has "sons in her womb" to marry them. The resolution of the book of Ruth hinges entirely on finding a kinsman-redeemer (Boaz) who is willing to marry Ruth to buy back the land and "raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance" (Ruth 4:5), culminating in the birth of a new son, Obed, who restores life and legacy to Naomi's empty house.
