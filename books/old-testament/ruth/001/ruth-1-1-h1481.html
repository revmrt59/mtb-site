DWELL - ( gur , Strong's H1481 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb gur refers to residing in a foreign land as a temporary visitor or resident alien, lacking native civil rights and depending entirely on the hospitality and protection of others. In Ruth 1:1, Elimelech takes his family to "dwell" in Moab to escape a local famine, framing their departure from Judah not as a permanent relocation but as a temporary survival strategy that ultimately tests their covenant faithfulness.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, identity, legal protection, and survival were deeply tied to one's ancestral land and tribe. To leave one's home territory was to step outside of this safety net. A person who did this became a ger (the noun form of gur), a resident alien.

• Unlike a passing tourist (orach) or a foreign merchant (nokri), a ger settled down for an extended period in a host community but never gained ancestral land rights.

• Because they possessed no land of their own, sojourners were socially vulnerable, often grouped in biblical law alongside orphans and widows as those who required special protection under God's covenantal care.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, gur means to sojourn, abide, dwell as a stranger, or live as a temporary resident alien.

• The term explicitly denotes dwelling in a place that is not one's original home.

• It carries the strong connotation of transience, emphasizing that the stayer's presence is dependent on the goodwill of the host population.

• It is distinguished from verbs indicating permanent settlement or citizenship, portraying a lifestyle of displacement where one remains an outsider.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently uses gur to describe the lives of the patriarchs.

• Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob lived as sojourners (gur) in the land of Canaan, pitching tents and moving as nomads without ever owning the land, except for burial plots.

• Israel's history is defined by this word; they "went down to Egypt to dwell" (Genesis 47:4) under Joseph's protection, which eventually turned into harsh bondage.

• The physical word picture associated with gur is that of a traveler who unpacks their bags but never takes the wheels off the cart. It is a state of constant provisionality.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, gur carries severe spiritual weight, especially when it involves leaving the land of promise.

• God promised to sustain His covenant people in the land of Canaan. Leaving the land of inheritance to "dwell" in a foreign territory often signaled a failure of faith, a reliance on sight rather than the promises of God.

• Moab was a nation born out of incest, historically hostile to Israel, and spiritually devoted to the pagan god Chemosh. To sojourn in Moab was to place oneself and one's family under pagan influence, outside the immediate sphere of the covenant community.

• Conversely, God uses the vulnerability of the sojourner to display His grace. The ultimate redemption in the book of Ruth occurs because a Moabitess sojourns back to Israel, proving that faith transcends geographic and ethnic borders.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:1, the famine in Bethlehem ("House of Bread") creates a stark irony. Elimelech, whose name means "My God is King," decides to "dwell" (gur) in Moab.

• The use of gur indicates that Elimelech's original intention was likely a brief, temporary stay—a quick escape until the famine subsided.

• However, the narrative exposes the danger of the temporary detour. What began as a temporary sojourn (gur) in verse 1 quickly degenerates into permanent settlement. By verse 4, the text shifts vocabulary, stating they "dwelt" (yashab—to settle down, marry, and establish a permanent household) there for about ten years.

• This transition from a temporary sojourn to permanent integration in a pagan land brings tragic consequences: the deaths of Elimelech and his two sons, leaving Naomi completely destitute.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:10 - "Now there was a famine in the land, and Abram went down to Egypt to dwell there, for the famine was severe in the land."

• Genesis 26:3 - "Dwell in this land, and I will be with you and bless you; for to you and your descendants I give all these lands, and I will perform the oath which I swore to Abraham your father;"

• Genesis 47:4 - "And they said to Pharaoh, 'We have come to dwell in the land, because your servants have no pasture for their flocks, for the famine is severe in the land of Canaan.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), gur is frequently translated by the verb paroikeo (to live alongside as an alien).

• This directly connects to the New Testament concept of the Christian life. In 1 Peter 2:11, believers are addressed as "sojourners (paroikoi) and pilgrims."

• Just as Elimelech was called to remember that Moab was not his home, Christians are reminded that this world is a place of temporary sojourn. The danger for the believer is identical to Elimelech's: turning a temporary sojourn into a permanent settlement.
