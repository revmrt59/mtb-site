CAME TO PASS - ( hayah , Strong's H1961 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb hayah fundamentally means to exist, happen, become, or come to pass. In the historical narrative of the Old Testament, its specific transitional form (wayehi—"now it came to pass") serves as a vital literary and theological bridge. In Ruth 1:1, this word is far more than a routine introductory phrase; it firmly anchors the subsequent narrative of grief, loyalty, and redemption in the harsh reality of actual human history. By utilizing hayah, the text signals that the events of Ruth’s life did not happen by random chance, but were part of an unfolding, divinely ordered reality governed by the self-existent God who active presence brings all history to pass.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew concept of being and existence is dynamic rather than static. Unlike classical Greek philosophy, which often views "being" as an abstract, unchanging state of existence, Hebrew thought understands existence as active, relational, and demonstrative. The root hayah does not merely point to the passive existence of an object, but to its active presence, its coming-into-being, or its manifestation in history. It presents a dynamic movement where something transitions from one state to another, or makes its presence actively felt in the world. When historical narratives open with the form wayehi ("and it came to pass"), it creates a word picture of a curtain rising on a stage, signaling that a significant, divinely supervised movement of history is about to unfold.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb hayah is a primary Hebrew root occurring over 3,500 times in the Old Testament. Its fundamental semantic range includes:

• To exist, to be, or to abide.

• To happen, occur, take place, or fall out.

• To become or be established (frequently when followed by the preposition le).

• To accompany or remain with someone (expressing active presence).

In the Qal stem, the verb most frequently denotes coming into existence or happening. When inflected as a waw-consecutive imperfect (wayehi), it functions as an introductory formula in historical books, translated as "now it came to pass," "and it happened," or "and it was." This construction acts as a narrative engine, initiating a new historical sequence and linking the events to the broader timeline of God's redemptive work.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The dynamic nature of hayah is illustrated in several distinct ways across the Old Testament:

• The Creative Command: In Genesis 1:3, the word is used twice: "Let there be (hayah) light; and there was (hayah) light." This demonstrates that the word carries the weight of creative power. When God speaks, absolute reality comes into being.

• The Covenantal Relationship: The verb is central to the recurrent covenant formula: "I will be (hayah) your God, and you shall be (hayah) My people" (Jeremiah 7:23). Here, the word pictures an active, binding, and living relationship, rather than a mere legal status.

• The Divine Presence: In commission narratives, such as with Moses or Joshua, God promises, "I will be (hayah) with you" (Exodus 3:12; Joshua 1:5). This usage offers a word picture of protective, personal companionship accompanying the believer into difficult assignments.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of hayah is tied directly to the self-revelation of God. When Moses asked God for His name at the burning bush, God replied, "I AM (hayah) WHO I AM (hayah)" (Exodus 3:14). The sacred name of God, Yahweh (YHWH), is etymologically derived from this same root.

Therefore, hayah points to:

• Aseity and Self-Existence: God is the only One who exists inherently of Himself. All other things "come to pass" because He causes them to be, but He simply is.

• Sovereign Providence: Because God is the ultimate "I AM," nothing in history happens outside of His sovereign decree. When events "come to pass" (wayehi), they do so under the watchful eye of the One who governs all historical developments.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:1, the verb hayah appears twice in rapid succession in the opening sentence: "Now it came to pass (wayehi), in the days when the judges ruled, that there was (wayehi) a famine in the land."

The first use ("came to pass") connects this intimate family story of Ruth, Naomi, and Boaz directly to the macro-narrative of Israel's covenant history. It transitions the reader from the chaotic, apostate era of the Judges into a specific household's journey.

The second use ("there was a famine") reveals the spiritual climate of the day. In the Old Testament, famine in the Promised Land was not a random meteorological accident; it was a covenantal consequence of Israel's rebellion (Deuteronomy 28:15-24). By stating that "there was a famine," the writer uses hayah to show that God's covenant warnings had actively materialized in history. Despite this dark setting of national failure and physical drought, the double use of hayah assures the reader that God's hand is still moving. He is present in the famine, and He is orchestrating the events that will ultimately lead to the birth of King David and, eventually, the Messiah.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3: "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light."

• Genesis 12:2: "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you and make your name great; and you shall be a blessing."

• Exodus 3:14: "And God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.' And He said, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel, "I AM has sent me to you."'"

• Joshua 1:5: "No man shall be able to stand before you all the days of your life; as I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will not leave you nor forsake you."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A structural analysis of the Book of Ruth reveals a beautiful narrative arc. The book opens in Ruth 1:1 with wayehi ("now it came to pass"), introducing a season of famine, flight, and death. However, the narrative progresses through grief and loyalty to a climax of redemption. The entire book is designed to show how the painful, everyday events that "come to pass" in our lives are being woven together by God. The "I AM" who rules over famines and empty fields is the same God who provides a redeemer in the end. For the believer, no event in life is a random occurrence; every chapter "comes to pass" under the supervision of a loving and sovereign Lord.
