MAN - ( 'ish , Strong's H376 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ish refers to an individual man, husband, or person of note, emphasizing personality, responsibility, and headship. In Ruth 1:1, it introduces Elimelech as a "certain man" of Bethlehem who, as the head of his household, made the critical decision to lead his family away from the Promised Land to the pagan fields of Moab during a famine. This designation highlights his personal agency and the heavy weight of his covenantal responsibility as a husband and father.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun 'ish denotes an individual, a male of status, a husband, or a person acting with volition and responsibility. Unlike adam (which often denotes mankind generically or dust-born humanity) or enosh (which highlights human frailty and mortality), 'ish emphasizes presence, strength, and existential standing. In the ancient Near East, an 'ish was the functional representative of his household, carrying the legal, spiritual, and social identity of his family.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The word refers to an individual male human being, distinct from women ('ishshah) and children.

• It denotes a husband in marital contexts, representing the head of the domestic unit.

• It means "anyone," "each," or "every" when used in a distributive sense to emphasize individual accountability.

• It designates a man of high standing, capability, or nobility, often contrasted with the lowly or common person.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Relational identity: The term is deeply connected to covenantal relationships, first appearing in Genesis 2:23 to describe the complementary bond between man and woman.

• Functional agency: It is used to describe heroes, champions, or leaders who stand out from the crowd due to their character or strength.

• Distributive responsibility: The word is used idiomatically to describe "each man" taking action, highlighting individual moral agency before God.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenantal Headship: As an 'ish, a man is positioned as the spiritual and physical provider for his household. His decisions directly impact the covenant community and his immediate family.

• Volitional Responsibility: The term underscores that human history is shaped by the moral choices of individual men who are accountable to God. When a leader acts outside of faith, the consequences ripple through generations.

• The Ultimate 'Ish: The Old Testament anticipates a "Man" of destiny—the suffering servant and the ultimate King—who would perfectly fulfill the covenantal role that earthly men like Elimelech failed to sustain.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:1, Elimelech is introduced as "a certain man of Bethlehem, Judah." The use of 'ish here is highly ironic and tragic. Bethlehem means "House of Bread," and Judah means "Praise." Yet, this "man" of covenant promise decides to leave the place of blessing due to a temporary famine.

• Rather than standing firm in faith in the land of inheritance, this 'ish takes his family to Moab, a historical enemy of Israel. His role as the 'ish (husband/head) means his failure to trust God leads his wife ('ishshah) and sons into spiritual compromise and physical death in a foreign land.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:23 - "And Adam said: 'This is now bone of my bones And flesh of my flesh; She shall be called Woman, Because she was taken out of Man.'"

• 1 Samuel 1:1 - "Now there was a certain man of Ramathaim Zophim, of the mountains of Ephraim, and his name was Elkanah the son of Jeroham, the son of Elihu, the son of Tohu, the son of Zuph, an Ephraimite."

• Psalm 1:1 - "Blessed is the man Who walks not in the counsel of the ungodly, Nor stands in the path of sinners, Nor sits in the seat of the scornful;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between 'ish and 'adam is crucial in Hebrew literature. 'Adam points to human frailty, whereas 'ish points to strength, character, and individuality.

• In the Book of Ruth, the tragedy of chapter 1 is the loss of the 'ish (both Elimelech and his sons die, leaving Naomi without her men). The restoration of the narrative comes through another 'ish—Boaz—who is described as a "man of great wealth" in Ruth 2:1, who acts righteously to redeem the family.
