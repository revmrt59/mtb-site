MOAB - ( Mo'ab , Strong's H4124 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:1, Moab represents a nearby pagan land of compromise, spiritual hazard, and tragic loss. When Elimelech leads his family from Bethlehem—the "House of Bread"—to the fertile plains of Moab during a famine, he exchanges the covenant territory of God's people for a region born of incest, plagued by idolatry, and historically hostile to Israel. This geographical transition highlights the danger of seeking worldly solutions to spiritual trials, setting the stage for a story of profound emptiness that only God's sovereign grace can redeem.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

• Moab's origin dates back to the catastrophic aftermath of Sodom's destruction. Lot’s eldest daughter, believing no men were left on earth to preserve their family line, intoxicated her father and conceived a son, naming him Moab. The nation descended from this union carried the permanent reminder of this incestuous origin.

• Geographically, Moab was a fertile plateau situated east of the Dead Sea, bounded by the deep Arnon gorge to the north and the Zered brook to the south. Though physically lush and often spared from the immediate severity of famines affecting Israel's hill country, it was spiritually dark.

• The Moabites worshiped Chemosh, a cruel deity requiring child sacrifice. Throughout Israel’s wilderness wanderings and the period of the Judges, Moab was a persistent adversary, hiring Balaam to curse Israel and later oppressing them under King Eglon.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The proper noun Mo'ab refers primarily to the son of Lot, the nation descended from him, and the geographical territory they inhabited east of the Dead Sea.

• The name is etymologically derived from the prefix mi- (from) and the noun ab (father), literally meaning "from a father" or "water of a father," pointing directly to the paternal origin of the nation through Lot.

• In biblical usage, the term designates both the geopolitical state of Moab and, collectively, the Moabite people who frequently clashed with the covenant nation of Israel.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Moab functions as a vivid biblical word picture for fleshly compromise and false security. It represents a place that is physically near to the Promised Land but spiritually estranged from the covenant of Yahweh.

• The prophets depict Moab as a symbol of haughtiness, self-sufficiency, and pride because of its natural mountain defenses and agricultural prosperity.

• Fleeing to Moab represents the temptation to bypass God's refining discipline. Rather than repenting under the famine sent by God, Elimelech chose the comfortable, pagan plains of Moab, illustrating how believers can be tempted to seek relief in the world rather than restoration through repentance.

### 4. Theological Implications

• The presence of Moab in the book of Ruth demonstrates the radical, barrier-breaking reach of God's saving grace. By covenant law, Deuteronomy 23:3 barred Moabites from entering the assembly of Yahweh to the tenth generation.

• Despite this covenant exclusion and Moab's tainted history, God redeems Ruth the Moabitess, demonstrating that faith overrides national and historical barriers.

• The narrative showcases how God sovereignly uses even the tragic, faithless detour of a family into a cursed land to bring forth the lineage of King David and ultimately Jesus Christ. The grace of God shines brightest when it redeems what was deemed unclean and hopeless.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:1, the mention of Moab serves as a sharp moral and spiritual contrast to Bethlehem. Bethlehem means "House of Bread" and Judah means "Praise," yet there is a famine in this land of promise.

• Elimelech’s decision to leave the land of covenant blessing to "dwell" (meaning to sojourn or live as an alien) in Moab reveals a lapse in faith. The narrative transition from Bethlehem to Moab is a downward spiritual trajectory.

• What began as a temporary journey to escape a famine ended in a decade-long settlement, resulting in the death of Elimelech and his two sons. Moab became a graveyard for those who sought life outside the borders of God's inheritance.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 19:37 - "And the firstborn bore a son and called his name Moab; he is the father of the Moabites to this day."

• Judges 3:12 - "And the children of Israel again did evil in the sight of the LORD. So the LORD strengthened Eglon king of Moab against Israel, because they had done evil in the sight of the LORD."

• Deuteronomy 34:5 - "So Moses the servant of the LORD died there in the land of Moab, according to the word of the LORD."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Moabites were closely related to the Israelites by blood through Lot, Abraham's nephew, making their frequent hostility and spiritual seduction of Israel particularly painful.

• The soil of Moab, while fertile, was ultimately a place of death for the Israelite family. The names of Elimelech's sons, Mahlon ("sickly") and Chilion ("pining/failing"), underscore the spiritual decay and ultimate futility of trying to flourish in a pagan land.

• Ruth’s absolute rejection of Moab, its gods, and its culture in Ruth 1:16 ("Your people shall be my people, and your God, my God") highlights the magnitude of her conversion. She willingly abandoned the security of her homeland to embrace the God of Israel.
