FAMINE - ( ra'ab , Strong's H7458 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun ra'ab denotes a severe shortage of food, hunger, or famine, which often serves in Scripture as a physical manifestation of covenantal breakdown, divine discipline, or a sovereign test of faith. In Ruth 1:1, this famine strikes Bethlehem—ironically meaning "House of Bread"—forcing Elimelech and his family to make a pivotal, faith-testing decision to migrate to pagan Moab.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, agrarian societies were entirely dependent on seasonal rainfall to water their crops and sustain their livestock. Unlike Egypt, which relied on the predictable flooding of the Nile, Israel depended directly on rain from heaven. A lack of rain quickly led to crop failure, dry pastures, and devastating famine. In this cultural context, famine (ra'ab) was not merely an economic hardship; it was a life-threatening crisis that disrupted the entire social fabric, brought commerce to a halt, and threatened families with starvation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun ra'ab means famine, hunger, or dearth. It refers to an extreme scarcity of food affecting an entire region or nation. The word is derived from the root verb ra'eb, which means to be hungry or to suffer hunger. Semantically, ra'ab describes the objective state of food shortage in a land, whereas the related noun ra'ab (with slightly different vocalization) can refer to the subjective sensation of hunger felt by an individual. It represents a state of barrenness and desolation where the earth fails to yield its strength.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, ra'ab occurs over 100 times, frequently associated with national catastrophe. It is repeatedly paired with other agents of divine judgment: the sword (warfare) and pestilence (disease). This "deadly trio" paints a picture of complete societal collapse. The prophets use ra'ab to depict a land that has been shut up by God, where the fields mourn, the storehouses are laid desolate, and the joy of the harvest is entirely cut off.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, famine in the Promised Land is deeply tied to the covenant relationship between Yahweh and Israel.

• Under the Mosaic Covenant, agricultural abundance was promised as a blessing for obedience, while drought and famine were explicitly warned of as consequences of disobedience and idolatry (Deuteronomy 28:23-24).

• Famine is rarely viewed in Scripture as a random ecological accident; it is frequently portrayed as the hand of God seeking to get the attention of His wandering people.

• However, God also uses famines as sovereign tools to test, refine, and guide His chosen instruments. Famines drove Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob to foreign lands, ultimately positioning Joseph in Egypt to save the covenant family. Thus, famine serves as a crucible where faith is tested against the temptation of self-preservation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:1, the occurrence of ra'ab carries profound literary and spiritual irony.

• The setting is "the days when the judges ruled," a historical period marked by repeated cycles of apostasy, oppression, and moral decay. The famine is a physical warning sign that Israel has broken covenant with God.

• The geography heightens the tragedy: the famine occurs in "Bethlehem in Judah." Beth-lehem literally means "House of Bread." The House of Bread has run out of bread. This stark reality reflects the spiritual emptiness of the nation at large.

• Confronted by this ra'ab, Elimelech faces a crisis of faith. Instead of repenting and waiting on the Lord in the land of promise, he chooses to escape the discipline of God by leaving for the pagan fields of Moab. The famine serves as the catalyst that exposes the spiritual condition of Elimelech's household and sets in motion a story of loss that only God's sovereign grace can redeem.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:10 - "Now there was a famine in the land, and Abram went down to Egypt to dwell there, for the famine was severe in the land."

• Genesis 41:57 - "So all countries came to Joseph in Egypt to buy grain, because the famine was severe in all lands."

• 1 Kings 18:2 - "So Elijah went to present himself to Ahab; and there was a severe famine in Samaria."

• Psalm 33:19 - "To deliver their soul from death, And to keep them alive in famine."

• Amos 8:11 - "'Behold, the days are coming,' says the Lord GOD, 'That I will send a famine on the land, Not a famine of bread, Nor a thirst for water, But of hearing the words of the LORD.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Physical vs. Spiritual Hunger: The ultimate development of ra'ab in the Old Testament occurs in the prophets, particularly Amos, where physical famine is used as a metaphor for spiritual starvation. When a nation repeatedly rejects God's Word, He sends a famine of "hearing the words of the LORD."

• Redemptive Reversal: The book of Ruth begins with a famine (ra'ab) that empties Naomi's life of her husband, sons, and sustenance. However, the story ends with the harvest, the provision of food, and the birth of Obed. God reverses the famine of the womb and the famine of the land, demonstrating that His redemptive purposes will prevail even over the consequences of covenant disobedience.
