COUNTRY - ( sadeh , Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh primarily refers to a field, open country, or territory. In Ruth 1:1, translated as "country," it refers to the foreign territory or arable lands of Moab. Elimelech’s decision to leave Bethlehem ("House of Bread") during a famine to dwell in the sadeh of Moab highlights a physical and spiritual transition from the covenant land of Israel to a foreign, pagan territory in search of sustenance.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word sadeh derives from an ancient root that suggests a level, open expanse of land, particularly arable land or cultivated fields. It represents the space outside the safety of walled cities where crops are grown, livestock graze, and wild beasts roam. In the ancient Near East, the sadeh was essential for survival as the primary source of agriculture, but it also represented exposure and vulnerability. To step out into the "fields" or "country" of a foreign nation meant leaving the protection of one's home community and covenant boundaries to live in an exposed, unfamiliar environment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun sadeh carries several closely related definitions:

• Cultivated ground, fields, or agricultural land used for growing crops.

• Open pastureland or uncultivated wilderness where wild animals live.

• The territory, domain, or region belonging to a specific nation or people group.

In Ruth 1:1, the word functions in the construct state (sedeh Moab), signifying the "territory of Moab" or the "fields of Moab." It denotes the geographical and political region of the Moabites, a fertile plateau situated east of the Dead Sea and the Jordan River.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word depicts several distinct pictures in Scripture:

• The Place of Provision: The fields where Naomi and Ruth later glean represent the place of daily bread and God's sovereign care.

• The Open, Unprotected Country: Unwalled and exposed territory, contrasting with the security of a fortified town or city.

• Foreign Territory: When combined with a national name (such as Moab or Edom), it describes the geopolitical borders and physical landscape of a foreign nation.

4. Theological Implications

The transition from the "land" (erets) of Israel to the "country" (sadeh) of Moab represents a significant theological crisis. Israel was the Promised Land, the physical sphere of God's covenant blessings, laws, and administrative presence. Moab was a historic adversary, born of incest and excluded from the assembly of Yahweh. By leaving the covenant land to live in the "fields of Moab," Elimelech prioritized immediate physical survival over long-term spiritual faithfulness. He took his family out of the sphere of covenant protection and placed them in a spiritually compromising environment.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:1, the contrast is stark. There is a famine in the "land" (the Promised Land, specifically Bethlehem, which means "House of Bread"). To escape this, Elimelech goes to the "country" (fields/territory) of Moab. This move is intended "to dwell" (to sojourn temporarily), but it results in a ten-year residency and tragedy. The "fields of Moab" proved to be a place of death for Elimelech and his two sons, demonstrating that seeking life outside the boundaries of God's covenant provision ultimately leads to emptiness.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The book of Ruth transitions from the tragic "fields of Moab" in chapter 1 to the redemptive "fields of Boaz" in Bethlehem in chapter 2. The same Hebrew word sadeh is used in both contexts. While the fields of Moab brought famine of soul, bereavement, and loss, the fields of Boaz brought harvest, protection, restoration, and ultimate redemption through the Kinsman-Redeemer.
