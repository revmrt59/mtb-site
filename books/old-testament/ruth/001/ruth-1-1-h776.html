LAND - ( 'erets , Strong's H776 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'erets refers broadly to the earth, a specific country, territory, or a localized piece of land. In the opening of the book of Ruth, it designates the promised land of Canaan—specifically the region of Judah—which is experiencing a devastating famine. This physical crisis of the land exposes a deeper spiritual crisis during the era of the Judges, highlighting the tension between God’s covenant promises of fruitfulness and the immediate reality of judgment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, land was the ultimate source of life, security, and identity. For the Hebrew people, 'erets was never merely neutral soil or real estate; it was a deeply theological entity. The land was the physical sphere of God’s covenant relationship with Israel. It was the inheritance promised to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. The productivity of the land was directly tied to Israel's spiritual fidelity. When the nation walked in obedience, the land flourished with milk and honey. When they rebelled, the land suffered drought, famine, and barrenness. Therefore, the physical condition of the land served as a spiritual mirror, reflecting the health of the nation's relationship with Yahweh.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'erets has three primary fields of meaning: the planet earth in its entirety (in contrast to the heavens), a specific geographical territory or country, and the physical ground or soil. In the context of Ruth 1:1, 'erets denotes the specific territory of Israel, particularly the tribal allotment of Judah. It refers to the covenant homeland, the place of God's presence and provision, which has suddenly become a place of scarcity.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Earth as Creation: The word represents the entire terrestrial globe under God’s absolute sovereignty, established and sustained by His power (Genesis 1:1).

• The Covenant Domain: Most frequently in the historical books, it refers to the Promised Land ('erets yisra'el). This is the land of rest, safety, and abundance that God swore to give His people as an inheritance.

• The Ground/Dust: It can represent the physical ground from which humanity was formed and to which we return in death.

• Literary Contrast: In Ruth 1, there is a deliberate contrast between the 'erets (the covenant land of Israel now suffering famine) and the sadeh (the fields/country of Moab where Elimelech seeks refuge). Leaving the 'erets represents leaving the sphere of God's revealed covenant blessings.

4. Theological Implications

The land belongs sovereignly to Yahweh; Israel was merely its steward and tenant. Because the land is God’s property, its fruitfulness is contingent upon covenant faithfulness. Under the Deuteronomic covenant, famine in the land is a clear, diagnostic sign of spiritual decay and divine discipline (Deuteronomy 28:23-24). The book of Ruth begins during the chaotic era of the Judges, a time when "every man did what was right in his own eyes." The famine in the 'erets is not a random ecological disaster; it is God’s disciplinary hand calling His wandering people back to repentance and dependence on Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:1, the famine in the 'erets sets the narrative engine in motion. It creates a profound irony: Bethlehem, which means "House of Bread," is located in this covenant land, yet it has no bread. Rather than repenting under God’s chastisement and waiting on His provision, Elimelech makes the fateful decision to abandon the 'erets of promise. He chooses physical survival over spiritual covenant alignment by migrating to pagan Moab. This movement represents a deeper spiritual drift—abandoning the place of God’s presence to seek satisfaction in a foreign world.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:1 - "Now the LORD had said to Abram: 'Get out of your country, From your family And from your father’s house, To a land that I will show you.'"

• Leviticus 25:23 - "The land shall not be sold permanently, for the land is Mine; for you are strangers and sojourners with Me."

• Deuteronomy 8:7 - "For the LORD your God is bringing you into a good land, a land of brooks of water, of fountains and springs that flow out of valleys and hills;"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The geographic journey in the book of Ruth mirrors a spiritual arc of apostasy and restoration. Leaving the 'erets leads to death, bereavement, and emptiness for Naomi's family in Moab. Conversely, the turning point of the story occurs when Naomi hears that the Lord has visited His people by giving them bread, prompting her return to the 'erets. The ultimate restoration of Naomi, the redemption of Ruth, and the birth of Obed (the grandfather of King David) all take place once they are firmly replanted in the soil of the Promised Land.
