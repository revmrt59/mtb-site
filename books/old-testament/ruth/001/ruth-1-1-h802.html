WIFE - ( 'ishshah , Strong's H802 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ishshah represents the foundational female identity in the Old Testament, translating as "woman" or "wife" depending on the grammatical context. In Ruth 1:1, it highlights Naomi's covenantal connection to her husband Elimelech as they depart from the Promised Land. This term sets the stage for a narrative of profound loss and ultimate redemption, tracing how a grieving widow ('ishshah) who loses her husband is ultimately restored through covenant faithfulness and the legal protection of marriage.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word 'ishshah is deeply relational and finds its origin in the Genesis account of creation. It is the feminine counterpart to 'ish (man). In Genesis 2:23, the first man exclaims, "She shall be called Woman ('ishshah), because she was taken out of Man ('ish)." This linguistic wordplay establishes an ontological link between the genders. They share the same nature, yet are distinct.

While some etymological roots suggest 'ishshah may derive from a word meaning "soft" or "weak," the usage in the Hebrew Scriptures points more directly to companionship, counterpart, and helper. A wife is designed to be a supportive partner, standing face-to-face in equal dignity with her husband.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term 'ishshah refers broadly to a female human being of any age or status, but its specific meaning is determined by context:

• Woman: An adult female, contrasted with a man ('ish) or a child.

• Wife: A married woman. In Hebrew, there is no distinct word for "wife" other than 'ishshah. Marriage is indicated when the word is paired with a pronominal suffix (e.g., "his woman" translates as "his wife") or when placed in a construct relationship with a husband's name.

• Female: Used biologically of animals in pairs within the covenantal instructions of the flood narrative.

In the legal and narrative contexts of the Old Testament, 'ishshah as "wife" implies a covenantal relationship protected by law, family, and community expectations.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs 'ishshah in several vivid conceptual frameworks:

• The Covenant Partner: The 'ishshah is the centerpiece of the domestic sphere. She is the one through whom a man's lineage is built up and secured.

• The Personification of Wisdom and Folly: In the Book of Proverbs, both Wisdom (Chokmah) and Folly (I evil) are personified as women ('ishshah), calling out to the simple. One builds her house, while the other tears it down.

• The Excellent Wife: The 'eshet chayil (virtuous wife/woman of valor) in Proverbs 31:10 and Ruth 3:11 depicts an active, industrious, wise, and godly partner who brings honor to her household.

### 4. Theological Implications

The role of the 'ishshah is central to the unfolding plan of redemption.

• The Seed of the Woman: In Genesis 3:15, the first gospel promise (protevangelium) declares that the seed of the 'ishshah will bruise the head of the serpent.

• Socio-Economic Vulnerability: In the ancient world, an 'ishshah who was separated from her 'ish (whether through divorce or widowhood) faced extreme vulnerability. Without a male protector, she had no direct legal standing or inheritance rights. The Law of Moses specifically guarded the widow, reflecting God's heart as a protector of the defenseless.

• Covenantal Symbolism: The relationship of the 'ishshah to her husband serves as a primary metaphor for Israel’s relationship with Yahweh. When Israel sinned by worshipping other gods, she was described as an unfaithful wife.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:1, Naomi is introduced not by her name, but as Elimelech's 'ishshah ("...he and his wife and his two sons").

• Shared Destiny: By introducing Naomi first as "his wife," the narrator emphasizes her shared destiny with her husband. When Elimelech decided to leave Bethlehem ("House of Bread") during a famine to dwell in pagan Moab, Naomi went with him under his spiritual and domestic headship.

• The Weight of Widowhood: This introduction sets up the tragic irony of verse 3: "Then Elimelech, Naomi’s husband, died; and she was left, and her two sons." Naomi transitions from being an 'ishshah covered by her husband's protection to a vulnerable widow in a foreign land.

• The Path to Restoration: The rest of the Book of Ruth operates on this tension. Naomi is a childless widow, but through her daughter-in-law Ruth, she seeks security. When Boaz eventually takes Ruth to be his 'ishshah (Ruth 4:13), the family line is redeemed, transforming their tragedy into a lineage that leads directly to King David and Jesus the Messiah.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:24: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and they shall become one flesh."

• Genesis 3:17: "Then to Adam He said, 'Because you have heeded the voice of your wife, and have eaten from the tree of which I commanded you...'"

• Proverbs 12:4: "An excellent wife is the crown of her husband, But she who causes shame is like rottenness in his bones."

• Proverbs 19:14: "Houses and riches are an inheritance from fathers, But a prudent wife is from the LORD."

• Proverbs 31:10: "Who can find a virtuous wife? For her worth is far above rubies."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Structural Arc of Ruth: The Book of Ruth begins with a wife ('ishshah) losing her husband in a pagan land (Ruth 1:1–5) and closes with a foreign widow becoming a beloved wife ('ishshah) in the Promised Land (Ruth 4:13).

• The Characterization of Ruth: Boaz calls Ruth an 'eshet chayil ("woman of virtue/valor") in Ruth 3:11, using the exact Hebrew phrase found in Proverbs 31:10. This signals to the reader that Ruth, though a Moabite stranger, is the ultimate fulfillment of the ideal wife.
