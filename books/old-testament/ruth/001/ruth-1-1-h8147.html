TWO - ( shenayim , Strong's H8147 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shenayim is the cardinal number "two," but in the opening of the Book of Ruth, it carries profound narrative weight and theological irony. It introduces Elimelech and Naomi's two sons, Mahlon and Chilion. In the ancient Near East, two sons represented a viable family unit, a double hope for the preservation of the family name, and the legal inheritance of ancestral land. By focusing on these "two sons," the narrator establishes a picture of a complete, albeit fragile, family unit. The subsequent death of both sons deepens the tragedy of Naomi's loss, reducing a family of four to a solitary widow, and setting the stage for God's dramatic work of redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew numeral shenayim is derived from a root that means to repeat, do a second time, or double. In Hebrew thought, the number two naturally represents a pair, a duplicate, or a division. It is the basic unit of companionship, partnership, and legal testimony. Culturally, having two sons was seen as a foundational blessing. It ensured that if one son met with disaster, the other could still carry on the father's name and inherit the family property. In the agrarian society of Israel, two sons also provided the necessary labor to maintain the family's land allotment. Thus, the "two sons" in Ruth 1:1 represent security, continuity, and future hope.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of shenayim is the cardinal number two.

• It functions as a numeral to count persons, animals, or objects.

• It can express the concept of a "pair" (such as two eyes or two hands) when used in the dual form.

• In the construct state (shene or shete), it means "two of" something, directly modifying the plural noun that follows.

• It can carry the idiomatic sense of "double" or "a second time" depending on the grammatical context.

• In Ruth 1:1, the construct form shene directly modifies "his sons" (banav), indicating precisely two male offspring.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word is utilized across the Old Testament to establish key biblical patterns:

• Covenant and Witness: The concept of two is deeply tied to the establishment of truth. God's law required the testimony of two or three witnesses to establish a matter. Moses received two tablets of stone containing the Ten Commandments, symbolizing the dual witness of the covenant.

• Companionship and Strength: Two represents mutual support, as highlighted in the wisdom literature where two are described as better than one because they have a good reward for their labor.

• Pairs in Creation and Preservation: The animals entered Noah's ark two by two, representing the preservation of life and the assurance of future multiplication.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the mention of "two sons" in the context of Ruth highlights the theme of covenant continuity and the threat of its extinction. Under the Abrahamic Covenant, the preservation of the family line was not merely a matter of personal sentiment; it was a matter of covenantal inheritance and the preservation of God's people in the Promised Land. The loss of both sons represents the ultimate threat to this covenant continuity. It signifies a complete dead end, a total erasure of Elimelech's name from Israel. The theological tension of the Book of Ruth rests on how God will reverse this total loss of the "two sons" and raise up a redeemer to restore the family line.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:1, the phrase "his two sons" introduces Mahlon and Chilion. Their names, which mean "sickly" and "pining" respectively, foreshadow their untimely deaths. The narrative uses the number two as a literary device to measure Naomi's progressive loss:

• They begin as a complete household: Elimelech, Naomi, and their two sons (Ruth 1:1).

• Elimelech dies, leaving Naomi with her two sons (Ruth 1:3).

• The two sons marry Moabite women (Ruth 1:4).

• The two sons die, leaving Naomi empty (Ruth 1:5).

The countdown from two to zero emphasizes Naomi's absolute bereavement. She is stripped of her husband and her two sons, leaving her with no legal connection to the land and no human means of survival.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:16: "Then God made two great lights: the greater light to rule the day, and the lesser light to rule the night. He made the stars also."

• Genesis 7:9: "two by two they went into the ark to Noah, male and female, as God had commanded Noah."

• Exodus 31:18: "And when He had made an end of speaking with him on Mount Sinai, He gave Moses two tablets of the Testimony, tablets of stone, written with the finger of God."

• Ecclesiastes 4:9: "Two are better than one, Because they have a good reward for their labor."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The number two reappears in significant pairings throughout the narrative of Ruth. There are two daughters-in-law (Orpah and Ruth) who face the decision to stay in Moab or go to Bethlehem. There are two potential redeemers (the unnamed close relative and Boaz) who must decide whether to redeem Elimelech's land and marry Ruth. Ultimately, the resolution of the story contrasts Naomi's original "two sons" who were lost with the birth of Obed, whom the neighborhood women declare is better to Naomi than "seven sons." God's redemption does not merely replace what was lost; it vastly exceeds it.
