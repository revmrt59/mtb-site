RULED - ( shaphat , Strong's H8199 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shaphat represents far more than the modern concept of legal arbitration; it encompasses the active restoration of order, governance, deliverance, and leadership. In the opening verse of the Book of Ruth, this word establishes the historical and spiritual backdrop of a chaotic era where fractured human leadership failed to maintain covenant order, setting a stark contrast to the quiet, sovereign, and restorative rule of God that unfolds throughout the narrative.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, a judge (shophet) was not a passive judicial figure confined to a courtroom, but an active, charismatic leader who ruled, governed, and delivered. The root of shaphat relates to the act of setting things straight, establishing equity, and restoring order to a chaotic or broken situation. The word picture is one of a champion or governor who steps into a state of lawlessness or oppression to vindicate the victim, punish the wrongdoer, and re-establish peace (shalom).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of shaphat is broad, carrying three primary facets of authority:

• To govern or rule: Exercising executive leadership and sovereign administration over a people group or nation.

• To deliver or vindicate: Championing the cause of the weak, oppressed, or disenfranchised, and restoring them to their rightful state.

• To decide or arbitrate: Hearing legal cases, deciding controversies, and executing righteous verdicts.

In its active verbal forms, the word shifts between the judicial act of pronouncing a verdict and the administrative act of ruling as a leader.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament scriptures, shaphat is used to describe the actions of Moses arbitrating daily disputes, the military-political leaders of the pre-monarchical era (the Judges), and the ultimate governance of Yahweh and His Messiah. The word picture of shaphat is closely tied to the cosmic and social order. When a leader shaphats correctly, society functions in harmony, boundaries are respected, and the vulnerable (widows, orphans, and strangers) are protected from exploitation. Conversely, when shaphat is neglected or corrupted, the land falls into moral and physical ruin.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shaphat reveals the character of God as the ultimate Sovereign and Guardian of cosmic justice. Unlike pagan deities who ruled through arbitrary whim, Yahweh's judgment is intrinsically linked to His righteousness, truth, and covenant faithfulness. When God arises to shaphat the earth, His goal is not merely punitive destruction, but the restoration of creation to its intended design. Furthermore, the human failures of those who "ruled" (shaphat) in Israel highlighted the limitations of human governance, pointing forward to the need for a perfect, eternal King from the line of David who would rule in absolute righteousness.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:1, the text employs a striking Hebrew wordplay (cognate accusative / infinitive construction): "in the days when the judges ruled" (bime shephot hashophetim), which literally translates to "in the days of the judging of the judges." This double use of the root shaphat serves as a profound historical and theological commentary. The era of the Judges was characterized by spiritual apostasy, idolatry, civil war, and moral anarchy, summarized by the refrain that "everyone did what was right in his own eyes" (Judges 21:25). The irony is heavy: those who were supposed to bring order (shaphat) were themselves lawless. The physical famine mentioned immediately after this phrase in Ruth 1:1 is the physical manifestation of covenantal disobedience. Thus, the word shaphat here highlights the failure of human leadership, setting the stage for God's quiet providence to work through Naomi, Ruth, and Boaz to bring about the ultimate Ruler.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 1:16 - "Then I commanded your judges at that time, saying, 'Hear the cases between your brethren, and judge righteously between a man and his brother or the stranger who is with him.'"

• 1 Samuel 7:15 - "And Samuel judged Israel all the days of his life."

• Psalm 96:13 - "For He is coming, for He is coming to judge the earth. He shall judge the world with righteousness, And the peoples with His truth."

• Isaiah 2:4 - "He shall judge between the nations, And rebuke many people; They shall beat their swords into plowshares, And their spears into pruning hooks; Nation shall not lift up sword against nation, Neither shall they learn war anymore."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition from the book of Judges to the book of Ruth represents a shift from macro-history (the national chaos of the shophetim) to micro-history (the quiet faithfulness of a single family in Bethlehem). While the national rulers failed to shaphat righteously, God was actively orchestrating a lineage of redemption. The ultimate resolution to the failed era of the "judging of the judges" is found at the very end of the Book of Ruth: the birth of Obed, the grandfather of King David, through whom the ultimate, righteous Judge and Ruler, Jesus Christ, would come.
