SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is the primary Old Testament term for speaking, declaring, or expressing an internal thought. In Ruth 1:10, it represents the emotional, outward verbalization of Orpah and Ruth's deep-seated devotion to Naomi. Caught in a moment of intense grief, their spoken words are not mere casual conversation, but a passionate, vocalized declaration of loyalty and an initial refusal to abandon their mother-in-law in her time of destitution.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root 'amar is used over 5,000 times in the Hebrew Bible, making it one of the most frequently occurring verbs in the Old Testament. At its most basic level, it describes the process of bringing forth words from the heart and mind into audible reality. In the ancient Near East, spoken words were viewed as dynamic and substantive, possessing the power to build up, tear down, create obligations, or establish covenants.

While other Hebrew words for speaking focus on the mechanical act of talking or the formal delivery of a message, 'amar acts as a direct window into the speaker's soul. It bridges the gap between inner intent and outer expression. In the narrative of Ruth 1:10, this word introduces the vocalized grief and determination of two young Moabite widows who stand at a physical and cultural crossroads, choosing to bind their voices to the fate of an elderly Hebrew woman.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of 'amar is to say, speak, utter, declare, or command. Its semantic range varies depending on the grammatical construction and context:

• To Say or Speak: The straightforward vocalization of words or dialogue in daily life.

• To Think / Say in One's Heart: When paired with the Hebrew word for "heart" (leb), it denotes internal dialogue, reasoning, or intention.

• To Command or Decree: When spoken by a figure of authority, such as God or a king, the word carries the weight of an absolute directive.

• To Intend or Promise: It can express a firm resolution or an agreement made between parties.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Creative Voice of God: The most famous theological word picture associated with 'amar is found in Genesis 1. When God "said" ('amar), "Let there be light," His spoken word immediately transformed chaos into cosmos. His voice carries inherent, creative authority.

• The Voice of Human Intent: On a human level, 'amar acts as a diagnostic tool for the heart. Jesus' later teaching that "out of the abundance of the heart the mouth speaks" is deeply rooted in the Old Testament usage of 'amar. The words spoken ('amar) by individuals reveal their true spiritual state, whether it be fear, rebellion, pride, or—as in the case of Ruth and Orpah—unswerving devotion.

4. Theological Implications

Theological truth is deeply embedded in the dialogue of Scripture. God has chosen to reveal Himself not through silent impressions alone, but through spoken, understandable words. He is a speaking God who invites humanity into dialogue.

In the book of Ruth, human speech is used by God's providence to weave a narrative of redemption. The choices made, the commitments spoken, and the blessings pronounced are the vehicles of covenant loyalty (hesed). In Ruth 1:10, the spoken word serves as a legal and moral commitment. By saying, "Surely we will return with you," the daughters-in-law are verbally putting their lives on the line, choosing the unknown God of Israel over the familiar comforts of Moab.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:10, 'amar is conjugated in the imperfect tense with a waw consecutive (literally, "and they said"), indicating a direct, immediate, and highly emotional reaction to Naomi's self-sacrificing plea. Naomi has just urged them to go back to their mothers' houses, kissing them as they wept aloud.

Their immediate response—"And they said to her..."—is not a calculated or formal diplomatic reply. It is a spontaneous burst of grief-stricken loyalty. At this exact moment, both Orpah and Ruth are united in their verbal vow. Though Orpah will eventually relent under the weight of Naomi's continued insistence, this initial verbalization demonstrates the profound relational bond and affection Naomi had fostered. Their speech here is a defense mechanism against parting; they use their voices to resist the bitter reality of separation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3: "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light."

• Genesis 22:11: "But the Angel of the LORD called to him from heaven and said, 'Abraham, Abraham!' So he said, 'Here I am.'"

• Ruth 1:16: "But Ruth said: 'Entreat me not to leave you, Or to turn back from following after you...'"

• Ruth 2:2: "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field...'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is helpful to contrast 'amar with dabar (another primary Hebrew verb for speaking). While dabar typically refers to the act of speaking a message or the matter of speech itself (focusing on the substance of what is spoken), 'amar is almost always followed by the exact, direct quotation of the speaker. This stylistic feature brings the biblical narrative alive. Rather than summarizing the conversation of these grieving women, the writer of Ruth uses 'amar to let us hear their exact words, capturing the intimacy, sorrow, and raw commitment of the moment.
