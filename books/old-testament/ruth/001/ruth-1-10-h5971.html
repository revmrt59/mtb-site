PEOPLE - ( 'am , Strong's H5971 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'am refers to a group of people bound together by common bloodlines, covenant, history, or social relationship, rather than merely a political or geographical entity. In Ruth 1:10, Orpah and Ruth declare their intention to return with Naomi to her "people," signifying their willingness to abandon their Moabite national and familial ties to join the community of Israel. This word highlights the deep relational and communal bonds that define identity in the ancient Near East, serving as a vital stepping stone to Ruth’s ultimate covenant commitment to God and His covenant community.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun 'am carries the foundational concept of kinship, relationship, and consanguinity. Unlike goy, which often describes a nation as a political or geopolitical body, 'am emphasizes a family of families, a community knit together by shared ancestry, culture, and mutual responsibility. The root is related to ideas of gathering or associating, suggesting a people who are gathered together under a common bond. In the ancient Near East, an individual's identity, safety, and purpose were entirely derived from their 'am. To be separated from one's 'am was to be highly vulnerable, while to be gathered to one's 'am (a common euphemism for death) meant returning to one's ancestral community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of 'am is "people," specifically denoting a collective group united by familial, national, or covenantal ties. It can refer to:

• A nation or ethnic group sharing a common lineage (such as Israel as the people of God).

• Relatives, kinsmen, or immediate family members.

• A specific localized crowd, assembly, or citizens of a particular town.

• In a specialized sense, the common citizens as distinguished from their leaders or rulers.

In the covenant language of the Old Testament, 'am is frequently paired with the divine possessive pronoun ("My people" or "the people of Yahweh"), transforming a sociological term into a profound theological description of Israel's unique status before God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The term 'am is used over 1,800 times in the Old Testament, illustrating its massive importance in defining the community of faith.

• The Family/Kinsman Picture: In its singular and plural forms, 'am is used to describe relatives and close family circles (e.g., Leviticus 21:1, where a priest is warned not to defile himself for his "people" or kin). This highlights that a "people" is not merely a collection of citizens, but an extended family.

• The Covenant Picture: When God calls Israel out of Egypt, He declares, "I will take you as My people, and I will be your God" (Exodus 6:7). Here, 'am becomes the vessel of divine election, portraying a community adopted by God.

• The Sheep and Shepherd Picture: The 'am is frequently pictured as a flock under the care of a shepherd, emphasizing their dependence, unity, and need for leadership (Psalm 95:7).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'am undergirds the biblical doctrine of the covenant community. God does not merely save individuals; He gathers a 'am—a people for His own possession. In the context of the book of Ruth, the word 'am bridges the gap between ethnic division and spiritual unity. It demonstrates that membership in God’s covenant people is not strictly restricted by physical genealogy but is open to those who, through faith and loyalty, join themselves to the 'am of God. This foreshadows the inclusion of the Gentiles into the household of faith, showing that those who were "not a people" are made "the people of God" through covenant relationship.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:10, Naomi has urged her widowed daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth, to return to their maternal homes in Moab. Their emotional response—"Surely we will return with you to your people"—uses 'am to express a willingness to cross cultural, national, and religious boundaries. To join Naomi's 'am meant leaving the security of Moab, embracing the status of foreign immigrants in Judah, and submitting to the social and spiritual laws of Israel. While Orpah ultimately relents, Ruth's subsequent declaration in verse 16 ("Your people shall be my people") seals this desire, elevating 'am from a mere social relocation to a total surrender of her identity. For Ruth, Naomi's people become her family, her home, and her primary community, which serves as the physical context for her faith in Naomi's God (Elohim).

7. Other Relevant Insights

A key linguistic contrast in the book of Ruth is between the "people" ('am) of Israel and the Moabites as a distinct nation. In the ancient mind, one could not easily change their 'am because it was tied to the land and the local deities. This makes the statement in Ruth 1:10, and its ultimate fulfillment in Ruth 1:16, culturally shocking. It reveals the magnetic pull of covenant loyalty (hesed). Ruth’s conversion is first expressed in terms of peoplehood before it is expressed in terms of theology, illustrating that biblical faith is always lived out in the context of concrete, local, human relationships.
