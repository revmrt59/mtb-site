RETURN - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub occurs over one thousand times in the Old Testament, representing one of its most theology-laden terms. At its core, the word means to turn back, return, or go back to a point of origin. In the narrative of Ruth, shub acts as a central literary and theological hinge. In Ruth 1:10, Orpah and Ruth declare their intention to "return" with Naomi to Judah. While initially representing a physical journey back to Naomi's homeland, this word ultimately serves as the linguistic canvas for repentance, covenant loyalty, and the redemptive homecoming of those who have wandered from the place of God's blessing.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• shub describes concrete, physical movement: turning around to go back the way one came. It is the movement of a traveler reversing direction to go home, a straying animal returning to the fold, or a debtor returning what was taken. In the Ancient Near East, geography was closely tied to identity, security, and deity. To leave one's land was to leave one's family and gods. To return meant a restoration of relationship, safety, and alignment with one's true origin. In the book of Ruth, Moab represents the place of tragedy, spiritual exile, and death, while Bethlehem (the "House of Bread") is the place of God's visitation and provision. Returning is not merely a change of location; it is a movement from death back to life.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• shub is a primary root verb meaning to turn back, return, turn away, or restore. It occurs in several verbal stems:

• In the Qal stem (as in Ruth 1:10), it means to return, turn back, or go back to a person, place, or condition.

• In the Hiphil stem (causative), it means to bring back, restore, or repay.

• In the Polel stem, it means to bring back, restore, or refresh (as in the restoration of the soul).

The word functions both literally (spatial movement) and metaphorically (spiritual, moral, or emotional turning). Spiritually, shub is the foundational Old Testament word for "repentance"—a total face-about, turning away from sin and turning back to Yahweh.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Shepherd and the Fold: A straying sheep returning to the shepherd's care.

• The Path or Way: Life is depicted as a path. Sin is wandering off the path; shub is returning to the right path.

• The Marriage Covenant: Prophetic literature uses shub to describe an unfaithful wife returning to her husband, highlighting the relational, covenantal depth of the word.

• Restoration of Fortune: The common Hebrew idiom shub shebut (restoring fortunes) depicts a complete reversal of judgment and a return to divine favor.

4. Theological Implications

• shub reveals the heart of God's covenant: He desires restoration, not destruction. Repentance is not merely feeling sorry; it is a physical and spiritual reorientation toward God. The word bridges the physical and the spiritual. Naomi's physical return to Bethlehem mirrors her spiritual return to the sphere of Yahweh's blessing. God is also said to shub (turn back) from His burning anger when His people shub (turn back) from their evil ways. God’s turning is an act of sovereign mercy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:10, Orpah and Ruth weep and tell Naomi, "Surely we will return with you to your people." This use of shub is highly ironic and poignant. Literally, Moabite women cannot "return" to Judah because they have never been there; Judah is not their point of origin. This reveals their deep love for Naomi; they view her home as their home. The word shub occurs twelve times in Ruth chapter 1, dominating the theological landscape of the chapter. It highlights the contrast between Orpah, who eventually "goes back" to her people and her gods (Ruth 1:15), and Ruth, who refuses to turn back, choosing instead to bind herself to Naomi and Naomi's God. Ruth's physical "return" to Bethlehem is a true spiritual conversion.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:15: "And she said, 'Look, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods; return after your sister-in-law.'"

• Psalm 51:13: "Then I will teach transgressors Your ways, And sinners shall be converted to You."

• Isaiah 55:7: "Let the wicked forsake his way, And the unrighteous man his thoughts; Let him return to the LORD, And He will have mercy on him; And to our God, For He will abundantly pardon."

• Malachi 3:7: "Yet from the days of your fathers You have gone away from My ordinances And have not kept them. Return to Me, and I will return to you,' Says the LORD of hosts. 'But you said, "In what way shall we return?"'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) often translates shub with epistrepho (to turn back/convert) or apostrepho. In the New Testament, epistrepho carries this exact double-meaning of physical turning and spiritual conversion (as seen in Peter's call to "repent and be converted" in Acts 3:19). The repetition of shub in Ruth 1 serves as a literary device called inclusio and thematic reinforcement. It forces the reader to ask: Who will truly return? What does it cost to return to God?
