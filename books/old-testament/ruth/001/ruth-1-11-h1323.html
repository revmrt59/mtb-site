DAUGHTERS - ( bath , Strong's H1323 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bath (plural banot) primarily denotes a female descendant, daughter, or young woman. In the context of Ruth 1:11, Naomi uses the plural form banot ("my daughters") as a tender, intimate term of address for her Moabite daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth. This term transcends mere legal or biological relation, reflecting a deep emotional bond forged through shared suffering, grief, and familial love. Naomi uses this affectionate title to release them from any perceived obligation to follow her back to Judah, prioritizing their welfare and potential for remarriage over her own comfort.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun bath is the feminine counterpart to ben (son), both derived from the root banah, meaning "to build." This etymological connection illustrates how children—both sons and daughters—build up and establish a household or family line. In ancient Israelite culture, daughters played a vital role in the preservation and expansion of the family unit, though their status was often tied directly to their relationships with fathers, husbands, or sons. In the ancient Near East, a daughter-in-law who lost her husband was in an incredibly vulnerable position, especially if she left her homeland. Naomi's affectionate address, calling Orpah and Ruth "my daughters," elevates their relationship from standard legal ties to a profound familial affection.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term bath refers to a female child or daughter in relation to her parents. Its semantic range also encompasses:

• A female descendant (granddaughter, female relative, or descendant of a specific ancestor).

• A young woman, maiden, or female inhabitant of a specific city or region (such as "daughter of Zion").

• A term of affectionate address used by an older person toward a younger woman.

In the plural form (banot), it denotes daughters, girls, or young women collectively. In Ruth 1:11, the pronominal suffix attached to banot produces benotay, literally translating to "my daughters," serving as a term of endearment and maternal care.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word bath is used in several vivid idiomatic ways throughout the Old Testament:

• The phrase "daughter of [a city or nation]" personifies a city or nation as a vulnerable young woman, depicting either her beauty, her distress, or her impending judgment.

• It is used metaphorically to describe the "daughters of song" (Ecclesiastes 12:4), referring to musical notes or vocal cords that fade with old age.

• In Hebrew idioms, bath can express age; for example, a female animal of a certain age is literally a "daughter of" that year.

• The term "daughter of an eye" (bath ayin) refers to the pupil of the eye, capturing the concept of that which is most precious, sensitive, and worthy of protection.

4. Theological Implications

The use of bath in the book of Ruth highlights the radical inclusion of foreigners within the sphere of covenantal love. Although Orpah and Ruth are Moabites—a nation traditionally excluded from the assembly of Israel—Naomi addresses them as "my daughters." This maternal embrace foreshadows the broader redemption story, where those who were once far off are brought near and adopted into God's family. Naomi's language reflects the heart of the covenant community, where relationships are defined by chesed (covenant faithfulness and lovingkindness) rather than mere biological descent. It demonstrates that the margins of God's redemptive plan are wider than national borders, welcoming the foreigner into the intimate security of familial love.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:11, Naomi's repeated use of "my daughters" (also found in verses 12 and 22) marks a pivotal emotional and structural turning point. Following the deaths of her husband, Elimelech, and her two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, Naomi is left destitute. As she prepares to return to Bethlehem, she urges her Moabite daughters-in-law to return to their mother's houses. By addressing them as "my daughters," Naomi expresses her profound affection and acknowledges the genuine grief they share. She is not dismissing them coldly; rather, she is selflessly releasing them. She argues that she has no more sons in her womb to offer them as husbands under the custom of levirate marriage. Her address "my daughters" is a pastoral picture of sacrificial love: she loves them enough to send them away to a place of greater security, choosing their future well-being over her own need for companionship and support in her old age.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The transition of Ruth from a "daughter-in-law" to a "daughter" in Naomi's language reflects the transformative power of shared adversity.

• In the ancient Near East, a widowed daughter-in-law had no legal standing in her deceased husband's family if there were no surviving male heirs. Naomi's insistence on calling them "daughters" shows that she is operating under a higher law of love and family loyalty rather than merely rigid legal parameters.

• The phrase "my daughters" in Ruth 1:11 highlights the contrast between Orpah's eventually pragmatic response (returning to Moab) and Ruth's extraordinary loyalty, which ultimately secures her place in the genealogy of Jesus Christ.
