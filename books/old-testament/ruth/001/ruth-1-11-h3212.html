GO - ( yalak , Strong's H3212 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yalak is a primary root meaning "to go," "to walk," or "to proceed." In the context of Ruth 1:11, it denotes physical movement, journeying, and companionship, but it carries deep emotional and relational weight. Naomi uses this verb to question the wisdom of Orpah and Ruth accompanying her back to Judah. Rather than representing a simple physical journey, this "going" signifies a major life transition, an abandonment of their homeland, and a commitment to an uncertain future. Naomi uses the term to highlight the sheer impracticality of their loyalty, urging them to walk back to their maternal homes instead of walking into hardship and foreign alienation with her.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Semitic culture, walking or journeying with someone was never merely about transportation; it symbolized fellowship, shared destiny, and covenant relationship. To "go" (yalak) with another person meant entering their sphere of life, sharing their trials, and binding one's future to theirs. The physical path traveled reflected the moral and spiritual direction of one's life. In the ancient Near East, a traveler on a road was vulnerable and entirely dependent on their companions. When Naomi questions why Ruth and Orpah would "go" with her, she is asking why they would tie their life paths to a woman who believes she has been emptied and cursed by the Almighty.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb yalak means to go, walk, come, depart, proceed, or move forward. It is closely related to and grammatically intertwined with the root halak.

• It primarily describes physical locomotion—the act of walking on feet or traveling from one geographical point to another.

• It carries a figurative meaning of lifestyle, conduct, and moral behavior, such as walking in truth or going after other gods.

• In the specific grammatical form found in Ruth 1:11 (teleknah, the imperfect second-person feminine plural), the word denotes an ongoing, active process of journeying: "Why will you keep going with me?"

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb yalak is used across the Old Testament to construct vivid pictures of movement and progression:

• The Flow of Nature: It describes rivers flowing (going) to the sea or the wind blowing (going) in its circuits.

• The Passage of Time: It represents the fading of life or the passing of days.

• Spiritual Direction: It is the standard verb used to describe walking in the law of the Lord or, conversely, "going" after foreign gods.

• Covenant Companionship: It depicts a disciple following a master, a wife following her husband, or a believer walking with God. The word picture is one of step-by-step progress along a designated path.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, "going" in the Old Testament is frequently tied to faith, obedience, and identity.

• The Call to Depart: The history of Israel begins with a command to yalak—Abraham was told to "go" out of his country to a land God would show him.

• The Path of Covenant Choice: For the Moabitess daughters-in-law, "going" with Naomi is a geographical movement that demands a spiritual and national migration. It requires crossing from Moab (a place of pagan worship and historical enmity with Israel) into Judah (the land of Yahweh’s covenant promises).

• Pragmatism vs. Faith: Naomi's use of yalak in verse 11 sets up the ultimate theological contrast of the chapter. She views the "going" purely through human, pragmatic, and economic lenses. She has no more sons to offer; therefore, there is no logical reason to "go." This sets the stage for Ruth’s transcendent response in verse 16, where she reclaims the same verb to express unconditional covenant loyalty: "wherever you go, I will go."

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:11, Naomi is at a point of deep despair, having lost her husband and both sons in Moab. She has decided to return to Bethlehem because she heard that the Lord had visited His people by giving them bread. Her daughters-in-law start out on the road with her. Naomi stops them, using yalak to challenge their motivations: "Why will you go with me?"

• The nuance is highly personal and rhetorical. Naomi is arguing that their companionship is logical nonsense. In that patriarchal culture, a young widow's survival depended on marriage and sons. Naomi cannot provide this.

• By asking "why will you go with me," Naomi is attempting to release them from any perceived familial obligation. She portrays herself as a dead end, urging them to choose a path of ease and security in Moab rather than a path of poverty in Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:4: "So Abram departed as the Lord had spoken to him, and Lot went with him."

• Exodus 33:14: "And He said, 'My Presence will go with you, and I will give you rest.'"

• Ruth 1:16: "But Ruth said: 'Entreat me not to leave you, Or to turn back from following after you; For wherever you go, I will go; And wherever you lodge, I will lodge; Your people shall be my people, And your God, my God.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural tension of the first chapter of Ruth is built entirely around verbs of motion: returning (shub) and going (yalak/halak).

• Elimelech’s family originally "went" (yalak) to dwell in Moab (Ruth 1:1), which led to tragedy and death.

• Naomi now "returns" (shub), and she commands her daughters-in-law to "go" (yalak) back to their mother's houses.

• Orpah eventually complies and "goes" back to her people and her gods, but Ruth refuses, choosing instead to "go" (yalak) into the unknown with Naomi, transforming a simple journey into a historic pilgrimage of faith.
