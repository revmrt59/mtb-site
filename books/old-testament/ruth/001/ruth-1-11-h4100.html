WHY - ( mah , Strong's H4100 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word mah (frequently combined with the preposition le to form lamah) serves as an interrogative pronoun meaning "why," "what," or "for what reason." In Ruth 1:11, Naomi uses this word rhetorically to challenge her daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth, to reconsider their decision to accompany her back to Judah. By asking "why," Naomi highlights the utter lack of practical, familial, or economic benefit in remaining loyal to her, painting a stark picture of her own empty womb and the legal impossibilities of providing them with future husbands.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root mah is the fundamental interrogative pronoun in the Hebrew language. When combined with the preposition le (meaning "to" or "for"), it becomes lamah, literally translating to "for what?" or "to what purpose?" In the ancient Near Eastern context, life was deeply structured around family protection, lineage, and economic survival. A widow without sons was highly vulnerable. Naomi’s use of "why" reflects the stark, pragmatic reality of ancient widowhood. It paints a picture of a road blocked by absolute biological and social impossibility, forcing Orpah and Ruth to confront the grim prospects of accompanying a destitute older woman.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word mah serves as an interrogative pronoun representing "what," "how," or "why." When prefixed with the preposition le (producing lamah), it specifically means "why," "wherefore," or "for what reason." It functions to ask for the cause, motive, or purpose of an action. Lexically, it is used to introduce rhetorical questions, express protest, or convey strong emotion, ranging from grief and despair to bewilderment. In the context of Ruth 1:11, it asks for a logical justification for a course of action that seems to defy common-sense survival.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In Hebrew literature, mah (and its composite lamah) is highly versatile. It appears in contexts of:

• Interrogation of purpose: Seeking the underlying reason for an event or choice.

• Lament and grief: Often used in the Psalms to cry out to God in times of distress.

• Rhetorical discouragement: Demolishing false hopes by showing the lack of a logical outcome.

In Ruth 1:11, the word picture is one of a dead-end street. Naomi uses "why" to construct a logical barrier. She is not asking for their subjective motives; she is presenting the objective futility of their loyalty. It is a rhetorical device designed to break their resolve for their own benefit.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Naomi's "why" reflects a crisis of faith and a focus on human limitation. Naomi’s perspective is entirely bound by the natural order: age, womb, and levirate customs. She cannot see past her own barrenness and the tragedy of her losses. Her "why" exposes the tension between human calculation and divine providence. While Naomi sees only the impossibility of her situation ("why go with me?"), the narrative of Ruth is positioning this very hopelessness as the canvas for God's extraordinary, covenantal redemption (hesed).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:11, the context is Naomi's return to Bethlehem from Moab after losing her husband and two sons. Naomi's "why" is a direct appeal to the self-interest of Orpah and Ruth. She is saying, "To what purpose will you go with me? What can I possibly offer you?" She reinforces this "why" with biological realities: "Are there still sons in my womb?" She is too old to have a husband, and even if she did, they could not wait for those sons to grow up. Therefore, "why" functions as an act of tough love—she is trying to release them from any obligation to her, believing that God's hand has gone out against her and that their association with her will only bring them ruin.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:18: "And Pharaoh called Abram and said, 'What is this you have done to me? Why did you not tell me that she was your wife?'"

• Exodus 5:22: "So Moses returned to the LORD and said, 'Lord, why have You brought trouble on this people? Why is it You have sent me?'"

• Psalm 22:1: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me? Why are You so far from helping Me, And from the words of My groaning?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is vital to note the contrast between Naomi's logical "why" and Ruth's ultimate response of faithful commitment. Naomi operates on the level of earthly cause-and-effect: without a husband or sons, there is no logical reason to stay together. Ruth’s silent answer to this "why" is not logical but covenantal. She rejects Naomi's pragmatism, demonstrating that love and loyalty transcend legal obligations, biological possibilities, or circumstantial logic.
