WOMB - ( me'eh , Strong's H4578 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word me'eh refers literally to the internal organs, bowels, or intestines, but is frequently used figuratively to represent the womb, the source of procreation, or the seat of deep-seated human emotions. In Ruth 1:11, Naomi uses this term to emphasize her physical inability to produce more sons for her widowed daughters-in-law, underscoring the absolute hopelessness of their situation under the social customs of levirate marriage. It points to the biological limits of human provision, setting the stage for God's sovereign and supernatural redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of me'eh points to the abdomen, the internal organs, or the bowels. In ancient Near Eastern thought, the internal organs were not merely viewed anatomically but were understood as the center of physical life, reproduction, and the deepest human feelings. Just as modern English speakers use the "heart" to represent the seat of emotion, Hebrew culture used the internal organs (me'im, the plural form) to describe intense feelings of compassion, distress, or yearning, as well as the literal physical source of offspring from both fathers (loins/descent) and mothers (womb/gestation).

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun me'eh (most commonly found in the plural form me'im) denotes the internal parts of the body. Its semantic range includes:

• The literal digestive tract, bowels, or stomach.

• The womb or the reproductive organs of a woman, serving as the source of childbirth.

• The male reproductive organs or loins, referring to fatherhood and physical descent.

• The emotional core of a person, translated as "heart," "affections," or "deepest feelings," representing grief, sympathy, or distress.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Procreative Source: When used of a father, me'eh describes offspring coming "from your own body" (Genesis 15:4). When used of a mother, it highlights the intimate place of gestation and birth, as seen in Isaiah 49:1, where the servant is called from the womb.

• The Seat of Emotion: In poetic and prophetic texts, when the me'im "churn" or "trouble" (Lamentations 1:20), it represents overwhelming sorrow or compassion. This physical description of internal movement vividly conveys how emotional pain manifests physically in the body.

• The Physical Abdomen: In narrative contexts, it refers to the literal stomach, as when Ehud's sword sank into Eglon's belly, or when the prophet Ezekiel is commanded to fill his stomach with the scroll of the word.

4. Theological Implications

The use of me'eh highlights the physical reality of God's covenant promises. When God promises Abraham an heir from his "own body" (Genesis 15:4), He anchors His covenant in historical, biological reality, not just spiritual metaphor. In the context of redemption, the "womb" (me'eh) represents the closed door that only God can open. Humanly speaking, Naomi's me'eh is barren and past the age of bearing, symbolizing the dead-end of the Elimelech line. God's ultimate intervention in the book of Ruth—enabling Ruth to conceive—shows that life and redemption do not depend on failing human biological capacity, but on the sovereign grace of Yahweh.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:11, Naomi asks, "Are there still sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands?" Here, me'eh refers specifically to her womb. The immediate context is Naomi urging Orpah and Ruth to return to Moab. Under the custom of levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25:5–10), if a married man died without children, his brother was obligated to marry the widow to carry on the deceased brother's name. Naomi uses me'eh to construct a reductio ad absurdum: even if she could instantly conceive and bear sons, it would be absurd for Orpah and Ruth to wait for those infants to grow to manhood. By pointing to her own empty, aging me'eh, Naomi is declaring that her biological ability to provide security, lineage, and future for her daughters-in-law is completely dead. This highlights the themes of emptiness and death that characterize chapter 1, preparing the reader for the surprising ways God will bring life out of emptiness later in the narrative.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 15:4 – "And behold, the word of the Lord came to him, saying, 'This one shall not be your heir, but one who will come from your own body [me'im] shall be your heir.'"

• Genesis 25:23 – "And the Lord said to her: 'Two nations are in your womb, two peoples shall be separated from your body [me'im]...'"

• 2 Samuel 7:12 – "When your days are fulfilled and you rest with your fathers, I will set up your seed after you, who will come from your body [me'im], and I will establish his kingdom."

• Psalm 71:6 – "By You I have been upheld from birth; You are He who took me out of my mother's womb [me'im]."

• Isaiah 49:1 – "Listen, O coastlands, to Me, and take heed, you peoples from afar! The Lord has called Me from the womb; from the matrix of My mother [me'im] He has made mention of My name."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition from the Hebrew me'im (bowels/womb) to the New Testament Greek equivalent splanchna (bowels/inward affection) is notable. In the New Testament, splanchna is the root word for "compassion" (e.g., splanchnizomai, "moved with compassion," as used of Jesus). Both languages locate the deepest yearnings of love, pity, and biological kinship in the internal organs, demonstrating a holistic view of human personhood where body and emotion are deeply integrated.
