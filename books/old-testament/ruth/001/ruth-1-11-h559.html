SAID - ( amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is the primary Old Testament word for "to say," "speak," or "command." In Ruth 1:11, it introduces Naomi’s pivotal, grief-laden plea to her daughters-in-law to return to Moab. Far from a casual utterance, this term here denotes an earnest, emotional, and persuasive appeal. Naomi speaks from a heart of deep bereavement and hopelessness, attempting to release Orpah and Ruth from any familial obligation to her. It marks a critical turning point in the narrative, testing the loyalty of her daughters-in-law and highlighting the desperate cultural reality of childless widows in the ancient Near East.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root 'amar occurs over 5,000 times in the Hebrew Bible, making it one of the most frequent verbs in the Old Testament.

• In its most basic sense, the word represents the externalization of thought through speech. It differs from the Hebrew verb dabar (to speak), which often emphasizes the act or substance of communication itself; 'amar focuses on the specific words uttered or the intent of the speaker directing their message to an audience.

• In the ancient Near Eastern context, spoken words possessed immense weight. They were not merely sounds but carried creative, legal, and relational power. To "say" something was to initiate action, establish covenants, or define relationships. In a narrative context like Ruth, dialogue is the primary vehicle for character development and theological themes.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb 'amar means "to say," "to speak," "to utter," "to command," or "to think" (literally, "to say in one's heart").

• In the Qal stem, which is the pattern used in Ruth 1:11, it denotes the straightforward act of speaking, declaring, or answering.

• It can carry the nuance of "to intend" or "to resolve" when a person speaks within themselves.

• Depending on the relationship between the speaker and the listener, it can express a command (when spoken by a superior), an entreaty or plea (when spoken by an inferior or someone in distress), or an answer.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Creative speech: 'amar is used to describe God’s creative speech in Genesis 1. Here, the spoken word carries sovereign, creative efficacy, instantly bringing reality into existence.

• Internal monologue: The word describes inner thoughts, where a character "says in his heart," showing that Hebrew thought views thinking as an internal speaking process.

• Covenantal declaration: In legal and covenantal settings, the verb introduces declarations of status, legal promises, or binding oaths.

• Dialogue progression: In Hebrew narrative, the verb acts as a regular marker that structures dialogue, signaling transitions between speakers and shifting the emotional momentum of the scene.

4. Theological Implications

• Spoken words reflect the character and heart of the speaker. When God speaks, His words are reliable, covenantal, and life-giving. When humans speak, their words reveal their faith, fear, grief, or rebellion.

• In the Book of Ruth, God’s active speech is rarely recorded; instead, His theological purposes are worked out through the everyday, faithful speech of ordinary people. The human dialogue introduced by 'amar becomes the arena of divine providence.

• Naomi’s speech in Ruth 1:11 reveals her theological crisis. Believing that the hand of the Lord has gone out against her, her words express a worldview temporarily darkened by tragedy, where she cannot see any hope or future redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:11, Naomi uses 'amar to introduce her first major plea to her daughters-in-law on the road back to Judah.

• The context is one of intense grief, relocation, and extreme vulnerability. Naomi has lost her husband and both sons, leaving her empty and without social protection.

• Naomi's words are designed to dissuade Orpah and Ruth from following her. By asking, "Are there still sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands?" Naomi appeals to the custom of levirate marriage, pointing out the biological impossibility of providing for them.

• Her speech is a mixture of realistic despair and selfless concern. She uses her words to release the young women from their obligations, choosing their potential welfare in Moab over her own comfort in Judah.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between Naomi's words in verse 11 and Ruth's words in verse 16 is striking. Naomi speaks of lack, impossibility, and bitterness; Ruth speaks of covenant loyalty, presence, and shared destiny. Both use the same verb to express their deepest convictions.

• In Hebrew narrative, dialogue is structured to show who holds the narrative initiative. Naomi's long speeches in chapter 1 show her trying to control the situation through her words, but Ruth's decisive speech in verses 16–17 silences Naomi, showing that Ruth's committed love overcomes Naomi's logical despair.

• Naomi's speech serves as a test of character. In ancient Near Eastern custom, a polite refusal was expected to be made multiple times before being accepted. Naomi's insistence tests the depth of her daughters-in-law's devotion, resulting in two different but understandable responses.
