STILL - ( od , Strong's H5750 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word od indicates continuance, repetition, or persistence in time or state, translated frequently as "still," "yet," "again," or "furthermore." In the context of Ruth 1:11, Naomi uses this word to underscore the stark, physical impossibility of her bearing more sons to serve as husbands for her widowed daughters-in-law. It highlights a painful boundary of human limitation and biological finality, setting the stage for Ruth's radical step of faith which transcends natural expectations.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word od functions as an adverb or substantive particle deriving from a root associated with duplication, iteration, or returning.

• It carries the primary concept of "turning back again" or "going around," implying a continuation of a state or a repetition of an action.

• It represents a temporal or logical extension—something that continues to exist, remains present, or occurs again.

• In ancient Near Eastern life, where family lineage and continuity were paramount, this word frequently framed discussions of posterity, survival, and the ongoing presence of hope or life.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The word signifies duration, continuance, or addition. It translates directly to "still," "yet," "again," "more," or "as long as."

• It serves to describe a state that is ongoing (e.g., "while I am still alive") or an addition to an existing quantity (e.g., "are there yet more").

• In negative constructions, it denotes cessation (e.g., "no more" or "never again").

• In interrogative clauses, such as Ruth 1:11, it raises a question about the possibility of continuation or addition under current circumstances, serving to emphasize the logical impossibility of a continued expectation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Od acts as a linguistic marker of continuity. It is used to contrast what was in the past with what persists into the present.

• It can paint a picture of physical stamina or life-force remaining in a person, such as when Jacob heard that Joseph was "still" alive (Genesis 45:26).

• It conveys the concept of repetitive cycles or ongoing actions, like the regular, cyclical promise that "while the earth remains [literally: still exists], seedtime and harvest... shall not cease" (Genesis 8:22).

• In laments or moments of desperation, the word highlights the depleting nature of hope or resources, pointing out that there is "no more" strength left.

4. Theological Implications

• Theologically, od represents the boundary line between human limitation and divine intervention. When humans declare that there is "no still"—no further hope, no remaining resources, and no more possibilities—God often steps in to create a new reality.

• Naomi's use of od reflects a worldview bound by natural, visible limits. She assesses her womb, her age, and her social status, concluding that the line of continuity has reached an absolute dead end.

• The narrative of Ruth reverses this barren "still." What Naomi sees as a closed loop of history is reopened by God's providential grace. The book moves from Naomi's declaration of "no more sons" to the ultimate birth of Obed, David, and eventually the Messiah, demonstrating that God's plan of redemption continues even when human possibilities are completely exhausted.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:11, Naomi is attempting to persuade Orpah and Ruth to return to Moab. Her husband and both her sons are dead; she is destitute and returning to Bethlehem empty-handed.

• Her rhetorical question, "Are there still sons in my womb?" relies on the Levirate marriage custom (Deuteronomy 25:5-10), where a deceased man's brother was expected to marry the widow to carry on the family name.

• By asking if there are still sons, Naomi points out the double impossibility: first, she has no other sons; second, even if she were to marry and conceive that very night, her daughters-in-law could not wait for those sons to grow up.

• The word od here marks the absolute end of biological and legal hope. It functions to crush any unrealistic optimism and force her daughters-in-law to face the grim reality of their situation.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 45:26 – "And they told him, saying, 'Joseph is still alive,' and he was governor over all the land of Egypt."

• Genesis 48:7 – "And as for me, when I came from Padan, Rachel died beside me in the land of Canaan on the way, when there was but a little distance to go to Ephrath..."

• Job 27:3 – "As long as my breath is in me, and the breath of God in my nostrils..."

• Psalm 146:2 – "While I live I will praise the Lord; I will sing praises to my God while I have my being."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between Naomi’s perspective of "nothing still remaining" and the reality of God's covenant faithfulness is a central literary motif in Ruth. Naomi looks at her physical lack (no od sons) and calls herself Mara (bitter). Yet, the covenant-keeping God of Israel has "still" reserved a remnant and a redeemer (Boaz) who will restore the family line.

• The word serves as a pastoral reminder that faith does not depend on visible reserves or "still" existing human options. Where human continuity ends, divine restoration begins.
