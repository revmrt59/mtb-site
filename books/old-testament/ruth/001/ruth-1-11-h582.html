HUSBANDS - ( enosh , Strong's H582 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word enosh (appearing in its plural form anashim, which serves as the plural for both enosh and ish) refers to humanity in its frailty, mortality, and weakness, or simply to "men" in a general sense. In the context of marriage, the plural anashim carries the specific nuance of "husbands"—those men covenantally bound to wives. In Ruth 1:11, Naomi uses this term to emphasize the sheer physical impossibility and temporal absurdity of Orpah and Ruth waiting for unborn sons to grow up and become their husbands. By using a word rooted in human frailty and temporal limits, the text highlights the desperate, vulnerable state of these childless widows in the ancient Near East and sets the stage for the redemption that transcends human limitation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root enosh evokes the concept of mortality, weakness, and transient human existence. Unlike adam (which points to man’s origin from the ground) or ish (which often emphasizes man’s strength, individuality, or role as husband), enosh points to the inherent frailty, vulnerability, and dependence of human beings. In the ancient Near Eastern world, a woman’s security, economic survival, and societal standing were entirely dependent on her connection to a male protector—either a father, a husband, or a son. Without these "men" (anashim), a woman was exposed to extreme vulnerability. In Ruth 1:11, the term refers to the potential husbands Naomi cannot provide, framing the widows' plight in terms of human helplessness and the hard realities of mortal existence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The singular noun enosh refers to a mortal, humanity, or a frail human being. The plural form anashim is the common plural for both enosh and the related word ish (man/husband). In the Hebrew Bible, anashim has a broad semantic range:

• Mankind or human beings in general, emphasizing mortality and weakness.

• Individual men or a group of men.

• Husbands, when used in a covenantal or familial context to denote male spouses.

In Ruth 1:11, the term is prefixed with the preposition le ("for/to"), rendering la'anashim, which translates contextually as "for husbands." The lexical focus is on male companions in the covenant of marriage who provide legal standing, protection, and the continuation of the family line.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word enosh is frequently used in poetic and wisdom literature to contrast the weakness of mortal man with the infinite power and eternity of God. It paints a picture of a creature whose life is fleeting, like grass, and who cannot save himself.

When the plural anashim is used for "men" or "husbands," it brings along this subtle backdrop of human limitation. In a familial context, "men" are the physical protectors, yet they are mortal and subject to death—as Naomi, Orpah, and Ruth have just experienced with the deaths of Elimelech, Mahlon, and Chilion. The word picture of waiting for "husbands" (anashim) in Ruth 1:11 underscores the vanity of putting hope in mortal flesh to solve their immediate crisis of survival.

4. Theological Implications

Theological implications of the use of anashim (derived from the roots of human frailty) in the book of Ruth underscore the theme of divine providence overriding human helplessness:

• The Frailty of Human Security: Naomi’s argument is built on the physical limits of human reproduction and aging. Her focus is entirely on the horizontal, mortal level: she is too old to have a husband, and even if she did, her daughters-in-law cannot wait for newborn sons to become mature anashim (husbands).

• The Covenant of Marriage: The passage assumes the levirate marriage custom (Deuteronomy 25:5-10), where a deceased man's brother must marry the widow to continue the family line. Naomi highlights the collapse of this human system of redemption, forcing the reader to look beyond human means to God’s sovereign provision.

• Contrast with Divine Redemption: While human anashim fail, die, and cannot be produced on demand, God remains the ultimate Protector and Redeemer of the widow.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:11, Naomi is attempting to persuade Orpah and Ruth to return to Moab. Her speech is filled with rhetorical questions designed to shatter any unrealistic hopes. The term la'anashim ("to be your husbands") is situated at the climax of her first logical argument. She points out the physiological impossibility of her bearing sons who could grow up to be their husbands. The nuance is highly pragmatic, bordering on despair. Naomi is saying, "Even if the laws of nature were suspended and I gave birth today, you would be ruined waiting for them to become grown men." By focusing on the physical impossibility of securing anashim, Naomi reveals her own spiritual despondency, viewing her circumstances solely through the lens of human limitation rather than divine possibility.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

To understand the semantic scope of enosh (H582) and its plural/cognate usage, consider these parallel occurrences:

• Psalm 8:4 - "What is man that You are mindful of him, And the son of man that You visit him?"

• Psalm 9:20 - "Put them in fear, O LORD, That the nations may know themselves to be but men."

• Psalm 90:3 - "You turn man to destruction, And say, 'Return, O children of men.'"

• Psalm 103:15 - "As for man, his days are like grass; As a flower of the field, so he flourishes."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The linguistic overlap between the plural of ish (man/husband) and enosh (mortal/weak man) in the form anashim creates a beautiful literary tension in the Old Testament. When a woman refers to her "husband" (ish), there is an association with strength and headship. However, when the plural anashim is used, it can carry a dual connotation of "husbands" and "frail mortals." Naomi’s daughters-in-law did not just lose "husbands" as legal entities; they lost their mortal protectors, leaving them utterly exposed. This tension is only resolved when Boaz enters the narrative as a "man of great wealth" (ish gibbor chayil), a strong redeemer who willingly steps into the void left by dead men to provide true, lasting security under the wings of the Lord God of Israel.
