TURN BACK - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub is a foundational Old Testament word meaning to turn back, return, or undergo a radical change of physical or spiritual direction. Occurring over one thousand times, it spans literal, physical movement—such as Naomi urging Orpah and Ruth to go back to their homeland—to profound theological turning, denoting repentance and covenantal return to God. In Ruth 1:11, it underscores the physical and relational crossroads faced by the daughters-in-law, serving as a literary pivot point that contrasts natural self-preservation with covenant-making loyalty.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The literal picture of shub is a complete reversal of physical direction. It depicts someone walking down a path, stopping, turning 180 degrees, and retracing their steps back to their starting point. In the ancient Near Eastern context, pathways were hazardous, and turning back often meant returning to the security of one's home, family, or city walls. It carries the physical weight of abandoning a journey and returning to a previous state or location of safety.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shub is a primary root occurring over 1,050 times in the Old Testament, making it the twelfth most frequent verb in the Hebrew Bible. Its basic meaning is to turn back, return, or go back.

• In the Qal stem (used in Ruth 1:11), it denotes a physical return to a place, a return to a previous state, or a spiritual turning back to God.

• In the causative Hiphil stem, it means to bring back, restore, or pay back.

• In the Polel stem, it signifies restoring, bringing back, or refreshing the life or soul.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Physical Pivot: This is the most common usage, representing a physical change of location. It describes people returning to their homes, armies returning from battle, or dust returning to the earth.

• The Relational Pivot (Repentance): Spiritually, shub is the definitive Old Testament word for repentance. It depicts a sinner turning away from a path of self-will and returning to the covenant relationship with Yahweh. It is not merely a mental or emotional change, but an active, visible reversal of behavior.

• The Sovereign Restoration: God is often the subject of shub, either turning away His anger from His people or returning His people to their land after exile, demonstrating His restoring grace.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shub demonstrates that covenant relationship with God is dynamic rather than static. When humanity walks away from God, the remedy is always a physical and spiritual turning back. Crucially, the Old Testament links human turning with divine turning: when people return to God in repentance, God promises to return to them in favor and restoration. Repentance (shub) is a return to a home, a design, and a relationship that was lost.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:11, Naomi commands her daughters-in-law to "turn back" (shub) to their mother's houses in Moab. In the broader narrative of Ruth 1, shub is the dominant keyword, appearing twelve times. It highlights the geographical and spiritual tension of the chapter. Naomi is returning to Bethlehem because the famine has ended, but she urges Orpah and Ruth to return to Moab for their own security. The word acts as a spiritual sieve. For Orpah, turning back (shub) means returning to her people and her gods (Ruth 1:15). For Ruth, refusing to turn back from following Naomi represents a conversion—a turning away from Moabite idolatry to cleave to Naomi’s God, Yahweh.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:19: "In the sweat of your face you shall eat bread Till you return to the ground, For out of it you were taken; For dust you are, And to dust you shall return."

• Psalm 23:3: "He restores my soul; He leads me in the paths of righteousness For His name's sake."

• Isaiah 55:7: "Let the wicked forsake his way, And the unrighteous man his thoughts; Let him return to the LORD, And He will have mercy on him; And to our God, For He will abundantly pardon."

• Malachi 3:7: "Yet from the days of your fathers You have gone away from My ordinances And have not kept them. Return to Me, and I will return to you," Says the LORD of hosts."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Septuagint (LXX) translates the Hebrew shub in Ruth 1:11 using the Greek verb epistrepho (specifically in the imperative form epistraphete, "turn back/return"). This is the exact Greek verb used in the New Testament to describe conversion and turning to God (as seen in Acts 3:19 and 1 Thessalonians 1:9). This linguistic connection reinforces that Ruth's refusal to turn back to Moab is structurally and theologically equivalent to a New Testament conversion.
