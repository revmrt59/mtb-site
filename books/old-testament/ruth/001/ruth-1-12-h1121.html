SONS - ( ben , Strong's H1121 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ben refers primarily to a male offspring, son, descendant, or member of a nation or group. In the ancient Near East, a son was not only a source of familial joy but the guarantor of lineage, the protector of the family estate, and the provider for widowed mothers. In the context of Ruth 1:12, Naomi uses the term to highlight her absolute inability to produce new heirs who could fulfill the levirate marriage custom, emphasizing her deep grief and the humanly hopeless situation facing her daughters-in-law.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun ben is one of the most frequently used words in the Old Testament. It is conceptually connected to the Hebrew verb banah, which means "to build." This relationship highlights a vital cultural concept: children, and sons in particular, build up the household, preserving the name, property, and legacy of the father. In ancient Israel, a son was the critical link between the past and the future. He inherited the ancestral land, which was a sacred trust from God, ensuring that the family's inheritance remained within the tribe. To be left without sons meant the extinction of the family name and the loss of their allocated portion of the Promised Land.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word ben carries several layers of meaning depending on the context:

• A direct male descendant of the first generation (a son).

• A grandson or more distant male descendant (grandson, great-grandson, etc.).

• Members of a specific nation, tribe, or group (e.g., "sons of Israel" or "sons of the prophets").

• A person characterized by a specific age, trait, or destiny (e.g., "a son of death" meaning one worthy of death, or "sons of valor" meaning brave men).

• The plural form banim can refer to children of both genders in general contexts, but in legal, genealogical, and inheritance contexts, it refers specifically to male heirs.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Building the House: Because of its linguistic connection to "building" (banah), the family is viewed as a physical structure. Sons are the structural building blocks that keep the household from collapsing into ruin.

• Arrows in a Quiver: Psalm 127 compares sons to arrows in the hand of a warrior. They provide defense, strength, and security for the parents as they face adversaries in the gate of the city.

• Olive Plants: Psalm 128 depicts sons as flourishing olive plants around a table, representing vitality, future abundance, and the continuous renewal of life.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenant and Inheritance: The lineage of Abraham and the preservation of Israel's tribes depended entirely on the gift of sons. God’s promise of a land and a great nation required descendants to inherit it.

• The Levirate Law: Under the Mosaic Law (Deuteronomy 25:5–10), if a married man died without a son, his brother was obligated to marry the widow. The firstborn son of this new union would carry the name of the deceased brother so that his name would not be blotted out of Israel.

• The Messianic Hope: God's redemptive plan is progressively revealed through genealogy. Every son born in the covenant line represents a preserved link leading toward the ultimate "Son of David" and "Son of God," who would bring eternal redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:12, Naomi is in a state of utter despair. Having lost her husband, Elimelech, and her two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, she is left without any male heirs to protect her or carry on the family name.

• When she asks, "if I should also bear sons," she is pointing out the biological and chronological absurdity of her daughters-in-law waiting for her to conceive, carry, and raise new sons to adulthood to act as levirate husbands.

• Naomi’s use of "sons" underscores her perceived empty state. In her mind, the line is dead. There are no sons, no husband, and no hope. This sets up the dramatic reversal of the narrative. God will provide a son not through Naomi's womb, but through the loyalty of the Gentile Ruth and the redemption of Boaz, demonstrating that God can raise up heirs and restoration from the ashes of complete ruin.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 21:2: "For Sarah conceived and bore Abraham a son in his old age, at the set time of which God had spoken to him."

• Deuteronomy 25:5: "If brothers dwell together, and one of them dies and has no son, the wife of the dead man shall not be married to a stranger outside the family; her husband’s brother shall go in to her, take her as his wife, and perform the duty of a husband’s brother to her."

• Psalm 127:3: "Behold, children are a heritage from the LORD, The fruit of the womb is a reward."

• Isaiah 9:6: "For unto us a Child is born, Unto us a Son is given; And the government will be upon His shoulder. And His name will be called Wonderful, Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace."

• Ruth 4:17: "Also the neighbor women gave him a name, saying, 'There is a son born to Naomi.' And they called his name Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The term ben forms the prefix of many Hebrew names, indicating lineage and character. For example, Benjamin means "son of the right hand," and Barnabas (Aramaic) means "son of encouragement."

• The structural symmetry of the book of Ruth is framed by the word ben. In chapter 1, Naomi laments the loss of her two sons and the impossibility of bearing more. In chapter 4, the story reaches its climax when the women of Bethlehem celebrate the birth of Obed, crying out, "There is a son born to Naomi!" (Ruth 4:17). What was biologically impossible for Naomi, God accomplished through redemptive grace, weaving this "son" into the direct lineage of Jesus Christ.
