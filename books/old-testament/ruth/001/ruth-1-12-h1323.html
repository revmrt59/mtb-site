DAUGHTERS - ( bat , Strong's H1323 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word bat (plural banot) primarily means "daughter" or "female descendant." In the book of Ruth, particularly in Naomi's address to Orpah and Ruth, the term transcends mere biological lineage. It serves as an affectionate, relational, and protective designation. By calling her Moabite daughters-in-law "my daughters," Naomi claims them as family, demonstrating a profound relational bond and maternal care that ignores ethnic divides, even as she urges them to return to their maternal homes for their own survival.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun bat is the feminine counterpart to ben (son), both deriving from the root verb banah, which means "to build." This etymological connection highlights the daughter's role in "building" a family and continuing the household lineage. In ancient Israelite society, daughters were central to the domestic structure, carrying forward family alliances and household traditions. However, the term was also frequently used idiomatically to denote female citizens of a specific city or women of a particular nation, emphasizing community identity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Bat denotes a female offspring, a female descendant, or a young woman. It carries several distinct semantic applications:

• Literal daughter (immediate female offspring).

• Granddaughter or female descendant within a lineage.

• An address of affection used by an elder toward a younger woman.

• A personification of a collective group or city (e.g., "daughter of Zion").

• A relational term expressing a familial bond of dependency, protection, and care.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Built House: Since bat is linked to the verb banah ("to build"), a daughter is viewed as a foundational pillar who builds the family house. This imagery is explicit in Ruth 4:11, where Rachel and Leah are described as those who "built the house of Israel."

• Metaphor for Communities: The phrase "daughter of [place]" portrays a city or nation as a cherished, vulnerable, and deeply loved female child under the care of God.

• Adoption of Affection: Naomi’s use of "my daughters" (benotay) establishes a covenantal-like family bond that overrides biological and national boundaries.

4. Theological Implications

• Inclusion and Covenant Love: The use of bat for Orpah and Ruth—who were Moabite women—demonstrates that relational love (chesed) transcends ethnic boundaries. Naomi does not address them as "foreigners" or even "daughters-in-law," but as her own daughters.

• Providential Reconstruction: God uses "daughters" who seemed to have no future to rebuild a devastated family line, ultimately leading to the lineage of David and Jesus Christ.

• Maternal Protection: Naomi's self-sacrificing love is revealed in her use of this term. She values their future, security, and potential to find "rest" in a new husband's house above her own comfort and companionship in her old age.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:12, Naomi is in a state of deep grief and bitterness, yet she addresses Orpah and Ruth with tender endearment: "Turn back, my daughters, go." The repetition of "my daughters" (also in verses 11 and 22) highlights Naomi's painful conflict: she deeply loves them and views them as her own flesh and blood, yet she believes she has no hope or future to offer them in Judah. Addressing them as "daughters" shows that they are not bound to her merely by legal contract (which ended with the deaths of her sons) but by genuine familial love. It validates their status within the family, making Orpah's departure and Ruth's absolute loyalty even more emotionally poignant.

• Ruth 2:8 - "Then Boaz said to Ruth, 'Listen, my daughter, will you not go to glean in another field, nor go from here, but stay close by my young women?'"

• Psalm 45:10 - "Listen, O daughter, consider and incline your ear; Forget your own people also, and your father's house;"

• Isaiah 52:2 - "Shake yourself from the dust, arise; Sit down, O Jerusalem; Loose yourself from the bonds of your neck, O captive daughter of Zion!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition of Ruth from "daughter" (Ruth 1:12) to "stranger/foreigner" (Ruth 2:10), then back to "daughter" in Boaz's eyes (Ruth 2:8; 3:10), and finally to one who is "better to you than seven sons" (Ruth 4:15) traces a powerful theological arc of redemption, adoption, and the restoration of worth in ancient Israelite society.
