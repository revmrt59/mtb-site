OLD - ( zaqan , Strong's H2204 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb zaqan denotes the process of growing old or reaching an advanced age. In Ruth 1:12, Naomi uses this term to describe her biological reality—she has passed the years of childbearing—to demonstrate the impossibility of providing new husbands for her widowed daughters-in-law. This declaration of physical exhaustion and social hopelessness serves as the narrative foil for God's unexpected, covenant-keeping redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb zaqan shares a root with the Hebrew noun for "beard" (zaqan), which was the physical marker of maturity, wisdom, and leadership in the ancient Near East. The concept of growing old is not merely chronological in the Hebrew worldview; it is biological, sociological, and spiritual. It marks the transition from physical strength and productivity to dependency, while simultaneously carrying the potential for wisdom, honor, and societal leadership.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb zaqan is a stative verb meaning "to be old" or "to grow old." The semantic range encompasses:

• The natural biological progression of human life toward its later stages.

• The physical decline of strength and bodily functions.

• The loss of reproductive capability in women.

• The attainment of the elder status within the community structure.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs zaqan to paint several distinct word pictures:

• Physical Decline: It describes the natural wearing down of the human body, such as failing eyesight, the inability to retain body heat, and barrenness (as seen in Sarah).

• The Inevitable End of Leadership: It marks the transition point where spiritual and political leaders must hand over their responsibilities to the next generation, as demonstrated by Joshua and Samuel.

• Social Dependency: To grow old in ancient Israel meant stepping back from hard manual labor and relying on the covenant family structure for care and legacy.

4. Theological Implications

While old age is often celebrated in the Old Testament as a crown of glory when found in the path of righteousness, it also serves as a stark reminder of human mortality, limitation, and the consequences of the Fall. Theologically, zaqan often marks the boundary where human capability ends and sovereign grace begins. When the human body is "old" and unable to produce life, God consistently steps into the barrenness to perform His redemptive work. Naomi’s old age represents a biological dead end, illustrating that redemption must come from an outside source—not from her own biological power, but from the covenant-keeping God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:12, Naomi uses zaqan to construct an ironclad, logical argument to persuade Orpah and Ruth to return to Moab. When she says, "for I am too old to have a husband," she is appealing to her biological reality. She is past the age of childbearing, which makes the practice of levirate marriage impossible. Even if she were to marry and bear sons immediately, her daughters-in-law could not wait for them to mature. The word zaqan here is heavy with grief, exhaustion, and finality. In a patriarchal society where a woman's security and value were inextricably linked to her husband and children, Naomi’s "oldness" is her confession of complete vulnerability and social death.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 18:13: "And the LORD said to Abraham, 'Why did Sarah laugh, saying, "Shall I surely bear a child, since I am old?"'"

• Joshua 23:2: "Joshua called for all Israel, for their elders, for their heads, for their judges, and for their officers, and said to them: 'I am old, advanced in age.'"

• 1 Samuel 8:1: "Now it came to pass when Samuel was old that he made his sons judges over Israel."

• 2 Samuel 19:32: "Now Barzillai was very old, eighty years old."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The verbal root zaqan is directly related to the noun zaqen, which is translated as "elder." While Naomi's physical "oldness" (zaqan) renders her weak and unproductive in her own eyes, her role as an elder (zaqen) in the family remains vital. Her physical limitations do not diminish her spiritual and relational value, as she eventually provides the strategic, wise counsel that guides Ruth to Boaz’s threshing floor in Ruth 3. God repurposes her weakness to secure the lineage of David and, ultimately, the Messiah.
