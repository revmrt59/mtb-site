BEAR - ( yalad , Strong's H3205 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yalad primarily means to bear, bring forth, give birth to, or beget. It is a fundamental covenantal word used throughout the Old Testament to describe the continuation of family lines, the fulfillment of divine promises, and the literal birth of children. In the narrative of Ruth, yalad is central to the themes of barrenness, family redemption, and the continuation of the Messianic lineage, highlighting Naomi's despair over her empty womb and the eventual restoration of her family line through Ruth.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb yalad is deeply rooted in the ancient Near Eastern context of family, lineage, and legacy. In a culture where a person's future, security, and memory depended entirely on their offspring, to "bear" children was seen as the ultimate sign of divine favor, while barrenness was viewed as a profound tragedy and a sign of potential judgment. The term evokes the painful yet hopeful process of labor and delivery, transitioning from empty expectation to the tangible reality of a newborn child who carries on the family name and inherits ancestral land.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of yalad is to give birth to, bear children (referring to the mother), or to beget (referring to the father). In the active Qal stem, it refers directly to the act of a mother bringing forth a child. In the intensive Piel stem, it means to act as a midwife, helping another to give birth. In the causative Hiphil stem, it means to beget (fathering a child) or to cause to bring forth. In the passive Niphal and Pual stems, it refers to being born. Lexically, the word denotes the biological transition of bringing new life into the world, but it carries immense legal and covenantal weight regarding inheritance, tribal continuity, and family identity.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word yalad is used extensively in genealogies to mark the progression of history and covenant lineages from generation to generation. It is also used metaphorically in Scripture to describe the labor pains of distress, judgment, or the birth of new spiritual realities. The physical imagery of labor pains is frequently paired with yalad to depict intense suffering that ultimately leads to a significant, often redemptive, outcome.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yalad is closely tied to the Abrahamic Covenant, where God promised to make Abraham's descendants exceedingly fruitful. Because human fertility is ultimately under God's sovereign control, every instance of yalad in a previously closed womb represents a direct, gracious intervention of Yahweh. The term underscores the reality that life is a gift from God. Furthermore, yalad points forward to the ultimate "seed" promised in Genesis 3:15, tracing the line of childbirth that would eventually culminate in the birth of the Messiah.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:12, Naomi uses yalad in a highly rhetorical, hypothetical argument to convince her daughters-in-law to return to Moab. She presents an impossible scenario to illustrate the hopelessness of their situation if they stay with her. Even if she were to find a husband "tonight" and immediately conceive and "bear" sons, she argues, it would be absurd for Ruth and Orpah to wait for these sons to grow up to perform the duty of levirate marriage. Naomi uses the physical impossibility of her bearing children at her advanced age to demonstrate that her womb is functionally dead, making her a dead end for their future security. This sets up a powerful literary contrast with the end of the book, where Yahweh miraculously enables Ruth to "bear" a son (Ruth 4:13), reversing Naomi's emptiness.

• Isaiah 9:6 - "For unto us a Child is born, Unto us a Son is given; And the government will be upon His shoulder."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The root of yalad is also the source of the noun yeled (child or boy) and toledot (generations or family histories). This linguistic connection reinforces that history is not a series of random events but a structured progression of generations brought forth under the providence of God. In the book of Ruth, the transition from Naomi's despair over her inability to yalad in chapter 1 to the community's celebration of Ruth's ability to yalad in chapter 4 demonstrates the movement from death to life, and from emptiness to fullness, which characterizes the gospel.
