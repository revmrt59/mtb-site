GO - ( yalak , Strong's H3212 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yalak means to walk, go, or depart. In Ruth 1:12, Naomi uses this imperative command to release her widowed daughters-in-law from any familial or legal obligation to her, urging them to return to their homeland of Moab. This is a profound moment of emotional and physical parting, highlighting the absolute absence of human hope for their future if they remain with her. The word functions as a loving but despairing release, setting the stage for Orpah's departure and Ruth's monumental decision to stay.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• yalak is one of the most common verbs of movement in the Old Testament. It carries the physical imagery of stepping, traveling, or journeying from one point to another. In the ancient Near East, walking or "going" somewhere often represented a major life transition, as travel was dangerous, arduous, and highly permanent. When Naomi commands her daughters-in-law to "go," she is telling them to walk away from her destiny and back toward their own domestic, cultural, and spiritual origins. It is a movement of definitive separation and relocation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root yalak refers to the act of walking, proceeding, or moving along a path. Depending on the context, it means to depart, to walk in a certain lifestyle, or to disappear. In the imperative form used in Ruth 1:12 (lekna), it functions as an urgent, compassionate directive. It signifies departing or returning to a place of origin, carrying the force of "go on your way" or "be on your way."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Movement: Often describes literal walking or journeying, such as Abraham going to the land God would show him.

• Metaphorical Conduct: Frequently used to describe one's lifestyle or spiritual walk, such as walking in the law of the Lord or going after other gods.

• Flowing Water: Used to describe rivers that run, flow, or stream.

• Vanishing: Used of clouds or morning dew that pass away and do not return.

4. Theological Implications

The use of yalak in the Book of Ruth highlights the theme of human decision-making under divine providence. Naomi’s command to "go" tests the loyalty and faith of both Orpah and Ruth. Covenant loyalty (chesed) is demonstrated not by coerced obedience but by voluntary commitment. By telling them to "go," Naomi strips away all external, societal pressure, forcing them to choose their path based on genuine devotion. Orpah's choice to "go" back to her gods contrasts sharply with Ruth's refusal to "go," illustrating that true faith and covenant love transcend cultural expectations and physical self-preservation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:12, the command lekna ("go") is coupled with the command to "turn back" (shobnah). Naomi emphasizes the futility of staying with her. She describes herself as "too old to have a husband" and notes that even if she had hope, conceived tonight, and bore sons, the girls could not wait for them to grow up. Therefore, "go" is an act of despairing love. Naomi is trying to spare them her bitter lot. She equates going back to Moab with returning to physical security, remarriage, and familiar gods, unaware that God is orchestrating a greater redemption back in Bethlehem.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:4: "So Abram departed as the Lord had spoken to him, and Lot went with him."

• Exodus 4:18: "So Moses went and returned to Jethro his father-in-law, and said to him, 'Please let me go and return to my brethren who are in Egypt, and see if they are still alive.'"

• Ruth 1:11: "But Naomi said, 'Turn back, my daughters; why will you go with me? Are there still sons in my womb, that they may be your husbands?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The imperative form of yalak (lek or lekna) is a call to action that often marks a critical fork in the road of a biblical narrative. In Genesis 12:1, the command lek sent Abraham into a future of faith. Here in Ruth 1:12, the command lekna is a backward call—a call to return to the pagan world. It highlights the tension between the easy path of returning to what is comfortable (yalak back to Moab) and the difficult path of moving forward into the unknown with the covenant God (yalak with Naomi to Bethlehem).
