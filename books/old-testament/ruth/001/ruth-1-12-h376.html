HUSBAND - ( 'ish , Strong's H376 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ish primarily denotes a man, a husband, or an individual of high standing, embodying strength, responsibility, and covenant relationship. In the context of Ruth 1:12, it refers specifically to a husband who can provide legal protection, family continuity, and economic security through the levirate marriage custom. Naomi uses the word to highlight the utter hopelessness of her situation, emphasizing that even if she were to immediately find a husband and conceive, it would be humanly impossible for her daughters-in-law to wait for those sons to grow up to redeem them.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The primary meaning of 'ish is an individual male or a husband, frequently contrasted with ishshah (woman or wife). Unlike adam, which often refers to humanity collectively, 'ish specifies a distinct individual of action, capability, and relational agency. Culturally, the 'ish in ancient Israel served as the functional head of the household, responsible for the preservation of the family line, the defense of the clan's inheritance, and legal representation at the city gates. The word picture conveys strength, covenant partnership, and active guardianship over the home.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The lexical definition of 'ish denotes an individual male person, a husband, or a man of high standing. The semantic range includes:

• An individual man (as opposed to a woman or child).

• A husband, indicating a covenantal marital relationship.

• A leader, champion, or person of social prominence.

• A distributive pronoun meaning "each" or "every."

In relational contexts, 'ish identifies the male partner in a marriage covenant, carrying the legal and relational duty to sustain and protect his wife.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Hebrew scriptures utilize 'ish to paint rich pictures of human and divine relationships:

• Creation and Oneness: In the Garden of Eden, the relationship between husband ('ish) and wife (ishshah) is defined by shared essence and mutual belonging. The woman is called ishshah because she was taken out of 'ish, presenting a word picture of perfect unity.

• The Protector of the Inheritance: In Israelite society, an 'ish was the guardian of ancestral land. To be without an 'ish was to be without a voice in the civil and legal spheres, leaving widows vulnerable to poverty and exploitation.

• The Covenant Partner: Unlike the word ba'al (which can mean master or owner), 'ish conveys a partnership characterized by intimacy, love, and reciprocal commitment.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of 'ish shines brightest in its covenantal application. God uses marital terminology to illustrate His relationship with Israel. He does not merely desire to be called Master (ba'al), but Husband ('ish), signaling an intimate, reciprocal covenant rather than one of cold ownership.

Furthermore, the absence of an 'ish in a household—as seen in the opening of the book of Ruth—represents a state of spiritual and physical death. Without an 'ish, there is no seed to carry on the family name, meaning the family line is cut off from the covenant promises of Israel. The redemption of the widow requires a kinsman-redeemer who must be a worthy 'ish to step into the gap, restore the inheritance, and raise up the name of the dead.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:12, Naomi stands on the road to Judah, pleading with her widowed daughters-in-law to return to Moab. She reasons, "Turn back, my daughters, go; for I am too old to have a husband. If I should say I have hope, if I should have a husband tonight and should also bear sons..."

Here, 'ish carries intense biological and legal weight. Naomi is referencing the custom of levirate marriage. For Naomi to have an 'ish "tonight" would mean immediate remarriage with the intent to conceive heirs. The nuance is one of absolute desperation. Even if she could instantly secure a husband and bear male heirs, Orpah and Ruth could not reasonably remain unmarried and wait for those boys to grow to manhood to redeem them. By using 'ish, Naomi emphasizes that her family line is biologically dead, her husband's land is lost, and she has no means to provide the security a husband represents.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a profound linguistic contrast in Hebrew between 'ish and ba'al. While both can be translated as "husband," ba'al emphasizes ownership, mastery, and domestic control. Conversely, 'ish emphasizes partnership, identification, and covenant connection.

This linguistic distinction heightens the narrative transition in the book of Ruth. Boaz is introduced in Ruth 2:1 as a "man of great wealth" ('ish gibbor chayil). He is not merely a potential ba'al who will take ownership of land, but the noble 'ish who will compassionately step into Naomi's empty domestic space, marrying Ruth to restore the name, legacy, and hope of the deceased.
