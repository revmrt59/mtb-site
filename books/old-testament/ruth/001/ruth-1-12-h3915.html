TONIGHT - ( laylah , Strong's H3915 )

Summary:

Naomi uses the word tonight (ha-laylah) in Ruth 1:12 to establish the absolute impossibility of her daughters-in-law finding a future through her. By presenting a hypothetical scenario where she marries and conceives this very night, Naomi argues that even in this best-case, instantaneous event, the resulting sons would take far too long to grow up. The word grounds Naomi's grief in immediate biological reality, highlighting her profound sense of hopelessness while setting up a narrative tension that God's providence will later resolve in the dark hours of a different night.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun laylah describes the period of darkness that follows the setting of the sun. In the ancient Near Eastern world, night was a time when productivity ceased, travelers sought safety, and the vulnerability of humanity was most keenly felt. It was also the designated time for rest, dreams, and marital intimacy. By introducing the idea of finding a husband "tonight," Naomi invokes the deeply personal, domestic, and private setting of the marriage bed. She points to the dark hours of the coming night not as a time of rest or hope, but as the starting point of an improbable timeline for raising heirs.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word laylah refers to "night" or "night season." When prefixed with the Hebrew definite article he (and vocalized as ha-laylah), it functions adverbially to mean "this night" or "tonight." The term denotes the opposite of day (yom). In legal, biological, and temporal contexts, it designates a specific, twenty-four-hour cycle's dark half. The focus is on the immediacy of the present night, emphasizing an action that would take place without any further delay.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Laylah occurs over 220 times in the Old Testament, representing both literal darkness and metaphorical themes of distress, judgment, and hiddenness.

• Literally, it establishes the boundaries of creation (Genesis 1:5) and serves as the backdrop for significant historical events, such as the Passover (Exodus 12:12).

• Figuratively, the night is a place of deep sorrow, weeping, and spiritual trial (Psalm 6:6; Job 30:17).

• In relational contexts, it represents the intimate hours of marital union (Genesis 30:15) and familial security. Naomi uses these relational associations of the night to craft her rhetorical argument about remarriage and conception.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, laylah is under the absolute sovereignty of God, who created the night and rules over its darkness (Psalm 74:16). Naomi's use of "tonight" reveals her spiritual despair; she can only see the night as a biological dead-end. She assumes that even if God granted the immediate miracle of a husband and conception tonight, human limitations would still thwart her lineage. However, the book of Ruth ultimately demonstrates that God works in the dark hours. The night Naomi views as hopeless is a precursor to another night in Bethlehem, where Ruth will seek Boaz at the threshing floor "tonight" (Ruth 3:2), turning Naomi’s hypothetical impossibility into a covenantal reality.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:12, Naomi is pleading with Ruth and Orpah to return to their maternal homes in Moab. She employs a reductio ad absurdum (reduction to absurdity) to shatter their loyalty. Her argument operates on two levels of impossibility: first, she is too old to have a husband; second, even if she had a husband this very night and immediately conceived sons, it would be foolish for the young widows to remain unmarried while waiting for those infants to grow to manhood. The word tonight serves as the anchor for this extreme hypothetical scenario. It strips away any romanticized hope of a quick fix, confronting the daughters-in-law with the stark, unyielding reality of their shared barrenness and poverty.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The literary symmetry of laylah in the book of Ruth is striking. The term first appears in Ruth 1:12 as a marker of absolute despair and impossibility. It reappears in Ruth 3:2 and 3:13 as the strategic window of redemption. In the ancient Near East, the night was often feared as a time of chaos. Yet, in the story of Ruth, the night is transformed from a time of barrenness (1:12) to a time of covenantal commitment under the wings of the Lord (3:9). The seed conceived in the dark of Ruth 3 eventually leads to the birth of Obed, restoring Naomi's empty life and establishing the line of David.
