SAY - ( 'amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is one of the most frequently used words in the Old Testament, primarily meaning "to say," "speak," "command," or "think." In the context of Ruth 1:12, it is used by Naomi in a conditional, self-reflective sense ("If I should say, 'I have hope'"). Here, 'amar expresses not just external speech but an internal evaluation or assertion of a possibility, underscoring Naomi's profound sense of hopelessness and the raw reality of her bitter circumstances.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

Occurring over 5,000 times in the Hebrew Scriptures, 'amar is a primary root found throughout Semitic languages. It is a fundamental building block of Hebrew narrative. In ancient Israelite culture, words were not mere empty sounds but carried substantial weight. To say something was to put a thought into motion or to give voice to an internal reality. The verb connects the internal world of the heart and mind with the external world of action and relationship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic meaning of 'amar is to say, speak, or utter. The semantic range includes several distinct nuances depending on the context:

• To say or speak: Verbalizing thoughts or messages to another person.

• To think: When paired with "in the heart," 'amar denotes internal monologue, reasoning, or intention (literally, "to say in one's heart").

• To command or decree: When spoken by a person of authority, such as a king or God, the verb carries the force of an order or legislation.

• To promise or intend: Declaring a future action with resolve.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Contrast with Dabar: While dabar emphasizes the act of speaking or the spoken word as an objective entity (a "thing" or "matter"), 'amar focuses on the specific content and message of what is uttered.

• Internal Dialogue: The verb frequently depicts characters deliberating with themselves. It serves as a window into the soul, revealing true motivations, doubts, and beliefs before they manifest in outward action.

• Covenantal Declaration: It is used to establish relationships, define boundaries, and utter commitments that bind parties together.

4. Theological Implications

In the Old Testament, speech is creative and powerful. When God says ('amar), reality changes; His words possess causative power (Genesis 1). In contrast, human speech often reveals human limitation, frailty, and spiritual condition. Naomi's use of 'amar in Ruth 1:12 highlights a profound theological struggle. She is expressing her internal despair and her perception that God's hand has gone out against her. For Naomi to say she has hope would be to speak a falsehood against her perceived reality, showing that human words cannot force a reality that God has seemingly closed off.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:12, Naomi is pleading with her daughters-in-law to return to Moab. She uses 'amar within a hypothetical condition: "If I should say..." (ki 'amarti).

• Naomi is painting a picture of extreme improbability. Even if she were to verbalize a hope—to declare that things could change—the physical realities of her age and biological limitations render such words empty.

• Here, 'amar highlights the tension between words of wishful thinking and the concrete, bleak reality of three destitute widows in the ancient Near East.

• Naomi uses the verb to strip away false optimism, urging Orpah and Ruth to make realistic decisions based on their survival rather than sentimentality. Her "saying" is an honest, albeit bitter, confrontation with their shared crisis.

7. Other Relevant Insights

Grammatically, the verbal root in Ruth 1:12 is in the Qal perfect first-person singular form, introduced by a conditional particle. This construction portrays a subjective, hypothetical claim that Naomi immediately dismisses as practically impossible. This highlights her integrity and maternal concern; she refuses to use empty, pious-sounding words to keep her daughters-in-law bound to her. She would rather speak the hard truth of her empty prospects than offer them false hope.
