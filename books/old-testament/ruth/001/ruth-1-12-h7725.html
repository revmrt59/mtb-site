TURN BACK - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub is a foundational Old Testament word meaning to return, turn back, or go back to a point of origin. It occurs over one thousand times in the Hebrew Bible, carrying both literal physical meanings of movement and profound spiritual metaphors of repentance, restoration, and turning back to God. In the context of Ruth 1:12, it is Naomi's urgent maternal plea to her daughters-in-law to abandon their journey with her to Judah and return to their ancestral home of Moab.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root shub depicts a physical reversal of direction. It is the action of a traveler turning 180 degrees to walk back along the path they just traversed. The word picture is one of movement, change of direction, and returning to a starting point.

• In the Ancient Near East, where identity, safety, and sustenance were deeply tied to one's clan and homeland, shub carried heavy emotional and social weight. Turning back meant returning to a place of known identity, family, and local deities.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The basic meaning is to turn back, return, or go back. It is used to describe returning to a place, a person, or a state of being.

• In the Qal stem (as in Ruth 1:12), it means to turn back, return to a place, or turn away from a course of action.

• In the Hiphil (causative) stem, it means to bring back, restore, or repay.

• In the spiritual sense, it denotes turning away from evil and returning to Yahweh (repentance), or conversely, turning away from Yahweh to idols (apostasy).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Return: Moving back to a geographical starting point, such as returning home from battle or journeying back to one's land.

• Covenantal Repentance: Prophets frequently use shub to call Israel to "return" to Yahweh, signaling a relational restoration.

• Relational Reversal: Turning away from a person or relationship, or reconciling and returning to them.

• Restoration of State: God "returning" the captives or restoring fortunes, indicating a return to a previous state of blessing.

4. Theological Implications

• Theologically, shub is the primary Old Testament term for repentance. It demonstrates that true repentance is not merely an emotional response or mental assent, but a decisive, physical turning back from one path to walk down another.

• In the book of Ruth, shub is a major thematic keyword, occurring twelve times. It highlights the tension between returning to Moab (paganism and temporary security) versus returning to Judah (the covenant land of Yahweh). The theological irony is that while Naomi tries to send her daughters-in-law back to their false gods, Ruth refuses to turn back, choosing instead to bind herself to the true God of Israel.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:12, Naomi uses the imperative form shobna ("turn back") for the second time in her dialogue. Naomi is utterly destitute, grieving the loss of her husband and sons, and convinced that the hand of Yahweh has gone out against her.

• She uses shub as a realistic, hopeless appeal. She believes she has nothing to offer Orpah and Ruth—no security, no husbands, no future. For Naomi, their "turning back" to Moab is the only logical path to survival and remarriage.

• The physical act of turning back to Moab represents a return to their mothers' houses and their gods (Ruth 1:15), emphasizing the high spiritual cost of the physical return that Orpah ultimately chooses and Ruth rejects.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:15 - "And she said, 'Look, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods; return after your sister-in-law.'"

• Ruth 1:22 - "So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess her daughter-in-law with her, who returned from the country of Moab."

• Joel 2:12 - "Now, therefore," says the LORD, "Turn to Me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning."

• Malachi 3:7 - "Return to Me, and I will return to you," says the LORD of hosts.

• Psalm 51:13 - "Then I will teach transgressors Your ways, and sinners shall be converted to You."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The repetition of shub in Ruth chapter 1 creates a literary framework. Naomi returns to Bethlehem, but she tries to force her daughters-in-law to return to Moab.

• The contrast between Orpah, who turns back to her people and her gods, and Ruth, who refuses to turn back from following Naomi, serves to highlight Ruth's extraordinary conversion. Ruth's refusal to turn back to Moab is a physical decision that represents a spiritual turning to Yahweh.
