HOPE - ( tiqvah , Strong's H8615 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word tiqvah carries the dual meaning of a literal cord or line, and a figurative expectation, hope, or longing. Rooted in the verb qavah, which means to bind together by twisting, or to wait eagerly for, tiqvah represents a hope that is not merely wishful thinking, but an active, expectant waiting based on a secure attachment. In Ruth 1:12, Naomi uses tiqvah to express her utter lack of future expectation or prospect of bearing sons to provide security, highlighting her profound grief and the perceived emptiness of her circumstances.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The physical background of tiqvah lies in the textile world of the ancient Near East. The root verb is qavah, which means to wait for, but historically carries the concrete action of twisting or plaiting strands together to make a strong cord or rope. Consequently, the noun tiqvah originally referred to a cord or measuring line. This concrete image of a taut cord pulled tight between two points serves as the perfect metaphor for hope. Biblical hope is not a vague, passive wish; it is a tense, expectant waiting that binds a person to a future outcome, just as a rope binds two objects together.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Tiqvah has a two-fold definition in the Hebrew scriptures:

• In a concrete sense, it refers to a cord, attachment, or line.

• In an abstract sense, it refers to an expectation, hope, prospect, or the thing that is longed for.

The semantic range encompasses the feelings of anticipation, the ground of one's confidence, and the ultimate outcome of that anticipation. Unlike the modern English concept of hope, which often implies uncertainty (e.g., "I hope it doesn't rain"), tiqvah denotes a robust, confident expectation that is anchored to a reliable promise or reality.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The dual nature of tiqvah is beautifully illustrated in the narrative of Rahab in Joshua 2. When Rahab hides the Israelite spies, they instruct her to bind a "line" (tiqvah) of scarlet thread in her window. This physical cord was her literal lifeline, but it also represented her existential "hope" of salvation from the impending destruction of Jericho. In the poetic books, particularly Job, tiqvah is used to describe a hope that has withered, been uprooted, or cut off like a thread, illustrating the extreme despair of losing one's future prospects. In contrast, the Psalms use the term to describe a secure, unshakable expectation that is anchored directly in God.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, tiqvah underscores that true biblical hope requires an object of attachment. Just as a physical cord must be fastened to something secure to hold weight, spiritual hope must be fastened to God.

• The Object of Hope: In Israel's theology, God Himself is the ground of expectation. When hope is anchored in Him, it cannot be cut off.

• The Tension of Waiting: Because tiqvah implies a stretched cord, the theological reality of hope involves enduring tension. Waiting on God is rarely passive; it involves active endurance under pressure.

• A Secure Future: True tiqvah guarantees that the believer's future is firmly held by God, contrasting sharply with the hopelessness of those who live apart from His covenant promises.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:12, Naomi is grieving the loss of her husband and her two sons in Moab. She urges her daughters-in-law, Ruth and Orpah, to return to their maternal homes, stating, "Turn back, my daughters, go—for I am too old to have a husband. If I should say I have hope, if I should even have a husband tonight and should also bear sons..."

In this context, Naomi uses tiqvah to refer to her realistic prospects of remarriage, pregnancy, and providing sons to carry on her family line. To Naomi, her life's "cord" has been completely severed. She sees no future, no security, and no possibility of restoration. She is expressing absolute hopelessness under the heavy hand of providence. The literary irony of the Book of Ruth is that while Naomi cannot see any remaining tiqvah, God is already at work behind the scenes, weaving a new redemptive cord through Ruth that will secure not only Naomi's future, but the lineage of the Messiah.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Joshua 2:18 - "unless, when we come into the land, you bind this line [tiqvah] of scarlet thread in the window through which you let us down..."

• Job 14:7 - "For there is hope [tiqvah] for a tree, if it is cut down, that it will sprout again, and that its tender shoots will not cease."

• Psalm 62:5 - "My soul, wait silently for God alone, for my expectation [tiqvah] is from Him."

• Proverbs 24:14 - "So shall the knowledge of wisdom be to your soul; if you have found it, there is a prospect, and your hope [tiqvah] will not be cut off."

• Jeremiah 29:11 - "For I know the thoughts that I think toward you, says the Lord, thoughts of peace and not of evil, to give you a future and a hope [tiqvah]."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The relationship between tiqvah (hope) and qavah (waiting) reveals that in Hebrew thought, hope is an action verb before it is a noun. One cannot have tiqvah without actively waiting (qavah) on God. This linguistic connection challenges modern believers to view hope not as an internal feeling, but as a disciplined posture of the soul, waiting expectantly for God to act according to His character.
