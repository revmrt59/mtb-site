DAUGHTERS - ( bath , Strong's H1323 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, the Hebrew word bath primarily denotes a female offspring or descendant. However, its usage extends far beyond biological relationship to signify deep relational warmth, adoption, and spiritual or covenantal care. In the book of Ruth, particularly in Ruth 1:13, Naomi uses the plural form banothay ("my daughters") as a tender term of endearment for her Moabite daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth. This expression highlights a profound bond of love and pastoral concern that transcends ethnic boundaries, revealing how covenant love (chesed) redefines family dynamics in times of grief, loss, and transition.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun bath is the feminine counterpart to the Hebrew noun ben (son). It is connected to the verbal root banah, which means "to build." Historically and culturally in ancient Israel, daughters were viewed as builders of the household, vital to the continuation, strength, and establishment of the family line. In the patriarchal culture of the ancient Near East, family identity and survival were paramount. To call someone "my daughter" who was not biological offspring was a powerful linguistic act of inclusion, welcoming them into the intimate sphere of the family's affection, responsibility, and protection.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word bath carries a rich semantic range across the Old Testament scriptures:

• A biological daughter or immediate female offspring.

• A female descendant or ancestor within a specific lineage (such as "a daughter of Abraham").

• The female inhabitants of a specific city or nation, personified collectively (such as the "daughter of Zion" or "daughters of Moab").

• A term of respect, affection, and pastoral endearment used by an elder toward a younger woman.

In Ruth 1:13, the word functions in this final category, elevated by a genuine adoptive bond. Naomi’s use of the pronominal suffix ("my daughters") emphasizes that although the biological links to these women (her sons, Mahlon and Chilion) are dead, the relational bond of family remains fully alive and functional in her heart.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, bath paints beautiful pictures of relationship and worth. It is used to describe the close-knit community of God's people, often referred to as the "daughter of My people" to express God's tender affection and deep grief over their suffering. It is also used to depict a prized possession or a highly protected entity, as seen in the phrase "apple of the eye," which is literally "daughter of the eye" in Hebrew, representing the pupil which is instinctively guarded. In Hebrew wisdom literature, the term is occasionally used by teachers to address disciples, imparting life-giving instruction with parental warmth.

4. Theological Implications

Naomi's address of Orpah and Ruth as "my daughters" reflects the heart of covenantal love (chesed). It demonstrates that in the economy of God's kingdom, relationship is not determined merely by biology, bloodlines, or ethnicity, but by shared commitment and mutual care. This foreshadows the New Testament reality of spiritual adoption, where believers from all nations are brought into the family of God, calling one another brothers and sisters, and being addressed as sons and daughters of the Living God. Naomi's willingness to release her daughters-in-law for their own security, despite her own deep loneliness, showcases a self-sacrificing love that mirrors the divine character.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:13, Naomi is urging Orpah and Ruth to return to their maternal homes in Moab. She recognizes that staying with her offers no prospect of physical security, provision, or remarriage, as she is too old to bear more sons who could fulfill the levirate marriage custom. Her exclamation, "No, my daughters; for it grieves me very much for your sakes," reveals her deep distress. The use of "my daughters" here is bittersweet. It acknowledges their close, loving bond while simultaneously attempting to sever their obligation to her. Naomi is releasing them from any cultural duty to care for an aging mother-in-law. Her pain is doubled: she grieves her own losses, but she grieves even more "for your sakes" because she perceives that the hand of the Lord has gone out against her, and she does not want them to suffer under her bitter circumstances.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The repetition of "my daughter" (bath) throughout the Book of Ruth acts as a thematic thread of redemption and acceptance. Naomi calls Ruth "my daughter" repeatedly (Ruth 2:2, 2:22, 3:1, 3:18), and Boaz uses the exact same address to Ruth (Ruth 2:8, 3:10, 3:11). This repetition signals Ruth's progressive integration into the covenant community of Israel. A foreign, marginalized Moabite widow is welcomed, protected, and honored as a true "daughter" in Israel, ultimately placing her in the genealogy of King David and Jesus Christ.
