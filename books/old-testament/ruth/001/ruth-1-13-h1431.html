GROWN - ( gadal , Strong's H1431 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb gadal primarily means to grow, become great, or be magnified. In the context of Ruth 1:13, Naomi uses the term to describe the physical maturation of potential future sons to manhood. Her rhetorical question highlights the painful absurdity of the young widows, Ruth and Orpah, waiting for infants to mature to marriageable age under the practice of levirate marriage. This word underscores the theme of helplessness and the humanly impossible situation Naomi's family faced, setting the stage for God's sovereign redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of gadal expresses the idea of becoming large, strong, or great. It denotes the process of physical maturation, the expansion of status, or the magnification of power. In ancient Near Eastern culture, the survival and legacy of a family rested heavily on sons growing to maturity. To be left without grown sons left a widow in a state of extreme social, legal, and economic vulnerability. The word evokes the image of a sapling growing into a sturdy tree, representing security, strength, and future hope.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb gadal possesses a rich semantic range across its various stems. In the Qal stem, it means to grow up, become great, or increase. In the Piel stem, it means to bring up, cause to grow, or make great. In the Hiphil stem, it means to make great, magnify, or do great things. In Ruth 1:13, the verb appears in the Qal imperfect form, referring specifically to the physical process of children maturing into manhood. The definition here focuses on the biological and chronological process of reaching full age.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The primary word picture of gadal in the Old Testament is physical development, such as a child growing into adulthood or a plant growing to full size. It is also used to describe the accumulation of wealth and influence, conveying a sense of expansion and rising status. When applied to God, it represents the magnification of His name, power, or lovingkindness, portraying Him as towering in greatness above all creation.

• Physical growth: Samuel growing before the Lord or Moses growing up in Egypt.

• Status and wealth: Abraham and Isaac growing prosperous and becoming great in the eyes of their neighbors.

• Spiritual magnifying: The expansion of God's reputation through His mighty deeds of deliverance.

4. Theological Implications

The theological implications of gadal in this context center on the limits of human strength and the boundlessness of divine grace. Naomi's focus is entirely on natural, biological growth, which is bound by time, physical capability, and her own barren womb. This highlights the absolute futility of human solutions to her plight. The narrative contrast is sharp: while Naomi sees the impossibility of waiting for sons to grow, God is quietly working to magnify His covenant faithfulness. The ultimate resolution of the story does not come through the natural growth of Naomi's nonexistent sons, but through the supernatural provision of a kinsman-redeemer, pointing forward to the ultimate Redeemer, Jesus Christ, who magnifies God's grace.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:13, Naomi asks, "Would you wait for them till they were grown?" She uses gadal to highlight the absurdity of the young widows staying with her. Even if she were to conceive and bear sons immediately, Ruth and Orpah would have to wait nearly two decades for those boys to reach marriageable age to fulfill the levirate marriage custom. Naomi uses this word to shatter any false hope her daughters-in-law might harbor. The word emphasizes the agonizing passage of time and the biological impossibility of Naomi's family line continuing through her own strength, illustrating her profound sense of emptiness and despair.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 21:8 - "So the child grew and was weaned. And Abraham made a great feast on the same day that Isaac was weaned."

• Exodus 2:10 - "And the child grew, and she brought him to Pharaoh’s daughter, and he became her son."

• 1 Samuel 2:21 - "And the Lord visited Hannah, so that she conceived and bore three sons and two daughters. Meanwhile the child Samuel grew before the Lord."

• 1 Samuel 3:19 - "So Samuel grew, and the Lord was with him and let none of his words fall to the ground."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The term gadal also highlights the thematic contrast of seasons in the book of Ruth. The story begins in famine and ends in harvest. The growth of crops in Bethlehem matches the eventual spiritual and familial restoration of Naomi's household. Furthermore, though Naomi believes her line is cut off because she has no grown sons, God is preparing to make the name of her descendant, David, great (gadal) throughout history, demonstrating that God's plan of redemption is never limited by human clocks or biological barriers.
