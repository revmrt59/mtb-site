LORD - ( Yahweh , Strong's H3068 )

Summary:

The name Yahweh is the personal, covenant name of the God of Israel, denoting His self-existence, eternal presence, and unwavering faithfulness to His people. In Ruth 1:13, Naomi uses this sacred name in her moment of deepest grief to express her conviction that the sovereign, covenant-keeping God has personally directed His hand against her. This highlights the painful tension between God's ultimate sovereignty over life's bitter trials and His enduring covenant relationship with those who suffer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Yahweh is rooted in the historical self-revelation of God to Moses at the burning bush in Exodus 3. When Moses asked for God's name, God responded with the phrase "I AM WHO I AM," linking His personal identity to His eternal, unchanging being. In the ancient Near East, names were not merely labels but disclosures of character, essence, and authority. By revealing the name Yahweh, God moved from being a distant, generic deity (Elohim) to the intimate, covenant-making Lord who binds Himself to His people. In Hebrew manuscripts, this name is written as the four-letter consonant group (the Tetragrammaton) YHWH, considered so sacred by the Jewish people that they refrained from pronouncing it, substituting the title Adonai (Lord) during public reading.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun is derived from the Hebrew root hayah (to be) or havah (to become). It functions as a third-person singular causative form, meaning "He brings into being" or "He who is." The lexical definition encompasses absolute self-existence, independence, and active presence. Unlike pagan deities who depended on nature and human devotion, Yahweh possesses life in Himself, remaining unchangeable in His character, purposes, and promises. It is the proper name of the one true God, denoting Him as the source of all existence, the redeemer of Israel, the keeper of covenant oaths, and the supreme ruler of history.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

As the most frequent name for God in the Old Testament, occurring over 6,800 times, Yahweh is deeply associated with relational and redemptive metaphors. It is used alongside word pictures of a shepherd leading his flock, a strong tower providing safety, a shield defending the helpless, and a husband loving an unfaithful bride. The name is consistently employed in contexts of divine covenant, solemn oaths, sacrificial worship, and personal prayer, emphasizing that God is accessible, communicative, and actively involved in the lives of His people.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of the name Yahweh rests on the unity of God's sovereignty and His relational fidelity. Because He is Yahweh, His decrees are immutable, and His promises are unbreakable. He does not act arbitrarily; rather, His actions are always consistent with His holy and loving character. The name guarantees that even when His providence seems dark or destructive, He remains bound to His people by chesed (covenant loyalty and lovingkindness). His sovereignty ensures that He is in control of all events, while His covenant name guarantees that His ultimate purposes for His people are for good and not for evil.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:13, Naomi exclaims to her daughters-in-law, "it grieves me very much for your sakes that the hand of the LORD has gone out against me!" Naomi does not attribute her intense suffering—the famine, the exile, the death of her husband, and the loss of both sons—to blind fate, bad luck, or the gods of Moab. With stark theological realism, she attributes her affliction directly to Yahweh. By using this specific name, Naomi acknowledges that the very God who promised to bless and protect His covenant people has brought disaster upon her household. This is a cry of covenant confusion: the hand of her own personal, promise-keeping God has turned against her. Yet, even in her bitterness, Naomi’s use of "LORD" shows she has not abandoned her faith; she still appeals to the ultimate authority of the God of Israel, setting the stage for Yahweh to later reverse her emptiness into fullness.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 3:15: "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations."'"

• Psalm 23:1: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

• Ruth 4:13: "So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and when he went in to her, the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son."

• Isaiah 42:8: "I am the LORD, that is My name; and My glory I will not give to another, nor My praise to carved images."

7. Other Relevant Insights

A profound literary and theological arc exists within the Book of Ruth regarding the name Yahweh. In chapter 1, Naomi laments that the hand of Yahweh has gone out against her, leaving her empty. However, the narrative progresses under the quiet, behind-the-scenes providence of the same Yahweh. By the end of the book, in Ruth 4:13, it is Yahweh who grants conception to Ruth, and in Ruth 4:14, the neighborhood women praise Yahweh for not leaving Naomi without a close relative. The very "hand" that Naomi felt was against her in Ruth 1:13 was actually working to weave a lineage that would lead to King David and ultimately to Jesus Christ.
