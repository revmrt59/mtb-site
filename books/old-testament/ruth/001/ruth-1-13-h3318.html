OUT - ( yatsa , Strong's H3318 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:13, this term expresses Naomi’s deep conviction that the sovereign hand of the Lord has actively extended itself in hostile discipline or painful providence against her. Rather than viewing her tragic losses in Moab as matters of mere chance, she recognizes that God's hand has gone forth to empty her, transitioning her from a state of blessing to bitter affliction.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Yatsa is one of the most frequent verbs in the Hebrew Bible, fundamentally denoting movement from an interior space to an exterior one. It paints a concrete physical picture of exiting, emerging, or departing. It is the word used for a person stepping out of a tent, a citizen marching out of a city gate, or the sun rising (going forth) in the morning sky. In military contexts, it specifically describes an army marching out of its stronghold to engage the enemy in battle. This concrete imagery of active, purposeful outward movement provides the backdrop for its theological and metaphorical uses.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of yatsa in the Qal stem is to go out, come forth, or depart. It indicates a physical change of location or a transition from one state to another. When applied to God, His word, or His hand, the verb shifts from a physical description to an anthropomorphic expression of His active power. The going forth of the hand of the Lord signifies the visible, history-shaping manifestation of divine decree leaving His heavenly sanctuary to accomplish His sovereign purposes on earth—whether for salvation or for judgment.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, the verb yatsa is employed in several distinct categories:

• The Exodus: It is the definitive verb used to describe Israel "coming out" of Egypt, depicting God's redemption of His people from the house of bondage.

• Creation and Nature: It describes the sun "going forth" on its daily circuit or water "gushing out" from a rock in the wilderness.

• Military Action: It regularly designates a king or commander "going out" to wage war against an adversary.

• Lineage and Birth: It describes descendants "coming forth" from the body of an ancestor, denoting lineage and family inheritance.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yatsa emphasizes that God is not a passive or distant observer of human history. His sovereign power actively "goes out" into the world. In the covenant history of Israel, the outgoing of God's hand was typically cause for celebration, as it brought deliverance from enemies. However, the covenant also contained warnings that if His people wandered into self-reliance or idolatry, His hand would "go out" against them in disciplinary judgment. Thus, the verb underscores the inescapable reality of divine providence: the same hand that goes out to rescue can also go out to discipline, always working to accomplish His ultimate redemptive purposes.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:13, Naomi laments to her daughters-in-law, "it grieves me very much for your sakes that the hand of the Lord has gone out against me." Here, Naomi uses yatsa as a painful military metaphor. She does not attribute the deaths of her husband and sons to bad luck, famine, or the local gods of Moab. Instead, she rightly recognizes the supreme sovereignty of Yahweh. To her, it feels as though God has mobilized His hand like an opposing army, marching out of heaven to strike her down and strip her of her family. The nuance is one of personal, targeted divine adversity. Though her perspective is clouded by grief and bitterness, her theology remains rooted in the absolute sovereignty of God.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 13:3 - "And Moses said to the people: 'Remember this day in which you went out from Egypt, out of the house of bondage...'"

• Leviticus 10:2 - "So fire went out from the Lord and devoured them, and they died before the Lord."

• Judges 2:15 - "Wherever they went out, the hand of the Lord was against them for calamity, as the Lord had said..."

• Isaiah 55:11 - "So shall My word be that goes forth from My mouth; it shall not return to Me void..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

While Naomi felt that the hand of the Lord had "gone out" only to destroy her happiness, the broader narrative of the book of Ruth reveals a beautiful irony. God’s hand did indeed go out, but not for her ultimate destruction. By bringing Naomi to the absolute end of herself, the outgoing of God’s hand set the stage for a greater "coming forth." Through the redemption of Ruth by Boaz, a descendant would come forth (yatsa) from this family line—King David, and ultimately, Jesus Christ, the Messiah, who would come forth from Bethlehem to redeem His people.
