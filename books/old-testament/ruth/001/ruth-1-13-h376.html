HUSBANDS - ( 'ish , Strong's H376 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, the Hebrew word 'ish primarily means "man" or "husband," denoting a male individual, often in contrast to a woman ('ishshah), or highlighting a husband in covenant relationship with his wife. In the context of Naomi’s desperate plea in Ruth 1:13, the term represents the secure, covenantal, and life-giving relationship of marriage that was essential for a woman's survival, social standing, and family lineage in ancient Israel. Naomi uses the term to highlight the bleak reality of her daughters-in-law remaining bound to her when she has no prospects of providing them with husbands to secure their future.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the culture of the ancient Near East, a woman’s social, economic, and legal protection was almost entirely tied to the men in her life—first her father, and then her husband ('ish). To be without an 'ish was to be highly vulnerable, exposed to extreme poverty, and without a lineage. The word 'ish contrasts with 'adam (generic mankind/humanity). 'Ish refers to an individual man, a person of significance, or specifically a husband. It carries a sense of strength, responsibility, and headship within the family unit.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew term 'ish denotes a male human being, an individual man, or a husband. When used in marital contexts, it functions as the standard term for "husband," emphasizing the legal, social, and relational bond between a man and a wife ('ishshah).

The semantic range of the word includes:

• An individual male person.

• A husband (in relation to a wife).

• A champion or man of valor (when contrasted with weakness or youth).

• "Each" or "every" (used as a distributive pronoun in various contexts).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The foundational relationship between 'ish (man/husband) and 'ishshah (woman/wife) is established in Genesis 2:23-24, where the woman is taken out of man, and they become one flesh. This establishes the Hebrew wordplay and the theological foundation of marriage.

The term is sometimes used to depict strength and moral responsibility, as when David charges Solomon to "show yourself a man" (1 Kings 2:2). As "husband," it represents the provider, protector, and covenant partner. Hosea uses the transition from Baali ("my master") to Ishi ("my husband") to describe Israel's restored, intimate covenant relationship with God (Hosea 2:16), illustrating a shift from fear-based servitude to loving, covenantal intimacy.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of the 'ish as husband underscores God's design for order, protection, and covenant faithfulness within the family. Marriage is not merely a social contract but a covenant pattern reflecting God's own relationship with His people.

In the book of Ruth, the absence of an 'ish (for Naomi, Ruth, and Orpah) signifies more than emotional loneliness; it represents a theological crisis of the cutting off of a family line in Israel. The lack of a husband threatens the inheritance of Elimelech's family land and the continuation of his name, which is a vital part of God's covenant promises to Israel regarding the land.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:13, Naomi asks her widowed daughters-in-law, "Would you stay for them from having husbands?" Naomi is pointing out the biological and temporal impossibility of her bearing more sons, and the absurdity of Orpah and Ruth waiting for hypothetical future sons to grow up.

By using 'ish, Naomi emphasizes the practical, protective, and reproductive necessity of marriage. For Ruth and Orpah, finding an 'ish in their home country of Moab is their only logical path to security (menuhah, rest) and survival. Remaining tied to Naomi means remaining without an 'ish, which in that era was a sentence to lifelong destitution. Naomi's selflessness is highlighted here; she urges them to seek their own security even if it means leaving her completely alone.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The levirate marriage custom (Deuteronomy 25:5-10) underlies Naomi's argument. The brother of a deceased man was expected to marry the widow to carry on the deceased husband's name. Naomi explains that she has no more sons to perform this duty, leaving them with no legal claim to an 'ish within her family line. Ultimately, Boaz steps into this role, not strictly as a levirate brother, but as a kinsman-redeemer (goel), becoming Ruth's 'ish and restoring the family's inheritance and name, demonstrating God's sovereign provision.
