THEM - ( lahen , Strong's H3860 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word lahen in Ruth 1:13 is a composite prepositional phrase meaning "for them" or "on account of them," functioning here as a demonstrative reference to Naomi's hypothetical future sons. In this poignant moment of grief and stark realism, Naomi uses this term to urge her daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth, to abandon any hope of securing levirate marriages through her. The word underscores the biological impossibility and logistical absurdity of them waiting decades for unborn infants to grow into marriageable men, driving home the depth of Naomi's perceived emptiness and the hand of God gone out against her.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word lahen is a compound formed by the preposition le (meaning "to" or "for") and the feminine plural pronominal suffix hen (meaning "them").

• In the patriarchal Ancient Near East, family continuity and security for widows were tightly bound to the institution of levirate marriage. If a married man died without a son, his brother was expected to marry the widow to carry on the deceased's name and preserve the family inheritance.

• Naomi's background is one of utter devastation: she has lost her husband, Elimelech, and both of her sons, Mahlon and Chilion, in the foreign land of Moab. Left with only her Moabite daughters-in-law, she is preparing to return to Bethlehem.

• By using lahen, Naomi points to the hypothetical sons she cannot produce, painting a vivid picture of the sheer futility of waiting for nonexistent heirs.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Lahen translates literally as "for them" or "on account of them."

• In grammatical terms, it consists of the inseparable preposition le prefixed to the third-person feminine plural pronoun hen.

• Though Naomi refers to hypothetical future sons (which typically would use the masculine plural lahem), she employs the feminine plural lahen. This grammatical shift serves either as a colloquialism, a deliberate stylistic choice emphasizing the female-dominated, widow-filled household, or a generic usage referring to the circumstances or the infants as a class.

• The semantic range of the root elements centers on direction, destination, benefit, or reference ("with respect to these things" or "for their sake").

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The word occurs twice in Ruth 1:13, acting as a rhetorical anchor in Naomi’s speech of despair.

• The first usage ("would you wait for them") depicts a posture of tense, prolonged waiting, visualizing two young women waiting in suspended animation for infants to mature into manhood.

• The second usage (often translated in the phrase "would you restrain yourselves for them") evokes a picture of confinement or self-imposed imprisonment, where young women shut themselves off from the possibility of marriage and childbearing, bound to a hopeless hope.

4. Theological Implications

• The use of lahen highlights the limitations of human covenant structures apart from divine intervention. Naomi relies on the strict, logical limits of the levirate law, concluding that her situation is entirely hopeless because her biological clock and lack of a husband make fulfillment impossible.

• It contrasts human despair with the unexpected ways of God's providence. While Naomi sees only the impossibility of "them" (the hypothetical sons), God is already preparing a different redeemer who is not bound by these biological constraints.

• This term illustrates the depth of Naomi's grief. She is so focused on the empty, non-existent "them" that she initially fails to see the value of the loyal daughter-in-law standing right in front of her.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:13, lahen carries an agonizing, rhetorical weight. Naomi is not offering a real option; she is using a reductio ad absurdum argument.

• The context is a bitter road of return. Naomi is attempting to release Orpah and Ruth from any perceived familial obligation. By asking, "Would you wait for them?" she forces them to confront the grim reality of their youth wasting away in perpetual widowhood.

• The feminine pronoun hen ("them" feminine) instead of the masculine hem ("them" masculine) deepens the sense of tragedy. It underscores that in Naomi's mind, everything left in her life is marked by femininity, emptiness, and the absence of male protectors or heirs.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:13 (First occurrence): "would you wait for them till they were grown?"

• Ruth 1:13 (Second occurrence): "Would you restrain yourselves from having husbands [literally: for them]?"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The grammatical mismatch (using the feminine suffix hen for prospective male sons) has puzzled commentators for centuries. Rather than a mere grammatical error, it reflects the colloquial, emotionally charged nature of spoken Hebrew in times of deep distress, where standard grammatical gender agreement can break down under the weight of grief.

• It serves as a literary foil to the rest of the book. The story begins with Naomi fixated on the non-existent "them" (unborn sons), but ends with the women of Bethlehem celebrating a very real son born to Ruth, who becomes the restorer of Naomi's life.
