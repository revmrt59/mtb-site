MUCH - ( meod , Strong's H3966 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word meod expresses the highest degree of intensity, force, or abundance, typically translated as "much," "very," "exceedingly," or "force." In Ruth 1:13, Naomi uses meod to describe the overwhelming, crushing weight of her grief. It does not merely signify a high quantity of sorrow, but a superlative quality of bitterness that has consumed her entire being, emphasizing how her tragedy is almost too heavy to bear.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word meod originally derives from a root associated with force, strength, or abundance.

• Unlike abstract English adverbs, Hebrew conceptualizes "very" or "much" as a tangible, forceful pressure or an overflowing capacity.

• In Deuteronomy 6:5, where Israel is commanded to love the Lord with all their "strength," the Hebrew word used is meod. Here, it functions as a noun representing one's utmost capacity, resources, and force.

• When applied to emotion, meod suggests a force so strong that it pushes a person to their absolute physical and emotional limits.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• As an adverb, meod functions as a superlative, intensifying the verb or adjective it modifies to the utmost degree. It denotes "exceedingly," "greatly," "extremely," or "beyond measure."

• As a noun, it signifies "might," "force," "abundance," or "power."

• In negative contexts, it magnifies sorrow, pain, or distress to a state of complete overwhelming weight. In positive contexts, it describes things that are "very good" or "exceedingly fruitful."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• meod acts as a linguistic magnifying glass, drawing attention to the ultimate expression of whatever state is being described.

• It is used to describe the utter totality of the global flood waters rising "exceedingly" on the earth.

• It describes the extreme physical beauty of individuals, such as Sarah or Absalom, highlighting a quality that surpasses the norm.

• When paired with verbs of weeping, trembling, or grieving, it illustrates an internal storm that completely shatters a person's outer composure, turning internal distress into an inescapable, crushing reality.

4. Theological Implications

• The word meod teaches us about the depth of human emotion recognized and validated by God. Scripture does not sanitize or minimize human suffering. By recording Naomi's "much" grief, the text honors the profound weight of her loss.

• meod serves as a theological marker of God's sovereignty in human calamity. Naomi believes that the hand of the Lord has gone out against her, making her grief "exceedingly" bitter. Her honesty before God, even in her intense bitterness, lays the foundation for a real relationship with a God who meets His people in their deepest depths of pain.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:13, Naomi tells her daughters-in-law, "it grieves me much for your sake." The Hebrew phrase is literally mar-li meod, meaning "it is exceedingly bitter to me."

• The bitter grief Naomi experiences is not selfish; she is devastated "for your sake," recognizing that her empty, childless state has dragged Orpah and Ruth into her own widowhood and bleak future.

• This meod intensity sets up the thematic contrast of the entire book of Ruth. The story begins in the deepest abyss of bitterness (meod) but ends in the overwhelming fullness of God's redeeming covenant love (chesed).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:31 - "Then God saw everything that He had made, and indeed it was very good."

• Genesis 7:19 - "And the waters prevailed exceedingly on the earth, and all the high hills under the whole heaven were covered."

• Deuteronomy 6:5 - "You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength."

• 1 Samuel 31:3 - "The battle became fierce against Saul. The archers hit him, and he was severely wounded by the archers."

• Psalm 46:1 - "God is our refuge and strength, A very present help in trouble."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In Hebrew literary style, doubling the word (meod meod) is the equivalent of adding "extremely" or creating an absolute superlative, as seen when God promises to multiply Abraham "exceedingly."

• In Ruth 1:13, the singular meod carries a heavy, mournful tone that slows down the narrative rhythm, inviting the reader to sit in the dust with Naomi before the redemption narrative begins.
