NO - ( al , Strong's H408 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:13, the Hebrew word al is a deprecatory adverb and particle of prohibition translated as "No." Unlike the absolute, permanent negative lo, al is a subjective, immediate, and emotional negative used to express urgent entreaty, pleading, or protective warning. In this context, Naomi uses it not as an authoritarian command, but as a heartfelt, sorrowful plea, begging her daughters-in-law to abandon their plans of self-sacrifice and return to their homelands for their own well-being.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew particle al is a negative adverb used primarily to express prohibition, deprecation, or entreaty. Unlike the absolute negative lo (which denies a fact or states an absolute, permanent law like "thou shalt not"), al is subjective, immediate, and highly emotional. It is used to urge someone not to do something or to express a passionate plea of "do not let it be so!" In the context of ancient Near Eastern dialogue, it carries an earnest, pleading, or protective tone rather than a cold, legalistic command. It represents a heartfelt intervention to halt a specific course of action.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word al serves as a deprecatory adverb meaning "no," "not," "do not," or "let not." It functions as a subjective negative, indicating a desire, wish, or emotional appeal that an action not take place. Lexically, it is distinct from the objective negative lo. While lo declares an objective state of non-existence or an absolute moral boundaries, al is used with the jussive or imperfect aspect of Hebrew verbs to express a passionate entreaty, a temporary or immediate prohibition, or a heartfelt appeal. In Ruth 1:13, it is used as a standalone interjection ("No, my daughters") to passionately protest and plead against their remaining with Naomi to their own detriment.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Old Testament, al is frequently used in moments of intense emotional appeal or warning.

• Pleading and Entreaty: It is the word used when a person begs another not to act in a certain way, such as when David begs God not to cast him away in His anger.

• Urgently Protective Prohibition: It functions to turn someone away from a self-destructive path. It acts like a protective hand reaching out to stop someone from stepping off a ledge.

• Rhetorical Refusal: In conversational dialogue, a standalone al combined with a vocative ("No, my brother" or "No, my daughters") functions as an emotional barrier, begging the listener to cease their current line of thinking or planned course of action.

4. Theological Implications

Theological use of al in personal relationships and prayers reveals a God who engages with the emotional, subjective realities of human life.

• God’s Protective Warnings: When God commands His people "fear not" (al-tira), He is not issuing a cold, legalistic decree but rather a tender, reassuring entreaty to lay aside anxiety.

• Heartfelt Intercession: The term demonstrates that biblical communication is deeply relational. Intercession often employs al when appealing to God's mercy (e.g., "do not destroy Your people").

• The Sorrow of Selfless Love: In the book of Ruth, Naomi's "no" reflects the painful, self-emptying nature of true love. She refuses to allow her daughters-in-law to ruin their lives out of loyalty to her, showing a maternal, sacrificial love that mirrors God's own covenant-keeping heart (hesed).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:13, Naomi is urging Orpah and Ruth to return to Moab. She paints a bleak picture of her own prospects: she is too old to have a husband, and even if she had sons tonight, would they wait until they were grown? Her sudden exclamation, "No, my daughters" (al benotay), is a turning point of deep emotional weight.

• Maternal Protection: Naomi is refusing to let them sacrifice their youth and future for a barren, bitter widow.

• Naomi’s Despair: She feels that the hand of Yahweh has gone out against her, and she does not want her daughters-in-law to be dragged down into her perceived divine judgment.

• The Contrast of Loyalty: It highlights the contrast between her "no" and the loyalty of the daughters-in-law. Orpah eventually yields to this loving prohibition, whereas Ruth famously rejects Naomi's self-sacrificing "no" with her own covenant commitment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 37:22 - "And Reuben said to them, 'Shed no blood, but cast him into this pit which is in the wilderness, and lay no hand on him'—that he might deliver him out of their hands, and bring him back to his father."

• 2 Samuel 13:12 - "But she answered him, 'No, my brother, do not force me, for no such thing should be done in Israel. Do not do this foolish thing!'"

• Psalm 51:11 - "Do not cast me away from Your presence, And do not take Your Holy Spirit from me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Grammatical Force: In Biblical Hebrew, al is always immediate and subjective, unlike lo, which is objective and permanent. When God says, "You shall not commit murder" in the Ten Commandments, He uses lo because it is an eternal, absolute moral law. When a prophet says, "Do not fear," he uses al because he is addressing an immediate, situational emotion.

• The Pain of "No": Naomi's use of al highlights the tragic ironies in the narrative. She attempts to push them away for their own good, yet Ruth's refusal to heed Naomi’s prohibition becomes the very means by which Naomi is redeemed.
