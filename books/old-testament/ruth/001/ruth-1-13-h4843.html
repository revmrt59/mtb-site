GRIEVES - ( marar , Strong's H4843 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb marar carries the primary meaning of being bitter, weeping bitterly, or experiencing deep emotional distress. In Ruth 1:13, Naomi uses this word to express the profound, stinging sorrow of her empty circumstances, declaring that her life has become intensely bitter because she believes the hand of the Lord has gone out against her, bringing grief not only to herself but also to her beloved daughters-in-law.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root marar is deeply connected to the sensory experience of taste. In the ancient Near East, bitterness was not merely an abstract emotion but a physical, gut-wrenching reality. It represents the sharp, unpalatable contrast to sweetness.

• When used metaphorically, marar describes life circumstances that have turned harsh, toxic, or agonizing. It evokes the image of drinking contaminated, brackish water or consuming wild, poisonous herbs that leave a lingering, painful aftertaste. This sensory background emphasizes that Naomi's grief is not a passing sadness but a heavy, systemic sorrow that colors every aspect of her existence.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb marar expresses the state of being bitter, showing bitterness, or causing distress. In its Qal stem, it means to be bitter or distressed. In the Hiphil stem, it means to make bitter, embitter, or deal bitterly. In the Piel stem, it means to provoke or make bitter.

• In the context of Ruth 1:13, the verb is used impersonally in the Qal perfect (marar-li), meaning "it is exceedingly bitter to me." The word denotes a grief so profound that it saturates the soul, leaving the sufferer raw, vulnerable, and completely spent.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Scripture employs marar to depict several distinct aspects of human suffering:

• Oppression and Servitude: Used to describe the agonizing labor of the Israelites under Egyptian taskmasters, who embittered their lives.

• Anguish and Mourning: Used to represent the deep weeping of those facing national or personal catastrophe.

• Internal Soul Distress: Used to portray a spirit broken by tragedy, where the sweetness of life has been completely stripped away.

4. Theological Implications

• The theological weight of marar rests on the sovereign source of life's circumstances. In the Old Testament, bitterness is rarely viewed as a product of random chance. Sufferers like Naomi recognize that God is sovereign over both blessing and affliction.

• Naomi's bitterness is directly linked to her view of God's providence: "the hand of the Lord has gone out against me." This demonstrates an honest, albeit painful, theology. She does not doubt God's power; rather, she feels the crushing weight of His heavy hand. This honesty in grief is a necessary stage of lament in Scripture, serving as the raw material that God eventually transforms into redemption and joy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:13, Naomi is speaking to Orpah and Ruth, urging them to return to their pagan mother-houses in Moab. Her use of marar ("it grieves me very much for your sakes") reveals a self-sacrificial dimension to her grief.

• She is not merely wallowing in self-pity; she is deeply pained that her own barrenness, widowhood, and perceived divine chastisement have brought hardship and hopeless futures to these younger women. She feels that her presence is a toxic influence, a source of bitterness (marar) that will poison their lives if they remain bound to her. This highlights the tragedy: Naomi's name means "pleasant," but she has become the very embodiment of bitterness.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 1:14: "And they made their lives bitter with hard bondage—in mortar, in brick, and in all manner of service in the field. All their service in which they made them serve was with rigor."

• Ruth 1:20: "But she said to them, 'Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me.'"

• Isaiah 24:9: "They shall not drink wine with a song; Strong drink shall be bitter to those who drink it."

• Zechariah 12:10: "And I will pour on the house of David and on the inhabitants of Jerusalem the Spirit of grace and supplication; then they will look on Me whom they pierced. Yes, they will mourn for Him as one mourns for his only son, and grieve for Him as one grieves for a firstborn."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The structural irony of the Book of Ruth relies heavily on the wordplay between Naomi (noam, meaning pleasantness or sweetness) and her state of bitterness (marar). This linguistic contrast sets up the entire narrative arc of the book.

• The story begins with sweetness turned to bitterness (marar), moves through Naomi renaming herself "Mara" (bitter), and culminates in the ultimate reversal. Through the kinsman-redeemer Boaz, God restores "sweetness" to Naomi's life, showing that the bitterest providences are often the soil in which God plants His most beautiful redemptive purposes.
