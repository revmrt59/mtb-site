RESTRAIN - ( agan , Strong's H5702 )

Summary:

In Naomi's bitter grief and desperate plea to her daughters-in-law in Ruth 1:13, the Hebrew word agan depicts a self-imposed confinement or restriction. Translated as "restrain" or "stay" in the NKJV, it refers to shutting oneself off from the normal hope of marriage, domestic security, and future family. It paints a picture of women voluntarily locking themselves into a perpetual state of waiting and barrenness, anchoring their lives to a dead hope rather than moving forward into the restoration God might provide.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root agan has the primary physical sense of bending, curving, or binding, which develops into the idea of anchoring or shutting oneself in. In broader Semitic languages and Rabbinic Hebrew, the root is intimately tied to the concept of an anchor (hogen) that holds a ship fast in one place, preventing any forward movement.

• This root provides the basis for the modern and Rabbinic Hebrew term agunah—the "chained wife." An agunah is a woman who is legally bound to a marriage that no longer exists in practice (because her husband is missing or refuses to grant a religious divorce), leaving her locked in a state of limbo, unable to move forward or remarry. Naomi uses this very imagery to warn Orpah and Ruth against chaining themselves to her hopeless situation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Agan is a verb meaning to shut oneself in, refrain from, or restrain oneself. It denotes a state of confinement, restriction, or self-imposed waiting that cuts off alternative opportunities.

• The word indicates a deliberate suspension of life’s normal course. In the context of marriage, it means to remain unmarried, deliberately withholding oneself from seeking a husband or building a family.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• As a hapax legomenon (a word that appears only once in the entire Hebrew Bible), the usage of agan in Ruth 1:13 is highly specialized and vivid.

• Naomi uses the word in a rhetorical question: "Would you stay for them from having husbands?" (NKJV). The word picture is one of self-imprisonment. Naomi asks Orpah and Ruth if they are willing to build a wall around their lives, locking themselves inside a house of mourning and barrenness, waiting for sons who are not yet born and may never be.

• It conveys the image of a ship anchored in a harbor that has dried up. Naomi's womb is dry, yet she asks if her daughters-in-law will drop anchor there and refuse to sail out into the open waters of life where they might find new husbands and security.

4. Theological Implications

• The use of agan highlights the tension between human limitations and divine providence. Naomi’s outlook is entirely bounded by her current physical reality. She sees no logical way forward, so she advises her daughters-in-law to avoid "restraining" themselves on her account. She views their potential devotion to her as a form of self-imposed captivity to grief and barrenness.

• Theological narrative contrasts this "restraint" (which leads to stagnation and death) with the risky, forward-moving faith later exhibited by Ruth. While Orpah chooses to return to her homeland to avoid this restraint, Ruth refuses to let Naomi’s bleak outlook determine her destiny. Ruth risks entering a foreign land without guarantees, showing that faith refuses to be anchored to past tragedies or present limitations.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:13, Naomi is trying to persuade Orpah and Ruth to return to Moab. She reasons with them using the levirate marriage customs. Even if she were to marry tonight and bear sons, would they wait (sabbar) until the sons grew up? Would they restrain (agan) themselves from marrying others?

• The grammatical form of te'agenah is a Niphal (passive/reflexive) future/imperfect, emphasizing the voluntary yet deeply restrictive nature of this restraint. Naomi is telling them that staying with her is equivalent to choosing a life of solitary confinement. Her advice is practical, loving, and deeply pessimistic: do not waste your youth, beauty, and childbearing years bound to a family that has been cut off by death.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Because agan (H5702) occurs only once in the Hebrew Bible (Ruth 1:13), there are no direct Hebrew verbal parallel verses. However, the Septuagint (LXX) translates te'agenah using the Greek verb kataschedesesthe (from katecho, meaning to hold back, detain, or restrain). To understand how this concept of restraint and holding back functions elsewhere, we can look at key NKJV verses using this Greek translation equivalent:

• Romans 1:18 - "For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of men, who suppress [Greek: katecho, to hold back or restrain] the truth in unrighteousness."

• 2 Thessalonians 2:6 - "And now you know what is restraining [Greek: katecho], that he may be revealed in his own time."

• Philemon 1:13 - "whom I wished to keep [Greek: katecho, to detain or hold back] with me, that on your behalf he might minister to me in my chains for the gospel."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between the two Hebrew verbs in Ruth 1:13 is instructive. Naomi asks, "Would you wait [sabbar] for them... Would you restrain [agan] yourselves...?" Sabbar implies an eager, hopeful waiting, whereas agan implies a painful, locked-in confinement. Naomi is showing that waiting for her hypothetical sons would not be a hope-filled expectation (sabbar), but a tragic, binding imprisonment (agan).

• In pastoral application, this word serves as a powerful metaphor for spiritual and emotional stagnation. Many believers find themselves "restrained" (agunah) by past griefs, broken dreams, or dead situations, refusing to move forward into the new seasons of fruitfulness that God has prepared for them.
