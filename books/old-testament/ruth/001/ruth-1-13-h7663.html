WAIT - ( sabar , Strong's H7663 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sabar carries the deep sense of waiting with eager expectation, hope, and intense observation. In Ruth 1:13, Naomi uses it to challenge her daughters-in-law, asking if they would realistically restrict their lives and put their futures on hold to wait for hypothetical sons she might bear. It highlights the tension between painful, empty waiting on human circumstances and the active, hopeful waiting that is ultimately redirected toward God's sovereign provision.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete imagery of sabar is rooted in the physical act of looking at something very closely, inspecting, or scanning the horizon. It is not a passive, idle passing of time, but an active, focused observation. Imagine a watchman scanning the horizon for the first sign of dawn, or an assessor carefully examining a structure to determine its condition. This physical action of inspecting and looking intently became the Hebrew foundation for the concept of mental and spiritual expectation. When a person waits (sabar), their spiritual eyes are locked onto the object of their hope, watching for the first sign of fulfillment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb sabar occurs only a handful of times in the Old Testament, representing a highly specific nuance of waiting.

• In the Qal stem, the word means to inspect, examine, or view closely.

• In the Piel stem (which is the intensive form used in Ruth 1:13), the meaning shifts to waiting expectantly, hoping, or tarrying with intense anticipation.

• It denotes a prolonged, focused state of looking forward to a specific outcome, holding onto the promise of what is to come.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The dual nature of sabar—moving from physical inspection to spiritual hope—reveals a profound biblical pattern.

• Physical Inspection: In Nehemiah 2:13 and 2:15, Nehemiah uses this word to describe how he "viewed" or "inspected" the broken-down walls of Jerusalem under the cover of night. He was examining the ruins with analytical, realistic scrutiny.

• Spiritual Expectation: In the Psalms, the word transitions to faith. It is used to describe the eyes of all creation looking expectantly to God for food, and the believer looking expectantly for God’s salvation.

• The Connection: Faith begins by realistically inspecting our brokenness (like Nehemiah inspecting the walls) and then turning our eyes expectantly toward God, waiting for Him to rebuild and restore what is broken.

4. Theological Implications

Waiting is a vital discipline of the spiritual life. Biblical waiting is never a form of lazy resignation; it is an active posture of faith.

• The Object of Waiting: The theological value of sabar is determined entirely by its object. When directed toward God, sabar is a life-giving expression of trust that yields sustenance and salvation.

• The Danger of Misplaced Hope: When this intense expectation is directed toward human solutions, changing circumstances, or hypothetical fortunes, it leads to disappointment and barrenness. Scriptural hope is an anchor because its object is the unchanging character of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:13, Naomi is in the depths of grief, bitterness, and despair. Having lost her husband and both of her sons in Moab, she is returning to Bethlehem empty-handed. When her daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth, insist on coming with her, Naomi presents a highly improbable, hyperbolic scenario. She asks if she were to marry tonight and give birth to sons, would Orpah and Ruth wait (sabar) until those sons grew up to marry them?

• Naomi uses sabar to expose the absurdity of waiting on human possibilities. She is telling them that looking expectantly at her barren life for a future husband is a dead end.

• The word choice highlights Naomi's spiritual blindness at this moment. She can only see the ruined walls of her life; she cannot see the providential hand of God working behind the scenes. While she correctly notes that waiting on her to produce sons is foolish, she fails to realize that waiting expectantly on the God of Israel is never in vain.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Psalm 104:27 - "These all wait for You, That You may give them their food in due season."

• Psalm 119:166 - "LORD, I hope for Your salvation, And I do Your commandments."

• Psalm 145:15 - "The eyes of all look expectantly to You, And You give them their food in due season."

• Isaiah 38:18 - "For Sheol cannot thank You, Death cannot praise You; Those who go down to the pit cannot hope for Your truth."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Contrast with other Hebrew terms: Unlike qavah (which emphasizes the tension and binding strength of hope) or yachal (which emphasizes painful, enduring patience), sabar uniquely emphasizes the visual and analytical focus of the mind. It is a waiting that calculates the reality of the situation and deliberately chooses to look toward a specific source of help.

• Pastoral Application: Naomi's question in Ruth 1:13 challenges every believer to audit their expectations. We must ask ourselves: What are we scanning the horizon for? Are we waiting expectantly on human systems, hypothetical relationships, or career advancements to rescue us? Or are we, like the psalmist, keeping our eyes locked expectantly on the Lord, knowing that He alone gives provision and salvation in due season?
