WEPT - ( bakah , Strong's H1058 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bakah refers to the physical act of weeping, crying, or shedding tears, often accompanied by audible lamentation. In the Old Testament, it expresses deep emotional distress, grief, repentance, or intense relational connection. In the context of Ruth 1:14, bakah captures the profound grief of three destitute widows—Naomi, Orpah, and Ruth—as they face a painful crossroad of parting, illustrating the raw human cost of tragedy before God's redeeming grace unfolds.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root bakah primarily denotes the physical shedding of tears, frequently accompanied by an audible raising of the voice.

• In ancient Near Eastern culture, grief was rarely quiet or private. Weeping was an active, somatic, and communal expression of sorrow.

• The word picture is one of water flowing from the eyes, symbolizing a heart poured out in helplessness, sorrow, or desperate prayer. It represents the boundaries of human strength where words fail and only tears can speak.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Bakah means to weep, lament, cry out, or shed tears in grief or distress.

• It is a common verb occurring over 100 times in the Hebrew Bible, spanning narrative, poetic, and prophetic genres.

• The term encompasses several shades of meaning depending on context: mourning for the dead, crying out in physical or emotional pain, weeping in repentance before God, or crying tears of joy and relief during a poignant reunion.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Audible Lamentation: Frequently paired with the phrase "lifted up their voice," indicating loud, uninhibited crying that could be heard from a distance.

• Ritual Mourning: Used to describe the formal period of weeping following a death, such as the mourning for Jacob or Moses.

• Supplication: Used when a petitioner weeps before the Lord to show sincerity and desperation, such as Hannah praying for a child or Hezekiah pleading for his life.

4. Theological Implications

• Tears are highly valued in the biblical narrative; God is never indifferent to the weeping of His people. He hears their cries and records their tears.

• Weeping is a marker of the fallen human condition, but it is never the final word for those who trust in Yahweh. Scripture presents a trajectory where weeping lasts for a night, but joy comes in the morning.

• In the covenantal framework, weeping often precedes redemption. The brokenness expressed through bakah prepares the human heart to receive and recognize God’s sovereign grace and restoration.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:14, the weeping is shared by Naomi, Orpah, and Ruth. This is the second time they weep aloud in this chapter, underscoring the compounding weight of their grief.

• The weeping in verse 14 marks a point of separation. The shared tears show that Orpah's departure was not due to a lack of love or emotional connection, but rather a pragmatic choice under overwhelming survival pressure.

• This communal weeping highlights the depth of the bond formed between these women through shared tragedy. It serves as a dark backdrop of total despair, against which Ruth's covenant-like commitment ("but Ruth clung to her") shines with extraordinary brilliance.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 45:14 - "Then he fell on his brother Benjamin’s neck and wept, and Benjamin wept on his neck."

• Deuteronomy 34:8 - "And the children of Israel wept for Moses in the plains of Moab thirty days."

• 1 Samuel 1:7 - "So it was, year by year, when she went up to the house of the Lord, that she provoked her; therefore she wept and did not eat."

• Psalm 137:1 - "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept when we remembered Zion."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The noun form baki (weeping) is closely related and often appears alongside the verb.

• The place name "Bochim" is derived from this root, meaning "Weepers," because the Israelites wept aloud there when confronted with their covenant unfaithfulness.

• In the Septuagint, bakah is consistently translated by the Greek verb klaio, which is the same word used of Jesus weeping over Jerusalem and at the tomb of Lazarus.
