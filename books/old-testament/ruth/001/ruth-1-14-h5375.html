LIFTED UP - ( nasa , Strong's H5375 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nasa is a rich, multi-faceted term meaning to lift, carry, bear, or take away. In the context of Ruth 1:14, it depicts Orpah and Ruth raising their voices in a shared, profound lament as they face a painful crossroads. This word moves beyond a mere physical action to convey the deep emotional weight of grief, devotion, and the relational burdens borne by these three widows. Understanding nasa opens a window into the theology of suffering, forgiveness, and the bearing of one another's burdens in covenant community.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete imagery of nasa is deeply rooted in ancient Near Eastern daily life. It pictures someone bending down, gripping a heavy object, and hoisting it upward to carry it on their shoulders. It is the work of a servant loading a beast of burden, a traveler lifting a pack, or a parent picking up a weary child. When applied to the voice, hands, or eyes, the word describes an intentional elevation—a redirection of energy and focus upward, away from the dust of defeat and toward an expressive, active release of what lies heavy on the heart.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of nasa is to lift up, raise, bear, carry, support, or take away. Depending on the grammatical context and the object of the verb, it functions in three primary semantic categories:

• Physical Lifting or Carrying: Lifting an object, carrying a load, or transporting a burden from one place to another.

• Vocal and Emotional Expression: Raising one's voice (nasa qol) in weeping, shouting, singing, or prayer, indicating a swelling of internal emotion that overflows into audible expression.

• Relational and Legal Actions: Bearing guilt or sin, carrying the responsibility of an office, or lifting up the face of another in forgiveness and restoration.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament utilizes nasa in several vivid metaphorical combinations:

• Lifting the Head (nasa rosh): Pictures the restoration of honor, dignity, or legal status to someone who was previously cast down or imprisoned.

• Lifting the Hands (nasa yadayim): Describes the posture of prayer, symbolizing complete dependence, vulnerability, and surrender to God.

• Bearing Sin (nasa avon): A sacrificial term describing the high priest or the sacrificial animal carrying the weight of the community's transgressions away from the camp.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nasa reveals the heart of God as both the Great Sustainer and the Sin-Bearer. God does not merely watch His people from a distance; He bends down to lift them up and carry them through wilderness journeys. Furthermore, nasa is central to the Old Testament understanding of forgiveness. Forgiveness is not the mere ignoring of wrongdoing; it is the literal "lifting off" and carrying away of the heavy, crushing weight of sin from the shoulders of the guilty sinner. This directly prefigures the substitutionary work of the Messiah, who carries our griefs and bears our iniquities.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:14, the phrase "they lifted up their voice, and wept again" marks a pivotal turning point in the narrative. The three widows—Naomi, Orpah, and Ruth—stand on the border of Moab and Judah. The use of nasa here highlights the sheer magnitude of their communal grief. Their sorrow is not a quiet, private sigh; it is a heavy, crushing weight that they must physically "lift" and release through loud, guttural lamentation.

The placement of nasa immediately before the divergent decisions of Orpah and Ruth is highly deliberate. Both women lift their voices and weep, but they choose to bear their emotional and physical burdens differently. Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye, choosing to lay down the burden of covenant loyalty in order to return to her familiar homeland. Ruth, however, clings to Naomi. Ruth chooses to lift and carry the weight of Naomi’s poverty, foreign identity, and empty future, demonstrating a covenant love (hesed) that is willing to bear the heaviest of relational loads.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 21:16: "Then she went and sat down across from him at a distance of about a bowshot; for she said to herself, 'Let me not see the death of the boy.' So she sat opposite him, and lifted up her voice and wept."

• Exodus 34:7: "...keeping mercy for thousands, forgiving iniquity and transgression and sin, by no means clearing the guilty..."

• Psalm 32:1: "Blessed is he whose transgression is forgiven, Whose sin is covered."

• Isaiah 53:4: "Surely He has borne our griefs And carried our sorrows; Yet we esteemed Him stricken, Smitten by God, and afflicted."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the ancient world, weeping was often a communal, loud, and physical event. The idiom "to lift up the voice" (nasa qol) demonstrates that emotions were not compartmentalized. To experience deep sorrow or great joy required the mobilization of the entire body. When pastors and teachers preach this text, they can highlight that God does not expect believers to carry their grief in silence. The scriptures normalize the lifting up of our voices in lament, showing that crying out to God is a holy and necessary step in the journey of faith and transition.
