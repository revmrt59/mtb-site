KISSED - ( nasaq , Strong's H5401 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nasaq primarily means to kiss, serving as a powerful physical expression of affection, respect, or farewell in the ancient Near Eastern world. In the poignant narrative of Ruth 1:14, Orpah’s kiss represents a tender but final goodbye to her mother-in-law, Naomi. While her kiss demonstrates genuine emotional attachment and familial respect, it stands in stark contrast to Ruth's deep, covenantal commitment ("clung to her"), illustrating how emotional affection alone is sometimes insufficient for lifelong, self-sacrificing loyalty.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• In ancient Near Eastern culture, a kiss (nasaq) was a highly significant physical gesture that transcended modern romantic connotations. It was used to establish, confirm, or close relational transactions.

• Family members kissed to show deep kinship, parents kissed children to convey blessing, and friends kissed to express deep emotional bonding.

• A farewell kiss (nasaq) was a standard cultural protocol when parting ways, especially when a separation was expected to be permanent. It was a physical boundary marker, signifying the closing of one chapter of life and the transition into another.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The Hebrew verb nasaq is defined as the physical act of kissing, touching, or joining together. Its primary semantic range centers on expressions of affection, reconciliation, submission, and leave-taking.

• A secondary, homonymous root of nasaq carries the meaning of being armed, equipped, or touching closely (as in armor scale-plates or the touching of wings), but the narrative usage of the term almost exclusively denotes kissing.

• Lexically, the term describes a formal gesture that seals a personal interaction, transferring the inner attitude of the heart into a tangible, observable action. It is an expression of goodwill, respect, and deep relational value.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The term appears 32 times in the Hebrew Bible, reflecting several relational dynamics:

• The Kiss of Reconciliation: Seen in the emotional reunion of Esau and Jacob, where years of hostility dissolve into tears and a physical embrace (Genesis 33:4).

• The Kiss of Covenant Homage: Samuel kisses Saul upon anointing him as king, signifying submission to God's chosen leader (1 Samuel 10:1). Similarly, Psalm 2:12 uses the phrase "Kiss the Son" to demand total allegiance and submission to the Messianic King.

• The Kiss of Parental Blessing and Leave-Taking: Laban complains that Jacob did not allow him to kiss his daughters and grandchildren goodbye (Genesis 31:28). Elisha requests permission to kiss his parents goodbye before embarking on a lifelong prophetic ministry with Elijah (1 Kings 19:20).

4. Theological Implications

• The contrast between Orpah’s kiss (nasaq) and Ruth’s clinging (dabaq) serves as a profound theological object lesson on the nature of discipleship.

• Orpah's kiss is not portrayed as empty or hypocritical; it is a sincere expression of love, grief, and respect. Yet, it represents a limit. Her devotion extends to the point of cultural expectation and genuine emotion, but stops short of covenantal self-sacrifice.

• Scripture often contrasts outward religious expressions with inward covenant loyalty. A kiss can be given while the heart ultimately returns to "her people and her gods" (Ruth 1:15). In contrast, true biblical faith requires a "cleaving" (dabaq)—the same word used for a husband joining his wife in Genesis 2:24—representing an unbreakable bond of loyalty that survives the most painful trials.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:14, the family stands at a literal and spiritual crossroads on the border between Moab and Judah. Naomi has repeatedly urged her daughters-in-law to go back to their maternal homes for their own security.

• The text states, "and Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth clung to her." This sentence is structurally balanced to emphasize the divergence of their paths.

• Orpah’s kiss is her final act of filial duty and affection. By kissing Naomi, she formally releases herself from the family unit of Elimelech and returns to her Moabite heritage. The kiss is sweet, but it is the point of departure. It marks the boundary where emotion ends and covenant commitment begins.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 31:28 - "And you did not allow me to kiss my sons and my daughters. In doing this you have now done foolishly:"

• Genesis 33:4 - "But Esau ran to meet him, and embraced him, and fell on his neck and kissed him, and they wept."

• Genesis 45:15 - "Moreover he kissed all his brothers and wept over them, and after that his brothers talked with him."

• 1 Samuel 10:1 - "Then Samuel took a flask of oil and poured it on his head, and kissed him and said: 'Is it not because the LORD has anointed you commander over His inheritance?'"

• 1 Kings 19:20 - "And he left the oxen and ran after Elijah, and said, 'Please let me kiss my father and my mother, and then I will follow you.' And he said to him, 'Go back again, for what have I done to you?'"

• Psalm 2:12 - "Kiss the Son, lest He be angry, And you perish in the way, When His wrath is kindled but a little. Blessed are all those who put their trust in Him."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In the New Testament, the Greek equivalent phileo and its compound kataphileo (to kiss fervently) mirror the Hebrew usage.

• The ultimate warning regarding the "kiss" is found in the betrayal of Jesus by Judas Iscariot (Luke 22:47-48). Judas uses the traditional sign of discipleship, affection, and loyalty to execute a betrayal. This highlights the reality that while a kiss (nasaq) is a beautiful cultural sign, its spiritual value is entirely dependent on the true loyalty of the heart behind it.
