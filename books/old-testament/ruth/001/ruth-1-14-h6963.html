VOICES - ( qol , Strong's H6963 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word qol refers to a voice, sound, or audible cry. In Ruth 1:14, it represents the collective, deeply felt lament of Naomi, Orpah, and Ruth as they face a heartbreaking crossroads. This vocalized grief highlights the genuine affection and shared suffering of these three widows, serving as the emotional peak of their shared journey before their paths diverge forever.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun qol is an onomatopoeic word that mimics the raw sound of a voice or cry. It emphasizes the physical production of sound that strikes the ear and demands attention. In the ancient Near East, grief was not a quiet, private affair but an active, loud, and communal expression. To "lift up" the voice in weeping was a physical outpouring of the soul's distress, using the voice as an instrument of lamentation. The sound of weeping bound the community together in shared pain, making the invisible weight of sorrow audible to all.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun qol denotes a voice, sound, noise, or call. It encompasses human speech, vocal crying, the sound of instruments, or natural phenomena like thunder. When paired with the Hebrew verb nasa ("to lift up"), it forms an idiom meaning to weep aloud, raise a cry, or shout. In contexts of grief, it represents the physical, audible manifestation of deep inner agony.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Voice of Nature and God: Qol is frequently used to describe thunder, representing the powerful, shaking "voice" of Yahweh echoing over the waters.

• The Voice of Mourning: It is paired with weeping to show overwhelming grief. This is not a silent tear, but a loud, heart-wrenching wail of despair that cannot be contained.

• The Voice of Warning: It is used for the trumpet blast or the watchman’s cry, signaling danger or calling an assembly together.

4. Theological Implications

The audible voice is the primary bridge between the internal soul and the external world. God hears the qol of the suffering, the weeping, and the brokenhearted. In the scriptures, the lifting up of the voice in distress often precedes divine intervention. In the book of Ruth, this shared cry shows the shared humanity and mutual grief of Israelite and Moabite women. It levels the playing field of suffering; tragedy knows no ethnic boundaries, and their shared qol unites them in their brokenness.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:14, the three women "lifted up their voices and wept again." The phrase "and wept again" indicates a recurrence of the intense weeping first mentioned in verse 9. Their voices represent unified grief. The three women are bound by a common loss, having all lost their husbands in Moab. The contrast immediately following the lifting of their voices is stark: Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye (her weeping leads to departure), while Ruth clings to her (her weeping leads to covenant). The "voice" of weeping transitions into the choices of covenantal commitment.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 21:17: "And God heard the voice of the lad. Then the angel of God called to Hagar out of heaven, and said to her, 'What ails you, Hagar? Fear not, for God has heard the voice of the lad where he is.'"

• Genesis 27:38: "And Esau said to his father, 'Have you only one blessing, my father? Bless me—me also, O my father!' And Esau lifted up his voice and wept."

• Judges 2:4: "So it was, when the Angel of the Lord spoke these words to all the children of Israel, that the people lifted up their voice and wept."

• Jeremiah 31:15: "Thus says the Lord: 'A voice was heard in Ramah, lamentation and bitter weeping, Rachel weeping for her children, refusing to be comforted for her children, because they are no more.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The phrase "lifted up their voice" is a common idiomatic expression in Hebrew narrative to denote a sudden, intense burst of emotion, usually grief, but occasionally joy or relief. In the Greek Septuagint (LXX), qol in Ruth 1:14 is translated by phone, which is the same Greek word used for voice, sound, or language in the New Testament.
