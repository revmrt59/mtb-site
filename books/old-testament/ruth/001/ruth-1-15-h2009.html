LOOK - ( hinneh , Strong's H2009 )

Summary:

The Hebrew particle hinneh is a demonstrative interjection used to draw immediate, vivid attention to a fact, sight, or situation. Often translated as "look," "behold," or "see," it serves to arrest the listener’s attention, shifting the focus to a crucial turning point or an undeniable reality. In narrative literature, it functions as a literary spotlight, signaling that what follows is of immense significance to the speaker and the audience. In Ruth 1:15, Naomi uses this word to urge Ruth to look at Orpah's departure, presenting it as a logical and expected path, thereby testing Ruth's resolve and setting the stage for one of the most magnificent confessions of faith in the Old Testament.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word hinneh is a demonstrative particle that functions like a pointing finger. It is derived from the shorter demonstrative particle hen and carries a sense of immediacy, proximity, and visual clarity. It is used to present a scene as if it is unfolding right before the eyes of the listener. Rather than merely conveying information, hinneh invites the listener to mentally or physically "stand and look" at a specific reality. It creates a sense of dramatic presence, bringing the past or a present observation into sharp, immediate focus.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• hinneh is an interjection meaning "behold," "look," "see," or "lo." It is used to introduce a new subject, a sudden turn of events, or a critical truth. It serves several grammatical and rhetorical functions:

• Drawing attention to a physical presence or an approaching person.

• Introducing an important statement of fact or a sudden realization.

• Serving as an attention-getter in dialogue, signaling that the speaker is about to make a point of vital importance.

• Presenting an immediate condition or consequence that requires a response.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, hinneh is used over a thousand times to create vivid narrative pictures. It is frequently used when a character makes a sudden discovery, such as Abraham looking up and seeing the ram caught in the thicket (Genesis 22:13). It is also the standard response of a servant answering a master's call: hineni, "Here I am" (literally, "Behold me"). This word picture represents readiness, alertness, and total availability. When used in dialogue, hinneh demands that the listener stop processing their own thoughts and focus entirely on the reality being pointed out by the speaker.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, hinneh often precedes a major divine revelation, a covenantal promise, or a significant shift in redemptive history. When God says hinneh, He is calling humanity to perceive His active presence, His judgment, or His grace. In the context of human decisions, it highlights the moment of testing or choosing. It underscores human responsibility by placing a stark, undeniable reality directly in front of an individual, forcing a decision.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:15, Naomi uses hinneh ("Look") as she pleads with Ruth to return to Moab. The context is heavy with grief, hopelessness, and cultural pragmatism. Naomi's husband and sons are dead; she is returning to Bethlehem empty-handed. Orpah has just made the painful but pragmatic choice to return to her homeland. Naomi points to the departing figure of Orpah and says, "Look, your sister-in-law has gone back."

By using hinneh, Naomi is not merely giving information; she is forcing Ruth to confront a visible, tangible example. She is saying, "Behold the practical path. See what is normal, expected, and safe. Your sister-in-law has made the sensible choice." This "look" serves as a severe mercy and a profound test of Ruth's devotion. It sets up a contrast: will Ruth look at the visible, easy path of return, or will she look with eyes of faith toward the unseen God of Israel? Ruth's response to this "look" is her famous declaration of loyalty, proving that her faith transcended mere sight.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

To understand the breadth of hinneh, we examine its usage in other key passages:

• Genesis 22:13: "Then Abraham lifted up his eyes and looked, and there behind him was a ram caught in a thicket by its horns."

• Isaiah 6:8: "Also I heard the voice of the Lord, saying: 'Whom shall I send, and who will go for Us?' Then I said, 'Here am I! Send me.'"

• Genesis 1:31: "Then God saw everything that He had made, and indeed, it was very good."

• Isaiah 7:14: "Therefore the Lord Himself will give you a sign: Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a Son, and shall call His name Immanuel."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (LXX), the Greek translation of the Old Testament, hinneh is almost always translated as idou or ide, which are imperative forms of the verb "to see" (often translated as "behold" or "look" in the New Testament). This Greek equivalent carries the exact same force of dramatic presentation and vivid appeal. It is the same word used by John the Baptist in John 1:29: "Behold! The Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!"
