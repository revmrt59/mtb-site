AFTER - ( achar , Strong's H310 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word achar (most frequently translated as "after" or "behind") is a spatial and temporal preposition that denotes direction, succession, or physical and spiritual pursuit. In Ruth 1:15, Naomi uses achar to urge Ruth to follow her sister-in-law Orpah back to her pagan gods and people. Ruth’s refusal to go "after" Orpah—and her decision instead to cling to Naomi—transforms this basic word of spatial direction into a profound marker of covenant loyalty, discipleship, and genuine conversion to the true God.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical root of achar refers to the backside, the rear, or what comes behind in space or time. In ancient Semitic thought, spatial concepts heavily influenced relational and spiritual realities. To walk "after" someone was to place oneself behind them, stepping directly into their footsteps. It is the visual posture of a follower, a servant, a disciple, or a worshiper. To go achar someone is to yield the lead to them, allowing their direction, destination, and pace to dictate your own.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

As a preposition, adverb, or conjunction, achar possesses several primary definitions:

• Spatially: Behind, at the back of, or following behind (e.g., walking behind someone).

• Temporally: After, afterwards, or in the future.

• Relationally/Metaphorically: In pursuit of, in imitation of, or loyal to.

• When paired with verbs of motion like "to go" (halak) or "to return" (shub), the word carries the idiomatic weight of allegiance. To "go after" a leader means to follow them with loyalty. To "go after" other gods is the standard Old Testament idiom for spiritual adultery and idolatry.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Shepherd and the Flock: Sheep naturally walk achar (behind) their shepherd, relying on his guidance and protection.

• The Soldier and the Commander: Troops march achar their leader into battle, indicating submission to authority and shared destiny.

• The Disciple and the Master: A student walks achar their teacher, physically following them to absorb their lifestyle, teachings, and wisdom.

• The Idolater and the Idol: Israel is frequently warned not to go achar (after) the gods of the surrounding nations, which represents a deliberate turning away from Yahweh to pursue false lovers.

### 4. Theological Implications

The word achar lies at the very heart of the biblical concept of discipleship and covenant fidelity. Following God is never represented as an abstract, static mental agreement; it is an active, kinetic movement of walking achar Him.

• It requires leaving something behind. You cannot walk achar Yahweh while remaining in the same place or pursuing a different path.

• It demands exclusive loyalty. In the ancient world, walking achar a deity meant adopting their culture, laws, and sacrificial system. In Ruth 1:15, Naomi presents Ruth with a choice of spiritual direction: return achar her pagan sister-in-law and the Moabite gods, or go achar Naomi and the God of Israel.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:15, Naomi uses achar as a test of Ruth's devotion. Orpah has just kissed Naomi goodbye and turned back toward Moab. Naomi points to Orpah's departing figure and tells Ruth, "Look, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods; return after your sister-in-law."

• The phrase "return after" (shub achar) is a direct invitation to return to Moabite idolatry. Naomi is presenting the easy, culturally expected path. To go achar Orpah meant returning to Chemosh, comfort, family, and security.

• Ruth’s famous response in verses 16–17 is a radical refusal to go achar Orpah. Instead, Ruth chooses to go achar Naomi. This choice is not merely sentimental; it is a full-scale conversion. By refusing to go back achar Orpah and her gods, Ruth pledges to walk achar Naomi’s God ("your God [shall be] my God").

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 22:13 - "Then Abraham lifted his eyes and looked, and there behind [achar] him was a ram caught in a thicket by its horns." (A clear physical, spatial use of the word).

• Deuteronomy 13:4 - "You shall walk after [achar] the LORD your God and fear Him, and keep His commandments and obey His voice..." (The classic covenantal command of spiritual pursuit and obedience).

• 1 Kings 19:20 - "And he left the oxen and ran after [achar] Elijah, and said, 'Please let me kiss my father and my mother, and then I will follow you.'" (A powerful picture of prophetic discipleship).

• Jeremiah 2:2 - "Go and cry in the hearing of Jerusalem, saying, 'Thus says the LORD: "I remember you, the kindness of your youth, the love of your betrothal, when you went after [achar] Me in the wilderness, in a land not sown."'" (God recalling Israel’s early, trusting devotion).

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A beautiful linguistic connection exists between the preposition achar and the Hebrew noun acharit, which means "future," "latter end," or "posterity." What we choose to follow after (achar) ultimately determines our acharit (our final end or legacy). Ruth chose to follow after a poor widow and a seemingly empty future in Israel rather than follow after the pagan comfort of Moab. Because she walked achar Naomi and Yahweh, God gave her a glorious acharit—she became the ancestor of King David and ultimately of Jesus Christ.
