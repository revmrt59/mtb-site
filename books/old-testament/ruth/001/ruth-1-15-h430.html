GODS - ( elohim , Strong's H430 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word elohim is a plural noun that can refer to the one true God of Israel, pagan deities, or supernatural beings. In Ruth 1:15, it refers specifically to the false, national gods of Moab, particularly Chemosh. Naomi uses this term to highlight the stark reality of Orpah's departure—she did not merely change geographic locations; she returned to her ancestral, pagan worship. This sets up a profound theological contrast with Ruth's subsequent confession of faith in the true God of Israel.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word elohim is grammatically plural, indicated by the masculine plural suffix -im. When referring to the one true God of Israel, it functions as a "plural of majesty" or "plural of intensity," representing the fullness of God's power and authority, and it is paired with singular verbs. However, when it refers to foreign deities, it functions as a literal plural, taking plural verbs or plural pronominal suffixes.

In the ancient Near East, nations were defined by their territorial deities. A person's identity, citizenship, and security were inextricably linked to the god of their land. For the Moabites, the chief deity was Chemosh. To return to Moab was to return to the sphere of influence and worship of Moab's national elohim.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The root meaning of elohim denotes strength, power, preeminence, or supreme authority. The semantic range of the word includes:

• The one true God, Yahweh, the Creator and Ruler of all.

• Generic foreign gods, idols, or pagan deities.

• Individual pagan gods or goddesses (such as Chemosh or Ashtoreth).

• Angels or heavenly beings inhabiting the divine realm.

• Human rulers, judges, or kings acting with delegated divine authority.

In Ruth 1:15, the word appears with a third-person feminine singular suffix (eloheha, "her gods"), referring specifically to the plural pantheon of Moab, headed by Chemosh.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture uses elohim to expose the vanity of pagan worship. When used of foreign deities, the term represents a tragic counterfeit of the true God. These "gods" are localized, limited, and dependent on human hands to fashion them and human strength to carry them. Unlike Yahweh, who created the heavens and the earth, the elohim of the nations are powerless idols that can neither speak, see, nor deliver their worshippers in times of distress.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of elohim in the Old Testament rests on the tension between monotheism and the polytheism of the surrounding nations. While the Bible recognizes that other nations serve various elohim, it consistently denounces these gods as non-entities, demons, or worthless creations.

Orpah’s return to her elohim emphasizes the gravity of her decision. Her choice was not merely a cultural or familial relocation; it was a spiritual apostasy. She chose the false, dead elohim of Moab over the living Elohim of Israel. This highlights the spiritual reality that neutrality is impossible when confronting the covenant God of Israel; one either moves toward Him in faith or retreats to the idols of this world.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:15, Naomi uses Orpah's departure to test Ruth’s resolve, saying, "Look, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods; return after your sister-in-law." Naomi presents the path of least resistance—returning to the familiar cultural and religious systems of Moab.

This sets up one of the most magnificent contrasts in Scripture. In the next verse (Ruth 1:16), Ruth answers Naomi's challenge by employing the same word, elohim, to declare her allegiance: "Your people shall be my people, and your God (elohim) my God (elohim)."

While Orpah returned to her plural, powerless elohim, Ruth renounced her heritage to claim the singular, true Elohim of Israel as her own. Ruth's confession demonstrates that she was not merely loyal to Naomi, but had experienced a genuine spiritual conversion, abandoning the territorial gods of her birth for the global, supreme Creator.

• 1 Kings 11:33 - "...because they have forsaken Me, and worshiped Ashtoreth the goddess of the Sidonians, Chemosh the god of the Moabites, and Milcom the god of the people of Ammon, and have not walked in My ways to do what is right in My eyes and keep My statutes and My judgments, as did his father David."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

Naomi’s exhortation to Ruth to return to her pagan "gods" reveals the depth of Naomi's spiritual despondency. Bitter and grieving, Naomi had lost sight of Israel’s missionary calling to be a light to the nations. Instead of inviting the Moabite women into the worship of Yahweh, she actively encouraged them to return to idolatry. This highlights the grace of God in Ruth’s conversion: Ruth’s faith did not catch fire because of Naomi's evangelistic zeal, but through the sovereign drawing of God, who overcomes even the failures of His covenant people.
