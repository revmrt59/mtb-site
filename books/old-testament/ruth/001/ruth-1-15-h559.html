SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is the primary Old Testament term for speech, communication, and verbal expression. In Ruth 1:15, Naomi uses this word to issue a poignant, realistic challenge to Ruth, urging her to follow her sister-in-law Orpah back to her people and her pagan gods. Far from a detached statement, this "saying" represents a moment of profound emotional and spiritual testing, forcing Ruth to consciously choose between the security of her homeland and the uncertain path of covenant loyalty to Yahweh and Naomi.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root 'amar is one of the most frequently used verbs in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 5,000 times. In ancient Semitic culture, words were not viewed as mere empty sounds or abstract symbols; they were considered extensions of the person speaking. To "say" something was to project one's inner thoughts, desires, and soul into the concrete world. When a person spoke, they initiated a relational exchange or set a reality in motion. In the narrative framework of the Old Testament, dialogue drives the plot and reveals the true condition of the human heart.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb means to say, speak, utter, command, declare, or think. In its most basic Qal stem, it represents the straightforward act of speaking to someone, typically introducing direct discourse. The semantic range includes:

• To express verbally (to speak aloud to another).

• To intend or design (to "say" in one's heart, meaning to think or plan).

• To command or demand (when spoken by a superior, such as a king or God).

• To call or name (designating a title or status).

In the context of Ruth 1:15, the word functions as a personal address of direct persuasion and counsel, wherein Naomi lays out the stark, humanly logical choice before Ruth.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb 'amar is used in several distinct theological and narrative word pictures across the Old Testament:

• The Creative Word: God speaks reality into existence. When God "says" ('amar), chaotic darkness yields to order and life.

• The Internal Voice: When a character "says in his heart," it denotes their private motives, fears, or plans that have not yet been spoken aloud to others.

• The Boundary-Setting Appeal: In relational dialogues, 'amar is used to establish boundaries, test loyalty, or offer releases from obligations, as seen in legal or familial transactions.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of 'amar in the book of Ruth lies in the contrast between human speech and divine providence. Throughout the first chapter, Naomi's speech is heavy with grief, bitterness, and natural pragmatism. When she speaks, she vocalizes a worldview of scarcity and loss, believing the hand of the Lord has gone out against her. Her words in verse 15 serve as a spiritual crucible. By telling Ruth to return to her "gods," Naomi's speech forces a defining moment. It demonstrates that God often uses the hard, realistic, and sometimes discouraging words of others to test, refine, and ultimately reveal the depth of our faith and devotion.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:15, Naomi's statement ("Look, your sister-in-law has gone back...") is her third and final attempt to persuade her daughters-in-law to return to Moab. Her speech is marked by absolute honesty and maternal selflessness; she has no husband, no sons, and no security to offer them. By "saying" this, Naomi removes all social pressure, guilt, or legal obligation from Ruth. The nuance of 'amar here is one of loving dismissal and intense testing. Naomi's words act as a mirror, forcing Ruth to look past superficial affection and decide if she is willing to embrace the God of Israel even when the human outlook is entirely bleak.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

It is helpful to contrast 'amar with the other primary Hebrew verb for speaking, dabar. While dabar generally emphasizes the substance, word, or matter of what is spoken (focusing on the objective message or word itself), 'amar focuses on the act of saying, the personal address, and the direct quote. Naomi’s use of 'amar highlights the deeply personal, face-to-face, and relational nature of her appeal to Ruth. She is not merely delivering a message; she is engaging in a direct soul-to-soul confrontation.
