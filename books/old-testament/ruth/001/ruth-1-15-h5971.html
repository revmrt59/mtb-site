PEOPLE - ( 'am , Strong's H5971 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'am represents more than a mere demographic or political group; it designates a family, a community bound by blood, covenant, and shared destiny. In the ancient Near East, one's identity was inextricably linked to their 'am and their national deity. In Ruth 1:15, Naomi uses this term to urge Ruth to return to her Moabite kinship and gods, setting the stage for Ruth’s historic, covenantal decision to renounce her biological heritage and adopt Naomi's 'am—and by extension, Yahweh—as her own.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun 'am is built upon a root that denotes association, companionship, and close proximity. It is etymologically related to the Hebrew preposition 'im, which means "with." The word picture behind 'am is not that of a cold political boundary or a census list, but of a group of people standing "with" one another—sharing life, history, table, and altar. It carries a warm, familial connotation, emphasizing a collective identity rooted in shared ancestry and mutual obligation. To belong to an 'am is to have a home, a safety net, and a defined place in the cosmic order.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term 'am refers to a nation, folk, people, or kindred. It designates a collective group of human beings united by common origin, language, territory, or covenant. While the Hebrew language has other words for nation—such as goy, which emphasizes the political, territorial, or institutional structure of a state—'am highlights the relational and consanguineous ties of the group. The semantic range also includes "kinfolk" or "relatives," referring to local clan members or paternal relatives.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, 'am appears over 1,800 times. It is used in several distinct ways:

• Family and Kinship: It can denote close blood relatives, as seen in formulas like "gathered to his people" upon death (Genesis 25:8).

• The Covenant Nation: It is the primary word used to describe Israel as God’s chosen family. God repeatedly calls Israel "My people" ('ammi), a term of endearment and exclusive possession.

• Common Soldiers or Citizens: It can refer to the general populace or the army in distinction from their leaders, kings, or priests (1 Kings 12:16).

The primary word picture is that of a flock under a shepherd, representing safety, guidance, and belonging under a sovereign head.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'am is central to the covenant formula: "I will be their God, and they shall be My people." This relationship is not based on Israel's size or strength, but on God's sovereign, electing love. The term highlights the corporate nature of biblical faith; God does not merely save isolated individuals, but He gathers a 'am to Himself. Furthermore, the book of Ruth demonstrates that membership in God’s 'am is not strictly locked by genetics. Through faith and covenant loyalty, a foreigner from an enemy nation (Moab) can be incorporated into the 'am of Yahweh, ultimately placing her in the messianic lineage of David and Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:15, Naomi uses 'am in tandem with elohim (gods): "Look, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods." Naomi is pointing out that Orpah’s return to her ethnic family is also a return to her religious system; in Moab, national identity and spiritual worship were inseparable. By urging Ruth to do the same, Naomi is testing Ruth’s resolve. Ruth’s response in verse 16 uses the exact same vocabulary to declare her conversion: "Your people ('am) shall be my people ('am), and your God (elohim), my God (elohim)." Ruth recognizes that to receive Naomi's God, she must also embrace Naomi's community, choosing to stand "with" Israel in place of Moab.

• Joel 2:27 - "Then you shall know that I am in the midst of Israel: I am the LORD your God and there is no other. My people shall never be put to shame."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), 'am is predominantly translated using the word laos (which denotes a chosen, specific people group, often used for Israel), whereas goy is translated as ethnos (from which we get "ethnic," referring to the nations or Gentiles). This distinction is carried into the New Testament, where the early church is described in 1 Peter 2:9-10 as a "holy nation" and the "people of God" (laos Theou). Ruth’s transition from a foreign nation to the 'am of God serves as a beautiful Old Testament type of the Gentile inclusion into the household of faith.
