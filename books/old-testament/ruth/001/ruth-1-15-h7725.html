RETURN - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub means to turn back, return, or go back to a starting point. It is a foundational Old Testament word denoting physical movement, spiritual repentance, and relational restoration. In the book of Ruth, shub functions as a structural and theological engine, driving the narrative of geographic return to the Promised Land and spiritual realignment with Yahweh. In Ruth 1:15, Naomi uses this term to urge Ruth to go back to her Moabite origin, setting up a critical crisis of loyalty, faith, and identity.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb shub is the twelfth most common verb in the Old Testament, occurring over 1,000 times. At its basic physical level, it describes a 180-degree pivot—an active turn to go back to a place, person, or previous state. The background of the word is deeply rooted in covenantal life, geography, and relationships. In agrarian and nomadic Israel, to "return" meant heading back to one's ancestral home, family land, or city of safety. Spiritually, when Israel wandered away from God's covenant, the prophets used shub as the primary call to "return" to Yahweh. It conveys that repentance is not a mere feeling of sorrow, but a physical and relational change of direction.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of shub depends on its grammatical usage:

• In its basic form (Qal stem), it means to turn back, return, go back, or turn away. It denotes physical movement back to a point of origin, such as returning to a land or home.

• In its causative form (Hiphil stem), it means to bring back, restore, pay back, or answer.

• Spiritually, the word describes a change of mind, allegiance, and direction—turning away from evil and turning toward God.

In Ruth 1:15, the verb is used in the Qal imperative form (shubi), functioning as a direct, urgent command from Naomi to Ruth to go back to her biological and religious roots in Moab.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Physical U-Turn: A traveler who realizes they are on the wrong road and turns around to head back to safety.

• The Relational Return: A wayward child returning home to a father, or an unfaithful spouse returning to their original partner.

• The Restoration of Life: The term is used to describe physical refreshment, as in "bringing back" one's breath, soul, or strength (Lamentations 1:11).

• The Book of Ruth Context: The verb occurs 12 times in chapter 1 alone. This repetition creates a powerful wordplay where characters are physically returning to Bethlehem, but also spiritually deciding whom they will "return" to as their ultimate Lord.

4. Theological Implications

• Repentance as Relational Realignment: Shub demonstrates that biblical repentance is highly active. It is not defined by passive regret, but by a concrete change of direction and allegiance.

• The Test of Faith: Naomi's command to shub serves as a test of Ruth's commitment. True covenant faith is demonstrated when it is tested by the opportunity to return to one's comfortable, pagan past.

• Sovereign Grace in Restoration: Even after tragedy, backsliding, and death in a foreign land (Moab), God allows a "return" to the place of blessing (Bethlehem, "the House of Bread"). God's grace makes restoration possible for those who turn back to Him.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:15, Naomi uses shub twice in close succession: "Look, your sister-in-law has gone back (shabah) to her people and to her gods; return (shubi) after your sister-in-law."

Naomi, speaking from a place of grief and bitterness, believes Yahweh has emptied her out. She tries to release her Moabite daughters-in-law from any obligation to her. Orpah chooses to return (shub) to Moab and her gods (such as Chemosh). Naomi uses Orpah's departure to pressure Ruth to do the same.

The theological tension here is profound. Naomi urges Ruth to "return" to false gods, but Ruth radically redefines the word. Instead of returning to Moab, Ruth binds herself to Naomi and Naomi's God. Ruth's physical journey to Bethlehem is a spiritual "return" to a God she has only recently come to know, proving that she is turning her back on her pagan past to seek refuge under the wings of the God of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:19: "In the sweat of your face you shall eat bread till you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; for dust you are, and to dust you shall return."

• Isaiah 55:7: "Let the wicked forsake his way, and the unrighteous man his thoughts; let him return to the Lord, and He will have mercy on him; and to our God, for He will abundantly pardon."

• Malachi 3:7: "Yet from the days of your fathers you have gone away from My ordinances and have not kept them. Return to Me, and I will return to you," says the Lord of hosts.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Contrast of Two Paths: Ruth 1 presents a stark contrast between two kinds of "returning." Orpah returns (shub) to her past (Moab and its idols), representing the easy path of returning to worldly comfort. Ruth refuses to return to her past, choosing instead to "return" with Naomi to an unknown future under the covenant of Yahweh.

• The Eschatological Return: The prophets frequently used shub to promise that Israel would physically return from Babylonian exile. This physical return was always intended to be accompanied by a spiritual return of the heart to God.
