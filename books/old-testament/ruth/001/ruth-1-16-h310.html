AFTER - ( 'acharey / 'achar , Strong's H310 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:16, the Hebrew preposition 'achar (occurring as me-'acharayikh, "from after you") represents far more than physical spatial proximity or walking order. While its root denotes the hind part, behind, or following behind someone, in this intimate covenantal context, it signifies a deep, lifelong commitment of discipleship, allegiance, and loyalty. Ruth’s refusal to turn back from "following after" Naomi is a total alignment of her path, her people, and her destiny with Naomi's, serving as a powerful Old Testament picture of true conversion and discipleship where one relinquishes their past to walk behind and with another into an unknown future.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete root of 'achar relates to the back or hind parts of an object or person. In ancient Near Eastern thought, spatial orientation and time were often conceptualized differently than today; the past was viewed as "in front of" because it is known and seen, while the future was "behind" ('achar) because it is unseen. Walking "after" someone meant placing oneself behind them, yielding leadership, protection, and direction to the one in front. It pictures a traveler walking in the dusty footsteps of a guide, trusting their path completely, or a disciple walking behind their master to absorb their teaching and lifestyle.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word functions primarily as a preposition, adverb, or conjunction. Lexically, it signifies "behind," "after," "following," or "afterwards." It denotes posterior position (physically standing behind someone) or chronological succession (occurring after an event). When used in conjunction with verbs of movement, such as "to walk" (halak) or "to pursue" (radap), it describes the act of following or aligning one's trajectory with another. In relational contexts, it transitions from a spatial description to an ethical and covenantal commitment of loyalty, devotion, and submission.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Following: Used frequently to describe soldiers marching behind their commander, or sheep following their shepherd. The leader sets the pace, chooses the path, and faces the dangers first.

• Spiritual Allegiance (Walking After God): The Old Testament frequently uses "walking after" (halak 'acharey) to describe religious devotion. This can be positive, as in walking after Yahweh in obedience, or highly negative, as in walking after false gods or idols. To walk after a god means to adopt their moral character, submit to their laws, and trust in their protection.

• Pursuit and Devotion: It implies an active, intense commitment where the follower refuses to allow any distance to grow between themselves and the one they follow.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of "following after" in the Old Testament lays the critical foundation for the New Testament doctrine of discipleship (akoloutheo). To follow after someone requires a deliberate denial of self-determination. It means going where you might not otherwise choose to go, because the leader has chosen that path. It involves trust, as the follower cannot see what lies ahead but relies on the wisdom and sight of the one leading. Furthermore, choosing to follow one person or God necessitates turning away from (and leaving "behind") other allegiances.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:16, Naomi has urged her daughters-in-law to return to their maternal homes and their local gods. Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye, but Ruth clings to her. When Ruth begs Naomi not to force her to "turn back from following after you," she uses the Hebrew construction me-'acharayikh (literally, "from after you"). This use of 'achar elevates the physical act of walking behind Naomi on the road to Bethlehem into a solemn covenantal vow. Ruth is not merely acting as a travel companion; she is redefining her entire identity. By refusing to turn back from "following after" Naomi, Ruth consciously leaves behind her Moabite heritage, her security, and her pagan gods. She commits to walk behind Naomi, accepting Naomi’s people as her people, and Naomi’s God (Yahweh) as her God. The physical direction of her steps reflects the spiritual direction of her soul.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The stark contrast between Orpah and Ruth is highlighted by this word. Orpah turned back from following Naomi (returning to her people and her gods), whereas Ruth chose to stay "after" her. This illustrates that true faith and conversion are marked by perseverance. Following after is not an emotional impulse but a resolute orientation of the will. In the economy of God's grace, Ruth’s decision to follow after a destitute widow led her into the lineage of King David, and ultimately, Jesus Christ, who would invite His disciples with the very same call: "Follow Me."
