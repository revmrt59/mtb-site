NOT - ( 'al, Strong's H0408 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle 'al functions as a subjective, deprecatory, or emotional negation, translating to "do not," "let not," or "no." Unlike the objective, permanent negation of lo', which represents absolute prohibitions, 'al is used to express a passionate appeal, an urgent plea of the will, or a warning to halt an action already in progress. In Ruth 1:16, when Ruth begs Naomi, "Entreat me not to leave you," this particle conveys her deep, emotional resolve and urgent plea to halt Naomi’s attempts to turn her back. It is not merely a statement of fact, but a cry of the heart expressing absolute, unyielding covenant commitment.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The particle 'al serves as a verbal boundary marker born of immediate, personal circumstances. It is a word of immediate relation, indicating a turning away or a desire to prevent an action from occurring. While standard negation describes a cold, hard fact of what is not, 'al carries the warm, breathing pulse of human and divine emotion. It is the language of petition, warning, or immediate command. In the ancient Near Eastern context, where relational dynamics and verbal appeals carried immense weight, using this specific negation signaled that the speaker was deeply invested in the outcome, throwing their own desire and will into the path of an ongoing action to stop it.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word 'al is a negative adverb or particle used primarily with the jussive or cohortative verb forms to express a deprecatory wish, a prohibition, or an urgent plea. It translates directly as "do not," "let not," or "may it not be." While its counterpart lo' indicates absolute, unconditional negation (such as "You shall not steal"), 'al denotes situational, subjective negation. It is used to:

• dissuade someone from a course of action they are contemplating or pursuing.

• plead for the cessation of an ongoing action.

• express a strong personal desire or emotional appeal that a certain outcome be avoided.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The usage of 'al paints several distinct word pictures across the Old Testament:

• The Shield of Comfort: In times of distress, God frequently uses 'al in the phrase "do not fear." It is not an abstract, eternal law, but a tender, timely intervention of the Shepherd telling His sheep to halt their immediate panic.

• The Urgent Interception: It acts as a verbal barrier thrown up to stop a disaster in progress, such as pleading with someone not to commit a crime or shed innocent blood.

• The Tearful Supplication: It is the voice of the powerless pleading with the powerful, begging for mercy, or asking that a harsh judgment not be executed.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'al reveals the deeply relational and dialogical nature of Biblical faith. God does not interact with humanity merely through cold, static decrees, but through situational commands that address our immediate emotional states (such as anxiety, fear, and grief). Similarly, human beings can appeal to God and one another with urgent, heartfelt petitions.

In the context of covenant loyalty (hesed), this particle demonstrates that covenant commitment is an active, passionate choice of the will. Ruth's use of 'al shows that faith does not quietly acquiesce to difficult circumstances or discouragement. Instead, true faith actively resists the pressure to turn back to old, comfortable idolatries, throwing up a passionate "do not" in the face of compromise.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:16, the context is saturated with grief, loss, and social vulnerability. Naomi has lost her husband and sons, and she is urging her daughters-in-law to return to Moab, where they might find security. Orpah has wept, kissed Naomi goodbye, and departed. Naomi continues to press Ruth, urging her to follow her sister-in-law’s example and return to her people and her gods.

Ruth's response, "Entreat me not ('al) to leave you," is a passionate, immediate jussive. Naomi’s urging is an ongoing action that Ruth finds agonizing. By using 'al, Ruth is begging Naomi to cease her persuasive efforts. It is a plea of the deepest affection and resolve. Ruth does not use the objective lo', which would sound like a dry statement of future fact ("I will not leave you"). Instead, she uses 'al, laying bare her soul and begging Naomi to stop pushing her away. It reveals that Ruth’s determination is unyielding; she refuses to even entertain the possibility of departure, making her subsequent vow of lifelong devotion one of the most powerful expressions of covenant love in Scripture.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

Grammatically, the distinction between 'al and lo' is critical for expositors. If a teacher treats all Hebrew negatives as identical, the emotional texture of the passage is lost. When preaching Ruth 1:16, highlighting this distinction allows the congregation to feel the weight of Ruth's plea. She is not merely informing Naomi of her travel plans; she is fighting for her relationship with Naomi and her adoption into the covenant family of Yahweh. The use of 'al highlights the tenderness and vulnerability of her appeal—she is not demanding or issuing an absolute decree, but pleading from a position of humble, yet fierce, devotion.
