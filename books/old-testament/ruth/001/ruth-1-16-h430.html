GOD - ( elohim , Strong's H0430 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:16, the Hebrew word elohim (translated as "God") marks a monumental covenantal pivot. Though grammatically plural, it is used here with a singular meaning to denote the one true God of Israel, Yahweh. Ruth's declaration, "Your God [shall be] my God," represents her voluntary abandonment of Chemosh (the national deity of Moab) and her absolute allegiance to the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. In the context of her deep affection for Naomi, this term transcends mere family loyalty; it is a profound confession of personal saving faith, demonstrating that the covenant of Yahweh is open to those from any nation who seek refuge under His wings.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word elohim is one of the most prominent titles for deity in the Old Testament. Etymologically, it is linked to the singular forms el and eloah, which derive from a root meaning "strong," "mighty," or "preeminent." While the "-im" ending is the standard masculine plural suffix in Hebrew, when applied to the God of Israel, elohim functions as a plural of majesty, intensity, or fullness. It does not indicate polytheism but rather represents the supreme concentration of power, majesty, and absolute sovereignty. In the ancient Near East, nations were defined by their local deities. For a Moabite like Ruth, leaving her homeland meant leaving her national god, Chemosh. By choosing Naomi’s elohim, Ruth was not simply adopting a new religious preference; she was renouncing her cultural heritage, her safety net, and her ancestral identity to bind herself to the King of the universe.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Elohim possesses a broad semantic range depending on the context:

• The one true God, the Creator and Sovereign Ruler of the universe.

• Pagan gods or foreign deities (plural).

• Divine beings, angels, or heavenly hosts.

• On rare occasions, human rulers, judges, or representatives of divine authority.

In Ruth 1:16, the term is used in its highest sense—referring to the personal, covenant-keeping God of Israel. It denotes the absolute, self-existent Deity who demands exclusive worship and who governs history with providential care.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Elohim occurs over 2,500 times in the Old Testament. It is the first name used for God in Scripture, establishing Him as the cosmic Creator who speaks reality into existence.

• The word depicts God as the transcendent, all-powerful Ruler. Unlike the covenant name Yahweh (which emphasizes God’s immanence and relational closeness), elohim highlights His supreme power over nature and nations.

• When paired with the possessive pronoun ("my God," "your God"), the transcendent Creator becomes a personal refuge. This shift transforms a vast cosmic title into an intimate, relational reality. Ruth does not just acknowledge the existence of Israel's elohim; she claims Him as her own sovereign guardian.

4. Theological Implications

• Gentile Inclusion and Grace: Ruth’s confession is a theological watershed. As a Moabite, she belonged to a nation born of incest and historically barred from the assembly of the Lord. Yet, her reclamation of elohim proves that God’s redemptive grace transcends ethnic, national, and geographic boundaries.

• Monotheistic Conversion: Ruth’s declaration is an explicit rejection of polytheism. In a world where people believed different gods ruled different territories, Ruth recognizes that Naomi’s elohim is not a localized deity bound to the soil of Canaan, but the universal God whose authority extends even over Moab.

• The Interplay of Love (Chesed) and Faith: Ruth’s faith was sparked by the human kindness and faith she witnessed in Naomi's family, despite their failures and tragedies. The invisible hand of elohim uses human relationships to draw lost souls into His covenant family.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:15, Naomi urges Ruth to return "to her people and to her gods (elohim)." Here, elohim is plural and refers to the false idols of Moab. In direct response, Ruth uses the exact same word in verse 16, but pivots its meaning completely: "Your people shall be my people, and your God (elohim) my God." Ruth intentionally contrasts the empty, localized elohim of her past with the living, true elohim of Naomi's present. This is not a passive accommodation to Naomi's culture; it is an active conversion. Ruth confirms this in the very next verse (v. 17) by swearing an oath using God's personal covenant name, Yahweh, sealing her dedication to elohim with the ultimate covenant signature.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the narrative of Ruth, God's direct intervention is rarely flashy; there are no parting seas or falling fire. Instead, elohim operates quietly through "hap" or providence—leading Ruth to the field of Boaz, prompting acts of kindness, and opening closed wombs. Ruth’s early decision to trust in elohim sets the stage for the unfolding of God’s grand redemptive plan, ultimately placing this Moabitess in the direct lineage of King David and Jesus Christ.
