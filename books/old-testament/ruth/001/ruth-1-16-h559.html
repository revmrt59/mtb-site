SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's 559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'āmar is one of the most frequently used words in the Old Testament, primarily meaning "to speak," "to say," "to command," or "to declare." In Ruth 1:16, it marks a pivotal, covenantal declaration of loyalty. Rather than a casual utterance, Ruth's "saying" here represents a profound, settled resolve of the heart, transitioning her from a foreign observer to an active participant in the covenant community of Israel.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root of 'āmar is ancient and foundational to Semitic languages. In the ancient Near East, spoken words were not regarded as mere empty breath; they were understood as dynamic forces that carried the weight, authority, and intention of the speaker. Once spoken, a word established a reality, a promise, or a command that could not easily be recalled. In a narrative context, 'āmar serves as the mechanism through which internal thoughts and desires are externalized and made binding. In the open-air setting of the road to Judah, Ruth's spoken words finalize her departure from Moab and her binding attachment to Naomi and Yahweh.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb 'āmar primarily means to speak, utter, say, or tell. Depending on the context, its semantic range expands to include:

• To declare or pronounce a formal promise or oath.

• To command or direct with authority (especially when spoken by God or a king).

• To intend, think, or say in one's heart (expressing inner determination).

• To call or name someone or something.

In the Qal stem, which is used in Ruth 1:16, the verb conveys the direct, active expression of speech. It is not passive hearing, but active, self-initiating communication.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs 'āmar in several distinctive ways that enrich our understanding of the word:

• The Creative Word: When God speaks, reality is formed. His saying is an act of creation and sovereignty.

• The Internal Monologue: Often, 'āmar combined with "in the heart" describes a person's secret intentions or thoughts (e.g., Genesis 27:41). It reveals the true character of the individual.

• The Covenantal Vow: In legal and relational transactions, 'āmar introduces solemn, binding declarations that define relationships, such as adoption, marriage, or vassal treaties.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'āmar underscores the biblical truth that faith must be confessed. Ruth’s internal loyalty to Naomi and her nascent faith in Naomi's God are brought into the light through her spoken words. Speech acts as the bridge between internal conviction and external covenant. By saying these words, Ruth legally and spiritually binds herself to Yahweh. This mirrors the biblical pattern where the verbal confession of the mouth aligns with the belief of the heart, establishing a covenant relationship.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:16, the narrative reaches a critical crossroads. Naomi has repeatedly urged her daughters-in-law to turn back to their mother's houses and their local gods. Orpah complies, weeping and departing. Ruth, however, clings to Naomi.

The text states, "But Ruth said..." (Hebrew: wattōmer rût). This is a waw-consecutive imperfect, which drives the narrative forward with immediate force. Ruth’s 'āmar is not a casual reply; it is a decisive interruption of Naomi's pleading. It introduces a highly structured, poetic vow of self-consecration. By "saying" these words, Ruth formally rejects her Moabite identity and claims Yahweh as her God. Her speech is the turning point of the entire book, shifting the narrative from hopeless tragedy to covenantal hope.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a powerful contrast between Orpah and Ruth in this scene. Orpah’s devotion is expressed through tears and a kiss of departure, but Ruth’s devotion is expressed through her 'āmar—her spoken, unyielding vow. While emotions are fleeting, the spoken covenantal word remains. Furthermore, Ruth's declaration stands as one of the most beautiful confessions of faith by a Gentile in the entire Old Testament, showing that entry into God's family is open to those who turn to Him in faith and confess Him with their lips.
