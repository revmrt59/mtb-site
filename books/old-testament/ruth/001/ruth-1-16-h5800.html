LEAVE - ( 'azab , Strong's H5800 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'azab means to leave, forsake, abandon, or loose. In covenantal and relational contexts, it signifies a deliberate departure or the breaking of a relational bond. In Ruth 1:16, Ruth uses this word to declare her absolute refusal to abandon Naomi. Instead of forsaking her mother-in-law to return to her homeland and pagan gods, Ruth chooses lifelong loyalty, demonstrating the covenant-keeping love (chesed) of Israel’s God through her unwavering commitment.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primitive root of 'azab originally carried the physical idea of loosening, releasing, or letting go. It was used to describe untying an animal, releasing a burden, or leaving something behind in a physical space. Over time, this concrete physical action of "letting go" developed into a powerful relational concept. Relational departure is rarely neutral in the Old Testament; it usually involves a conscious choice to sever a connection with a person, place, or covenantal obligation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb 'azab occurs over 200 times in the Hebrew Bible. Its semantic range includes several distinct nuances depending on the context:

• To depart from a person, place, or state of being.

• To abandon, desert, or forsake someone in distress (such as a friend, spouse, or child).

• To neglect or abandon a duty, covenant, or commandment.

• To leave behind, hand over, or commit something to another's care.

• To loose, release, or let go (as in assisting a fallen beast of burden).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently uses 'azab to describe the broken relationship between Israel and Yahweh. When Israel turns to idols, they "forsake" the Lord and His covenant. Conversely, 'azab is used in the comforting promises of God, who repeatedly assures His people that He will never "leave" or "forsake" them.

A primary domestic word picture of 'azab is found in the creation mandate of marriage. A man must "leave" ('azab) his father and mother to cleave to his wife. This establishes a profound relational parallel: leaving one party is necessary to bind oneself permanently to another.

4. Theological Implications

Ruth’s refusal to 'azab Naomi represents a human expression of the divine attribute of chesed—covenantal, loyal love that refuses to let go. In a fallen world characterized by self-preservation, Ruth's refusal to abandon a destitute widow is a reflection of God’s own heart.

Furthermore, Ruth’s decision is highly theological because of what she must leave behind in order to not 'azab Naomi. To remain faithful to Naomi, Ruth must permanently leave ('azab) her country, her family, and her gods. She trades the security of her homeland for the vulnerability of a foreign widow, demonstrating that true covenant loyalty requires a costly, sacrificial departure from one's past.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:16, Naomi is pleading with her daughters-in-law to return to Moab, pointing out that she has no more sons to offer them. Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye and departs, but Ruth clings to her. When Naomi presses Ruth to follow Orpah's example, Ruth replies: "Entreat me not to leave ('azab) you, or to turn back from following after you."

The grammatical structure of Ruth's plea is intense and absolute. By using 'azab, Ruth is not merely talking about walking in a different direction; she is rejecting the idea of abandoning Naomi to her grief and poverty. For Ruth, to 'azab Naomi would be a moral and relational failure. Her refusal to leave is the catalyst for her conversion, as she immediately links her physical presence with Naomi to a spiritual allegiance to Naomi's God: "Your people shall be my people, and your God, my God."

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 24:27: "And he said, 'Blessed be the LORD God of my master Abraham, who has not forsaken His mercy and His truth toward my master...'"

• Psalm 22:1: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?"

• Proverbs 3:3: "Let not mercy and truth forsake you; bind them around your neck, write them on the tablet of your heart,"

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a stark contrast in the text of Ruth 1 between "leaving" ('azab) and "cleaving" (dabaq). Ruth 1:14 notes that Orpah kissed Naomi goodbye, but Ruth "clung" (dabaq) to her. This is the exact word pair used in Genesis 2:24 ('azab and dabaq). The narrative presents Ruth’s choice as a covenant-making moment. Ruth understands that to cleave to Naomi and the Lord, she must fully leave behind her past life.
