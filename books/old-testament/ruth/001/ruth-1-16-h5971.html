PEOPLE - ( 'am , Strong's H5971 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun 'am refers to a group of people bound together by common descent, covenant, language, or political unity. In the context of Ruth 1:16, it denotes the national and religious community of Israel. By declaring "your people shall be my people," Ruth is not merely choosing a new citizenship but is identifying with Yahweh’s covenant community, relinquishing her Moabitish national identity to be folded into the family of God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, nationhood, kinship, and religion were inseparable. A person's identity was completely wrapped up in their 'am (people) and their local deity. To leave one’s homeland was to cut oneself off from the protection of one's ancestral gods and familial safety net. When Naomi urged her daughters-in-law to return to their "people" and their "gods" (Ruth 1:15), she was presenting the realistic, culturally logical choice. Ruth's refusal to do so, and her pledge to join Naomi's 'am, was a radical act of self-abandonment. She was willing to become a vulnerable resident alien in Judah, choosing the covenant community of Israel over her own bloodline and heritage.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun 'am designates a collective group united by consanguinity (blood relation) or a shared covenant destiny. The term refers to a nation, people, kin, or relatives. It carries a strong relational and familial nuance, contrasting with the Hebrew word goy, which more frequently highlights a political, political-territorial, or geopolitical nation-state (often used of Gentile nations). While goy focuses on the external organization of a nation, 'am focuses on the internal relationship of its members. It emphasizes a community of individuals bound together as a family, most notably Israel as the chosen family of God.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, 'am is used to depict the intimate relationship between God and His chosen flock. It functions as a collective singular noun, treating a vast group of individuals as one cohesive unit.

• The Family Scale Writ Large: In its earliest uses, 'am can refer to one's paternal relatives or clan. When a person dies, they are "gathered to their people" (Genesis 25:8). This paints a picture of death not as annihilation, but as being reunited with the ancestral community.

• The Covenant Congregation: In the wilderness, Israel is referred to as the "people of the assembly." They are a distinct community carved out of the world, defined not by their geographic borders (as they had none in the wilderness) but by their relation to Yahweh.

4. Theological Implications

The word 'am is central to the overarching covenant formula of Scripture: "I will be your God, and you shall be My people" (Leviticus 26:12). The relationship is characterized by mutual belonging and responsibility.

• Covenantal Exclusivity: As Yahweh's 'am, Israel was to be holy—set apart from the surrounding goyim (nations). This holiness was not for isolationism, but to serve as a light to the nations.

• Inclusion of the Gentile: Ruth’s declaration is a monumental theological milestone. It demonstrates that membership in God’s 'am is not strictly biological or racial, but spiritual. By faith, loyalty, and confession, a Moabite woman—belonging to a nation historically barred from the assembly of Yahweh (Deuteronomy 23:3)—is fully incorporated into the covenant people, eventually entering the direct messianic lineage of David and Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:16, the word 'am occurs in a parallel structure: "your people ('am) shall be my people ('am), and your God, my God." The order is highly significant. Ruth recognizes that to belong to Naomi's God, she must also belong to Naomi's people. She does not attempt to worship Yahweh in isolation or while clinging to her Moabite identity. Her conversion is complete; she embraces the community, the laws, the social standing, and the struggles of Israel. In a time when Israel itself was compromising its identity (during the period of the Judges), this Moabitess ironically models what it truly means to be the 'am of God through her sacrificial covenant love (chesed).

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 6:7 - "I will take you as My people, and I will be your God. Then you shall know that I am the LORD your God who brings you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians."

• Deuteronomy 7:6 - "For you are a holy people to the LORD your God; the LORD your God has chosen you to be a people for Himself, a special treasure above all the peoples on the face of the earth."

• 2 Samuel 7:23 - "And who is like Your people, like Israel, the one nation on the earth whom God went to redeem for Himself as a people, to make for Himself a name..."

• Hosea 2:23 - "...Then I will say to those who were not My people, 'You are My people!' And they shall say, 'You are my God!'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

For the Christian teacher and pastor, Ruth's adoption into the 'am of Israel is a beautiful shadow of the New Testament doctrine of graft-in salvation (Romans 11). Believers in the church age, once "not a people," have now become the "people of God" (1 Peter 2:10). Ruth’s decision challenges the modern, highly individualized view of faith. True conversion leads to a love for the local church and the global body of Christ. Like Ruth, we cannot say we love the God of the covenant while rejecting or isolating ourselves from the people of the covenant.
