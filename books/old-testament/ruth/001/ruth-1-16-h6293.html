ENTREAT - ( paga` , Strong's H6293 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb paga` fundamentally describes a forceful meeting, encounter, or collision, carrying meanings that range from physically striking an enemy to making urgent intercession. In the context of Ruth's passionate plea to Naomi, the word denotes an intense, importunate entreaty where Ruth begs Naomi to stop pressing her to go back. It highlights a profound, covenantal loyalty that refuses to be shaken or turned away by discouragement.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root paga` carries a raw, physical primary meaning of "striking," "falling upon," or "meeting" someone or something. It describes an energetic encounter or collision. This can be a peaceful meeting of boundaries, a violent attack where one falls upon an enemy, or a persistent encounter where one runs up to someone to make an urgent request. In conversational and relational contexts, this physical concept of "hitting" or "impacting" shifts to a verbal pressing. To entreat someone is to assail them with requests, to press them hard with a petition, or to importune them.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew verb occurs 46 times in the Old Testament. Its semantic range is diverse but interconnected by the core concept of contact:

• To meet, encounter, or reach (a boundary or landmark).

• To fall upon, attack, or strike down (with physical violence).

• To plead, urge, entreat, or make intercession (assailing with words of petition).

• To cause to light upon or lay upon (in the causative Hiphil form).

In Ruth 1:16, the verb is in the Qal imperfect form (al-tipge`i-bi), combined with the preposition be (against/upon me), which means "do not urge me" or "do not press me."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The physical and verbal usages of paga` enrich our understanding of the word’s depth:

• Physical Boundary: The word is used to describe how tribal borders "reach" or "meet" one another in the allotment of the Promised Land (Joshua 16:7). The boundary physically encounters the next territory.

• Physical Assault: The word describes a physical onslaught or lethal blow. For example, when Gideon commands his son to fall upon the captive kings of Midian, and when they ask Gideon to do it instead, the word used for "fall upon us" is paga` (Judges 8:21).

• Verbal Intercession: The transition from physical striking to verbal pleading is seen in the concept of intercession. To intercede is to "meet" or "strike" a deal with a superior on behalf of someone else, stepping in to block a blow or press a petition (Jeremiah 7:16).

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, paga` reveals that intercession and earnest entreaty are not casual, polite suggestions. They are high-stakes, energetic acts of intervention. When a believer entreats God or intercedes for others, they are stepping into the pathway, throwing themselves into the situation to make a powerful spiritual impact. In the context of Ruth, this intense verb underscores the nature of covenantal love (hesed). True biblical loyalty is not passive; it is persistent, aggressive in its devotion, and willing to press through obstacles and discouraging words to remain faithful.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:16, Naomi has repeatedly urged her daughters-in-law to return to their maternal homes in Moab. Orpah eventually kisses Naomi goodbye and departs, but Ruth clings to her. When Naomi pushes one final time, Ruth replies, "Entreat me not to leave you."

By using paga`, Ruth is telling Naomi, "Do not collide with me with your arguments," "Do not hit me with your discouragements," or "Do not force me to abandon you." Naomi’s loving attempts to dismiss Ruth are felt by Ruth as a painful, physical pressure. Ruth's response is an importunate plea for Naomi to stop pushing her away, demonstrating that her resolve to stay is unbreakable.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Jeremiah 7:16: "Therefore do not pray for this people, nor lift up a cry or prayer for them, nor make intercession to Me; for I will not hear you."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In modern Hebrew, the root paga has evolved to primarily mean "to hurt," "to damage," or "to offend." This modern development highlights the friction present in the ancient root. Ruth’s use of the word in Ruth 1:16 shows that Naomi's insistence was genuinely painful to her heart. It was a pressure that she had to actively resist with a counter-pressure of absolute devotion, showing that entering into covenantal faithfulness often requires resisting the well-meaning advice of others to turn back.
