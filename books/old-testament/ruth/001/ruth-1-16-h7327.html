RUTH - ( Re'uth , Strong's H7327 )

Summary:

The Hebrew name Re'uth (Ruth) means "companion," "friend," or "friendship." It represents deep covenantal loyalty, relational devotion, and selfless commitment. Within the narrative, Ruth embodies the very meaning of her name, choosing to bind herself to her mother-in-law Naomi and to Yahweh, the God of Israel. Her name serves as a thematic key to the entire book, pointing to the power of loyal love (chesed) and redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Ruth is etymologically derived from a Hebrew root associated with ra'ah or re'a, which means a friend, companion, or fellow. The noun form re'uth carries the sense of companion or female associate. In the Ancient Near East, names were not merely labels but often reflected a person's character, destiny, or role in the narrative. While her sister-in-law Orpah's name is associated with the back of the neck (suggesting turning one's back, which she eventually does), Ruth's name signals her destiny as the ultimate loyal companion. She is the foreigner who binds herself to a destitute Israelite widow, displaying a radical commitment that redefines family and community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name refers specifically to Ruth the Moabitess. Lexically, the name means "friendship" or "female companion." It is built on the root r-'-h, which refers to pasturing, tending, or associating with someone. Thus, a re'a is a friend with whom one shares life. In its feminine form, it underscores the quality of companionship, mutual care, and partnership. In the Hebrew Bible, this proper noun occurs exclusively in the Book of Ruth to refer to this specific historical figure, and twice in the genealogy of Jesus in the New Testament (as Rhouth in Greek).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Because the name is a proper noun, its usage is primarily historical and narrative-driven. However, the word picture behind the name is rich with pastoral imagery. A companion in Hebrew thought is not merely a casual acquaintance but someone who shares a covenantal bond, walks alongside another in times of adversity, and protects the other's interests. The word picture suggests a shepherd (ro'eh) tending to sheep—implying care, protection, and constant presence. Ruth's actions paint this picture vividly: she refuses to leave Naomi, gathers grain in dangerous fields to feed her, and seeks the redemption of Naomi's family line.

4. Theological Implications

The name Ruth has profound theological significance. It represents the inclusion of the Gentiles into the covenant community of Israel and the line of the Messiah. As a Moabitess, Ruth belonged to a nation banned from the assembly of Yahweh. Yet, through her faith and companionship, she is redeemed and becomes an ancestor of King David and Jesus Christ. This demonstrates that faith and loyal love (chesed) transcend national and ethnic barriers. Furthermore, Ruth is a human agent of God's providence; her companionate love is a tangible expression of Yahweh’s wings under which she has come to take refuge.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:16, the name is introduced in a moment of dramatic tension. Naomi has urged her daughters-in-law to return to their Moabite families and gods. Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye, but Ruth clings to her. When Ruth speaks her famous words of devotion ("Entreat me not to leave you..."), her actions perfectly fulfill the meaning of her name. She is not merely a daughter-in-law by legal definition; she chooses to be Naomi's lifelong companion. Her name underscores the irony that an outsider from Moab acts as a truer companion to an Israelite widow than Naomi’s own kin.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

As a proper noun, the name occurs only in the Book of Ruth in the Old Testament. Key uses include:

• Ruth 1:14: "Then they lifted up their voices and wept again; and Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth clung to her."

• Ruth 2:2: "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.'"

• Ruth 3:9: "And he said, 'Who are you?' So she answered, 'I am Ruth, your maidservant. Take your maidservant under your wing, for you are a close relative.'"

• Ruth 4:13: "So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and when he went in to her, the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Hebrew canon, the Book of Ruth is placed in the Ketuvim (Writings) immediately following the Book of Proverbs. This placement creates a powerful literary connection: the "virtuous woman" (eshet chayil) of Proverbs 31 is immediately followed by Ruth, who is explicitly called a "virtuous woman" (eshet chayil) in Ruth 3:11. Her name "companion" thus links the abstract wisdom of Proverbs 31 with a real-life historical example of a woman who lived out wisdom through loving-kindness, hard work, and loyalty.
