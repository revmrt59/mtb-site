TURN BACK - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub is one of the most significant and frequently used words in the Old Testament, fundamentally meaning to turn, return, go back, or retreat. In Ruth 1:16, it is translated in the NKJV as "turn back," describing Orpah's decision to return to her people and her gods, in stark contrast to Ruth's fierce loyalty and refusal to abandon Naomi. Culturally and spiritually, this word forms the theological pivot of the Book of Ruth, representing not just physical movement, but a choice of allegiance, identity, and covenant faith.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew language is deeply concrete, expressing abstract ideas through physical, sensory actions. The verb shub is a motion verb that paints a vivid physical picture: a traveler turning around 180 degrees to head back to their starting point, or a path that curves back upon itself. It suggests a complete reversal of direction, a literal turning back or returning to a previous location or state. In the ancient world, physical movement and geographic orientation were deeply tied to one's identity and allegiance. A road was not merely a physical path but a metaphor for a person's life journey, moral conduct, and loyalties. To turn back on a path meant to undo a journey, to reverse a commitment, or to return to one's original home and gods.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shub occurs over 1,000 times in the Hebrew Bible, representing one of the most common and versatile roots. The fundamental semantic range of the verb in the Qal stem is to return, to turn back, or to turn away.

• Spatial Return: Physical movement back to a place of departure or origin, such as returning home or going back to a country.

• Relational/Spiritual Reversal: A spiritual turning, which can express either positive repentance (turning back to God) or negative apostasy (turning back from following God to false ways).

• Dynamic Actions: To repeat an action, to do something again, or to answer (physically turning back a word).

In Ruth 1:16, the verb appears in the Qal infinitive construct (lashub), expressing the physical and personal action of turning back from accompanying Naomi.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Path of Physical Return: The verb often depicts a physical homecoming. It is used for the return of the remnant from exile, the return of a dove to the ark, or the return of dust to the earth.

• The Volitional Turn of Repentance: Shub serves as the primary theological term for repentance. It represents a conscious decision to turn away from sin and idols and turn toward Yahweh. It is an active, visible change of life's direction rather than a merely passive feeling of sorrow.

• The Tragic Turn of Apostasy: The verb is also used negatively to describe Israel’s tendency to turn back from following God, turning back to their previous rebellion, stubbornness, or idolatry.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shub represents the great invitation of covenant history: the call to return to Yahweh. The covenant relationship is dynamic, requiring active loyalty. When God's people wander, the prophets consistently declare the promise of restoration if the people will turn back to Him.

In the Book of Ruth, shub is the primary thematic thread of chapter 1, appearing twelve times. The narrative begins with Naomi's decision to "return" from Moab because she heard that Yahweh had provided food for His people. The physical movement of returning to Bethlehem is a picture of returning to the place of covenant blessing, provision, and divine presence. Naomi urges her daughters-in-law to "return" to their mothers' homes. While Orpah chooses to turn back to her people and her gods, Ruth's refusal to turn back demonstrates a true, deep-seated conversion. She rejects the physical and spiritual "turning back" to Moab, choosing instead the God of Israel.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the immediate context of Ruth 1:16, Naomi has just pointed out that Orpah has gone back to her people and her gods, and she commands Ruth to "return after your sister-in-law." Ruth's response is an absolute refusal to let go of Naomi or Naomi's God: "Entreat me not to leave you, or to turn back from following after you."

The use of shub here contrasts two paths. For Orpah, turning back meant returning to safety, security, family, and the pagan gods of Moab. For Ruth, turning back would mean the same physical comfort but spiritual regression. By refusing to turn back, Ruth makes a definitive covenant commitment. The physical act of moving forward with Naomi into the Promised Land represents a permanent spiritual transition. She leaves behind her pagan past once and for all, stepping under the protective wings of Yahweh.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:19: "In the sweat of your face you shall eat bread till you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; for dust you are, and to dust you shall return."

• Ruth 1:15: "And she said, 'Look, your sister-in-law has gone back to her people and to her gods; return after your sister-in-law.'"

• Psalm 23:3: "He restores my soul; He leads me in the paths of righteousness for His name's sake."

• Joel 2:12: "'Now, therefore,' says the LORD, 'Turn to Me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning.'"

• Malachi 3:7: "Yet from the days of your fathers you have gone away from My ordinances and have not kept them. Return to Me, and I will return to you, says the LORD of hosts."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• Emotion Versus Commitment: Both Ruth and Orpah wept and expressed deep affection for Naomi. However, Orpah ultimately turned back, whereas Ruth clung to her. This illustrates that emotional warmth and tears do not equate to covenant commitment. True faith is demonstrated by a refusal to turn back when the cost of following God is high.

• The Metaphor of the Boundary: Moab and Israel were divided by the Jordan River and the Dead Sea. To turn back was to cross back over the boundary into a land of idolatry. Ruth’s determination to not turn back highlights the necessity of crossing spiritual boundaries with no intention of returning to the old life.

• The Restorative Heart of God: Even though the book of Ruth begins with tragedy, famine, and death in a foreign land, the repetition of shub reminds the reader that restoration is always possible when one turns back to the land of promise and the God of covenant faithfulness.
