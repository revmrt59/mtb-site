WHEREVER - ( asher , Strong's H834 )

Summary:

The Hebrew relative pronoun asher functions in combination with prepositions to mean "wherever." In Ruth's famous declaration of loyalty to Naomi, this word serves as an unconditional, open-ended commitment. By saying "wherever you go" and "wherever you lodge," Ruth relinquishes control over her own destination and living conditions, binding her future entirely to Naomi's. It marks a transition from a conditional relationship to a covenantal, sacrificial bond of love (chesed), demonstrating absolute devotion that transcends geographical, cultural, and national boundaries.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The particle asher is the primary relative pronoun in Biblical Hebrew, serving to connect clauses and define relationships between nouns and their following modifiers. It is essentially a word of connection. Rather than pointing to a specific, static location, it functions as a bridge, linking an action to a place or condition. In the ancient Semitic mind, space and destination were not merely coordinates on a map but were deeply tied to relationship and destiny. When combined with prepositions—such as the directional 'el ("to") or the locational be ("in")—asher expands to mean "wherever" or "in whatever place." It signals a relational coordinate: the destination is not defined by geography, but by the presence of the person being followed.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word asher is an indeclinable relative particle. Its fundamental meaning is "which," "who," "that," or "where." When prefixed with the preposition 'el (yielding 'el-asher), it functions as a relative local conjunction meaning "whithersoever," "to the place which," or "wherever." When prefixed with the preposition be (yielding b'asher), it expresses state or position: "in the place which," "wherein," or "wherever." In Ruth 1:16, both forms are utilized successively: 'el-asher teleki elek ("to where you go, I will go") and u-b'asher talini alin ("and in where you lodge, I will lodge"). The lexical range is entirely relational and locational, expressing a total transfer of personal boundaries to the path and dwelling of another.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Old Testament, asher serves as a grammatical hinge, but in poetic and covenantal contexts, it paints a picture of absolute alignment. When used in contexts of journeying, it acts as a blank check of commitment.

• The "Path of Devotion" Picture: In Hebrew thought, walking (halak) represents one's lifestyle, ethics, and destiny. By placing asher before "you go," the speaker subjects their entire lifestyle and future path to the path of the other.

• The "Dwelling of Rest" Picture: Lodging (lun) refers to staying overnight, finding temporary shelter, or settling down permanently. Combining asher with this verb means that security, rest, and home are no longer defined by a homeland, but by the immediate presence and safety shared with the companion.

4. Theological Implications

Ruth’s use of asher represents the language of covenant conversion. In the ancient Near East, deities were localized and tied to specific lands. To cross a border was to change gods. By committing to go "wherever" Naomi goes, Ruth is not merely choosing a geographical destination; she is consciously crossing a theological boundary.

The grammatical flexibility of asher allows for an unlimited scope of surrender. It is a word of radical chesed (covenant faithfulness). Ruth does not say, "I will go with you to Bethlehem," which would limit her commitment to a specific, known destination. By using the indefinite "wherever," she surrenders her sovereignty over her future. This mirrors the Abrahamic call to go to a land that God would show him. It is an act of saving faith, culminating in her declaration: "Your people shall be my people, and your God, my God."

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:16, the context is Naomi's bitter insistence that her daughters-in-law return to their mothers' houses and their local gods in Moab. Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye and returns, but Ruth clings to her. Naomi attempts to draw a boundary of despair, but Ruth dissolves that boundary using asher. The repetition of the word is emphatic:

• 'el-asher teleki elek ("to-wherever you go, I-will-go"): This addresses the journey, the active movement into an unknown and potentially hostile foreign land.

• u-b'asher talini alin ("and-in-wherever you-lodge, I-will-lodge"): This addresses the stationary reality of daily life—poverty, widowhood, and marginalization.

The use of asher indicates that Ruth makes no conditions. Whether the journey leads to prosperity or further grief, and whether the lodging is a stable or a home, Ruth's commitment is absolute.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Septuagint (LXX), the Greek translation of the Old Testament, the Hebrew 'el-asher in Ruth 1:16 is translated using the Greek relative adverb hou ean ("wherever" / "whithersoever"). This construction combines the relative adverb hou ("where") with the conditional particle ean, which introduces a subjunctive verb ("you might go"). This grammatical structure in Greek perfectly preserves the indefinite, open-ended nature of the Hebrew original. It signifies that no matter what conditions arise, the decision is already settled. In New Testament theology, this same absolute, unconditional following is demanded by Jesus of His disciples, who are called to follow the Lamb wherever He goes.
