LORD - ( Yahweh , Strong's H3068 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:17, the Moabitess Ruth invokes the sacred, personal, and covenant-making name of Israel's God, Yahweh (translated as "LORD"), to seal her unbreakable vow of loyalty to Naomi. Rather than swearing by a general deity or her native Moabite gods, Ruth deliberately binds herself to the God of Israel. This profound invocation marks her formal entry into the covenant community, demonstrating that her loyalty to Naomi is rooted in a saving faith in the one true God who keeps His promises.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The name Yahweh is the sacred, personal name of God revealed to Israel. It is etymologically connected to the Hebrew verb hayah, which means "to be" or "to exist." When God revealed this name to Moses at the burning bush, He explained it as "I AM WHO I AM," signifying His self-existence, eternal presence, and absolute independence. Unlike the pagan deities of the ancient Near East, who were local, limited, and dependent on human care, Yahweh is the self-sustaining Creator who acts sovereignly in history. The physical representation of this name in ancient Hebrew script (the Tetragrammaton, YHWH) was held in such high reverence that copyists and speakers took extreme care to preserve its sanctity, associating it with the very breath of life and the unshakeable foundation of reality.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Yahweh is the proper noun representing the one true God of Israel. It denotes the eternal, self-existent, and unchangeable One who initiates and maintains covenant relationships with His people. While Elohim emphasizes God's power as the Creator of the universe, Yahweh is His personal name, expressing His relational closeness, holiness, and redeeming love. It is the name used when God binds Himself to oaths, promises, and redemptive acts. In English translations, it is traditionally rendered as "LORD" in all capital letters to distinguish it from Adonai (Master or Lord).

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Yahweh occurs over 6,800 times in the Old Testament, making it the most frequent designation for God in scripture. The name is rich with metaphorical associations that depict God's character:

• The Shepherd: He guides, feeds, and protects His flock (Psalm 23:1).

• The Rock: He is an immovable, stable foundation and refuge in times of trouble (Deuteronomy 32:4).

• The Strong Tower: He represents safety, security, and an impregnable defense for those who trust in Him (Proverbs 18:10).

• The Husband/Redeemer: He is tenderly and fiercely committed to His covenant people, pursuing them even when they stray (Isaiah 54:5).

### 4. Theological Implications

The name Yahweh carries profound theological weight, centered on covenant faithfulness (hesed).

• Unchanging Faithfulness: Because Yahweh is self-existent, His character and promises never change. He remains faithful to His covenant even when His people are faithless.

• Redemptive Initiative: Yahweh is the God who hears the cries of the oppressed and steps down into history to deliver them. The name is fundamentally linked to the Exodus, establishing God as the supreme Redeemer.

• Exclusivity and Sovereignty: The name demands absolute allegiance. To know Yahweh is to recognize that there is no other god beside Him, and that He alone rules over history, nations, and individual lives.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:17, Ruth declares: "The LORD [Yahweh] do so to me, and more also, if anything but death parts you and me." This is the theological climax of Ruth’s confession.

• A Shift in Allegiance: Orpah returned "to her people and to her gods" (Ruth 1:15), but Ruth refuses to return to Chemosh, the national god of Moab. By invoking Yahweh, Ruth consciously and publicly transfers her allegiance from the pantheon of Moab to the sovereign Lord of Israel.

• A Covenant Oath: Swearing by Yahweh was the most solemn oath an Israelite could make. Ruth uses a standard Hebrew self-imprecation formula ("The LORD do so to me, and more also"), meaning she invites God's severe judgment upon herself if she fails to keep her word.

• True Conversion: This is not merely an expression of human affection for Naomi; it is a confession of faith. Ruth recognizes Yahweh as the supreme judge of her life, showcasing that a Gentile outsider has grasped the reality of covenant loyalty (hesed) far better than many in Israel during the chaotic period of the Judges.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 3:15: "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations."'"

• Psalm 23:1: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

• Proverbs 18:10: "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; The righteous run into it and are safe."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

Because ancient scribes considered the name Yahweh too sacred to pronounce aloud, they substituted the word Adonai (Lord) when reading the Scriptures. In the Middle Ages, Hebrew vowel points from Adonai were combined with the consonant letters of YHWH, leading to the hybrid pronunciation "Jehovah." Modern scholarship recognizes Yahweh as the most accurate historical pronunciation. In the New Testament, the Greek word Kyrios (Lord) is used by the Septuagint and the New Testament writers to translate YHWH, a title that is directly applied to Jesus Christ (e.g., Philippians 2:9-11), affirming His full divinity and equality with the covenant God of Israel.
