MORE - ( yasaph , Strong's H3254 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yasaph fundamentally means to add, increase, or do something again. In the context of ancient Near Eastern oaths, such as Ruth’s intense pledge of loyalty to Naomi in Ruth 1:17, it is used in a self-imprecatory formula ("May the LORD do so to me, and more also"). Here, it represents a solemn invocation of divine judgment, asking God to multiply consequences upon the speaker if she fails to keep her commitment. It elevates a human promise into a sacred covenant bound under the highest spiritual authority.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root yasaph carries the physical and conceptual image of piling things on top of one another, increasing a quantity, or repeating an action. In agrarian society, it was used to describe adding to a harvest, increasing herds, or extending the borders of land. It suggests accumulation and expansion. When imported into the realm of covenants and swearing, yasaph moves from a simple mathematical or physical addition to a moral and spiritual multiplier. To "add" to a curse meant to heap additional judgments or punishments beyond the initial terms, signaling absolute, unreserved commitment.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary definition of yasaph is to add, increase, repeat, or do further. It functions in various grammatical stems:

• In the Qal stem, it means to add or increase.

• In the Hiphil stem (as seen in Ruth 1:17 with the jussive yosip), it often functions as an auxiliary verb meaning "to do again" or "to do further." When used in oath formulas, it means "to add to" a punishment or judgment.

The word denotes the expansion of a previous boundary or measure. It is the root behind the proper name Joseph (Yoseph), which means "May He [the LORD] add."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs yasaph in several distinct ways to paint pictures of growth, repetition, and intensification:

• Cumulative Addition: Adding days to a king's life (as with Hezekiah) or adding grief to sorrow.

• Repeated Actions: To "add to do" something means to do it again, such as the Israelites "again" doing evil in the sight of the LORD.

• Covenantal Security: Adding to God's words is strictly forbidden, as His word is already perfect and complete.

• Oath Formulas: The phrase "May God do so to me, and more [add] also" is a standard idiomatic expression of ultimate commitment. The speaker invites God to execute a specific penalty, and then "add" further unspoken terrors if the vow is broken.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, yasaph in covenantal oaths reveals the seriousness of verbal commitments before a holy God. By invoking the covenant name of God (Yahweh) alongside yasaph, Ruth acknowledges that God is the active executor of justice and the guarantor of oaths.

• God as the Sovereign Multiplier: He is the one who adds blessings or multiplies curses based on covenant faithfulness.

• Unconditional Loyalty: Using this formula shows that human relationships under God are not mere social contracts but are theological in nature. Ruth binds her destiny to Naomi's under the sovereign hand of Yahweh, who holds the power to add life or death.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:17, Ruth is a Moabitess who has forsaken her ancestral gods to follow Naomi and cling to Yahweh. The use of the covenant name Yahweh combined with yasaph is the climax of her confession of faith. She does not merely promise to stay with Naomi; she places her life in Yahweh’s hands. By saying "the LORD do so to me, and more also," she uses a standard Hebrew oath format, demonstrating her rapid assimilation into the language and covenant theology of Israel. The nuance of yasaph here is a complete surrender of self-will. She leaves no loophole; if she deserts Naomi, she invites Yahweh to heap His wrath upon her.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• 1 Samuel 3:17: "Eli said, 'What is the thing that the LORD has spoken to you? Please do not hide it from me. God do so to you, and more also, if you hide any word from me of all the things that He said to you.'"

• 1 Samuel 14:44: "Saul answered, 'God do so and more also; for you shall surely die, Jonathan.'"

• 2 Samuel 3:35: "And when all the people came to persuade David to eat food while it was still day, David took an oath, saying, 'God do so to me, and more also, if I taste bread or anything else before the sun goes down!'"

• 1 Kings 19:2: "Then Jezebel sent a messenger to Elijah, saying, 'So let the gods do to me, and more also, if I do not make your life as the life of one of them by tomorrow about this time.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The translation of yasaph in this oath formula varies slightly across English versions (e.g., "and more also," "and worse," "and severely"), but the core concept remains the same: the addition of divine judgment. Interestingly, while Israelites frequently used this formula, Ruth’s use of it is highly striking because she is a foreigner. Her adoption of this solemn Hebrew oath indicates that she is not merely making a sentimental promise but is entering into a formal, binding covenant before the God of Israel.
