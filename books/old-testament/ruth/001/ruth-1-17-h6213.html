DO - ( asah , Strong's H6213 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb asah is a foundational Old Testament word meaning "to do," "to make," "to fashion," or "to accomplish." In Ruth 1:17, it functions within a solemn oath formula where Ruth calls down a self-imprecation: "The LORD do so to me, and more also..." Here, asah carries the weight of divine execution and covenant consequence, as Ruth asks Yahweh to bring judgment upon her if she fails to stay faithful to Naomi. It reflects a complete surrender to God's sovereign hand, turning a personal promise into a sacred covenant under God's active oversight.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verb asah is one of the most frequently used words in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 2,600 times. Unlike the word bara (which is used exclusively of God creating out of nothing), asah typically involves working with existing materials, executing a plan, or carrying out a deed. It represents the physical translation of an idea, command, or covenant into historical reality.

In the ancient Near East, calling on a deity to "do" something within an oath was a legal and spiritual standard. It recognized that covenants were not merely agreements between humans but were supervised by a divine third party who acts as the executor of justice. When Ruth uses this word, she is not speaking of a passive observation; she is appealing to Yahweh as the active Agent of justice who physically executes the consequences of broken vows.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb asah has a broad semantic range that is heavily determined by its context:

• To make or fashion: Producing physical objects, such as Noah making the ark, or God making the garments of skin for Adam and Eve.

• To do or perform: Carrying out a task, executing a duty, or keeping a commandment.

• To produce or yield: Used of land yielding crops or trees producing fruit.

• To execute or bring about: Carrying out a decree, judgment, or act of retribution.

• In oath formulas: To inflict, execute, or bring down a specified (or implied) curse upon someone.

In the context of Ruth 1:17, asah means to actively execute or bring about divine judgment. It is an appeal for God to perform a physical action of retribution if the covenant is broken.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, asah paints a picture of hands-on, active execution. Where dabar (word) represents the spoken promise or decree, asah is the physical performance of that word.

• The Creator at Work: God "made" (asah) the firmament, the sun, and the moon. This presents Him not merely as an abstract thinker, but as an active Builder who shapes history and the cosmos.

• The Obedient Servant: When Noah did (asah) all that God commanded, or when the Israelites did (asah) according to the Law, it pictured faith transformed into physical, observable obedience.

• The Sovereign Judge: When God "does" (asah) judgment, He is executing justice in the sight of the nations, ensuring that His moral law is actively maintained.

### 4. Theological Implications

The use of asah in Ruth 1:17 carries profound theological weight:

• God as Executor of Covenants: Ruth understands that Yahweh is not a passive spectator. He is the active Guardian of oaths. By asking Him to "do so" to her, she recognizes His power to intervene directly in human affairs to uphold justice.

• Conversion and Yahweh’s Sovereignty: Ruth, a Moabite, does not swear by Chemosh (the god of Moab) but by Yahweh. She recognizes Yahweh's unique authority to "do" or execute judgments. This marks her formal entry into a covenant relationship with the living God of Israel.

• The Reality of Retribution: In Hebrew theology, blessings and curses are not magical forces; they are the active works of God. Ruth’s oath recognizes that if she abandons Naomi, she is subject to the active, physical discipline of a holy God.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:17, the phrase is "The LORD do so to me, and more also..." (Yhwh ya'aseh-li ko weyo'sip). This is a classic Hebrew self-imprecation.

The exact nature of the curse is left unsaid ("do so") as a form of euphemism. Ancient speakers avoided naming specific disasters (like death or disease) to prevent bringing those evils into existence by mouth. Instead, the phrase "do so" acted as a placeholder for the absolute worst possible divine retribution.

By utilizing asah in this formula, Ruth establishes the absolute permanence of her commitment. She is saying, "May Yahweh actively strike me down with the most severe punishment if I allow anything but death to separate us." It elevates her commitment to Naomi from a warm, emotional promise to a binding, high-stakes covenant under the threat of divine execution.

• 1 Kings 19:2: "Then Jezebel sent a messenger to Elijah, saying, 'So let the gods do to me, and more also, if I do not make your life as the life of one of them by tomorrow about this time.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Contrast with Pagan Oaths: In 1 Kings 19:2, Jezebel uses the plural form of the verb with pagan deities ("let the gods do..."). In contrast, Ruth uses the singular form with the unique covenant name of Israel's God: "The LORD [Yahweh] do so..." This highlights her specific theological alignment with Israel's monotheism.

• The Power of the Implied Curse: The structure of the Hebrew oath shows that a vow was considered a matter of life and death. The "doing" of the curse was entirely in the hands of God, illustrating the ancient Israelite belief that human words have physical consequences when bound by the name of Yahweh.
