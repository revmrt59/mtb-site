PARTS - ( parad , Strong's H6504 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb parad fundamentally means to divide, separate, or disperse. In the context of covenantal commitment, it represents the ultimate severance of a relationship. In Ruth 1:17, Ruth uses this term in her solemn oath to Naomi, declaring that only physical death has the power to sever their shared life and destiny. This word highlights the absolute, indivisible nature of true biblical devotion, which refuses any self-initiated separation and submits only to the sovereign boundary of death.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root meaning of parad relates to the act of dividing or spreading out. It depicts physical separation, such as paths diverging, rivers branching off into separate streams, or people scattering in different directions.

• In the agrarian ancient Near East, the word was used to describe the separation of herds, flocks, or family units to prevent conflict or find wider pastureland, such as the separation of Abraham and Lot.

• The word picture is one of a single unit breaking into distinct, independent parts that no longer walk or function together.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• In the Qal stem, parad means to divide, separate, or scatter.

• In the Niphal stem, it means to separate oneself, divide, or be divided.

• In the Piel stem, it means to go aside or associate with in a negative or idolatrous sense.

• In the Hiphil stem—which is used in Ruth 1:17—it means to make a division, separate, or sever.

• The core lexical definition in this specific context is the act of putting a wedge or barrier between two closely joined entities, resulting in their complete disconnection.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Diverging Paths: It describes the physical parting of ways between individuals who can no longer travel together, such as the separation of Jacob and Laban or Abram and Lot.

• Natural Dispersal: It is used to describe the branching of rivers (Genesis 2:10) or the scattering of nations after the flood (Genesis 10:5).

• Bones out of Joint: In Psalm 22:14, the word is used in a passive sense to describe bones being "out of joint" or separated from their proper alignment, conveying intense physical and emotional distress.

4. Theological Implications

• The Indissolubility of Covenant: Ruth’s use of parad elevates her commitment from a mere emotional attachment to a binding covenant. By invoking the covenant name of God (Yahweh) alongside this verb, she insists that her union with Naomi and Naomi's God cannot be dissolved by human will.

• Death as the Only Legitimate Separator: In biblical theology, covenantal bonds—whether marriage or deep communal commitments—are designed to be lifelong. Only God, who holds the keys to life and death, has the authority to bring such a separation.

• A Shadow of Christ’s Love: This total refusal to be parted foreshadows the steadfast love (chesed) of God, and ultimately the New Testament promise that nothing—not even death—can separate the believer from the love of God in Christ Jesus.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:17, parad occurs in the Hiphil (causative) form. Ruth states: "The LORD do so to me, and more also, if anything but death parts you and me."

• The grammatical construction is a self-imprecatory oath. Ruth invokes a curse upon herself if she allows any circumstance, trial, or human influence to cause a separation between them.

• By specifying "death" as the only agent of parad, Ruth shuts the door on all other potential reasons for leaving: economic hardship, cultural alienation, childlessness, or the search for a new husband. Her commitment is absolute and unconditional.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 13:9 - "Is not the whole land before you? Please separate from me. If you take the left, then I will go to the right; or if you go to the right, then I will go to the left."

• Genesis 13:11 - "Then Lot chose for himself all the plain of Jordan, and Lot journeyed east. And they separated from each other."

• 2 Samuel 1:23 - "Saul and Jonathan were beloved and pleasant in their lives, and in their death they were not divided; they were swifter than eagles, they were stronger than lions."

• Proverbs 16:28 - "A perverse man sows strife, and a whisperer separates the best of friends."

• Proverbs 17:9 - "He who covers a transgression seeks love, but he who repeats a matter separates friends."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Greek translation of the Old Testament (LXX) translates parad in Ruth 1:17 using the verb diachorizo, which means to sever, part, or separate entirely.

• This stands in sharp contrast to Orpah, who "kissed her mother-in-law" and turned back to her people and her gods (Ruth 1:14). Orpah allowed the natural difficulties of life to part her from Naomi, whereas Ruth’s love clung to her, refusing any separation short of the grave.
