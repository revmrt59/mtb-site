BURIED - ( qabar , Strong's H6912 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:17, the Hebrew word qabar (translated "buried") represents far more than the physical act of interment; it signifies a permanent, covenantal commitment of lifelong loyalty. By declaring that she will be buried where Naomi is buried, Ruth binds her destiny, her identity, and her final resting place to Naomi and the God of Israel, completely severing her ties to her homeland of Moab.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, burial was a matter of supreme theological and familial importance. To be buried in one's ancestral land, alongside one’s ancestors, was the ultimate expression of belonging, peace, and identity. Conversely, to be buried in a foreign land, or worse, to remain unburied, was viewed as a curse and a sign of divine abandonment. When Ruth declares her desire to be buried alongside Naomi, she is not merely making a sentimental statement about her funeral arrangements. She is performing a profound act of self-renunciation. She voluntarily forfeits her right to be buried with her own ancestors in Moab, choosing instead to lie forever in the soil of Israel, a land where she was technically an outsider.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew verb qabar means to bury, inter, or place a deceased body into a grave, tomb, or sepulcher. In the passive stem (Niphal) used in Ruth 1:17 ('eqqaber), it means "I will be buried" or "to be laid to rest." The word refers to the physical finality of death and the subsequent act of committing the body to the earth. It denotes completion, the sealing of a life's journey, and the permanent occupation of a physical space in death.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Hebrew Bible, qabar is used to mark the transition from active life to historical legacy. It is frequently associated with the patriarchs and matriarchs, whose burials in the cave of Machpelah served as a physical claim of faith in God's promises.

• The act of burial is a picture of honor, dignity, and covenant preservation.

• It stands in stark contrast to being left as refuse on the ground, which symbolized judgment.

• To share a burial site with someone was a physical manifestation of undivided loyalty and shared destiny. It pictured two lives so completely intertwined that even the dust of their bones would mingle in the same soil.

4. Theological Implications

Ruth’s vow to be buried with Naomi is the ultimate expression of her conversion. In the ancient world, gods were deeply tied to specific lands. By choosing Israel as her permanent resting place, Ruth declares that Naomi’s God, Yahweh, is her God not only in life but also in death. She trusts her physical remains to the soil of Yahweh's covenant land, demonstrating a faith that looks beyond the grave. Her choice reflects the covenantal concept of hesed—a steadfast, loyal-love that does not fail, even when circumstances are bleak, and which endures beyond the span of a single lifetime.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:17, the word "buried" serves as the climax of Ruth's famous staircase vow of loyalty. She begins with companionship ("where you go, I will go"), moves to domestic life ("where you lodge, I will lodge"), progresses to social and spiritual alignment ("your people shall be my people, and your God, my God"), and concludes with physical death ("where you die, I will die, and there will I be buried"). By ending with her burial, Ruth leaves no loophole. There is no return to Moab after Naomi passes away. Ruth's commitment is irreversible; she is bound to Naomi and her God for the rest of her life, and her very bones will remain in Israel as a lasting testament to that commitment.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The grammar of Ruth's vow includes a self-maledictory oath: "The LORD do so to me, and more also, if anything but death parts you and me." By invoking the covenant name of God (Yahweh) and sealing it with the promise of her burial, Ruth legally and spiritually binds herself to Israel. This contrasts sharply with Orpah, who kissed Naomi and returned "to her people and to her gods" (Ruth 1:15). Ruth’s willingness to be buried in Israel shows that her conversion was absolute; she did not look back to her old life, but forward to a new inheritance among God’s people.
