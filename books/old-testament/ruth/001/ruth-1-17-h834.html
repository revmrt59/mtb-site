WHERE - ( 'asher , Strong's H834 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:17, the English word "where" translates the Hebrew relative pronoun 'asher prefixed with the preposition be (ba'asher), functioning as a locative relative adverb meaning "in the place that" or "wherever." In the context of Ruth's famous vow to Naomi, this word signifies more than mere geographical coordinates; it expresses an absolute, unbreakable covenantal solidarity. By binding her own ultimate fate—her death and burial—to the exact location of Naomi's demise, Ruth relinquishes her homeland, her identity, and her former gods, choosing complete identification with Naomi and the God of Israel.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word 'asher is the primary Hebrew relative pronoun, equivalent to "who," "which," "that," or "what." On its own, it functions as a grammatical bridge connecting clauses. However, when prefixed with the preposition be (meaning "in" or "at"), it becomes ba'asher, transitioning from a simple relative pronoun to a specific locative indicator: "in the place where" or "wherever."

• In the Ancient Near East, a person's identity, security, and spiritual destiny were inextricably tied to their native soil and local deities. To leave one's land was to abandon one's gods. By utilizing ba'asher to designate the place of her death and burial, Ruth is not merely choosing a travel destination; she is committing to a total, life-ending relocation that redefines her entire existence, securing her loyalty to Naomi's family and God even beyond the grave.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The relative pronoun 'asher functions as an indeclinable particle of relation.

• When combined with the preposition be, the construction ba'asher carries the specific spatial meaning of "in the place which," "where," or "wherever."

• The semantic range includes introducing relative clauses of place, time, or manner, depending on the prefixed preposition. In its locative sense (ba'asher), it pinpoints a concrete, physical reality rather than an abstract concept.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The use of ba'asher in Ruth 1:17 acts as a grammatical anchor of devotion. It occurs twice in this short speech (v. 16: "wherever ['asher] you go"; v. 17: "where [ba'asher] you die").

• The first use in verse 16 ('asher) governs movement and life: "Wherever you go, I will go."

• The second use in verse 17 (ba'asher) governs stillness and death: "Where you die, I will die, and there will I be buried."

• This progression creates a complete word picture of lifelong companionability. It spans from active daily journeying to the finality of the grave. Burial in antiquity was a sacred act of tribal and familial identification; being buried "where" Naomi is buried means Ruth permanently joins Naomi’s family, forfeiting her right to be returned to her Moabite ancestors.

4. Theological Implications

• Covenant Loyalty (Hesed): The use of ba'asher highlights the physical, earthy nature of biblical hesed (lovingkindness/loyalty). Ruth's loyalty is not a sentiment felt from a distance; it is a physical presence that goes "where" Naomi goes and stays "where" Naomi dies.

• Conversion and Faith: In the ancient mind, changing one's "where" meant changing one's god (Ruth 1:15-16). By choosing Naomi’s "where," Ruth makes a radical theological confession, turning away from Chemosh of Moab to take refuge under the wings of Yahweh, the God of Israel.

• Inclusion of the Gentiles: Ruth's determination to die and be buried in the land of Israel foreshadows the inclusion of Gentiles into the covenant family of God. Her physical placement in Israel's soil positions her directly in the lineage of King David and Jesus Christ.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:17, the term ba'asher is contrasted with "there" (sham): "Where [ba'asher] you die, I will die, and there [sham] will I be buried." This grammatical parallel creates an airtight seal on Ruth's promise.

• The immediate context is Orpah's departure. Orpah kissed Naomi goodbye and returned to her people and her gods. Ruth, however, clings to Naomi. The word "where" serves as the dividing line between temporary affection (Orpah) and covenantal commitment (Ruth). Ruth's vow is unconditional; she does not ask where Naomi is going, what the living conditions will be, or if there is a future for them. The physical location of Naomi's life and death is sufficient for her.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 21:17: "And God heard the voice of the lad. Then the angel of God called to Hagar out of heaven, and said to her, 'What ails you, Hagar? Fear not, for God has heard the voice of the lad where [ba'asher] he is.'"

• Judges 17:9: "And Micah said to him, 'Where do you come from?' So he said to him, 'I am a Levite from Bethlehem in Judah, and I am on my way to find a place to stay wherever [ba'asher] I can.'"

• 2 Kings 8:1: "Then Elisha spoke to the woman whose son he had restored to life, saying, 'Arise and go, you and your household, and stay wherever [ba'asher] you can stay; for the LORD has called for a famine, and it will also come upon the land for seven years.'"

• Ecclesiastes 8:4: "Where [ba'asher] the word of a king is, there is power; and who may say to him, 'What are you doing?'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The construct of ba'asher shows that biblical faith is never merely intellectual; it is deeply incarnational. It matters "where" one stands.

• The Greek Septuagint (LXX) translates ba'asher in Ruth 1:17 using the relative adverb hou, meaning "where" or "in which place." This reinforces the direct, uncomplicated spatial transfer of loyalty from Moab to Israel.

• In Hebrew thought, to be buried in a foreign land was a tragedy, a sign of exile and curse. By choosing to die and be buried "where" Naomi dies, Ruth willingly accepts exile from her native Moab to find her true spiritual home in Israel.
