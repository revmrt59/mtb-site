SPEAKING - ( dabar , Strong's H1696 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb dabar refers to the act of speaking, conversing, or communicating a message. In Ruth 1:18, it describes Naomi’s cessation of her verbal efforts to persuade Ruth to return to Moab. Seeing Ruth’s unyielding determination, Naomi stops her pleading and arguments. This transition from urgent verbal persuasion to respectful silence marks a pivotal moment of acceptance, paving the way for their shared journey of faith to Bethlehem.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew thought, words are not merely empty sounds or abstract ideas; they are concrete realities. The root noun form (dabar) can mean both "word" and "thing" or "matter." Consequently, the verb dabar carries a sense of substance and power. To speak is to bring something of weight into existence. While another common Hebrew verb for speaking, amar, focuses primarily on the specific content or the direct quotation of what is said, dabar emphasizes the act, process, and relationship of communication. It is the verbal output that establishes a connection, issues a command, or exerts influence between two parties.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb dabar occurs over 1,100 times in the Hebrew Bible, primarily in the intensive Piel stem (dibber).

• It denotes the physical and relational act of talking, conversing, or communicating with someone.

• It expresses various nuances depending on the preposition that follows, such as speaking to (el), speaking with (im), or speaking against (be).

• In administrative or covenantal contexts, it refers to declaring decrees, pronouncing judgments, or making promises.

• In the context of Ruth 1:18, the infinitive form ledabber combined with the verb hadal ("to cease" or "to stop") means to refrain from further verbal persuasion, arguments, or attempts to influence.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Substance of Speech: Because dabar relates to bringing about a state of affairs, speaking is often depicted as an action of laying down a pathway or ordering one's steps.

• Divine Speech: When God speaks (dabar), His words carry creative and covenantal force. His speaking is synonymous with His doing; His spoken word cannot fail.

• Human Persuasion: In interpersonal relationships, dabar is used for heart-to-heart communication (speaking "to the heart," al-leb), counseling, or, as in the book of Ruth, intense verbal pleading to influence another's destiny.

4. Theological Implications

The use of dabar highlights the boundary of human persuasion in the economy of God's grace. Naomi used her words (dabar) to test, warn, and protect her daughters-in-law, speaking from a place of deep grief and human logic. However, human speech reaches its limit when confronting a divinely inspired covenantal commitment. Ruth's vow was a powerful declaration of faith that transcended Naomi's logical arguments. Naomi's subsequent silence demonstrates that there comes a point where human counsel must yield to the sovereign work of God in an individual's heart. Silence becomes the only appropriate response to a faith that is clearly orchestrated by God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:8–17, Naomi had been speaking persistently, deploying three distinct arguments to urge Orpah and Ruth to turn back to Moab. She spoke of her empty womb, her lack of a future husband, and the hand of the Lord that had gone out against her. Her words were heavy with grief, designed to break their resolve for their own benefit. While Orpah succumbed to this verbal pressure, Ruth resisted it with her magnificent confession of faith.

In verse 18, when Naomi saw that she was determined to go with her, she stopped speaking to her. The cessation of dabar is not an act of cold rejection or angry silence. Rather, it is a moment of quiet surrender. Naomi recognizes that her words of discouragement have no more power against Ruth's ironclad resolve. The "speaking" ends because the matter is settled; Ruth's covenantal love (hesed) has silenced Naomi's bitter grief.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:4: "So Abram departed as the LORD had spoken to him, and Lot went with him."

• Exodus 4:10: "Then Moses said to the LORD, 'O my Lord, I am not eloquent, neither before nor since You have spoken to Your servant...'"

• Deuteronomy 6:7: "You shall teach them diligently to your children, and shall talk of them when you sit in your house, when you walk by the way, when you lie down, and when you rise up."

• Joshua 23:14: "And you know in all your hearts and in all your souls that not one thing has failed of all the good things which the LORD your God spoke concerning you."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The grammatical construction in Ruth 1:18 is wattihdal ledabber eleha (literally, "and she ceased to speak to her"). The verb hadal (to cease/leave off) paired with dabar shows a complete halt of a continuous action. This implies that Naomi's verbal campaign to send Ruth back was energetic and relentless until this precise moment. This sudden silence highlights the profound weight of Ruth's commitment. It also illustrates a valuable pastoral principle: there is "a time to keep silence, and a time to speak" (Ecclesiastes 3:7). When God is clearly at work drawing someone to Himself, further human debate must cease.
