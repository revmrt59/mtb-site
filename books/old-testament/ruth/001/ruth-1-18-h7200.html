SAW - ( ra'ah , Strong's H7200 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb ra'ah means to see, perceive, observe, or understand. In Ruth 1:18, it describes Naomi recognizing and coming to terms with Ruth's unshakable resolve to accompany her to Bethlehem. More than merely registering optical input, Naomi's "seeing" represents an inner perception and realization of Ruth's complete devotion. This profound realization causes Naomi to cease her attempts to persuade Ruth to return to Moab, marking a pivotal transition in their relationship and journey.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root ra'ah is the primary Hebrew verb for physical sight, occurring over 1,300 times in the Old Testament. It spans a broad semantic range from simple optical observation to deep spiritual insight, perception, and even prophetic vision. In the ancient Near Eastern context, seeing was closely linked with knowing and experiencing; to see something was to encounter its reality firsthand. It often carries the sense of looking with attention, inspecting, experiencing, or recognizing a profound truth.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The basic lexical definition of ra'ah is to see, look at, inspect, perceive, understand, or consider. When used of mental perception, it means to realize, discern, or comprehend. In the Qal stem, which is used in Ruth 1:18, it denotes the active apprehension of a situation. It is the sensory realization of an objective truth that results in a change of posture or action by the observer.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical sight: Used of God seeing the light at creation (Genesis 1:4).

• Inspection: God coming down to observe the tower of Babel (Genesis 11:5) or inspecting human sin (Genesis 18:21).

• Experiencing: To "see" life or "see" death (Psalm 89:48), indicating full participation in those states.

• Perception and Insight: To "see" wisdom (Ecclesiastes 1:16) or perceive a moral reality.

• Provision: Abraham declaring that God will "provide" (literally, "see to it" or "be seen") on the mount of the Lord (Genesis 22:14).

4. Theological Implications

In the Old Testament, sight is deeply tied to understanding, faith, and divine action. When God "sees" human affliction, He acts to redeem (Exodus 3:7). Conversely, when humans "see" what God is doing, it leads to faith and obedience. Correct seeing aligns one's perception with divine reality. Naomi's ability to "see" Ruth's determination reflects a transition from her self-absorbed grief ("the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me") to an acknowledgment of the loyal covenant love (chesed) physically standing right in front of her.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:18, Naomi "saw" (ra'ah) that Ruth was determined (steadfastly minded) to go with her. Ruth had just made her magnificent covenant declaration ("Your people shall be my people, and your God, my God"). Naomi's seeing is not a casual glance; it is the moment she realizes Ruth's mind is fully made up and cannot be changed by further discouragement. Naomi recognizes that Ruth's commitment is not a fleeting emotional reaction but a permanent, covenantal decision. This perception brings an immediate end to Naomi's insistence that Ruth return to Moab. It marks a turning point in their journey where Naomi yields to Ruth’s steadfast love.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:4: "And God saw the light, that it was good; and God divided the light from the darkness."

• Exodus 3:7: "And the Lord said: 'I have surely seen the oppression of My people who are in Egypt, and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters, for I know their sorrows.'"

• 1 Samuel 16:7: "But the Lord said to Samuel, 'Do not look at his appearance or at his physical stature, because I have refused him. For the Lord does not see as man sees; for man looks at the outward appearance, but the Lord looks at the heart.'"

• Psalm 34:8: "Oh, taste and see that the Lord is good; Blessed is the man who trusts in Him!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Greek equivalent in the Septuagint (LXX) for ra'ah in Ruth 1:18 is eidon (from horao), which also carries the double meaning of physical sight and mental/spiritual comprehension. There is a literary irony in the Book of Ruth: Naomi returns "blinded" by her bitterness, claiming she went out full and returned empty, yet here she is forced to "see" the immense blessing of Ruth's devotion. It is the first step toward Naomi's eyes being opened to God's silent provision.
