BETHLEHEM - ( Bet Lechem , Strong's H1035 )

Summary:

Bethlehem, literally meaning "House of Bread," is a small but spiritually monumental city located in the hill country of Judah. In the book of Ruth, it serves as both the geographical setting and the theological anchor of the narrative. It represents the place of God's covenant provision, the ancestral soil of Davidic kingship, and the prophetic birthplace of the Messiah. The movement of the characters in Ruth is defined by their tragic departure from and eventual, grace-filled return to this "House of Bread," illustrating God's sovereign capacity to restore His people from famine to absolute fullness.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The name Bethlehem is a compound of two common Hebrew words: bayit (meaning "house" or "place") and lechem (meaning "bread," "food," or "grain"). Situated approximately five miles south of Jerusalem, Bethlehem was originally known as Ephrath or Ephrathah, a term associated with fruitfulness. The region is characterized by fertile valleys, terraced hillsides, and abundant wheat and barley fields, making the name "House of Bread" an accurate description of its agricultural identity. It was a pastoral and farming community, deeply dependent on seasonal rains and God's covenant blessings for its survival.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Bethlehem is a proper noun referring primarily to Bethlehem-Judah (to distinguish it from a northern town of the same name in the territory of Zebulun). The lexical meaning is strictly "House of Bread." The root l-ch-m in ancient Semitic languages occasionally carries the connotation of "fighting" or "war" (as in the Arabic lahm, meaning meat), but in the biblical Hebrew context, it universally denotes bread as the basic staple of physical life. Thus, Bethlehem is defined as the place of life-giving sustenance and divine provision.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The name Bethlehem functions as a powerful literary and spiritual word picture in the Old Testament:

• The Irony of Famine: The narrative of Ruth begins with a devastating irony—a famine in the "House of Bread" (Ruth 1:1). This indicates a spiritual and physical breakdown in the land of Israel during the period of the Judges, where the place designated for abundance becomes barren due to the nation's covenant unfaithfulness.

• The Place of Return: Throughout the first chapter of Ruth, Bethlehem represents the place of repentance and restoration. To return to Bethlehem is to return to the sphere of God's presence, blessing, and community.

• The Source of Kingship: Bethlehem is established as the home of Jesse and the birthplace of David, setting up a historical and theological trajectory where the ultimate ruler of Israel emerges from a humble agricultural village.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Bethlehem is the stage where God demonstrates His providence, redemption, and covenant loyalty (chesed).

• Divine Provision: The return of Naomi and Ruth to Bethlehem coincides with the beginning of the barley harvest, showing that God has indeed "visited His people in giving them bread" (Ruth 1:6).

• The Line of the Messiah: The redemption of Ruth by Boaz in Bethlehem secures the genealogical line that leads directly to King David and, ultimately, to Jesus Christ.

• Prophetic Fulfillment: Bethlehem's smallness in comparison to the major cities of Judah highlights God's pattern of choosing the weak and humble to confound the mighty. This culminates in the birth of Jesus Christ, the true "Bread of Life" (John 6:35), who came down from heaven and was born in the physical "House of Bread" to offer spiritual sustenance to a starving world.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:19, the text states, "Now the two of them went until they came to Bethlehem. And it happened, when they had come to Bethlehem, that all the city was excited because of them."

• The repetition of "Bethlehem" twice in this single verse emphasizes the arrival at the destination of hope.

• The phrase "all the city was excited" (from the Hebrew root hum, meaning to stir up, make a noise, or be in an uproar) highlights the tight-knit, covenantal nature of the Bethlehem community. The return of Naomi—empty, bitter, and accompanied by a Moabite foreigner—creates a massive stir among the townsfolk.

• In this context, Bethlehem represents the mirror of Naomi's past and the stage for her future restoration. It is the community that will witness her transformation from "Mara" (bitter) back to "Naomi" (pleasant) through the redeeming work of Boaz.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

For preaching and teaching, Bethlehem serves as a powerful illustration of the danger of leaving the place of God’s appointment. Elimelech left the "House of Bread" for the fields of Moab during a trial, only to find death and emptiness. Naomi's restoration only began when she turned her face back toward Bethlehem. This teaches believers that even when the "House of Bread" seems empty due to temporary trials, it is far better to remain under God's covenant care than to seek relief in the pagan lands of compromise. God's provision always returns to Bethlehem.
