HAPPENED - ( hayah , Strong's H1961 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb hayah is a foundational covenantal and narrative word, most fundamentally meaning "to be," "to become," or "to exist." In historical-narrative contexts like Ruth 1:19, it functions in the narrative tense as wayehi ("and it happened" or "and it came to pass"), serving as a literary hinge that transitions the reader into a significant event. Rather than denoting mere random chance, this word in the biblical narrative emphasizes the unfolding of God's active providence in historical reality. In the context of Ruth's arrival in Bethlehem, it marks a pivotal transition from Naomi's bitter past of loss in Moab to the unfolding of redemption in the Promised Land.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verb hayah is not a static verb of being, like those often found in Western philosophy, but a dynamic verb of activity, presence, and historical manifestation. In Hebrew thought, to "be" is to act, to show oneself, or to come onto the scene of history. The root does not merely describe existence in an abstract sense; it indicates a concrete occurrence or the assumption of a new state or role.

In narrative literature, when hayah is used in the historical tense (wayehi), it serves as a structural signpost. It signals to the reader that the stage is set, a transition is occurring, and the next event is of great significance. It acts as a divine pointer, highlighting that what "happened" is a concrete link in the chain of history.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of hayah is broad but integrated, encompassing several primary categories of meaning:

• To Exist or Be Present: To have historical reality or to stand in a particular relation to someone or something.

• To Become: To change state, status, or identity, often followed by the preposition le (e.g., "to become a nation").

• To Happen, Occur, or Take Place: To fall out in history, to come to pass, or to arise.

In Ruth 1:19, the verb is used in this third sense of occurrence. It describes the historical event of Naomi and Ruth's arrival in Bethlehem and the immediate, public reaction it provoked. It denotes a real-time event breaking into human history.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, hayah is used to draw a straight line between the decree of God and the reality of history. The most famous word picture of this verb occurs in the creation account, where God speaks and the reality "is" or "becomes" (Genesis 1:3).

• The Command and the Reality: When God speaks, hayah is the immediate result. This establishes that what "happens" in the universe is a direct reflection of divine authority.

• The Structural Pivot: In historical narratives, "and it happened" (wayehi) serves as a camera transition, moving the focus of the story to a new scene or a critical development. It acts as an anchor for the listener, signaling that a major plot point is unfolding.

### 4. Theological Implications

The verb hayah carries immense theological weight because it is etymologically and conceptually linked to the sacred covenant name of God, Yahweh (from the root hawah, closely related to hayah). When God reveals His name to Moses in Exodus 3:14, He uses this verb: "I AM WHO I AM" (Ehyeh Asher Ehyeh).

This link carries profound implications:

• Sovereignty Over "Happenings": Because Yahweh is the self-existent, active Creator who causes all things to be, nothing "happens" by blind fate, luck, or chaotic chance. Every occurrence (hayah) is ultimately held within the hand of Him who is (hayah).

• Covenant Presence: The verb frequently appears in the covenant promise, "I will be (hayah) your God, and you shall be (hayah) My people." The very existence and identity of Israel are tied to this verb of active, relational being.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:19, Naomi returns to Bethlehem empty, grieving, and destitute, accompanied by her Moabite daughter-in-law. When they arrive, the text states, "And it happened, when they had come to Bethlehem, that all the city was excited because of them."

The contextual nuances of hayah in this passage are rich:

• Divine Alignment: The "happening" of their arrival coincides perfectly with the beginning of the barley harvest (Ruth 1:22). The timing is not accidental. The narrative marker wayehi ("And it happened") alerts the reader that Naomi’s arrival is the starting point of God's restoration process.

• A Public Stir: The verb introduces the immediate, dramatic shift in Naomi's situation. After years of silent suffering in Moab, her arrival "happens" with a public uproar. God is bringing her private grief into the public sphere of Bethlehem, where community redemption can take place.

• Providence Over Fate: While Naomi believes the Almighty has dealt bitterly with her, the word hayah quietly reminds the reader that her steps are being directed. Her arrival in Bethlehem is the exact historical junction where God's covenant loyalty (chesed) will begin to manifest.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3: "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light."

• Genesis 12:2: "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you and make your name great; and you shall be a blessing."

• Genesis 15:1: "After these things the word of the LORD came to Abram in a vision, saying..."

• Joshua 1:1: "Now it came to pass after the death of Moses the servant of the LORD that the LORD spoke to Joshua..."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• Contrast with Pagan Fate: Unlike the Greek concept of fate (moira), which was an impersonal, uncaring force to which even the gods were subject, the Hebrew hayah positions every historical event under the personal oversight of a loving, covenant-keeping God.

• Pastoral Application: For the believer, there are no throwaway moments or random occurrences. The things that "happen" in our lives—even the arrivals that feel bitter or empty, like Naomi's return—are part of a larger historical canvas that God is painting. Our "happenings" are safe in the hands of the Great "I AM."
