ALL - ( kol , Strong's H3605 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun kol signifies totality, the entirety, or the whole of a designated group, quantity, or period. Derived from a root meaning "to complete" or "to make perfect," it serves as a sweeping grammatical tool to indicate that nothing of the category under discussion is excluded. In Ruth 1:19, when "all the city" is stirred by Naomi's return, the word functions as a collective hyperbole, illustrating the deep, communal impact of her tragedy and homecoming upon the entire population of Bethlehem.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun kol originates from the root verb kalal, which means to complete, perfect, make whole, or crown. This root carries the imagery of assembling scattered elements into a singular, cohesive, and finished unit. In the ancient Hebrew mindset, a community, family, or city was not merely a collection of isolated individuals but an organic, interconnected whole. Therefore, when kol modifies a group or location, it paints a picture of a unified body reacting or moving in complete solidarity, leaving no part unaffected.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, kol expresses totality and is translated as "all," "every," "the whole," "any," or "each." Its specific meaning is determined by the grammatical structure of the sentence:

• When followed by a singular definite noun (sharing the definite article "the"), it means "the whole of" or "all of" (e.g., "all the city," "the whole land").

• When followed by a singular indefinite noun, it means "every" or "each" (e.g., "every man," "every tree").

• When followed by a plural noun, it means "all" (e.g., "all the people," "all things").

In Ruth 1:19, kol precedes the singular definite noun ha-ir ("the city"), meaning "the whole of the city" or "all the city."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

As one of the most frequently used words in the Hebrew Bible, kol functions as an expansive brushstroke. It is used to define the boundaries of covenants, the extent of God's creation, and the absolute nature of His commands.

• It is used to describe the absolute scope of God's ownership: "all the earth is Mine."

• It is used to denote the complete span of a person's life or experience: "all the days of my life."

• In historical narratives, it often serves as a rhetorical device to show widespread consensus or emotional intensity. By stating that "all" the people wept or "all" the city was moved, the text emphasizes a shared national or civic experience that transcends individual differences.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, kol highlights the absolute demands of God's covenant and the holistic nature of the community He forms. God does not demand partial obedience; He demands love with "all" the heart, soul, and strength.

Furthermore, in the book of Ruth, kol underscores the communal accountability of God's people. Naomi's departure during a famine and her subsequent return are not isolated family matters. The covenant community of Bethlehem suffers together, hungers together, and is restored together. The use of kol reveals that in God's economy, the suffering of one member is felt by the entire body, and the restoration of one is a matter of interest for the whole assembly.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:19, the text states, "And it happened, when they had come to Bethlehem, that all the city was excited because of them."

• Naomi had left Bethlehem years prior with a husband and two sons—wealthy in family, though fleeing famine. She returns destitute, widowed, childless, and accompanied only by a foreign daughter-in-law.

• The phrase "all the city" indicates that Naomi’s family was well-known, and her sudden, tragic return was a matter of intense public conversation.

• The word "excited" (Hebrew hum) conveys a noisy stir or commotion. By coupling kol with this verb, the narrator captures the sudden buzzing of voices at the city gates and in the streets. The whole town is caught up in a wave of shock, recognition, and sympathy, prompting the women of the city to ask, "Is this Naomi?"

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 6:5: "You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength."

• Psalm 103:19: "The LORD has established His throne in heaven, And His kingdom rules over all."

• Isaiah 53:6: "All we like sheep have gone astray; We have turned, every one, to his own way; And the LORD has laid on Him the iniquity of us all."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The emphasis on "all the city" in Ruth 1:19 sets up a structural and thematic bookend for the end of the narrative. While chapter 1 opens with "all the city" stirred by Naomi's grief and emptiness, chapter 4 closes with Boaz redeeming Ruth before "all the people" at the gate (Ruth 4:9, 11). The public sorrow of Naomi's return is answered by a public celebration of redemption, showing that God's restoration of the individual ultimately blesses the entire community.
