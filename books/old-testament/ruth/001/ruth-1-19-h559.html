SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:19, the word "said" translates the Hebrew verb 'amar, a primary term denoting verbal expression, declaration, or internal thought. In this specific passage, the word is conjugated as a feminine plural verb, capturing the collective voice of the women of Bethlehem as they cry out in astonished recognition of Naomi's return. The word highlights the communal shock of Naomi’s physical and emotional transformation from a blessed matriarch to a destitute, grieving widow, setting the stage for her raw expression of sorrow and the unfolding narrative of redemption.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew verb 'amar is one of the most frequently used words in the Old Testament, occurring over 5,000 times. At its core, it represents the externalization of internal thought, acting as the primary medium of communication, relationship, and revelation. In ancient Near Eastern oral culture, speaking was not merely the transmission of cognitive data but an event that established reality, initiated action, or expressed deep-seated communal emotion. To say something was to put it into motion. In the context of a small, close-knit agricultural community like Bethlehem, the spoken word served as the primary keeper of history, reputation, and social covenant.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of 'amar is "to say," "to speak," "to utter," or "to declare." Depending on the grammatical context, its semantic range includes:

• To speak or converse: The basic exchange of words between individuals.

• To command or decree: Used of kings, authorities, or God when issuing an order.

• To think or intend: Often rendered as "to say in one's heart," representing internal monologue or planning.

• To promise or designate: Declaring an intention that binds the speaker to a future action.

In the Qal stem, which is used in Ruth 1:19, the verb carries the simple, active sense of speaking. Because it is conjugated as a feminine plural (wa-to'marna), it specifically denotes a group of women speaking together.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament utilizes 'amar to depict several distinct types of communication:

• The Creative Voice of God: The word is famously used in the creation narrative where God's spoken word brings physical reality out of nothingness. His speech carries inherent creative power.

• The Internal Monologue: When a character "says in his heart," 'amar describes the secret thoughts, fears, or schemes of a person before they manifest in outward action.

• The Communal Chorus: In narrative literature, when a group "says" something in unison, it represents a shared cultural or emotional consensus. In Ruth 1:19, this collective speech functions like a Greek chorus, voicing the collective conscience, memory, and emotional state of the entire village.

### 4. Theological Implications

Speech in the Old Testament reflects the image of a communicating God. Humans use speech to form covenants, express grief, and bear witness to God's providence. In the book of Ruth, speech marks pivotal shifts in the narrative—moving from silent suffering to vocal lament, and ultimately to praise. The women’s question, "Is this Naomi?" sets the stage for Naomi's raw lament about her emptiness. It demonstrates how human words can mirror the heavy realities of a broken world while remaining under the silent, governing sovereignty of God, who will ultimately change the conversation of these same women from mourning to blessing by the end of the book.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:19, the verb 'amar carries a tone of bewildered astonishment. Ten years prior, Naomi ("Pleasant") had left Bethlehem with a husband and two sons, representing wealth, status, and security. She returns as a destitute, childless widow accompanied only by a foreign Moabite woman. The collective exclamation of the women—"Is this Naomi?"—is not a simple request for identification. It is an expression of shock at how severely life and grief have ravaged her. The verb here acts as a narrative mirror, reflecting the stark contrast between Naomi’s past and her present, forcing her to confront her pain and prompting her to reply, "Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Frame of the Book: The use of 'amar by the women of Bethlehem creates a literary bracket for Naomi’s journey. In Ruth 1:19, they speak in shocked grief ("Is this Naomi?"). In Ruth 4:14, the same group of women speak in jubilant praise ("Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you this day without a close relative!").

• A Community of Women: The feminine plural form of the verb highlights the gendered spaces of ancient Israel. It was the women of the village who greeted Naomi, recognized her pain, and ultimately validated her restoration at the birth of Obed.
