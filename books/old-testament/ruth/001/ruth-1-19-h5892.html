CITY - ( ir , Strong's H5892 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:19, the word ir ("city") refers to Bethlehem, portraying it not merely as a geographic location or a collection of physical buildings, but as a tight-knit, living community of people. When Naomi and Ruth arrive, the entire "city" is stirred, highlighting the deep relational ties, the shared history, and the communal accountability of God's covenant people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, a "city" (ir) was distinct from an open village or encampment primarily by having some form of defense, such as a wall, watchtower, or natural barrier, alongside a centralized social structure. It was a place of permanent habitation, safety, and commerce. Bethlehem, though historically small, is designated as an ir, emphasizing its status as an established settlement with its own elders, gates, and community life. The word conjures an image of a protective enclosure where lives are deeply intertwined, and where news travels fast because of physical proximity.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew noun ir (plural arim) refers to a town, city, or inhabited place. The semantic range spans from small, fortified encampments to sprawling urban centers. It denotes an organized community characterized by permanent dwellings. Unlike open fields or temporary camps, an ir possesses social organization, often centered around a gate where legal and civil transactions occur. Frequently, the word is used metonymically to represent the inhabitants of the city rather than the physical structures.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, ir is used in several vivid ways:

• A Place of Refuge and Defense: Strongholds and walled cities represented safety from enemies and wild animals.

• The Inhabitants: Often, the "city" is personified or used to describe the collective response of its citizens (e.g., a city mourning, rejoicing, or trembling).

• The City of God: Jerusalem is frequently called the "city of the Lord" or the "holy city," symbolizing the dwelling place of God among His people.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of the ir reflects God's design for human community. God does not merely relate to individuals in isolation; He relates to communities. In the context of Israel, the city was the locus of covenant life, where the Law of Moses was to be lived out corporately. The social fabric of Bethlehem—with its shared grief, agricultural cycles, and legal responsibilities (such as the kinsman-redeemer provision)—demonstrates how God uses the structures of human community to accomplish His redemptive purposes.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:19, the "city" is Bethlehem. When Naomi returns after ten years in Moab, having lost her husband and sons, her arrival causes an immediate, collective stir. The text states that "all the city was excited." This indicates that Bethlehem was small enough that everyone knew Naomi, yet large enough to have a distinct public identity. The physical layout of the ancient city, where houses were clustered together and daily life occurred in public spaces, meant Naomi's return could not be hidden. The reaction of the "city" sets up the communal backdrop for the rest of the narrative, where the townspeople and elders will witness and ratify Ruth and Boaz's redemption story.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 4:17: "And he built a city, and called the name of the city after the name of his son—Enoch."

• Joshua 6:3: "You shall march around the city, all you men of war; you shall go all around the city once. This you shall do six days."

• 1 Samuel 16:4: "So Samuel did what the Lord said, and came to Bethlehem. And the elders of the town trembled at his coming, and said, 'Do you come peaceably?'"

• 2 Samuel 5:7: "Nevertheless David took the stronghold of Zion (that is, the City of David)."

• Psalm 48:1: "Great is the Lord, and greatly to be praised in the city of our God, in His holy mountain."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The Hebrew root of ir is distinct from words for open villages (chatzerim). An ir had a defined boundary, which created a clear distinction between the "inside" (the community, safety, and covenant life) and the "outside" (the wild, fields, and foreign lands). For Naomi, leaving the ir of Bethlehem for Moab was a departure from safety and community. Returning to the ir represents her return to the sphere of God's covenant blessings and the protective care of His people.
