COME - ( bo' , Strong's H0935 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb bo' is a foundational and highly versatile word in the Old Testament, primarily meaning "to go in," "to enter," or "to come." In the context of Ruth 1:19, it marks a monumental transition from Moab—the land of tragic exile and spiritual compromise—back to Bethlehem, the "House of Bread." This arrival is not merely a geographic relocation but a profound homecoming, representing a return to covenant territory, the community of faith, and the sphere of God's providential care.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, travel was perilous, long, and fraught with uncertainty. To "come" to a city, especially after a journey across rugged terrain and deep ravines like those separating Moab from Judah, was a physical relief and a significant milestone. The word bo' evokes the image of crossing a threshold, leaving behind the open, dangerous road to enter the safety, shelter, and community of a walled town or home. In the ancient mind, entering a city gate was an entry into a distinct sphere of authority and relationship. For Naomi and Ruth, coming to Bethlehem meant crossing the boundary from foreign isolation back into the covenant community of Yahweh.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb bo' is a primitive root occurring over 2,500 times in the Hebrew Bible. Its primary semantic range includes:

• To enter, go in (as into a house, city, or tent).

• To come, arrive at a destination.

• To bring in, lead, or introduce (in the causative Hiphil stem).

• To go down or set (referring to the sun).

In Ruth 1:19, the verb is used in the Qal stem (denoting simple active action) in two forms: first as a finite verb ("went until they came") and second as an infinitive construct ("when they were come"). This double usage emphasizes the completion of their journey. They did not just travel toward Bethlehem; they successfully entered and arrived inside its boundaries.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, bo' is used in various rich, figurative ways:

• Relational and Covenantal Entry: The word describes entering into a covenant or an oath (Deuteronomy 29:12). To "come" into a covenant is to bind oneself to another in a formal relationship.

• Liturgical Entry: It is frequently used for entering the sanctuary or coming into the presence of God to worship (Psalm 100:2).

• Life Transitions: It describes the setting of the sun, which serves as a metaphor for the closing of a day or the end of a life.

• Sexual Intimacy: It is used euphemistically to describe physical intimacy and the consummation of marriage ("to go in to" someone).

### 4. Theological Implications

The arrival of Naomi and Ruth in Bethlehem marks a critical theological turning point in the narrative of redemption:

• The Geometry of Repentance: True repentance (shub) is always paired with arrival (bo'). It is not enough to leave Moab; one must come to Bethlehem. Coming to the place of God's promise demonstrates active faith and submission to His covenant.

• The Grace of Inclusion: Ruth, a Moabitess, physically enters Israel. This physical entry is a picture of her spiritual entry into the covenant family of God. Her arrival in Bethlehem is a prophetic foreshadowing of the Gentiles "coming" to the light of Israel's God.

• Providentially Timed Arrival: They arrived at the beginning of the barley harvest (Ruth 1:22). Their coming coincided with God's provision, demonstrating that when God’s people return to Him, they find His grace already at work to meet their needs.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:19, the repetition of bo' is stylistically designed to highlight the gravity of their arrival. "Now the two of them went until they came (bo') to Bethlehem. And it came to pass, when they were come (bo') to Bethlehem..."

This repetition emphasizes that despite the grief, bitterness, and loss experienced in Moab, God sustained them to reach their destination.

Furthermore, their arrival immediately agitates the city. The text states that "all the city was excited because of them." The "coming" of these two widows—one a broken Judean matriarch who left full and returned empty, and the other a foreign convert—forces the community to confront the reality of Naomi's suffering and the remarkable loyalty of Ruth. It transitions the story from private grief to public, communal redemption.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 7:1: "Then the LORD said to Noah, 'Come into the ark, you and all your household, because I have seen that you are righteous before Me in this generation.'"

• Joshua 24:11: "And you went over the Jordan and came to Jericho. And the men of Jericho fought against you..."

• Psalm 24:7: "Lift up your heads, O you gates! And be lifted up, you everlasting doors! And the King of glory shall come in."

• Psalm 100:2: "Serve the LORD with gladness; Come before His presence with singing."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A powerful literary contrast exists between bo' (coming/entering) and yatsa' (going out). In Ruth 1:21, Naomi laments, "I went out (yatsa') full, and the LORD has brought me home (shub) empty." Elimelech's family "went out" from the land of promise during a famine, seeking bread in pagan Moab, only to find death. By "coming" back to Bethlehem, Naomi and Ruth reverse this tragic exit. Though they come back empty-handed, their arrival in the place of God's presence ensures that they will soon be filled.
