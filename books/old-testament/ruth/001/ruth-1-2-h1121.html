SONS - ( ben , Strong's H1121 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ben (plural banim) primarily denotes a male offspring, son, or descendant. In ancient Israel, sons were the essential links for preserving the family name, securing ancestral land inheritance, and ensuring household survival. In the context of Ruth 1:2, the mention of Elimelech and Naomi's "two sons," Mahlon and Chilion, establishes the potential for future legacy and hope, which is tragically shattered when both sons die in Moab, setting the stage for God's dramatic redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The noun ben is derived from the verbal root banah, which means "to build." This etymological connection provides a beautiful word picture: a son is one who "builds" the house. In the ancient Near East, a family was not viewed merely as a collection of individuals, but as a continuous "house" or household. A father built his house by fathering sons, who would in turn carry on the name, cultivate the family land, and pass the inheritance to the next generation. Without a son, a family line would collapse, its name would be blotted out, and its ancestral land would be permanently lost to other clans.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term ben possesses a wide semantic range in the Old Testament, representing several distinct relationships:

• Immediate Male Offspring: A direct physical son born to a father and mother.

• Grandson or Descendant: Successive generations of male descendants (often translated as "children of" or "descendants of," such as the "sons of Israel").

• Member of a Class or Guild: A person belonging to a specific group, profession, or character trait (e.g., "sons of the prophets" or "son of wickedness").

• Age Designation: Used idiomatically to express age (e.g., "a son of ninety years" means ninety years old).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament scriptures, ben serves as a focal point for covenant continuity.

• The Arrow in the Quiver: Sons are described as "arrows in the hand of a warrior" (Psalm 127:4), representing strength, defense, and the projection of the family's influence and heritage into the future.

• The Olive Flank: Sons are likened to "olive plants all around your table" (Psalm 128:3), symbolizing vitality, productivity, and the promise of long-term fruitfulness.

• The Heir: The son is the legal heir. In the patriarchal structure, the eldest son received a double portion of the inheritance to ensure he could support the wider family, especially widowed mothers and unmarried sisters.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the concept of the "son" is central to the unfolding drama of biblical redemption:

• The Seed Promise: God’s covenant with Abraham relied entirely on the provision of a ben (Isaac), through whom the nations would be blessed. This culminates in the ultimate Ben—Jesus Christ, the Son of Abraham, the Son of David, and the unique Son of God.

• Covenant Stewardship: God owns the land of Israel; clans were merely stewards of their designated portions. The ben was the steward-in-training, tasked with keeping the land within the tribe as a testimony of God's faithful provision.

• Fatherly Discipline: God uses the relationship of a father to a ben to describe His covenant relationship with Israel, demonstrating both deep affection and necessary discipline (Deuteronomy 8:5; Proverbs 3:12).

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:2, the narrator carefully details the household of Elimelech: his wife is Naomi, and his "two sons" are Mahlon and Chilion. In the ancient world, Elimelech is considered a wealthy and secure man, despite the famine, because he has two sons. They are the guarantee that the family's portion in Bethlehem of Judah will remain in the family name.

However, the names of these "two sons" hint at the impending tragedy. Mahlon likely means "sickly" or "weakness," and Chilion likely means "pining," "failing," or "consumption." Rather than "building" the house of Elimelech, these two sons represent the fragile and failing nature of human security apart from the land of promise. When Elimelech dies, Naomi’s hope rests entirely on these two sons. When they also die in the fields of Moab (Ruth 1:5), the "house" of Elimelech is completely demolished. Naomi is left empty, highlighting the desperate need for a kinsman-redeemer to raise up a ben to restore the dead man's name.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The cultural pressure to produce a ben explains the ancient practice of levirate marriage (Deuteronomy 25:5-6). If a married man died without a son, his brother was obligated to marry the widow. The firstborn son of this union would legally carry the name of the deceased husband so that "his name may not be blotted out from Israel." This law undergirds the entire legal plot of the book of Ruth, leading to the birth of Obed, who is celebrated by the women of Bethlehem as a "son" born to Naomi (Ruth 4:17).
