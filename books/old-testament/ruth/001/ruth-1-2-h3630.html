CHILION - ( Kilyon , Strong's H3630 )

Summary:

The name Chilion, borne by the younger son of Elimelech and Naomi, means "pining," "wasting away," or "completion." Derived from a root indicating consumption or exhaustion, the name stands as a somber literary and historical marker in the narrative of Ruth, foreshadowing the tragic demise of Elimelech's lineage in the plains of Moab and highlighting the fragility of a family that wanders from the land of promise.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The narrative of Ruth opens during the turbulent era of the Judges, a time marked by spiritual decay, political instability, and physical famine in Israel. In Hebrew culture, names were highly significant, often reflecting the physical condition of a child at birth, the circumstances of the family, or a prophetic anticipation of the child's destiny. The name Chilion, alongside his brother Mahlon, introduces a tragic tone to the opening of the scroll. Rather than embodying strength, endurance, or hope, these names carry connotations of physical vulnerability and short life spans, signaling the impending collapse of the household.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper noun Chilion is derived from the primary Hebrew root verb kalah, which means to be complete, to come to an end, to vanish, to waste away, or to be consumed. The noun form of this root, kilyon, refers to a failing of the eyes, consumption, or destruction. Therefore, the lexical meaning of Chilion is "pining," "frailty," "wasting away," or "completion." It depicts a state where vitality is steadily depleted until nothing remains.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The root kalah is frequently used in the Old Testament to depict the utter exhaustion of resources or physical strength, such as eyes failing from weeping or a soul longing and fainting for God's presence. In the context of Chilion's name, the word picture is that of a candle burning down to its end, or a vessel being completely emptied of its contents. When paired with his brother Mahlon (whose name means "sickly" or "infirmity"), the two names form a poetic portrait of doom: the family begins in sickness (Mahlon) and ends in complete consumption and wasting away (Chilion).

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of Chilion's name and subsequent death lies in the consequences of leaving the covenant land of Israel. Elimelech leads his family out of Bethlehem ("House of Bread") during a famine to seek sustenance in Moab, a nation historically hostile to Israel and under divine displeasure. This journey, intended to preserve life, results instead in the "wasting away" (Chilion) of the family's male heirs. Scripturally, this underscores the spiritual truth that departing from God's place of provision and covenant promises in pursuit of worldly security leads only to spiritual and physical exhaustion and death.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:2, the mention of Chilion is juxtaposed with the family's status as "Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah." This creates a sharp literary irony: Ephrathah means "fruitful" and Bethlehem means "house of bread," yet the sons born there are named "sickly" and "wasting away." The text highlights that despite their prestigious heritage and the abundant promises of their homeland, their departure to Moab seals their destiny. The narrative move from "remained there" in verse 2 to "both Mahlon and Chilion also died" in verse 5 demonstrates that the names were not merely descriptive of their infant health, but prophetic of their ultimate end outside the Promised Land.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:2: "The name of the man was Elimelech, the name of his wife was Naomi, and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion—Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah. And they went to the country of Moab and remained there."

• Ruth 1:5: "Then both Mahlon and Chilion also died; so the woman survived her two husbands and her two sons."

• Ruth 4:9: "And Boaz said to the elders and all the people, 'You are witnesses this day that I have bought all that was Elimelech’s, and all that was Chilion’s and Mahlon’s, from the hand of Naomi.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

While Chilion's life and name represent devastation, the book of Ruth is ultimately a story of redemption and reversal. The "completion" of Elimelech's line through death is met by the "completion" of God's redemptive plan through Boaz, the kinsman-redeemer. In Ruth 4:9, the public restoration of Chilion’s inheritance through Boaz's redemption of Naomi and Ruth demonstrates that God's grace can reclaim what human frailty and sin have allowed to waste away, weaving even the names of the dead into the royal lineage of King David and ultimately Jesus Christ.
