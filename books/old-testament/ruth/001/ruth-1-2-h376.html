MAN - ( 'ish , Strong's H0376 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ish in Ruth 1:2 refers to an individual man, husband, or person of note. In this context, it establishes Elimelech's specific identity, covenantal standing, and headship over his household. His journey from Bethlehem to Moab represents a tragic failure of spiritual leadership by the "man" of the family, setting the stage for the narrative's deep need for a redeemer.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word 'ish stands in contrast to other Hebrew terms for humanity, such as 'adam (generic mankind made from dust) or 'enosh (frail, mortal humanity). It emphasizes the individual male, particularly in terms of his strength, responsibility, and relationship to others as a husband or leader. In ancient Near Eastern culture, the 'ish was the head of the house (bet 'av), holding covenantal responsibility before God for the protection, provision, and spiritual direction of his household. The word evokes the dignity of individual agency and the weight of familial stewardship.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meanings of 'ish include:

• An individual male, distinguishing a man from a woman or child.

• A husband, denoting the covenantal male partner in marriage.

• A person of high status, wealth, or valor (contrasted with the common or weak man).

• An individual person, often used in the distributive sense of "each one" or "every man."

In Ruth 1:2, the term is used twice in its singular form to designate Elimelech as the specific male head of his household, establishing his legal and historical existence.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Relational Complement: The word 'ish is first used in Scripture alongside 'ishshah (woman/wife) in Genesis 2:23, illustrating a designed, complementary relationship where the two correspond to one another.

• The Representative Head: In legal and historical contexts, 'ish represents the legal entity of the family. The decisions of the 'ish legally bind his household.

• The Man of Valor: When paired with terms of strength, 'ish denotes a champion, warrior, or leader who stands ready to defend his inheritance.

4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of 'ish in the Book of Ruth centers on covenantal headship and individual responsibility. Elimelech, as the 'ish of the family, was tasked with guarding his family's spiritual and physical inheritance in the Promised Land. By choosing to abandon Bethlehem ("House of Bread") during a famine to reside in the pagan fields of Moab, he failed in his role as the covenant leader.

The subsequent deaths of Elimelech and his two sons emphasize the vulnerability of a household left without an 'ish to protect and redeem them in ancient Israel. The narrative's ultimate resolution hinges on the introduction of Boaz, who is introduced as a different kind of 'ish—a "man of great wealth" and covenant integrity who successfully fulfills the role of redeemer.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:2, the phrase "The name of the man was Elimelech" grounds the narrative in historical reality. This is not a generalized myth; it is the specific history of a real man who held a real inheritance in Bethlehem. The use of 'ish highlights the tragedy of what follows. The man who should have been the source of life, continuity, and protection for Naomi dies in a foreign land. The loss of the 'ish leaves Naomi and her daughters-in-law completely marginalized, driving the desperate need for a kinsman-redeemer who can step into the empty role of the 'ish and restore the family's name and inheritance.

7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a literary contrast developed throughout the book of Ruth between the failure of the first 'ish (Elimelech) and the faithfulness of the ultimate 'ish (Boaz). While Elimelech's actions lead to death, emptiness, and exile, Boaz's actions as a righteous 'ish bring life, fullness, and restoration. This pattern ultimately points to the ultimate Man, Jesus Christ, who acts as the true Elder Brother and Husband of His people, redeeming them from spiritual destitution.
