COUNTRY - ( sadeh , Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh primarily refers to an open field, cultivated land, or a specific territory or region. In Ruth 1:2, translated as "country," it denotes the territory or rural tableland of Moab. Rather than representing a politically defined nation-state in the modern sense, the word evokes the physical landscape, fertile fields, and agricultural regions of Moab. This stands in stark, ironic contrast to Bethlehem ("House of Bread"), which was suffering from famine, driving Elimelech and his family to seek sustenance in the foreign fields of a historic enemy.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun sadeh is rooted in the physical landscape of the ancient Near East. It portrays open land, cultivated fields, or a broad territory. Unlike the secure, walled cities (iyr) or the barren, uncultivated wilderness (midbar), the sadeh represents a place of agricultural labor, sustenance, seasonal crop growth, and vulnerability. In historical narratives, it frequently denotes the specific region or rural territory belonging to a particular nation or tribe. It is the ground that must be tilled for survival, yet it lies outside the protective gates of the city, exposing those within it to the elements, wild beasts, and foreign nations.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The lexical definition of sadeh (and its alternate poetic form saday) encompasses several distinct layers of meaning:

• Cultivated ground or land used for crops, agriculture, grain, and vineyards.

• An individual field, plot, or parcel of land owned by a specific person or family.

• Open country, pastures, or uncultivated wilderness where wild animals roam and livestock graze.

• A geographical territory, land, or region associated with a specific nation, city, or people group (such as the "country of Moab" or the "field of Edom").

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The word occurs frequently to illustrate agricultural life and spatial dynamics in ancient Israel. It is the primary setting for sowing, reaping, grazing, and battle. It carries a strong pastoral and agricultural word picture, evoking the scent of the soil, the labor of the farmer, and the provision of daily food. When used of a foreign territory, it shifts from an individual agricultural plot to a broader geopolitical landscape. It represents the entire life-sustaining countryside of that region, emphasizing the physical, earth-bound reality of relocating to an unfamiliar soil.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, sadeh is deeply tied to the concepts of inheritance, covenant blessing, and divine judgment. In the Pentateuch, God promises to bless the fields of those who remain obedient to the covenant, while disobedience results in barren fields, pests, and famine. The departure of Elimelech's family from the land of Israel to the sadeh of Moab represents a physical and spiritual abandonment of the covenant land. The family exchanges the promised fields of inheritance for the pagan fields of Moab. However, the ultimate restoration in the narrative occurs when Naomi and Ruth return, and Ruth is led by divine providence to the fertile sadeh of Boaz in Bethlehem, showing how God redeems and blesses the faithful through the yield of the earth.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:2, the construct phrase sedeh mo'ab ("the country of Moab") refers to the high plateau and rolling agricultural tableland situated east of the Dead Sea and Jordan River. This area was known for its rich, fertile soil and reliable rainfall, making it a natural refuge during a famine in Judah. The use of sadeh here highlights the literary irony of the opening chapter: Bethlehem ("House of Bread") has no bread, forcing Israelite covenant-holders to seek sustenance in the pagan fields of Moab. The family does not merely visit; they settle down in these foreign fields, exposing themselves to the spiritual dangers of idolatry and the physical losses of death and bereavement.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The word sadeh serves as a structural and thematic bracket for the entire book of Ruth. The narrative begins with a desperate migration to the foreign "country" (fields) of Moab (Ruth 1:1–2, 22), where death and emptiness occur. It transitions dynamically in chapter 2 to the domestic "field" of Boaz in Bethlehem, where life, kindness, and abundance are found. This geographical movement mirrors the spiritual journey of the characters—moving from exile and loss in a pagan territory back to life, covenant community, and redemption in the fertile fields of the Promised Land.
