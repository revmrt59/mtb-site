WIFE - ( 'ishshah , Strong's H802 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ishshah refers to a woman or, more specifically within a relational context, a wife. In Ruth 1:2, it identifies Naomi's covenant relationship with Elimelech, establishing her social and legal standing within the family unit before the tragedy of widowhood. It highlights the divine design of companionship and the vulnerability of a woman in the ancient Near East when this marital bond is severed.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The term 'ishshah is the feminine counterpart to the Hebrew word ish (man). In the creation narrative of Genesis, the woman is named 'ishshah because she was taken out of man (ish). This linguistic connection creates a vivid word picture of essential unity, correspondence, and mutual completion. The word carries no inherent sense of subordination of value, but rather points to a matching partner. Throughout the Old Testament, the word transitions seamlessly between "woman" (denoting gender) and "wife" (denoting marital status), depending on the presence of a possessive pronoun or relational context.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word 'ishshah refers to an adult female human being. When used in construct with a man's name or with a pronominal suffix (such as "his wife"), it specifically denotes a married woman who is bound to her husband in a covenant relationship. The semantic range includes a single woman, a bride, a wife, or a widow. In legal contexts, it refers to a spouse with recognized rights, protections, and responsibilities within the patriarchal household structure.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In ancient Israelite culture, the 'ishshah was the cornerstone of the domestic sphere. The scriptures picture her not merely as a passive dependent, but as an active builder of the household, a source of wisdom, and a partner in covenant faithfulness. The word is used metaphorically in several ways:

• As personified Wisdom (Chokmah) in the book of Proverbs, inviting the simple to life and understanding.

• As personified Folly, leading the unwary to the chambers of death.

• In prophetic literature, Israel is pictured as the 'ishshah (wife) of Yahweh, emphasizing the intimacy and exclusivity of God's covenant with His people, as well as the grief of spiritual adultery.

4. Theological Implications

Theological truths anchored in this word include:

• Creation Order: The relational nature of humanity is defined through the ish and 'ishshah relationship, reflecting God’s design for human flourishing, companionship, and multiplication.

• Covenant Representation: Marriage serves as an earthly model of divine faithfulness. The term 'ishshah carries the weight of solemn vows and familial obligations.

• Redemptive Security: In the ancient Near Eastern social structure, a 'ishshah found her primary physical protection, economic security, and inheritance rights through her husband. The loss of this status through widowhood represents one of the deepest vulnerabilities in the biblical world, making the care of widows a key measure of Israel's spiritual health.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:2, Naomi is introduced as the 'ishshah (wife) of Elimelech. This introduction establishes her initial state of security, honor, and identity within the covenant community of Ephrathites from Bethlehem. However, this status is quickly shattered in the verses that follow. By establishing her as a 'ishshah in verse 2, the text sets up a stark contrast with verse 3, where Elimelech dies and Naomi is left without her husband. She transitions from a protected 'ishshah to a vulnerable widow. The narrative of Ruth is ultimately a journey of redemption where the status of 'ishshah is restored to the family line through Ruth's eventual marriage to Boaz, illustrating God's providential care for the helpless.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:24: "Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and they shall become one flesh."

• Proverbs 12:4: "An excellent wife is the crown of her husband, But she who causes shame is like rottenness in his bones."

• Proverbs 31:10: "Who can find a virtuous wife? For her worth is far above rubies."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition of Naomi and Ruth from wives to widows, and then Ruth's restoration as a wife, showcases the Hebrew concept of goel (the kinsman-redeemer). Under levirate marriage customs, the redemption of land was tied to the redemption of the 'ishshah of the deceased. Boaz's willingness to take Ruth as his 'ishshah (Ruth 4:13) not only secures her physical future but also preserves the name and inheritance of the dead, ultimately leading to the birth of Obed and the lineage of David and Jesus Christ.
