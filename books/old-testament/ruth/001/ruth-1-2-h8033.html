THERE - ( sham, Strong's H8033 )

Summary:

The Hebrew adverb sham ("there") designates a specific, physical location away from the speaker, but in the narrative of Ruth 1:2, it carries deep theological and existential weight. It marks the transition of Elimelech's family from Bethlehem ("House of Bread") in Judah to the pagan country of Moab. In this context, "there" represents a place of compromise, loss, and spiritual exile, demonstrating how a simple geographic marker can signify a departure from the land of promise and the immediate sphere of God's covenant blessings.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The adverb sham is a fundamental Semitic word used to point to a location that is relatively distant or distinct from the current standpoint of the narrator or speaker. Unlike "here" (poh), which indicates presence, intimacy, and immediacy, sham establishes distance. In the historical and geographical landscape of the Old Testament, "there" often serves as a boundary marker separating the sacred space of the Promised Land from the outside world. In Ruth 1:2, "there" is Moab—a historical adversary of Israel born of incest, characterized by idolatry. This geographical shift highlights the physical and spiritual distance Elimelech and his family put between themselves and the covenant community.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word sham functions primarily as an adverb of place meaning "in that place," "at that spot," or "thither" (indicating motion toward a place). It occurs over 800 times in the Hebrew Bible. While it is a particle of place, it can also possess temporal nuances ("at that time" or "then") or even situational ones, pointing to a state of being or a specific juncture in a narrative. In Ruth 1:2, it is strictly locational, referencing "the country of Moab," indicating both the destination of their journey and the place of their prolonged settlement.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, sham is used to draw sharp contrasts between two locations, functioning as a literary bracket to emphasize what happens "away" from home.

• In the patriarchal narratives, "there" is frequently the place where altars are built or where divine encounters occur (such as Bethel or Moriah). In those instances, "there" is sanctified by God's presence.

• Conversely, in the context of exile and straying, "there" becomes a place of spiritual compromise and death. In Ruth, "there" in Moab is where the family seeks temporary refuge but ends up settling down. It becomes a graveyard for Elimelech, Mahlon, and Chilion, turning a temporary stay into a permanent tragedy.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, sham in Ruth 1:2 underscores the danger of seeking provision outside of God’s covenant boundaries. Bethlehem was experiencing a famine, which was a sign of covenant discipline under the Mosaic law. Instead of repenting "here" in the Land, Elimelech led his family "there" to Moab. This geographical departure illustrates the spiritual reality of backsliding. "There" represents the place of self-preservation apart from faith. It proves that when we choose our own "there" over God's "here," we invite spiritual famine, even if physical bread is temporarily abundant.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

The narrative movement in Ruth 1:1–2 builds toward this word. The family travels from Bethlehem-Judah to the fields of Moab. The verse ends with the sobering phrase: "and they remained there." The Hebrew construction indicates more than a brief visit; it denotes a settled state. What began as a temporary journey to "sojourn" (gur) degenerated into a permanent dwelling (sham). The nuance of "there" in this passage is one of stagnation and tragedy. It is "there" in Moab that Naomi is stripped of her husband and her two sons, leaving her empty. The word highlights that "there" is a place of spiritual vulnerability and loss.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:10: "Now there was a famine in the land, and Abram went down to Egypt to dwell there, for the famine was severe in the land."

• Genesis 28:11: "So he came to a certain place and stayed there all night, because the sun had set."

• Genesis 35:1: "Then God said to Jacob, 'Arise, go up to Bethel and dwell there; and make an altar there to God, who appeared to you when you fled from the face of Esau your brother.'"

• Psalm 137:1: "By the rivers of Babylon, There we sat down, yea, we wept When we remembered Zion."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between "here" (poh) and "there" (sham) is highly instructional for preaching. "Here" is the place of God's presence, temple, and covenant people. "There" is the place of the world, pagan influence, and isolation. In the book of Ruth, the journey back from "there" (Moab) to "here" (Bethlehem) is initiated when Naomi hears "in the country of Moab that the LORD had visited His people in giving them bread" (Ruth 1:6). Repentance is essentially leaving "there" to return "here," to the place of God’s altar and provision.
