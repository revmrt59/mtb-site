NAMES - ( shem , Strong's H8034 )

Summary:

In ancient Hebrew culture, a name (shem) was far more than a label for identification; it represented a person’s character, reputation, authority, and destiny. In Ruth 1:2, the introduction of the family's names sets a profound thematic stage for the narrative, contrasting their rich, hopeful meanings with the tragic reality of their immediate circumstances.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word shem carries the primary idea of a memorial, a monument, or a distinct mark of identity and ownership. It refers to that which is established, definite, and recognizable. To have a name is to exist, to have status, and to possess a recognized place in community and history. In the ancient Near East, naming was an act of authority, foresight, and prophecy. Parents chose names that reflected their prayers, the circumstances of a child’s birth, or prophetic hopes for the child's future. Thus, a name was not merely a passive identifier but an active presence, carrying the essence of the person wherever they went.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun shem has several dimensions of meaning:

• An individual designation, appellation, or personal name.

• Reputation, fame, or renown (the value of one's standing in the community).

• Memory, memorial, or the posterous continuation of a family line (extremely important in the covenant community of Israel).

• Presence, authority, or character, especially when used in relation to the name of Yahweh.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Shem is frequently used to describe the preservation of a deceased man's lineage. To "blot out" a name is to condemn someone to historical non-existence and ultimate ruin. Conversely, to "raise up" a name is to secure a legacy and inheritance.

• The word is often paired with "memorial" or "remembrance," emphasizing that a name is how a person remains alive in the covenant community's memory.

• In theological contexts, the "Name of the LORD" is synonymous with His revealed character, power, and saving presence. To call upon His name is to appeal to His covenant faithfulness.

4. Theological Implications

The book of Ruth is deeply concerned with the preservation of the shem (name) of the dead. The levirate-like marriage of Boaz and Ruth is specifically designed to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance, ensuring that the name of the deceased does not perish from Israel. Names in scripture also highlight God’s sovereign, providential irony. The names in Ruth 1:2 represent a theological tension between human identity and divine reality. The tragedy of the family leaving the land of promise is amplified by the contrast between what their names declare and what their lives experience.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:2, the family's names are heavy with theological irony, preparing the reader for the tragic shift in the narrative:

• "Elimelech" means "My God is King." Yet, he leaves the land of promise during a famine to seek food in pagan Moab, behaving as though he has no King to provide for him.

• "Naomi" means "Pleasant" or "Sweet," but her life path leads her to bitterness, prompting her later to demand, "Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara [Bitter]" (Ruth 1:20).

• "Mahlon" likely means "Sickly" or "Weak," and "Chilion" means "Pining" or "Failing"—prophetic descriptions of their premature deaths in Moab.

By listing these names in Ruth 1:2, the author establishes the theme of identity, loss, and the threat of total extinction of the family’s shem in Israel. The rest of the book serves as a redemptive journey to restore the shem of this household, proving that even when human names fail, the Name of the Lord remains faithful.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:2: "I will make you a great nation; I will bless you and make your name great; and you shall be a blessing."

• Exodus 3:15: "Moreover God said to Moses... 'This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations.'"

• Proverbs 22:1: "A good name is to be chosen rather than great riches, loving favor rather than silver and gold."

• Ruth 4:10: "Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife, to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance, that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brethren and from the gate of this place. You are witnesses this day."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The preservation of a name in ancient Israel was directly tied to land ownership. Land was a gift from Yahweh, and the permanent loss of a family's name from their inherited portion of land was seen as a spiritual and communal tragedy. The ultimate resolution of the book of Ruth is the inclusion of Ruth and Boaz's son, Obed, into the lineage of David, thereby magnifying the shem of this once-endangered family to royal and messianic heights.
