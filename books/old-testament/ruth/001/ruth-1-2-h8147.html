TWO - ( shenayim , Strong's H8147 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shenayim is the cardinal number "two," denoting a pair, double, or a couplet. In the narrative of Ruth 1:2, it refers to Elimelech and Naomi's "two sons," Mahlon and Chilion. This word highlights the completeness of Elimelech’s immediate family unit and underscores the devastating scale of Naomi's subsequent loss. Within Hebrew narrative, the number two often functions structurally to set up a contrast, a partnership, or a vulnerability, which in this context emphasizes the tragic dissolution of a family lineage in a foreign land.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• shenayim is the dual form of the root meaning "second" or "repeat." In Semitic thought, the number two naturally signifies a pair, a witness, or a division. It represents the smallest unit of community or partnership beyond the individual. In the agrarian and patriarchal culture of ancient Israel, having "two sons" was a sign of blessing, security, and the guaranteed preservation of the family name and inheritance. It represented a double hope for the future, ensuring that if one son met with misfortune, the other could carry on the family line.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of shenayim is "two" or "a pair." It functions as a cardinal numeral. Depending on the grammatical context, it appears in the masculine (shenayim) or feminine (shettayim) form, and frequently in the construct state (shene or shete) when immediately preceding a noun. The word denotes:

• A literal count of two entities (such as two people, two animals, or two objects).

• A pair of closely associated things (like two eyes or two hands).

• A double portion or a repeating element.

In Ruth 1:2, it appears in the masculine construct form (shene), bound directly to "sons" (banim), defining the precise limit of Elimelech's male heirs.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture frequently uses the number two to establish legal, relational, or narrative dynamics:

• Legal Witness: Biblical law requires the testimony of "two" (shenayim) witnesses to establish a matter, representing truth and confirmation.

• Companionship and Strength: Two are better than one, providing mutual support and defense in labor and travel.

• Narrative Contrast: In Old Testament narratives, pairs of sons often represent contrasting paths or destinies (e.g., Cain and Abel, Ishmael and Isaac, Jacob and Esau). While Mahlon and Chilion do not represent moral opposites, their joint destiny as a pair emphasizes the comprehensive nature of the tragedy that strikes Naomi.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the "two sons" represent the fragile thread of covenant continuity for Elimelech's household. In the ancient Near East, a family's survival and land inheritance depended entirely on male heirs. By specifying that Elimelech had "two sons," the text emphasizes that he was blessed with a double portion of security. The subsequent death of both sons is not merely a personal tragedy for Naomi; it represents the complete severance of the family's covenantal link to the land of promise. The theological weight of "two" here highlights the transition from a state of double blessing to absolute emptiness, setting the stage for God's ultimate work of redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the immediate context of Ruth 1:1–5, the mention of "two sons" heightens the dramatic irony and grief. Elimelech leaves Bethlehem ("House of Bread") during a famine to preserve his family. He has "two sons," which should have guaranteed his legacy. However, the move to Moab results in the death of Elimelech, followed by the marriages of the "two sons" to Moabite women, and finally, the death of both sons (Ruth 1:5). The repetition of "two" in these opening verses—two sons, two wives, two daughters-in-law—creates a literary symmetry of grief. When both sons die, the double hope is entirely extinguished, leaving Naomi utterly destitute and reliant on Yahweh's providence.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The dual form in Hebrew is a unique grammatical feature used for things that naturally occur in pairs (like eyes, hands, or sandals). While the word shenayim is the general numeral "two," its application to people often carries a narrative emphasis on companionship, division, or total representation. In Ruth, the "two" sons (Mahlon and Chilion) represent the entirety of Elimelech's legacy. Their names, often understood to mean "sickness" (Mahlon) and "pining/destruction" (Chilion), further reinforce the tragic arc of this pair. The loss of both sons leaves the family line completely severed, making Naomi’s situation humanly hopeless.
