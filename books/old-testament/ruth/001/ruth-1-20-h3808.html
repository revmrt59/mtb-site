NOT - ( lo' , Strong's H3808 )

Summary:

The Hebrew negative particle lo' (Strong's H3808) is the primary adverb of negation in the Old Testament, representing absolute, permanent, and objective denial. Translated as "not" or "no," it stands in contrast to temporary or subjective negative particles. In the narrative of Ruth, negation marks the profound transition from emptiness to fulfillment. While Naomi’s emotional plea in Ruth 1:20 uses a deprecative negative to reject her name, the absolute negation of lo' throughout the opening chapter establishes her state of total deprivation. This absolute "not" forms the stark backdrop against which God's covenant loyalty (chesed) eventually shines, reversing human impossibility into divine redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word lo' is a simple, two-letter Hebrew particle (lamed-alef) that acts as the absolute negative. Unlike languages with complex negative inflections, Hebrew relies on this short, powerful word to establish an uncompromising boundary or a state of complete non-existence.

• Historically and culturally, it is the word of unconditional prohibition and permanent reality—most famously heard in the "Thou shalt not" commandments of the Decalogue.

• In a narrative setting, it creates a stark backdrop of lack, refusal, or impossibility. It represents a closed door, an empty womb, or a dry well that cannot be altered by human effort.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• lo' functions as an adverb of negation meaning "not," "no," or "neither."

• It expresses objective, absolute negation, indicating that a state of affairs does not exist or that an action is permanently prohibited.

• It is distinct from 'al (Strong's H408), which is used for subjective, immediate, or deprecative wishes (e.g., "do not let this happen" or the immediate plea in Ruth 1:20, "Do not call me Naomi"). While 'al pleads against a temporary or undesired state, lo' declares an objective, permanent fact or an absolute standard.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Absolute Prohibition: Used in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20) to denote permanent moral boundaries.

• Permanent Inability or Unchanging Reality: Used to describe fixed truths or absolute limitations (e.g., God is "not" a man that He should lie).

• Narrative Contrast: In Hebrew storytelling, lo' establishes a state of deficit (e.g., Sarah had "no" child) that only divine intervention can resolve. It paints a picture of human helplessness and absolute closure.

4. Theological Implications

• The theology of negation: Before God acts in creation or redemption, there is often a pronounced lo'. The word emphasizes human limitation; when humans say "not" or experience "no," it sets the stage for God's sovereign "yes."

• In the history of redemption, absolute negatives highlight the gravity of covenant failure and the absolute necessity of divine grace. Human effort cannot (lo') save or reverse the consequences of the fall.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:20, Naomi cries, "Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara..." While her direct plea to the women of Bethlehem uses the immediate, deprecative negative 'al (Strong's H408) because she is expressing a strong personal wish, the underlying theology of absolute negation (lo', Strong's H3808) defines her entire spiritual condition in the surrounding context.

• In Ruth 1:13, Naomi uses lo' to deliver a crushing, absolute refusal of hope: "No (lo'), my daughters; for it grieves me very much..." She is declaring that there is absolutely no objective, realistic hope for remarriage or offspring through her.

• By looking at the transition from the absolute "no" (lo') of barrenness, death, and exile in chapter 1 to the ultimate provision of a redeemer in chapter 4, the author of Ruth demonstrates that God is capable of overturning the most absolute human negatives. The woman who claimed she had "not" any hope ends up holding a child who secures the line of the Messiah.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 20:13: "You shall not murder."

• Numbers 23:19: "God is not a man, that He should lie..."

• Deuteronomy 8:3: "...that He might make you know that man shall not live by bread alone..."

• Ruth 1:13: "...No, my daughters; for it grieves me very much for your sakes that the hand of the Lord has gone out against me!"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The structural beauty of Hebrew narrative often pairs lo' with a subsequent ki ("but" or "for") to create a dramatic pivot. When Naomi declares what is not true, she forces the listener to look forward to what must be done by God.

• The absolute negative reminds teachers and preachers that biblical hope does not arise from a modification of human potential, but from the complete reversal of human impossibility.
