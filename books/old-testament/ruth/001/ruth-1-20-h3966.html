VERY - ( me'od , Strong's H3966 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word me'od primarily functions as an adverb meaning "exceedingly," "greatly," or "forcefully," intensifying the verb or adjective it modifies to its highest degree. In Ruth 1:20, Naomi uses it to describe how "very bitterly" (hemar... me'od) the Almighty has dealt with her. Rather than a mere grammatical modifier, this word captures the profound, overwhelming depth of Naomi's grief, underscoring her perception of complete devastation and the intense weight of her personal loss.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun form of me'od originally carries the sense of "force," "abundance," "might," or "strength." In its earliest usage, it represents the concept of bringing something to its maximum capacity. For example, in the central confession of Israelite faith (the Shema), God is to be loved with all of one's "strength" (me'od). This refers to a person's utmost physical energy, resources, and entire existence. When me'od functions as an adverb, it projects this sense of total capacity and ultimate intensity onto the action or state it describes, signifying that a condition has reached its absolute boundary or limit.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• As an adverb of degree, me'od means exceedingly, extremely, greatly, or intensely.

• As a substantive noun, it means force, abundance, or might.

• In grammatical construction with an adjective or a verb, it functions as a superlative, raising the description to the highest possible degree of intensity.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Superlative of Creation: In Genesis, when God surveys His completed work, He declares it not just good, but "very good" (tov me'od), indicating complete, flawless perfection and ultimate aesthetic and functional harmony.

• The Superlative of Distress: In times of emotional or physical distress, me'od describes an overwhelming flood of grief, fear, or anger that completely consumes an individual's capacity to cope, serving as a verbal exclamation point indicating that the condition has surpassed normal human boundaries.

• The Superlative of Abundance: It is used to describe immense physical quantities, extreme physical beauty, or absolute certainty, demonstrating that the subject under discussion has reached a peak that cannot easily be exceeded.

### 4. Theological Implications

Naomi's use of me'od in relation to God's providence reveals a complex theological truth. She attributes her extreme suffering directly to the Almighty (Shaddai). She does not view her trials as random occurrences of bad luck, but as the deliberate, sovereign hand of God. This highlights the biblical reality of lament: God allows His people to express the absolute fullness (me'od) of their pain directly to Him, recognizing His sovereignty even while walking through deep darkness. The word underscores the depth of human frailty when confronted with severe suffering, emphasizing that our grief can reach an extreme limit before God begins His work of redemption.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:20, Naomi states, "for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me." The Hebrew phrase is hemar li me'od. The verb marar means to be bitter, and the addition of me'od indicates a bitterness that is absolute, heavy, and seemingly irreversible. Naomi has lost her husband, her two sons, her wealth, and her status, returning to Bethlehem empty-handed. Her name "Naomi" means "pleasant," but she demands to be called "Mara" (bitter) because the intensity (me'od) of her circumstances has redefined her identity in her own eyes. The adverb highlights the stark contrast between the fullness she once enjoyed and the absolute, "exceeding" emptiness she now feels.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:31: "Then God saw everything that He had made, and indeed it was very good. So the evening and the morning were the sixth day."

• Genesis 12:14: "So it was, when Abram came into Egypt, that the Egyptians saw the woman, that she was very beautiful."

• Deuteronomy 6:5: "You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your strength."

• 1 Samuel 30:6: "Now David was greatly distressed, for the people spoke of stoning him, because the soul of all the people was grieved, every man for his sons and his daughters. But David strengthened himself in the LORD his God."

• Psalm 46:1: "God is our refuge and strength, A very present help in trouble."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• Me'od is sometimes doubled in Hebrew (me'od me'od) to express the absolute superlative, meaning "exceedingly beyond measure" (as seen in the description of the floodwaters in Genesis 7:19). Naomi's single use of me'od still carries immense weight, but it leaves room in the narrative arc for God's eventual, even greater restoration.

• While Naomi's focus in chapter 1 is on how very bitterly God has dealt with her, the book of Ruth concludes with an abundant, very great redemption that far outmeasures her temporary bitterness, demonstrating that God's capacity for grace is always greater than the depth of human suffering.
