MARA - ( mara , Strong's H4755 )

Summary:

Mara is a feminine proper name meaning "bitter" or "bitterness," derived from the root marar (to be bitter). In the book of Ruth, Naomi adopts this name to express her deep grief, sense of bereavement, and perceived abandonment by God after losing her husband and two sons. It represents a raw, honest outcry of a soul crushed by affliction, shifting her identity from "pleasant" (Naomi) to "bitter" (Mara) as she struggles to reconcile her tragic circumstances with the sovereign hand of the Almighty.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Mara comes from the Hebrew root marar, which means to trickle, drip, or be bitter to the taste. In the ancient Near East, names were deeply tied to a person's character, state of being, or destiny. To change one's name was to declare a fundamental shift in identity or life circumstances. Naomi's self-imposed name change to Mara paints a vivid picture of a life that has turned sour and distasteful. The physical sensation of tasting something harsh and unpalatable is mapped onto the emotional and spiritual reality of her soul. This is not mere self-pity, but an ancient cultural expression of lament, acknowledging a profound transition from sweetness and abundance to scarcity and sorrow.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term mara (transliterated as mārā') means "bitter" or "sad." It functions as a proper noun in Ruth 1:20, specifically chosen for its phonetic and semantic contrast with No'omi (Naomi), which means "my pleasantness" or "delight." The underlying root marar denotes the physical taste of bitterness, the emotional state of mourning, or the harshness of difficult labor and oppression. In this context, the lexical meaning carries both a passive sense of suffering affliction and an active sense of grieving a devastating loss.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

In the Old Testament, bitterness is frequently used as a metaphor for intense suffering, mourning, and spiritual distress.

• The root is used to describe the "bitter" waters of Marah in the wilderness (Exodus 15:23), which the Israelites could not drink until God miraculously sweetened them.

• It describes the bitter weeping of a parent mourning an only son (Zechariah 12:10).

• It represents the harsh life of slavery in Egypt, where the taskmasters made their lives bitter with hard bondage (Exodus 1:14).

• In Ruth 1:20, the word picture is highly relational: Naomi views her bitterness not as a random twist of fate, but as a condition directly brought about by the Almighty (Shaddai), who has "dealt very bitterly" with her.

4. Theological Implications

The adoption of the name Mara highlights the theological tension between God's sovereignty and human suffering. Naomi does not deny God's existence or power; rather, she attributes her bitter state directly to Him. This reveals a robust, albeit painful, theology of sovereignty: God is the one who gives, and He is the one who takes away. It also illustrates the legitimacy of lament in the life of faith. Scripture does not sanitize Naomi's grief or rebuke her for her raw honesty. Mara represents a necessary stage of grief where a believer expresses real pain to God without turning away from Him. Ultimately, Mara serves as the dark backdrop against which God's loyal love (hesed) and providential redemption will shine. Her bitterness is temporary, though she cannot see it yet; God is already at work behind the scenes to turn her bitterness back into sweetness.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:20, the word functions as an antonymous wordplay. Naomi commands the women of Bethlehem, "Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara." She rejects her original name because "pleasantness" no longer matches her reality. She left Bethlehem full—with a husband and two sons—but returned empty. The use of "Mara" is immediately qualified by her statement: "for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me." The Hebrew verb for "dealt very bitterly" (hemer) shares the same root. Naomi's name change is a public testimony of her perceived reality. It shows her vulnerability and her refusal to wear a mask of false piety before her community. Her focus is entirely on her losses, blinding her temporarily to the presence of Ruth, who stands beside her as a channel of God's future blessing.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because H4755 is used only once in the Old Testament as a proper name (Ruth 1:20), the following verses demonstrate key uses of the closely related root words H4751 (mar - bitter) and H4754 (marah - to be bitter/rebel), which carry the exact same semantic weight of bitterness and grief:

• Exodus 15:23 – "Now when they came to Marah, they could not drink the waters of Marah, for they were bitter. Therefore the name of it was called Marah."

• Proverbs 14:10 – "The heart knows its own bitterness, And a stranger does not share its joy."

• Isaiah 38:17 – "Indeed it was for my own peace That I had great bitterness; But You have lovingly delivered my soul from the pit of corruption, For You have cast all my sins behind Your back."

• Zechariah 12:10 – "Then they will look on Me whom they pierced. Yes, they will mourn for Him as one mourns for his only son, and grieve for Him as one grieves for a firstborn."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between "Naomi" (pleasant) and "Mara" (bitter) serves as a pastoral reminder that faith is not a linear experience of constant joy. Godly people experience seasons of "Mara." However, the Book of Ruth ends not with bitterness, but with Naomi holding a grandchild, her life restored and her "pleasantness" returned, showing that God's ultimate plan for His people is redemption, not perpetual bitterness.
