BITTERLY - ( marar , Strong's H4843 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb marar conveys the experience of intense grief, harshness, and deep sorrow. In Ruth 1:20, Naomi uses this word in its causative form to describe how God's sovereign providence has brought extreme distress and affliction into her life, transforming her pleasant existence into one of profound emotional pain.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root marar fundamentally relates to the sense of taste, describing something sharp, acrid, or bitter to the palate, which is the direct opposite of sweet.

• In the ancient Near Eastern world, physical taste was intimately connected to emotional and existential realities. Bitterness was a vivid metaphor for suffering, oppression, bereavement, and heavy labor.

• The term evokes the image of drinking a harsh, unpalatable cup of sorrow or enduring life circumstances that are heavy, chafing, and toxic to the human spirit.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb marar means to be bitter, to make bitter, or to show bitterness.

• In the basic verbal stem (Qal), it describes a state of grief or weeping bitterly.

• In the causative stems (Piel and Hiphil), it means to cause bitterness, to make life bitter, or to deal bitterly with someone.

• In Ruth 1:20, the verb appears in the Hiphil stem (hemar), meaning "has caused bitterness" or "has dealt very bitterly." It denotes the active causing of deep emotional suffering and hardship.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Bondage: The word is used to describe the harsh, crushing labor of the Israelites under Egyptian taskmasters, who made their lives bitter with hard bondage.

• Emotional Mourning: It portrays the weeping of a soul in deep mourning, such as weeping bitterly over a loss, a national disaster, or the death of a firstborn.

• Hostile Attack: It is used metaphorically to describe the fierce, wounding attacks of archers shooting at their target, representing severe provocation and hostility.

4. Theological Implications

• Naomi’s use of marar in relation to the Almighty (Shaddai) highlights a robust theology of divine sovereignty. She does not attribute her losses to blind fate, bad luck, or the gods of Moab. She recognizes that the ultimate sovereign of her life is Yahweh.

• This usage reveals that biblical faith permits the honest expression of grief and anguish directly to God. Lament is not a denial of faith, but a painful acknowledgment of God's active hand in all of life's circumstances.

• While bitterness can morph into a sinful attitude of resentment, Naomi’s cry is a raw, honest lament. She acknowledges that the Almighty has brought this bitter cup to her lips, yet she returns to Bethlehem—the house of bread—placing herself in the path of His eventual mercy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:20, Naomi plays on the contrast between her name "Naomi" (which means pleasant, sweet, or delightful) and "Mara" (bitter, a noun from the same root marar). She demands that her community stop calling her "Pleasant" and instead call her "Bitter," because "the Almighty has dealt very bitterly" with her.

• The context is one of absolute bereavement: she has lost her husband (Elimelech) and both of her sons (Mahlon and Chilion) in a foreign land. She returns empty-handed, destitute, and without an heir.

• The grammatical intensive use of the Hiphil verb highlights that her distress is not mild or passing; it is a profound, life-altering grief that has permeated her entire identity.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 1:14 - "And they made their lives bitter with hard bondage—in mortar, in brick, and in all manner of service in the field."

• Job 27:2 - "As God lives, who has taken away my justice, And the Almighty, who has made my soul bitter,"

• Isaiah 22:4 - "Therefore I said, 'Look away from me, I will weep bitterly; Do not labor to comfort me Because of the plundering of the daughter of my people.'"

• Genesis 49:23 - "The archers have bitterly grieved him, Shot at him and hated him."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The name "Mara" (Ruth 1:20) links back to the waters of Marah in Exodus 15:23, which the Israelites could not drink because they were bitter. Just as God miraculously sweetened the waters of Marah, the narrative of Ruth moves from the bitterness of Naomi's grief to the sweetness of redemption through Boaz and the birth of Obed. Naomi's bitterness is real, but it is not God's final word for her.

• The use of the divine title Shaddai (the Almighty) alongside marar indicates Naomi's absolute confidence in God's power. She does not doubt His capability; she is reeling from how He has chosen to exercise that capability in her life.
