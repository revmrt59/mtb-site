NAOMI - ( No'omiy , Strong's H5281 )

Summary:

The name Naomi means "my pleasantness" or "delight," deriving from a root word that conveys beauty, favor, and sweetness. In the context of the Book of Ruth, her name stands in stark, intentional contrast to her self-proclaimed name "Mara" (bitter). Naomi's journey from pleasantness to bitterness, and ultimately back to restoration, serves as a powerful narrative arc illustrating God's providence, loyal love, and His ability to redeem the emptiest areas of our lives.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Naomi is derived from the Hebrew root n-'-m, which means to be pleasant, lovely, delightful, or beautiful. In ancient Israelite culture, names were not merely labels but often reflected a person's character, destiny, or the parents' circumstances and prayers at the time of birth. By naming her Naomi, her parents bestowed upon her an identity associated with sweetness, favor, and joy. This name set a high expectation of a life filled with domestic peace, prosperity, and communal favor in Bethlehem (which itself means "House of Bread").

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper noun No'omiy translates literally as "my pleasantness" or "my delight." It is closely related to the noun no'am, which means "pleasantness," "beauty," or "favor," often used in the Old Testament to describe the favor or beauty of Yahweh (such as in Psalm 90:17, "the beauty of the Lord"). The root verb na'em means "to be agreeable" or "to be pleasant." Thus, Naomi's name carries the heavy lexical weight of divine favor, sweetness, and communal delight, representing everything desirable in a life lived under God's covenant blessings.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The name Naomi occurs 21 times in the Old Testament, exclusively within the Book of Ruth. The word picture associated with her name is one of sweetness and harmony.

• The Contrast of Names: The narrative of Ruth uses Naomi's name as a literary and theological foil. When she leaves Bethlehem due to famine, she leaves as "Pleasant." When she returns, she is grieving, destitute, and widowed, prompting her to reject her name.

• The Shift to Mara: Naomi requests to be called Mara, meaning "bitter," which is derived from the root marar (to be bitter). This word picture contrasts the sweetness of her past covenant life with the perceived bitterness of her present providential circumstances.

4. Theological Implications

The theological significance of Naomi's name lies in the theme of divine sovereignty, providence, and covenant faithfulness (chesed).

• The Providence of God: Naomi's life illustrates that God's covenant people are not exempt from severe suffering, emptiness, and grief. Naomi attributes her bitterness directly to the Almighty (Shaddai), recognizing His absolute sovereignty over her losses.

• From Emptiness to Fullness: Though Naomi claims she went out full and returned empty, the story reveals that God's plans for her "pleasantness" were not canceled but merely paused. The restoration of her family line through Ruth and Boaz, leading to the birth of Obed (and eventually David and the Messiah), demonstrates that God's ultimate purpose for His people is redemption, turning bitterness back into eternal pleasantness.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:20, the use of Naomi is deeply ironic and poignant. Naomi returns to Bethlehem after ten years in Moab. The women of the city are stirred and ask, "Is this Naomi?" (Ruth 1:19). Her response is a raw, emotional rejection of her identity: "Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me."

• The tension between her name ("pleasant") and her state ("bitter") highlights her internal crisis of faith. She feels that God has turned against her, making her name a mockery of her reality.

• The use of the name Shaddai (Almighty) twice in verses 20 and 21 emphasizes her belief that the God of limitless power has specifically brought about her affliction. Yet, her return to Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest hints that the "pleasantness" of her name is about to be restored by the very God she thinks has afflicted her.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:2 – "The name of the man was Elimelech, the name of his wife was Naomi, and the names of his two sons were Mahlon and Chilion—Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah. And they went to the country of Moab and remained there."

• Ruth 1:21 – "I went out full, and the Lord has brought me home again empty. Why do you call me Naomi, since the Lord has testified against me, and the Almighty has afflicted me?"

• Ruth 4:14 – "Then the women said to Naomi: 'Blessed be the Lord, who has not left you this day without a close relative; and may his name be famous in Israel!'"

• Ruth 4:17 – "The neighbor women gave him a name, saying, 'There is a son born to Naomi.' And they called his name Obed. He is the father of Jesse, the father of David."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Cultural Significance of Bitterness vs. Sweetness: In Hebrew thought, bitterness represents affliction, captivity, and death (reminiscent of the bitter herbs eaten during Passover to remember Egyptian bondage). In contrast, pleasantness represents life, peace, and divine blessing.

• The Redeemer's Role: Boaz acts as the goel (kinsman-redeemer) who redeems the land of Elimelech and Naomi. This act of redemption legally and socially restores Naomi's status, showing that biblical pleasantness is restored not through self-effort, but through the intervention of a redeemer.
