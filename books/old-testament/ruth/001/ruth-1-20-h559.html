SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H0559 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb 'amar is a foundational Old Testament word meaning "to say," "speak," "declare," or "command." In Ruth 1:20, it marks a pivotal moment of raw, vulnerable communication where Naomi redefines her identity from "pleasant" (Naomi) to "bitter" (Mara) in the wake of severe personal loss. This verb highlights not just the utterance of words, but the deep, intentional expression of a wounded heart crying out to her community and to God.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root 'amar is one of the most frequently used verbs in the Hebrew Bible. At its core, it refers to the externalization of internal thoughts. It is more than just making vocal sounds; it involves the formulation of an idea, intent, or decree and the intentional delivery of that message to an audience.

• In ancient Near Eastern culture, spoken words carried substantial weight, often functioning as binding agreements, prophecies, or deeply felt declarations of reality. When someone of Naomi's status in the community "said" something upon her return to Bethlehem, it was not casual conversation; it was a formal declaration of her spiritual and physical state.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary meaning of 'amar is "to say," "to speak," or "to utter."

• Depending on the grammatical context, it can also mean "to command" (especially when spoken by a king or God), "to promise," "to intend," or "to think to oneself" (often expressed as "said in his heart").

• In the Qal stem (as found in Ruth 1:20), it represents the simple, active expression of speech, denoting the direct address of one person to another.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Divine Speech: 'amar is famously used in Genesis 1 ("And God said, 'Let there be light'"), where speech is the vehicle of creative power.

• Internal Monologue: Frequently, the word is used to describe thought. To "say in one's heart" is the Hebrew idiom for thinking or reasoning internally.

• Formal Declaration: In historical narratives, the word introduces authoritative declarations, legal testimony, or emotional outpourings that change the course of relationships.

4. Theological Implications

• Speech as an indicator of the soul: Scripture emphasizes that out of the abundance of the heart, the mouth speaks. When 'amar is used of human beings in distress, it reveals their underlying theology and emotional processing.

• Lament and Honesty before God: Naomi's speech in Ruth 1:20 demonstrates that biblical faith allows room for honest, painful expression. She does not hide her grief or sugarcoat her pain; she speaks her reality directly, acknowledging God's sovereignty even while wrestling with the bitterness of her circumstances.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:20, Naomi uses 'amar to interrupt the excited chatter of the women of Bethlehem who ask, "Is this Naomi?"

• Her response, "Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara," is a direct counter-speech. The verb introduces a formal renaming ceremony of her own making.

• By "saying" this, Naomi is not merely venting; she is legally and socially defining her current existence. She attributes her bitterness directly to the sovereign hand of the Almighty (Shaddai), showing that her spoken words are a form of honest, albeit grieved, theological reflection.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:3 - "Then God said, 'Let there be light'; and there was light."

• Genesis 22:2 - "And He said, 'Take now your son, your only son Isaac, whom you love...'"

• Exodus 3:14 - "And God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM.' And He said, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel...'"

• Psalm 14:1 - "The fool has said in his heart, 'There is no God.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• In Hebrew narrative, the dialogue introduced by 'amar is highly structured. When Naomi speaks here, it is the first time she addresses the community of Bethlehem since her departure. Her words break years of silence and shift the focus from the town's speculation to her personal, lived tragedy.

• The contrast between what she "says" here and the quiet, loyal actions of Ruth highlights the tension of the narrative: Naomi speaks of emptiness, while the physical presence of Ruth standing beside her represents the beginning of God's provision.
