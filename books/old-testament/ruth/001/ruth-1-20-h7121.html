CALL - ( qara , Strong's H7121 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qara fundamentally means to call out, summon, name, or proclaim. In ancient Israel, naming was not merely a matter of convenience, but an expression of a person's essence, character, or destiny. In Ruth 1:20, Naomi uses this word to demand a shift in her communal identity, rejecting her birth name "Naomi" (pleasant) and demanding to be called "Mara" (bitter) to reflect her internal grief and perceived empty state under God's providence.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb qara is one of the most frequent and expressive terms in the Old Testament, representing the vocal act of inviting, naming, or declaring. In ancient Near Eastern culture, to call something by a name was an act of defining its existence, role, and relationship to others. In the creation account, God called the light day and the darkness night, establishing their boundaries and functions. Calling someone by a name is an assertion of character or a reflection of historical reality. In the context of the book of Ruth, Naomi's return to Bethlehem is a public event. The community's greeting ("Is this Naomi?") prompts her to use qara to redefine how others should view and address her in her state of affliction.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb qara has a broad semantic range that includes several distinct nuances:

• To call out, cry aloud, or raise one's voice.

• To summon, invite, or gather individuals to a specific place or task.

• To proclaim, announce, or read aloud publicly.

• To name, designate, or assign an identity.

In Ruth 1:20, the verb is used in the imperative form ("Do not call... call...") in the sense of assigning a name or designating an identity that matches a current reality. It refers to the public recognition of a person's status.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Call of God: God calls individuals by name to commission them for service (such as Moses at the burning bush or Samuel in the tabernacle), denoting selection, relationship, ownership, and purpose.

• Naming of Children: The term is frequently used in birth narratives where a parent names a child based on the circumstances of their birth or a prophetic hope (such as Rachel naming Ben-Oni or Leah naming her sons). Naomi reverses this birth-naming practice by renaming herself in her old age and widowhood.

• Summoning to a Feast: The word represents inviting guests to a shared meal or sacrifice, portraying fellowship and community.

4. Theological Implications

• Identity and Sovereignty: Naomi’s desire to change her name from "Pleasant" to "Bitter" shows how deep suffering can distort a believer's perception of their identity before God. She believes the Almighty (Shaddai) has dealt bitterly with her, and she wants her name to proclaim this theological stance to her community.

• The Power of Naming: Throughout Scripture, God changes names (Abram to Abraham, Jacob to Israel) to align with His redemptive covenant promises. Naomi, acting out of grief, attempts to change her own name to reflect her current pain, bypassing the ultimate redemptive story God is still writing.

• Grace Over Bitter Proclamation: Although Naomi calls herself "Bitter," God does not accept this as her permanent identity. The narrative demonstrates that God is actively working to restore her to "Pleasantness" through Boaz and Obed, showing that our self-proclaimed names of bitterness do not override God's ultimate decree of blessing.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:20, Naomi is speaking to the women of Bethlehem who excitedly ask, "Is this Naomi?" Her name means "my pleasantness" or "sweetness." The word qara occurs twice in this verse: "Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara." By using qara, Naomi is demanding that the community stop acknowledging her old, joyful identity and start recognizing her present desolation. She is instructing her community to validate her grief. Her bitterness is so consuming that she wants every greeting in the marketplace to serve as a memorial to her loss.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Hebrew verb qara shares a homophonic relationship in some contexts with qarah (to happen, meet, or befall), though they are linguistically distinct roots. Naomi feels that bitter things have "befallen" her, and she now demands to be "called" bitter.

• The imperative use of qara by Naomi indicates her active agency in her grief. She is not passively suffering; she is actively reshaping her social narrative.
