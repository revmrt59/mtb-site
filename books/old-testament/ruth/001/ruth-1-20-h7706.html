ALMIGHTY - ( Shaddai , Strong's H7706 )

Summary:

The title Shaddai (Almighty) portrays God as the all-powerful, sovereign Ruler who governs the affairs of humanity, possessing ultimate power to bless, sustain, or chasten. In the book of Ruth, Naomi uses this title to express her deep grief, acknowledging that her bitter circumstances are not the result of blind fate or random tragedy, but are ultimately under the sovereign administration of the Almighty. Though she feels emptied by His heavy hand, the title itself carries a dual assurance: the God who has the sovereign power to empty also possesses the absolute sufficiency to fill, sustain, and restore His covenant people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The linguistic background of Shaddai carries several rich associations that contribute to its depth. A prominent historical connection is to the Hebrew verb shadad, which means "to destroy," "to devastate," or "to exhibit violent power." This portrays God as an irresistible, unstoppable force whose power cannot be opposed by any human or spiritual agency. Another connection is to the Akkadian word shadu, meaning "mountain." In this light, the title depicts God as the "One of the Mountain," representing His unchanging stability, eternal strength, and status as an immovable refuge for His people. An ancient secondary association links the word to the Hebrew shad (breast), which symbolizes a life-giver, nurturer, and provider. While the primary biblical emphasis remains on God's transcendent, sovereign power, these backgrounds collectively present a God who is both infinitely powerful to rule and infinitely sufficient to nurture and sustain those in covenant with Him.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Shaddai denotes the "Almighty" or "All-Sufficient One." Lexically, it refers to the supreme Deity who possesses absolute power and dominion over all creation. It describes a God who is entirely self-sufficient, requiring nothing outside of Himself to accomplish His will, and who is fully capable of fulfilling His promises, dispensing blessings, or executing sovereign discipline. In the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament (the Septuagint), Shaddai is frequently translated as Pantokrator (the Ruler over All) or Hikanos (the All-Sufficient One), reinforcing His limitless authority, supreme power, and capacity to sustain His people through any trial.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Shaddai occurs 48 times in the Old Testament, predominantly in the book of Job (31 times) and in the patriarchal narratives of Genesis.

• The Sovereign over Nature and Fertility: In the Genesis narratives, the title is used when God commands the laws of nature to bring about fertility, life, and multiplication where human limitations make it impossible (e.g., Genesis 17:1, 28:3).

• The Irresistible Sovereign in Judgment: In the prophetic books, Shaddai represents an overwhelming, destructive power that executes holy justice, against which no human army or nation can stand (e.g., Isaiah 13:6, Joel 1:15).

• The Hand of Discipline: In the book of Job, the title is frequently used by Job and his friends to describe the sovereign source of human suffering and divine chastening, recognizing that prosperity and adversity both come from His hand.

4. Theological Implications

• The Sovereignty of God in Suffering: The theology of Shaddai establishes that God is sovereign over both blessing and calamity. Human suffering does not occur outside of His dominion. When adversity strikes, the believer is reminded that God remains in complete control, governing all events according to His holy purposes.

• Covenant Sufficiency: Shaddai is the God who is more than enough. He does not depend on human strength, wealth, or ideal circumstances to fulfill His covenant. When human resources are completely exhausted, the power of the Almighty becomes most visible.

• The Dual Nature of Divine Dealing: The power to empty and the power to fill belong to the same God. The theology of Shaddai prevents a one-dimensional view of God, showing that the same hands that permit wounding and emptying are the hands that bind up, heal, and restore.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:20, Naomi returns to Bethlehem from Moab empty-handed, having lost her husband and both of her sons. When the women of the city excitedly call her by her name "Naomi" (which means "Pleasant" or "Sweet"), she immediately objects: "Do not call me Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me."

By using the title Shaddai, Naomi demonstrates a profound, albeit painful, theological conviction. She does not attribute her losses to bad luck, economic downturn, or the power of Moabite deities. Instead, she traces her circumstances directly to the hand of the covenant God. She recognizes that it is Shaddai who has sovereignly emptied her.

The contextual irony of her statement lies in the character of the title she chooses. While Naomi uses Shaddai to express her grief over being broken and depleted, Shaddai is the very title associated with fruitfulness, multiplication, and divine provision. By crying out to Shaddai, Naomi inadvertently appeals to the only One who possesses the absolute power and sufficiency to turn her bitterness into sweetness, empty her womb no longer, and restore her family line—a redemption that unfolds beautifully through the rest of the narrative.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 17:1: "When Abram was ninety-nine years old, the LORD appeared to Abram and said to him, 'I am Almighty God; walk before Me and be blameless.'"

• Genesis 35:11: "Also God said to him: 'I am God Almighty. Be fruitful and multiply; a nation and a company of nations shall proceed from you, and kings shall come from your body.'"

• Job 5:17: "Behold, happy is the man whom God corrects; therefore do not despise the chastening of the Almighty."

• Joel 1:15: "Alas for the day! For the day of the LORD is at hand; it shall come as destruction from the Almighty."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Septuagint Translation of Ruth 1:20: The translators of the Septuagint chose the Greek word ho Hikanos (the Sufficient One / the One who is Enough) to translate Shaddai in this verse. This translation highlights a powerful pastoral truth: even when Naomi felt entirely emptied and broken, she was still in the hands of the One who is more than enough to sustain her.

• The Covenant Contrast: In Ruth 1:20-21, Naomi alternates between the covenant name Yahweh (translated "the LORD") and Shaddai ("the Almighty"). Yahweh highlights God’s relational intimacy and covenant loyalty, while Shaddai highlights His transcendent, cosmic power. Even in her deepest bitterness, Naomi's theology remains anchored; she knows that the God of intimate covenant love (Yahweh) is also the God of absolute sovereign power (Shaddai).
