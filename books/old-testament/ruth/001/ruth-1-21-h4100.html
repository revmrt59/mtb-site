WHY - ( mah , Strong's H4100 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word mah is a primary interrogative pronoun most frequently translated as "what," but when used in contexts of distress or questioning purpose, it functions as "why" or "how." In Ruth 1:21, Naomi uses this word not as a simple request for information, but as a poignant cry of grief, protesting her identity. Having returned empty and widowed from Moab, she challenges the women of Bethlehem for calling her "Naomi" (Pleasant), insisting that her circumstances no longer match her name because the Almighty has dealt bitterly with her.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• mah is one of the most common words in the Hebrew Bible, serving as the foundational interrogative. Its vocalization shifts depending on the prefix or the consonant that follows it. It seeks to get to the essence of a matter, demanding an explanation or exposing an incongruity between reality and expectation. In the ancient Near East, a person's name was not merely a label but a reflection of their character, destiny, or current state of being. Naomi's name means "pleasant, sweet, or delightful." To be addressed by this name while carrying the crushing weight of bereavement and poverty felt like an unbearable irony. Her use of mah ("why") functions as a rhetorical and emotional shield, highlighting the painful gap between her name's meaning and her current raw reality.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word functions primarily as an interrogative pronoun. Its primary meanings include:

• "What?" (inquiring about an object, event, or concept).

• "How?" (expressing exclamation, wonder, or intensity, as in "How beautiful!").

• "Why?" (questioning the reason, purpose, or justification for an action, often conveying protest, complaint, or dismay).

In the context of Ruth 1:21, the word is used as an adverbial interrogative of protest. It challenges the validity of an action, asking, "On what ground?" or "To what purpose do you call me Naomi?"

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• mah can express various emotional registers depending on its context:

• Despair and Lament: When a speaker feels overwhelmed by suffering, mah is used to question God's delay or man's hostility.

• Indignation and Protest: It can demand a defense from an accuser, challenging the basis of an action.

• Awe and Admiration: It can introduce an exclamation of wonder, such as praising the majesty of God's name.

In Ruth 1:21, the word paints a picture of a soul in deep lament. It is a linguistic marker of disorientation. Naomi is disoriented by the sovereignty of God, which has stripped her of husband, sons, and wealth. The question "Why?" marks her struggle to reconcile her past identity with her present misery.

4. Theological Implications

Naomi's "why" exposes the reality of honest grief in the life of a believer. Scripture does not sanitize the pain of the saints. By questioning her name, Naomi is ultimately questioning the hand of God behind her circumstances. She attributes her empty state directly to Yahweh ("the LORD has brought me home again empty... the Almighty has afflicted me").

Theologically, this "why" demonstrates that suffering can distort our perception of identity and covenant relationship. Naomi believes God has turned against her as an adversary ("testified against me"). Yet, the use of the divine titles Yahweh (the covenant-keeping God) and Shaddai (the Almighty) reveals that even in her bitter "why," her faith remains anchored in the reality of God's absolute sovereignty. Her protest is addressed to the only One who has the power to change her situation.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:21, the context is Naomi's homecoming to Bethlehem after ten years in Moab. The town is stirred, asking, "Is this Naomi?" (Ruth 1:19). Naomi reacts to their recognition with a heavy-hearted correction. She contrasts her departure ("I went out full") with her return ("brought me home again empty"). Her "why" (mah) in verse 21 ("Why do you call me Naomi...") is a defense mechanism against a title she feels she no longer deserves. She instructs them to call her Mara (bitter) instead. The nuance of mah here is not a search for an academic answer; the women of Bethlehem cannot answer "why" they call her Naomi other than that it is her name. Rather, it is an expression of cognitive and spiritual dissonance. Naomi is saying, "It is a mockery to call me 'pleasant' when the Lord has made my life so bitter."

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:18: "And Pharaoh called Abram and said, 'What (mah) is this you have done to me? Why (mah) did you not tell me that she was your wife?'"

• Exodus 14:15: "And the LORD said to Moses, 'Why (mah) do you cry to Me? Tell the children of Israel to go forward.'"

• Psalm 2:1: "Why (mah) do the nations rage, And the people plot a vain thing?"

• Psalm 42:5: "Why (mah) are you cast down, O my soul? And why (mah) are you disquieted within me? Hope in God, for I shall yet praise Him For the help of His countenance."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The narrative irony of Naomi's "why" is that she cannot see the end of the story. She claims the Lord has brought her back "empty," yet Ruth is standing right beside her. Ruth, who has pledged lifelong covenant loyalty to Naomi and her God, will become the instrument of Naomi's restoration. Naomi's bitter question shows how grief can blind us to God's immediate provisions. The very emptiness Naomi laments is the blank canvas upon which God is about to paint His greatest masterpiece of redemption, leading to the birth of Obed, Jesse, David, and ultimately, Jesus Christ.
