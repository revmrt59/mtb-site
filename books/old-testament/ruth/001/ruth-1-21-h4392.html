FULL - ( male' , Strong's H4392 )

Summary:

The Hebrew adjective male' describes that which is filled to capacity, abundant, or complete. In Naomi’s lament in Ruth 1:21, the term represents her former state of life-abundance—possessing a husband, two sons, and a secure place within her covenant community before famine and tragedy stripped her of these blessings. Naomi uses this term to contrast her past relational completeness with her current state of physical and emotional emptiness, highlighting her raw, honest perception of God’s sovereign dealing with her life.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, fullness was directly tied to concrete realities: a successful harvest, a cistern overflowing with water, a quiver full of children, or a household abundant with livestock and provisions. Conversely, emptiness was a sign of drought, famine, barrenness, and divine displeasure. To be "full" was to experience shalom—a state of wholeness, security, and integration within the community and family structure. For an Israelite woman, fullness was deeply relational, realized through a husband’s protection and the birth of sons to carry on the family name and inheritance.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The adjective male' denotes a state of being replete, fully filled, or having reached maximum capacity. It describes physical objects that are filled to the brim, such as vessels, houses, or lands, as well as abstract concepts like a complete span of time, a heart filled with emotion, or a person endowed with attributes like wisdom or the presence of God. It stands in direct semantic opposition to the Hebrew word reiqam ("empty"), emphasizing the stark transition from a state of total abundance to one of complete deprivation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Abundant Provision: The word describes houses filled with goods, wells filled with water, and fields ready for harvest, illustrating God's tangible, covenantal blessings upon His people.

• Character and Spiritual Endowment: It is used to describe individuals who are filled with intangible qualities, such as Joshua being filled with the spirit of wisdom, or a person filled with justice or praise.

• Physical Space and Divine Presence: It characterizes the temple or the earth being filled with the glory, majesty, or train of Yahweh, indicating a presence so dense and complete that no room remains for anything else.

4. Theological Implications

The concept of male' in the Old Testament underscores the sovereignty of God as the ultimate source of both fullness and emptiness. Human fullness is fragile, temporal, and subject to change under the providential hand of God. When Naomi laments her transition from fullness to emptiness, she recognizes that her life is not governed by random chance or fate, but by the direct action of Yahweh. This Hebrew concept teaches that God sometimes allows His servants to be emptied of self-sufficiency and worldly security so that they might be prepared to receive a deeper, more enduring fullness that comes from His sovereign grace alone.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:21, Naomi cries out, "I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty." There is profound irony in her statement. Historically, Naomi and Elimelech left Bethlehem ("House of Bread") because of a famine—meaning they left because they were physically empty. Yet, Naomi looks back and realizes that despite the famine, she was "full" because she had her husband and her two sons. Her family was her true abundance. Returning to Bethlehem, the land is now physically "full" (the barley harvest has begun), but Naomi herself feels utterly empty. The juxtaposition of male' (full) and reiqam (empty) captures the depth of her grief and her struggle to reconcile God's past goodness with her present sorrow.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Deuteronomy 6:11: "houses full of all good things, which you did not fill, hewn-out wells which you did not dig, vineyards and olive trees which you did not plant—when you have eaten and are full,"

• Deuteronomy 34:9: "Now Joshua the son of Nun was full of the spirit of wisdom, for Moses had laid his hands on him; so the children of Israel heeded him, and did as the LORD had commanded Moses."

• Ecclesiastes 11:3: "If the clouds are full of rain, they empty themselves upon the earth; and if a tree falls to the south or the north, in the place where the tree falls, there it shall be."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural architecture of the Book of Ruth relies heavily on the tension between fullness and emptiness. The narrative begins with emptiness (famine in chapter 1), moves to the hope of fullness (the beginning of the barley harvest in 1:22), progresses through Ruth filling her apron with grain from Boaz's field (chapters 2 and 3), and culminates in absolute fullness in chapter 4 with the marriage of Ruth and the birth of Obed. Naomi’s empty lap is ultimately filled with a grandson who will preserve her family line, demonstrating that God's ultimate plan for His covenant people is restoration, redemption, and eternal fullness.
