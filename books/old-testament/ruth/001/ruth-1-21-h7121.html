CALL - ( qara , Strong's H7121 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb qara means to call, summon, invite, or proclaim. In the context of naming, it signifies designating an identity, establishing a character, or declaring a state of being. When Naomi asks her community not to call her "Naomi" (pleasant) but instead "Mara" (bitter) in Ruth 1:21, she is requesting a public recognition of her altered life circumstances and her deeply felt spiritual and emotional state.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root qara is one of the most frequent verbs in the Hebrew Bible. In the ancient Near East, naming was far more than a means of identification; it was an exercise of authority, a description of a person's character, or a prophetic declaration of their destiny. To "call" a name was to assign function, value, and identity. When God created the cosmos, He immediately "called" the elements by name, establishing their purpose. Thus, when Naomi speaks of what people "call" her, she is addressing her community's public assessment of her life, her status, and her identity before God and neighbor.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary lexical ranges of qara include:

• To cry out, shout, or make a loud vocal utterance.

• To summon, invite to a feast, or assemble a group.

• To read aloud or proclaim a public decree.

• To designate, name, or call a person or object by a specific title.

In the Qal stem, when paired with a person's name, it refers to the act of calling or naming someone. In Ruth 1:21, the verb appears in the prefix (imperfect) form, indicating an ongoing or habitual action: "Why do you keep calling me Naomi?"

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Call of God: qara represents God's active summoning of individuals to service, such as calling Moses from the burning bush or calling the young Samuel in the tabernacle.

• The Cry of Faith: It depicts the desperate, vocal cry of human prayer to Yahweh in times of distress, portraying an intimate, vocalized dependence on God.

• Public Proclamation: It is used for calling a solemn assembly or proclaiming a fast, representing a communal call to spiritual alignment.

• Naming and Sovereignty: When parents name their children or rulers name their subjects, it demonstrates authority and care. Naomi's request to change her name is a plea for her community to align their vocabulary with her perceived reality.

4. Theological Implications

Identity and destiny are intertwined with names in Scripture. Naomi’s name means "my pleasantness" or "sweetness." To bear this name while standing widowed, childless, and destitute felt like a theological contradiction and an unbearable irony. By asking to be called "Mara" (bitter), she is making a theological statement about God's treatment of her. She believes that the Almighty (Shaddai) has dealt bitterly with her. This reveals the raw, honest lament common in Hebrew poetry and narrative, where believers do not hide their pain behind polite spiritual jargon but openly voice their sorrow to and about God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:20-21, Naomi has returned to Bethlehem after ten years in Moab. The town is stirred, asking, "Is this Naomi?" She responds, "Do not call (qara) me Naomi; call (qara) me Mara." She repeats the verb in verse 21: "Why do you call (qara) me Naomi, since the LORD has testified against me...?" Naomi is rejecting a title that belongs to her past. She refuses to live a lie or maintain a facade of "pleasantness" when her soul is empty. She demands that her community acknowledge her grief. The tragedy of her empty hands is contrasted with the fullness of her past, making the name "Naomi" feel like an painful mockery of her current existence.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between Naomi's self-designation ("Mara") and her original name ("Naomi") highlights the narrative arc of the book of Ruth. While Naomi insists on being called "bitter," the author of the book never actually calls her Mara; she is referred to as Naomi throughout the rest of the story. This subtle narrative detail suggests that God was not finished with her, and her season of bitterness would not define her ultimate identity.

• The Septuagint translates qara in Ruth 1:21 with the Greek verb kaleo, which similarly denotes calling, summoning, or naming.
