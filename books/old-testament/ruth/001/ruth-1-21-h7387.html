EMPTY - ( reyqam , Strong's H7387 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:21, Naomi uses this word to express the absolute depletion of her family, security, and hope. It is an adverb describing the condition of being sent away or returning completely empty-handed, devoid of substance, resources, or success. This poignant term sets up a profound theological contrast between human emptiness and God's sovereign, filling grace, highlighting Naomi's deep grief while laying the groundwork for the Lord's eventual restoration.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The adverb reyqam is derived from the root verb riq, which means to pour out, empty, or draw out (as a sword). The word picture is that of a container—such as a jar, a vessel, or a purse—that has had its entire contents completely poured out, leaving absolutely nothing behind. It represents a state of total evacuation. In the ancient Near East, returning or being sent away reyqam carried a sense of deep shame, vulnerability, and legal or social failure. It was the physical reality of having no food, no wealth, and no heirs to secure one's future.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Reyqam functions as an adverb meaning "empty," "empty-handed," "without success," "in vain," or "without cause." Lexically, it describes the state of a person who goes or is sent away without the material goods, family members, or blessings that they reasonably expected to possess or retain. It signifies a complete lack of substance, pointing to an existential and material vacuum.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, reyqam is used in three primary contexts:

• Legal and Social Protections: The law of God demanded that discharged Hebrew servants must not be sent away reyqam (empty-handed), but generously supplied from the flock, threshing floor, and winepress.

• Worship and Covenant Duties: Israelites were commanded not to appear before the Lord reyqam during the annual feasts; they were to bring an offering in proportion to how the Lord had blessed them.

• Military and Physical Defeat: It describes soldiers returning from battle without spoil, or a sword returning "empty" because it failed to accomplish its purpose.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, reyqam contrasts sharply with God's design for fullness and blessing. In the Old Testament, fullness (male') is a sign of divine favor, covenant keeping, and life. Emptiness (reyqam) is associated with exile, famine, judgment, and death. Naomi’s declaration that she went out full but returned empty attributes both states to the hand of Yahweh. This reveals a rugged, honest faith: she does not believe in blind fate but in a sovereign God who governs both the giving and the taking. Furthermore, this emptiness serves as the necessary canvas for God’s grace. In the economy of God, spiritual and physical emptiness is often the prerequisite for experiencing the miraculous, filling redemption of covenant love (hesed).

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:21, Naomi cries out: "I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty." The contrast is stark and literary.

• "I went out full": When Naomi left Bethlehem ("House of Bread") due to famine, she was physically poor, but she was "full" because she had a husband (Elimelech) and two sons (Mahlon and Chilion). She had a family, a legacy, and a future.

• "The LORD has brought me home again empty": She returns to Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest, surrounded by physical food, yet she herself feels completely reyqam. She has lost her husband and both sons. She has no grandchildren. In her estimation, she is a barren branch with no legal protector, no financial standing, and no lineage.

• The Irony of Grace: Naomi is so blinded by her grief that she declares herself completely "empty" while Ruth, her faithful daughter-in-law who has pledged her life to her, is standing right next to her. God has not left her entirely empty; He has provided the instrument of her future fullness in Ruth, who will eventually bear a son to fill Naomi's empty arms.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 31:42 - "Unless the God of my father, the God of Abraham and the Fear of Isaac, had been with me, surely now you would have sent me away empty-handed."

• Exodus 23:15 - "You shall keep the Feast of Unleavened Bread... and none shall appear before Me empty;"

• Deuteronomy 16:16 - "Three times a year all your males shall appear before the LORD your God in the place which He chooses... and they shall not appear before the LORD empty-handed;"

• 2 Samuel 1:22 - "From the blood of the slain, from the fat of the mighty, the bow of Jonathan did not turn back, and the sword of Saul did not return empty."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural symmetry of the Book of Ruth revolves around the transition from emptiness to fullness. The narrative begins with famine (emptiness) in the land and emptiness in Naomi's family. It transitions through the loyalty of Ruth and the redeemer-character of Boaz, culminating in chapter 4, where Boaz gives Ruth six measures of barley so she does not return to her mother-in-law reyqam (Ruth 3:17). The book ends with Naomi holding a baby boy (Obed) in her lap, her emptiness fully reversed by the Lord's abundant provision.
