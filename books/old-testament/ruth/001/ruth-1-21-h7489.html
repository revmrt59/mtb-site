AFFLICTED - ( ra'a' , Strong's H7489 )

Summary:

Naomi returns to Bethlehem grieving the loss of her husband and sons. In her deep pain, she declares that the Almighty has "afflicted" her. The Hebrew verb used here is ra'a', which carries the primary sense of bringing distress, calamity, or brokenness. Rather than viewing her grief as a series of random tragedies, Naomi directly attributes her painful circumstances to the sovereign hand of God. The word highlights the profound tension between God's sovereign control and the real, shattering pain of human suffering, which ultimately serves as the backdrop for His redemptive grace.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root verb ra'a' fundamentally relates to that which is bad, spoiled, or dysfunctional, standing in direct contrast to that which is good (tov). In its concrete origins, the word suggests something that has been broken, shattered, or made useless. When applied to human experience in its causative form, it represents the act of bringing calamity, adversity, or ruin upon someone. It paints a picture of a life that has been disrupted and broken by heavy adversity, stripped of its original wholeness, functionality, and utility.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew verb ra'a' (specifically in the Hiphil stem as used in Ruth 1:21) means to treat badly, to bring harm, to cause disaster, or to afflict. While the simple form of the verb refers to being morally bad or displeasing, the causative form shifts the focus to the action of inflicting adversity or distress. In the context of divine action, it refers to the sovereign act of God bringing hard, painful, and shattering circumstances into the life of an individual or a nation. It does not imply moral evil on God's part, but rather the heavy weight of His severe providences.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, ra'a' is employed in three primary dimensions:

• Moral and Ethical: Doing what is evil or wicked in the sight of the Lord.

• Relational: Treating another person harshly, violently, or unjustly.

• Circumstantial and Providential: Bringing calamity, disaster, or grief upon a person.

In this third sense, the word acts as a vivid word picture of crushing weight. It describes an experience where the pleasantness of life is utterly broken, leaving behind a state of ruin, emptiness, and bitter grief.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, Naomi's use of ra'a' introduces a profound truth regarding the sovereignty of God over human suffering:

• Divine Sovereignty over Calamity: Scripture does not shield God from the responsibility of His children's trials. Naomi correctly understands that her losses did not escape God's notice or fall outside His sovereign plan. He is the one who has allowed the emptying.

• The Character of the Afflicter: Naomi addresses God as Shaddai (the Almighty)—the God of all-sufficiency and bounty. There is a deep theological tension in her words: the God who has the power to bless is also the one who has sovereignly chosen to afflict.

• The Purpose of Affliction: Though Naomi feels ruined and finished, the broader narrative of Ruth reveals that God’s "afflicting" is never the final word. The shattering of her old life in Moab was the necessary prelude to her integration into the lineage of the Messiah. God's wounding is often the severe mercy that prepares the way for His greatest blessings.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:21, Naomi’s use of ra'a' occurs within a raw, emotional lament. Having left Bethlehem "full" with a husband and two sons, she returns "empty." She instructs the women of the city not to call her Naomi ("pleasant") but Mara ("bitter"). When she states that the Almighty has "afflicted" her, she is processing the shattering of her family. The word captures the intensely personal nature of her grief. She does not merely feel unlucky; she feels actively targeted by the hard hand of God. The context shows that Naomi's theology is accurate regarding God's sovereignty, but her perception is limited; she cannot yet see the hidden threads of redemption being woven through her affliction.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The linguistic connection between ra'a' (to afflict/bring calamity) and ra' (the common noun for evil or adversity) highlights a crucial distinction in Hebrew thought. While God is never the author of moral evil, He is explicitly stated to be the sovereign controller of circumstantial adversity (ra'). This ancient worldview did not seek to defend God's reputation by claiming He is passive in suffering; instead, it found comfort in the fact that even the most bitter afflictions are entirely under His almighty hand and subject to His ultimate redemptive purposes.
