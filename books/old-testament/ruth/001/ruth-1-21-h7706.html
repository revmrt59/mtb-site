ALMIGHTY - ( Shaddai , Strong's H7706 )

Summary:

The title Shaddai (Almighty) reveals God as the all-powerful, sovereign Ruler who possesses ultimate dominion over all circumstances, both to bless and to afflict. In Ruth 1:21, Naomi uses this name to express her profound grief, recognizing that her bitter losses did not occur by chance, but under the sovereign hand of an all-powerful God. While she views this power as the source of her current affliction, the narrative ultimately reveals that the Almighty's sovereignty works through sorrow to bring about redemption and abundant life.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The origin of the title Shaddai is ancient and multi-faceted. One possible root is shadad, which means to destroy, devastate, or display overpowering strength. This emphasizes God's absolute, unstoppable power as Judge and Ruler. Another potential connection is to the Akkadian word shadu, meaning "mountain," depicting God as an immovable, towering fortress of strength. Historically, some have connected it to the word shad (breast), implying a God of nurturing sufficiency who supplies all needs. In the patriarchal narratives, El Shaddai is the God who commands nature, promises fertility, and guarantees covenant fulfillment despite human impossibility.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term Shaddai means "Almighty," "All-Powerful," or "Sovereign One." It refers to the supreme power of God to govern all creation, execute His will, and fulfill His covenant promises. In the Septuagint, the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, Shaddai is frequently translated as Pantokrator ("the Almighty" or "Ruler of All") or Hikanos ("the Sufficient One" or "the All-Powerful").

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Shaddai occurs 48 times in the Hebrew Old Testament. It is heavily concentrated in the book of Job (31 times), where it highlights God's incomprehensible sovereignty in the midst of human suffering.

• It is often paired with El (God) as El Shaddai (God Almighty), particularly in Genesis, where God reveals Himself to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob to guarantee covenant blessings, fruitfulness, and multiplication.

• The word picture is that of an ultimate, sovereign King whose decrees cannot be thwarted, whose power controls both natural forces and human destinies, and whose nature is the ultimate source of security and provision for His people.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereignty in Suffering: The title Shaddai underscores that God is sovereign over both blessing and calamity. When believers experience deep loss, they do not look to a weak or indifferent deity, but to the Almighty, who holds absolute sway over all life.

• Covenant Faithfulness: The Almighty does not use His power arbitrarily. His power is tethered to His covenant faithfulness. Even when His actions seem severe or painful, His ultimate purpose is to bring His covenant promises to pass.

• The Paradox of Divine Power: Shaddai can strip away human self-sufficiency ("empty") so that His people are forced to rely solely on His divine sufficiency.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:21, Naomi has returned to Bethlehem from Moab after losing her husband and both sons. She changes her name from Naomi ("pleasant") to Mara ("bitter") because the Almighty (Shaddai) has dealt very bitterly with her.

• Naomi's use of Shaddai alongside Yahweh (the covenant name of God) is highly intentional. By using Shaddai, she acknowledges that her trials—famine, exile, and widowhood—were not random strokes of bad luck. They were directed by the hand of the all-powerful God.

• Her theology is correct in its recognition of God's absolute sovereignty over her pain, but her perspective is incomplete. She views the Almighty's power strictly as an instrument of affliction and emptiness. The rest of the book of Ruth serves as a narrative correction: the Almighty who emptied her is the very same Shaddai who will powerfully provide a redeemer, fill her empty arms, and restore her life, demonstrating that His sovereign power ultimately serves His redeeming love.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Contrast of Names: In Ruth 1:20-21, Naomi oscillates between Yahweh (the LORD) and Shaddai (the Almighty). Yahweh emphasizes His covenant-making, relational presence with Israel, while Shaddai emphasizes His cosmic power and ultimate authority to distribute destiny. Naomi feels the heavy, crushing weight of His cosmic power, momentarily losing sight of His relational, covenant love.

• The Redemptive Arc: This passage teaches pastors and counselors that God allows His children to express raw grief and honest confusion about His actions. Naomi's faith is strained but not broken; she still directs her complaints to the only One who has the power to reverse her fortunes.
