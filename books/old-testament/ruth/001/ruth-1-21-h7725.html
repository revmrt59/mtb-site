BROUGHT ME HOME AGAIN - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub is a foundational Old Testament word meaning to turn, return, go back, or restore. In the Hiphil (causative) stem used in Ruth 1:21, it signifies "caused to return" or "brought back." Naomi uses this word to describe her bittersweet return to Bethlehem. While she went out full with a husband and two sons, she recognizes that the Lord "brought her home again" stripped of her earthly security. This highlights both the sovereign hand of God in her affliction and the quiet covenant grace of her homecoming to the place of promise.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The verb shub is the twelfth most frequent verb in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over one thousand times. At its physical root, it paints a picture of movement: a traveler turning around to head back to their starting point, or a displaced person returning to their ancestral home. It implies a reversal of direction. In the ancient Near East, returning home was not merely a change of address; it was a return to one's source of identity, security, family inheritance, and covenant community. In the book of Ruth, this physical turning back carries immense spiritual and relational weight, as Naomi leaves the pagan fields of Moab to return to the land of Judah.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

In its basic Qal stem, shub means to turn back, return, or turn away. In the Hiphil (causative) stem, the verb means to cause to return, bring back, restore, or recompense. In Ruth 1:21, the verb appears as hešîbanî (the Hiphil perfect form with a first-person singular suffix). The literal meaning is "he has caused me to return" or "he has brought me back." The translation "brought me home again" accurately captures the relational and geographical target of this causative action. It expresses Naomi's conviction that her return was not merely a human trek, but a divine deportation.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb shub functions in three primary ways across the Old Testament:

• Physical Relocation: A simple change of physical position or returning to a geographic origin, such as returning to a house, land, or country.

• Spiritual Repentance: The act of turning away from sin and turning back to God. It is the primary Old Testament term for repentance, describing a total reorientation of life toward covenant faithfulness.

• Restoration and Reversal: The act of bringing back something lost, such as restoring property, turning back captivity, or reviving a weary soul.

### 4. Theological Implications

The use of shub in Ruth 1:21 carries profound theological significance:

• The Sovereignty of God in Affliction: Naomi does not credit her return to personal choice, economic necessity, or luck. She states that "the LORD" brought her home again. She recognizes God as the ultimate cause of both her stripping and her returning.

• The Necessity of Being Emptied for Restoration: The process of shub often involves being emptied before being filled. God brings Naomi back "empty" so that she can be filled with His covenant provision rather than her own self-sufficiency.

• Covenant Fidelity: Returning to Bethlehem is a return to the sphere of God's blessing. Even when His providence feels heavy or bitter, returning to the Lord's land and His people is the only path to ultimate redemption.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:21, Naomi experiences a deep personal crisis of identity. She left Bethlehem "full" with a husband and two sons, but she is brought back "empty." The literary placement of shub in this chapter is highly deliberate. The word occurs twelve times in chapter 1 alone, functioning as the theological and narrative engine of the scene. While Naomi repeatedly commands her daughters-in-law to "turn back" (shub) to their Moabite mothers and gods, Ruth refuses to turn back from Naomi. Ironically, Naomi complains that God has brought her back empty, yet she is accompanied by Ruth, who will become the very instrument God uses to restore Naomi's life.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:19: "In the sweat of your face you shall eat bread till you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; for dust you are, and to dust you shall return."

• Psalm 23:3: "He restores my soul; He leads me in the paths of righteousness for His name's sake."

• Joel 2:12: "Now, therefore," says the LORD, "Turn to Me with all your heart, with fasting, with weeping, and with mourning."

• Malachi 3:7: "Return to Me, and I will return to you," says the LORD of hosts. "But you said, 'In what way shall we return?'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The structural repetition of shub in Ruth 1 highlights the contrast between those who turn back to pagan security and those who press forward into covenant relationship. Orpah kisses Naomi and "turns back" (shub) to her people and her gods (Ruth 1:15). In contrast, Ruth cleaves to Naomi, refusing to turn back. By returning to Bethlehem, Naomi and Ruth are positioned for the arrival of the Kinsman-Redeemer, proving that even a return marked by bitter grief is a return to the place of hope.
