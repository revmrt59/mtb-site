MOAB - ( mo'ab , Strong's H4124 )

Summary:

Moab is a historical region east of the Dead Sea and the name of the nation descended from Lot through his eldest daughter. In the book of Ruth, Moab serves as a place of temporary physical refuge that quickly turns into a scene of devastating loss, setting the stage for Naomi's empty return and Ruth's remarkable journey of faith. The mention of Moab in Ruth 1:22 underscores the geographical and spiritual journey from a pagan land back to the house of bread (Bethlehem), highlighting God's sovereign grace in redeeming an outsider from a cursed nation.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

Moab refers both to the progenitor of the Moabite people and the mountainous plateau east of the Dead Sea where they settled. The name originates from the incestuous union between Lot and his eldest daughter following the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah. Semitically, the name suggests "from father," serving as a perpetual reminder of the family's shameful origin. Geographically, Moab was a fertile tableland situated directly across the Jordan Rift Valley from Judah. Although nearby, it was culturally, religiously, and politically distinct from Israel. Moab's fertility made it an attractive refuge during famines in Canaan, but its spiritual atmosphere was defined by the worship of Chemosh, a pagan deity associated with child sacrifice.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper noun mo'ab refers to the territory of Moab, the nation of Moab, or the eponymous ancestor of the Moabites. Lexically, the term designates the region bounded on the west by the Dead Sea, on the north by the Arnon River, and on the south by Edom. The people of Moab are characterized in biblical history as persistent adversaries of Israel, yet they shared a distant kinship through Abraham’s nephew, Lot.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, Moab represents more than just a political entity; it serves as a geographic symbol of compromise, idolatry, and divine judgment. During the wilderness wanderings, Moabite women seduced Israelite men into idolatry at Baal-Peor, leading to a devastating plague. In Israel's historical memory, Moab is often a picture of proud security and carnality, sitting undisturbed like wine settled on its dregs. Yet, in the narrative of Ruth, Moab is transformed from a symbol of pagan rejection into a background of surprising grace. It is the place where Elimelech's family loses everything, stripping away their earthly security so they might seek the Lord’s provision.

4. Theological Implications

The presence of Moab in the ancestry of David, and ultimately Jesus Christ, carries profound theological weight. Under the Mosaic Law, Moabites were barred from entering the assembly of the Lord to the tenth generation because of their hostility to Israel during the Exodus. However, the story of Ruth demonstrates that the covenant boundaries of Israel are not purely ethnic but are open to those of faith. When the Moabitess Ruth pledges allegiance to Naomi's God, her Moabite heritage is superseded by her adoption into the covenant family. This reveals that God's redeeming grace transcends national curses and ancient animosities, foreshadowing the inclusion of the Gentiles into the family of God.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:22, the phrase "who returned from the country of Moab" marks a pivotal transition in the narrative. The term "country of Moab" (sedeh mo'ab, literally "the fields of Moab") contrasts sharply with Bethlehem, "the house of bread." Elimelech's family left the place of God's promise for the pagan fields of Moab to escape a famine, only to find death and bereavement. By the end of chapter 1, Naomi returns "empty" from Moab, accompanied by Ruth the Moabitess. The repetition of Ruth's national origin in connection with Moab emphasizes the stark improbability of the redemption that is about to unfold in the barley fields of Bethlehem. Moab represents the dark valley of bereavement and foreign exile from which Naomi is delivered, and the unlikely home from which God extracts a vessel of covenant faithfulness.

• Jeremiah 48:11: "Moab has been at ease from his youth; He has settled on his dregs, And has not been emptied from vessel to vessel, Nor has he gone into captivity."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transition from Moab to Bethlehem is not merely a change in geography, but a movement from spiritual death to life. In Moab, three Hebrew men—Elimelech, Mahlon, and Chilion—die and are buried, symbolizing the dead end of seeking life apart from the Promised Land. Naomi's return from Moab at the beginning of the barley harvest represents the dawning of hope. The biblical narrative uses Moab as a dark foil to magnify the brightness of God's chesed (covenant love), showing that even the most desolate spiritual backgrounds can be redeemed when one turns toward the living God.
