HARVEST - ( qatsiyr , Strong's H7105 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word qatsiyr refers to the act of harvesting, the season of harvest, or the crops harvested. In Ruth 1:22, it marks a critical turning point in the narrative, signifying the transition from Naomi's bitter season of famine and loss in Moab to a season of abundance, provision, and divine restoration in Bethlehem. It represents not only an agricultural cycle but also the unfolding of God's providential grace for the empty and marginalized.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun qatsiyr derives from the root verb qatsar, which means "to reap, harvest, or shorten." In the agrarian society of ancient Israel, the harvest was the lifeblood of the community. It was a period of intense, backbreaking physical labor, but also a time of supreme joy, feast, and celebration of God's faithfulness. The agricultural year was punctuated by distinct harvests, starting with the barley harvest in the spring (coinciding with Passover) and moving into the wheat harvest (coinciding with Pentecost), before concluding with the grape and fruit harvests in autumn. The word picture of qatsiyr evokes images of sickles cutting standing grain, sheaves being bound, and threshing floors filled with abundance.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun qatsiyr carries three primary definitions depending on the context:

• The act or process of harvesting: The physical work of cutting and gathering crops.

• The time or season of harvest: The specific period in the agricultural calendar when crops are ripe for gathering.

• The crop harvested: The yield, harvest, or produce itself (such as grain or boughs).

In Ruth 1:22, qatsiyr refers specifically to the harvest season ("the beginning of barley harvest"), establishing the chronological setting for the events that follow.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, qatsiyr is used in both literal and figurative contexts:

• Literal Agriculture: It frequently describes the seasonal gathering of grains (wheat and barley) as commanded in the seasonal feasts of Israel.

• A Picture of Divine Judgment: Prophets often use the harvest metaphorically to describe the ripeness of a nation for judgment. When a nation's wickedness is fully ripe, God "puts in the sickle" for a harvest of retribution.

• A Picture of Spiritual Consequence: Scripture uses qatsiyr to illustrate the law of sowing and reaping. Sowing wickedness leads to a harvest of trouble, while those who sow in tears will reap a harvest of joy.

### 4. Theological Implications

The harvest is intimately tied to God's covenantal provision and His character of hesed (lovingkindness). Under the Mosaic Law, the harvest was not the exclusive property of the landowner. God commanded that the edges of the fields must not be gathered, and any dropped sheaves must be left for the poor, the stranger, the widow, and the fatherless (Leviticus 19:9-10). Thus, the qatsiyr was designed by God to be an instrument of social welfare and communal grace. It served as a physical reminder that the earth belongs to the Lord, and His people are merely stewards called to mirror His generosity.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:22, the arrival of Naomi and Ruth in Bethlehem "at the beginning of barley harvest" serves as a profound literary and spiritual pivot.

• Famine to Feast: The book of Ruth opens with a devastating "famine in the land" (Ruth 1:1) which forced Elimelech's family to leave Bethlehem (literally, "House of Bread") for Moab. The mention of qatsiyr signals that the famine is officially over and the House of Bread is full once again.

• Empty to Full: Naomi returned to Bethlehem crying out, "I went out full, and the Lord has brought me home again empty" (Ruth 1:21). The very next sentence notes the beginning of the barley harvest. The harvest is the physical answer to Naomi's emptiness, demonstrating that God was already preparing to fill her empty hands and heart.

• Divine Timing: The barley harvest is the earliest harvest of the spring, occurring around the time of Passover. This season of redemption and new life provides the perfect backdrop for Ruth, a Gentile Moabite widow, to find refuge under the wings of the God of Israel.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 8:22: "While the earth remains, / Seedtime and harvest, / Cold and heat, / Winter and summer, / And day and night / Shall not cease."

• Proverbs 10:5: "He who gathers in summer is a wise son; / He who sleeps in harvest is a son who causes shame."

• Isaiah 9:3: "You have multiplied the nation / And increased its joy; / They rejoice before You / According to the joy of harvest, / As men rejoice when they divide the spoil."

• Jeremiah 5:24: "Let us now fear the Lord our God, / Who gives rain, both the former and the latter, in its season. / He reserves for us the appointed weeks of the harvest."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

The mention of "barley harvest" specifically carries cultural significance. Barley was a hardier, less expensive crop than wheat, often associated with the diet of the poor and marginalized. Because the barley harvest was the first of the grain harvests, it meant that the gleaning laws were immediately active. This precise timing allowed Ruth to immediately go out into the fields of Boaz to secure food for herself and Naomi, setting in motion the providential encounters that would lead to the lineage of King David and ultimately, Jesus Christ.
