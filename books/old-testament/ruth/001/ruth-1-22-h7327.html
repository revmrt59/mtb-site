RUTH - ( Rûṯ , Strong's H7327 )

Summary:

The name Ruth represents loyalty, companionship, and the grace of God extending beyond Israel's borders. Derived from a root meaning "friendship" or "companion," her name beautifully mirrors her character and her pivotal role in redemptive history as a faithful daughter-in-law, a righteous Gentile, and an ancestor of King David and Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Ruth (Rûṯ) is likely a contraction of the Hebrew word re'ût, meaning "companion," "associate," or "female friend," derived from the root ra'ah (to associate with, tend, or shepherd). In the ancient Near East, names were often descriptive of a person's character, destiny, or role. For Ruth, her name serves as a perfect linguistic portrait of her life. She is the ultimate companion—not only to her grieving mother-in-law, Naomi, but also to the covenant community of Israel. Her story is set during the dark, chaotic period of the Judges, where "everyone did what was right in his own eyes." Against this backdrop of national and spiritual unfaithfulness, Ruth's name and actions shine as a brilliant beacon of steadfast love, devotion, and covenant loyalty.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, the name Rûṯ means "friendship," "companion," or "desirable companion." It functions as a proper feminine noun in the Old Testament, designating the Moabitess who left her homeland, family, and gods to cling to the God of Israel and her mother-in-law. The underlying root emphasizes close association, mutual care, and relational binding. Rather than being a mere label, the name encapsulates the theological and narrative essence of her identity: she is the faithful friend who chooses covenant relationship over personal safety, ease, and cultural familiarity.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The name Ruth appears 12 times in the Old Testament, exclusively within the book that bears her name. The narrative presents her name as a living word picture of chesed—the covenant-keeping, loyal love of God.

• The picture of clinging: When Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye, Ruth "clung" (dabaq) to her (Ruth 1:14). This verb is the same used for marriage in Genesis 2:24, illustrating the depth of her companion-like commitment.

• The picture of refuge: Boaz describes her as one who has come to take refuge under the "wings" of Yahweh, the God of Israel (Ruth 2:12). Her identity as a friend and companion is directly linked to her faith in God.

• The picture of redemption: Ruth's willingness to be a companion to Naomi leads her to Boaz, the kinsman-redeemer, portraying how human companionship and loyalty open the door for divine redemption.

4. Theological Implications

Theological implications of Ruth's name and story are profound:

• Inclusion of the Gentiles: Ruth is a Moabitess. Deuteronomy 23:3 placed strict restrictions on Moabites entering the assembly of Israel. Yet, through her faith-filled companionship, Ruth is welcomed into the community, demonstrating that God's grace and covenant family are ultimately defined by faith and loyalty rather than ethnic origin.

• The Lineage of the Messiah: Ruth's companionate faithfulness leads to her marriage with Boaz. This union produces Obed, the father of Jesse, the father of David (Ruth 4:17). Thus, Ruth the Moabitess is placed directly in the genealogy of Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:5), illustrating how God uses the humble, the foreign, and the loyal to accomplish His grand redemptive plan.

• Human Companion as a Reflection of Divine Grace: Ruth's unwavering devotion to Naomi reflects Yahweh's unwavering devotion to His people. In a time of national apostasy, Ruth models the true heart of covenant relationship.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:22, the text states, "So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess her daughter-in-law with her, who returned from the country of Moab." The placement of her name here is highly significant. Naomi left Bethlehem full but returned empty, bitter, and renaming herself Mara (bitter). Yet, the narrator pointedly notes that Ruth "returned" with her. Although Naomi felt utterly abandoned by God, the presence of Ruth—the "companion"—proves that God had not abandoned her. Ruth represents God's provision of comfort in the midst of grief. Furthermore, the tag "the Moabitess" stands in stark contrast to her name's meaning. She is an outsider by birth, yet she acts as the ultimate true companion to an Israelite widow, setting the stage for the dramatic reversal of their fortunes in Bethlehem during the barley harvest.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Ruth 1:14: "Then they lifted up their voices and wept again; and Orpah kissed her mother-in-law, but Ruth clung to her."

• Ruth 2:2: "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.' And she said to her, 'Go, my daughter.'"

• Ruth 4:13: "So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife; and when he went in to her, the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The book of Ruth is one of only two books in the Protestant canon named after women (the other being Esther). While Esther is a Hebrew woman who saves her people in a foreign court, Ruth is a foreign woman who enters the Hebrew community and preserves the messianic line. Her name and character stand as an enduring monument to the truth that faith transcends cultural boundaries, and that quiet, everyday loyalty is often the vehicle through which God accomplishes His most extraordinary works.
