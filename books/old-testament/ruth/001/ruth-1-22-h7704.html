COUNTRY - ( sadeh , Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh, translated as "country" in Ruth 1:22, refers to the open fields, cultivated land, or territory of Moab. In the narrative of Ruth, it represents a foreign place of temporary physical refuge that ultimately resulted in bereavement and emptiness for Naomi. Returning from the "country of Moab" marks a pivotal geographical and spiritual transition, as Naomi and Ruth journey back to the covenant land of promise and the hope of the barley harvest in Bethlehem.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The primary meaning of sadeh is an open field, plain, or cultivated soil. It stands in contrast to walled cities, mountainous regions, or uncultivated wilderness. In the ancient Near East, the sadeh of a nation (such as the "country of Moab" or "field of Edom") refers to its fertile agricultural territory, valleys, and tablelands. It is the place where crops are sown, flocks are pastured, and wild animals roam. In the Book of Ruth, the "fields of Moab" represent a foreign, pagan territory that was agriculturally fertile while Judah suffered from famine.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun sadeh designates:

• The open, unfenced countryside as opposed to the city.

• Cultivated land used for growing crops or grain.

• Pastureland for grazing livestock.

• The specific territory, region, or domain of a nation or tribe (e.g., the country/field of Moab).

In Ruth 1:22, the plural construct form (sedeh) indicates the rural territory, agricultural plains, and homeland of the Moabites.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Cultivated Fields: The word often denotes the place of farming and agricultural labor. It is where human effort meets the natural fertility of the ground to produce grain, vines, and olives.

• The Wild: It is used for the open country where wild beasts live ("beasts of the field") and where battles are fought.

• Geographic Territory: When associated with a people group, it defines their national borders and agricultural plains (Genesis 36:35).

• Vulnerability and Exposure: Because fields are open and unfenced, they represent places of vulnerability, away from the protection of city walls.

4. Theological Implications

The "fields of Moab" represent a place outside the Promised Land and the direct sphere of covenant blessings. For an Israelite family, leaving Bethlehem (meaning "House of Bread") to settle in the sadeh of Moab is a movement away from God's inheritance in search of physical security. While Moab provided physical sustenance during a famine, it became a place of death, loss, and spiritual barrenness for Naomi's family. Geographically and spiritually, returning from the fields of Moab to Bethlehem signifies repentance, restoration, and a return to Yahweh's providential care.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:22, the repetition of "returned from the country of Moab" emphasizes the completion of Naomi's tragic ten-year detour. The author structures the narrative to contrast the "fields of Moab" with the "fields of Bethlehem" (Ruth 2:2-3). Naomi left full and returned empty. By leaving the pagan sadeh of Moab, Ruth and Naomi arrive in Bethlehem precisely at the beginning of the barley harvest, setting the stage for Ruth to glean in the sadeh of Boaz. The transition from the foreign country to the covenant field of redemption highlights God's sovereignty and grace.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 4:8 - "And it came to pass, when they were in the field, that Cain rose up against Abel his brother and killed him."

• Genesis 25:27 - "So the boys grew. And Esau was a skillful hunter, a man of the field; but Jacob was a mild man, dwelling in tents."

• Genesis 36:35 - "And Hadad the son of Bedad, who defeated Midian in the field of Moab, reigned in his place."

• Ruth 2:2 - "So Ruth the Moabitess said to Naomi, 'Please let me go to the field, and glean heads of grain after him in whose sight I may find favor.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

A literary thread connects the "country [fields] of Moab" (sedeh Moab) in Ruth 1 with the "field" (sadeh) of Boaz in Ruth 2. Ruth leaves the fields of Moab, where she had no legal right to inheritance, and enters the fields of Bethlehem as a poor gleaner. In the fields of Boaz, she finds protection, provision, and ultimately her kinsman-redeemer. This linguistic connection highlights the movement from exile to redemption.
