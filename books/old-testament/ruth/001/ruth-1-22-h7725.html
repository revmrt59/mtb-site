RETURNED - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub is one of the most vital theological words in the Old Testament, denoting a physical turning back, a return to a point of origin, or a spiritual turning to God in repentance. In the narrative of Ruth 1:22, it marks the pivotal transition of Naomi and Ruth from the barren spiritual exile of Moab back to the covenant blessings of Bethlehem at the beginning of the barley harvest, beautifully illustrating how God welcomes and restores the broken who return to Him.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical picture behind shub is the act of turning around 180 degrees and retracing one's steps back to the starting point. It assumes a previous departure; one cannot return unless they first went away. In the ancient near-eastern mind, geography and theology were deeply intertwined. Leaving the land of Israel—the sphere of God's covenant promise—was viewed as leaving the place of God's immediate protection and blessing. The physical act of turning one's back on a foreign land to walk back toward the Promised Land is the primary visual metaphor for spiritual restoration. It is the posture of a traveler who realizes they have gone down a dead-end path and makes the conscious, physical decision to go back home.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shub occurs over one thousand times in the Old Testament, making it the twelfth most frequent verb in the Hebrew scriptures. Its primary meanings include:

• To turn back, return, or go back to a person, place, or condition.

• To repeat an action, often translated as "again" when paired with another verb.

• To turn away from a course of action, such as turning away from evil or turning away from God's commands.

• In its causative forms, to bring back, restore, refresh, or make restitution.

• In its relational and spiritual applications, it is the standard Old Testament term for repentance—turning back to the covenant relationship with Yahweh.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs shub in several vivid conceptual categories:

• The Geographical Return: The physical movement of people returning to their homeland, such as Israel returning from the Babylonian exile, or Jacob returning to Canaan after his years with Laban.

• The Relational Turning: The reconciliation between estranged parties. When Jacob and Esau meet after years of separation, their relationship experiences a shub—a turning back to brotherhood.

• The Turning of Divine Wrath: God is frequently described as "turning" His anger away from His people when they cry out to Him. Conversely, when His people refuse to turn from their sins, He permits the consequences of their departure to unfold.

• The Restoration of the Soul: God's word and His presence are described as having the power to shub (restore or refresh) the inner life of the believer, bringing vitality back to a weary spirit.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shub lies at the heart of the biblical doctrine of repentance. Unlike some Hellenistic concepts of repentance that focus primarily on an intellectual change of mind, the Hebrew concept demands a change of direction and action. Repentance is not merely feeling sorry; it is a physical and volitional realignment of one's life.

Furthermore, shub highlights the grace of God. The possibility of returning implies that the door is open. Even when God's people depart due to faithlessness, panic, or sin, God remains committed to His covenant. He waits for their return, ready to transition them from judgment to mercy, from famine to harvest.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1, various forms of the verb shub occur twelve times, serving as the dominant thematic keyword of the chapter.

In Ruth 1:22, the word is used twice: once for Naomi and once for Ruth.

• Naomi's Return: For Naomi, the return is bittersweet. She left Bethlehem full (with a husband and two sons) and returns empty, bitter, and grieving. Yet, her physical return to Bethlehem is the first step toward her restoration. She returns to the covenant community just as Yahweh is visiting His people with bread.

• Ruth's "Return": Ruth's return is highly unusual and structurally profound. As a Moabitess, Ruth had never lived in Bethlehem; physically, she was not "returning" to Israel, but rather leaving her homeland for the first time. By stating that Ruth "returned from the country of Moab," the author uses shub to signal Ruth's spiritual conversion. She is turning away from the pagan gods of Moab to bind herself to the Lord. Her physical journey with Naomi is a spiritual homecoming to the true God.

• The Timing: Their return coincides with "the beginning of barley harvest." This detail signals that their physical turning back to the land of promise is met immediately with God’s physical provision, setting the stage for their ultimate redemption.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Isaiah 55:7 - "Let the wicked forsake his way, And the unrighteous man his thoughts; Let him return to the LORD, And He will have mercy on him; And to our God, For He will abundantly pardon."

• Hosea 14:1 - "O Israel, return to the LORD your God, For you have stumbled because of your iniquity;"

• Malachi 3:7 - "Yet from the days of your fathers You have gone away from My ordinances And have not kept them. Return to Me, and I will return to you," Says the LORD of hosts.

• Psalm 23:3 - "He restores my soul; He leads me in the paths of righteousness For His name’s sake."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Link to the New Testament: The Greek equivalent often used in the Septuagint (LXX) for shub is epistrepho (to turn back/convert) or metanoeo (to repent). In the New Testament, the Parable of the Prodigal Son in Luke 15 is the ultimate narrative expression of shub. The son "comes to himself," physically turns around, and returns to his father's house, mirroring Naomi and Ruth's journey back to Bethlehem.

• The Envelope Structure of Ruth 1: The chapter begins with a departure (going out) because of famine and ends with a return (coming back) at the harvest. This literary structure assures the reader that despite the tragedy, losses, and bitterness of the journey, returning to God's place and God's people always leads to restoration.
