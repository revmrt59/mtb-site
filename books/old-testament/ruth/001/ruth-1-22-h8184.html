BARLEY - ( se'orah , Strong's H8184 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word se'orah refers to barley, a major cereal crop of the ancient Near East known for its hardiness and early ripening. In the narrative of Ruth 1:22, the arrival of Naomi and Ruth in Bethlehem "at the beginning of barley harvest" serves as a physical and theological turning point. It marks the transition from famine, bereavement, and emptiness in Moab to agricultural abundance, communal restoration, and covenant redemption in the Promised Land. Barley is the food of the poor, signaling God's immediate provision for the vulnerable and initiating the events that lead to the lineage of David and Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Barley (se'orah) is named for its distinctive appearance, deriving from a root meaning "hairy" or "bristly" because of the long, hair-like awns that project from the ears of the grain.

• It was one of the staple crops of ancient Israel, highly valued for its ability to grow in poorer soils and more arid climates than wheat.

• In the agricultural calendar of Israel, the barley harvest began in the spring, around the month of Abib/Nisan (March/April), immediately following the Passover. It was the first of the cereal crops to be harvested, preceding the wheat harvest by several weeks.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The noun se'orah (often appearing in the plural form se'orim) refers strictly to the barley plant or the harvested grain.

• It denotes a basic food staple, typically associated with the diet of the common people, the poor, and domestic animals due to its lower cost and coarser texture compared to wheat.

• In commercial and legal contexts, it served as a measure of value, used in land valuations and grain offerings.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• A Sign of Divine Visitation: The presence of barley signifies God's mercy in lifting a famine. Rain and harvest are direct signs of God keeping His covenant promises to bless the land of Israel.

• The Portion of the Poor: Because barley was cheaper than wheat (often valued at half the price), it represents humility and survival. Gideon's dream of a tumbling loaf of barley bread (Judges 7:13) pictured his own humble, overlooked status as the instrument of God's deliverance.

• The Firstfruits of Deliverance: The presentation of the first sheaf of the barley harvest (the Omer) on the day after the Sabbath during the Feast of Unleavened Bread symbolized consecration and the anticipation of a full harvest.

4. Theological Implications

• Transition from Curse to Blessing: Famine was a covenant curse for disobedience (Deuteronomy 28). The return of the barley harvest in Bethlehem ("House of Bread") signifies that God has visited His people to give them bread, demonstrating grace and restoration.

• Providential Timing: The arrival of Ruth and Naomi exactly at the start of the barley harvest is not a coincidence, but God's sovereign orchestration. It provides the legal and social framework (gleaning) for Ruth to meet Boaz.

• Redemptive Inclusion: God's law allowed the poor, strangers, and widows to glean the edges of the barley fields (Leviticus 19:9-10). The barley harvest becomes the theater where God's law of compassion meets human obedience and kindness, leading to the redemption of a foreign outcast.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:22, the phrase "at the beginning of barley harvest" functions as a literary hinge. The first chapter is dominated by themes of death, departure, emptiness, and bitter weeping.

• The mention of "barley harvest" instantly shifts the tone from despair to hope. Bethlehem, which Naomi left because it had no bread, now has bread in abundance.

• This setting prepares the reader for Chapter 2, where the practical need for food drives Ruth into the field of Boaz, initiating the process of her kinsman-redeemer rescue.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 9:31 - "Now the flax and the barley were struck, for the barley was in the head and the flax was in bud."

• Judges 7:13 - "And Gideon came, and there was a man telling a dream to his companion. He said, 'I have had a dream: To my surprise, a loaf of barley bread tumbled into the camp of Midian; it came to a tent and struck it so that it fell and overturned, and the tent collapsed.'"

• Ruth 2:17 - "So she gleaned in the field until evening, and beat out what she had gleaned, and it was about an ephah of barley."

• 2 Kings 4:42 - "Then a man came from Baal-Shalisha, and brought the man of God bread of the firstfruits, twenty loaves of barley bread, and newly ripened grain in his knapsack. And he said, 'Give it to the people, that they may eat.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

• An ephah of barley, which Ruth gleaned in a single day (Ruth 2:17), was an extraordinarily generous amount—roughly one-half to two-thirds of a bushel (about 30 to 50 pounds). This indicates the extreme generosity of Boaz and points to the overflowing grace of God toward those who take refuge under His wings.

• The agricultural connection between the barley harvest (associated with Passover/Firstfruits) and the subsequent wheat harvest (associated with Pentecost) frames the entire timeline of the book of Ruth, illustrating a season of prolonged safety, provision, and growing relationships.
