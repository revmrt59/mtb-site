HUSBAND - ( ish , Strong's H0376 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ish primarily means "man" or "husband," denoting an individual male, often in the context of covenant relationship and responsibility. In Ruth 1:3, it refers specifically to Elimelech as Naomi's husband, highlighting his role as her provider, protector, and covenant partner. His death shatters Naomi's social and economic security, setting the stage for a narrative centered on vulnerability, loyalty, and redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word ish is one of the most common words in the Old Testament, representing a man of substance, standing, or individuality, as contrasted with adam (which refers to mankind generically) or enosh (which emphasizes human frailty). In the context of marriage, ish represents the husband who stands in a covenantal relationship with his wife. In the ancient Near East, a husband was not merely a romantic partner; he was the legal, economic, and physical covering for his wife. The ish held the responsibility of securing the family’s inheritance, maintaining the household, and preserving the family line. The word picture of ish as a husband evokes the image of a strong, nurturing pillar upon whom the security of the family rests.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

At its core, ish refers to an individual male person. When used in relation to a woman, it transitions from meaning simply "man" to meaning "husband." It signifies a male counterpart in a marital union, denoting a relationship of mutual belonging, though with a distinct role of leadership and provision. In legal and covenantal contexts, the term denotes the head of the household who has the authority to make decisions that bind the family, yet is also responsible for their well-being and redemption.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament frequently uses ish to describe the intimate bond of marriage. Unlike the word baal, which can also mean "husband" but carries connotations of ownership, mastership, and lordship, ish emphasizes a personal, companionate, and covenantal connection.

• In the Garden of Eden, the relationship is established between ish (man) and ishshah (woman), reflecting a shared nature and face-to-face equality of being.

• Prophets use the term to describe the restored relationship between God and Israel, where God desires to be called Ishi ("my husband") rather than Baali ("my master"), illustrating a move from fear-based servility to intimate covenant relationship.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, the presence or absence of an ish in ancient Israel determined a woman's social standing and security. The death of Naomi's ish in Ruth 1:3 leaves her in a state of extreme vulnerability. In the biblical framework, God presents Himself as the father to the fatherless and the defender of widows, steps in to protect those who have lost their ish. Furthermore, the loss of the husband sets up the need for a redeemer (goel). The story of Ruth is not just a romance, but a theological journey from the grief of a lost ish to the redemption found when a new ish (Boaz) steps in to fulfill the role of the kinsman-redeemer, restoring the family land and lineage.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:3, the text records: "Then Elimelech, Naomi's husband, died." The Hebrew phrase is ish Naomi ("the man of Naomi"). The context here is heavy with grief and irony. Elimelech’s name means "My God is King," yet he left the Promised Land due to a famine, seeking security in Moab. Rather than finding life, he dies, leaving his wife unprotected in a foreign land. By calling Elimelech "Naomi's husband" at the moment of his death, the narrator emphasizes the deep personal and economic loss Naomi suffered. She is no longer covered by her ish. The tragedy deepens as her two sons also die, leaving her completely cut off from any male protection, which underscores her deep emptiness when she later returns to Bethlehem.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Hebrew text of Ruth 1:3, the word order places the emphasis on Elimelech’s relationship to Naomi: "Elimelech, the husband of Naomi." This highlights that his identity in this specific moment of the narrative is defined by his relationship to her. His death is not narrated as a national event, but as a devastating household tragedy. In the ancient world, a widow without a husband or sons was among the most marginalized of individuals. Naomi's transition from having an ish to having none drives her to rename herself Mara ("bitter"), showcasing how central the ish was to a woman's identity, joy, and peace in that culture.
