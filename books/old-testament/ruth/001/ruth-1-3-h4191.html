DIED - ( muth , Strong's H4191 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb muth means to die, expire, or perish, denoting the cessation of physical life. In the narrative of Ruth 1:3, it refers to the death of Elimelech, Naomi’s husband. This word establishes the stark, tragic reality of Naomi's vulnerability in a foreign land. Rather than finding life and security in Moab, she finds bereavement, leaving her and her sons in a precarious socio-economic position during a time of famine and national distress.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word muth is the primary Hebrew root for death, occurring over 800 times in the Old Testament. It reflects the harsh reality of human mortality introduced in Genesis.

• Historically and culturally, the death of a husband in ancient Israel and the ancient Near East was not merely a personal tragedy but a social and economic catastrophe for the surviving family. Without a male protector and provider, a widow was among the most marginalized members of society, highly vulnerable to exploitation, poverty, and starvation.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The verb muth is a common Semitic root meaning "to die" or "to be dead."

• In the Qal stem (as in Ruth 1:3), it signifies the natural or violent cessation of physical life, the act of dying, or the state of being dead.

• In the Hiphil stem, it means "to put to death" or "to kill."

• In the Polel stem, it means "to deal a death-blow" or "to dispatch."

• The core meaning denotes a definitive separation from the land of the living, marked by the departure of the breath of life or spirit.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Muth is used contrastively with chayah (to live) to outline the boundaries of human existence under God's sovereignty.

• It is often associated with descending into Sheol (the realm of the dead), returning to the dust, or being "gathered to one's people."

• The word pictures of death in the Old Testament represent it as a powerful, sometimes personified enemy, yet one that is ultimately subject to the sovereign authority of Yahweh.

4. Theological Implications

• Death in the Old Testament is inextricably linked to the fall of humanity and the curse of sin. Genesis 2:17 warned that eating of the forbidden tree would result in surely dying.

• The occurrence of muth in the genealogies and narratives of Scripture serves as a recurring reminder of human frailty and the consequences of leaving the place of God's covenant blessing.

• Theologically, Elimelech's death highlights the futility of human schemes. He left Bethlehem ("House of Bread") because of famine to find life in Moab, but instead encountered death. God's covenant people cannot find true life or security by abandoning the Land of Promise.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:3, muth acts as a major turning point in the narrative. Elimelech's death strips Naomi of her primary source of identity, security, and protection in a foreign, pagan land.

• The phrasing is brief and blunt: "Then Elimelech, Naomi’s husband, died." The narrator offers no medical explanation or lengthy mourning description, emphasizing the sudden and severe transition from a family seeking self-preservation to a widow facing extreme vulnerability.

• This death sets up the double tragedy in Ruth 1:5, where her two sons also die, intensifying the crisis and underscoring Naomi's complete emptiness, which prepares the reader for the redemptive hand of God later in the book.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:17 - "but of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die."

• Genesis 5:5 - "So all the days that Adam lived were nine hundred and thirty years; and he died."

• Genesis 50:26 - "So Joseph died, being one hundred and ten years old; and they embalmed him, and he was put in a coffin in Egypt."

• Deuteronomy 34:5 - "So Moses the servant of the LORD died there in the land of Moab, according to the word of the LORD."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The repetition of muth in Ruth 1 (v. 3, v. 5, and v. 17) contrasts sharply with the themes of life, birth, and redemption that dominate the end of the book.

• Naomi's self-assessment in Ruth 1:21 ("I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty") is directly rooted in the reality of muth taking her husband and sons. The narrative trajectory moves from death and emptiness to life and fullness through the provision of a kinsman-redeemer.
