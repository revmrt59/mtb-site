WAS LEFT - ( sha'ar , Strong's H7604 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb sha'ar means to remain, survive, or be left behind as a remnant. In Ruth 1:3, it highlights Naomi's vulnerable survival after the death of her husband, Elimelech, leaving her isolated with her two sons in a foreign land. This term underscores both her severe loss and the silent, preserving grace of God that keeps her alive for a future redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root sha'ar carries the concrete imagery of a remnant—that which is left over after a sweeping event has occurred, such as a harvest, a battle, or a plague. It is often used in agricultural and military contexts to describe what remains after a field is reaped or an army is decimated. In the ancient Near East, survival depended heavily on family structure and community. To be "left" behind meant more than mere physical existence; it signified being stripped of one's primary source of protection, identity, and economic security. In Naomi's case, being "left" in the pagan land of Moab after her husband's death underscores her profound vulnerability, transitioning her from a secure wife to a dependent widow.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb sha'ar functions primarily in the Niphal (passive) and Hiphil (active) stems. In the Niphal stem, as used in Ruth 1:3, it denotes being left behind, remaining as a survivor, or being spared. The lexical definition centers on survival in the wake of subtraction or destruction. It indicates that while others have perished or departed, a small portion or a single entity remains. The word does not imply a passive, casual staying behind, but rather a survival through a critical, life-altering transition.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, sha'ar is used to paint vivid pictures of survival and remnant theology. It describes the small remnant of people left in the land of Israel after foreign invasions and exile. It is used of Noah and those who remained with him in the ark during the global deluge, surviving the cosmic judgment of God. It also depicts the sole survivor of a military conflict who escapes to bring news of defeat. These word pictures convey that those who are "left" are often the sole link to the future, carrying the weight of what has been lost and the hope of what might be rebuilt.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, sha'ar is the linguistic foundation for the doctrine of the remnant. This doctrine demonstrates that even in the darkest times of judgment, exile, or death, God does not allow His people to be utterly destroyed. While the experience of being "left" is marked by grief and depletion, it also signals God's preserving grace. Naomi was left, but she was not utterly consumed. The presence of her two sons, and eventually her return to Bethlehem, demonstrates that God preserves a remnant through whom He will unfold His redemptive purposes, ultimately leading to the lineage of David and the Messiah.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:3, the writer uses sha'ar twice in close proximity (v. 3 and v. 5). In verse 3, after Elimelech dies, Naomi "was left" with her two sons. The nuance here is bittersweet; she has suffered a devastating loss, yet she is not entirely alone. However, in verse 5, after her sons also die, she is "left" without her two sons and her husband. The repetition of sha'ar intensifies her isolation, charting her descent from partial bereavement to absolute destitution. The contextual nuance shifts from surviving with family support to surviving in complete legal and social helplessness.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 7:23: "Only Noah was left, and those who were with him in the ark."

• 1 Samuel 16:11: "There remains yet the youngest, and there he is, keeping the sheep."

• 2 Kings 24:14: "None remained except the poorest people of the land."

• Nehemiah 1:3: "The survivors who are left from the captivity in the province are there in great distress and reproach."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the Near Eastern context, the survival of Naomi and her daughters-in-law highlights the levirate marriage customs and the importance of a kinsman-redeemer. The repetition of being "left" prepares the reader for the entrance of Boaz. The word sha'ar emphasizes that Naomi was left with nothing but her life and her daughters-in-law, stripping away all human security so that when redemption occurs, it is clearly seen as a work of God's sovereign grace. It shows that being "left" is not the end of the story, but the starting point for God's renewal.
