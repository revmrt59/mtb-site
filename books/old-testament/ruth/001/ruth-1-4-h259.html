ONE - ( 'echad , Strong's H0259 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'echad primarily signifies "one" or "single," representing numerical oneness, unique singularity, or a unified plurality. In the narrative of Ruth 1:4, it serves a structural and relational purpose, introducing the first of two Moabite daughters-in-law, Orpah, and setting the stage for a contrast in covenant loyalty. While simple in its numerical function here, the word carries deep theological weight throughout the Old Testament, representing both the absolute uniqueness of Yahweh and the profound unity of His people.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word 'echad is one of the most fundamental terms in the Hebrew language, appearing over 900 times in the Old Testament. Its background is rooted in the concept of unity, order, and enumeration. It distinguishes a single unit from a collective or points to a unified whole made of distinct parts. In ancient Semitic thought, numbering was not merely abstract math but a way to establish order, identity, and relational positioning within a narrative or family structure.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word functions as a cardinal number meaning "one," an ordinal number meaning "first," and an indefinite article meaning "a" or "certain." It carries the semantic range of "each," "single," "unique," or "alone." It is also used to express compound unity, where multiple entities form a singular, cohesive group, such as "one flesh" in marriage or "one people."

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Numerical Oneness: It is used to count individual items or people, ensuring distinct identity.

• Compound Unity: It describes a collective singularity. When a husband and wife become "one flesh," they do not lose their distinct identities but form a unified relational entity. Similarly, the tabernacle curtains are joined to form "one" tabernacle.

• Uniqueness and Exclusivity: It highlights incomparable singularity, as in the Shema, where Yahweh is declared to be "one"—unique, peerless, and requiring undivided devotion.

4. Theological Implications

• The Nature of God: The declaration in Deuteronomy 6:4 reveals God's absolute uniqueness. He is not one among many pagan deities; He is the only true God, self-existent and undivided in His essence.

• The Concept of Covenant Unity: 'echad models the unity God desires for His covenant community. Just as God is one, His people are called to a unified purpose, heart, and mind.

• Providential Ordering: In historical narratives, the pairing of "one... and the other" often introduces a literary foil, showing how God works through distinct individual choices to accomplish a singular, unified plan of redemption.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:4, 'echad is translated as "one" ("the name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other Ruth"). It operates as a literary and relational anchor. The narrative introduces Orpah and Ruth as a pair, yet distinguishes them. By naming "the one" and "the other," the text prepares the reader for the divergent paths these two women will take. Though they begin in the same status (Moabite wives of Elimelech's sons), their eventual choices split. 'echad highlights that Orpah, though part of this tragic family unit, ultimately remains distinct from the line of promise, while Ruth becomes knit into the genealogy of the Messiah.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:24 - "Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and they shall become one flesh."

• Deuteronomy 6:4 - "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is one!"

• Ecclesiastes 4:9 - "Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their labor."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew narrative, contrasting "the one" ('echad) with "the second/other" (hasenit) is a common literary device to highlight destiny and character. Examples include Rachel and Leah, or Hannah and Peninnah. This device emphasizes human responsibility and divine election within the family structure, illustrating how two individuals starting in the same circumstance can end with entirely different spiritual legacies.
