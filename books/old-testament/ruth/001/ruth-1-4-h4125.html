MOAB - ( Moabiy , Strong's H4125 )

Summary:

The Hebrew term Moabiy designates a member of the nation of Moab, a people descended from Abraham's nephew, Lot. In the narrative of Ruth, this word highlights the foreign, pagan origin of the women chosen by Naomi's sons, setting up a sharp contrast between Israel's covenant expectations and God's expansive, sovereign grace. Though historically associated with hostility toward Israel and exclusion from the assembly, the presence of a Moabitess in the lineage of David and Jesus Christ demonstrates that God's mercy transcends ethnic and historical barriers for those who seek refuge under His wings.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The background of Moab begins in Genesis 19:37 with the incestuous union between Lot and his eldest daughter after the destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah. The daughter named the child Moab, which literally means "from father." The descendants of this child settled in the high, fertile plateau east of the Dead Sea, bounded by the Arnon gorge to the north and the Zered brook to the south.

Historically, Moab was a frequent adversary of Israel. During the wilderness wanderings, Moab refused to let Israel pass through their land, hired Balaam to curse them, and enticed Israelite men into idolatrous revelry and sexual immorality at Baal-Peor. Because of this persistent hostility and their failure to show hospitality, Deuteronomy 23:3 commanded that no Moabite could enter the assembly of the Lord, even to the tenth generation. This background of ancestral shame and ongoing hostility made the word "Moabite" a term of deep spiritual and national reproach in Israel.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew noun Moabiy (feminine Moabiyyah, plural Moabiyyot) is an ethnic and geographical gentilic noun meaning "Moabite," "Moabitess," or "belonging to Moab."

In Ruth 1:4, the plural feminine form Moabiyyot is used to describe the women whom Mahlon and Chilion married. It indicates their national origin, their cultural upbringing, and their default devotion to Chemosh, the chief god of the Moabites. The word serves as a legal and theological marker, identifying these women as outsiders to the covenant of Israel.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, the term is used primarily to maintain ethnic and religious boundaries. To call someone a Moabite was to remind Israel of:

• Spiritual compromise and foreign idolatry (1 Kings 11:1).

• Excluded status from the temple and public worship (Nehemiah 13:1).

• God's judgment on pride and self-sufficiency, as Moab was frequently condemned by the prophets for its arrogance (Isaiah 16:6; Jeremiah 48:29).

In the Book of Ruth, the word is used like a persistent surname. Ruth is rarely mentioned simply as "Ruth"; she is repeatedly called "Ruth the Moabitess" (Ruth 1:22; 2:2; 2:21; 4:5; 4:10). This repetitive usage serves as a literary and spiritual foil. It constantly reminds the reader of her origin, making her exceptional character, humility, and faith all the more striking against the dark backdrop of her nation's history.

### 4. Theological Implications

The presence of the word Moabiy in the opening movement of the Book of Ruth introduces massive theological themes:

• The Supremacy of Grace over Law: While Deuteronomy 23:3 excludes the Moabite from the assembly, Ruth's story shows that the spirit of the law welcomes the foreign convert who expresses genuine faith. Grace bypasses the ancestral curse when a heart turns to Yahweh.

• Inclusion of the Gentiles: Long before the New Testament declared that the gospel is for all nations, the genealogy of David included a Moabite woman. God was actively weaving the nations into His grand redemptive tapestry.

• Providence in Tragedy: Elimelech's family chose to leave the Promised Land and settle in Moab due to a famine—a move that looked like spiritual compromise and resulted in tragedy. Yet, God sovereignly used this backsliding journey to bring a daughter of Moab into the lineage of the Messiah. God's redemptive purposes are not thwarted by human failure.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:4, the phrase "women of Moab" is loaded with tension. Elimelech has died, and his two sons, away from the spiritual accountability of Bethlehem, take Moabite wives. For the original Hebrew reader, this detail would evoke immediate concern. Marrying foreign women was historically the first step toward idolatry and spiritual ruin for Israelite men.

The text does not explicitly condemn the marriages, but it immediately records the death of both sons within ten years, leaving Naomi completely bereaved. The presence of "Moab" in this verse represents the ultimate end of self-reliance: a journey that began in search of bread ends in a foreign graveyard, leaving behind three childless widows. The stage is set for a miracle of restoration, starting with the most unlikely candidate—a Moabitess.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between the two Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth, is highly instructive. Both started in the same spiritual condition, but Orpah returned "to her people and to her gods" (Ruth 1:15), while Ruth abandoned Moab's identity to embrace Yahweh. This demonstrates that ethnic background does not determine spiritual destiny; personal faith does.

• By the time Boaz marries Ruth, her reputation as a "virtuous woman" (Ruth 3:11) has completely overshadowed her identity as a "Moabitess" in the eyes of the community. True faith redefines a person's identity in the eyes of God and His people.
