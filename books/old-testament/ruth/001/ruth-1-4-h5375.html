TOOK - ( nasa , Strong's H5375 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nasa fundamentally means to lift, carry, or take up. In the context of Ruth 1:4, it is used idiomatically for marriage ("took wives"), suggesting the initiation of a domestic covenant where the husband lifts up or carries the responsibility of a wife into his home. In the broader narrative of Ruth, this word underscores the weight of the decisions made by Elimelech's sons in Moab, highlighting how human choices carry both immediate relational realities and long-term covenantal consequences.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The root meaning of nasa is physical elevation—the act of lifting an object, raising one's eyes, or carrying a heavy burden. It paints a picture of active effort and the assumption of responsibility. When someone lifts something, they bear its weight. In the ancient Near Eastern culture, taking a wife involved a formal transition where the bride was "lifted" or taken from her father's house and brought under the protective covering and authority of her husband's household.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb nasa has an exceptionally broad semantic range, spanning several primary categories:

• To lift up, raise, or elevate (such as hands, voice, eyes, or head).

• To bear, carry, or support a load or burden.

• To take, take away, carry off, or remove.

• To forgive or take away guilt and sin (by bearing the iniquity of another).

• In the specific phrase nasa ishah (literally, "to lift/take a wife"), it means to marry or take a woman as a spouse. This idiom is primarily found in later Biblical Hebrew alongside the older, more common idiom laqach ishah.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs nasa in several evocative theological and practical word pictures:

• Lifting hands in prayer or priestly blessing, signifying the raising of the heart toward God.

• Bearing guilt or sin, depicting a person carrying the heavy moral weight of their own transgression.

• Forgiveness, where God "lifts" or carries away the heavy burden of sin, removing it from the offender.

• Carrying physical objects, such as the Levites carrying the ark of the covenant on their shoulders.

• Metaphorically "lifting up" one's soul, which speaks of directing one's ultimate desire and hope toward God.

4. Theological Implications

The usage of nasa throughout Scripture weaves together the concepts of election, responsibility, and atonement. When God "lifts up" His people, He carries them through difficult seasons, just as a father carries his son. When a priest or a sacrificial substitute "bears" (nasa) iniquity, it points directly to substitutionary atonement, anticipating Christ who "bore" the sins of many. In the context of marriage, nasa reminds us that entering a covenant is not merely about personal satisfaction but about taking up the joyful burden of care, protection, and provision for another.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:4, Mahlon and Chilion "took (nasa) wives of the women of Moab."

First, the use of nasa instead of the more standard early Hebrew laqach ("to take") is stylistically typical of post-exilic Hebrew or regional dialects.

Second, the context is heavy with tragedy. Elimelech's family left the Promised Land due to famine. After Elimelech's death, his sons "took" Moabite wives (Orpah and Ruth). Deuteronomy 23:3 forbade Moabites from entering the assembly of the Lord. While marrying Moabite women was not explicitly forbidden under the same terms as marrying Canaanites, it was highly controversial and spiritually risky, as Moabites worshiped Chemosh.

The word nasa here signals that these young men initiated these unions on their own initiative in a foreign land. They "carried" these foreign wives into their domestic life, importing the spiritual complex of Moab into an already compromised Israelite household, bearing the spiritual weight of their choices.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The contrast between nasa ("to lift/carry") and laqach ("to take/seize") in marriage contexts: While laqach emphasizes the act of acquiring, nasa subtly emphasizes the ongoing duty of supporting, carrying, or sustaining the spouse.

In the book of Ruth, the tragedy of Mahlon and Chilion "taking" these wives is quickly followed by their deaths, leaving three barren widows. The story shifts from these men "taking" (nasa) wives to Naomi and Ruth being "carried" or sustained by the providence of Yahweh through Boaz, who ultimately redeems the family line.
