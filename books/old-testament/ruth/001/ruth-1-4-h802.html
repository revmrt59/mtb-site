WIVES - ( 'ishshah , Strong's H802 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ishshah (plural nashim) is the primary Old Testament term for "woman" or "wife." Derived from roots suggesting softness, vulnerability, or relational alignment with man ('ish), the word carries deep theological significance starting in Genesis. In the context of Ruth 1:4, the term refers specifically to foreign wives taken by Elimelech's sons, Mahlon and Chilion. This highlights a critical turning point in the narrative where covenant boundaries are crossed, setting the stage for a story of redemption, loyalty, and God's sovereign grace extending beyond Israel's borders.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word 'ishshah represents the feminine counterpart to 'ish (man). In the creation narrative of Genesis 2:23, the term is poetically linked to 'ish because she was "taken out of Man." This linguistic connection underscores an ontological equality and a relational design, emphasizing companionship, partnership, and mutual dependence. In the Ancient Near East, a woman's identity was closely tied to her household, first under her father's house and later under her husband's covenant protection. The plural form, nashim, is used in Ruth 1:4 to denote the Moabite women who entered into marital unions with the Hebrew emigrants, Mahlon and Chilion.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of 'ishshah is "woman" or "wife," with the specific translation determined entirely by the context. When accompanied by a possessive pronoun or in a construct state with a man's name, it invariably means "wife." The semantic range includes:

• An individual female human being, regardless of marital status.

• A married woman or wife, representing a legal and covenantal union.

• Adjectivally, to describe female characteristics or roles (e.g., a "virtuous woman" in Proverbs 31:10).

In Ruth 1:4, the word occurs in the plural nashim in a verbal construction ("they took to themselves wives"), which explicitly denotes marriage rather than casual association or temporary cohabitation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Hebrew Scriptures, 'ishshah is used over 780 times. It serves as a central vehicle for expressing relational and theological realities:

• The companion of man: Formed from the rib of Adam, she is the "helper comparable to him" (Genesis 2:18).

• The mother of all living: Associated with life-giving, nurturing, and the continuation of the covenant lineage.

• Metaphorical use: Israel is frequently depicted as the 'ishshah (wife) of Yahweh, symbolizing covenant intimacy, devotion, and, at times, unfaithfulness (Jeremiah 3:1; Hosea 2:2).

• Personified wisdom and folly: In the book of Proverbs, both Wisdom and Folly are personified as women (Proverbs 9:1, 13), representing competing voices calling out to the hearts of people.

4. Theological Implications

The use of 'ishshah in the Old Testament is deeply theological. It begins with the affirmation of the equal dignity of women, created in the image of God (Genesis 1:27). In the context of Israel's covenant law, marriage to an 'ishshah was intended to be a sacred reflection of God's faithfulness. However, marriages to foreign nashim (wives) from pagan nations were strictly warned against in Israel's history because they often led to spiritual compromise and idolatry (Deuteronomy 7:3-4). The theological tension in Ruth 1:4 lies in the fact that these "wives" are Moabites—descendants of Lot through an incestuous union and historical enemies of Israel who worshiped Chemosh. Yet, God's sovereign grace ultimately redeems this compromised situation, integrating one of these Moabite wives, Ruth, into the lineage of the Messiah.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:4, the phrase "they took wives of the women of Moab" uses nashim (the plural of 'ishshah). The context is one of tragedy and compromise. Elimelech's family has left the Promised Land due to a famine and settled in pagan Moab. Following Elimelech's death, his sons Mahlon and Chilion marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth. The text does not explicitly condemn these marriages, but the historical and biblical context of Deuteronomy 23:3 (excluding Moabites from the assembly) casts a shadow of spiritual compromise over the act. The sudden deaths of both sons shortly thereafter leave these three women as widows in a state of extreme vulnerability, shifting the narrative focus to the profound bond of loyalty (chesed) that develops between Naomi and her Moabite daughter-in-law, Ruth.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:24 - "Therefore a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife ('ishshah), and they shall become one flesh."

• Proverbs 12:4 - "An excellent wife ('ishshah) is the crown of her husband, but she who causes shame is like rottenness in his bones."

• Proverbs 31:30 - "Charm is deceitful and beauty is passing, but a woman ('ishshah) who fears the LORD, she shall be praised."

• Ruth 4:13 - "So Boaz took Ruth and she became his wife ('ishshah); and when he went in to her, the LORD gave her conception, and she bore a son."

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the ancient Near Eastern legal framework, "taking a wife" (nasa' 'ishshah) involved formal betrothal and a bride price, establishing a legal covenant between families. In the book of Ruth, the transformation of Ruth's status from a foreign "wife" of a deceased Israelite in Moab, to a destitute widow, and finally to the "wife" of Boaz, the kinsman-redeemer, showcases the Hebrew concept of levirate marriage and redemption. Ruth's journey demonstrates that faith and covenant loyalty (chesed) supersede ethnic origins, allowing a foreign 'ishshah to become a mother in the line of King David and Jesus Christ.
