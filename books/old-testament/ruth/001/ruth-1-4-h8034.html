NAME - ( shem , Strong's H8034 )

Summary:

In the Old Testament, a name is far more than a convenient label; it represents the very essence, character, reputation, and destiny of a person, as well as their enduring legacy. In Ruth 1:4, the introduction of the names of Orpah and Ruth establishes their identities and foreshadows their contrasting choices in the narrative. The concept of the "name" (shem) serves as a foundational theological and structural motif throughout the Book of Ruth, where the ultimate goal of redemption is to preserve the name and inheritance of the deceased, demonstrating God's covenant faithfulness in keeping His people from being forgotten.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Semitic culture, a name (shem) was intimately bound up with existence. To have no name was to not exist, while to have one's name wiped out was the ultimate tragedy, equivalent to complete annihilation. Names were frequently given to reflect the circumstances of a child’s birth, the parents' prayers, or a prophetic hope for the child's future. The word shem likely derives from a root associated with breath, sign, or conspicuousness, suggesting that a name is a visible sign of a person’s inner breath or life. It is the outward expression of an inward reality.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun shem refers fundamentally to an individual designation or proper name. Beyond simple identification, the semantic range includes:

• Reputation or Fame: A person's standing, renown, or character in the eyes of others (being "men of name" or having a "good name").

• Memory or Memorial: The preservation of a person's existence in the memory of succeeding generations.

• Presence and Authority: Representing the person themselves, particularly when referring to the "Name of Yahweh," which signifies His active presence, power, and glory among His people.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs shem in several vivid conceptual ways:

• Naming as an Act of Authority: When Adam names the animals (Genesis 2:19), he demonstrates his God-given dominion over them. To receive a name from a superior is to be placed under their care and authority.

• The Monument of the Name: In Isaiah 56:5, God promises faithful outcasts "a place and a name (shem) better than that of sons and daughters... an everlasting name." Here, shem acts as a spiritual monument that outlasts physical descendants.

• The Name as a Tower: Proverbs 18:10 describes the "name of the Lord" as a strong tower. This picture portrays the Name not as a magical formula, but as a place of safety, representing God's character, covenant, and protective power.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shem is central to the covenant relationship between God and Israel. God reveals Himself through His Name (Yahweh), which represents His unchanging faithfulness, holiness, and mercy.

In the Book of Ruth, the theological weight of shem shifts toward the preservation of family lines. Under the Mosaic covenant, land and lineage were sacred trusts. If a man died without an heir, his "name" was in danger of being blotted out from Israel. The levirate marriage custom was specifically designed to "raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance" (Ruth 4:10). Thus, the human action of redeeming a name reflects the divine character of Yahweh, who redeems His people and preserves their name in His covenant book.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:4, the narrator introduces the two Moabite women: "the name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other Ruth."

• This specific introduction sets up a literary and thematic contrast. The name Orpah (possibly related to the Hebrew word for "neck" or "back of the neck") foreshadows her ultimate decision to turn her back and return to Moab.

• The name Ruth (associated with "friendship" or "companion") perfectly mirrors her character of steadfast love (chesed) as she cleaves to Naomi and the God of Israel.

• Furthermore, placing these names immediately after the mention of Elimelech's death and right before the deaths of Mahlon and Chilion emphasizes the fragile state of these families. The narrative introduces these names at a moment of impending extinction, raising the dramatic question: Will these names survive, or will they be lost to history?

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:19 - "Out of the ground the Lord God formed every beast of the field and every bird of the air, and brought them to Adam to see what he would call them. And whatever Adam called each living creature, that was its name."

• Exodus 3:15 - "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The Lord God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you." This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations.'"

• Ruth 4:10 - "Moreover, Ruth the Moabitess, the widow of Mahlon, I have acquired as my wife, to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance, that the name of the dead may not be cut off from among his brethren and from his position at the gate. You are witnesses this day."

• Proverbs 22:1 - "A good name is to be chosen rather than great riches, loving favor rather than silver and gold."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In the ancient Near East, to be nameless was to be of no account. This is why the genealogies of the Bible, which can seem dry to modern readers, were deeply comforting to ancient Israelites. The preservation of these lists of names (shem) in Scripture serves as a permanent memorial before God. In the final chapter of Ruth, the public restoration of the family name at the town gate (Ruth 4:14-15) shows that the community viewed the preservation of Elimelech's name as a triumph of communal covenant love and divine providence.
