YEARS - ( shanah , Strong's H8141 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word shanah refers to a year, a measure of time determined by the solar cycle and the agricultural seasons, but it carries a deeper theological significance of change, repetition, and the passing of human opportunity. In the context of Ruth 1:4, the "ten years" spent by Elimelech’s family in Moab represents a protracted period of compromise, waiting, and cumulative tragedy outside the Promised Land. This duration underscores God's patience, the full measure of human testing, and the eventual arrival of a turning point in His redemptive plan.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word shanah is closely linked to the idea of change, repetition, or doubling. It derives from a root verb meaning "to repeat," "to do again," or "to change."

• This dual aspect of the root reflects how ancient Israelites viewed a year: a repeating cycle of seasons (seedtime and harvest, summer and winter) and a period that brings inevitable change, aging, and transformation to human life.

• In the agrarian society of ancient Israel, a year was not merely a chronological calendar count but an experiential cycle of sowing, harvesting, early rains, and dry seasons. It marked the rhythm of life under God's providential care.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Shanah designates a year, the measure of time completed by the earth's revolution around the sun, marked by the agricultural cycle.

• In the plural form (shanim), it refers to years, a long period of time, a lifetime, or age in general.

• The word can denote a specific calendar year (such as the year of a king's reign) or an indefinite but significant span of time characterized by specific experiences, such as years of famine or years of plenty.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The cyclical nature of shanah serves as a picture of God's faithfulness. The repeating seasons demonstrate His ongoing preservation of the earth.

• It is used to measure human life span, portraying the brief, passing nature of human existence compared to God's eternity.

• It is used in agricultural and covenantal laws, such as the Sabbatical Year (every seventh year) and the Year of Jubilee (every fifty years), where the cycle of years brings ultimate release, liberty, and restoration.

4. Theological Implications

• Shanah reminds God's people of the temporal nature of earthly life. Time is a gift from God, and the accumulation of years is a sign of His mercy and sustaining grace.

• In salvation history, years are the canvas upon which God paints His sovereign plan. He is the Lord of the years, overseeing both the years of judgment and the years of favor.

• The phrase "ten years" in Ruth 1:4 carries a structural weight in biblical narrative. Ten is often a biblical number associated with testing, completeness, or a full measure of responsibility. These ten years in Moab represent a complete cycle of trial and loss before God visits His people to give them bread.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:4, the phrase "about ten years" is filled with tragic irony. Elimelech's family originally went to Moab to escape a temporary famine, expecting a short stay. Instead, their stay stretched into a decade of displacement.

• These ten years represent a slow erosion of their identity and connection to the covenant land. During this time, Mahlon and Chilion married Moabite women, settling deep into pagan territory.

• The "ten years" serve to highlight the complete loss of Elimelech's male heirs. Over this decade, the family's resources, health, and lineage are completely stripped away until Naomi is left entirely destitute. It marks the long, painful, silent period of God's apparent absence before His grace bursts forth in Bethlehem.

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Hebrew calendar is lunisolar, meaning the years are calculated by the sun but the months by the moon. This required intercalary months to keep the agricultural seasons aligned with the calendar shanah.

• The spiritual lesson of the "ten years" in Ruth is that delay is not denial. Though Naomi felt the years were wasted and bitter, God was quietly preparing the lineage of David and ultimately the Messiah during those very years of exile.
