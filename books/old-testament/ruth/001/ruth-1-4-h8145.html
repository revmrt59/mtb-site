OTHER - ( sheniy , Strong's H8145 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sheniy, translated as "other" in Ruth 1:4, is an ordinal adjective that primarily means "second." In contexts where two individuals or objects are compared or listed, it functions to identify the second of the pair ("the one... and the other"). In the opening movement of the Book of Ruth, this word designates Ruth as the second Moabite wife taken by Elimelech's sons, placing her in immediate literary parallel with Orpah. Far from being a mere statistical filler, this ordinary word serves as a quiet narrative pivot. It introduces the woman who, despite being the "second" wife of a younger son in a tragic emigrant family, would ultimately become the primary human agent of covenant faithfulness and the direct ancestress of King David and Jesus Christ.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The word sheniy is derived from the root shanah, which carries the basic meaning of "to repeat," "to do again," or "to double." The word picture behind sheniy is not merely one of numerical value, but of sequence, relationship, and duplication. It points to that which follows a first, establishing a pair or a progression. In ancient Near Eastern thought, numbering and order were deeply tied to status, function, and inheritance. To be the "first" carried inherent privilege, while being the "second" or the "other" often meant occupying a subordinate or dependent position. In narrative contexts, however, the introduction of a "second" element sets up a comparative relationship, inviting the reader to watch how the two entities will diverge.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, sheniy functions primarily as the ordinal number "second." It is used to designate:

• The second in a sequence of time (e.g., the second day, the second month, or the second year).

• The second of two persons, groups, or objects (translated in the NKJV as "the other" or "the second").

• A double portion or a second helping of something.

• A person of second rank or next in authority (such as a second priest or deputy).

In Ruth 1:4, the word appears in its feminine form (shenit), agreeing with the implied noun "wife" or "woman." It is paired with the Hebrew word echad ("one"), creating the classic idiomatic construction: "the name of the one [was] Orpah, and the name of the other [second, shenit] Ruth."

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, sheniy is employed to establish order, structure, and covenant history:

• Creation and Order: It marks the "second day" of creation in Genesis 1:8, separating the waters above from the waters below.

• Pairs in Marriage: It is used in Genesis 4:19 to introduce Lamech's bigamous marriages: "the name of one was Adah, and the name of the other [sheniy] Zillah."

• Sanctuary and Ritual: In the construction of the Tabernacle and Temple, sheniy is used repeatedly to describe parallel curtains, rings, and the "other" cherub (1 Kings 6:25), emphasizing symmetry, beauty, and precise holiness.

• Secondary Authority: It describes those who hold the office of second-in-command, such as the second-ranking priests who guarded the temple threshold (2 Kings 25:18).

### 4. Theological Implications

The biblical use of sheniy often carries a profound theological subversion. While ancient cultures idolized the firstborn and the primary, the God of Israel repeatedly bypassed the "first" to choose, bless, and elevate the "second" (the sheniy).

• Abel's offering was accepted over Cain's (the firstborn).

• Isaac was chosen over Ishmael.

• Jacob was blessed over Esau.

• Ephraim was elevated over Manasseh.

• David, the youngest and last, was anointed king over his older brothers.

By introducing Ruth as the "other" or "second" wife, the text prepares the reader for this recurring biblical pattern. Orpah is listed first, but it is the "second" wife who becomes the vessel of redeeming grace. This reminds the church that human order, priority, and status do not dictate divine election or usefulness. God frequently delights in taking those who are considered secondary or "other" by the world and placing them at the center of His redemptive purposes.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:4, the narrator writes, "the name of the one was Orpah, and the name of the other Ruth." At this opening stage of the narrative, the two women are presented with complete structural equality. Both are Moabite women, both marry sons of Elimelech, and both are quickly widowed. No moral or spiritual distinction is initially made between the "one" and the "other."

However, this structural pairing serves as a powerful literary foil. By placing Orpah and Ruth side-by-side as a parallel pair, the author heightens the drama of their subsequent choices in verses 14–18. When Naomi urges both daughters-in-law to return to Moab, their paths radically diverge. The "one" (Orpah) kisses her mother-in-law and returns to her people and her gods. The "other" (Ruth) clings to Naomi, uttering one of the most magnificent confessions of faith in Holy Scripture. The designation of Ruth as the "other" sets up the stark contrast between standard human self-preservation and extraordinary, covenant-keeping love (chesed).

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 4:19 – "Then Lamech took for himself two wives: the name of one was Adah, and the name of the other Zillah." (Here sheniy is used in a identical grammatical construction to introduce a second wife).

• Leviticus 5:10 – "And he shall offer the second as a burnt offering according to the prescribed manner." (Demonstrates the ritual sequence where the first bird is for a sin offering, and the "second" is for a burnt offering).

• 1 Kings 6:25 – "And the other cherub was ten cubits; both cherubim were of the same size and shape." (Highlights the perfect symmetry and equal value of the two cherubim in the Holy of Holies).

• Ecclesiastes 4:15 – "I saw all the living who walk under the sun; they were with the second youth who stands in his place." (Illustrates political succession and the rise of a younger, secondary successor).

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In pastoral application, the transition of Ruth from the "other" to the primary subject of the story speaks directly to the nature of God's calling. In the eyes of Moab and Bethlehem, Ruth was an outsider, a secondary character, and a widow of low estate. Yet, in the economy of God's kingdom, those who are willing to lose their lives for the sake of the Lord find them. Ruth's willingness to accept the lowly place of the "other"—clinging to a destitute widow and gleaning in the corners of fields—becomes the very pathway through which God exalts her, making her a mother in Israel and a key link in the genealogy of Jesus Christ (Matthew 1:5).
