SONS - ( yeled , Strong's H3206 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun yeled refers to a child, boy, youth, or offspring, emphasizing the relationship of birth and descent. In the context of Ruth 1:5, it refers to Naomi’s two sons, Mahlon and Chilion, who have grown to adulthood, married, and died. By choosing yeled (child or offspring) instead of the more common ben (son) or ish (man), the author highlights the deep maternal bond and the profound, agonizing depth of Naomi’s loss. She did not just lose two adult heirs; she lost her "babies," the children she carried, birthed, and nurtured.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The root verb is yalad, which means "to bear, bring forth, or beget."

• Yeled is the concrete result of this action—a child born of a mother. It carries a strong emotional and familial connotation, invoking the image of infancy, parental nurture, and the innate vulnerability of offspring.

• In ancient Near Eastern culture, children were the ultimate sign of divine blessing, continuity of the family name, and economic security. To lose a yeled was to lose one's future, hope, and legacy.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Yeled denotes a male child, boy, youth, or offspring.

• The semantic range spans from a newborn infant (Exodus 2:6) to a young child (Genesis 21:8) to older youth or young men (1 Kings 12:8, where Rehoboam consults the "young men" who grew up with him).

• In the plural, it can denote children of both genders or offspring in general, emphasizing the parent-child relationship rather than legal standing or age.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The word is used to picture vulnerability and the deep instinctual love of a mother. For example, in Exodus 2:6, Pharaoh's daughter recognizes Moses in the basket as one of the Hebrews' "children" (yeled), invoking immediate pity for his helpless state.

• In Isaiah 9:6, it is used in the famous Messianic prophecy: "For unto us a Child (yeled) is born..." representing the vulnerability of the incarnation alongside the ultimate hope of redemption.

• In contrast, it can also picture immature counsel, as seen in the "young men" (yeladim) who advised Rehoboam (1 Kings 12:8), contrasting their foolishness with the wisdom of the elders.

4. Theological Implications

• Yeled represents the gift of life and the generational covenant. God's covenantal promises are passed down through the yeladim (children).

• The loss of a yeled represents the interruption of covenant blessing. Naomi’s loss of both yeladim signifies a complete death of her family line, leaving her in a state of spiritual and physical exile, seemingly cut off from God's favor.

• Ultimately, the restoration of children in the lineage of Ruth (the birth of Obed) points to God’s sovereignty in bringing life out of death, redeeming the lost yeled with a new child who would secure the line of David and the Messiah.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:5, Mahlon and Chilion are adult men who have been married for about ten years (Ruth 1:4). Normally, the Hebrew word ben (son) or ish (man) would be expected to describe them.

• The deliberate use of yeled (literally "her two children/born ones") emphasizes Naomi’s maternal perspective. To a mother, her children are always her "babies."

• This word choice intensifies the grief of the passage. Naomi is not just a widow who lost two male heirs; she is a bereaved mother who has outlived the children she gave birth to and raised. The text strips away their adult status to lay bare the raw tragedy of maternal bereavement.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 21:15 - "And the water in the skin was used up, and she placed the boy (yeled) under one of the shrubs."

• Exodus 2:6 - "And when she opened it, she saw the child (yeled), and behold, the baby wept."

• 2 Samuel 12:16 - "David therefore pleaded with God for the child (yeled), and David fasted and went in and lay all night on the ground."

• Isaiah 9:6 - "For unto us a Child (yeled) is born, Unto us a Son is given..."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between yeled (child) in Ruth 1:5 and the birth of the ben (son) in Ruth 4:17 highlights the literary and theological arc of the book. The book opens with the tragic death of Naomi's two yeladim (children) in Moab and closes with the joyful birth of a ben (son) in Bethlehem, who is declared to be a "restorer of life" to Naomi.
