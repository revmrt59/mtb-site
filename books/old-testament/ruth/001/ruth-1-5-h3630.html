CHILION - ( kilyôn , Strong's H3630 )

Summary:

Chilion is the proper name of one of the two sons of Elimelech and Naomi in the book of Ruth. Derived from a Hebrew root meaning to finish, fail, or waste away, his name literally translates to "pining," "failing," or "consumption." In the narrative of Ruth, Chilion’s name stands as a somber literary and historical marker of the fragility of human life apart from the covenant land. Along with his brother Mahlon ("sickly"), Chilion represents the tragic consequence of Israel's spiritual and physical wandering during the period of the Judges, highlighting the absolute necessity of divine preservation and redemption.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In ancient Hebrew culture, names were highly significant, often carrying prophetic weight, describing the physical circumstances of a child's birth, or expressing the spiritual state of the parents. Chilion was born during the turbulent period of the Judges, a time characterized by spiritual anarchy and physical famine in Israel. His name reflects a atmosphere of despair. Born into a household that would eventually abandon the Promised Land for the pagan fields of Moab, Chilion's name paints a vivid picture of a family whose strength and legacy were gradually wasting away.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The proper name kilyôn is derived from the primary Hebrew verb kālah, which carries the meaning of ending, ceasing, vanishing, perishing, or being completely spent. Lexically, the name Chilion means "pining," "failing," "frailty," or "consumption."

The name shares an immediate root connection with the common Hebrew noun killyôn (Strong's H3631), which refers to a wasting away of the eyes or a decree of destruction. Thus, the lexical force of the name denotes a state of progressive depletion, a life that gradually empties out until nothing remains.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The semantic root of Chilion depicts several vivid word pictures in the Old Testament:

• The Failing Candle: The verb root describes a lamp running out of oil or a fire burning down to cold ashes. It represents the cessation of vital energy.

• Failing Eyes: In Hebrew thought, when eyes "fail" (using the cognate noun), it describes a person who has looked so long for help or deliverance without finding it that their vision grows dim from exhaustion and grief.

• The Finished Work: The root can also mean to complete or finish. In Chilion’s case, this completion is tragic—his life and his father's household in Moab reached a dead end, completely finished without heirs to carry on the family name until God intervened through Boaz.

### 4. Theological Implications

The presence of Chilion in the text raises critical theological lessons for the church:

• The Cost of Compromise: Elimelech's family left Bethlehem ("House of Bread") to seek sustenance in Moab, a nation historically hostile to Israel and under divine displeasure. The names of his sons—Mahlon ("sickly") and Chilion ("pining")—serve as a theological commentary on the spiritual and physical decay that accompanies a departure from God's place of blessing and covenant.

• The Frailty of Human Schemes: Human plans to survive apart from God's covenantal boundaries ultimately "waste away" (Chilion) and "fail."

• The Backdrop for Grace: Chilion’s death, along with his brother’s, created a state of absolute emptiness for Naomi. This total ruin of human hope is the exact canvas upon which God paints His narrative of redeeming grace. The death of the "pining one" clears the way for the arrival of the redeemer.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:5, the death of Chilion is stated with stark, unembellished brevity: "Then both Mahlon and Chilion also died." In the context of the opening chapter, the narrator uses these names to build a heavy sense of irony and doom. The family sought life in Moab but found only the grave.

The sequence of deaths—first the father Elimelech, then the two sons—leaves Naomi completely destitute. The textual placement of Chilion’s death emphasizes the utter end of Elimelech's line. The "pining" son has finally met his namesake end, leaving the family name extinct in the eyes of the law, setting up the urgent need for a Levirate redeemer.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

Because H3630 is a proper noun referring specifically to this biblical character, its occurrences are limited to the opening of the book of Ruth:

• Ruth 1:2 - "The name of the man was Elimelech, the name of his wife Naomi, and the names of his two sons Mahlon and Chilion—Ephrathites of Bethlehem, Judah. And they went to the country of Moab and remained there."

• Ruth 1:5 - "Then both Mahlon and Chilion also died; so the woman survived her two sons and her husband."

• Additionally, the closely related cognate noun (H3631) demonstrates how this word operates in broader Hebrew thought:

• Deuteronomy 28:65 - "And among those nations you shall find no rest, nor shall the sole of your foot have a resting place; but there the Lord will give you a trembling heart, failing eyes, and anguish of soul."

• Isaiah 10:22 - "For though your people, O Israel, be as the sand of the sea, A remnant of them will return; The destruction decreed shall overflow with righteousness."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• Thematic Contrast: The names of Naomi's family members stand in sharp contrast to the characters introduced later in the book. While Mahlon and Chilion represent sickness and wasting away, Boaz represents "strength" and Obed represents "servant/worshiper" who becomes a "restorer of life" to Naomi (Ruth 4:15).

• LXX Translation: In the Greek Septuagint, the name is transliterated as Chelaiōn, preserving the phonetic connection to the Hebrew root of frailty.

• Sermon Application: A pastor can use Chilion's name to illustrate the "Moab modern parallel"—how pursuing worldly security apart from God's will results in spiritual consumption and the wasting away of our labors, whereas returning to God's house brings abundant life.
