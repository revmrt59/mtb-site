HUSBAND - ( 'ish , Strong's H376 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word 'ish primarily means "man" or "husband," denoting an individual male, often in contrast to a woman ('ishshah) or in relation to a wife as her companion, protector, and covenant partner. In Ruth 1:5, it refers to Elimelech, Naomi's deceased husband, highlighting the devastating loss of her primary relational, legal, and economic anchor in a patriarchal society.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word 'ish reflects the concept of an individual in his strength, personality, and relational capacity. It stands in contrast to adam, which more frequently refers to humanity generically or man in relation to the ground (adamah). In the ancient Near East, a husband ('ish) was the head of the household (bet'av), responsible for securing the family's survival, guarding the ancestral land inheritance, and ensuring the continuation of the family name. The covenantal relationship between an 'ish (husband) and an 'ishshah (wife) forms the bedrock of biblical family structure, instituted by God in creation to reflect mutual dependence, companionship, and physical oneness.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word 'ish refers to an individual male human being, a husband, or a person of high standing. When used in a marital context, it designates a husband who is legally and covenantally bound to his wife. Its broad semantic range includes:

• An individual man (in distinction from a woman or child).

• A husband (specifically in relation to his wife).

• A champion, warrior, or man of distinction (e.g., a "man of valor").

• Any person or "each one" (used as a distributive pronoun).

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The relationship between 'ish (man/husband) and 'ishshah (woman/wife) is built on a profound Hebrew wordplay that highlights ontological equality and functional distinction.

• As a husband, the 'ish is portrayed as a source of "rest" (menuchah) and security for his wife. The home of a husband is meant to be a sanctuary of peace and protection.

• The word is also used metaphorically of God's relationship with His people. When God speaks of restoring Israel, He describes a shift in her language where she will no longer call Him Baal ("my master") but 'ish ("my husband"), indicating a move from servile fear to intimate, covenantal love.

4. Theological Implications

The loss of an 'ish (husband) in ancient Israel was a theological and social crisis. Because women did not typically inherit land or hold independent legal standing, a widow was exceptionally vulnerable to poverty, exploitation, and social erasure.

• God repeatedly identifies Himself as the "defender of widows," stepping into the void left by the deceased 'ish to provide protection and justice.

• The book of Ruth demonstrates how God uses the covenant faithfulness (chesed) of individuals to restore what was lost. The narrative moves from the loss of Naomi's 'ish (Elimelech) to the provision of a new 'ish (Boaz) for Ruth, showing God's redemption of a collapsed family line.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:5, the word 'ish appears with a pronominal suffix (mê-'î-šāh), translated as "her husband." The context of this verse is one of absolute grief and social death.

• The narrative notes that Elimelech, Naomi’s husband, died first (v. 3). Her sons then married Moabite women, lived for about ten years, and also died (v. 4-5).

• The phrasing "so the woman survived her two sons and her husband" emphasizes Naomi's total bereavement. She is stripped of her past (her husband), her present (her economic security), and her future (her sons). By losing her 'ish, Naomi loses her legal covering and social identity in Israel, setting the stage for her bitter return to Bethlehem and her desperate need for a kinsman-redeemer.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:6: "She also gave to her husband with her, and he ate."

• Genesis 3:16: "Your desire shall be for your husband, And he shall rule over you."

• Ruth 1:3: "Then Elimelech, Naomi's husband, died; and she was left, and her two sons."

• Hosea 2:16: "And it shall be, in that day," Says the LORD, "That you will call Me 'My Husband,' And no longer call Me 'My Master.'"

7. Other Relevant Insights

It is valuable to contrast 'ish with baal (H1167), another Hebrew word translated as "husband." While baal emphasizes ownership, mastery, and authority, 'ish emphasizes partnership, companionship, and covenantal intimacy. While both terms are legally valid for a husband, 'ish carries a warmth and relational depth that highlights the personal bond between a man and his wife, rather than merely his legal rights over her.
