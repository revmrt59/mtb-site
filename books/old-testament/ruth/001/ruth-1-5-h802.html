WOMAN - ( ishshah , Strong's H802 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ishshah denotes a female human being, a wife, or a woman in various life stages, serving as the feminine counterpart to ish (man). In the opening of the Book of Ruth, this term shifts from describing general female relationships to focusing poignantly on Naomi. Stripped of her husband and her sons, Naomi is left utterly vulnerable in a patriarchal world. The designation of Naomi as "the woman" in Ruth 1:5 underscores her extreme isolation, bereavement, and survival, setting the stage for God's providential redemption through covenant loyalty.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew noun ishshah is the primary term for "woman" or "wife" in the Old Testament. Linguistically, it stands in complementary relation to ish (man). In the ancient Near East, a woman’s identity, protection, social standing, and economic security were intimately bound to her male relatives—first her father, then her husband, and eventually her sons. To be an ishshah without an ish or sons was to occupy one of the most perilous positions in society, devoid of legal advocacy, inheritance rights, and social protection. In the historical and cultural context of Ruth, Naomi's survival as an ishshah in a foreign land highlights the absolute destitution of her circumstances.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word ishshah refers to:

• An adult female human being, distinct from a male.

• A wife, representing a woman joined to a husband in marriage.

• A female companion or female animal (in specific ritual or narrative contexts, though rare).

In Ruth 1:5, the word functions as a singular noun with the definite article (ha-ishshah, "the woman"). Here, the word means "the woman" in a state of complete bereavement, denoting Naomi as one who has been stripped of the relationships that previously defined her status in the home and the community.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Creation Pattern: In Genesis 2:23, ishshah is presented as the one taken out of ish, establishing the essential, corresponding, and equal dignity between man and woman.

• The Covenantal and Legal Usage: Throughout the Torah, ishshah is used to outline the rights, protections, and boundaries governing wives, mothers, and widows, showing God's deep care for the structural vulnerabilities of women in ancient society.

• The Bereaved Survivor: In Ruth 1:5, Naomi is not called "the mother" or "the widow" at the exact moment of her sons' deaths; she is called "the woman." This word picture strips away her domestic titles. She is reduced to her most basic, bare biological identity, emphasizing her profound loneliness and vulnerability.

4. Theological Implications

• The Vulnerability of the Marginalized: God’s law consistently demands care for the widow, the orphan, and the stranger. By reducing Naomi to "the woman" who survived, the narrative sets up a profound theological truth: God’s grace starts at the very bottom of human helplessness.

• Providential Reversal: The transition of Naomi from an empty "woman" (ishshah) in Ruth 1:5 to a grandmother embraced by her community in Ruth 4:14 demonstrates how God restores those who are socially and relationally dead.

• Covenant Faithfulness (Hesed): The story of this specific ishshah reveals that God does not abandon those who have lost their earthly protectors. He raises up redeemers to restore life, heritage, and hope.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:5, the word occurs at the absolute nadir of Naomi's tragedy: "Then both Mahlon and Chilion also died; so the woman survived her two sons and her husband."

• The Hebrew verb translated "survived" (sha'ar) literally means "was left" or "remained." Naomi is "left behind" as a solitary ishshah.

• The author lists her losses in reverse chronological order: her sons first, then her husband. This literary structure emphasizes her complete isolation. She has no husband to protect her and no sons to carry on the family name or provide for her old age. Calling her "the woman" instead of "Naomi" or "their mother" underscores her absolute destitution in the pagan land of Moab.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:23 - "And Adam said: 'This is now bone of my bones And flesh of my flesh; She shall be called Woman, Because she was taken out of Man.'"

• Genesis 3:15 - "And I will put enmity Between you and the woman, And between your seed and her Seed; He shall bruise your head, And you shall bruise his heel."

• Ruth 3:11 - "And now, my daughter, do not fear. I will do for you all that you request, for all the people of my town know that you are a virtuous woman."

• Proverbs 31:30 - "Charm is deceitful and beauty is passing, But a woman who fears the Lord, she shall be praised."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The structural symmetry of the Book of Ruth begins with a "woman" (ishshah) stripped of everything (Ruth 1:5) and ends with the "women" (nashim, plural of ishshah) of Bethlehem celebrating a birth that restores life to Naomi (Ruth 4:14).

• The term ishshah is used of Ruth when Boaz calls her a "virtuous woman" (eshet chayil) in Ruth 3:11. This forms a beautiful literary bracket: Naomi is the broken ishshah who has lost her home, while Ruth is the worthy ishshah through whom God will rebuild Naomi's household and the lineage of David.
