TWO - ( shenayim , Strong's H8147 )

Summary:

In Ruth 1:5, the word "two" (shenayim) emphasizes the complete and devastating loss experienced by Naomi in the land of Moab. Having already lost her husband, the death of her "two" sons represents the total extinction of her immediate family line and the loss of her social and economic security. Far from a mere statistical designation, this number underscores the depth of her grief and the vacancy left in her life, setting the stage for God's remarkable covenant redemption.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew number shenayim is the cardinal number two. Its root is closely associated with repetition, duplication, or doing something a second time. In the ancient Near Eastern context, numbers carried both literal and symbolic weight. The number two often signifies a basic unit of testimony, companionship, or completeness in pair. In family dynamics, having two sons represented a double blessing and a secure future, ensuring that the father's name and inheritance would be preserved even if one son met an untimely end. For Naomi, the presence of two sons was her final safety net in a foreign land.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The word shenayim functions primarily as a cardinal numeral meaning "two." Depending on its grammatical state, it can appear in the absolute form (shenayim for masculine, shtayim for feminine) or the construct form (shene or shte). In Ruth 1:5, it appears in the masculine construct form (shene), bound directly to "sons" (baneha), meaning "the two of her sons." The term denotes:

• A pair or couple.

• Double of something.

• Two distinct entities forming a complete set in a specific context.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, shenayim is used in several distinct theological and practical word pictures:

• The Ark of Noah: The preservation of life was secured by bringing "two of every sort" into the ark, representing the minimum required for the continuation of creation.

• Covenant Witness: Legal and historical truth was established on the testimony of "two or three witnesses."

• Companionship: The wisdom literature highlights "two" as a defense against isolation and vulnerability, noting that two are better than one.

• Double Portion: The firstborn inheritance required a double portion, indicating favor and provision.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of "two" in the opening of Ruth is profound. In Hebrew thought, the death of one son is a tragedy; the death of "two" sons is a catastrophic erasure of the family's future. It signifies:

• Absolute Emptying: Naomi is stripped of her husband and both of her sons. The text uses "two" to show that there is no remaining heir, no backup plan, and no human leverage. Naomi is left utterly empty, which sets up the theological theme of God filling the empty.

• The Failure of Human Schemes: Elimelech left Bethlehem to preserve his family of four (himself, his wife, and his two sons). By the end of verse five, the three men are dead. The "two sons" who were meant to carry the name in Moab instead become graves in Moab.

• Covenant Vulnerability: Without male advocates, Naomi and her two daughters-in-law are exposed to extreme poverty. The number highlights the desperate need for a kinsman-redeemer (goel).

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:5, the text reads: "Then both Mahlon and Chilion also died; so the woman survived her two sons and her husband."

The writer intentionally structures the sentence to emphasize Naomi's isolation. The verse begins with "both" (literally, "the two of them") and ends by describing them as her "two sons." By sandwiching the names of the sons between these numerical references, the narrative highlights the complete loss of her maternal identity and security. The word shenayim acts as a painful reminder of what she once possessed in abundance. The "two sons" who were once her hope are now gone, leaving her with "two" daughters-in-law (Ruth and Orpah), shifting her source of human hope from her own flesh and blood to two foreign women.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 6:19 - "And of every living thing of all flesh you shall bring two of every sort into the ark, to keep them alive with you; they shall be male and female."

• Joshua 2:1 - "Now Joshua the son of Nun sent out two men from Acacia Grove to spy secretly, saying, 'Go, view the land, especially Jericho.'"

• Ecclesiastes 4:9 - "Two are better than one, Because they have a good reward for their labor."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In Israelite society, the loss of both sons meant that Elimelech’s property would likely revert to other relatives, and the family line would be blotted out under heaven. This cultural reality explains Naomi's bitter lament in Ruth 1:20-21. When she returns to Bethlehem, she does not just grieve as a mother; she grieves as a woman whose legal existence has been virtually erased. The restoration of her family line through Ruth and Boaz at the end of the book directly answers the tragedy of losing her "two sons" in verse five.
