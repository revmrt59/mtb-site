BREAD - ( leḥem , Strong's H3899 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word leḥem refers to bread, grain, or food in general, serving as the essential staple of physical life and nourishment in the ancient Near East. Throughout the Old Testament, it represents God's providential care, covenant faithfulness, and sustenance. In Ruth 1:6, the return of leḥem to Bethlehem—a name meaning "House of Bread"—after a devastating famine signals that Yahweh has visited His people. This restoration of food demonstrates God's mercy, ends His people's period of chastisement, and initiates Naomi and Ruth’s journey back to the land of promise and ultimate redemption.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, leḥem was not a optional side dish or a culinary afterthought; it was the primary source of daily calories and the literal sustainer of human life. Producing bread required a laborious, multi-step process: plowing the soil, sowing wheat or barley seed, waiting for the early and latter rains, harvesting, threshing, winnowing, grinding the grain into flour, kneading the dough, and baking it over an open fire or in a clay oven. Because the entire cycle was highly vulnerable to weather and pests, the presence of bread was a direct indicator of peace, climate stability, and divine favor. A total lack of bread meant starvation, economic collapse, and societal ruin.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The noun leḥem fundamentally refers to bread, food, or grain used for making bread. It is derived from the verbal root laḥam, which carries the primary sense of consuming, feeding on, or eating. In its broadest usage, leḥem represents food of any kind, including meat, fruit, or the general nourishment of cattle. However, in the vast majority of its occurrences, it refers specifically to loaves of baked grain (barley or wheat). Lexically, it designates the indispensable daily food portion required for human survival, often paired with water to represent the bare essentials of physical life.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament utilizes leḥem in several rich literal and figurative contexts:

• The Staple of Life: It is frequently described as the "staff of bread" or that which "strengthens man's heart," illustrating its role as a structural support for physical existence.

• Manna as Heavenly Bread: In the wilderness, God rained down manna, which is described as leḥem from heaven, highlighting supernatural provision when human resources were exhausted.

• Sacrificial Showbread: In the Tabernacle and Temple, twelve loaves of leḥem (the "bread of the Presence") were placed on a golden table before the Holy of Holies, symbolizing the continuous communion, fellowship, and sustenance God provides for the twelve tribes of Israel.

• Metaphor of Adversity: Conversely, times of spiritual and physical judgment are characterized by the "bread of adversity" or the "bread of tears," indicating sorrow, grief, and deprivation.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, leḥem is deeply tied to the covenant relationship between Yahweh and Israel. Under the Mosaic Covenant, agricultural abundance and bread were signs of obedience and blessing, whereas famine and the removal of bread were signs of covenant disobedience and divine discipline.

Furthermore, the provision of leḥem highlights God's absolute sovereignty over creation. Humanity labors, but it is God who gives the rain and causes the earth to yield its harvest.

Ultimately, physical leḥem points forward to spiritual realities. The historical events in Bethlehem ("House of Bread") set a theological trajectory that culminates in the New Testament, where Jesus Christ, born in Bethlehem, declares Himself to be the true leḥem of life who comes down from heaven to give eternal life to the world.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:6, the word leḥem is loaded with dramatic and geographic irony. The narrative begins in Bethlehem (Bet-leḥem), which literally translates to the "House of Bread." Yet, because of Israel's spiritual decline during the era of the Judges, a severe famine struck, rendering the House of Bread empty of bread. This prompted Elimelech and Naomi to depart the land of promise for pagan Moab.

When Ruth 1:6 reports that Naomi "heard... that the Lord had visited His people in giving them bread," the word leḥem signals the reversal of the curse and the return of covenant blessing. God's "visitation" (paqad) is characterized by grace; He has not abandoned His people. The physical presence of leḥem back in Bethlehem is the catalyst for Naomi's return. It proves that the Lord has restored His favor to the land, setting the stage for the redemption of Naomi's family line and the inclusion of Ruth the Moabitess into the lineage of the Messiah.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:19: "In the sweat of your face you shall eat bread till you return to the ground..."

• Exodus 16:4: "Then the LORD said to Moses, 'Behold, I will rain bread from heaven for you...'"

• Deuteronomy 8:3: "So He humbled you, allowed you to hunger, and fed you with manna which you did not know nor did your fathers know, that He might make you know that man shall not live by bread alone; but man lives by every word that proceeds from the mouth of the LORD."

• Psalm 104:15: "And wine that makes glad the heart of man, oil to make his face shine, and bread which strengthens man's heart."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Law of Hospitality: In ancient Israelite culture, breaking leḥem with someone was a sacred act of hospitality and peace. To share one’s bread was to offer protection and establish a covenantal bond of friendship.

• Harvest Connection: The return of leḥem in Ruth 1:6 prepares the reader for the barley and wheat harvests mentioned later in the chapter. Ruth’s act of gleaning behind the reapers to secure grain for leḥem becomes the physical means by which Boaz shows his kindness, directly fulfilling God's promise to provide bread for the vulnerable (the widow, the poor, and the stranger).
