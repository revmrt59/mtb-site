MOAB - ( Mo'ab , Strong's H4124 )

Summary:

The name "Moab" refers both to the son of Lot—born of an incestuous union with his eldest daughter—and the historical region and nation situated east of the Dead Sea. In the book of Ruth, Moab represents a place of temporary physical refuge from famine that ultimately becomes a graveyard of Israeli hope and family lineage, setting the stage for God's surprising grace to break through via a Moabitess widow.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• Historically, Moab is the elevated plateau east of the Dead Sea, bounded by the deep gorge of the Arnon to the north and the valley of Zered to the south.

• The origin of the name connects to Lot's family history, where the eldest daughter named her son Moab, which is etymologically understood as "from father."

• This incestuous origin cast a long shadow over Israel's relationship with the Moabites, who were closely related neighbors but often fierce adversaries.

• Geographically, Moab was a fertile land of rolling hills that received more regular rainfall than the wilderness regions of Israel, which explains why Elimelech looked to it as an agricultural sanctuary during a famine in Judah.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The proper noun Mo'ab refers to the geographic territory east of the Jordan and the Dead Sea, as well as the ethnic group descending from Lot.

• Lexically, the name means "from father" or "of his father," pointing directly to his patriarchal origin.

• In the Hebrew Bible, it is used to designate the physical country or fields of Moab (sadeh Mo'ab) and the collective nation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Moab is portrayed in Scripture with a dual identity: a relative of Israel yet a spiritual and military threat.

• During the wilderness wanderings, Moab refused Israel passage and hired Balaam to curse them, and later enticed Israelite men into idolatry and sexual immorality at Baal-Peor.

• In the historical and prophetic books, Moab represents a pagan territory outside the land of covenant promise. Going to Moab signifies leaving the place of God's inheritance and protection to seek sustenance in a spiritually compromised territory.

• Consequently, Moab functions as a spiritual word picture for compromise, self-sufficiency, and looking to the world for provision instead of trusting the covenant God of Israel.

4. Theological Implications

• Sovereignty over foreign nations: Though a pagan nation worshipping the god Chemosh, Moab is ultimately subject to Yahweh's purposes and judgment.

• Grace over exclusion: Despite the covenant restriction banning Moabites from entering the assembly of Yahweh to the tenth generation, Ruth the Moabitess becomes an ancestor of King David and Jesus Christ. This demonstrates that faith and covenant loyalty (chesed) supersede ethnic barriers and ancestral curses.

• The cost of backsliding: Elimelech's family sought life in Moab but found only death and bereavement. This reinforces the theological truth that true life and security are found in God's presence, not in worldly bypasses.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:6, Naomi "arose with her daughters-in-law that she might return from the country of Moab, for she had heard in the country of Moab that the LORD had visited His people by giving them bread."

• Moab is twice designated in this verse as the "country" (literally, the "fields") of Moab.

• The contrast is sharp: Naomi hears in the land of compromise (Moab) that Yahweh has visited His covenant people in the land of promise (Bethlehem, Judah) with bread.

• Moab serves as the turning point of the narrative. It is the place of Naomi's deepest grief, but also the starting point of her repentance and return as she turns her back on the pagan fields and sets her face toward the house of bread.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 19:37 – "And the firstborn bore a son and called his name Moab; he is the father of the Moabites to this day."

• Deuteronomy 34:5 – "So Moses the servant of the LORD died there in the land of Moab, according to the word of the LORD."

• Ruth 1:1 – "Now it came to pass, in the days when the judges ruled, that there was a famine in the land. And a certain man of Bethlehem, Judah, went to dwell in the country of Moab, he and his wife and his two sons."

• Ruth 1:22 – "So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess her daughter-in-law with her, who returned from the country of Moab. Now they came to Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Prophetic warnings: In the prophets, Moab is frequently condemned for its extreme pride and arrogance, which stemmed from its natural geographic security and agricultural wealth.

• The geography of repentance: The journey from Bethlehem to Moab required descending into the Jordan Rift Valley (about 1,200 feet below sea level) and climbing up the steep cliffs of the Moabite plateau. Return required the reverse. This arduous physical climb back to Judah mirrors the difficult but necessary process of spiritual repentance.
