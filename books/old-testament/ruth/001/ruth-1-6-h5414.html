GIVING - ( nathan , Strong's H5414 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb nathan is a foundational covenant word representing the act of giving, placing, or granting. In Ruth 1:6, it highlights God's sovereign grace and provision as He "visits" His people to give them bread. This divine action brings an end to the famine in Bethlehem—the "House of Bread"—and initiates the restoration of Naomi's hope, illustrating that God is the ultimate source of life, sustenance, and covenant faithfulness.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The verb nathan occurs over 2,000 times in the Old Testament, making it one of the most common and versatile words in the Hebrew Scriptures. At its concrete root, the word evokes the physical picture of hand-to-hand transfer—one person extending their open hand to place an object into the hand of another. It represents a deliberate, personal transaction. In ancient Near Eastern culture, the act of giving established or reinforced relationships, carrying connotations of authority, stewardship, and grace. The giver must possess the right or ownership of the resource before they can bestow it upon another.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary semantic range of nathan encompasses to give, grant, bestow, deliver, set, or permit.

• It denotes the physical transfer of tangible gifts, tribute, or daily provisions.

• It signifies the granting of non-tangible assets, such as mercy, favor, wisdom, or authority.

• It is used in legal or covenantal contexts to mean "to establish" or "to dedicate" (such as setting apart the Levites or giving a law).

In Ruth 1:6, the word appears in the infinitive construct form (lateth), functioning as a gerund or purpose clause. It expresses the specific action and outcome of God's gracious visitation: "to give" or "in giving" them food.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, nathan is used to describe both human and divine actions, but its most profound theological usage occurs when Yahweh is the subject.

• The Land as a Gift: The land of Canaan is repeatedly described as the land that Yahweh "gives" to Abraham and his descendants.

• Creation and Sustenance: God is depicted as the one who gives rain, gives breath to humanity, and gives food to all living creatures.

• The Law and Covenant: God gives His commandments and gives His covenant as a framework of relationship.

When God is the one giving, the word depicts an overflowing storehouse of divine favor. It shows that humanity is entirely dependent on the open hand of the Creator.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, nathan in Ruth 1:6 underscores the doctrines of common grace, divine providence, and covenant restoration.

• The End of Judgment: Under the Mosaic covenant, famine was a consequence of disobedience and spiritual apostasy (Deuteronomy 28:23-24). The restoration of food through God's "giving" indicates that the period of chastisement had ended, and God was once again extending covenant mercy to His people.

• Divine Initiative: The text does not credit the return of crops to human agricultural skill, favorable weather patterns, or political changes. It is attributed directly to the hand of God. Redemption begins with divine initiative.

• Sovereign Provision: God is the owner of the earth. When He "gives" bread, He is exercising His rightful ownership to sustain His covenant people, demonstrating that even in times of national spiritual decay, He remains faithful to His promises.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:6, the use of nathan is highly deliberate and rich with literary irony.

• The Wordplay on Bethlehem: Naomi and her family had abandoned Bethlehem, which literally means "House of Bread," because there was no bread. The famine had emptied the house. When Naomi hears that Yahweh has visited His people "in giving (nathan) them bread (lehem)," the irony is beautifully resolved. The House of Bread is filled once again because the Lord has given bread.

• The Catalyst for Return: This report of God's giving is the precise turning point of the narrative. It is the gospel—the good news—that Naomi hears in the far country of Moab. This hearing prompts her to "arise" and "return." God's act of giving bread physically sustains the land, but spiritually, it draws the prodigal back to the place of blessing.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 1:29: "And God said, 'See, I have given you every herb that yields seed which is on the face of all the earth...'"

• Leviticus 26:4: "then I will give you rain in its season, the land shall yield its produce, and the trees of the field shall yield their fruit."

• Psalm 104:27: "These all wait for You, That You may give them their food in due season."

• Psalm 136:25: "Who gives food to all flesh, For His mercy endures forever."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The theme of God "giving bread" in the Old Testament points directly forward to the New Testament fulfillment in Jesus Christ. In the wilderness, God gave Israel manna. In Bethlehem (the House of Bread), God ultimately gave His Son. Jesus connects these themes in John 6, declaring that He is the "true bread from heaven" which the Father "gives" for the life of the world. The physical bread given to Bethlehem in Ruth 1:6 kept a family alive to preserve the Davidic line, which ultimately brought forth the Bread of Life to satisfy the spiritual hunger of all humanity.
