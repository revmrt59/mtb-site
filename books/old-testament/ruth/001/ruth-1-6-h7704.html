COUNTRY - ( sadeh , Strong's H7704 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to an open field, cultivated land, or the defined territory of a specific nation or people group. In the book of Ruth, it designates the "country" or "fields" of Moab, representing a foreign, pagan territory where Elimelech's family sought refuge during a famine. This setting serves as a place of initial survival that quickly turned to tragedy and death, ultimately prompting Naomi to return to her homeland when she heard that the Lord had visited His people by giving them bread.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

In the ancient Near East, sadeh evoked the image of open, expansive land, distinct from walled cities, rugged wilderness, or barren desert. It represented arable land, pastureland, or a specific geographic region defined by its inhabitants. In an agrarian society, the sadeh was the primary source of life and livelihood, where crops were sown, tended, and harvested. When used to describe a foreign nation—such as the "country" or "fields" of Moab—it denotes the physical territory, sovereign borders, and cultural-religious sphere of that people.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The Hebrew word sadeh refers to an open field, a cultivated plot of ground, or a broad territory belonging to an individual, clan, or nation. The semantic range spans from small, private agricultural plots of land to vast national territories. In the context of Ruth 1:6, the word is used in its plural form (sedeh) to denote the territorial region of Moab, a high plateau situated east of the Dead Sea and the Jordan River.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, sadeh is frequently contrasted with walled cities, representing the unprotected space where agricultural labor is performed, flocks are grazed, wild beasts roam, or battles are fought.

• Culturally, the sadeh was the place of daily labor and dependence on seasonal rains.

• Metaphorically, a fertile field represents divine blessing, fruitfulness, and life, while a devastated field represents judgment, drought, and spiritual emptiness.

• In the narrative arc of the book of Ruth, the word shifts from the "country [fields] of Moab" (associated with famine, exile, compromise, and death) to the "field of Boaz" in Bethlehem (associated with redemption, abundance, safety, and covenant faithfulness).

### 4. Theological Implications

The geographical movements between different fields in the book of Ruth mirror spiritual realities. Elimelech's decision to leave the fields of Bethlehem ("the house of bread") during a famine to dwell in the pagan fields of Moab represents a reliance on sight rather than faith. Though Moab's fields offered physical sustenance, they became a place of spiritual isolation and family tragedy.

Theological restoration begins when Naomi, residing in the foreign fields of Moab, hears that Yahweh has visited His people in Judah by giving them food. This highlights God’s sovereign control over the fertility of the earth and underscores that true security is found not in foreign abundance, but under the covenant wings of Yahweh in the Land of Promise.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:6, the term "country of Moab" describes the place of Naomi’s exile. The text emphasizes her physical presence in this foreign territory at the exact moment she receives the good news of Bethlehem’s harvest. The contrast is sharp: while living in the tragic "fields" of Moab—where she buried her husband and two sons—she hears that the fields of Judah are once again overflowing with God's provision. This prompts her immediate decision to arise and depart from the place of her affliction to return to the land of covenant blessing.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In ancient Near Eastern culture, lands and territories were deeply tied to territorial deities. To reside in the sadeh of Moab was to live within the cultural and religious domain of Chemosh, the chief god of the Moabites. Therefore, Naomi and Ruth's journey from the sadeh of Moab back to the sadeh of Judah was not merely a geographic relocation, but a profound spiritual repatriation—a declaration of turning away from foreign idols to seek refuge under the wings of the Lord God of Israel.
