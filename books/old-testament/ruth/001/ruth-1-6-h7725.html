RETURN - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub is one of the most significant and frequently used words in the Old Testament, fundamentally meaning to turn back, return, or go back to a starting point. In the book of Ruth, and specifically in Ruth 1:6, it functions as a powerful literary and spiritual motif. It denotes not just a physical relocation from the pagan land of Moab back to Bethlehem, but a profound theological pivot—a returning to the covenant land, the covenant community, and ultimately, the covenant God who has visited His people with provision.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The physical imagery behind shub is that of a traveler who has departed from a home, a path, or a relationship, and makes a decisive 180-degree turn to retrace their steps back to the point of origin. It is a motion of reversal. In the ancient Near Eastern world, leaving one's ancestral land was a matter of extreme gravity, often associated with a loss of identity, protection, and divine blessing. Therefore, to "return" was not merely a change of geographic coordinates; it was a restoration of life, security, covenant alignment, and relationship.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• Shub is a primary root verb occurring over 1,000 times in the Old Testament, making it the twelfth most common verb in the Hebrew Scriptures. Its lexical range includes:

• To turn back, return, or go back (intransitive).

• To bring back, restore, refresh, or retrieve (transitive/causative).

• To turn away from, depart, or backslide (when used with a negative trajectory).

In its most basic literal sense, it describes physical movement back to a previous location. Theologically, however, it is the foundational Hebrew term for repentance. True repentance in the Old Testament is not merely an emotional state of sorrow, but a concrete, active turning away from sin and a turning back toward Yahweh.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs shub in several vivid conceptual categories:

• The Return from Exile: It is the key word used to describe Israel's physical and spiritual restoration to the Promised Land after captivity.

• The Restored Soul: In passages like Psalm 23:3, it is used to describe the Shepherd who "restores" (literally, "causes to return") the soul of the weary believer.

• The Divine Pivot: It describes God "turning" from His burning anger or "returning" to His people in mercy when they seek Him.

• The Relational Compass: It functions as a spiritual compass. When humanity wanders into the wilderness of self-will, shub is the command and invitation to come home to the Creator.

### 4. Theological Implications

The theological weight of shub in Ruth 1:6 lies in the intersection of human action and divine initiative. Naomi's decision to return is prompted by the news that "the LORD had visited His people in giving them bread." Human returning is always a response to divine visitation.

Furthermore, shub illustrates that repentance and restoration require abandoning the false security of "Moab" (the place of compromise, compromise, and spiritual compromise) to seek the mercy of God in "Bethlehem" (the House of Bread). It shows that no matter how far one has traveled in the wrong direction, or how much has been lost along the way, God's covenant grace provides a pathway back to restoration.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth chapter 1, variations of shub occur 12 times, serving as the literary heartbeat of the narrative. In verse 6, the word marks the dramatic turning point of the opening chapter.

Elimelech’s family had gone "to dwell" in Moab because of a famine—a step that led to death, emptiness, and despair. Naomi is left destitute. The phrase "that she might return" represents her awakening. She realizes that Moab has nothing left for her, and she turns her face back toward the covenant land.

Crucially, this physical return sets the stage for a spiritual return. For Naomi, it is a return to her people and her God. For Ruth, the Moabite daughter-in-law, this physical "returning" with Naomi becomes a radical journey of conversion, where she turns away from her native gods to take refuge under the wings of the God of Israel.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

There is a powerful literary contrast in Ruth chapter 1 between those who turn back to their old lives and those who return to God. Naomi urges both her daughters-in-law to "go, return each to her mother's house" (1:8). Orpah eventually kisses Naomi and "returned to her people and to her gods" (1:15). Orpah's return is a regression to paganism. Ruth, however, refuses to return to Moab, choosing instead to "return" with Naomi to Bethlehem. Through this wordplay, the narrative demonstrates that true shub is not merely about returning to what is comfortable or familiar, but turning toward the living God, even when the future is completely unknown.
