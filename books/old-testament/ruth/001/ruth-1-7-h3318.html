WENT OUT - ( yatsa , Strong's H3318 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yatsa is a high-frequency action word meaning to go out, exit, emerge, or depart from a specific location. In the narrative of Ruth 1:7, it marks a pivotal physical and spiritual departure as Naomi exits the foreign, pagan land of Moab to return to Judah, the land of Yahweh’s covenant and provision. This departure is not merely a change of address, but a decisive step of faith, moving from a place of death, emptiness, and tragedy toward hope and restoration.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The verb yatsa is a primary root in the Hebrew Scriptures, occurring over a thousand times. Its most basic concrete physical meaning is to move from an inside space to an outside space—such as exiting a tent, a city, a house, or a geographic territory.

• In ancient Near Eastern culture, departing from one's homeland or household was a high-stakes action. A person's identity, safety, and economic security were entirely bound up in their local family structure and land. To "go out" from a place meant cutting ties of security and stepping into vulnerability.

• Historically, yatsa is the foundational verb used for the Exodus, where Israel "went out" from Egypt. Thus, the word carries a historical resonance of deliverance, leaving bondage, and embarking on a journey toward God’s promised heritage.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The root verb yatsa signifies the act of coming or going forth, proceeding, or departing.

• In the Qal stem (as found in Ruth 1:7), it means to go out, go forth, or depart from a place.

• In the Hiphil (causative) stem, it means to bring out, lead forth, or deliver (frequently used of God bringing Israel out of Egypt).

• The semantic range includes:

• Physical departure from a boundary, city, or land.

• The rising or coming forth of natural elements (like the sun rising or water flowing from a spring).

• The birth or descent of offspring (coming forth from one's body).

• Formal pronouncements or commands proceeding from a ruler’s mouth.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Yatsa is frequently paired with bo (to enter or come in) to describe the full spectrum of daily activity or leadership (e.g., "going out and coming in" representing daily life or administrative oversight).

• It is used metaphorically for plants sprouting or budding (going forth from the ground), showcasing life emerging from hiddenness.

• It is also used in judicial contexts where a verdict "goes forth" from a court, indicating a decisive, authoritative movement that alters reality.

• The word pictures a boundary crossing. To yatsa is to cross a threshold, leaving behind what was behind and entering an entirely new sphere of existence.

4. Theological Implications

• Theologically, yatsa highlights the transition from exile to redemption. When God’s people "go out" from places of rebellion, judgment, or pagan influence, it is an act of repentance and realignment with God's covenant.

• It underscores divine sovereignty and human agency. While Naomi is the one physically walking out, her departure is prompted by the news that "the Lord had visited His people in giving them bread" (Ruth 1:6). Her physical "going out" is a response to God's gracious "coming down" to restore His people.

• The Exodus motif is subtly woven into this departure. Just as Israel went out of Egypt empty-handed and was provided for by God, Naomi goes out of Moab bereaved and empty, yet she is being led back to the place where fullness will be restored.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:7, yatsa is used to describe Naomi's physical departure: "Therefore she went out from the place where she was..." The text emphasizes leaving the "place where she was" to highlight a clean break with Moab.

• Moab was a place of compromise, famine, bereavement, and death for Naomi's family. To stay in Moab was to remain in a spiritual graveyard.

• The verb initiates the physical journey of restoration. It is the exact tipping point of the narrative. Up to this point, Naomi's story has been one of descending, settling, and losing. With yatsa, the direction reverses; she departs the land of loss to return (shub) to the land of promise.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 12:4: "So Abram departed as the Lord had spoken to him, and Lot went with him. And Abram was seventy-five years old when he departed from Haran."

• Exodus 12:41: "And it came to pass at the end of the four hundred and thirty years—on that very same day—it came to pass that all the armies of the Lord went out from the land of Egypt."

• Isaiah 55:12: "For you shall go out with joy, and be led out with peace; the mountains and the hills shall break forth into singing before you, and all the trees of the field shall clap their hands."

• Micah 5:2: "But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah, though you are little among the thousands of Judah, yet out of you shall come forth to Me the One to be Ruler in Israel, whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The contrast between yatsa (going out) and shub (returning) in the book of Ruth is striking. Naomi "goes out" of Moab to "return" to Bethlehem. The two movements together constitute the biblical pattern of repentance: leaving behind the far country and returning to the Father’s house.

• It is worth noting that Naomi’s daughters-in-law initially "went out" with her. However, only Ruth persisted, showing that a temporary "going out" from paganism is not the same as a lifelong covenantal "return" to the true God.
