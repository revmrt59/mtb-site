PLACE - ( maqom , Strong's H4725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew noun maqom designates a physical space, locality, or dwelling where someone stands, resides, or takes up a position. In the context of Ruth 1:7, it marks the geographic and spiritual departure point for Naomi and her daughters-in-law. It represents Moab—a place of exile, famine, compromise, and profound loss—which Naomi must leave behind to return to Judah, the place of God's covenant presence and provision. This transition from one "place" to another mirrors the spiritual journey of repentance, moving away from a site of spiritual barrenness to enter the place of restoration.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The noun maqom is derived from the root verb qum, which means "to arise," "to stand," or "to establish." Etymologically, a maqom is a "standing place" or an "established spot." It is the physical ground upon which someone stands or the specific territory where something is set up. In ancient Near Eastern thought, a place was not merely an abstract coordinate on a map; it was defined by what occurred there, who ruled over it, and the relationships experienced within its boundaries. Thus, a person's maqom represents their current state of existence, safety, or displacement.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The semantic range of maqom includes:

• A physical locality, spot, or geographic region.

• A town, city, or country of residence.

• A home, dwelling place, or room to inhabit.

• A sacred site, shrine, or sanctuary designated for worship.

In Ruth 1:7, maqom refers specifically to the land of Moab where Naomi had dwelt for ten years. It signifies the physical home she established with her husband and sons, which ultimately became a place of three family graves.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout the Old Testament, maqom is used to paint powerful pictures of spiritual reality through physical geography:

• The Place of Divine Encounter: Jacob slept at a "certain place" and woke up to realize that the maqom was the gate of heaven, renaming it Bethel (Genesis 28:11-17). Here, a common physical space becomes holy ground because of God's presence.

• The Place of Holy Ground: Moses is commanded to remove his sandals because the maqom where he stands is holy (Exodus 3:5).

• The Place of Rest: The promised land is described as the maqom of rest that God prepared for Israel, contrasting with the wilderness and Egypt.

• The Place of Security: Standing in a "broad place" symbolizes deliverance, safety, and freedom, whereas a "narrow place" indicates distress and restriction.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, maqom reminds us that God works within the boundaries of physical human history and geography. Where we reside matters to God.

• Geography Reflects Spiritual Alignment: In the Old Testament, leaving the Land of Promise (Judah) for a pagan territory (Moab) often resulted in spiritual and physical ruin. Naomi's departure from "the place where she was" represents a necessary turning back (repentance) to the place of covenant blessing.

• God’s Sovereignty over Space: God determines the boundaries of our dwelling places. He uses physical displacement—famine, exile, and relocation—to break self-sufficiency and draw His people back to Himself.

• The Empty Place vs. The Filled Place: Moab had become an empty maqom for Naomi. By returning to Bethlehem ("the house of bread"), she moves back to the place where God is actively visiting His people with life and provision.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:7, the narrative emphasizes that Naomi "went out from the place where she was." The storyteller purposefully does not name Moab in this specific clause, referring to it simply as "the place." This highlights Naomi's emotional and spiritual detachment from the land of her exile. It was no longer a home; it was merely "the place where she was"—a site of grief, widowhood, and childlessness. Leaving this maqom requires courage. It is an act of naked faith, as Naomi has no physical security waiting for her in Judah. The journey is not just a change of address, but a deliberate abandonment of a pagan environment to throw herself upon the mercy of Yahweh in His designated place of blessing.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 22:3 - "And he arose and went to the place of which God had told him."

• Genesis 28:17 - "And he was afraid and said, 'How awesome is this place! This is none other than the house of God, and this is the gate of heaven!'"

• Exodus 3:5 - "Then He said, 'Do not draw near this place. Take your sandals off your feet, for the place where you stand is holy ground.'"

• Joshua 5:15 - "Then the Commander of the Lord’s army said to Joshua, 'Take your sandal off your foot, for the place where you stand is holy.' And Joshua did so."

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

In Hebrew narrative style, transitions are often marked by movement between different maqoms. When God intends to transform a life, He frequently calls the individual to leave their current maqom. Abraham had to leave his place (Genesis 12:1), Jacob had to flee his place (Genesis 28:10), and Naomi had to exit her place of tragedy. These geographic disruptions serve as the crucible for spiritual transformation, demonstrating that our ultimate resting place is found not in a earthly territory, but in the presence of God Himself.
