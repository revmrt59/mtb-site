RETURN - ( shub , Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shub means to turn back, return, or go back to a starting point. In the book of Ruth, and specifically in Ruth 1:7, it functions as a central thematic word, describing Naomi's physical relocation from Moab back to Bethlehem, which simultaneously symbolizes a spiritual return to the covenant land, community, and blessing of Yahweh.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The concrete, physical imagery of shub is that of turning around and heading back in the opposite direction. It implies a conscious decision to leave a current path or destination and retrace one's steps to the point of origin. In the ancient Near East, where geography, family, and religion were deeply intertwined, returning to one's homeland was not merely a change of address; it was a return to one's identity, protection, and gods. In the narrative of Ruth, Naomi's physical turnaround on the road from Moab represents a reversal of her husband Elimelech's original, faithless decision to leave the Promised Land during a famine.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shub is one of the most frequent words in the Old Testament, occurring over 1,000 times. Its primary lexical meaning is to return, turn back, go back, or bring back. It covers several distinct semantic ranges:

• Physical movement: Going back to a geographical location or home.

• Relational restoration: Turning back to a person after a period of separation or estrangement.

• Spiritual conversion: Turning away from evil, idols, or foreign lands to return to Yahweh in covenant faithfulness (often translated as "repent" or "be converted").

• Restoration of state: Returning to a previous condition of blessing, prosperity, or life.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Throughout Scripture, shub is utilized to paint vivid pictures of restoration and repentance:

• The path of repentance: It describes a traveler who realizes they are on a dangerous, incorrect road, stops, turns 180 degrees, and walks back to the safe, original path.

• The covenant relationship: It depicts a spouse returning to a marriage covenant, or children returning to their father after wandering.

• Creation and mortality: It is used of humanity returning to the dust of the earth from which they were taken.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, shub lies at the very heart of the Old Testament concept of repentance. Repentance is not merely intellectual regret or emotional sorrow; it is an active, physical, and relational turning back to God. Because God's covenant with Israel was bound to the physical Promised Land, departing from the land was often synonymous with departing from God's immediate presence and blessing. Conversely, returning (shub) to the land represents a renewal of faith in Yahweh’s sovereign provision and a submission to His covenant rule.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:7, the physical and spiritual dimensions of shub converge. Naomi has spent ten years in Moab, during which she lost her husband and both sons, leaving her empty and destitute. Upon hearing that Yahweh has visited His people by providing bread in Bethlehem, Naomi decides to "return to the land of Judah."

This word is the literary and theological engine of Ruth 1, appearing twelve times in this single chapter. When Naomi sets out "to return," she is transitioning from a place of death, famine, and pagan compromise (Moab) to the place of life, harvest, and covenant promise (Judah). Interestingly, while Naomi is returning to her home, her Moabite daughter-in-law, Ruth, is "returning" with her. For Ruth, this physical journey is a first-time turning to Yahweh, as she abandons her national gods to find refuge under the wings of the God of Israel.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Isaiah 55:7: "Let the wicked forsake his way, And the unrighteous man his thoughts; Let him return to the LORD, And He will have mercy on him; And to our God, For He will abundantly pardon."

• Hosea 14:1: "O Israel, return to the LORD your God, For you have stumbled because of your iniquity;"

• Malachi 3:7: "Yet from the days of your fathers You have gone away from My ordinances And have not kept them. Return to Me, and I will return to you," says the LORD of hosts."

7. Other Relevant Insights

The repetition of shub in the dialogue of Ruth 1 highlights the contrast between Naomi's despair and Ruth's burgeoning faith. Naomi repeatedly urges her daughters-in-law to "go, return" (shub) to their mothers' houses and their foreign gods (Ruth 1:8, 11, 12, 15). Orpah yields and "returns to her people and to her gods" (Ruth 1:15). However, Ruth refuses to turn back, choosing instead to bind herself to Naomi and Naomi's God. The literary design uses the repetition of shub to emphasize that true covenant loyalty requires a complete abandonment of one's past and an unwavering turning toward Yahweh.
