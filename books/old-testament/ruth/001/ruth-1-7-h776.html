LAND - ( erets , Strong's H0776 )

Summary:

Naomi's return to the "land of Judah" in Ruth 1:7 is more than a geographical relocation; it represents a physical and spiritual return to the domain of God's covenantal presence, provision, and promise. The Hebrew word erets denotes concrete, physical territory, but within the redemptive narrative of Scripture, it carries immense theological weight. Returning to this land signifies a transition from the tragic, foreign soil of Moab—a place of famine, death, and exile—back to the sphere of Yahweh's sovereign care and covenant faithfulness, where Israel's inheritance is realized.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew word erets represents one of the most foundational concepts in the Old Testament, occurring over 2,500 times. At its most basic level, it describes the physical ground beneath our feet, the soil that produces food, or the earth in contrast to the heavens. In the ancient Near Eastern world, land was not merely property to be bought and sold; it was the very source of life, national identity, and security. For Israel, erets was intimately tied to the covenant. To be in the land was to be in the place of God's blessing, safety, and direct rule. To be cast out of the land, or to leave it voluntarily, was equivalent to entering a spiritual wasteland where the visible blessings of Yahweh's covenant were obscured.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The term erets has three primary levels of meaning depending on the context:

• The earth in its entirety, as opposed to the heavens (Genesis 1:1).

• The dry land or ground, as opposed to the seas (Genesis 1:10).

• A specific geographical territory, country, or national boundary (Genesis 12:1).

In Ruth 1:7, erets is used in this third sense, referring specifically to the "land of Judah." It marks out the physical, sovereign territory assigned to the tribe of Judah, which was the covenant homeland of Elimelech's family.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

Scripture uses erets to construct powerful pictures of spiritual reality:

• The Land of Promise: Erets is the physical stage upon which the drama of redemption is played out. It is the tangible inheritance promised to Abraham and his descendants.

• The Place of Resting: The land is often pictured as a place of rest (menuhah) provided by God after wandering or exile.

• The Fruitful Garden vs. The Wasteland: Under God's blessing, the erets flows with milk and honey. Under His judgment, the same erets spits out its inhabitants or suffers under famine, mirroring the spiritual state of the people.

4. Theological Implications

The theology of erets is deeply rooted in ownership and stewardship. The land belongs ultimately to Yahweh; the Israelites are merely tenants and strangers living under His grace (Leviticus 25:23).

• Covenantal Alignment: Living in the land required obedience. Famine in the land (as seen in Ruth 1:1) was a covenantal warning sign of spiritual decline, prompting a call to repentance.

• Exile and Return: Leaving the land of promise for a foreign nation (Moab) represented a departure from the sphere of the covenant. Conversely, returning to the land (erets) is a physical picture of repentance (teshuvah), which literally means "to return." When Naomi and Ruth set foot on the road to Judah, they are returning to Yahweh Himself.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:7, the mention of "the land of Judah" serves as a sharp contrast to the "country of Moab" mentioned earlier in the chapter. In the Hebrew text, Moab is repeatedly referred to as the sedeh (fields/countryside) of Moab, implying a temporary, foreign, or pastoral region. In contrast, Judah is called the erets—a word signifying a established, covenantal homeland. Naomi had heard in Moab that Yahweh had visited His people by giving them bread (Ruth 1:6). Therefore, leaving the place of death and entering the "land of Judah" is a journey from emptiness to fullness, from curse to blessing, and from isolation back into the community of faith.

7. Other Relevant Insights

In the ancient world, gods were local deities whose power was thought to be confined to specific territories. By returning to the erets of Judah, Naomi is not just returning to her family; she is returning to the active, manifest dominion of Yahweh. Ruth’s willingness to accompany Naomi into this erets—leaving her own native land and gods behind—underscores the depth of her conversion. She abandons her identity tied to the soil of Moab to claim an inheritance in the soil where Yahweh rules.
