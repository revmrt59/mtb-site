LORD - ( Yahweh , Strong's H3068 )

Summary:

The name Yahweh (translated "LORD" in all capital letters) is the personal, covenantal, and self-revealed name of the one true God of Israel. Expressing His eternal self-existence, absolute faithfulness, and relational intimacy, this sacred name signifies that God is always present with His people to redeem and sustain them. In Ruth 1:8, Naomi invokes this specific covenant name to ask for divine favor and loyal kindness upon her Moabite daughters-in-law, showing that Yahweh's grace, covenant love, and relational care transcend national and cultural boundaries even in times of profound loss.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The name Yahweh is the Tetragrammaton, consisting of the four Hebrew consonants Y-H-W-H. Out of extreme reverence for the holiness of this name, post-exilic Jewish tradition avoided pronouncing it aloud, substituting the title Adonai ("Lord") during public readings. This practice is reflected in modern English translations, which render the name as "LORD" in capital letters.

Unlike generic titles for God, such as Elohim (which emphasizes His supreme power, majesty, and role as Creator), Yahweh is God’s unique personal name. It was explicitly revealed to Moses at the burning bush in Exodus 3:13-15. When Moses asked for God’s name, God replied, "I AM WHO I AM," linking the name Yahweh to the Hebrew verb meaning "to be." It represents God not as an abstract force or a distant deity, but as the living, active, and present Lord who enters into binding relationships with human beings.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The name is derived from the archaic Hebrew verb hayah (or hawah), meaning "to be," "to exist," or "to happen." Formally, it is a third-person singular imperfect form, which can translate as "He is," "He will be," or "He actively causes to be."

Lexical analysis defines the name through several key dimensions:

• Self-Existence and Eternity: He possesses life inherent in Himself, independent of creation. He is the ultimate reality, without beginning or end.

• Unchangeableness: He is eternally consistent in His character, purposes, and holy nature.

• Covenant Faithfulness: The name is explicitly tied to His redemptive promises. When God acts under the name Yahweh, He acts in accordance with His sworn covenants, demonstrating His truthfulness and reliability.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Yahweh occurs over 6,800 times in the Old Testament, making it the most frequently used name for God. It is often paired with specific historical contexts or descriptive titles to paint vivid word pictures of His relationship with His people:

• The Covenant Partner: He is the God who "remembers His covenant forever" (Psalm 105:8).

• The Shepherd: Yahweh is described as a shepherd who guides, feeds, and protects His flock (Psalm 23:1), illustrating tender, personal care.

• The Strong Tower: Proverbs 18:10 depicts the name of Yahweh as a physical fortress where the righteous find safety, emphasizing security, refuge, and absolute protection.

• The Redeemer: He is the one who rescues His people from bondage, not because they deserve it, but because He is faithful to His own character and word.

4. Theological Implications

• Relational Proximity: God is not a distant, detached cosmic architect. The use of Yahweh indicates that He desires to be known personally, named, and called upon by His creatures.

• Immutable Grace: Because Yahweh is self-existent and self-determining, His grace does not depend on human merit. He loves and redeems because it is His nature to do so. He remains faithful even when His people are faithless.

• Revelation of Character: In Exodus 34:6-7, God proclaims "the name of the LORD," defining Yahweh as "merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abounding in goodness and truth." Thus, the name itself is shorthand for perfect, loyal love (chesed) and unmerited mercy.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:8, Naomi speaks to Orpah and Ruth: "Go, return each to her mother’s house. The LORD deal kindly with you, as you have dealt with the dead and with me."

This invocation carries profound contextual weight:

• Bitter Grief and Faithful Theology: Naomi is in Moab, devastated by the deaths of her husband and sons. She feels empty and afflicted. Yet, in her deepest grief, she does not abandon the covenant name of God. She appeals to Yahweh, demonstrating that her faith, though strained, remains anchored in the personal God of Israel.

• Covenant Kindness to Outsiders: Naomi prays that Yahweh will show "kindness" (Hebrew: chesed, meaning loyal, covenant-keeping love) to these Moabite women. This is a remarkable theological moment. Naomi is asking the specific covenant God of Israel to bless non-Israelite women in a foreign land. It signals to the reader that Yahweh's reach, mercy, and loyal love are not confined by national borders, but are available to all who act with love and integrity.

• The Source of Hope: By using the name Yahweh, Naomi points to the only true source of restoration. Though she feels the Almighty (Shaddai) has dealt bitterly with her (Ruth 1:20), her prayer in verse 8 acts as a prophetic seed. Yahweh will indeed answer this prayer, showing extraordinary chesed to Ruth, and ultimately to Naomi herself, through the provision of Boaz.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 2:4 - "This is the history of the heavens and the earth when they were created, in the day that the LORD God made the earth and the heavens,"

• Exodus 3:15 - "Moreover God said to Moses, 'Thus you shall say to the children of Israel: "The LORD God of your fathers, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob, has sent me to you. This is My name forever, and this is My memorial to all generations."'"

• Psalm 23:1 - "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want."

• Proverbs 18:10 - "The name of the LORD is a strong tower; The righteous run to it and are safe."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Contrast of Divine Names: In the book of Ruth, Naomi oscillates between using Yahweh (the name of covenant closeness and grace) and Shaddai (the Almighty, emphasizing sovereign power and discipline). In Ruth 1:20-21, she claims Shaddai has afflicted her, but her blessing in Ruth 1:8 is a prayer for Yahweh to show mercy. This tension highlights the believer's struggle to reconcile God's painful providences with His unchanging covenant love.

• Quiet Sovereignty: Throughout the book of Ruth, Yahweh rarely speaks or performs overt, miraculous signs (like parting seas or sending fire). Instead, His presence is felt through the daily actions of ordinary people showing kindness, demonstrating that Yahweh works silently through human relationships and ordinary faithfulness to accomplish His grand redemptive purposes.
