GO - ( yalak , Strong's H3212 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb yalak (Strong's H3212) is a fundamental term of motion meaning "to go," "to walk," or "to journey." In Ruth 1:8, Naomi employs this verb in its imperative plural form as she commands her widowed daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth, to depart and return to their Moabite families. This instruction marks a decisive turning point in the narrative, transforming a physical journey into a testing of relational loyalty and faith. While the term primarily denotes physical movement, its contextual placement in the book of Ruth highlights a profound transition from duty and shared grief to personal destiny, setting the stage for Ruth's subsequent voluntary decision to align herself with the God of Israel.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew verb yalak is closely associated with the companion root halak, representing physical movement, progression, and the conduct of daily life. In the ancient Near East, where travel was laborious and undertaken primarily on foot, a journey was never trivial. To "go" or "walk" required physical exertion, a chosen path, and a specific destination. In patriarchal culture, walking also served as a legal and covenantal metaphor; to walk through a land was to claim ownership of it, and to walk with someone implied shared destiny, protection, and fellowship. When Naomi commands Orpah and Ruth to "go," she is releasing them from their shared path of family obligation, telling them to walk away from her own journey back to Judah and to forge a different path in their native land of Moab.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• yalak possesses a robust semantic range centered on movement and lifestyle:

• Physical Departure: The primary meaning is to travel, walk, or depart from one location to proceed to another.

• Directional Imperative: In the imperative form, the verb functions as an urgent command or invitation to set out, leave, or take action immediately.

• Personal Conduct: Metaphorically, the verb describes how a person lives or "walks" through life, denoting their ethical decisions, religious devotion, and moral path.

• Relational Release: In personal dynamics, it signifies the act of sending someone away or permitting them to depart without further obligation.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• The Path of Life: Hebrew literature regularly utilizes yalak to describe life as a journey on a path. One either walks in safety on the path of the righteous or stumbles along the path of the wicked.

• Divine Guidance: The verb frequently depicts God leading His covenant people, such as carrying them through the wilderness or directing their footsteps in truth.

• Flow and Spread: In a non-human sense, yalak is used to describe the flowing of water, the movement of boundaries, or the spreading of a fragrant aroma, capturing the concept of unstoppable, natural progression.

4. Theological Implications

• Walking with God: Throughout the Old Testament, walking (yalak) is the physical expression of a faithful relationship with the Creator. It represents active, progressive obedience rather than static belief.

• Covenantal Separation: In the ancient world, departing from one's family meant departing from their gods. When Naomi tells her daughters-in-law to "go," she is not just offering them geographical release; she is acknowledging that going back to Moab means returning to their national deity, Chemosh. This highlights the spiritual gravity of the decision before them.

• Sovereignty and Decision: The use of yalak in salvation history shows that while human beings choose where they will go, the sovereign Lord directs their steps. Ruth's refusal to "go" back to Moab, and her choice instead to "go" to Judah with Naomi, illustrates a supernaturally guided conversion. Her physical steps reflect a deeper spiritual migration into the covenant family of Yahweh.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:8, Naomi's command, "Go, return..." (lekna shobna), reflects a mixture of deep pastoral care, sorrow, and bitter pragmatism. Having lost her husband and both sons, Naomi is utterly destitute. As she prepares to travel back to Bethlehem, she recognizes that two foreign, Moabite widows will face extreme social marginalization, poverty, and prejudice in Israel. By commanding them to "go," Naomi acts with remarkable selflessness. She relinquishes her legal right to their support, choosing their financial security and potential for remarriage over her own companionship. This command serves as the ultimate loyalty test: Orpah obeys the command and goes back, while Ruth resists, transforming the verb yalak from a command of separation into a lifelong promise of shared steps.

• Psalm 23:4: "Yea, though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil; For You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me."

7. Other Relevant Insights

• Rhetorical Force: Naomi pairs the imperative "go" (lekna) with "return" (shobna). This double command functions as a strong rhetorical push, urging her daughters-in-law to make a clean break from her.

• Voluntary Action: The passage highlights the reality of human responsibility. Naomi does not drag her daughters-in-law to Judah, nor does she force them to leave. They are given the freedom to choose their path. This emphasizes that true faith and covenant loyalty cannot be coerced; they must be chosen freely.
