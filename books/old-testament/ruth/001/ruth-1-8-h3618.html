DAUGHTERS-IN-LAW - ( kallah , Strong's H3618 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word kallah refers to a young woman who has entered a family through marriage, translating to "daughter-in-law," "bride," or "spouse." In the opening chapter of the book of Ruth, this word establishes the profound covenantal and relational boundaries that exist between Naomi and her two Moabite daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth. Despite the devastating loss of their husbands, the use of kallah underscores their formal integration into Naomi's household. The term represents a status of protection, expectation, and family belonging that makes Ruth's eventual decision to remain with Naomi an act of extraordinary covenant loyalty.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The word kallah is derived from the verbal root kalal, which means to complete, crown, make perfect, or bring to completion. This root paints a beautiful picture of the role of a young bride or daughter-in-law in the ancient Near East. A kallah is the one who completes a household, bringing the potential for new life, continuity, and blessing to a family line. She is viewed as "crowned" with honor and responsibility as she transitions into her husband's family. In Israelite society, a daughter-in-law was not viewed merely as an in-law by marriage, but as a daughter fully adopted into the covenantal protection and identity of her husband’s house.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

Lexically, kallah has a dual focus depending on the context of the relationship being described:

• Bride or Spouse: In poetic, prophetic, and wisdom literature, the term represents a newly married woman in relation to her husband. It conveys intimacy, joy, and the fresh beauty of a new marriage.

• Daughter-in-law: In historical narratives and legal codes, the word designates a female who has married into a family, viewed from the perspective of her husband's parents.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

The Old Testament employs kallah to illustrate relationships of intense devotion and covenantal responsibility.

• The Family Circle: In the legal codes of the Pentateuch, a daughter-in-law is protected under strict holiness guidelines. She is treated with the same moral boundaries as a natural daughter, signifying her total integration into the family circle.

• The Adorned Bride: Prophetic literature frequently uses kallah to picture Israel adorned in beauty, waiting for her Bridegroom, Yahweh. It evokes images of bridal jewelry, wedding garments, and joyful celebration.

• The Beloved Spouse: In the Song of Solomon, the word is used repeatedly as an endearing term of affection, highlighting the exclusivity, beauty, and safety of marital love.

4. Theological Implications

Theologically, kallah is deeply intertwined with the concepts of covenant (chesed) and redemption. In Israel, the survival of a family name was paramount. A kallah who became a widow without children faced extreme vulnerability but also held a unique claim on the family for redemption (the levirate marriage). The term reminds us of God's design for the family as a place of refuge and continuity. Furthermore, the transition of Israel from a wilderness wanderer to God's own "bride" (kallah) illustrates God's redeeming love, raising His people from obscurity to a position of crowned honor.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:8, Naomi addresses Orpah and Ruth as her "two daughters-in-law." This context is steeped in tragedy. The three women are widows in Moab, stripped of male protection and economic provision. By using the term kallah, the text emphasizes that although Mahlon and Chilion are dead, the relational bond between Naomi and these foreign women remains intact. Naomi's subsequent plea for them to return to Moab is a selfless act; she is releasing her kallot (plural of kallah) from their family obligations so they might find "rest" and security in new marriages. Orpah kisses her mother-in-law goodbye, but Ruth refuses to break the bond of the kallah, choosing instead to bind her destiny to Naomi and Naomi's God.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The transformation of the kallah is the narrative engine of the book of Ruth. Ruth begins as a grieving, childless kallah (daughter-in-law) in a foreign land. Through her faithful devotion, she travels to Bethlehem, gleans in the fields of Boaz, and eventually becomes a kallah (bride) once again. This redemption story culminates in the birth of Obed, securing the family line of Elimelech, which ultimately leads to King David and Jesus Christ.
