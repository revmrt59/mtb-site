DEAD - ( mut , Strong's H4191 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb mut fundamentally means to die, expire, or be put to death. In Ruth 1:8, it appears as a plural participle (hammetim), referring specifically to those who have died—Naomi's husband, Elimelech, and her two sons, Mahlon and Chilion. In this poignant pastoral context, the term goes beyond a clinical observation of mortality; it serves as a painful marker of grief, shattered hopes, and the severe vulnerability of three widows in the ancient Near East, while simultaneously highlighting the profound loyalty and kindness (chesed) shown by Orpah and Ruth to their departed husbands.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The root mut is one of the most common verbs in the Hebrew Bible, occurring over 800 times. It describes the cessation of physical life, whether of humans or animals. In the ancient Near Eastern context, death was not just an individual event but a communal tragedy. For a family in ancient Israel, the death of a husband and sons meant the cutting off of the family line, the loss of ancestral land inheritance, and absolute economic destitution for the surviving women. The background of mut in the book of Ruth is set against the dark spiritual and social backdrop of the period of the Judges, where famine and death ravage a family that fled Bethlehem for Moab.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary meaning of mut in the Qal stem is to die, perish, or become dead.

• When used as a participle (met), it functions substantively to mean "one who is dead" or "the dead."

• In the Hiphil stem (causative), it means to put to death, kill, or execute.

• In the Hophal stem (passive causative), it means to be put to death or executed.

• In Ruth 1:8, the word occurs as a plural masculine participle with the definite article (hammetim), directly translating to "the dead ones." It refers specifically to the deceased husbands: Elimelech, Mahlon, and Chilion.

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Mut is frequently paired with concepts of mourning, burial, and the preservation of a deceased husband's name through levirate marriage customs.

• It is often used in contrast to life (chayyim), representing the absolute boundary of earthly existence.

• The Scriptures use mut to depict the inevitability of human mortality resulting from the Fall in Genesis.

• In poetic and wisdom literature, mut occasionally visualizes a descent into Sheol, the place of the dead, where physical strength and earthly opportunities cease.

### 4. Theological Implications

• The presence of the "dead" in Ruth 1:8 highlights the severe consequences of life in a fallen world and, potentially, the consequence of leaving the Promised Land for Moab.

• Theologically, death (mut) sets the stage for the demonstration of covenant loyalty (chesed). Naomi prays that Yahweh will show chesed to Ruth and Orpah just as they showed chesed to "the dead" (their late husbands) and to Naomi. True covenant love transcends the grave; the widows' kindness to their husbands while they were alive, and their continued care for Naomi, reflects God's own enduring faithfulness.

• The ultimate resolution of the book of Ruth involves overcoming the tragedy of the "dead" through redemption (goel) and the birth of Obed, which ensures that the name of the dead (Mahlon) is not blotted out from Israel, eventually leading to the lineage of David and Jesus Christ.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:8, Naomi is urging her Moabite daughters-in-law to return to their mothers' homes. When she mentions "the dead," she is referring to Mahlon and Chilion (and possibly Elimelech).

• The nuance here is deeply emotional and pastoral. Naomi recognizes that Orpah and Ruth did not abandon their husbands during their illnesses or trials in Moab. Their kindness (chesed) was active toward those who are now "dead."

• The word serves as a structural hinge in the narrative: it transitions the story from the grief of past deaths to the desperate hope of future survival and redemption. It shows that even in the face of the finality of mut, human kindness and divine providence can bring forth new life.

### 6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 23:3: "Then Abraham stood up from before his dead, and spoke to the sons of Heth, saying,"

• Genesis 35:18: "And so it was, as her soul was departing (for she died), that she called his name Ben-Oni; but his father called him Benjamin."

• Ruth 1:5: "Then both Mahlon and Chilion died; so the woman survived her two sons and her husband."

• Ruth 4:5: "Then Boaz said, 'On the day you buy the field from the hand of Naomi, you must also buy it from Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead, to perpetuate the name of the dead through his inheritance.'"

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

• The repetition of terms related to death in the first chapter of Ruth (five times in verses 3, 5, 8, 12, 17) establishes a literary atmosphere of complete bereavement. This heavy repetition emphasizes the magnitude of Naomi's loss and the depth of the hopelessness from which God's redemption will rescue her.

• In the ancient Near East, the cult of the dead was highly prominent among Israel's neighbors, including Moab. However, the Hebrew text avoids any mystical or ancestor-worship connotations. The dead are simply described as those who have passed away, and the focus remains entirely on covenant faithfulness (chesed) to the living and honoring the memory of the deceased through ethical, legal means.
