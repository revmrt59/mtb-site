SAID - ( 'amar , Strong's H559 )

Summary:

In the opening of Ruth 1:8, the Hebrew verb translated as "said" initiates a pivotal moment of relational release and self-sacrificing love. Rather than a mere verbal exchange, this word carries the weight of a maternal directive, a prayer for divine blessing, and a painful discharge of duty. As Naomi addresses her grieving daughters-in-law, her spoken words bridge the bitterness of her personal loss with her deep desire for their future security, modeling a costly love that seeks the welfare of others even in the midst of profound personal grief.

Full Study:

### 1. Word Picture / Background

The Hebrew verb 'amar is one of the most frequently used words in the Old Testament, occurring thousands of times. Far from a dry, mechanical act of speech, 'amar represents the externalization of a person's inner thoughts, intentions, or will. In the ancient Near East, spoken words were viewed as dynamic forces that carried real substance and consequence. This was especially true when spoken by a family matriarch or patriarch. In the context of a family crisis, the words of the head of the household functioned as authoritative instructions, blessings, or legal releases. When Naomi speaks to her daughters-in-law on the road back to Judah, her words are not casual conversation; they are a formal, weighty declaration designed to reshape the destiny of her family.

### 2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The primary meaning of 'amar is to say, speak, utter, or declare. Depending on the grammatical context and the relationship between the speaker and the listener, its semantic range expands significantly:

• To speak or utter a statement.

• To command, direct, or bid someone to do something.

• To think or resolve within oneself (literally, "to say in one's heart").

• To intend, promise, or designate.

In Ruth 1:8, the word appears in the Qal imperfect consecutive form (watto'mer), which is the standard narrative tense in Biblical Hebrew. It functions to carry the storyline forward, introducing a speech that is both an authoritative maternal directive ("Go, return") and a priestly prayer of blessing ("The Lord deal kindly with you").

### 3. Usage and Word Pictures

The verb 'amar is used in several distinct ways across the Old Testament to convey different aspects of communication:

• The Creative Spoken Word: The word is used of God's creative power, where His speech immediately translates into material reality. When God speaks, existence conforms to His will.

• The Voice of Authority: When used by kings, prophets, or parents, the word carries the force of a command that demands obedience or response.

• The Internal Voice: Frequently, the biblical writers use the phrase "said in his heart" to describe internal deliberation, planning, or belief. In this sense, speech is the direct mirror of the soul.

In the book of Ruth, 'amar serves as the primary literary vehicle for the dialogue that drives the plot. The narrative contains very little description of physical action; instead, the theological and relational weight of the story is carried almost entirely by what the characters say to one another.

### 4. Theological Implications

Theologically, 'amar reminds us that the God of the Bible is a speaking God, and human beings, made in His image, reflect this through the creative and destructive power of their own tongues. In Ruth 1:8, Naomi's speech represents a profound theological act. Amidst her grief and spiritual disorientation—where she believes the hand of the Lord has gone out against her—she still uses her speech to invoke the covenant kindness (chesed) of Yahweh upon her Pagan daughters-in-law. Her words become the means through which God’s grace is articulated. Even when Naomi feels abandoned by God, her spoken blessing over Orpah and Ruth acts as a channel of divine favor, demonstrating that human speech can minister grace even in moments of deep personal brokenness.

### 5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In the immediate context of Ruth 1:8, Naomi's 'amar initiates a painful separation. Naomi, Orpah, and Ruth are standing on the border between Moab and Judah. By speaking these words, Naomi is actively releasing her daughters-in-law from any filial obligation to care for her in her old age. In ancient Israelite culture, childless widows were among the most vulnerable members of society. By telling Orpah and Ruth to "Go, return each to her mother's house," Naomi is making a decision that guarantees her own isolation and poverty for the sake of their potential happiness and security. Her speech is characterized by selfless love; she uses her authority not to cling to them for survival, but to set them free.

### 7. Other Relevant Insights

A striking contrast exists between Naomi's words in Ruth 1:8 and her later words in Ruth 1:20-21. In verse 8, her speech is outward-focused, full of prayer, blessing, and concern for others. In verse 20, when she speaks to the women of Bethlehem, her speech turns inward, filled with bitterness as she declares that the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with her. This illustrates the complex nature of grief: the same voice that is capable of speaking profound blessings of faith over others can also struggle with deep sorrow and confusion when reflecting on its own circumstances. The narrative does not condemn Naomi for this tension, but showcases the raw honesty of her communication.
