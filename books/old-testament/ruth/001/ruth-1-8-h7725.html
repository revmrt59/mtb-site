RETURN - ( shuv, Strong's H7725 )

Summary:

The Hebrew verb shuv is one of the most frequent and crucial words in the Old Testament, meaning to turn back, return, or go back to a point of origin. In its physical sense, it denotes movement back to a geographical starting point. Spiritually, it serves as the foundational Old Testament term for repentance—turning back to God after going astray. In Naomi’s plea in Ruth 1:8, the word functions as a critical turning point where Naomi urges her Moabite daughters-in-law to abandon their journey to Judah and return to their ancestral home and gods, setting up a profound contrast between natural survival and spiritual covenant loyalty.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

The physical imagery behind the root shuv is a 180-degree turn. It describes a traveler who stops, turns around, and retraces their steps back to their starting point. The word paints a picture of restoring a previous state, relationship, or location. It implies that a boundary has been crossed or a journey undertaken, and the only way to find resolution is to reverse direction and go back. In the ancient world, leaving one's homeland meant leaving one's security, family, and national gods; therefore, to shuv (return) was a high-stakes decision involving identity, survival, and faith.

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

The verb shuv occurs over 1,000 times in the Hebrew Scriptures. In its basic active form (Qal), it means to turn back, return, or repeat. It expresses physical movement back to a previous place, person, or condition. In its causative form (Hiphil), it means to bring back, restore, or pay back. Spiritually, it denotes turning away from sin and turning back to Yahweh. Rather than a mere emotional state of regret, shuv requires a concrete, physical change in direction and behavior.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Physical Return: Used of Jacob returning to Canaan (Genesis 31:3), Israel returning to Egypt in captivity, or a person returning to their house.

• Metaphorical/Cosmic Return: Used to describe the mortality of humanity returning to the dust from which we were created (Genesis 3:19; Psalm 90:3).

• Relational/Spiritual Return: Used of Israel returning to God after periods of apostasy, which is the heart of the prophetic call to repentance.

• Covenantal Restoration: Used of God returning to His people when they repent, or God restoring the fortunes of His people (often translated as "restoring captivity").

4. Theological Implications

Theological recovery is deeply tied to shuv. Repentance in the Old Testament is not merely feeling sorry; it is a redirection of one's entire life. When Israel sinned, they walked away from the covenant; to be restored, they had to physically and spiritually turn back to God.

In the book of Ruth, shuv is a dominant literary thread, occurring 12 times in the first chapter alone. This repetition underscores the movement between Moab (a place of judgment, exile, and death) and Bethlehem (the house of bread and covenant life). The story of Ruth is fundamentally a story of returning to the place of God's blessing and experiencing His restoration.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

In Ruth 1:8, Naomi urges Orpah and Ruth to "return each to her mother's house." Naomi, operating out of deep grief, bitterness, and a sense of hopelessness, believes she has nothing left to offer them. She commands them to turn back to Moab. This request is not merely geographical; it is a test of covenant loyalty. To return to Moab meant returning to Moabite gods (Chemosh) and abandoning the God of Israel (as Naomi notes in verse 15). Orpah eventually complies and returns (shuv), choosing physical security. In contrast, Ruth refuses to turn back from following Naomi, choosing instead to bind herself to Naomi's people and Naomi's God. Ruth's refusal to shuv to Moab represents her ultimate conversion and turning to Yahweh.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Genesis 3:19: "In the sweat of your face you shall eat bread / Till you return to the ground, / For out of it you were taken; / For dust you are, / And to dust you shall return."

• Deuteronomy 30:2: "and return to the LORD your God and obey His voice, according to all that I command you today, you and your children, with all your heart and with all your soul,"

• Isaiah 55:7: "Let the wicked forsake his way, / And the unrighteous man his thoughts; / Let him return to the LORD, / And He will have mercy on him; / And to our God, / For He will abundantly pardon."

• Malachi 3:7: "Return to Me, and I will return to you," says the LORD of hosts.

7. Other Relevant Insights

The structure of Ruth chapter 1 centers entirely on the word shuv. The movement begins with Naomi hearing that Yahweh has visited His people with bread, prompting her to "return" from the country of Moab (v. 6). The dialogue on the road to Judah consists of Naomi trying to persuade her daughters-in-law to go back. By the end of the chapter, Naomi describes herself as having gone out full, but the Lord bringing her back empty (v. 21). The chapter concludes by summarizing the journey: "So Naomi returned, and Ruth the Moabitess her daughter-in-law with her, who returned from the country of Moab" (v. 22). This intense concentration of the word establishes "return" as the primary motif of redemption in the narrative.
