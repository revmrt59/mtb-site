EACH - ( ishshah , Strong's H802 )

Summary:

The Hebrew word ishshah primarily denotes a female human being ("woman") or a married woman ("wife"). In specific grammatical contexts, particularly when used distributively in instructions or descriptions, it functions as the pronoun "each" or "every woman." In Ruth 1:8, Naomi uses this word to address her two daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth, directing them to return "each" to her mother’s house. This word choice personalizes her instruction, shifting the focus from a collective group to their individual identities, decisions, and maternal homes. It highlights Naomi's pastoral tenderness and her respect for their individual futures rather than treating them as a single, bound unit.

Full Study:

1. Word Picture / Background

• The word ishshah is the feminine counterpart to the Hebrew word ish (man). In the creation account of Genesis 2:23, the woman is named ishshah because she was taken out of man (ish), establishing an essential unity, equal value, and complementary relationship.

• While the word primarily carries biological, marital, or relational designations, Hebrew grammar utilizes gendered nouns to express distributive action. When a group of women is addressed, ishshah serves as a distributive pronoun meaning "each woman" or "every single female."

• The word picture of sending "each" woman back to her "mother’s house" evokes the intimate, secure, and nurturing sphere of the maternal home, which stood in contrast to the public, legal, and patriarchal sphere of the "father's house."

2. Meaning / Lexical Definition

• The primary definition of ishshah is an adult female human being, translated as "woman" (Genesis 2:23).

• The second major meaning is a married woman, translated as "wife" (Genesis 2:24, Proverbs 18:22).

• The third meaning is distributive or reciprocal. When used in coordinate clauses or direct address to females, it translates as "each," "every one," or "one to another."

• In Ruth 1:8, the grammatical construction employs ishshah distributively. Naomi does not address Orpah and Ruth as an undifferentiated pair; she addresses them as individual women, bidding "each" to return to her own mother's household.

3. Usage and Word Pictures

• Reciprocal Idiom: In descriptions of the tabernacle and Ezekiel's visions, where elements are grammatically feminine (such as curtains or wings of cherubim), the phrase "a woman to her sister" (ishshah el-ahotah) is used idiomatically to mean "one to another" or "each to the other." This word picture conveys perfect alignment, unity, and mutual connection.

• Individual Agency: By using ishshah distributively, the text emphasizes individual moral agency. Each woman stands accountable for her own path, her own faith, and her own familial decisions.

• The Mother's House: The pairing of ishshah with "her mother's house" (bet immah) is a rare and comforting word picture. While ancient Near Eastern society was patriarchal, the mother's house was the place of comfort, marital preparation, and female solidarity. Naomi directs each woman to the place where they can find maternal solace for their grief.

4. Theological Implications

• Dignity of the Individual: The distributive use of ishshah underscores that God does not view people merely as undifferentiated groups. Orpah and Ruth are two distinct souls. God’s providence deals with "each" woman according to her heart, her choices, and her faith.

• Covenantal Freedom: Naomi's release of "each" woman demonstrates that covenant love (hesed) does not operate through coercion. Naomi releases them to their own agency, showing that true devotion must be chosen freely, as Ruth eventually demonstrates.

• Redemption in the Ordinary: The focus on the ordinary lives of "each" woman shows that the grand narrative of redemption (which eventually leads to King David and Jesus Christ) is woven through the personal, domestic decisions of individual women.

5. Contextual Nuance in the Passage

• In Ruth 1:8, Naomi, Orpah, and Ruth are standing at a literal and metaphorical crossroads on the border of Moab and Judah.

• Naomi's use of ishshah ("each") is a selfless act of pastoral care. By telling them to return "each to her mother's house," Naomi relinquishes her own rights as a mother-in-law. Under levirate customs, these women were bound to the family of Elimelech. Naomi breaks this expectation, prioritizing the emotional, marital, and physical survival of "each" woman over her own need for companionship and survival in Judah.

• This setting creates a literary foil. The distribution of "each" leads to two entirely different outcomes: one ishshah (Orpah) kisses her mother-in-law and returns to her home, while the other ishshah (Ruth) clings to her, illustrating that individual choices shape eternal destinies.

6. Key Uses in Other Verses (NKJV)

• Exodus 26:3 - "Five curtains shall be coupled to one another, and the other five curtains shall be coupled to one another." (Used twice in this verse to describe the reciprocal coupling of feminine-noun curtains).

• Exodus 26:5 - "...that the clasps may take hold of one another." (Used to describe the interlocking connection of the tabernacle loops).

• Leviticus 18:18 - "Nor shall you take a woman as a rival to her sister, to uncover her nakedness, her sister being yet alive." (Used in the primary sense of a "woman" or "wife" in relational laws).

• Ezekiel 1:9 - "Their wings touched one another." (Used to describe the wings of the living creatures touching each other in unified movement).

• Ezekiel 3:13 - "...and the noise of the wings of the living creatures that touched one another..." (Used to describe the close, synchronized contact of the wings).

7. Other Relevant Insights

• The Contrast of Destiny: The distributive "each" prepares the reader for the divergent paths of the two daughters-in-law. It highlights that being in the same family, experiencing the same tragedies, and hearing the same testimonies does not guarantee the same spiritual outcome. Each person must choose whom they will serve.

• The Goal of Rest: In Ruth 1:9, the word ishshah is used again distributively: "The Lord grant that you may find rest, each of you in the house of her husband." This links their current status as individual grieving widows (ishshah) to their future hope of finding security and rest as wives (ishshah) in new homes.
